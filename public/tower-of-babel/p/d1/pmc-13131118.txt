Anticoagulation with bivalirudin is associated with improved biocompatibility in patients supported on microaxial flow pumps
Authors: Michaela Asher, Emma Morganroth, Amit Iyengar, Michael Catalano, Cindy Song, David Rekhtman, Max Shin, Omar Toubat, Samuel Kim, Alyson Brown, Joyce Wald, Aditya Parikh, Mauer Biscotti, Marisa Cevasco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate whether an institutional shift from anticoagulation with heparin to bivalirudin in Impella 5.5 patients altered bleeding or thrombotic rates, transfusion dependency, or device biocompatibility.MethodsAll patients supported by an Impella 5.5 between August 2022 and December 2024 were reviewed retrospectively. Heparin was used before December 2023, at which point the switch was made to bivalirudin. Groups were defined by anticoagulant initiated by postimplantation day 2, and patients with crossover or anticoagulation pauses for >2 days were excluded from the laboratory analyses. Baseline characteristics, anticoagulation details, complications, and clinical outcomes were compared between the heparin and bivalirudin groups. Biocompatibility was assessed via laboratory values recorded over the first 14 days of Impella support or until device explantation in patients supported for <14 days.ResultsAmong the 168 patients, 83 (49%) received heparin and 85 (51%) received bivalirudin. Baseline characteristics were similar in the 2 groups (P > .05), as were rates of thrombotic events (13% vs 14%; P = .871), total bleeding events (37% vs 30%; P = .275), and transfusion requirements (57% vs 51%; P = .433). However, bivalirudin patients demonstrated significantly faster rates of platelet recovery and lactate dehydrogenase reduction after Impella implantation (all P < .05), had fewer insertion site bleeds that required reoperation (10% vs 1%; P = .015), and were more often within their partial thromboplastin time goal (on 24% vs 41% of device-supported days; P ≤ .001).ConclusionsSystemic anticoagulation with bivalirudin in Impella 5.5 patients is associated with clinically meaningful improvements in postoperative thrombocytopenia and biocompatibility, with easier management, fewer insertion site bleeds, and more time within the target anticoagulation range.

Central Message

Anticoagulation with bivalirudin in Impella 5.5 patients is associated with reduced thrombocytopenia, fewer insertion site bleeds, and more time within a therapeutic partial thromboplastin time range compared to heparin.

Perspective

Systemic anticoagulation with bivalirudin in Impella 5.5 patients is associated with clinically meaningful improvements to postoperative thrombocytopenia and biocompatibility, with easier management, fewer insertion site bleeds, and more time within the target anticoagulation range. With no increased risk of thrombosis, these findings suggest a benefit of bivalirudin use regardless of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia positivity.

Cardiogenic shock is a life-threatening condition marked by inadequate tissue perfusion owing to severe cardiac dysfunction, most commonly from acute myocardial infarction, decompensated heart failure, or valvular disease.1 Despite advances in pharmacologic and interventional therapies, refractory shock often necessitates the use of temporary mechanical circulatory support (MCS) devices in selected patients.2

One such device, the Impella 5.5 (Abiomed), provides left ventricular unloading by augmenting forward flow, stabilizing patients for recovery or as a bridge to durable support.3 The Food and Drug Administration has approved device support for up to 14 days in the United States, and device support is approved for up to 30 days in Europe. Prolonged support is becoming increasingly common in practice.4,5

An important consideration with Impella support is the need for anticoagulation. ISHLT guidelines currently recommend a heparinized purge solution, with or without the addition of systemic intravenous unfractionated heparin (UFH) (IIC recommendation).5,6 However, the risks of bleeding complications and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) increase with the duration of UFH exposure, which has prompted many centers to adopt bivalirudin for anticoagulation, especially in patients with known or suspected HIT.7

Indeed, direct thrombin inhibitors (DTIs), such as bivalirudin and argatroban, are recommended in the context of HIT, but guidelines do not offer recommendations on its use as a primary anticoagulant for patients without contraindications for heparin or for patients expected to receive prolonged Impella support.5 Nonetheless, recent studies in extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) and durable left ventricular assist device populations have suggested that bivalirudin may offer advantages, including more a stable anticoagulation platform with fewer bleeding complications, along with avoiding the risk of HIT.8,9

In line with these data and anecdotal experience, our institution transitioned from routine UFH-based anticoagulation to bivalirudin in Impella 5.5 patients in December 2023. Here we report our institutional findings for Impella 5.5 patients anticoagulated with bivalirudin compared to heparin, investigating biocompatibility across the first 14 days of device support, the incidence of thrombotic and bleeding complications, and clinical management. At the time of writing, this constitutes the largest comparison of anticoagulation strategies in patients supported by the Impella 5.5 device.

Methods

Inclusion Criteria

This retrospective review included all adult patients who underwent Impella 5.5 placement at the University of Pennsylvania between August 2022 and December 2024. Impella 5.5 patient selection was performed by a multidisciplinary team consisting of a heart failure cardiologist, a cardiac surgeon, and a cardiac anesthesiologist. Indications for device placement included all forms of refractory cardiogenic shock, most commonly ischemic or nonischemic cardiomyopathy. At our institution, the Impella 5.5 is used as first-line MCS, to escalate other forms of MCS, and as a left ventricular vent.

Patient baseline characteristics, treatment details, adverse events, and survival were compared using an intention-to-treat analysis. In this assessment, groups were defined by anticoagulation (heparin or bivalirudin) established by postoperative day (POD) 2. To prevent confounding by anticoagulation changes that could impact laboratory values, a per-protocol grouping was then established to assess biocompatibility over time. In this subanalysis, patients were excluded if significant changes to protocol occurred, including anticoagulation interruptions >2 days or crossover from one anticoagulant to the other. For all analyses, patients were excluded if systemic anticoagulation was not initiated by POD 2 or if they were transferred to an outside hospital before Impella explant. The University of Pennsylvania’s Institutional Review Board deemed the study protocol exempt from review and exempt from consent (approval 854491; approved December 2023).

Device Placement and Management

At the University of Pennsylvania, Impella devices were implanted via a surgical cutdown of the right axillary artery, using an 8- to 10-mm Dacron graft anastomosed in an end-to-side fashion.3,10, 11, 12 In rare cases, direct aortic placement was performed. Preimplantation cross-sectional imaging was obtained to assess vascular access, and intraoperative fluoroscopic and echocardiographic guidance was used to position the device.10

Prior to December 2023, our institution used UFH for anticoagulation in all patients without contraindications. In this protocol, anticoagulation was delivered via a purge solution of 5% dextrose water with 25 U/mL of heparin. An additional systemic heparin drip was then initiated and titrated as needed to achieve a therapeutic partial thromboplastin time (PTT). Given device-dependent variations in purge flow rates, our institution found that this protocol resulted in inconsistent PTTs and management difficulty. Heparin use also led to many costly HIT workups that impaired patient care and listing for transplantation, with ultimately zero confirmatory results. Therefore, this strategy was abandoned in favor of anticoagulation with systemic bivalirudin and no additional anticoagulants in the purge solution. In this protocol, a systemic bivalirudin drip was uniformly initiated, and the purge solution consisted of 5% dextrose water with 25 U/mL of bicarbonate. Daily PTT measurements were obtained for all patients, targeting 40 to 50 seconds initially and then progressively increased to allow for higher anticoagulation levels (PTT 50-60 seconds) over subsequent days.

Hemodynamic monitoring included the brief use of pulmonary artery catheters, and speed levels were chosen based on desired hemodynamic support and review of echocardiograms throughout the period of device support. Impella circuits were regularly monitored for placement signal changes, alarms, motor current, and purge flow/pressure abnormalities.

Data Collection and Assessment

Patient information, treatment details, and clinical outcomes were obtained from a prospectively maintained database and subsequently validated through retrospective review of the electronic health record. Laboratory values were documented throughout the first 14 days of device support. The primary endpoint of this study was device biocompatibility, and secondary endpoints included ease of management and adverse hematologic events.

Biocompatibility was assessed with a linear mixed-effects model using fixed effects for baseline values, day, group, and their interaction, with random intercepts for each patient. This enabled an examination of static values and rates of change in laboratory assays across the first 14 days of device support. For patients supported for <14 days, “missing” values were treated as missing at random and handled without imputation. No data after device explant or transplantation were included in this analysis.

Secondary endpoints and all other group comparisons were analyzed using the χ2, Student t, or Kruskal-Wallis test as appropriate. One-year survival was estimated with Kaplan-Meier assessment, and missingness is shown in Table E1. For all analyses, statistical significance was defined as P ≤ .05 using Stata/BC 17.0 (StataCorp). Figures were created with support from Stata and Excel (Microsoft).

The primary outcome of this study, laboratory-based biocompatibility, was assessed using a per-protocol grouping that excluded 11 patients with significant changes to anticoagulation (8 heparin, 3 bivalirudin; P = .110) (Table 2). Information for each excluded patient and the rationale for anticoagulation changes are shown in Table E2 and Figure E1. Prior to Impella implantation, there were no differences in baseline laboratory test results (Table E3), and after implantation, thrombocytopenia was common in both groups (Figure 2). Importantly however, patients supported on bivalirudin showed a significantly faster rate of platelet recovery, seen on POD 9 to 14 (P < .05). In addition, although both groups initially had significantly elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels after Impella implant, patients managed on bivalirudin also saw a significantly faster rate of recovery after POD 1 (P < .05). Discussion

Our study investigated the effects of anticoagulation with heparin versus bivalirudin on biocompatibility, adverse hematologic events, and clinical management in patients supported by the Impella 5.5 at a large tertiary medical center. The 168 patients included 83 (49%) anticoagulated with heparin and 85 (51%) anticoagulated with bivalirudin. Demographics, cardiac status, heart failure etiology, and cardiac risk factors largely did not differ between the 2 groups (Table 1). Patients anticoagulated with bivalirudin experienced a faster recovery from postoperative thrombocytopenia and LDH elevation, fewer operative insertion site bleeds, and more frequent maintenance within their target PTT range, although there were no differences in rates of overall bleeding and thrombotic events (Tables 2 and 3, Figure 2). Thus, in this retrospective study, systemic anticoagulation with bivalirudin was associated with a clinically meaningful improvements to device biocompatibility, surgical bleeding complications, and clinical management without an increased risk of thrombotic events.

Heparin is the current guideline-directed standard of care for Impella 5.5 anticoagulation, producing its effects by inactivating thrombin and factor Xa through an antithrombin-dependent mechanism.5,6,13 Heparin offers the clinical benefits of a short half-life, minimal renal interaction, and quick reversibility with protamine. Particularly important for anticoagulation in MCS, it also carries an ionic charge that reduces the adhesion of proteins to artificial surfaces.5,13 However, clinical challenges of heparin-based Impella management include variable dose sensitivity and frequent bleeding complications, documented in up to 54% of patients.2,6,13, 14, 15, 16 In addition, heparin use carries the risk of HIT, with studies citing an incidence of up to 10% in the Impella population.17 In cases of HIT, a DTI such as bivalirudin is recommended, yet there are no guidelines for its use in the absence of HIT.6,13 Nonetheless, first-line anticoagulation with bivalirudin has become increasingly common in the MCS field, prompting the need for comparative analyses in the Impella 5.5 population.9,16,18,19

The benefits of bivalirudin include a more predictable anticoagulant effect, the ability to bind to both free and clot-bound thrombin, a short half-life, and a lack of an immune-mediated thrombocytopenia.16,20 Its use as a primary anticoagulant irrespective of HIT has been explored most robustly in the ECMO and durable ventricular assist device literature, where studies have shown a greater latency to thrombosis, reductions in major bleeding events, and fewer transfusion requirements in patients receiving bivalirudin.9,19 However, prior studies investigating the use of bivalirudin for the Impella series have failed to capture management ease and biocompatibility or to provide a statistically rigorous comparison, as they have been limited to case reports, small cohorts, and studies including now-outdated Impella models more prone to mechanical hemolysis and thrombocytopenia.5,7,8,21,22 Our study represents the largest comparison of anticoagulation with heparin versus bivalirudin for modern Impella support reported to date.

The primary endpoint of our study investigated laboratory trends over the first 14 days of Impella 5.5 support and demonstrated significant improvements to platelet and LDH recovery with bivalirudin use. Similar to prior literature that has documented rates of postoperative thrombocytopenia in up to 86% of Impella patients, owing largely to mechanical shear stress and consumptive coagulopathy, both groups demonstrated a platelet nadir between PODs 3 and 4, timing consistent with mechanical stress as the primary mechanism (Figure 2).18,20,23 However, by PODs 9 to 14, the bivalirudin-based patients experienced a significantly faster rate of platelet recovery. Although mechanistic causality cannot be inferred from this retrospective study, such findings are clinically meaningful, as prolonged thrombocytopenia increases a patient’s risk of hemorrhagic events, transfusion dependence, and the ability of providers to provide anticoagulation to a therapeutic range.18,23, 24, 25 Improved biocompatibility was also demonstrated, with faster rates of LDH reduction in patients receiving bivalirudin, supporting overall the suggestion that bivalirudin facilitates improved Impella biocompatibility via reduced hemolysis (Figure 2).

Despite these favorable signals, no differences in hemoglobin decline or indirect bilirubin elevation were observed between the 2 groups. As nonspecific markers, this likely reflects the multifactorial anemia seen in all critically ill patients, unrelated to anticoagulation strategy. Although not used routinely at our institution, the measurement of plasma free hemoglobin is recommended to more specifically assess device-related hemolysis and future studies may benefit from the use of this test.6 In addition, although patients with significant anticoagulation changes were excluded from laboratory assessments, these events represent clinical indicators of reduced biocompatibility and thus should be acknowledged (Figure E1, Table E2).

Consistent with the previously discussed laboratory findings, we observed improved clinical management in the bivalirudin group, as these patients were more often within their goal therapeutic PTT range. Such an improvement demonstrates advances in patient care and anticoagulation quality, importantly reducing patients’ thrombotic and bleeding risk. Similar improvements to therapeutic time have been seen in bivalirudin versus heparin comparisons in ECMO-dependent patients, and likely are due to a combination of bivalirudin’s purge-independent delivery and more uniform pharmacologic effect (Table 2).26,27

In addition, our findings demonstrate that bivalirudin patients experienced fewer insertion site bleeding events that required reoperation (Table 2). Most commonly axillary hematomas, prior studies have reported an incidence of up to 10 to 15% in Impella 5.5 patients.28,29 Whereas our heparin cohort demonstrated a stable frequency at 10%, our bivalirudin-based cohort demonstrated a significant improvement to only 1% (Table 3). Given the presence of multiple bleeding risk factors beyond anticoagulation present in the study population (impaired renal function, hypertension, diabetes, advanced age, etc) (Table 1), and the association of hematomas and perioperative bleeding with worse outcomes, longer intensive care unit stays, and infection, this reduction is a promising observation with clinical significance.6,28,30 Furthermore, in patients that went on to require cardiopulmonary bypass, our institution anecdotally noted a much lower incidence of intraoperative antithrombin III deficiency in bivalirudin patients compared to heparin patients. Overall, a longer time spent in the therapeutic window, a more stable and predictable anticoagulation platform irrespective of purge flow, and a reduced rate of surgical bleeding complications demonstrate that anticoagulation with systemic bivalirudin in Impella 5.5 patients leads to easier clinical management, reduced resource waste, and fewer adverse events.

Limitations of this study include missing documentation in the electronic health record and provider variations. In addition, owing to our institutional transition away from heparin in December 2023, there is a significant era effect that may confound our findings. In particular, both concomitant ECMO and dialysis were more common in the earlier heparin group, each of which may contribute to worsening thrombocytopenia (Table 3).15,24,31 Because the groups did not differ in cardiac status, lower rates of these factors in the later bivalirudin group may reflect improved provider confidence and skill managing Impella-supported patients. Ultimately, we are reassured against any critical impact by the overall low rates of ECMO and dialysis utilization. In addition, although PTT is clinically the most commonly used monitoring assay for bivalirudin, its accuracy may be reduced owing to a plateau effect at higher DTI levels or interference from underlying coagulopathy or autoimmune conditions. Although not routinely used at our institution, more specific assays, such as plasma dilute thrombin time, may benefit future studies.32

In summary, our study examining the differential effects of anticoagulation with heparin versus bivalirudin for the Impella 5.5 found a significant benefit from bivalirudin use, as bivalirudin-treated patients showed faster recovery from postoperative thrombocytopenia and LDH elevation, suggesting improved biocompatibility with the Impella 5.5 device. In addition, clinical management was improved in the bivalirudin cohort, with reductions to rates of surgical implant site bleeding and increased time in a therapeutic anticoagulation range. Overall, our findings support bivalirudin as a favorable alternative to heparin in all Impella 5.5 patients regardless of HIT, although future prospective and randomized studies are warranted to inform standardized anticoagulation protocols.
