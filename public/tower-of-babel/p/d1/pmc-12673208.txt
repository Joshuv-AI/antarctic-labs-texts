Clinical and genetic features of Gitelman syndrome patients with hyperuricemia
Authors: Lei Zhang, Ning Lv, Hongqiang Zhao, Bingbin Zhao, Xiaoyan Peng, Lubin Xu, Minxian Wang, Limeng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGitelman syndrome (GS) is a rare inherited salt-losing tubulopathy caused by dysfunction of the thiazide-sensitive sodium-chloride cotransporter (NCC). Hyperuricemia is frequently observed in patients with GS, yet its risk factors and associations with GS remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the percentage, clinical characteristics, and genetic mechanisms in GS patients with hyperuricemia.MethodsWe reanalyzed the GS cohort at Peking Union Medical College Hospital, investigated the baseline clinical, laboratory, and genetic data of GS patients, and utilized the Illumina Human Asian Screening Array-24+v1.0 gene chip and IMPUTE2 for single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis.ResultsThe average serum uric acid of 132 GS patients was 352.3 ± 96.9 μmol/l (5.9 ± 1.6 mg/dl), with 21.2% hyperuricemia [mean 486.1 μmol/l (8.2 mg/dl)] and two cases of gout. The GS patients with hyperuricemia had higher body mass index (BMI) (24.3 ± 3.9 vs 21.7 ± 3.5 kg/m2, P = .001), lower fractional excretion of uric acid (FEUA) (median 4.22% vs 6.17%, P < .001), and urinary calcium/creatinine ratio (0.05 vs 0.12 mmol/mmol, P = .004). In GS patients with or without hyperuricemia, the impairment of NCC function indicated by the value of increase of chloride excretion fraction (△FECl) in the hydrochlorothiazide test was median 0.49% vs 0.82% (P = .10). We focused on the SLC12A3 genotype and 33 SNPs linked to uric acid levels and found no significant genetic differences between patients with or without hyperuricemia.ConclusionsHyperuricemia is common in patients with GS in China, associated with elevated BMI and potentially more severe NCC dysfunction.Keywords: body mass index, Gitelman syndrome, hyperuricemia, sodium-chloride cotransporter

INTRODUCTION

Hyperuricemia has grown in importance as a global public health concern. Of Chinese adults, 14.0% were estimated to have hyperuricemia in 2018–2019 [1], and the global prevalence rate has been reported to range from 2.6% to 36% with significant demographic diversity and regional differences [2, 3]. Hyperuricemia is associated with gout, chronic kidney disease (CKD), stroke, cardiovascular diseases (CVD), diabetes, and increased mortality [4, 5]. A national health and nutrition examination survey in the USA revealed similar mortality risks for hyperuricemia and diabetes [6]. Serum uric acid could also be influenced by the intake of purine-rich foods such as fructose-sweetened beverages and excessive alcohol, and physical inactivity, which can concurrently contribute to the risk of CKD, CVD, and diabetes.

Thiazide diuretics, which have been widely prescribed as first-line medications to treat hypertension for >60 years [7], are known to increase serum uric acid. Gitelman syndrome (GS) (OMIM 263800), a rare inherited salt-losing tubulopathy caused by dysfunction of the sodium-chloride cotransporter (NCC) and the pharmacological target of thiazide diuretics, is expected to present with hyperuricemia. However, previous reports on GS complicated with hyperuricemia were mainly case reports of gout cases, with serum uric acid levels fluctuating between 580 and 1000μmol/l [8–11]. There is no literature outlining a thorough correlation between GS and hyperuricemia, and the prevalence, risk factors, and pathophysiology of hyperuricemia in GS remain unclear. In addition, although multiple single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) linked to uric acid levels were identified with ethnic differences [12, 13], the genetic background influencing uric acid levels in GS still requires clarification.

Herein, we aimed to identify the percentage and detailed clinical characteristics of GS patients with hyperuricemia, as well as their genetic features. We also analyzed NCC dysfunction, genotype, and uric acid-linked SNPs in these patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

We have reanalyzed the GS cohort at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) since 2005, as a cross-sectional study analyzing baseline data of patients at diagnosis of GS until 2022 (Fig. 1). The diagnostic evidence of GS included both characteristic clinical features and DNA sequence analysis with SLC12A3 mutations. The typical clinical manifestations included chronic hypokalemia concomitant with inappropriate renal potassium wasting and metabolic alkalosis. Second-generation sequencing (n = 32) and direct sequencing were employed for gene testing. Exclusion criteria included patients with renal potassium wasting caused by other etiologies, including secondary tubulointerstitial injury induced by drugs, connective tissue disease, and monoclonal gammopathy, use of thiazide or furosemide, Cushing’s syndrome, primary aldosteronism, renal artery stenosis, renin-secreting tumors, and malignant hypertension. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee on Human Studies at PUMCH (approval number JS-1233–2). All patients gave informed consent.

Flowchart of study population selection. The asterisk * denotes hyperuricemic patients; the hash # denotes non-hyperuricemic patients.

Clinical data

The individual clinical manifestations were documented by an experienced nephrologist using a standardized symptom questionnaire adapted from a previously validated face-to-face interview tool [14]. We systematically categorized patients’ clinical symptoms into constitutional, neuromuscular, cardiovascular, urinary, osteoarticular, and digestive manifestations. We evaluated the serum and urinary biochemical parameters, and calculated the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation. Uric acid was analyzed using a uricase-peroxidase coupled assay, and hyperuricemia was defined as a fasting serum uric acid level exceeding 420 μmol/l (7 mg/dl) without using urate-lowering drugs in this study [1], while hypomagnesemia was any recorded serum total magnesium level below 0.7 mmol/l. The lowest serum potassium and magnesium levels referred to the lowest recorded values prior to study enrollment. The fractional excretion of uric acid (FEUA) was determined from serum and urinary concentrations of urate and creatinine using the formula FEUA = (urine uric acid/creatinine) × (serum creatinine/uric acid). A urinary calcium/creatinine ratio of <0.2 mmol/mmol or 0.12 mmol/mmol in adults and children >5 years of age was considered hypocalciuria [15, 16]. The hydrochlorothiazide (HCT) test was administered following the reference [17]. Briefly, after an overnight fast, the subjects were asked to drink 10 ml/kg body weight of water within 15 minutes immediately before the test. Two continuous 30-minutes urine samples were collected to calculate the “basal” clearance. Then, 50 mg of HCT was administered orally, and six additional 30-minute clearance tests were performed. Blood samples were obtained at 60 and 240 minutes. The fractional excretion of chloride (FECl) was evaluated. Blunt response to HCT was defined as the net increase of chloride fractional excretion (△FECl) < 2.86% according to our previous study [18, 19]. The HCT test exhibits excellent diagnostic performance for GS [20], and may facilitate evaluating the degree of NCC dysfunction.

SNPs analysis

We tested 87 patients using an Illumina Human Asian Screening Array-24+v1.0 (ASA) gene chip containing 741 988 SNPs according to the Illumina Infinium HTS chip standard operating procedures. Then, after quality control procedures for genotyping data, 80 patients’ samples met the required quality thresholds for inclusion in the final SNP analysis. All genetic data sets used in this study were based on the human genome Hg19 assembly. Genotype imputation was performed for the SNPs not detected by the ASA chip with the 1000 Genomes Project phase 3 East Asian population (1000G3 EAS) (https://mathgen.stats.ox.ac.uk/impute/1000GP_Phase3.html) by IMPUTE2. The quality control was conducted per the following steps: (i) sample call rate >90%, (ii) sex check, (iii) excluded samples with an inbreeding coefficient ± 6SD from mean of ancestry distribution, (iv) excluded samples with heterozygosity from the median of ancestry distribution, (v) excluded duplicate variants, (vi) SNP call rate ≥95%, and (vii) HardyWeinberg equilibrium P value >10−6.

Statistical analysis

Data with a normal distribution were expressed as means ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (25th, 75th percentiles). When comparing clinical, laboratory, and genotype differences between patients with or without hyperuricemia, the significance of normal distribution parameters with homogeneity of variance was analyzed using a two-sample t-test, and that of non-normal distribution parameters was analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Pearson’s Chi-square test, the continuity-corrected Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test was used as appropriate for categorical variables. Differences in SNPs associated with uric acid levels were evaluated by a paired samples t-test. The association between serum uric acid levels and clinical parameters was evaluated by Pearson or Spearman’s correlation analysis. A binary logistic regression model was used to analyze associations between hyperuricemia and relevant covariates; we also performed imputation for variables with missing data exceeding 10%. Statistical significance was a two-sided P value <0.05 calculated with SPSS software version 25.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA).

RESULTS

Overall characteristics of GS patients with hyperuricemia

This study included 132 GS patients with baseline uric acid data (Fig. 1). Eight patients without baseline serum uric acid had consistent clinical characteristics with the 132 patients (Supplementary Table S1). The average serum uric acid of the 132 patients at diagnosis of GS was 352.3 ± 96.9 μmol/l (5.9 ± 1.6 mg/dl), with 28 cases (21.2%) of hyperuricemia. In addition, the average uric acid level in hyperuricemia patients at baseline without administrating urate-lowering drugs was 486.1 ± 58.8 μmol/l (8.2 ± 1.0 mg/dl), while in non-hyperuricemia patients, it was 316.3 ± 69.7 μmol/l (5.3 ± 1.2 mg/dl). These patients had a median age of 31.0 (23.0, 41.7) years of age, 54.5% males, the lowest serum potassium level of 2.3 ± 0.5 mmol/l, 78.6% hypomagnesemia, 73.5% hypocalciuria, and 65.2% suffering symptoms from ≥3 systems.

The median age at diagnosis of GS, gender, suffering symptoms, lowest serum potassium, magnesium, and alkalosis were comparable between patients with hyperuricemia or without, while hyperuricemic patients had a lower urine calcium/creatinine ratio (0.05 vs 0.12 mmol/mmol, P = .004). eGFR was comparable between patients with hyperuricemia or without (median 119.5 vs 118.5 ml/min/1.73 m2, P = .90). Kidney stones were present in two cases of patients without hyperuricemia. There was no significant difference in the complications of hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia between groups, while body mass index (BMI) was higher in the hyperuricemic group (24.3 ± 3.9 vs 21.7 ± 3.5 kg/m2, P = .001).

Exploring the mechanism for hyperuricemia in GS

Clinical features related to GS and hyperuricemia, and renal tubular function between patients with hyperuricemia or not were explored (Table 1). Patients who tested FEUA (n = 82) had generally consistent clinical characteristics with patients who did not test it (Supplementary Table S2), except for the gender composition‌. FEUA was lower in patients with hyperuricemia (median 4.22% vs 6.17%, P < .001). We further conducted the correlation analysis and observed consistent results with Table 1 that the serum uric acid levels were associated with BMI (r = 0.39, P < .001), urine Ca/Cr (r = −0.27, P = .003), and FEUA (r = −0.64, P < .001). In regression analyses, BMI (OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.40, P = .004) and urinary calcium/creatinine ratio (OR 0.004, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.53, P = .03) were significantly associated with an increased risk of hyperuricemia adjusted by age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, total cholesterol, bicarbonate, eGFR, and lowest serum potassium and magnesium. The regression analysis after urinary calcium/creatinine ratio (11% missing) imputation demonstrated consistent results. In addition, a higher level of baseline arterial lactate in the hyperuricemic patients (n = 9) was detected (2.36 ± 0.63 vs 1.58 ± 0.56 mmol/l, P = .001) than in patients without hyperuricemia (n = 45) (reference range 0.5–1.6 mmol/l).

SLC12A3 genotype

We detected 98 distinct mutations throughout SLC12A3, including 70 missense mutations, seven nonsense mutations, nine splice site mutations, five small deletions, one small indel, four small insertions, two gross deletions, and heterozygosity in 28 patients (21.2%). In mutant allele frequency analysis of the null variants, including nonsense, frameshift, canonical ±1 or 2 splice sites, initiation codon, and single exon or multi-exon deletion, there was no significant difference in patients with or without hyperuricemia (10.9% vs 17.9%, P = .21). We further analyzed the high-frequency mutations and found Asp486Asn (14.5%) and Thr60Met (10.9%) to be >10% in the hyperuricemic patients, with a similar frequency of 13.8% (P = .89) and 9.7% (P = .80) in non-hyperuricemic patients.

SNPs linked to uric acid

The 80 patients included in the SNP analysis exhibited generally consistent baseline clinical characteristics with the other 52 patients, except for eGFR (Supplementary Table S3). When focused on 33 SNPs reported to be associated either positively or negatively with serum uric acid levels, derived from two genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of East Asian populations [12, 21], including 10 SNPs detected by the ASA chip and 23 imputed sites, we did not observe the SNPs located on the gene SLC12A3. No significant SNPs frequency differences (P = .38) (Fig. 2) were in the SNP’s background analysis, including patients with (n = 14) or without hyperuricemia (n = 66).

DISCUSSION

This study is the first to report the clinical and genetic characteristics and risk factors of hyperuricemia in a relatively large sample from a GS patient cohort. Hyperuricemia is more common in GS patients than in the general population in China, associated with elevated BMI and potentially more severe NCC dysfunction. No relationship was detected between hyperuricemia and uric acid-linked SNPs in this study.

This study presented detailed uric acid data in 132 GS patients, revealing that reduced FEUA and urine calcium/creatinine ratio, and elevated BMI were associated with hyperuricemia. Besides, hyperuricemic GS patients exhibited a relatively stronger thiazide reaction than non-hyperuricemic patients. Thiazide-related adverse side effects primarily include hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, alkalosis, and hyperuricemia [22–24] through inhibition of uric acid efflux and increasing uric acid reabsorption, and secondary volume depletion [25]. As the target of thiazide diuretics, decreased NCC activity also underlies GS but lacks a detailed association with hyperuricemia except for a few gout cases [8–11]. Recently, inconsistent data on uric acid in GS were reported, including a generally normal range of serum uric acid in a European cohort and proximal tubular dysfunction of hypouricemia secondary to severe hypokalemia [26, 27]. Our findings suggest that the FEUA is associated with the level of NCC dysfunction for three possible reasons. First, reduced FEUA may be the parallel relationship between sodium and urate reabsorption in the proximal tubule, secondary to volume insufficiency and sodium loss from NCC dysfunction, which is mediated partly by an anion exchange process, such as urate-hydroxyl and urate-lactate exchange proteins [28–30]. Second, hyperlactatemia following metabolic alkalosis has been shown to decrease the renal excretion of uric acid, presumably by the depression of tubular secretion of uric acid [31, 32]. Third, enhanced urate reabsorption transporter 1 (URAT1) activity in GS, secondary to RAS activation-induced increased expression of sodium-hydrogen exchanger (NHE3), may lead to decreased luminal pH and consequently hyperuricemia [28]. In contrast, SGLT2 inhibitors are uricosuric through URAT1 inhibition and potentially their glucosuric and insulin-lowering effect [33]. However, SGLT2 inhibitors are not suitable for GS patients due to the risk of worsening renal salt wasting. Therefore, avoiding volume depletion with additional salt supplementation may facilitate managing hyperuricemia in GS.

Obesity is closely related to hyperuricemia, with several studies identifying a positive association between BMI and the risk of hyperuricemia [34]. Potential mechanisms include reduced renal uric acid clearance and impaired glucose and insulin metabolism, which compromise the renal handling of uric acid and stimulate the synthesis of uric acid [35]. Weight loss of ⩾10 kg could result in a roughly four times higher chance of obtaining a serum urate level ≤360 μmol/l in a given individual [36]. Thus, weight control is another important therapeutic target for GS patients with hyperuricemia.

In GS patients with hyperuricemia, the reduced urinary calcium might be secondary to the severity of NCC impairment. Calcium is carefully regulated by the kidney, with 98%–99% of the filtered load of calcium reabsorbed by the proximal tubule and the thick ascending limb paracellularly and transcellularly, and the distal tubules exclusively via the transcellular route [37]. Possible reasons for hypocalciuria in GS patients included enhanced Ca2+ reabsorption in the proximal tubule secondary to a volume contraction [38, 39], hypoparathyroidism partly caused by low serum magnesium [26], enhanced transcellular transportation in distal convoluted tubules due to hyperpolarization of the apical membrane of distal tubular cells [40], and activation and increased expression of several calcium channels of the distal nephron [41, 42], regulated by a variety of hormones such as aldosterone [43] and fibroblast growth factor 23 [44, 45] related to NCC inhibition, increased secreted uromodulin (UMOD)[46, 47], and impaired phosphorylated form of claudin-16 [42].

We did not observe a significant difference between patients with or without hyperuricemia in 33 SNPs associated with uric acid levels in two large-scale GWAS of Asian populations. The rs4148155 (ABCG2), a recently reported SNP in hypertensive patients who developed hyperuricemia after using thiazides from the UK Biobank [48], also showed negative results in our study.

Several well-established healthy dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean and DASH diets, have been shown to lower serum urate levels [49]. However, a cross-sectional analysis of NHANES data found that the direct effect of diet on hyperuricemia is weak. Still, obesity plays a critical mediating role in the relationship between diet and hyperuricemia [50]. Besides, volume insufficiency is one of the mechanisms by which NCC dysfunction leads to hyperuricemia. Therefore, further assessment of GS patients’ energy intake, dietary composition, and fluid intake may help explore confounding factors in the relationship between BMI, NCC dysfunction, and uric acid levels. Spironolactone, triamterene, or amiloride was used in our cohort. Prior studies of spironolactone and triamterene have shown conflicting results on serum uric acid levels, and amiloride seems not to alter the uric acid levels [51]. We compared the potassium-sparing diuretics usage between patients with or without hyperuricemia, and did not observe a significant effect of them on serum uric acid. Interestingly, nephrolithiasis was observed in two non-hyperuricemic GS patients. Given the generally non-elevated FEUA and hypocalciuria in GS, the increased urinary excretion of other ions, including magnesium and phosphate [15, 26], might contribute to the formation of kidney stones in GS patients, which needs further clarification.

The testing rates of FEUA, HCT, and uric acid-linked SNPs were relatively low in this study. As our understanding of GS deepened, we have refined and expanded the testing indicators progressively. Besides, each patient’s condition and agreement determined that only a subset of patients would undergo the HCT test. Moreover, the growing availability of genetic testing in recent years has reduced the diagnostic significance of the HCT test.

Our study has some limitations. First, this is a single-center study, and although GS is a rare disease, the overall sample size is still limited, especially for the cases with the HCT test data. Also, >700 mutations in the SLC12A3 gene encoding NCC have been reported, located in various domains and affecting protein function through different mechanisms [7], including impairments in protein folding, trafficking, or membrane expression, which may lead to varying electrolyte imbalance and uric acid metabolism disorder. However, the number of GS patients included in the genotype and SNP analysis is relatively small, and the SNP analysis was based on East Asian populations, limiting generalizability. Inclusion of more GS patients is necessary to validate the associations between NCC dysfunction and hyperuricemia, and to elucidate genotype-phenotype correlations in GS. Second, the cross-sectional design allowed identification of potential risk factors for hyperuricemia, but cannot establish causal relationships.

In conclusion, hyperuricemia is common in patients with GS in China, associated with elevated BMI and potentially more severe NCC dysfunction, but without a relationship to uric acid-linked SNPs in this study. It sheds light on the crosstalk between urate handling and NCC dysfunction, which deepens the understanding of GS complications.
