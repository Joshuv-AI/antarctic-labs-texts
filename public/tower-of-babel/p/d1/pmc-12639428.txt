Refining Active Surveillance Eligibility in Intermediate-risk Non–muscle-invasive Bladder Cancer: The Impact of Time to First Recurrence
Authors: Marco Finati, Nicola Schiavone, Biagio Barone, Roberto Contieri, Antonio Fanelli, Francesco Cinelli, Anna Ricapito, Nicola d’Altilia, Ugo Giovanni Falagario, Richard Naspro, Savio Domenico Pandolfo, Felice Crocetto, Ciro Imbimbo, Carlo Bettocchi, Luigi Cormio, Giuseppe Carrieri, Benjamin Pradere, Gian Maria Busetto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The International Bladder Cancer Group (IBCG) scoring system has been proposed for identifying patients suitable for active surveillance (AS) within the intermediate-risk (IR) non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) population. Given that early recurrence has been associated with higher progression rates, we aimed to assess whether better integration of recurrence-free survival (RFS) into the IBCG risk stratification may further improve AS eligibility criteria. We retrospectively identified 241 recurrent IR-NMIBC patients (during the period 2013–2022) who were eligible for AS at the time of the first transurethral resection of a bladder tumor. Kaplan-Meier and multivariable Cox regression analyses evaluated the impact of IBCG risk factors and recurrence timing in predicting high-grade (HG) progression-free survival (PFS), while discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index). Patients who recurred within 6 mo had significantly worse PFS than those with recurrence at >12 mo from diagnosis (3-yr PFS: 42% vs 74%, p = 0.007), while no difference in progression rates were observed for recurrence between 6 and 12 mo or at >12 mo. At a multivariable analysis, RFS was independently associated with HG progression and outperformed the IBCG risk group in predictive accuracy (C-index 0.78 vs 0.70). Integration of both factors in the model resulted in the highest performance (C-index 0.79). The time to first recurrence adds prognostic information beyond the IBCG criteria when selecting IR-NMIBC patients eligible for AS. Patients recurring within 6 mo should not be considered for surveillance, while those with later recurrences may remain eligible if no more than one IBCG risk factor is present. Prospective validation of the AS criteria in the IR cohort at each recurrence will further help refine patients’ selection and follow-up.Patient summaryIn this study, we looked at patients with bladder cancer who might be monitored (active surveillance) without immediate treatment. We found that if the cancer comes back within 6 mo, there is a higher chance that it will become more aggressive.

Active surveillance (AS) involves close monitoring without immediate transurethral resection of a bladder tumor (TURBT) or adjuvant treatment, following the initial diagnosis of low-grade tumor disease [1,2]. While AS has increasingly been considered for selected patients, its safety in intermediate-risk (IR) non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) patients remains uncertain, largely due to the heterogeneity of recurrence and progression rates in this group [3,4]. To address this, the International Bladder Cancer Group (IBCG) has clinically validated a scoring system for predicting recurrence and progression in IR-NMIBC [5,6], and this tool has also been proposed for identifying patients suitable for AS [2,[7], [8], [9]].

Although recurrence within the 1st year is recognized as a risk factor within the IBCG criteria, we hypothesized that a more precise assessment of recurrence timing could improve patient selection for AS. Given that early recurrence has been associated with higher progression rates [[10], [11], [12]], we aimed to assess whether better integration of recurrence-free survival (RFS) into the IBCG risk stratification may further improve AS eligibility criteria.

We relied on a Institute Review Board-approved, prospectively maintained database collecting data on patients treated with TURBT at two academic centers. We focused on patients with a primary diagnosis of low-grade (World Health Organization 1973: grade [G]1–2) Ta, from 2013 to 2022, who were eligible for AS at the time of first TURBT [13], according to the Bladder Italian Active Surveillance (BIAS) protocol (absence of hematuria, fewer than five tumors, maximum diameter <1 cm, and negative urinary cytology) [14]. According to the European Association of Urology NMIBC guideline recommendations, patients underwent follow-up with cystoscopic and urinary cytology at 3 and 9 mo after the first TURBT, and then yearly for the first 5 yr [3]. Data on subsequent intravesical treatment were also recorded. We evaluated RFS, defined as any NMIBC occurred at TURBT during follow-up, high-grade (HG; G3) progression-free survival (PFS) and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC). Recurrence was considered confirmed only upon TURBT, and RFS was set at the time of histopathological verification. Patients who did not progress were censored at the date of the last cystoscopy, or—if they experienced a nonprogressive recurrence—at the date of the last TURBT.

Of the 575 patients with Ta low-grade bladder cancer initially eligible for AS, 241 (42%) experienced at least one recurrence and were included in the study. In this context, AS is considered to begin at the time of recurrence, when the clinical decision is made regarding whether to observe or resect the recurrent tumor. For statistical analyses, the IBCG score was applied at the time of recurrence, considering frequent recurrence (more than one recurrence per year), early recurrence (<12 mo), multifocal disease, and failure of previous intravesical treatment. Patients were stratified further according to the number of IBCG risk factors: 0, 1–2, and >2, based on previously published methodology [8,9]. We estimated the risk of progression (HG progression [HGP]) of disease based on the IBCG risk categories and RFS, using Kaplan-Meier curves. The observation period was defined from the first recurrence to either progression at TURBT or last cystoscopy for patients without progression. A multivariable Cox regression analysis tested the impact of the IBCG risk group (0, 1–2, and > 2) and RFS in predicting HGP, adjusted for age, sex, and tumor grade (G1 vs G2) at diagnosis. Restricted cubic spline was used to visually present the predicted hazard ratio (HR) for HGP at the increase of RFS and the number of positive IBCG factors. To compare the predictive value of RFS and IBCG risk groups, we developed two separate models—one including RFS and the other including the IBCG risk group, and discrimination of each model was evaluated using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index). C-index also calculated overall discrimination when incorporating both RFS and the IBCG risk group into a combined model. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (Posit PBC, Boston, MA, USA).

Overall clinicopathological characteristics are depicted in Supplementary Table 1. The median (interquartile range) follow-up for patients free from progression was 43 (27–84) mo. The majority of patients showed one to two IBCG risk factors (66%) and recurred after 12 mo from diagnosis (57%). There were overall 73 cases of progression, of which nine progressed to MIBC. The median (interquartile range) time to progression was 38 (20–76) mo for patients with zero IBCG risk factors, 24 (10–50) mo for those with one to two risk factors, and 14 (9–58) mo for those with more than two risk factors. At Kaplan-Meier curves, patients who recurred within the first 6 mo (3-yr PFS: 42%, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 24–71%) exhibited worse PFS than those who recurred >12 mo after diagnosis (3-yr PFS: 74%, 95% CI: 65–85%, p = 0.007; Fig. 1A), while those who recurred between 6 and 12 mo (3-yr PFS: 70%, 95% CI: 58–83%) showed similar progression rates to those recurring at >12 mo (p = 0.9; Fig. 1A). Both patients with one to two (3-yr PFS: 63%, 95% CI: 55–73%, p = 0.009) or more than two (3-yr PFS: 64%, 95% CI: 50–83%, p = 0.032) IBCG risk factors had higher progression rates than those with zero (3-yr PFS: 86%, 95% CI: 76–96%) IBCG risk factors (Fig. 1B). At multivariable Cox regression analyses (Table 1), patients with RFS of <6 mo showed HRs for progression, when compared with both those who recurred between 6 and 12 mo and those with recurrence at later than 12 mo. Conversely, IBCG risk factors showed a robust increase in progression until two risk factors, while subsequent addition of further risk factors did not result in an increase of HGP (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Specifically, time and frequency of recurrence were the two most important predictors of progression among the IBCG criteria (Table 1). The Cox model integrating RFS showed higher discrimination (C-index: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.74–0.83) than the model using the IBCG criteria (C-index: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.65–0.78). We demonstrated that the time to first recurrence had the highest discrimination in predicting HGP, and its integration into the IBCG scoring system and existing guideline algorithms might better refine AS eligibility criteria. For instance, very early recurrence (<6 mo) identified patients at a higher risk of progression, while those who experienced recurrence between 6 and 12 mo had a progression risk comparable with those who remained recurrence free for at least 12 mo. For these reasons, we believe that the IBCG early recurrence criteria could be refined to improve stratification of patients at a very high risk of progression, ideally those with RFS of <6 mo. Future studies should definitively address whether patients with very early recurrence should be excluded from AS, regardless of the IBCG risk score, while those with mid-to-late recurrence might still be preferred to undergo AS following the IBCG criteria.

Although our study expands upon the previous literature, it is not without limitations. First, because our cohort was exposed to regular TURBT at any recurrence, we cannot draw definite conclusions regarding long-term safety and efficacy of AS. Second, only a small proportion of our patients underwent an early single intravesical instillation of mitomycin after TURBT, and even a smaller percentage received an induction course after recurrence. Although this reflects the low-risk nature of the population at initial diagnosis (all patients initially included were eligible for AS, which inherently selected individuals without significant risk features warranting an induction course) for intravesical therapy. However, the decision to withhold further induction courses resulted in slightly higher progression rates than those previously reported in the literature [9] and may have affected the discriminatory accuracy of the IBCG criteria, as prior failure of intravesical treatments is a significant risk factor within the IBCG scoring system. Third, the lack of pathological data from subsequent TURBT made it impossible to verify which patients remained eligible for AS at the time of recurrence, in terms of both the number and the maximum size of recurrent tumors. Prospective validation of the BIAS criteria in the IR cohort at each recurrence will further help refine patients’ selection and follow-up—not only to maintain the oncological safety of AS, but also to support its implementation, preserve quality of life, and avoid overtreatment in patients with multiple comorbidities.
