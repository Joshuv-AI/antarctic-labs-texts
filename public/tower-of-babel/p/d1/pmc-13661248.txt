MR-based skull parametric modeling for blood-brain barrier opening in non-human primates
Authors: Sergio Jiménez-Gambín, Fotios Tsitsos, Sua Bae, Robin Ji, Elisa E Konofagou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFocused ultrasound (FUS) transcranial procedures require of simulation planning using the computed tomography (CT) of the skull, leading to ionizing radiation. Zero echo time (ZTE) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a safe alternative for blood-brain barrier opening (BBBO) planning in non-human primates (NHP), with good contrast for bone imaging. In this study, we applied a classical coordinate-to-coordinate linear-regression method to obtain the ZTE-to-CT relationship for six NHP skulls. First, we compared ZTE-based (pseudo-CT/pCT) and CT-based simulations ex-vivo, with differences of , , 0.49± 0.00 mm and 0.00± 0.00 mm for the focal volume, attenuation, axial and lateral shifts, respectively. The differences between pCT simulations and experiments were , 1.74± 0.57 mm and 0.30± 0.11 mm for the focal attenuation, axial and lateral shifts, respectively. Finally, pCT-based and CT-based simulations for in-vivo planning resulted in differences of , , , 0.71± 0.16 mm and 0.04± 0.02 for the focal volume, attenuation, in-focus BBBO volume, sonicated skull thickness, and Skull Density Ratio, respectively, where pCT and CT predicted a and of BBBO volume, respectively. Introduction

Microbubble-enhanced focused ultrasound (FUS) transcranial procedures for the non-invasive, localized, transient and safe disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) have been conventionally planned by simulations using a computed tomography (CT) scan of the subject’s skull: not only for transcranial FUS estimation but for aberration compensation1,2. However, CT also requires high patient exposure to ionizing radiation, thus raising regulatory obstacles for research studies. In addition, the ionizing nature of these scans implies a limitation for the skull imaging over time, as the acoustic properties and geometry of the skull might be susceptible of change due to aging, and an up-to-date skull map would be needed for an accurate treatment planning. Moreover, the skull map obtained from CT has to be aligned with respect to the reference MRI used for treatment planning3, which implies the skull map has to be transformed in 3D, and this registration can lead to alignment errors for the simulation prediction step.

Alternatively, MRI has been reported to be feasible for skull acquisition. Given the non-ionizing nature of MRI, it is a safe and patient-friendly imaging method for the skull acquisition. Moreover, MR images are inherently aligned and oriented to the reference planning MRI as they are acquired during the same session, and therefore, the skull orientation in the simulation is the most accurate as no 3D transformation is required. Consequently, the single-session pre-treatment imaging stage would provide a more patient-friendly, cost- and logistically-efficient procedure.

The generation of the synthetic or pseudo-CT (pCT) in Hounsfield Units (HU) from the MRI scan intensity distribution can be achieved by a variety of methods. Atlas-based technique with machine-learning (ML) pattern recognition makes use of a separate CT scan, that is accordingly deformed and registered to a patient’s MRI, in order to have a pair of corresponding CT and MRI maps4–8. However, the CT scan does not correspond to the same patient, which leads to anatomical registration errors. Classification-based methods consist of thresholding the different intensity regions of air, soft-tissues and bone within the MRI9,10. As air and bone tissues have a similar T2 relaxation time and it is difficult to separate them, some classification-based methods incorporate the use of histogram thresholding, neighbor-voxel evaluation, or morphological operations to effectively extract only bone9–21. And for a heterogeneous bone acquisition from the MRI, some classification-based methods also apply a linear regression method to convert from MR intensity to HU. Finally, deep learning and neural network approaches have been shown to provide the best performance for pCT generation18,22–29. These advanced deep-learning approaches require at least a few tens18 () of co-registered pairs of CT and MRI samples for the network training to be accurate and applicable to new sets of images. On the contrary, classification-based linear-regression methods can be a faster, simpler and only solution for small datasets, and transcranial FUS applications with low30 () incidence angles onto the skull, where a rough estimate of the skull acoustic properties might be sufficient for the accurate prediction of the transcranial focal parameters.

To the best of our knowledge, all reported methods have been tested in human skull datasets, in simulations, but not validated against a ground-truth transcranial application such as BBB opening. In this study, we evaluated the feasibility of a fast and simple classification-based method with morphological operations and linear-regression, using the zero ZTE-MR sequence, in a dataset of 6 NHP skulls (2 ex-vivo, 4 in-vivo), for simulation planning in BBB opening procedures using a single-element FUS transducer. On the one hand, we chose the ZTE sequence as it has been shown to provide good contrast for skull bone imaging among other MRI-based sequences, as cortical bone has low proton density and very short T2 relaxation time, making MRI challenging using conventional sequences9. On the other hand, we selected the classification-based method, and not a deep learning one for two reasons: 1) simple and fast conversion technique to be tested for the first time in NHP skull samples, and 2) limited number (n=6) of NHP samples. We first performed pCT- and CT-based simulations compared to pressure-field measurements of the FUS propagation through an ex-vivo NHP skull, and finally, we compared pCT and CT-based simulations with the BBB opening volume and location after different FUS-BBBO sonications on two different NHPs. This proof-of-concept study showed the excellent agreement, given the reduced sample size limiting a more complete consideration of head anatomy, NHP age, or skull thickness variation, of using a ZTE-MRI sequence to model the 3D skull bone for BBB opening simulation planning as compared to a CT-based skull model.

Discussion

The results obtained from this direct and simple method of conversion from ZTE-MRI intensity to CT intensitiy (pCT) for skull imaging are very positive and reliable, demonstrating its feasibility for a completely non-ionizing technique both accurate simulation planning and BBBO prediction in NHP model. While firstly, ex-vivo calibrations of pressure-field measurements allowed the validation of the pCT model considering the set of 6 skull samples, secondly, several in-vivo BBBO procedures showed the simulation planning to be accurate for BBBO volume prediction and transcranial attenuation. The most relevant advantage of this method, apart from its simplicity, is the reduced number of samples needed to have the ZTE-to-CT conversion coefficients, unlike the more complex methods based on neuronal networks and deep learning, making it more accessible to the research community. Another advantage is the ZTE image being inherently aligned with the baseline MRI used for procedure planning in the neuronavigation system. Both skull ZTE and baseline MRI are acquired in the same scanner at the same session, therefore they have the same orientation and alignment, eliminating then potential sources of error which may occur when aligning the CT image to the reference baseline MRI. Moreover, this is a fully non-ionizing imaging technique, which means that several pCT scans could be acquired over time, if needed, in order to have the most updated skull geometry, porosity and properties, which would allow the most accurate simulation planning and prediction for each NHP or patient (in the clinic), regardless of the time-point the treatment is performed with respect to the screening date, unlike CT, which is currently acquired only once at the screening visit.

The most relevant limitation of this technique for the in-vivo scenario is the partial loss of information at thinner skull layers, in particular at the skull base in this study. This skull base, which is considerably thinner than the upper bone regions such as the frontal, parietal, occipital or temporal skull bones, is not accurately captured by the ZTE-MRI sequence, and consequently, the FUS beam reflection at the skull base is weaker for the pCT model. Note that the large focus of the employed transducer due to the low center frequency of 250 kHz is also a limiting factor for this pCT to CT comparison study, whereas using a higher frequency with a smaller focal size away from the skull base would not lead to these interferences, and thus the comparison would be very similar to the ex-vivo case where no skull base is involved in the FUS targeting. The present study is accordingly more complete since it contemplates how the skull base can also affect the resulting focal amplitude. For additional clarification, in the case simulated it should be noted that a weaker reflection at the skull base due to a thinner skull layer (and relatively smaller than the wavelength) will lead to a weaker constructive interference, and therefore, a lower focal pressure, signifying higher attenuation. The opposite would happen in the case of a stronger reflection at the base due to a thicker bone layer, i.e., a stronger interference, and therefore, a higher resulting focal pressure, signifying lower attenuation (higher focal pressure due to effective lower losses). This is supported by the resulting average skull thickness across the sonicated region captured by the pCT and CT models (see Fig. 5-d), with 2.68±0.28 mm and 3.38±0.13 mm, respectively, where the pCT thickness was 0.71±0.16 mm thinner. This means that skull layers or regions with a thickness of 0.71±0.16 mm or below will not be captured by the ZTE-MRI sequence. Note that the in-vivo cases results of averaged thickness, attenuation and focal shift were calculated by excluding the case of NHP #1 - target B (see Fig. 4-b1,b2,b3, Fig. 5 and n Multimedia file) because, for this particular trajectory, the sonicated average pCT-based skull thickness of 2.11 mm (or ) matched very closely with the quarter-wavelength () at the fundamental frequency considering the mean sonicated skull sound speed, leading to a maximum and considerably higher transmission33, and consequently lower calculated total attenuation for the pCT (see Fig. 5-c for NHP #1 - target B) compared to the CT. For all other three sonication cases, both the pCT and CT sonicated average skull thickness values lied considerably far from λ /4, ranging between λ /3.1 and λ /2.2, and were more similar between pCT and CT. Therefore, the ratio of sonicated average skull thickness to the wavelength is also important and has to be considered; in particular when the skull thickness is close to λ /4, where the transmission considerably increases (transcranial attenuation decreases). Another aspect directly related to the influence of standing waves on focus self-interference is the target location, where in Fig. 6-g2 it is well centered in the brain, showing a cleaner and more homogeneous focal pressure distribution, in contrast with Fig. 6-a2, where a similar trajectory (on other NHP) but deeper target, closer to the skull base, results in a spotted focus with clear heterogeneous pressure distribution. In addition, these same cases (Fig. 6-a2 vs Fig. 6-b2 and Fig. 6-g2 vs Fig. 6-h2) show how the standing wave generation decreases for the pCT skull model when compared to the CT one, which might indirectly explain the weaker reflection from the thinner captured skull base from the ZTE-MRI sequence. Moreover, since there is an attenuation increase observed in the ex-vivo case as well, given the absence of skull base in that case, it makes more sense that there are actually two factors to consider: on one hand, the intrinsic heterogeneous distribution of bone density captured by the ZTE-MRI sequence, with denser cortical skull values for the resulting pCT (see the error between CT and pCT HU maps shown in Fig. 8-f), which differs from the distribution captured by the CT, leading to a slightly shifted histogram distribution to higher values (peaks towards the right of the pCT histogram shown in Fig. 8-e3), even though the average histogram value is similar as a result of the conversion method; on the other hand, the shear wave generation effect (skull base thickness and target location dependent). Therefore, the denser (harder) cortical property is the only factor of attenuation increase for the ex-vivo case, while both the denser cortical skull layer and the weaker focal reflection at the skull base contribute jointly to the attenuation increase in the in-vivo case. For a complete understanding and visualization of the simulated pressure-field distribution across the sagittal and coronal planes for each NHP procedure, Fig. 6 shows the predicted maps for both pCT and CT models. While for the cases shown in Fig. 6-a1,a2,b1,b2,e1,e2,f1,f2,g1,g2,h1,h2 both pCT-based and CT-based foci had a similar and homogeneous pressure distribution, with the highest pressure values in intense yellow color, only the case in Fig. 6-c1,c2,d1,d2 showed a clear difference in the pressure homogeneity within the focal region between pCT-based and CT-based pressure maps, where there was a very local maximum focal pressure generated right above the skull base for the pCT model (white arrow in Fig. 6-d1,d2). This limitation occurring at the skull base, not being fully captured by this ZTE-MRI sequence, leads to a trade-off in relevant information regarding the simulation prediction when FUS is reflected at the skull base. This wave reflection is not relevant for an ex-vivo setup since the skull is a fragment and it is not an enclosed cavity, but for BBBO planing, the wave naturally reflects inside the cavity and generates standing waves, which is considerably influential for long pulse sonications, interfering with the naturally elongated focus of our employed transducer and therefore resulting in a different focal pressure and thus, different computed transcranial attenuation. However, this effect only occurred for one of our BBBO prediction cases. Unlike the large focal size and low frequency used in human trials working well for the human brain size3, a smaller focal size transducer at a higher frequency might be more suitable for this NHP model34,35, adapted to the smaller brain size, and resulting in a negligible standing wave effect, with minimal reflections at the skull base and not interfering with the focus.

Regarding the larger BBBO observed as compared to the predicted focal volume (Fig. 5-a), note that depending on the brain tissue sonicated (white, gray matter, ventricles, cerebrospinal fluid, etc.), the threshold of pressure for BBBO is different32,35. In addition, the simulated focal volume might not exactly predict the 100% of BBB opening volume, since the simulated total transcranial attenuation is most likely underestimated due to shear waves not considered in this simplified k-space simulation method, making the lower BBBO volume compared to the predicted focal volume interpretable.

In addition, uncertainty bounds must be established for the employment of the proposed method to other studies in order to ensure a successful and accurate pCT-based simulation prediction, given the reduced sample size and the observed limitations, in particular with the FUS-BBBO case of NHP #1 - target B (Fig. 6-c,d), which was post-hoc excluded for the averaging of slope and intercept for the linear fits. Two main considerations to take into account: on the one hand, avoiding a trajectory through the pCT skull where the average sonicated thickness is very close to the quarter wavelength. This will prevent the thickness-related considerable maximization of the transcranial transmission and therefore, incorrectly underestimating the attenuation prediction compared to the CT, which is the exact opposite trend as found for all other in-vivo and ex-vivo cases. We recommend to ensure a pCT thickness of at least λ /3.1 from our observations. On the other hand, avoiding the tail of the focus reaching the base of the skull, where complex shear wave generation might considerably differ from CT ones (given the pCT cannot accurately capture skull layers thinner than 0.71 mm) and directly affect the maximum focal pressure value generated transcranially, and corresponding predicted attenuation.

Lastly, this fully non-ionizing skull imaging procedure would be highly acknowledgeable in the clinic, where the treatment of brain diseases such as Alzheimer’s or Parkinson’s would benefit from subsequent skull imaging for the most updated skull properties, given that these brain disorders take many years to progress, and therefore, ensuring a more accurate and successful BBBO procedure. Future work to be explored can include the incorporation of more NHP datasets and BBBO procedures for repeatability, as well as human scans for its validation and applicability in ongoing clinical trials.

Conclusion

This study showed the excellent accuracy of ZTE-MRI sequence to model the 3D skull bone for BBB opening simulation planning as compared to a CT-based skull model. In particular, the difference between pCT- and CT-based simulations of the FUS targeting through an ex-vivo NHP skull fragment was , , 0.49± 0.00 mm and 0.00± 0.00 mm, for the focal volume, focal attenuation, axial and lateral focal shifts, respectively. The difference between pCT-based simulations and experiments was , 1.74± 0.57 mm and 0.30± 0.11 mm, for the focal attenuation, axial and lateral focal shifts, respectively. Finally, we compared pCT- and CT-based simulations to the BBB opening volume and location for four different FUS procedures on two separate NHPs trough their intact skull, and the difference was , , and 0.71± 0.16~mm, for the focal volume, focal attenuation, in-focus BBB opening volume, and sonicated skull thickness, respectively. The pCT-based simulation correctly predicted an of BBB opening volume, compared to provided by CT-based simulation. In conclusion, the study demonstrated the simplicity, accuracy and feasibility of a direct conversion method from ZTE-MRI intensity to CT intensity, resulting in a fully non-ionizing NHP skull imaging employed for the planning and prediction of BBBO procedures.

Methods

CT and ZTE skull acquisition

Two ex-vivo and four in-vivo NHP skulls (rhesus macaques of average weight of 13.4 kg, obtained from the California National Primate Research Center (CNPRC) and LABS of Virginia, INC) were evaluated in this study, and they underwent a CT and a ZTE-MRI scan. The CT scanner used was the Siemens Biograph64_mCT 4R with a spatial resolution of 0.60×0.35×0.35 mm, while the MRI system used was the 3 T GE SIGNA Premier MR with an echo time of 0 ms, a repetition time of 598.424 ms, a flip angle of 1, voxel size of 0.4×0.47×0.47 mm. First, in order to compare between different skull imaging modalities, the goal was to have both CT and ZTE scans in Hounsfield Units (HU), which correspond to the default unit of a conventional CT scan. Therefore, starting with the CT modality, the map of the CT-based skull was obtained by thresholding the CT intensity in HU by making zero all values below 400 HU (cancellous bone threshold)36.

Subsequently, in order to proceed with the conversion method from ZTE intensity to HU, the first step consisted of segmenting the skull tissue from the raw ZTE map following the pipeline shown in Fig. 7. The skull coordinates correspond to the higher intensities (higher-intensity pixels in Fig. 7-a), but excluding the air surrounding the NHP’s head which also presents a high ZTE intensity. Then, an N3 bias filter (shown in Fig. 7-b) was applied to the raw ZTE image to correct for the intensity irregularities inherent to the MR scanner9, resulting in a filtered ZTE image (shown in Fig. 7-c). The normalized histogram of ZTE intensities showing the difference between the raw and the filtered ZTE images is shown in Fig. 7-d. Then, to isolate the higher ZTE intensity values corresponding to the skull, thresholding was applied to the filtered ZTE image by isolating the ZTE intensities comprised between peak+2·FWHM and peak−2*FWHM9, where peak and peak correspond to the peak ZTE intensity values of soft tissues and air, respectively, and FWHM and FWHM correspond to the full width at half maximum decay from the peak intensity of soft tissues and air, respectively (see histogram on Fig. 7-d). The resulting histogram-thresholded ZTE image is shown in Fig. 7-e, where the skull is segmented, but also other smaller regions, such as the skin-air interfaces. Finally, a connected-component labeling (bwlabel, bwconncomp and regionprops functions in Matlab) was applied to completely isolate the skull tissue (see yellow and green components in Fig. 7-f) by selecting the larger volume components, and subsequently, a mask filling (bwareaopen function in Matlab) was employed to recover trabecular bone regions filtered out by histogram-thresholding. The final ZTE image containing only the skull coordinates is shown in Fig. 7-g, and its corresponding intensity normalized histogram is shown in Fig. 7-h.

Once the skull is segmented, both the CT- and ZTE-based skull maps are co-registered in 3D for the coordinate-to-coordinate evaluation. Therefore, for each NHP skull, the CT image was registered to the ZTE one by performing a rigid body FSL registration37. Examples of a same axial slice through the CT- and ZTE-based initial skull maps are shown in Fig. 8-a,b, respectively. Once each pair of CT and ZTE skull maps were registered, the ZTE map was normalized for each skull separately in order to make the proposed method widely usable independently of the MR scanner employed. Then, the linear ZTE-CT intensity relationship was obtained for each skull by comparing each coordinate in the normalized ZTE map with its respective coordinate in the CT map. Computational model

We used a pseudo-spectral simulation method with k-space dispersion correction to numerically integrate the linearized constitutive relations of acoustics, implemented in the acoustic package of the k-Wave toolbox (GPU-optimized) for Matlab38,39. This method was selected as it provides low numerical dispersion as compared with finite-differences methods40. We used an isotropic numerical grid with a spatial step in 3D of Δh = 0.49 mm, which corresponds to a spatial sampling of 12 grid points per wavelength in water for a frequency of 0.25 MHz, leading to around 8 grid points per average skull thickness. The heterogeneous density and sound speed of the skull were converted from the CT scan in Hounsfield units using the linear-piecewise polynomials proposed in41,42. We assumed homogeneous density and sound speed values within the brain and muscle volumes, where density matched that of water (1000 kg/m), while the sound speed was 1560 m/s and 1600 m/s43–45, respectively. The absorptions at the working frequency for the skull, the brain and the muscle were set to 0.68 dB/cm46, 0.19 dB/cm44,45 and 0.75 dB/cm44, respectively. Note that the brain and epicranial muscle were segmented from the CT-scan using the ITK-SNAP software47. These segmentations were based on the CT-scan as it provides a higher contrast due to the skull brightness compared to surrounding soft tissues.The numerical stability number or Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) number was set to 0.2 for all simulated cases, always ensuring numerical convergence34.

Pressure-field measurements

Prior to the BBBO procedure, we performed pressure-field measurements through an ex-vivo configuration to validate the pCT-based skull simulation. We used one of the two ex vivo NHP skull fragments used in this study to assess the relationship between the transcranial attenuation, the focal size and the focal shift as compared to the simulation. We tested three different trajectories through the parietal skull region to obtain a more realistic assessment along different thicknesses and incidence angles. First, the skull fragment was degassed for 24 h. Then, it was submerged in a degassed water tank and aligned to the transducer with a customized 3D-printed skull holder, which ensured the same relative position of the skull with respect to the transducer in the simulation. Note that this skull holder was obtained from the simulation grid, where the outer surface of the skull already oriented according to the trajectory was obtained and converted into an STL 3D-printable model. Acoustic pressure-field was then generated by a FUS single-element transducer (Sonic Concepts, H-231, 0.25 MHz, 15 cycles) and measured using a needle hydrophone (Onda, HNP-0200) attached to a computer-controlled, three-dimensional positioning system (Velmex Inc., Lachine, QC, CAN). A custom coupling cone was designed and 3D-printed to fix the skull holder to the transducer at a known position. The single-element FUS transducer, cone, skull holder, and skull, were positioned at the bottom of the degassed water tank, with the transducer pointing upwards. This setup maintained the skull fragment in the same position by its own weight. The FUS transducer was driven by a function generator (Keysight 33500B, Santa Rosa, CA, USA) and a 50-dB power amplifier (A075, ENI Inc., Rochester, NY, USA). The pressure map was obtained by 2D raster scans with a resolution of 0.33 mm laterally and 1.0 mm axially.

BBB opening procedure

All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Columbia University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol AC-AABM7560) prior to all performed studies and were in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines for animal welfare. Two male rhesus macaques (weights: 14.2 kg and 13.5 kg, ages: 8 y.o. and 29 y.o.) were sonicated in three separate sessions: NHP 1 was sonicated at two targets (one per hemisphere) in two separate sessions (one target per session), and NHP 2 was sonicated at two targets (one per hemisphere) but in the same session. For each session, the corresponding NHP was initially sedated with a mixture of ketamine (10 mg/kg) and atropine (0.02 mg/kg) through intramuscular injection. Once sedated, the animal was intubated and catheterized via the saphenous vein. Anesthesia was induced and maintained throughout the experiment using inhalable isoflurane mixed with oxygen (1–2%). The system was comprised of a single-element FUS transducer (Sonic Concepts H-231, f0 = 0.25 MHz, OD = 110 mm, F = 110 mm, and ID = 44 mm) confocally mounted with a linear array transducer that is connected to an ultrasound data acquisition platform (Vantage 256, Verasonics) for passive acoustic mapping (PAM) and cavitation dose monitoring. This system was attached to a robotic arm (UR5e, Universal Robots), and tracked in 3D, as well as the NHP head, using a neuronavigation system (Brainsight, Rogue Research), ensuring accurate positioning and targeting34. The FUS transducer was driven by a function generator (KeySight 33500B, Santa Rosa, CA, USA) and a power amplifier (A075, ENI Inc., Rochester, NY, USA). Then, for the BBBO procedure, the predicted attenuation from the CT-based skull simulation model was employed. The pCT-based simulation was perfomed as well for comparison. Afterwards, 0.05 mL/kg Definity microbubbles were injected intravenously and 250-kHz FUS (derated peak negative pressure: 0.2 MPa, PRF: 2 Hz, pulse length: 10 ms) was applied for 2 minutes. For NHP 2, two microbubble injections were preformed, one per sonicated target. Then, T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired before and after intravenous administration of 0.2 mL/kg gadodiamide contrast agent (Omniscan, GE Healthcare Bronk, NY, USA) and post-FUS BBBO was assessed approximately 60 min post-sonication (T1-MRI 3-D spoiled gradient-echo, TR/TE: 20/1.4 ms, flip angle: 30, number of excitations NEX: 2, spatial resolution: 0.5×0.5 mm, slice thickness: 1 mm with no inter-slice gap)48. BBBO was quantified by comparing pre- and post-contrast scans3,49. Safety outcomes were assessed with axial T2-weighted MRI (TR/TE: 3000/80 ms, flip angle: 90, NEX: 3, spatial resolution: 0.4×0.4 mm, slice thickness: 2 mm with no inter-slice gap) and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI; TR/TE: 19/27 ms; flip angle: 15, NEX: 1, spatial resolution: 400×400 mm; slice thickness: 1 mm with no inter-slice gap). All scans were performed in a 3-T clinical MRI scanner.
