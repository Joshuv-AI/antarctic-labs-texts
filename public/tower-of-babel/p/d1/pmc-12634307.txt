Restless legs syndrome and inflammatory bowel disease: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Seyedeh Marzieh Banihashemian, Samira Pirzad, Mohadeseh Poudineh, Faezeh Sharafi, Shaghayegh JamshidiRastabi, Seyed Abdolvahab Banihashemian, Seyedeh Elham Jafari, Mohammadsadegh Jafari, Yasaman Tamaddon, Saeid Najafi, Fatemeh Mosayyebi, Shayan Ashrafi, Asal Moftakhar, Iman Elahi Vahed, Mohammad Rahmanian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a prolonged inflammatory disorder that impacts the digestive system. To refer to one neurological complication, restless leg syndrome (RLS) manifests as movement impairment and leg restlessness. This situationprompted a need for a systematic review and meta-analysis to explore the correlation of these two diseases.MethodsA systematized search of relevant studies that were published up to September 2024 was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The search strategy included keywords related to IBD, RLS, and their synonyms using MeSH terms. Data extraction and quality assessment were performed using a predefined protocol. All statistical analyses were performed using R software with the package “meta.”ResultsThe pooled prevalence of RLS in IBD patients was 0.15 (0.08, 0.27), with a high heterogeneity indicated by I² = 98.8 % (p-value < 0.001). The subgroup analysis demonstrated notable variations depending on the geographic location. The prevalence of RLS in Crohn's disease (CD) was 0.17 (0.05, 0.45) with a high heterogeneity (I²: 98.9 %, P-value < 0.0001), and the prevalence of RLS in ulcerative colitis (UC) was 0.18 (0.06, 0.43) with a high heterogeneity (I²: 98.2 %, P-value < 0.0001). Positive associations have been found between CD and subsequent RLS in both sexes, as well as between UC and RLS, specifically in women.ConclusionThis analysis can provide compelling evidence to support any potential association between IBD and RLS, highlighting the importance of recognizing and addressing the potential comorbidity between these two conditions in clinical practice.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic inflammatory disease that causes inflammation of the digestive system. There are two principal diseases: ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD) (Li et al., 2023). Between 1990 and 2019, the number of people living with IBD rose significantly from 3.3 million to 4.9 million, highlighting the growing disease burden and its implications for patient care (Wang et al., 2023). These diseases can lead to severe psychosocial and physical symptoms for patients, impacting society through workplace absenteeism, increased healthcare expenses, and missed school days (Jairath and Feagan, 2020). Up to 50 % of all patients with IBD Manifestations outside the intestine, affecting the rheumatologic, musculocutaneous, hepatobiliary, and other systems (Gordon et al., 2024).

IBD patients can develop neurological clinical signs, whether as a component of the disease or via marginal complications like anemia (Resál et al., 2021).

Restless leg syndrome (RLS) is identified by an unpleasant restless feeling affecting the legs in a temporary way relieved by motion in the legs (Mosli et al., 2020, Manconi et al., 2021). The episodes are often associated with tingling, burning, pins-and-needles, or sore sensations that intensify during periods of rest and improve with movement (Gossard et al., 2021). This discomfort occurs mostly at night and during rest and could also impede sleep (Vlasie et al., 2022). Epidemiological researches have shown a varied worldwide prevalence of RLS, Varying between 0.96–2.9 % in countries like Japan and India to 5–29 % within the general populations In the United States, Canada, and Western Europe (Innes et al., 2011, Guo et al., 2017, Garcia-Borreguero and Cano-Pumarega, 2017). The syndrome is often idiopathic, with no identifiable underlying cause, though it is frequently associated with a familial predisposition. However, researchers have that RLS may also occur as a result of several comorbid conditions, including iron and ferritin deficiency, impaired kidney function, pregnancy and eclampsia, multiple sclerosis, peripheral neuropathy, and disorders of the digestive system such as gastrectomy and IBD (Liu et al., 2022). Recent studies have pointed to potential links between RLS and obesity (Lin et al., 2018), diabetes (Cuellar and Ratcliffe, 2008), renal failure and hemodialysis (Unruh et al., 2004), pregnancy (Uglane et al., 2011), reduced ferritin levels or iron deficiency (Frauscher et al., 2009), inherited neuropathy (Pizza et al., 2012), and medications such as beta-adrenergic antagonists, anti-epileptic drugs (Trenkwalder et al., 2016).

Chronic systemic inflammation, iron deficiency from intestinal blood loss or malabsorption, and the effect of chronic disease on sleep regulation are some of the processes that point to a potential connection between IBD and RLS. RLS is more common in people with gastrointestinal and chronic inflammatory diseases in the past, although results in IBD in particular have been mixed and based on small sample sizes (Weinstock et al., 2012). Our comprehension of whether RLS is an underappreciated extraintestinal symptom of IBD is limited by this gap in the literature. We speculate that, because of overlapping inflammatory and metabolic pathways, Patients with IBD have a higher rate of RLS than the general population. In this systematic review and meta-analysis performed initially, we aim to synthesize current evidence to improve comprehension of the simultaneous occurrence of RLS in patients with IBD, providing insights that may guide future research and therapeutic strategies.

Methods

This study was carried out following the standard methodology outlined in the Cochrane Handbook (Cumpston et al., 2019) and was reported As per the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) recommendations (Page et al., 2021). The study’s research plan has been Listed on the Open Science Framework (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/F3QRJ).

Search strategy

We searched PubMed, the Scopus database, the Web of Science database (WOS), and Google Scholar until September 2024. The search strategy used a wide range of terms relating to IBD and RLS. The search strategy used a comprehensive approach using the following terms from Medical Subject Headings (MeSH): “Crohn Disease,” “Inflammatory Bowel Diseases,” “Colitis, Ulcerative,” and “Restless Legs Syndrome.” Details can be found in Table S1. To improve the thoroughness of our literature search, we utilized both forward and backward citation searching methods. Forward citation searching identifies more recent studies that have cited the studies since their release. Conversely, backward citation searching entails examining the reference lists of the selected studies to discover additional relevant research that may have been overlooked initially, thereby ensuring the inclusion of essential foundational studies. By utilizing resources such as WOS and Google Scholar, this approach allows us to capture the latest advancements in research and emerging trends.

Study selection

The eligibility assessment of the studies was conducted by S.P. and S.J., who independently conducted the title and abstract and the full-text screening according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. In cases of disagreement, a third reviewer (M.R.) was consulted. By initial screening through abstracts, studies that weren’t directly related to the main topic were excluded. Studies were included if they met all of these criteria: (Li et al., 2023) The study must be original; (Wang et al., 2023) studies were included if they investigated the association between RLS and IBD in patients, regardless of whether a significant relationship was found, to capture the full spectrum of evidence; (Jairath and Feagan, 2020) the study group must have a confirmed clinical evaluation of IBD, with either UC or CD manifestations, or patients diagnosed with restless legs syndrome; (Gordon et al., 2024) any individual enrolled with no sex limitation or ethnic origin was included (Resál et al., 2021). A clinical gastroenterological diagnosis confirming IBD must have been conducted. A detailed summary is provided in Table 1. Short surveys, errata, conference reports, book chapters, and review articles were excluded from the reference search studies. Data extraction

The data extraction form was designed carefully by S.B. and M.P. They extracted data from the included studies that met our eligibility criteria, and any disagreements were resolved through consensus. The following information was extracted: the first author name, year of publication, country of origin, study duration, number of patients suffering from IBD and without IBD, number of control group participants, median age of the study and control groups, number of participants of each sex, diagnosis criteria for RLS and IBD, and the RLS prevalence in IBD patients and non-IBD participants.

Risk of bias assessment

S.B. and S.P. evaluated the quality of the included studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI)'s critical appraisal tools (Institute JB, 2018). Items including measures of exposures, study population, extent of outcomes, confounding factors, statistical analysis, and follow-up data were assessed.

Statistical analysis

To investigate RLS prevalence in individuals with inflammatory bowel disease, we gathered data from multiple studies and combined the percentages of patients suffering from RLS in each report. We used a random-effects model to pool data from these various studies to obtain an overall RLS prevalence estimation in IBD sufferers. The assessed heterogeneity was applied by using the I² statistic. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis has been applied to examine the influence and effect of each separated study on the cumulative effect. The assessment of any bias in publication was evaluated by funnel plot inspection and employing Egger’s test. Subgroup analyses based on study design, age, and country were also performed to explore any variables contributing to heterogeneity. Complete statistical analyses were performed using R software with the package “meta.”

Results

Study selection

By utilizing the search methodology, a total of 175 research studies (11 from Google Scholar, 44 from Web of Science, 93 from Scopus, and 24 from PubMed) were collected from the database. After removing duplicate studies and screening the titles and abstracts, 121 articles were retrieved for full-text assessment. Lastly, 10 studies comprised the current systematic review and meta-analysis. Risk of bias assessment

All ten studies were evaluated for quality using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist. The assessment results can be found in Supplementary Tables S2, S3, and S4. The research by Mosli et al. (2020) showed strong methodological rigor but had limitations, such as the lack of matching cases and controls and unreliable outcome assessment (Mosli et al., 2020). The studies by Becker et al., 2018a, Becker et al., 2018b and Loosen et al. (2023b) revealed inconsistencies in exposure measurement, and Becker et al.'s outcomes were not reliably measured. However, both showed overall methodological strengths (Becker et al., 2018b, Loosen et al., 2023a). Most cross-sectional studies, including those by Hoek et al. (2015), Takahara et al. (2017), and Pourramzani et al. (2021), demonstrated strong rigor (Takahara et al., 2017, Hoek et al., 2015, Pourramzani et al., 2021), although limitations were observed. The Kitakata et al. (2022a) and Weinstock et al. (2010) studies did not use standardized criteria for condition assessment, with the latter also lacking clear inclusion criteria (Kitakata et al., 2022a, Weinstock et al., 2010). Schindlbeck et al. failed to measure exposure adequately, and AlHarbi et al. (2021) did not account for confounding factors (Schindlbeck et al., 2017, AlHarbi et al., 2021). Nevertheless, the methodological rigor of the included studies varied but included several strengths that enhance their credibility. For instance, studies such as Loosen et al. (2023b) employed large sample sizes (e.g., 35,988 patients) and utilized standardized diagnostic criteria to ensure consistent identification of RLS. Additionally, Takahara et al. Several studies, such as the study by Loosen et al (Loosen et al., 2023a)., Schindlbeck et al (Schindlbeck et al., 2017)., Hoek et al (Hoek et al., 2015)., Pourramzani et al (Pourramzani et al., 2021)., Takahara et al (Takahara et al., 2017)., and Kitakata et al (Kitakata et al., 2022b)., have explored the relationship between RLS and types of IBD, including CD or UC. One study found no significant association between low, moderate, or high hemoglobin levels and RLS, with hemoglobin levels as an indicator for iron status, a factor linked to the pathophysiology of RLS (Loosen et al., 2023c). while another study observed a significant prevalence of clinical remission in addition to mucosal healing (MH) in RLS sufferers. Although MH was not directly linked to RLS, clinical remission and MH could potentially have protective effects against RLS (Kitakata et al., 2022a). Although Kitakata et al. suggest that clinical remission and mucosal healing in IBD patients may have a protective effect against RLS, this remains a novel and underexplored area (Kitakata et al., 2022a).

Additionally, studies have found a positive relation between CD and the occurrence of RLS in both genders and also between UC disease and the occurrence of RLS in all the cases studied, specifically in women. However, no remarkable relation was found between the two CD or UC diseases and Parkinson's disease (PD) in the entire study population, although an increasing slope in the percentage of PD cases was observed in men who suffered from CD (Loosen et al., 2023a).

In terms of secondary causes of RLS, iron deficiency (ID) was most prevalent, followed by a deficiency of folic acid (FD) and vitamin B12 (VB12D). Despite some improvements in RLS symptoms after iron supplementation, no significant trend was observed at week 11. The intensity of RLS symptoms, measured by the IRLS (Abetz et al., 2006) score, wasn’t significantly different among patients with CD and UC, although it was noted that patients who were 45 and older had remarkably higher IRLS scores (Mosli et al., 2020) (Table 3).“Further RLS risk factors “

The sensitivity analysis showed that omitting any single study did not substantially change the pooled prevalence estimate of RLS, which remained stable between 13.1 % and 18.0 %, with consistently high heterogeneity (I² > 92 %) and non-significant influence tests (p > 0.7), confirming the robustness of the overall meta-analysis results.

Prevalence of RLS in ulcerative colitis

The pooled prevalence was 0.18 (0.06, 0.43) with a high heterogeneity (I²: 98.2 %, P-value < 0.0001) (Fig. 2C). Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test indicate the presence of asymmetry, suggesting publication bias (p = 0.0156) (Fig. 2D). Subgroup analysis revealed significant differences based on study design but not based on country (Table 3).

The sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the pooled prevalence estimate of RLS remained relatively stable, ranging from 13.3 % to 24.9 % when individual studies were omitted. The overall pooled estimate was 18.0 % (95 % CI: 5.9–43.4 %), with high heterogeneity (I² = 98.2 %). No single study had a disproportionate impact on the results, confirming the robustness of the meta-analysis despite substantial between-study variability (tau² = 1.36).

Prevalence of RLS in Crohn’s disease

The pooled prevalence was 0.17 (0.05, 0.45) with a high heterogeneity (I²: 98.9 %, P-value < 0.0001) (Fig. 2E). Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test did not indicate the presence of asymmetry (p = 0.1822) (Fig. 2F). Subgroup analysis revealed significant differences based on study design and country (Table 4).

The sensitivity analysis revealed that removing individual studies did not substantially alter the overall pooled prevalence of RLS, which ranged from 14.4 % to 23.9 % across leave-one-out analyses. The overall pooled estimate was 17.3 % (95 % CI: 5.1–45.0 %), with high heterogeneity (I² = 98.9 %). Notably, omitting Loosen et al. (2023b) reduced heterogeneity to 84.1 % (tau² = 0.1831), suggesting it was a key contributor to between-study variability; however, no single study had a disproportionate influence on the effect size, indicating the results are robust.

Discussion

Considering the observed co-occurrence of inflammatory processes and neurologic symptoms, the high incidence of RLS in individuals with IBD suggests a common underlying pathophysiology between them.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the prevalence and severity of RLS in patients with IBD, including both CD and UC.

Our findings reveal that RLS affects approximately 15 % of patients with IBD, with a slightly higher prevalence in UC (18 %) compared to CD (17 %), exceeding the estimates for the general population. A meta-analysis by Broströme et al (Broström et al., 2023). reported the prevalence of IBD to be 3 % (95 % CI: 1.4 %-3.8 %) in the general adult population. These findings align with previous research by Takahara et al., Hoek PD et al., Weinstock et al., and Alharbi et al., who reported an elevated RLS prevalence in IBD studies (Takahara et al., 2017, Hoek et al., 2015, AlHarbi et al., 2021, Weinstock et al., 2010).

However, the prevalence of RLS among IBD patients varies significantly across studies. Reported rates range from 8 % in UC patients (Becker et al., 2018a) to 30 % in CD patients (Weinstock et al., 2010). Hoek PD et al. identified that RLS was 2.5 times more prevalent in CD patients than in healthy controls (Hoek et al., 2015). Also, Takahara et al. reported RLS rates of 17.6 % in CD and 21.7 % in UC, both markedly higher than the Japanese population (Takahara et al., 2017). For example, Nomura et al (Nomura et al., 2008). reported the prevalence of RLS to be 1.8 % in a rural community in that country. Loosen et al. demonstrated a positive association between IBD and RLS, with this relationship being significant in the CD population. However, in UC patients, the association with RLS was significant only in women, suggesting potential sex-specific differences in susceptibility within the UC subgroup (Loosen et al., 2023a). Gender and metabolic factors may play a role in RLS severity. Mosli et al. identified sex and metabolic disorders as contributors to worsening RLS symptoms (Mosli et al., 2020). Becker et al. and Purramezani et al. also found a strong link between RLS severity and female sex, supporting the idea that hormonal and physiological factors may influence its development (Becker et al., 2018a, Pourramzani et al., 2021, Berger et al., 2004). However, Alharbi et al. did not observe a female predisposition, suggesting that other factors may shape this association and require further investigation (AlHarbi et al., 2021).

Nevertheless, some studies reported conflicting findings. Mosli et al. found no significant association between RLS and IBD, suggesting that population-based variations may influence the observed associations (Mosli et al., 2020). Differences between studies highlight the need for standardized criteria for diagnosis and large-scale, multiethnic, longitudinal studies to accurately measure the burden of RLS in IBD and better define its underlying pathophysiology.

Differences between UC and CD are notable. Becker et al. found that moderate to severe RLS is more common in CD than UC, likely due to widespread systemic inflammation in CD and its broader neurological impact (Becker et al., 2018a). Hoek PD et al. reported that RLS prevalence is higher in CD patients with severe disease, particularly those who had undergone CD-related surgery. This association suggests systemic inflammation, gut permeability, and disease burden contribute to RLS in CD (Hoek et al., 2015).

The impact of RLS on quality of life is significant among IBD patients. Schindlbeck et al. reported that individuals with RLS in the IBD population experience more sleep disturbances, fatigue, and pain, which may be attributed to systemic inflammation and potential disruptions in CNS iron regulation and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (Schindlbeck et al., 2017, Takahara et al., 2017, Hoek et al., 2015, Weinstock et al., 2010). Additionally, RLS has been shown to negatively affect physical functioning, a fundamental aspect of quality of life (Hoek et al., 2015).

Sleep disturbances in IBD are commonly attributed to abdominal pain, diarrhea, acid reflux (Zimmerman, 2003), nocturnal bowel activity (Salwen-Deremer et al., 2022), and psychiatric comorbidities such as depression and anxiety (Yu et al., 2023). However, Takahara et al. found that poor sleep was significantly more prevalent in the RLS group (62.5 %) compared to the non-RLS group, suggesting that RLS may independently contribute to sleep disruption in IBD (Takahara et al., 2017). Additionally, systemic inflammation in IBD has been linked to sleep disturbances through elevated circulating inflammatory markers (Wilson et al., 2015). These findings highlight the need for targeted management of sleep disorders in IBD patients with RLS to improve quality of life and disease outcomes.

The association between RLS and IBD involves multiple mechanisms, including iron metabolism disturbances, chronic inflammation, gut dysbiosis, and metabolic dysfunction. RLS has been closely linked to iron metabolism disturbances, with increasing evidence suggesting that functional brain iron deficiency plays a key role in its pathogenesis. Even in studies where systemic iron deficiency is not consistently associated with RLS, a history of iron deficiency is more frequently observed in patients with IBD and RLS, indicating a potential role in disease susceptibility (Becker et al., 2018a, Weinstock et al., 2010).

While some studies have shown reduced iron levels in specific brain regions in RLS, an MRI-based meta-analysis has produced conflicting results (Loosen et al., 2023a, Dauvilliers and Winkelmann, 2013, Earley et al., 2000). However, the issue may not be total iron content but rather its bioavailability. One proposed mechanism suggests that iron becomes sequestered in mitochondria, making it unavailable for neuronal function, leading to oxidative stress, dopaminergic dysfunction, and hyperglutamatergic activity—key features of RLS. Additionally, dysregulated hepcidin levels may impair iron transport across the blood-brain barrier, further restricting iron availability in the CNS. This mechanism aligns with findings in IBD, where chronic inflammation and altered iron homeostasis contribute to systemic and possibly central iron dysregulation (Beliveau et al., 2022). Because low brain iron concentration may play a role in altered dopamine levels, providing a unifying condition for RLS, disruptions in iron homeostasis could directly impair the dopaminergic pathways crucial for motor control and sensory processing, further exacerbating symptoms (Rama and Kushida, 2004). Supporting the role of iron metabolism disruptions in RLS, Becker et al. reported that intravenous iron supplementation significantly reduced RLS severity in IBD patients, particularly within the first four weeks of treatment.

Alharbi et al. identified low hemoglobin levels as an additional risk factor for RLS, while Kitakata et al. found no such association. Low hemoglobin levels, often resulting from chronic iron deficiency or anemia, which is common in IBD due to intestinal blood loss or malabsorption, are a known risk factor for RLS, as iron is critical for dopamine synthesis in the central nervous system (Mahadea et al., 2021a, Connor et al., 2017). Since ferritin plays a key role in iron metabolism and CNS iron homeostasis, assessing ferritin, transferrin saturation, and hepcidin levels in IBD patients with RLS may help determine whether functional iron deficiency or iron misdistribution contributes to this association (Chenini et al., 2020).

The gut–immune–brain axis may further link IBD to RLS. Chronic inflammation and gut microbiota alterations contribute to neuroimmune activation and dopaminergic dysfunction, increasing RLS susceptibility (Loosen et al., 2023a). Chronic systemic inflammation in IBD, mediated by pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein (CRP), has been implicated in the pathogenesis of RLS. Elevated levels of IL-6 and CRP have been observed in RLS patients undergoing hemodialysis, further supporting the role of chronic inflammation in RLS development (Higuchi et al., 2015). IL-6 stimulates hepcidin production, inhibiting intestinal iron absorption and reducing iron availability, a key process in RLS pathogenesis (Nemeth et al., 2004). Chronic inflammation in IBD further exacerbates systemic iron depletion, mainly due to chronic intestinal bleeding, thereby increasing the risk of RLS (Fertrin, 2020, Mahadea et al., 2021b). Takahara et al. found that CNS iron deficiency, influenced by inflammatory cytokines and gut dysbiosis, plays a significant role in RLS development.

Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) may also contribute to RLS, particularly in CD patients. Weinstock et al. suggested bacterial overgrowth triggers systemic inflammation, IL-6 release, and hepcidin production, reducing CNS iron levels. This mechanism is further supported by studies demonstrating that RLS symptoms improve following antibiotic treatment in patients with SIBO, linking gut microbiome alterations to RLS (Weinstock et al., 2010). SIBO also produces nitric oxide, which can disrupt the integrity of the intestinal barrier, enter systemic circulation, and trigger inflammation or immune alterations (Van Hul et al., 2024). Additionally, as a potent neurotransmitter in the nervous system, nitric oxide may contribute to the onset, exacerbation, and pathophysiology of RLS symptoms (Takahara et al., 2017, Jiménez-Jiménez et al., 2015). G. Baskol et al. have found lower levels of nitric oxide along with higher levels of oxidative stress markers in RLS patients, which leads to vascular dysfunction and brain hypoperfusion (Baskol et al., 2012). It has also been suggested that nitric oxide synthase (NOS) expression is elevated in the substantia nigra of RLS patients and the spinal cord of an animal model resembling RLS patients (Jiménez-Jiménez et al., 2015, Clemens et al., 2005). Strengthening this association, nitric oxide has been shown to activate the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway, which has been implicated in RLS pathogenesis (Patton et al., 2011) and is similarly activated in active IBD (Cummins and Crean, 2017). The biological impact of increased NO production in inflammatory disease may therefore be primarily mediated by its effects on tissue hypoxia and oxygen-sensing mechanisms like HIF, as NO may interfere with downstream signaling mechanisms, including HIF and O₂ distribution and sensing mechanisms, due to its significant similarities with O₂ (Olson and van der Vliet, 2011). This suggests that UC-induced hypoxia may play a role in the onset or worsening of RLS, potentially linking chronic intestinal inflammation to neurological dysfunction through shared inflammatory and hypoxic pathways. Nevertheless, given the limited evidence, any proposed links between nitric oxide or hypoxia-inducible pathways and RLS in IBD patients should be considered speculative and require further rigorous investigation to establish their validity.

As mentioned above, disrupting the intestinal barrier in IBD leads to increased permeability, which worsens inflammation. This breakdown allows lipopolysaccharides to pass from the intestinal lumen into the bloodstream, triggering the release of proinflammatory cytokines. These cytokines interact with key CNS receptors, potentially driving neuroinflammatory processes and linking gut dysfunction to neurological involvement in IBD (Guerville and Boudry, 2016).

Endocrine and metabolic imbalances play a significant role in RLS severity, with thyroid dysfunction, diabetes, and iron deficiency as interconnected factors. Alharbi et al. identified elevated TSH as a risk factor for worsening RLS, likely due to its reciprocal regulation with dopamine, a key neurotransmitter in RLS pathophysiology (AlHarbi et al., 2021). Dopamine inhibits TSH secretion, and disruptions in this balance may contribute to disease progression. Iron deficiency further exacerbates this imbalance by reducing dopamine bioavailability and impairing thyroid hormone metabolism, worsening symptoms (Pereira et al., 2010). Mosli et al. also found that diabetes and hypothyroidism were linked to more severe RLS, suggesting that sex hormones, insulin resistance, and thyroid dysfunction contribute to disease progression by modulating dopaminergic pathways and iron metabolism (Mosli et al., 2020).

Alharbi et al. identified obesity (BMI >30 kg/m²) as the second-highest risk factor for RLS in IBD patients, supporting prior evidence of a potential link between obesity and RLS (Yildiz et al., 2017). Research suggests that iron metabolism and the dopaminergic system may be altered in both conditions, indicating shared pathophysiological mechanisms (AlHarbi et al., 2021).

Individuals with obesity exhibit fewer dopamine D2 receptors, which may disrupt dopaminergic signaling and impair motor control, increasing RLS susceptibility (Lin et al., 2018).

Vitamin B12 deficiency, a known cause of neuropathy, has been linked to RLS pathogenesis. Mosli et al. reported a significant association between RLS and vitamin B12 deficiency, emphasizing the impact of nutritional deficiencies on symptom severity. However, Alharbi et al. found that IBD patients with RLS had higher mean vitamin B12 levels, which may challenge this association. This contrast could be due to widespread micronutrient supplementation in IBD patients, potentially masking an underlying deficiency (Mosli et al., 2020, AlHarbi et al., 2021).

Age may influence the progression of RLS, as older IBD patients often experience worsening symptoms over time, possibly due to cumulative inflammation and neurodegenerative changes (Becker et al., 2018a, Pourramzani et al., 2021). Alharbi et al. further supported this by identifying a higher risk of RLS in individuals aged 46–59 in Saudi Arabia, suggesting an age-related susceptibility (AlHarbi et al., 2021). However, another cohort study in Saudi Arabia by Mosli et al. did not find this association, highlighting the need for further research to clarify the role of age in RLS development among IBD patients (Mosli et al., 2020).

These findings highlight the complex nature of RLS in IBD. A comprehensive clinical assessment is essential to identify and manage contributing factors. Future research should focus on targeted therapies that address inflammation, iron metabolism, gut dysbiosis, and metabolic dysfunction to improve RLS outcomes in IBD patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The most notable is the high heterogeneity (I² > 94 %) across included studies, reflecting differences in study design, diagnostic criteria, population demographics, and disease subtypes. Cross-sectional studies exhibited the most significant heterogeneity (I² = 95.5 %), likely due to variations in data collection methods and reliance on self-reported RLS diagnoses, increasing the risk of misclassification. Additionally, studies focusing on Crohn's disease showed more significant variability than those on ulcerative colitis, possibly due to CD's diverse clinical presentation and the systemic nature of CD.

The generalizability of the results is also influenced by geographic variability and healthcare system differences. The publication bias (p < 0.0001) suggests that studies with negative findings may be underrepresented, potentially leading to overestimating the IBD-RLS association. Additionally, the predominance of cross-sectional studies limits the ability to determine causality between IBD severity and RLS onset. Without longitudinal data, the directionality of the IBD-RLS relationship remains unclear, as active IBD may contribute to RLS development, or RLS itself may influence disease progression through sleep disturbances and systemic inflammation. Future research should prioritize longitudinal cohort studies to clarify these causal pathways.

Standardizing RLS diagnostic criteria across studies will improve comparability and reduce misclassification bias. Expanding research to include multiethnic and diverse populations will enhance the validity of findings, ensuring broader applicability across different patient groups.

Despite these limitations, this study's systematic review and meta-analysis methodology provide a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence. Subgroup analyses (UC vs. CD, age, and study design) help mitigate potential confounders, reinforcing the robustness of the findings. While variability among studies remains challenging, the results consistently highlight a clinically significant association between RLS and IBD, warranting further investigation into its underlying mechanisms and potential therapeutic implications.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis support the complex relationship between IBD and RLS, implicating chronic inflammation, CNS iron deficiency, gut dysbiosis, and metabolic disturbances as potential underlying mechanisms. While the overall prevalence of RLS in IBD remains debated, clinically significant RLS is notably more common, contributing to sleep disturbances, fatigue, and diminished quality of life.

Notably, CD patients may experience more severe RLS symptoms than UC patients, likely due to systemic inflammation, increased gut permeability, and elevated cytokine activity. Targeted management strategies, including iron supplementation, antibiotic therapy for SIBO, and metabolic interventions, may help alleviate RLS symptoms and reduce the overall disease burden in IBD patients. Managing sleep disturbances in IBD patients is essential, as poor sleep can drive inflammatory cytokine production, worsening colitis, and promoting disease relapse. Breaking this cycle through optimized RLS management may improve both neurological symptoms and IBD outcomes.

Further research is crucial to establish causality and optimize treatment approaches for this underdiagnosed comorbidity in IBD populations.
