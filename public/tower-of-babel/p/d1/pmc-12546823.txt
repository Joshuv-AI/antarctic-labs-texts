Liquid-matter relaxor ferroelectrics by design
Authors: Xinxin Zhang, Yu Zou, Minghui Deng, Xiang Huang, Fan Ye, Xiujuan Liu, Erxiang Xu, Yang Shen, Satoshi Aya, Mingjun Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKnowing that traditional relaxor ferroelectrics stem from crystalline materials, one may ask whether they stabilize the liquid-matter analogy. The perspective is challenging and may enable a generic design of fluidic and flexible relaxor ferroelectrics. Here, we unveil a novel polar matter state, dubbed the nematic relaxor ferroelectric, by artificially introducing polar nanoregions with nematicity into a dielectric nematic environment. We observe clear signatures of a nematic relaxor, which exhibits a high field-induced polarization of 1.1 μC·cm−2 at 5 V·μm−1, over a wide temperature range (>30 K). The relaxor demonstrates two regimes: (1) stable relaxor against high electric fields (> 5 V·μm−1) at high temperatures over a 30 K range; (2) relaxor with a field-induced transition to a ferroelectric state at low temperatures. Based on the Landau-Ginzburg-Devonshire theory, we reconstruct free-energy landscapes from the electric field vs polarization curves. We observe a continuous transformation of energy landscapes from a double-well shape to a single-well shape, characterized by a broadening of the energy bottom, corresponding to a shift from nematic ferroelectrics to nematic relaxors with significant polarization fluctuations.Subject terms: Ferroelectrics and multiferroics, Ferroelectrics and multiferroics, Liquid crystals

Introduction

The discovery of ferroelectricity, dating back over a century1,2, was a monumental event that sparked fundamental research on phase-property relationships in polar matters3 and led to the development of various revolutionary technologies4. The term “ferroelectricity” broadly refers to systems carrying permanent electric polarization, but it does not delve into the details of microscopic polar states. From a paraelectric state, polar regions composed of microscopic electric polarizations are known to be able to nucleate through the first-order transition or emerge as fluctuating polar clusters in the quasi-second-order transitional manner. The former and latter cases correspond to normal and relaxor ferroelectrics (RFEs)3, respectively. The Landau–Ginzburg–Devonshire (LGD) theory provides a theoretical foundation for capturing the physical essence of the distinct ferroelectric nature5,6. In the LGD approach, a normal ferroelectric is described by a double-well free-energy landscape as a function of the polarization P below the Curie transition point, where a non-zero spontaneous polarization Ps should be specified in equilibrium (Fig. 1a). On the other hand, strong temporal or spatial fluctuations or both characterize an RFE. The corresponding free-energy landscape exhibits a broadening energy bottom about ∣P∣=0 (Fig. 1a), providing fluctuating values of P. Differentiating the Helmholtz free energy density F with respect to P allows us to investigate how polarization property changes under the application of an electric field, i.e., the calculated P-E curve (Fig. 1b). Vice versa, the experimentally accessible P-E hysteresis curves can be used to reconstruct the free-energy landscapes, thus quantitatively exploring and determining potential polar states. A regular ferroelectric shows an S-shaped P-E curve. Experimental P-E loops merely measure envelopes of the ideal S-shaped curve due to fast screening of polarizations by free charges or domain formations7,8. An RFE exhibits a shirked S-shaped P-E curve, where the permanent polarization remains at ∣E∣=0. Noteworthy, RFE is distinct from the paraelectric systems in ways that the field-induced polarization is as high as the normal ferroelectrics9, and the dielectric permittivity is ultrahigh owing to strong polarization fluctuations10,11.

In the past hundred years until 2020, experimental observation of ferroelectricity had been limited to semi-crystalline, crystalline-state materials or semi-solid smectic liquid crystals (LCs)4,12–15. The absence of fluidity provides systems with lower symmetries and higher positional orders. The deformation and replacement of crystalline lattices under specific temperatures trigger symmetry breaking. A normal ferroelectric appears when the symmetry-broken polar lattices are arranged uniformly. Meanwhile, the introduction of polar nanoregions (PNRs) into a non-polar matrix is one of the most widely accepted models to describe the structure of RFEs (Fig. 1c), which arises as a result of heterogeneity of lattice deformations in crystalline materials16,17. However, these scenarios do not apply to highly fluidic liquid-matter systems where positional orders are lost. Regardless of some theoretical perspectives of potential polar liquids18, it has been widely accepted that ferroelectricity is absent in fluids.

The recent discovery of the ferroelectric nematic (NF) fluids19–23 has just extended the concept of ferroelectricity to real liquids. With a unique mechanism, the nematic ferroelectricity is stabilized through a strong correlation between the long-range nematic orientational order and enhanced intermolecular dipolar interactions22,24–26. Additional coupling parameters, such as chirality and positional orders, can also serve as critical influencers in generating new liquid-matter ferroelectric states, including splay nematic24,27 and twist-bend ferroelectric nematic28 fluids. Here, we discover and add a distinct liquid-matter member to the broad group of ferroelectrics, dubbed the nematic relaxor ferroelectric (nRFE; Fig. 1c). We provide a general design strategy and characterization methodologies, particularly based on the reconstruction of the free-energy landscape, for the new matter states. Its stabilization is realized through a collective fluctuation of the colinear alignment of nematic PNRs, hereafter abbreviated as nPNRs, along the orientated apolar nematic background. The mechanism differs from the traditional RFEs, where PNRs orient randomly and deformational fluctuations of local crystalline lattices provide the polarization fluctuation. Although the fluid ferroelectrics are fundamentally different from solid-state ferroelectrics in that there is no lattice to constrain the orientation of the polarization field, they share similar collective alignment of polar order and electromechanical effects.

Results

Concept of nRFE: dispersing nPNRs by local heterogeneity of polarity

Typically, solid-state RFEs are achieved by introducing chemical heterogeneity. For example, metal ion doping is an essential pathway to induce spatial heterogeneity in crystalline lattices of ferroelectric ceramics, where either amorphous or apolar lattices coexist with short-range polar lattices, thereby leading to heterogeneity in local polarity. Several RFE nanostructures known so far include PNRs embedded in an apolar matrix (e.g., BiFeO3-SrTiO3 films)29, polymorphic PNRs (e.g., BiFeO3-BaTiO3-SrTiO3 films)30, and isolated slush-like polar clusters (e.g., Bi(Mg0.5Ti0.5)O3-SrTiO3)31. Similarly, in polymer RFE, e.g., poly(vinylidene fluoride−trifluoroethylene−chlorofluoroethylene) (PVDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer, copolymerizing three fluorine-containing monomers is proposed to trigger chemical heterogeneity, which breaks the long-range ferroelectric order and results in localized PNRs32–34. Inspired by these successes, but distinct in concept, we conceive an ambitious idea of realizing unprecedented liquid-matter RFEs by directly introducing heterogeneity of polarity. Noteworthy, unlike solid-state ferroelectrics, where the polar order is generated indirectly through building unsymmetric crystalline lattices, the ferroelectric order in the NF fluids is a direct result of the spontaneously-biased orientation of molecular electric dipoles made of highly polar LC molecules. This distinction suggests that a scenario involving the induction of heterogeneity of polarity may be possible through direct molecular mixing of apolar and polar molecules. This approach seems infeasible for solid-state ferroelectric systems, as the orientation of local polarity cannot be uniquely determined or manipulated artificially. Then, the critical challenge is whether one can realize the proper length scale of polar regions. Two extreme and unfavorable cases would be: (1) apolar and polar molecules are soluble at the molecular level: the size of polar regions is too tiny at nanometers and the system shows a paraelectric nature; (2) apolar and polar phases phase-separate macroscopically: the size of polar regions is over micrometers and the system behaves like normal polydomain ferroelectrics. One should quickly find that to develop liquid-matter RFE states, polar regions should satisfy a condition staying in the middle between (1) and (2), indicating that carefully adjusting the solubility of apolar and polar molecules or regions is essential.

Mean-field simulation

To validate this concept, we first performed mean-field simulations based on an extended Oseen-Frank free energy formalism (see “Methods”)35–37, which considers two-component phase-separated systems composed of nPNRs dispersed in the apolar background. In these simulations, we assume the presence of stable PNRs, as later supported by experimental observations, and focus on exploring their influence on polarization structures and dielectric behavior. While the nPNRs favor a spontaneous ferroelectric orientational order, the apolar background prefers a paraelectric ground state. Similar to solid-state RFE systems, the stability of each regime can be defined by different sets of the Landau free-energy coefficient (see “Methods”)38,39. The nematic elasticity and polar interactions are also taken into account for each region35,38. In the apolar nematic background, the Landau and nematic elastic free-energy terms are used. In the nPNRs, as well as the Landau and nematic elastic free-energy terms, the free-energy terms of electrostatic interaction40 and polarization gradient41 are included. We choose distinct sets of Landau coefficients for apolar and polar regions38,39, thereby enabling us to describe the local stability of paraelectric and ferroelectric orders, respectively. The flexoelectric effect is included in the effective splay elastic constant27 (see “Methods”). To implement our numerical simulations, we set an apolar nematic background orienting along the y-axis (Fig. 2a), mimicking a well-aligned nematic state, and then randomly embedded nPNRs with a defined characteristic size (200–400 nm). The formation of nPNRs with well-defined size arises from the interplay between polarization stabilization within the nPNRs and the energy penalties associated with both polarization gradients and depolarization fields at the apolar–polar interface. If a polar region becomes too small, the large polarization gradients at its boundary significantly increase the energy cost due to the polarization gradient energy, thereby disfavoring highly localized polar domains. Conversely, suppose a polar region grows too large. In this case, the polarization mismatch across the apolar/polar interface cannot be sufficiently compensated, resulting in long-range depolarization fields and an increase in electrostatic energy. This energy balance restricts the growth of nPNRs beyond a characteristic size.

When an nPNR comes into contact with an apolar regime (Fig. 2a), the discontinuity of polarity on the interface immediately generates bound charges. The charges then produce an induced electric field, i.e., depolarization field, which penetrates the apolar regime, leading to an anisotropic spatial distribution of induced polarizations over a length ξ in the range of micrometers along the orientational field in the apolar neighborhood (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Fig. 1a, c). That is, PNRs spontaneously polarize the vicinity of inherently apolar nematic regimes. How far the induced polarization would propagate in the apolar regime depends on the magnitude of the spontaneous polarization in nPNR. Namely, ξ is determined by the stability of nPNR described by Landau coefficients (see “Methods”). If the spontaneous polarization in nPNR is too small, the induced electric field is too weak to produce polarizations in the apolar regions. As the spontaneous polarization in an nPNR increases, ξ increases (Supplementary Fig. 1d).

Next, we incorporate nPNRs into an apolar background, and investigate their impact on the polarization structure and dielectric response by varying the volume fraction and size (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 2). As a general trend, the dielectric permittivity of the systems, ε defined as the statistical magnitude of polar fluctuations, i.e., ε=1+4π/3kBT(⟨P¯2⟩−⟨P¯⟩2) (see “Methods”), increases significantly from several tens to several thousand with increasing the volume fraction, c, of nPNRs at small concentrations, suggesting a dramatically enhanced collective polar fluctuation. After reaching a maximum value at a specific volume fraction cs, ε then decreases with increasing c (Fig. 2c). The radius of nPNRs plays an important role in revising the dielectric behaviors. When the radius of each nPNR becomes smaller while keeping the polarity of nPNRs fixed, corresponding to a more dispersive nPNR, ε becomes larger, and cs remains nearly unchanged (Fig. 2c). In other words, decreasing the size of PNRs enhances polar fluctuations.

To reveal the origin of obtaining such RFE behaviors with high dielectric permittivity, we visualize numerically calculated spatial distributions of polar fluctuations σ (Fig. 2d–f, see “Methods”). While polarization fluctuations inside nPNRs are suppressed, strong fluctuations occur in the apolar regimes with induced polarization. In the apolar nematic regimes without induced polarizations, the polar fluctuation is also considerably smaller than in the apolar regimes with induced polarization. These consequences are attributed to the competition between the inherent polar fluctuation and its suppressors. Inside each nPNR, the ferroelectric order with a high magnitude of spontaneous polarization (>3 μC·cm−2) tends to penalize any gradient of the polarization field through one of the Landau free-energy terms, i.e., the polarization gradient h∇P2 41. This effectively eliminates polar fluctuations (σ) in nPNRs (moss-green-colored areas in Fig. 2d–f), leading to a negligible contribution to the large dielectric permittivity. In the unpolarized apolar nematic regimes, on the other extreme, the equilibrium polarization is zero, resulting in limited polar fluctuations as σ→0 (white-green-colored area in Fig. 2d–f). However, in their intermediate state, the polarized regions in the apolar regimes show strong collective fluctuations (amber-colored areas in Fig. 2d–f). This striking nature arises due to a synergetic effect of the proper strength of the induced polarization and its energetically favored coupling to the depolarization field42. In these regions, the induced polarizations align syn-parallel to the depolarization field, thereby saving free energy from the electrostatic interaction term (fdep=−12P⋅Edep; Fig. 2d–f; see “Methods”). Thanks to the relatively small but finite magnitude of polarizations, the effect of the polarization gradient term is minor and does not severely suppress polar fluctuations.

Consequently, domains within PNRs act as symmetry-breaking perturbations that locally trigger polar fluctuations in the apolar matrix. The collective coupling of these fluctuations results in enhanced dielectric responses, which explains the observed high dielectric permittivity. Decreasing the radius of nPNRs, i.e., increasing the surface-to-volume ratio of apolar-polar interfaces, is equivalent to increasing the number of both the locally-polarized areas and the inter-connections between them, leading to more substantial polar fluctuations (see Fig. 2d, e). At the special volume fraction cs, the induced polarized area can nearly fill all the apolar space, thereby maximizing the fluctuation, so as the ε value (Supplementary Fig. 2; Supplementary Discussions). The magnitude of polarity is also important. The stronger the polar order in nPNRs is, the stronger the depolarization field is generated for obtaining spatially more extended polarized areas in apolar regimes (see Fig. 2e, f). Therefore, the variation of the polarity of nPNRs results in a shift of cs because the condition of the complete filling of the induced polarized areas in space changes. Admittedly, our simulations focus on the macroscopic effects of PNRs, while a deeper understanding of their thermodynamic stability and microscopic origin remains an important direction for future theoretical investigations.

Experimental design of nRFE

To experimentally realize the proposed nRFE, which was shown above to be theoretically possible, we attempt to systematically tune the Landau free-energy difference between apolar paraelectric nematic and polar ferroelectric phases by adjusting the solubility of polar and apolar LC molecules. When the difference in either the chemical structure or the molecular polarity is too significant, macro-phase separation occurs quickly. On the contrary, complete miscibility would be reached, eliminating the polar nature under the condition of a slight molecular polarity difference. In the intermediate range, with the appropriate compatibility of paraelectric and ferroelectric molecules, we have a significant opportunity to achieve an nRFE state, which is independent of chemical structure. As representative examples, three paraelectric nematic molecules (Z2, HAE, and E7, see Supplementary Fig. 3a–c for their chemical structures) with varying dipole moments and dielectric permittivities are explored to tune the solubility condition. Z2 and commercial HAE are fluorinated nematic LC mixtures, while E7 is a commercial paraelectric nematic mixture with a cyano end group. The dipole moment values calculated by density functional theory increase sequentially from Z2 (~2 Debye (D)) to HAE (3–4 D) and E7 (~5 D) (Fig. 3a, b). The variance of dipole moment values matches the magnitude sequence of their dielectric permittivity at 1 kHz and 25 °C, i.e., Z2 < HAE < E7 (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Figs. 4–5). On the other hand, RM734, DIO, and a fluorinated NF (denoted as FN) LC (see Supplementary Fig. 3d for the chemical structures) are investigated as the polar molecules. RM734 (11.8 D) is a highly polar aromatic ester with a nitro end group20. Due to the high chemical structure incompatibility between nitro-functionalized aromatic ester and apolar fluorinated molecules, macro-phase separation takes place below 60 °C in the mixtures of Z2/RM734 or HAE/RM734 (Supplementary Figs. 6–7 for PLM texture evolution of Z2/RM734 mixtures; Supplementary Fig. 8 for PLM texture evolution of HAE/RM734 mixtures; Supplementary Fig. 9 for dielectric spectrum of HAE/RM734 mixtures; Supplementary Fig. 10 for P-E loop test of HAE/RM734 mixtures). Another widely studied NF LC, DIO, is a fluorinated aromatic molecule with a relatively low dipole moment, i.e., 9.5 D21. DIO exhibits ideal mixing with all three paraelectric nematic molecules. However, no polarity features are captured in the nematic phase of DIO’s mixtures when the weight fraction of DIO is below 70% in E7/DIO (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12, and 17a for the PLM textures, dielectric spectrum and P-E loop test of E7/DIO mixtures, respectively) and Z2/DIO systems (Supplementary Figs. 13, 14, and 17b for the PLM textures, dielectric spectrum and P-E loop test of Z2/DIO mixtures, respectively), or the polarization value is relatively small (<0.5 μC·cm−2) in HAE/DIO mixture system (Supplementary Figs. 15, 16, and 17c for the PLM textures, dielectric spectrum and P-E loop test of HAE/DIO mixtures, respectively). Apparently, the polarity difference between the DIO molecule and other paraelectric nematic molecules is not significant enough to drive polar domain formation.

Hence, the FN molecule with the highest dipole moment value (13.8 D) and a partially fluorinated chemical structure becomes the optimal choice. It exhibits a direct transition from the isotropic liquid (Iso) to the NF state upon cooling, with the phase sequence Iso-[96 °C]-NF (Fig. 3a, b), characterized by a broad temperature range and excellent low-temperature stability of the NF phase. Figure 3d–f illustrates the matter state evolution of Z2/FN, HAE/FN, and E7/FN mixtures with respect to temperature and composition, respectively. We expect and later confirm that the HAE of intermediate dipole moment value mixed with FN is the most promising system to satisfy the solubility condition for nRFE formation, as in the mean-field simulation. At high concentrations of FN LC (>65% in Z2/FN, >70% in HAE/FN and >80% in E7/FN), a general trend of the matter phase sequence could be identified: Iso-NF (Supplementary Figs. 18–20 for PLM textures of Z2/FN, HAE/FN and E7/FN mixtures, respectively; Supplementary Figs. 21–23 for P–E loops of Z2/FN, HAE/FN and E7/FN mixtures, respectively). The characteristic banded textures of the NF state, with distinct domain walls, were observed using polarized light microscopy (PLM) (Supplementary Figs. 18–20). Decreasing the weight fraction of FN (50–70%), an apparently nematic state emerges between Iso and NF phases in Z2/FN (Fig. 3d) and HAE/FN (Fig. 3e). In strong contrast, the isotropic liquid state dominates the large area of E7/FN diagram with intermediate weight fractions of FN LC (Fig. 3f). It could be rationalized that the complete miscibility of E7 and FN frustrates both of the two driving forces of nematic order formation, i.e., shape anisotropy in E7 and strong dipolar interaction in FN43. When the weight fraction of apolar nematic LC dominates (wt% of FN < 50%), the NF state vanishes at all observation temperatures, and PLM reveals a simple Iso-nematic transition. The PLM textures of every mixing concentration in three systems are shown in Supplementary Figs. 18–20. All the HAE/FN and E7/FN mixtures exhibit excellent stability (>3 days) even at room temperature. However, crystallization usually occurs in Z2/FN mixtures within 5 h at relatively low temperatures (<40 °C), probably due to the poorer miscibility of Z2 and FN at low temperatures resulting from the dramatic molecular polarity difference.

Ultra-high dielectric permittivity with enhanced dispersion

We then investigated the dynamic polarization behavior of these nematic mixtures. A clear feature of ultra-high dielectric permittivity with enhanced dispersion could be identified, giving a strong hint to the expected nRFE state. Specifically, Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figs. 24–25 show the dielectric permittivity as a function of the temperature and frequency in HAE/FN mixtures (the samples denoted as HxFy, where x and y represent the weight fraction of HAE and FN, respectively). Particular attention was paid to the central part of the matter state diagram with FN weight fraction of 30%–70% (Fig. 3e). We observe a dramatic increase of ε following the increased weight fraction of FN from H7F3 to H6F4, H5F5, and H4F6. The maximum ε value at 1 kHz even exceeds 500 in H5F5. Note that only nematic-like defect-free texture was captured by PLM in a parallel-aligned slab at the whole temperature range, and negligible SHG activity could be detected in these nematic states (Supplementary Fig. 25e), indicating that no macroscopic polar domains beyond micrometer size exist. Such a high ε value in H5F5 strongly suggests the existence of polar domains in this unconventional nematic state. The dielectric spectra of H4F6, H5F5, and H6F4 are further resolved into separate diffuse dielectric relaxation profiles at various frequencies (Fig. 4b–d). The unusually broad and rounded maximums of temperature (Tmax) in dielectric permittivity were identified, with strongly frequency-dependent features. In particular, the corresponding dielectric relaxation peaks move toward a lower frequency range with decreasing temperatures. Additionally, with increasing test frequency, the peak values of ε gradually decrease. We also demonstrate the temperature dependence of the imaginary part of dielectric permittivity (ε”) in H5F5 (Supplementary Fig. 26a), and observe a similar strong dielectric dispersion. Another common feature in canonical RFEs is that the Tmax dependence on external field frequency follows the Vogel–Fulcher–Tammann (VFT) law44,45. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 26b and c, a VFT relationship could well describe the Tmax dependence on frequency in our nRFE system, suggesting that the PNRs fluctuate above a static freezing temperature (181.7 K). These merits, including high dielectric permittivity, diffuse phase transition, frequency dispersion dielectric behavior, and the absence of macroscopic polar domains, are precisely the characteristics of RFEs, particularly nRFE in this liquid matter. Further quantitative investigations of the relaxor-like nematic state were conducted to roughly determine the nRFE region in the phase diagram (Supplementary Discussion 3). Burns temperatures (TB) marked by the red dash-dotted line can be determined at the point where derivation from Cuire–Weiss law begins42, which is considered as the onset of local polarization (Fig. 3d, e). The areas below the red dotted lines are thus determined as the phase regions where the relaxor properties of the system are pronounced relative to the non-relaxor N phase (on the top) and pure NF phase (at the right).

A parameter of ΔTmax, defining the frequency-dependent shift in dielectric relaxation, could help analyze the extent of diffusive characteristics in these materials46,47. ΔTmax is closely related to the size dispersion of polar domains. The higher value of ΔTmax corresponds to a more diffusive phase domain and results in a relaxation feature. Here, ∆Tmax = Tmax (100 kHz) – Tmax (500 Hz), where 100 kHz and 500 Hz were chosen as the optimal high and low frequencies. The value of ∆Tmax is approximately 30 K in H5F5. The compositional dependence of ∆Tmax in HAE/FN mixtures is presented in Fig. 4c. The maximum value of ∆Tmax in the nRFE state was found in 30 wt% of FN LC, and decreases with composition variation. Compositions with a lower FN LC fraction produce mostly small PNRs, while increasing the FN fraction would result in the rapid growth of polar domains. When the NF state is reached in compositions with the majority of FN, the phase dispersion behavior is gradually weakened, with ∆Tmax approaching zero (Fig. 4e).

We compared the relaxation frequency and splay elastic constant K11 dependence on temperature of H5F5 with the reported RM734 and DIO. When near the N-NF or N-Ns phase boundary, the relaxation frequencies of RM734 and DIO drop sharply (Supplementary Fig. 27a), due to the pre-transitional polar fluctuations in the N phase near the phase boundary. In contrast, the relaxation frequencies drop uniformly with decreased temperature in H5F5. Regarding the K11, it does not fluctuate significantly throughout the whole temperature range before the nRFE-NF transition in H5F5 (Supplementary Fig. 27b). In contrast, for RM734 and DIO, the elastic constant K11 decreases sharply when near the N-NF or N-NS phase boundary. The absence of a dramatic drop of relaxation frequency or K11 at the whole nRFE temperature range (33–70 °C) manifests that the nRFE state is distinct from the pre-transitional polar fluctuations near the N-NF phase boundary, as in RM734 and DIO48,49. Due to the complexity of the splay softening phenomenon, the clear relation of the K11 parameter to the nRFE state needs to be further confirmed.

This nRFE state was also revealed in the Z2/FN system by dielectric permittivity within the FN weight fraction range of 40%–60% (Supplementary Figs. 29–31), with observation of similar high dielectric constant and frequency dispersion in Z4F6. The piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) was further utilized to analyze the domain structures in NF and nRFE (Supplementary Fig. 32). The pure FN has an approximate single-domain structure (large-sized domains over micrometer scale) (Supplementary Fig. 32a–c), while the nRFE LCs (Z4F6 in Supplementary Fig. 32d–f; H5F5 in Supplementary Fig. 32g–i) show nano-sized domains. The nanoscale domains in H5F5 exhibit a more uniform distribution and smaller size compared to those in Z4F6. According to the PFM phase information (middle array in Supplementary Fig. 32), the FN shows a strong and relatively uniform negative phase signal, indicating a single domain with uniform polarization direction. On the contrary, the piezoelectric response of nRFE (H5F5) is relatively weak and spatially heterogeneous, which may indicate the nanoscale inhomogeneity of composition and polarity. We note that a dispersed polymer network was introduced in these PFM measurements to fix the fluidic LC materials. We thus do not rule out the possibility that the observed heterogeneity could arise from polarity and compositional fluctuations in a conventional paraelectric nematic that are ‘frozen in’ by the photopolymerization process. More advanced characterization methods would be explored to present solid evidence for the existence of PNRs in nRFEs.

Regarding the E7/FN system, the RFE features are not significant. The ε values at 1 kHz are much lower (<80) in all the nematic compositions (Supplementary Figs. 33–34). The ∆Tmax values representing frequency-dependent shift are pretty low (<10, Fig. 4c). Apparently, the nematic E7 with relatively high polarity enables overwhelming miscibility between E7 and FN, suppressing the formation of FN polar nematic nanoregions (Supplementary Fig. 35).

Flattened free-energy landscape: Heterogeneity-driven strong polar fluctuation

To determine whether the strong field-induced polarization observed in experiments is a result of polar fluctuations, as in the typical RFE scenario, we rely on the LGD theory7. This theory is instrumental in reconstructing the free-energy landscapes of the material systems. We compare the experimental P-E curves (Fig. 5a) to the LGD theory, a crucial step in our analysis. As a general approach, the P-E curves in ferroelectric phases can be described by the derivative of a double-well Landau-type potential with two finite equilibrium polarization values, i.e., F∣P∣=−E∣P∣+α∣P∣2+β∣P∣4+γ∣P∣6+… up to six orders for simplicity that satisfies the condition of ∂F/∂∣P∣=0 and ∂2F/∂∣P∣2>0. The well-known negative capacitance, naturally arising from the theoretical S-shaped P-E curve, is not reflected in the experimental P-E curves due to the unstable transient states caused by the immediate screening of charges5,7. Therefore, we can use the LGD theoretical calculation to fit the envelope of the experimental P-E curves. For the paraelectric and RFE states, a single-well Landau-type potential centered at ∣P∣=0 should be used instead to fit the experimental data. According to the flatness of the free-energy bottom around ∣P∣=0, the degree of fluctuation can be evaluated quantitatively (Supplementary Figs. 36 and 37 for the calculated P-E hysteresis loops and reconstructed free-energy landscapes of four basic LC materials, respectively; Supplementary Figs. 38 and 39 for HAE/FN mixtures; Supplementary Figs. 40 and 41 for Z2/FN mixtures; Supplementary Figs. 42 and 43 for E7/FN mixtures).

a The calculated P–E lines (dotted lines) for FN (50 °C), H5F5 (75 °C), H6F4 (75 °C), and HAE (30 °C) with the experimental data (solid lines). b The reconstructed free-energy landscapes for HAE, H6F4, H5F5, and FN systems. c The predicted effective dielectric permittivity ε, calculated from the reconstructed free-energy curves, as a function of temperature for numerous HAE/FN systems.

In Fig. 5a–c, we take HAE/FN systems as representative examples for the liquid-matter RFE demonstration. For the neat apolar nematic HAE, the P-E curve is purely paraelectric in the whole temperature range of the nematic phase over about 100 K (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 35b), characterized by a sharp single-well potential (Fig. 5b). In such a state, the apolar state, i.e., P=0, is very stable even under thermal or external perturbation. With increasing the doping of highly polar FN molecules, while the nematic phase remains optically the same as the apolar nematic phase, the field-induced polarization continuously increases. The variation rate of the induced polarization as a function of the electric field significantly increases upon increasing the ratio of FN molecules (Fig. 5a), corresponding to a more flattened single-potential well in the reconstructed free-energy landscape (Fig. 5b). These results confirm that the size of PNRs is below the optical resolution, and their high performance at low electric fields, below 50 MV·m−1 is comparable to that in solid-state RFEs9,33,50. The reconstructed free-energy landscapes confirm a continuous flattening of the free-energy bottom around ∣P∣=0 (Fig. 5b) with increasing the doping ratio of FN molecules (Supplementary Fig. 38), corresponding to significantly-enhanced polar fluctuation51. When the doping of FN molecules exceeds 70%, however, the system tends to form a bulk NF ferroelectric, and only a small window of an nRFE-like state is present in the vicinity of the N-NF transition. Figure 5b shows the temperature variation of the free-energy landscape in the pure FN system. Within about a 5 K temperature window (105–100 °C), a flattened free-energy bottom but with a much narrower distribution of polar fluctuations compared to those of H6F4 and H5F5 is observed. Then, the systems jump into the NF state with a double-well potential.

More quantitatively, the polar fluctuation is physically synonymous with the dielectric permittivity that obeys ε−1=ε0∂2E∂P2P→0. Figure 5c shows the temperature dependencies of the effective dielectric permittivity for HAE/FN systems, calculated from the reconstructed free-energy curves. As expected from the discussions above, the dielectric performance peaks at 40–50 wt% of FN molecules (i.e., H4F6 and H5F5). At lower FN concentrations (<40 wt%), fewer nPNRs form, resulting in a smaller coverage of polarized regions in an apolar background. This limits collective polarization fluctuations, resulting in a smaller ε. In contrast, at higher FN concentrations (>50 wt%), the polarization gradient energy becomes more prominent because the polarized regions have a higher average polarization strength, which suppresses collective polarization fluctuations, i.e., smaller ε. Therefore, the maximum of ε requires an intermediate FN concentration.

It is worth noting that the nRFE demonstrates two regimes in terms of the electric-field response depending on temperature. At high temperatures, the nRFE state is stable enough even under high electric fields. At low temperatures, especially close to the nRFE-NF transition, the nRFE shows a field-induced transition to a ferroelectric state. For instance, the RFE behavior can be observed over a broad temperature range (33–70 °C) in H5F5 when the applied electric field is relatively low, as indicated by the dielectric spectrum. However, the linearly-induced polarization under the electric field observed in the P-E curve was only observed at temperature ranges of 60–70 °C at 1 kHz (Supplementary Fig. 22e), followed by the appearance of a hysteresis resembling a ferroelectric phase. As the test frequency decreases, the onset temperatures exhibiting apparent hysteresis decrease (Supplementary Figs. 22e, 44, and 45), probably related to polarization relaxation dynamics. Relatively large hysteresis starts to occur at 60 °C, 50 °C, and 45 °C at 1 kHz (Supplementary Fig. 22e), 500 Hz (Supplementary Fig. 44), and 200 Hz (Supplementary Fig. 45), respectively. This type of field-induced RFE- ferroelectric like phase transition is widely reported in other inorganic ceramic systems as a relaxor is considered as a precursor to a ferroelectric phase52–54. To further understand the phase behavior under strong electric field, we have carried out the field-dependent SHG measurement for H5F5 (Supplementary Fig. 46). There is no SHG signal at any of the test temperatures for H5F5 up to 5 V·μm−1. The applied electric field covers the whole range in the P-E loop measurement. The absence of a field-induced increase of SHG means that no macroscopic ferroelectric order, at least over a range of the micrometer scale, is developed. The field-induced ferroelectric domains are small enough (fluctuating spatiotemporally) compared to the optical wavelength and no SHG is generable. Another possible, although less likely, scenario for the absence of SHG may be that nPNRs, which are several hundred nanometers in size but less than the optical length because they are invisible under microscopes, exhibit randomly distributed up or down polarization. This situation would result in a destructive interference of the SHG signals from individual nPNRs, even if individual nPNRs are SHG active. We have also attempted to improve the resistance of nRFE to strong electric field by adding 10% AUUQU-4-N55 to the FN molecule (named as FN’ as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3e), which causes the right shift of nRFE-NF or N-NF phase boundary in the phase diagram of HAE/FN’ (Supplementary Fig. 47) comparing with the original HAE/FN system (Fig. 3e). The temperature dependence of P-E loops of H5F’5 (mixture of 50 wt% HAE and 50 wt% FN’) recorded at 200 Hz is shown in Supplementary Fig. 48. As expected, this H5F’5 exhibits improved resistance to strong electric field and possess wider temperature ranges (35-70 °C) of nRFE state with very low hysteresis. The polarization value could reach 2 μC·cm−2 under an electric field of 5 V·μm−1 at 35 °C while maintaining a narrow hysteresis loop. The maximum ε value at 1 kHz even exceeds 200 in H5F’5 at this temperature (Supplementary Fig. 47).

Summary and outlook

In this work, we discover a novel class of liquid-matter nRFE by incorporating PNRs into apolar nematic backgrounds, and provide a generic design and fabrication principle. Through a combination of theoretical modeling and experimental validation, we demonstrated the emergence of relaxor behavior in nematic systems, with clear signatures of high dielectric permittivity, intense field-induced polarization, and apparent frequency dispersion behavior. The Landau-Ginzburg-Devonshire theory was applied to reconstruct free-energy landscapes, revealing a shift from double-well to flattened energy profiles, characteristic of fluctuating polar domains in nRFE systems. The study highlights the potential of tuning molecular polarity and solubility to achieve these unique ferroelectric states in liquid environments, providing new pathways for designing flexible, fluidic materials with ferroelectric properties. This work lays the foundation for advancing the development of liquid-matter ferroelectrics and broadening their technological relevance.

Methods

Sample mixing procedure

The mixture materials are prepared by mixing the commercial or homemade apolar nematic LCs (Z2, HAE and E7) independently with a ferroelectric nematic LC (FN: Iso-[86 °C]-NF) by weight, the steps were as follows: the samples were proportionally weighed and completely dissolved with appropriate chloroform, after 1 min of oscillation, 10 min of ultrasound, the mixtures were put into a blast drying oven and dried at 60 °C. The molecular structures and details are shown in the Materials section in Supplementary Information.

Sample preparation in the LC cell

The mixture of materials is filled into homemade LC cells. The cell thickness is controlled by using spacer particles or polymeric films. The surface of the glasses is spin-coated with a planar aligner, KPI-3000 (Shenzhen Haihao Technology Co. Ltd).

PLM observation

The textures of samples were observed by polarizing light microscope OLYMPUS BX-51. All samples were placed on a hot stage INSTEC MK700 and photographed using a Nikon D5300 camera.

Dielectric spectroscopy

Dielectric spectroscopy was conducted in ITO-homeotropic (Supplementary Figs. 49 and 50) LC cells with an electrode area of 1 cm2 and a cell thickness of 20 μm, applying an electric field perpendicular to the plane of the ITO glass. The voltage applied to the ITO cell is 50 mV. Dielectric spectroscopy was obtained by using an LCR meter (4284 A, Agilent). The collection of the frequency and temperature sweeping data was automated by homemade software written in LabVIEW.

P–E hysteresis loop measurement

The polarization measurements were conducted using a ferroelectric property analyzer (TOYO FCE10-S, Japan) with a Sawyer-Tower circuit. Samples were injected into ITO cells with a cell thickness of 20 μm, without an alignment layer, using a triangular wave.

SHG measurements

A pulsed laser (MPL-III-1064-20 μJ, Changchun New Industries Optoelectronics Tech. Co., Ltd; wavelength 1064 nm, energy 20 μJ, repetition rate 100 Hz) is used as the light source for SHG. Either a λ/2 or a λ/4 plate adjusts the polarization state of the fundamental light before entering the samples. The incident light is kept as a parallel beam with a diameter of 1 mm. The transmitted SH light is collected by a photomultiplier tube (DH-PMT-D100V, Daheng New Epoch Technology, Inc.). The temperature dependence of the SH signal is recorded using a homemade LabVIEW program, which enables control of the temperature (temperature fluctuation less than 0.05 °C) and logging of the SH signal at desired temperature intervals.

Sample preparation in PFM

Ten percent by weight of commercially available LC monomer RM257 and photoinitiator were added to the LCs (FN, Z4F6, H5F5, and HAE), completely dissolved with appropriate chloroform, after 1 min of oscillation, 10 min of ultrasound, the mixtures were put into a blast drying oven and dried at 60 °C. After drying for 24 h, the mixture was poured into a homemade cell with a 5 μm thick polymer film obtained through a photopolymerization reaction under a 365 nm ultraviolet lamp.

PFM measurement

PFM measurements were performed using a commercial scanning probe microscope (MFP-3D, Asylum Research) equipped with conductive Pt/Ir-coated silicon cantilevers (Arrow-CONTPt, NanoWorld).
