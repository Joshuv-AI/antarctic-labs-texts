Genome-wide comparison of traditional and commercial Mehraban sheep populations reveals differences in genetic structure, linkage disequilibrium, and inbreeding patterns
Authors: Leyla Eskandari, Ahmad Ahmadi, Reza Talebi, Masoumeh Naserkheil, Seyyedeh Azadeh Javadi Taklimi, Fatemeh Zare, Tomasz Szmatoła, Masoud Shirali, Mohammad Reza Ghaffari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the genetic architecture of sheep under different breeding systems is crucial for enhancing productivity and maintaining adaptive capacity. We analyzed 145 Mehraban sheep from commercial (n = 97) and traditional (n = 48) populations utilizing the OvineSNP50 BeadChip array. The analysis revealed that the linkage disequilibrium (LD) was numerically higher on average in the traditional population at both short and long distances (mean r² = 0.249 at < 10 kb; 0.037 at 5 Mb) in comparison to the commercial population (mean r² = 0.220 and 0.027, respectively), suggesting a smaller historical effective population size (Ne). Principal component and admixture analyses validated strong genetic difference, with the commercial population showing moderate admixture from traditional lines. Principal component and admixture analysis confirmed significant genetic difference, with the commercial population exhibiting considerable admixture from traditional lines. Analysis of runs of homozygosity (ROH) revealed more numerous and longer ROH segments in commercial animals (total ROH ~ 43,768 Mb, FROH = 0.183 ± 0.052) compared to traditional population (11,741 Mb, FROH = 0.139 ± 0.066), potentially indicating a potential increase in inbreeding and prolonged artificial selection. ROH islands contained 1,586 genes in commercial population and 797 genes in traditional population, with quantitative trait loci (QTL) for muscle development, carcass yield, and milk production enriched in commercial population, whereas traditional population harbored QTL for fertility, immune response, and wool characteristics. Functional analysis further identified Hippo signaling, TCA cycle, and growth-related pathways in commercial population, as well as immune (IL-17, TNF), reproductive, and DNA repair pathways in traditional population. These findings demonstrate how breeding systems shape genome-wide diversity, inbreeding, and functionally relevant loci, highlighting the importance of traditional populations as reservoirs of adaptive alleles for sustainable improvement of commercial flocks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13177-w.

Introduction

Native small ruminant breeds are essential for food security, cultural heritage, and adaptation to diverse and often harsh environment. Sheep (Ovis aries) were domesticated from the Asiatic mouflon (Ovis orientalis) approximately 10,500 years ago in the Middle East, specifically southeastern Anatolia and the Iranian Zagros [1]. Currently, Iran harbors around 27 indigenous sheep breeds that are suitably adapted to local agro-ecological conditions, however, numerous breeds exhibit relatively low productivity in terms of fertility, growth rate, carcass quality, and feed efficiency [2, 3].

Assessing genetic diversity and population structure is crucial for designing sustainable breeding initiatives and preserving native genetic resources. Although pedigree records have traditionally been used to estimate genetic variation, their reliability is often limited by incomplete recording, particularly in small ruminant systems relying on natural mating [4]. Molecular markers, particularly single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), offer a robust alternative by enabling genome-wide assessment of diversity, inbreeding, and demographic history [5]. High-throughput SNP arrays, such as the ovine 50 K BeadChip, have enabled standardized large-scale genotyping and comparative analyses across various studies. Notably, the Sheep HapMap project, which includes approximately 3,000 animals from 74 breeds globally, has yielded valuable insights into global patterns of genetic diversity and adaptation [6].

Understanding genome-wide patterns of linkage disequilibrium (LD) is particularly important for genomic prediction, genome-wide association studies (GWAS), and the conservation of animal genetic resources. The extent and decay of LD influence the power and transferability of genomic selection across populations [7]. In sheep, pedigree information is often incomplete due to multi-sire natural mating systems [8], making genomic approaches especially valuable. Runs of homozygosity (ROH), defined as long homozygous segments inherited from common ancestors, provide direct insight into genomic inbreeding through the FROH coefficient, independent of pedigree data [9–11]. Moreover, shared ROH regions (ROH islands) may signify genomic regions under selection subject to adaptation or economically important traits [12–14]. The distribution patterns of ROH length can also be utilized to infer historical effective population size (Ne), offering insight into population history and breeding practices [15].

Prior genomic investigations on Iranian sheep have shown that, despite apparent genetic similarity among breeds, substantial within-breed variation exists, reflecting hidden reservoirs of adaptive potential [16–18]. However, differences in ecological conditions, production systems, and breeding histories among Iranian indigenous sheep breeds are expected to result in distinct ROH architectures and selection patterns [14–16, 19]. This within-breed diversity is particularly important in Iran, where indigenous sheep populations are exposed to diverse climatic and management conditions.

This study investigates the genomic structure of Mehraban sheep reared under two contrasting breeding systems: traditional village-based management and commercial breeding. Using medium density ovine SNP chip data, we analyzed LD decay, historical Ne, ROH patterns, and FROH to evaluate how breeding practices impact genetic diversity, inbreeding levels, and population structure. The identification of ROH islands further enables the detection of putative genomic regions under selection that may be relevant to both productivity and environmental adaptation [14]. These findings aim to help the development of breeding strategies that enhance performance while preserving the adaptive capacity of this culturally and economically important sheep population.

Materials and methods

Animal management, sample collection, and DNA extraction

This study included 145 Mehraban lambs (1–2 years old, with a balanced sex ratio: 50% male and 50% female) from two distinct breeding systems in Hamedan Province, Iran: a commercial nucleus farm in Bahar city and multiple traditional village-based flocks in Nahavand County. All sampling was conducted within a single year to avoid temporal bias. A total of 97 lambs were sampled from the commercial farm (Latitude: 34°54’25.92” N, Longitude: 48°26’29.04” E), which operates as a closed nucleus flock under intensive management with ~ 10 breeding rams and ~ 500 ewes. Rams are selected for growth-related traits, while ewes are primarily retained based on survival, general health, and maternal performance; weak lambs are routinely removed during early rearing. Mating is natural, and artificial insemination is not used. Pedigree records are incomplete, and a higher degree of relatedness (e.g., half-sibs or full-sibs) cannot be excluded due to the limited number of breeding rams (~ 10). In contrast, 48 lambs were obtained from four distinct traditional village-based flocks in Nahavand County (Latitude: 34°11’2.40” N, Longitude: 48°22’12.59” E), with approximately 10–12 lambs sampled per flock. These flocks are managed under semi-intensive conditions with natural mating, no systematic performance recording, and no structured selection or culling strategy. Pedigree information is unavailable. Lamb survival and reproductive success are largely influenced by environmental pressures and natural selection, particularly during the pre-weaning period. The limited relatedness among animals across these four independent flocks minimizes the risk of cryptic relatedness biasing the genomic analyses.

In both systems, blood samples (1 mL per lamb) were collected aseptically from the jugular vein into EDTA tubes. Genomic DNA was extracted following the protocol described by Talebi et al. [20], ensuring consistency in DNA quality for downstream genomic analyses. DNA purity checked by OD260/OD280 ratio using a Nanodrop and quality was assessed by gel electrophoresis using a 1% TAE agarose gel.

Genotyping, data preparation and filtration

Genotyping was performed using the OvineSNP50 BeadChip array (Illumina, USA), which carries 54,241 SNPs, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Raw intensity data were converted to genotypes with GenomeStudio (Genotyping Module v1.9.4). Quality control and filtering were conducted in PLINK v1.90 [21], and SNPs were removed sequentially based on: non-autosomal location (3,424 SNPs), call rate < 95% (800 SNPs), minor allele frequency < 0.02 (8311 SNPs; applied to exclude rare variants with low statistical power and higher susceptibility to genotyping error), and deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium at p < 10⁻⁶ (228 SNPs). After filtering steps, 41,478 autosomal SNPs were retained for downstream analysis. Missing genotypes were not imputed; the call-rate filter (> 95%) was instead applied at both the SNP and the individual level so that all retained variants and animals exceeded this threshold, and PLINK’s pairwise-complete handling was used in subsequent analyses. Remaining SNPs were remapped to the Ovine reference genome (OAR_v4.0) using positional data from the Sheep HapMap project.

To evaluate LD decay with distance, SNP pairs were grouped into 10 physical distance bins: 0–20 kb, 20–40 kb, 40–60 kb, 60–100 kb, 100–200 kb, 200–500 kb, 500–1000 kb, 1000–2000 kb, 2000–4000 kb, and 4000–6000 kb. The average r² value within each bin was then computed separately for the studied populations. Detailed statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

where E(r²) is the mean LD between SNP pairs and c is the recombination fraction between loci. The recombination rate was approximated from physical distance, assuming 1 cM per Mb (1 × 10⁻⁸ per bp). Population structure and relatedness analysis

Population structure was investigated using ADMIXTURE. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to visualize major axes of genomic variation and broad genetic differentiation among individuals, and not to infer genetic relatedness. PCA was carried out in PLINK v1.9 [21], and the resulting eigenvectors were visualized in R with ggplot2 and scatterplot3d [24]. ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 [25] was used to estimate ancestry proportions under a maximum-likelihood framework, testing K values from 2 to 6, with the optimal model determined by cross-validation error. To ensure consistent clustering across runs, outputs were aligned and averaged using CLUMPP v1.1.1 [26]. This method, conceptually similar to STRUCTURE [27], provides insights into genetic substructure and shared ancestry among populations.

Diversity metrics and Wright’s inbreeding coefficient

Genetic diversity was assessed by calculating observed heterozygosity (HO) and expected heterozygosity (HE) in PLINK v1.9 [21]. The proportion of polymorphic markers (PN) was estimated as the average fraction of non-missing SNPs per individual within each population. Runs of homozygosity (ROH) analysis

Runs of homozygosity (ROH) were detected using the detectRUNS package in R, applying the consecutive-SNP approach: a minimum of 20 consecutive homozygous SNPs, a maximum gap of 1 Mb between adjacent SNPs, and a minimum ROH length of 500 kb; up to one opposite homozygote and one missing genotype were allowed per run. Given the average inter-marker spacing of ~ 50–60 kb on the OvineSNP50 BeadChip, the 20-SNP rule alone implies a typical lower bound of ~ 0.95–1.1 Mb in regions of average density, so the 500 kb cut-off acts as a backstop only in locally denser segments rather than as the primary constraint (validated by comparing ROH proportions in dense vs. sparse regions; Figure S1). We recognize that a minimum 1 Mb minimum is the predominant selection in published sheep ROH analyses utilizing this chip. Nevertheless, minimum ROH lengths at or below 0.5 Mb on the OvineSNP50 have been applied employed in sheep using the same sliding-window framework, including 0.5 Mb in nine Polish sheep breeds [29] and 250 kb in five French dairy sheep breeds and subpopulations [30].

We retained the 0.5 Mb cut-off because it allows detection of short, dense ROHs that are informative for the comparative interpretation between the two management systems studied here. Short ROHs reflect heterogeneous breeding histories and management practices rather than recent consanguinity, and excluding them would discard population-distinguishing signal. Following Lencz et al. [31] and Purfield et al. [32], the strict α = 0.05 minimum-SNP rule for our marker density and sample size would be ~ 41 SNPs; our 20-SNP rule is more permissive, but is paired with the 0.5 Mb backstop and with strictly comparative (rather than absolute) interpretation of ROH metrics.

Up to one opposite homozygote and one missing genotype were allowed per run. Identified ROHs were grouped into five length classes (0.5–2 Mb, 2–4 Mb, 4–8 Mb, 8–16 Mb, and > 16 Mb) using a custom R script. (2008) methodology. To identify genomic regions enriched for homozygosity, the frequency of each SNP appearing in ROHs was calculated across individuals. SNPs within the top 1% of the ROH frequency distribution were used to identify genomic regions with strong enrichment of homozygosity. Contiguous SNPs shared across individuals were defined as ROH islands and used for exploratory analyses. These regions may reflect the combined effects of selection pressure and demographic history and are intended to highlight candidate regions rather than provide definitive evidence of selection [14, 29, 33].

Annotation of genetic variants and enrichment analysis

Genetic variants were annotated with SnpEff v3.4 [34] and classified into functional categories such as intergenic regions, introns, missense and synonymous mutations, stop gain/loss variants, and regions within 5 kb upstream or downstream of genes. For clarity, variants in upstream/downstream regions and UTRs were grouped into broader categories. A custom AWK script was then used to compare these regions with the sheep QTL database (release 53; https://www.animalgenome.org/cgi-bin/QTLdb/OA/index). Gene ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analyses were performed using KOBAS-i [35] with annotations from the KEGG database.

Results and discussion

Overview of genotyping outcomes

Out of the 54,241 SNPs on the OvineSNP50 BeadChip, 3,424 mapped to non-autosomal chromosomes and subsequently excluded; an additional 9,339 SNPs were removed by the call-rate, minor-allele-frequency and Hardy–Weinberg filters. Following filtration, 41,478 autosomal SNPs across 145 individuals were retained for population genomic analyses. The overall per-individual genotyping rate of retained SNPs was 98.91%.

Linkage disequilibrium and effective population size

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) was estimated using r² for commercial and traditional populations. At distances up to 10 kb, the mean r² between adjacent SNPs was higher in the traditional population (0.249) than in the commercial population (0.220). The traditional population consistently retained higher LD across longer distances, with mean r² values of 0.055 at 350 kb and 0.037 at 5 Mb, in contrast to 0.032 and 0.027 in the commercial population, respectively. In both populations, LD decayed rapidly with increasing marker distance, but the decay rate was faster in the commercial group, where r² dropped below 0.03 at ~ 500 kb, whereas the traditional group maintained r² > 0.04 beyond 3 Mb (Fig. 1 and Table S1). These observed patterns align with the hypothesis that smaller effective population sizes and historical bottlenecks maintain extended LD, whereas larger Ne and recombination accelerate LD breakdown [36, 37].

Effective population size (Ne) estimates from LD also revealed contrasting demographic trajectories. About 2,000 generations ago, Ne was ~ 5,889 in the commercial group and ~ 4,807 in the traditional group (Fig. 2 and Table S2). Both populations showed gradual declines over time, however reductions intensified in recent generations. At ~ 300 generations ago, Ne was ~ 3,412 in the commercial group and ~ 2,069 in the traditional group. In the latter generations, Ne dropped to ~ 447 in commercial and ~ 264 in traditional populations, highlighting a sharper contraction in the latter. The higher historical Ne in commercial groups likely reflects structured breeding programs and greater genetic diversity, whereas the smaller and declining Ne in traditional populations indicates vulnerability to inbreeding and drift, consistent with other reports in livestock under restricted breeding [5, 23]. LD and LD-based Ne estimates are influenced by demographic history, sample size, population structure, and marker density, and assume a uniform recombination rate (1 cM/Mb) [38, 39]. Accordingly, Ne estimates reflect historical effective population size under these simplifying assumptions, rather than census size or exact demographic history, and are most informative for comparative interpretation between populations, while recognizing that differences in sample size or relatedness structure may also contribute to observed LD patterns.

The combined LD and Ne results confirm that breeding history has shaped genomic architecture in these populations. Commercial animals are characterized by rapid LD decay and larger historical Ne, while traditional animals show extended LD and sharper recent reductions in Ne. From an applied perspective, extended LD in traditional populations suggests that fewer markers may be sufficient for GWAS, while higher marker density is required in commercial populations for accurate genomic selection [7, 40]. Conservation strategies are particularly important in traditional populations to mitigate genetic erosion and preserve long-term adaptive potential.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA was used to assess the genetic structure of traditional and commercial Mehraban sheep populations. The first principal component (PC1) explained 35.2% of the total genetic variation, while the second component (PC2) accounted for 12.7% (Fig. 3). Traditional population (blue) clustered tightly on the left side of the plot, whereas commercial population (red) formed a distinct cluster on the right, indicating strong genetic differentiation. Similar to earlier studies, PCA proved effective in detecting population stratification within breeds, with PC1 reflecting broad divergence associated with management history and PC2 capturing finer-scale variation [41–43]. These results demonstrate the usefulness of PCA in unraveling population structure and guiding breeding strategies in Mehraban sheep. The separation observed between traditional and commercial groups likely reflects differences in breeding management, selection intensity, and gene flow rather than divergence between distinct breeds.

Population admixture

Admixture analysis was conducted on 145 Mehraban sheep to evaluate population structure. The model with K = 2 ancestral clusters produced the lowest cross-validation (CV) error (CV = 0.31), providing the optimal fit for our data. Figure 4 illustrates that individuals were predominantly assigned to two distinct clusters corresponding to the traditional (Cluster 1, red) and commercial (Cluster 2, blue) populations. Individuals are ordered along the x-axis according to their population origin, with commercial animals positioned on the left and traditional animals on the right, to facilitate visual comparison. Traditional population displayed a high proportion of ancestry from Cluster 1, with minimal contribution from Cluster 2, indicating genetic homogeneity within this group. In contrast, commercial population was predominantly assigned to Cluster 2, while several individuals exhibited varying levels of admixture with Cluster 1. At K = 3 and K = 4, no additional substructure was detected within either population, confirming genetic homogeneity within the traditional and commercial groups. This evident separation between traditional and commercial populations aligns with findings in other Iranian sheep breeds, where indigenous and commercial lines typically form distinct clusters at K = 2 [19, 44]. The strong cohesion of the traditional group underscores the genetic integrity of indigenous Mehraban sheep, while the admixture observed in commercial animals likely reflects crossbreeding strategies intended to enhance productivity, similar to patterns reported for Moghani sheep crossbreds [3, 45].

Observed heterozygosity (HO) was marginally higher in traditional population (0.357 ± 0.01) relative to the commercial population (0.352 ± 0.01), while expected heterozygosity (HE) was identical in both populations (0.360 ± 0.00), indicating a moderate and consistent level of genetic diversity. The polymorphic marker ratio (%PN), representing the proportion of polymorphic markers in each population, was slightly higher in the commercial population (1.01 ± 0.02) than in the traditional population (1.00 ± 0.02), indicating broadly similar levels of marker polymorphism between groups. However, values near 1 in both populations imply that Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium is largely maintained. Wright’s inbreeding coefficient (Fis) was low in both groups, with a slightly higher mean Fis in commercial population (0.022) compared to traditional population (0.008), suggesting a slightly greater heterozygote deficiency in the commercial population. Maximum Fis was also higher in commercial population (0.16 vs. 0.132), though both populations included individuals with negative Fis values, indicating an excess of heterozygosity in some individuals (min Fis: −0.034 in traditional; −0.035 in commercial). These patterns are in line with pedigree-based studies of Iranian sheep. For instance, Yavarifard et al. [46] reported an average inbreeding coefficient of 1.69% in Mehraban sheep, with effective population sizes rising from 363 to 5740 across breeding periods, suggesting that both inbreeding and demographic expansion shaped genetic structure. Previous studies have reported associations between inbreeding and growth traits in sheep; however [45–47], such relationships were not evaluated in the present study.

In our study, a slightly higher Fis observed in commercial sheep population (Table 1) likely reflects differences in breeding structure and population management compared with the traditional system. Our results also parallel genome-wide studies of Iranian local breeds, which consistently report moderate heterozygosity and relatively low inbreeding compared to intensively selected commercial breeds. For example, Barani et al. [8] showed that the Zel breed had the highest heterozygosity and effective population size among three Iranian breeds, while Baluchi sheep displayed elevated inbreeding due to stronger artificial selection pressures. In line with these studies, our observed heterozygosity values are comparable to or slightly higher than those reported for Iranian sheep by Eydivandi et al. [16] and Talebi et al. [14], who reported that pure Moghani sheep had significantly higher HO and lower Fis compared to crossbred populations such as Booroola×Moghani or Texel×Moghani crosses. Our results indicated that traditional population maintain a more balanced genetic structure, whereas commercial population, shaped by selection and admixture, show higher polymorphism but greater risk of heterozygote deficiencies. This highlights the need for conservation-focused breeding programs to preserve the genetic integrity of traditional Iranian sheep while managing gene flow from commercial breeds to enhance productivity without eroding long-term diversity.

Runs of homozygosity (ROH)

To explore the inbreeding patterns and demographic history of the sheep populations, we analyzed the ROH profiles of traditional (n = 48) and commercial (n = 97) groups. The number and length of ROH segments differed markedly between the two groups (Fig. 5A and B). The average (± SD) number and cumulative length of ROH per individual across five ROH length classes (0.5–2, 2–4, 4–8, 8–16, and > 16 Mb) are summarized in Table S4 for both populations. This table reports both individual- and population-level ROH metrics, including class-specific ROH length and the corresponding FROH values, allowing detailed comparison of ROH burden and distribution between populations. Traditional individuals typically exhibited a higher number of shorter ROH segments, clustered at lower total and mean ROH lengths. In contrast, commercial individuals displayed both longer and more numerous ROH segments. Figure 5A shows that many commercial individuals exceed 75 ROH segments with total ROH lengths surpassing 200 Mbps, whereas most traditional individuals remain below these thresholds. Similarly, Fig. 5B reveals that commercial individuals tend to have higher mean ROH lengths, suggesting more recent or intense inbreeding. These patterns suggest different breeding practices. Traditional flocks may preserve greater genetic diversity through less controlled mating systems, leading to older and more fragmented ROH. Conversely, commercial population, often subjected to intensive directional selection and managed within closed breeding structures show evidence of more recent inbreeding and reduced effective population sizes, reflected in both ROH count and length. Similar contrasts between local and intensively managed breeds have been observed in Chinese sheep [48], Iranian fat-tailed populations [14, 15, 44], and European commercial breeds [33], supporting the interpretation that ROH length distributions help distinguish ancient from recent inbreeding.

In agreement with our results, several studies have shown that short ROH segments are generally the most prevalent in sheep populations. For example, Purfield et al. [33] reported that most ROH detected in six commercial meat breeds were shorter than 10 Mb, although their number and frequency varied substantially among breeds. Similarly, Signer-Hasler et al. [49] identified 17,719 ROH in eight Swiss local breeds, with segments between 1 and 10 Mb being the most common length class. A large-scale analysis of 68 global sheep populations by Nosrati et al. [50] also reported relatively low genomic inbreeding (mean FROH ≈ 0.09) and substantial variation in ROH abundance among breeds. More recently, Ma et al. [51] showed that many European breeds tend to have higher numbers and longer total lengths of ROH than Asian breeds, reflecting differences in breeding intensity and effective population size. Together, these studies support the interpretation that variation in ROH number and length primarily reflects breeding management, demographic history, and selection intensity.

Table 2 and Table S4 present the distribution of ROH lengths across five size categories in traditional and commercial Mehraban sheep. The total length of ROH segments was markedly higher in the commercial group (43,767.83 Mb) compared to the traditional group (11,741.43 Mb), indicating a higher genome-wide homozygosity in the former. Bar plots of ROH class frequencies (Fig. 6) confirm that the majority of ROH segments in both groups fall within the shortest class (0.5–2 Mb), comprising approximately 77% in traditional and 69% in commercial population, a pattern consistent with observations in multiple sheep populations worldwide where short ROH dominate the genomic landscape. However, the commercial population showed a higher proportion of ROH segments in longer length categories, particularly in the > 16 Mb class. Supporting this, Fig. 6 demonstrates a clear trend of increasing average ROH length with class size, with commercial population showing consistently greater mean values, especially in the upper length classes. Similarly, Fig. 7 illustrates that the mean ROH lengths across all classes were consistently higher in commercial population than in traditional population. This pattern is further highlighted in Fig. 7, which compares the mean ROH lengths between populations within each class. Notably, even in the shortest class (0.5–2 Mb), commercial individuals had longer ROH segments on average, and the gap widened with increasing ROH size class. Because commercial animals were sampled from a single closed nucleus flock, relatedness may inflate ROH measures, and unequal group sizes may affect population summaries. Therefore, ROH estimates are interpreted comparatively between populations rather than as absolute values.

Long ROHs are widely recognized as indicators of recent inbreeding due to shared haplotypes inherited from close ancestors [10, 13, 33]. Comparable enrichment of long tracts has been reported in Texel- and Booroola-cross Moghani lambs [14] and in European commercial breeds subject to intensive sire use and strong directional selection [33]. The extended homozygosity in commercial Mehraban sheep reflects higher genomic inbreeding and long-term breeding practices, including demographic constraints, whereas the fragmented ROH patterns in traditional flocks reflect older inbreeding events, similar to other native breeds.

Genomic inbreeding coefficient estimated from ROH (FROH)

The genomic inbreeding coefficient (FROH), calculated across all ROH segments, revealed clear differences between traditional and commercial Mehraban sheep, as summarized in Table 3 and Table S4. FROH was higher in the commercial group (0.183 ± 0.052) than in the traditional one (0.139 ± 0.066), indicating a greater total length of ROH per individual in the commercial group. To further assess differences in ROH metrics between the two rearing systems, a pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests was performed. All p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method, with statistical significance defined as FDR < 0.05. The results confirmed that both total ROH length and FROH were significantly higher in the commercial population compared to the traditional population (p < 0.001; Figure S2). This pattern is also visible in the violin plot (Fig. 8). Most traditional individuals had low FROH values, with a few outliers showing relatively high inbreeding. In contrast, commercial individuals displayed FROH values spanning a broader range, with many individuals in the low-to-medium range and several with high FROH, resulting in a right-shifted distribution that indicates a greater extent and heterogeneity of ROH within individuals in the commercial population, rather than shared homozygous regions across animals. When comparing ROH length classes in Table 3, the longest category (> 16 Mb), typically reflecting more recent inbreeding events, was very similar between groups (0.0194 ± 0.0100 vs. 0.0208 ± 0.0116), indicating that differences in FROH are primarily driven by short and medium ROHs, which reflect heterogeneous breeding histories and management practices rather than recent consanguinity. At the chromosome level, FROH was consistently higher in the commercial group, with the largest differences on chromosomes 11, 13, 18, 25, and 26 (Figs. 9A, B). Boxplots (Figs. 9C, D) further illustrate that commercial animals not only carry higher FROH but also show greater variation and more outliers. As discussed for LD and ROH analyses, FROH estimates should also be interpreted comparatively between populations, as relatedness structure and demographic history may influence absolute values. Traditional Mehraban sheep, however, maintain lower and more uniform levels of autozygosity, reflecting weaker selection and a larger effective population size. Using higher-density SNP arrays could provide more detailed information on shorter ROH segments, potentially revealing additional insights into more ancient inbreeding patterns.

These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting higher FROH values and longer ROHs in breeds subjected to intensive artificial selection and demographic contraction. For example, Barani et al. [8] observed elevated FROH and longer ROHs in Baluchi sheep compared with other Iranian sheep breeds analyzed in the same study, while populations with larger Ne tended to show lower values. Similarly, Liu et al. [48] reported that Chinese Large-tailed Han sheep exhibited the highest FROH and a greater proportion of long ROHs (> 5 Mb) among the Chinese sheep populations examined, reflecting inbreeding associated withbreed development and management practices. In contrast, native breeds under weaker selection pressure generally show lower genomic inbreeding. For instance, Kizilaslan et al. [52] reported very low FROH in Akkaraman (~ 0.002) and Moghani (~ 0.04) sheep, and Talebi et al. (2025) found even lower values in pure and crossbred Moghani populations with Booroola and Texel ancestry. Eydivandi et al. [16] also reported substantial variation in FROH across breeds, ranging from Suffolk (~ 0.054) to Balouchi (~ 0.008). Together, these studies highlight how breeding strategies leave distinct signatures on ROH patterns and genetic diversity. A similar conclusion was drawn by Szmatola et al. [29], who analyzed nine Polish breeds and found short ROHs to be most common, a hallmark of historical inbreeding. Their results mirror our observation that commercial Mehraban sheep display a relatively higher contribution of short ROHs to total genomic inbreeding compared with traditional flocks, a pattern consistent with historical selection, demographic constraints, and the progressive fragmentation of ancestral homozygous segments through recombination.

Genomic regions within ROH islands, overlapped with ovine QTL

Analysis of ROH islands revealed notable differences in the genomic architecture of commercial and traditional Mehraban sheep populations. In total, 1,586 and 797 genes were identified within ROH islands of commercial and traditional populations, respectively (Fig. 10 and Table S5), while the genomic coordinates (start–end positions) of ROH islands and their associated genes are reported in Table S6. Although the number of genes identified within ROH islands differed between commercial and traditional populations, gene counts alone do not imply differences in selection pressure. These values are presented as descriptive features of ROH genomic content, and ROH islands are interpreted as candidate regions rather than definitive signatures of selection, as they may also arise from variation in recombination rate, local SNP density, or SNP-array features [10].

Interestingly, despite the difference in absolute counts, the biotype distribution of genes within ROH islands was highly consistent between populations: protein-coding genes predominated (74.64% vs. 74.71%), followed by lincRNAs (~ 8.5%) and snRNAs (~ 5.8%) (Fig. 11) This pattern is consistent with previous livestock studies reporting that ROH regions are enriched for protein-coding genes, while non-coding RNAs occur less frequently and may contribute regulatory functions [33, 53].

The overlap of ROH islands with ovine quantitative trait loci (QTL) revealed clear contrasts between traditional and commercial Mehraban populations (Table 4). In the traditional group, ROH islands were mainly associated with QTL for body condition score, mean fiber diameter, lambing potential, pregnancy rate, and semen volume (Table 4). These traits support fitness and resilience in low-input systems, where both natural selection and farmer-driven choices favor reproductive performance and survival. The ROH islands were spread across chromosomes (Fig. 12), suggesting weak selection pressures and the influence of demographic factors such as drift or founder effects. Detailed information on each ROH island, including chromosomal location, start and end SNPs, and the number of SNPs, is provided in Table S7. In contrast, commercial Mehraban sheep showed ROH overlaps mostly with QTL for milk yield and composition, muscle development, and carcass quality (Table 4). Here, the ROH islands were more clustered on specific chromosomes that contain production-related QTL, reflecting strong directional selection in structured breeding programs that focus on economic traits. These contrasting patterns are consistent with observations in other sheep populations, such as Italian Noticiana sheep [54], where ROH islands overlapped with QTL for traits related to fitness and productivity in extensive systems. Similarly, crossbred commercial populations, such as Texel and Booroola, show enrichment of ROH in regions linked to production traits, reflecting the impact of artificial selection [14]. Comparable associations have also been documented in other meat and dairy breeds, where ROH islands coincide with QTL for growth, carcass, or milk traits [33, 55]. Overlaps with wool-related QTL, including mean fiber diameter and clean fleece weight, were observed in both Mehraban populations, highlighting the shared importance of wool traits and consistent with reports in Chinese and European sheep [56].

Collectively, these findings illustrate the dual role of ROH islands: in traditional population, they capture genomic regions related to adaptation, reproduction, and survival, whereas in commercial population they predominantly reflect production-oriented targets of artificial selection. The coexistence of unique and shared ROH regions demonstrates the balance between natural adaptation and human-driven selection, and maintaining diversity in fitness-related ROH regions of traditional population may serve as a valuable genetic reservoir for enhancing resilience in commercial lines [33].

Gene ontology and pathway enrichment analysis

To examine the functional consequences of selection and inbreeding in the two groups, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment on genes located within ROH islands in traditional and commercial populations (Fig. 13 and Table S8). Nonetheless, these route enrichment findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, rather than confirmatory evidence of pathway-level selection.

In the traditional group, enriched pathways were primarily linked to immune regulation, reproductive processes, and cellular maintenance. For example, IL-17 (MAP3K2, RAF1), TNF (GPI), and NOD-like receptor signaling (HSP90AB1, CXCL8) pathways highlight the importance of immune defense in less intensively managed flocks. Reproductive functions were also represented, including oocyte meiosis (CCNE2) and progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation (HSP90AB1, CDC25A, RAF1). In addition, several cancer-related pathways including colorectal (PMAIP1, CDKN1A, MSH3, RAF1, DCC), gastric (ERBB2, CDH17, CCNE2, CDKN1A, RB1, WNT5A, RAF1), and chronic myeloid leukemia (CDKN1A, RAF1, RB1), were enriched, reflecting roles in cellular maintenance, DNA repair, and cell proliferation [57, 58]. These pathways highlight candidate genes within ROH hotspots that may contribute to resilience and reproductive success, traits particularly relevant for adaptation to extensive production systems. However, KEGG enrichment is exploratory; pathway annotation bias and unequal gene representation may affect results, so these findings indicate potential functional roles rather than direct causal effects. Accordingly, the QTL and ROH islands in traditional flocks reflect genomic regions associated with traits important for overall fitness and productivity rather than implying unique selective pressure on fertility [14, 50, 59]. Similar observations have been made in Tibetan and African sheep, where ROH overlap immune and reproductive genes associated with local adaptation [60, 61]. By contrast, the commercial group displayed a distinct enrichment profile reflecting intensive selection for production traits. Several “cancer-associated” pathways such as EGFR tyrosine kinase resistance (PDGFD, SOS1), pancreatic and prostate cancer (TGFB2, RB1, CCNE2, PDGFD, SOS1), and non-small cell lung cancer (RB1, SOS1), were prominent. While described in human disease contexts, these genes regulate cell growth, tissue development, and survival, suggesting candidate contributions to muscle accretion and carcass yield rather than asserting direct causality. The Hippo signaling pathway (AREG, TGFB2, BTRC, FRMD6, AFP, MOB1B), a master regulator of organ size and myogenesis, was also enriched, reinforcing evidence that commercial selection has targeted alleles linked to tissue growth [62–64].

Immune-related pathways were also significant, including NF-κB, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and chemokine signaling, with key genes such as CXCL1, CNTF, TGFB2, ACVR1, TNFSF15, CSF1, PPBP, and TNFRSF25. This reflects correlated selection for disease resilience in dense farming systems, where immune pressures differ from extensive grazing conditions [65, 66]. Additionally, pathways related to metabolism and cardiovascular function were enriched, including the TCA cycle (SUCLG2), selenocompound metabolism (PSTK, TXNRD3), cardiomyopathies (LAMA2, MYL3, TTN), and glycosaminoglycan/glycosphingolipid biosynthesis (ST3GAL1). These enrichments highlight candidate genes and pathways potentially involved in energy metabolism and tissue robustness, which may contribute to feed efficiency and growth performance in commercial breeds [29].

Pathway enrichment based on ROH-island gene lists is exploratory and intended for hypothesis generation. The detection of ROH islands is sensitive to sample size, relatedness within sampled flocks, and local SNP density, and KEGG/GO enrichment is itself subject to annotation bias and unequal pathway representation. With sample sizes of n = 97 (commercial) and n = 48 (traditional), the enriched pathways reported here should therefore be interpreted as candidate biological themes consistent with the contrasting selection histories of the two management systems, rather than as statistically validated evidence of pathway-level selection. Confirmatory testing in larger, independent samples, ideally using complementary signatures of selection (e.g., XP-EHH, Fst scans), is required before any of these candidate pathways can be considered established.

Conclusions

Our genome-wide comparison of traditional and commercial Mehraban sheep populations highlights the significant impact of breeding practices on genetic diversity, inbreeding, and functional loci. Traditional population exhibited higher genetic integrity, slower LD decay, and lower FROH, reflecting their resilience and older inbreeding under extensive management. Commercial population exhibited longer ROH, higher FROH, and faster LD decay, consistent with historical inbreeding, long-term breeding practices and directional selection for production traits such as muscle development, milk yield, and carcass quality. The ROH islands and QTL overlaps reveal that commercial population have been shaped by selection targeting growth and feed efficiency. Nonetheless, traditional one retains genomic regions associated with immunity, reproduction, and survival, serving as a reservoir of adaptive alleles. These findings provide valuable insights for breeding and conservation programs, highlighting the importance of preserving traditional population to safeguard adaptive potential while improving commercial productivity. Nevertheless, LD-, ROH-, and FROH-based estimates are influenced by demographic assumptions, recombination heterogeneity, and relatedness, and should be interpreted comparatively rather than as precise demographic values.
