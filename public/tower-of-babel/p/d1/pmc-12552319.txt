Dermatofibrosarcoma Protuberans (DFSP): Diagnostics and Molecular Pathology
Authors: Piotr Remiszewski, Joanna Taczała, Marcin Rosiński, Anna Szumera-Ciećkiewcz, Bartłomiej Szostakowski, Anna M Czarnecka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Opinion StatementDermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a rare, low-grade mesenchymal neoplasm that arises in the dermis and subcutaneous tissue. Clinically, DFSP presents as a slow-growing cutaneous plaque or nodular mass that is often initially mistaken for a benign dermatological condition, such as a keloid, a dermatofibroma or morphea. Due to its local aggressiveness and proclivity for subclinical spread and recurrence, accurate diagnosis and complete surgical excision are critical. Although DFSP rarely metastasises, its morbidity primarily stems from locally invasive growth and potential disfigurement due to extensive resections. The pathognomonic COL1A1-PDGFB fusion, which can be detected using fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) or reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), sustains autocrine PDGFRβ activation and can be used as a diagnostic marker and a target for the drug imatinib. Emerging genomic studies have revealed additional fusions, such as COL1A2-PDGFB and FBN1-CSAD, as well as mutations, such as CDKN2A/B deletions, that correlate with more aggressive, fibrosarcomatous (FS) variant. We reviewed the diagnostics of DFSP, including histopathology, immunohistochemistry (e.g. CD34, factor XIIIa, S100 and PRAME) as well as clinical presentation and recommended imaging modalities (e.g. ultrasound, MRI and PET/CT). To provide a better understanding of DFSP we discussed the molecular basis of DFSP, including the main genetic drivers and downstream signalling pathways, such as Ras-MAPK, PI3K-Akt and FGFR. Moreover, as there is no definitive staging system for DFSP, we proposed a novel one, integrating the presence of FS differentiation.Keywords: Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, COL1A1-PDGFB gene fusion, Imatinib mesylate therapy, Differential diagnosis, Fibrosarcomatous transformation

Introduction

Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a rare, slow-growing soft tissue sarcoma of the skin (STS), accounting for around 1–1.8% of all STS cases [1, 2]. DFSP predominantly affects young to middle-aged adults and shows a slight male predominance with a higher incidence reported among Black individuals [1, 3]. This slow-growing mesenchymal skin tumour typically presents as indurated plaque, nodule, or multinodular cutaneous mass [4]. The term “protuberant” in its name refers to the lumps of tissue that form under the skin. Clinically, DFSP can vary from plaques or small nodular lesions to large, irregular masses. It is a locally aggressive tumour with a tendency for local recurence, but rarely metastasises, occurring in approximately 1–4% of cases (often less than 5%) [5]. Although rare, metastasis is more likely to occur in certain circumstances. For example, it is more likely to occur if the tumour is large or located in specific areas, such as the head and neck [6]. Classic DFSP has a favourable prognosis, with a five-year progression-free survival (PFS) rate of around 93% [7]. However, the prognosis is significantly worse for fibrosarcomatous transformation (FS), a high-grade variant, with a 5-year PFS rate of just 33% and a 14.4–57% risk of metastasis; (median 6.8 years), 5-year OS was 64% (median time for OS was not reached) [7, 8]. LR occurs in up to 58% of FS-DFSP cases, and though it is uncommon in classic DFSP, distant metastasis becomes a notable concern with FS change [7]. Surgical excision remains the cornerstone of treatment. Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS) offers the lowest LR rates thanks to complete margin evaluation. Studies have shown that the recurrence rate for MMS is significantly lower (around 1–2%) than for wide local excision (WLE; 7–30%) [1, 9–11]. Yet, WLE with margins of 2–3 cm is an acceptable alternative [12]. For locally advanced or unresectable disease, imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor that targets the COL1A1-PDGFB fusion gene, is an effective treatment, particularly in cases where the genetic mutation has been confirmed. Objective response rates are over 50% as was shown by our clinic [7, 13, 14]. Therefore, a correct diagnosis is essential as FS differentiation affects both prognosis and the therapeutic strategy. To guide the use of targeted therapies like imatinib, molecular confirmation via FISH or RT-PCR for COL1A1-PDGFB fusion is recommended as stated in multiple guidelines including ESMO and NCCN [12, 15–18].

Clinical presentation

Although DFSP is usually superficial and confined to the dermis, it can invade fascia, muscle, periosteum and bone [19]. The tumours often have asymmetrical, subclinical horizontal finger-like extensions in the skin, making margin control difficult [20]. Diagnosis is often delayed because the lesions are slow-growing and may initially appear benign [21]. Some cases of DFSP do not protrude above the skin and may present as non-protuberant plaques which can last for an average of 7 years before the lesion becomes bulky and nodular [22]. The tumours can occur at any age, but are most common in patients between the ages of 20 and 50 [5, 23]. The most common site is the trunk (50–60%), followed by the extremities (20–30%) (the distribution among the extremities is often reported as fairly equal), and the head and neck (10–15%) [24, 25]. In the head and neck, the most common sites are the supraclavicular area and the forehead [26]. DFSP has also been reported in other areas such as the face, within the breast tissue, in the pubic region and vulva, and even in the oral cavity (including areas such as the cheek) [13–19]. The size of DFSP tumours at diagnosis varies, but the mean is around 2–3.5 cm [27–29]. However, tumours have been reported much larger, with a case report with DFSP on the trunk reaching nearly 40 cm in size [30]. DFSPs present as multinodular lesions [31]. In the early stages, DFSP lesions are often painless [31], but as the tumour progresses, especially in the rapidly growing variant, it can become painful and may ulcerate or bleed [32]. During pregnancy, DFSP frequently exhibits an accelerated growth rate, a phenomenon that may be attributed to the hormonal changes associated with gestation [33]. Furthermore, DFSP may be misdiagnosed as other benign conditions (Table 1), such as scars, due to its similar clinical appearance [77, 78], bruises [79], morphea [52], dermatofibroma [34], hemangioma [80] and other benign soft tissue tumours because of its slow growth and non-specific appearance. It may also be misdiagnosed as nodular fasciitis [81], fibroma [82], neurofibroma [72, 83], hematoma [84], schwannoma [85], low-grade fibrosarcoma [86], synovial sarcoma [87, 88], spindle cell melanoma [64], fibrosarcoma [89], or atypical fibroxanthoma [90]. Due to the rarity of this condition, general practitioners are not as familiar with the presentation and may misinterpret it as a benign condition, leading to delays in referral to dermatologists or oncologists. General practitioners need to be aware of slow-growing, non-painful skin lesions, especially those that persist or grow rapidly despite standard treatment, which may indicate DFSP.

Diagnosis

In DFSP, ultrasound is used for initial assessment, preoperative planning and, in some cases, postoperative monitoring for recurrence [91, 92]. DFSP typically presents as a hypoechoic or heterogeneously hypoechoic mass with well-defined margins. The lesion often infiltrates the subcutaneous fat, forming a honeycomb pattern characteristic of DFSP. Doppler ultrasound may show increased vascularity within the tumour, indicating its infiltrative and proliferative nature [93]. High-frequency probes (10–20 MHz) provide excellent resolution for delineating tumour depth, subcutaneous extension and possible invasion into deeper structures such as muscle or fascia [94]. Ultrasound can reveal peri-tumoural oedema, which is common in DFSP, and can help identify subtle extensions beyond clinically visible margins [95].

Diago et al. [96]. evaluated the use of ultrasound in the assessment of DFSP in 30 cases and demonstrated a high concordance with histopathology for measuring tumour depth. Tumours appeared predominantly as oval, hypoechoic lesions, with finger-like projections identified in 73.3% of cases and a posterior hyperechoic area corresponding to honeycomb invasion in 53.3%. Ultrasound depth measurements (mean 7.3 ± 4.0 mm) agreed closely with histopathological depths (mean 8.3 ± 4.2 mm), with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.97. The sensitivity for detecting muscle or fascia invasion was 81.8% and the specificity was 100%, highlighting the value of ultrasound in the preoperative planning and surgical management of DFSP. In another study [97], recurrent DFSP were more likely to be located in deeper structures (62.9%) and presented as oval, lobulated or irregular masses. Both groups had hypervascularity on Doppler imaging. Ultrasound showed a diagnostic accuracy of 88.6% for recurrent DFSP but only 17.4% for primary DFSP.

MRI, on the other hand, is more suitable for preoperative assessment, especially for larger or recurrent tumours (Table 2). It provides a precise understanding of tumour depth and lateral spread, with a sensitivity of 67% and specificity of 100% [91]. On T1-weighted images, DFSP lesions typically appear hypointense relative to subcutaneous fat, while on fat-saturated T2-weighted sequences, tumours show slightly heterogeneous hyperintense signals with patchy hypointense areas. These features are critical for distinguishing DFSP from surrounding tissue [95].Postoperatively, MRI is highly effective in identifying recurrences, showing nodular, homogeneous, well-defined lesions with marked contrast enhancement (Fig. 1) [95]. analysed the imaging findings in 27 patients with DFSP, including 18 cases with CT and 9 cases with MRI. Tumours ranged from 2 to 17 cm in diameter, with the majority being well-circumscribed, solitary, and infiltrating subcutaneous fat without calcification. Contrast-enhanced CT scans showed moderate to marked heterogeneous enhancement in 48.1% of cases and moderate homogeneous enhancement in 14.8%. On MRI, T1-weighted images showed isointense lesions in 9 cases, while T2-weighted images showed hyperintense or mixed signal intensities. DFSP was characterised histopathologically by spindle cells in storiform arrangement, with CD34 positivity observed in all cases. There were no metastases at diagnosis and 92.6% of cases showed no recurrence during a follow-up period of 6 months to 3 years.

CT imaging is generally reserved for specific cases, such as suspected bone invasion or pulmonary metastases [103]. For example, CT has demonstrated a positive predictive value (PPV) of 100% for identifying periosteal margin positivity in cases of DFSP located on the scalp, although its sensitivity is lower at 25% [109] CT is beneficial in preoperative planning for large or complex tumours, providing clear anatomical details to guide surgical strategies [110].

Although not a routine diagnostic tool for DFSP, PET scans have demonstrated utility in specific scenarios, such as assessing treatment response or detecting metastases. For example, in one case where FDG-PET imaging was used to monitor response to imatinib therapy, tumour FDG uptake decreased significantly within two weeks, correlating with clinical improvement [105]. PET/CT has also been shown to be effective in delineating disease extent, particularly in high-grade or fibrosarcomatous DFSP, where aggressive metabolic activity can be identified [111]. The diagnosis of DFSP is not always straightforward and is often delayed. Clinical suspicion based on the appearance of the lesion is usually followed by biopsy for confirmation [112]. Incisional or excisional biopsy is preferred, whereas fine-needle aspiration is usually avoided due to its relative inaccuracy [113]. Diagnosis requires histopathological analysis, and the use of immunohistochemical stains may be helpful. DFSP shows a diffuse infiltration of the dermis and subcutaneous tissue by spindle-shaped cells in a storiform pattern together with tumour cells, typically spreading along the septa of the subcutaneous adipose tissue [114]. The tumour cells are usually CD34 positive [115].Molecular tests such as fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) or multiplex reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) are useful in confirming difficult diagnoses of DFSP by detecting specific chromosomal translocations and fusion gene transcripts [116, 117]. These tests can confirm the presence of a COL1A1-PDGFB fusion, which is characteristic of DFSP [118].

There is no definitive staging system for DFSP. Some studies use the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging protocol for soft tissue sarcomas, or a simple distinction between primary tumour and local recurrence. The American Musculoskeletal Tumour Society (MSTS) system is based on histological grade, local extension and the presence of metastases, and because DFSP is almost always low-grade, it is usually staged as IA (low-grade tumour confined to an anatomical compartment) or IB (low-grade tumour not confined to an anatomical compartment). The NCCN classification is based on local, regional and distant disease, and the 2018 German guidelines classify by primary tumour (localised disease), lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis. Pathology

Microscopically, classic DFSP typically shows a proliferation of uniform spindle cells arranged in a storiform pattern [119]. These cells are elongated with mild hyperchromasia, small to inconspicuous nucleoli, and scant cytoplasm​ (Fig. 2)​ [120]. DFSP spindle cells infiltrate the dermis and subcutaneous tissue, often creating a honeycomb pattern within the subcutaneous fat​ [121, 122]. Immunohistochemically (IHC) tumour cells consistently express CD34, which is characteristic DFSP marker​​ [121]. CD34 expression helps distinguish DFSP from other spindle cell neoplasms spindle cell lipoma, solitary fibrous tumour, neurofibroma, peripheral nerve sheath tumour, angiosarcoma and myxoid tumour. Although CD34 positivity is not exclusive to DFSP and can be seen in other tumours like spindle cell lipomas and solitary fibrous tumours​. Other IHC markers including SMA, Desmin, Cadesmon, S100, SOX10, FXIIIa, STAT6 are used in the differential diagnosis to exclude benign lesions (i.e. dermatofibromas), low-grade sarcomas (i.e. leiomyosarcoma), melanoma (i.e. S100, SOX10, HMB45) [49] or malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours (i.e. H3K27me) (Table 4) [120, 133].

The tumour microenvironment in DFSP includes variable lymphocytic infiltration, though it is generally sparse compared to other malignancies. There is limited involvement of macrophages and natural killer (NK) cells, which suggests that DFSP might not be targeted by conventional immunotherapy [120, 133] such as checkpoint inhibitors like anti-PD-1/PD-L1 (e.g. pembrolizumab or nivolumab) or anti-CTLA-4 therapies (e.g. ipilimumab). These therapies work by blocking inhibitory signals on T cells, thereby reactivating immune responses against tumours. However, in DFSP, the sparse lymphocytic infiltration and limited presence of cytotoxic immune cells suggest a reduced likelihood of response to these agents [144]. Checkpoint inhibitors are most effective in tumours with a high mutational burden or robust immune infiltration, neither of which is characteristic of DFSP. This low presence of immune cells, often referred to as an ‘immune-cold’ tumour microenvironment, suggests that DFSP may require strategies aimed at converting the tumour to an ‘immune-hot’ phenotype [145].

However, the presence of the COL1A1-PDGFB fusion gene in DFSP cells opens the possibility for targeted therapies using tyrosine kinase inhibitors​​; in routine histopathological diagnostics this fusion gene is detected in fluorescence in situ method (FISH) and is very helpful in the diagnosis confirmation [2, 4, 122, 146]. This fusion gene not only aids in diagnosis, but also serves as a critical therapeutic target for imatinib mesylate, a potent tyrosine kinase inhibitor specifically designed to block the activity of PDGFRβ [147]. The COL1A1-PDGFB fusion gene leads to overproduction of platelet-derived growth factor beta (PDGFB), which drives tumour growth through an autocrine signalling mechanism that promotes uncontrolled cell proliferation and survival. Imatinib binds to the ATP-binding site of PDGFRβ, preventing its activation and subsequent downstream signalling. This targeted approach is particularly beneficial in cases where surgical resection is not feasible due to tumour location, size or recurrence with positive margins. Clinical studies have shown significant tumour shrinkage and disease stabilisation in patients with unresectable or metastatic DFSP when treated with imatinib at doses of 400 to 800 mg/day (importantly [147], there seems to be no difference between 400- or 800-mg daily doses) [148].Thus, the presence of the COL1A1-PDGFB fusion gene not only underpins the molecular pathogenesis of DFSP, but also provides a specific avenue for effective pharmacological intervention.

Accurate diagnosis of DFSP requires careful histopathological examination and an appropriate panel of immunohistochemical stainings, supplemented by molecular techniques such as FISH or reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) to detect specific gene fusions [117].

Genetics and molecular biology

DFSP is associated with specific mutations related to chromosomes 17 and 22 which may appear as supernumerary ring chromosomes or chromosomal translocation t(17; 22) (q22; q13) (Table 6) [27, 159]. It results in a gene fusion of COL1A1 (17q21) with PDGFB (22q13) occurs (Fig. 3). As a consequence, there is observed an overexpression of PDGFβ which causes continuous activation of his receptor. A correlation exists between the aforementioned mutation and activation of the Akt-mTOR pathway [160] which appears in many sarcomas [161] and is involved in the tumourigenesis of DFSP [27]. Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) or multiplex reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) can be used in diagnosis to detect fusion COL1A1-PDGFB [27, 162]. Whole genome sequencing revealed that DFSP is associated with many other genetic alterations, often concerning mutations common in other cases of tumours [156].

Molecular mechanism of COL1A1-PDGFB fusion gene and imatinib action in DFSP: (1) formation of COL1A1-PDGFB fusion gene; (2) autocrine PDGFB signaling loop in tumour cells; (3) Imatinib’s mechanism of action.

DFSP is characterised by abnormal activation of specific signalling pathways that drive tumour growth and development [152, 156]. The most important of these is the platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) [118, 156]. The majority of DFSP cases involve a chromosomal translocation, specifically a fusion of the COL1A1 gene with the PDGFB gene, most commonly involving chromosomes 17 and 22 [163]. This fusion results in the production of a COL1A1-PDGFβ fusion protein, which leads to continuous activation of the PDGFR. The fusion protein acts as an autocrine factor, binding to the PDGFR on the surface of DFSP cells, which in turn stimulates cell growth and proliferation [164]. The PDGFR has an extracellular domain that binds the growth factor ligand, a transmembrane domain and a cytoplasmic domain with tyrosine kinase activity [165]. Upon ligand binding, the receptor activates intracellular signalling cascades that trigger processes such as proliferation, chemotaxis and apoptosis. Imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, is effective in treating DFSP because it blocks the ATP-binding site of the PDGFR, reducing its enzymatic activity and inhibiting the growth of DFSP cells [166, 167].

In addition to the PDGFR signalling pathway, the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) signalling pathway also plays a role in DFSP [168]. DFSP cells express FGFRs, in particular FGFR1 and FGFR2, as well as their ligands, fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) [169]. Binding of FGFs to FGFRs leads to autocrine or paracrine activation of the signalling pathway. In autocrine activation, FGFs produced by DFSP cells bind to FGFRs on the same cells, leading to continuous signalling [170]. In paracrine activation, FGFs produced by stromal cells activate FGFRs on DFSP cells [164]. The COL1A1-PDGFB fusion protein can also enhance FGFR signalling. Activation of the FGFR signalling pathway promotes cell proliferation, survival and migration, contributing to tumour growth. Several FGFR inhibitors are being investigated as potential therapies for DFSP.

Activation of the PDGFR initiates several downstream signalling pathways (Table 7). The Ras-MAPK pathway is activated by PDGFR and is important for cell proliferation and differentiation. Its activation by PDGFR signalling contributes to the continuous proliferation of DFSP cells [164]. The PI3K-Akt pathway is also activated by PDGFR and is important for cell survival and resistance to apoptosis, promoting tumour progression and resistance to therapy [174, 179]. Understanding these signalling pathways is crucial for the development of targeted therapies for DFSP.

Conclusions

An accurate diagnosis of DFSP depends on recognising its characteristic histopathology, namely storiform spindle cells with diffuse CD34 positivity, and confirming the pathognomonic COL1A1-PDGFB gene fusion via FISH or RT-PCR. This fusion enables the use of targeted therapy with imatinib, particularly in advanced or unresectable cases. Clinicians should remain vigilant for DFSP in the presence of persistent, slow-growing dermal lesions, particularly those that do not respond to standard treatment. Distinguishing DFSP from benign mimics necessitates the integration of imaging techniques (e.g. MRI and ultrasound), IHC and molecular diagnostics. Identifying FS transformation is crucial because it significantly worsens prognosis, including much higher LR and metastatic rates. Emerging fusion variants (e.g. COL1A2-PDGFB and FBN1-CSAD) and genetic alterations (e.g. CDKN2A/B deletions) may inform future risk stratification and therapeutic resistance. Doctors should prioritise molecular confirmation in cases where diagnosis is uncertain or the tumour is aggressive, and be aware that conventional immunotherapies are not very effective due to the immune-cold microenvironment of DFSP. In clinical practice, early biopsy, molecular testing and multidisciplinary planning are critical for the effective diagnosis and treatment of DFSP.
