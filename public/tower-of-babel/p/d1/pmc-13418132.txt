A nomogram for estimating prevalent gallstone risk in patients with type 2 diabetes: development and validation in a multicenter cohort
Authors: Huan-Shan Hong, Da-Qing Zhu, Ying Huang, Li-Hui Kang, Yu-Meng Zhou, Jun-Tao Tan, Rui-Qiong Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGallbladder stone (GBS) is a frequent complication in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), yet effective tools for baseline risk estimation remain lacking. This study aimed to develop and validate a clinically practical nomogram for individualized GBS risk assessment in T2DM patients using multicenter real-world data.MethodsIn this retrospective multicenter study, 1,762 T2DM patients with an intact gallbladder who underwent abdominal ultrasonography at two tertiary hospitals were included. The primary center provided a training set (n = 914) and internal validation set (n = 392), and the second center provided an external validation cohort (n = 456). Logistic regression was used to build a nomogram for ultrasound-detected GBS at the index visit, and its performance was evaluated by ROC analysis, calibration, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsSix factors—age ≥ 60 years, longer diabetes duration, elevated HbA1c, FPG, TG, and presence of diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN)—were independently associated with GBS. The nomogram showed good discrimination in the training (AUC = 0.82), internal (0.81), and external (0.82) validation cohorts. Calibration curves demonstrated consistency between predicted and actual risk, and DCA confirmed clinical usefulness.ConclusionsThis nomogram offers a simple, non-invasive tool for estimating baseline GBS risk in T2DM patients, and may assist in risk stratification, targeted monitoring, and preventive counseling in outpatient diabetes care.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02335-6.

Introduction

Gallbladder stone (GBS) disease is one of the most prevalent disorders of the digestive system, affecting approximately 10–20% of the adult population worldwide [1, 2]. Its occurrence is closely linked to a range of metabolic and systemic risk factors, including obesity, insulin resistance, and diabetes mellitus. Among these, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has emerged as a significant contributor to gallstone formation [3].

Patients with T2DM commonly exhibit dysregulated lipid metabolism, impaired gallbladder motility—partly due to autonomic neuropathy—and chronic low-grade inflammation [4]. These factors promote cholesterol supersaturation in bile, ultimately facilitating the development of gallstones [5, 6]. Large-scale epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated that T2DM is independently associated with an elevated risk of both asymptomatic and symptomatic GBS, particularly in individuals with prolonged disease duration and inadequate glycemic control [7, 8].

While the T2DM‑GBS association is well documented, risk prediction tools tailored to populations with diabetes remain limited. A prior nomogram for GBS in T2DM provided an initial step toward risk stratification but was limited by its single-center derivation and lack of external validation [9]. Moreover, it did not include diabetes-specific complications such as autonomic neuropathy, which directly impairs gallbladder emptying. Existing models developed in general populations often rely on non-routine variables or lack multicenter validation, limiting their utility in routine outpatient diabetes care [10, 11].

To overcome these limitations, this study aimed to develop and externally validate a practical nomogram for estimating the baseline risk of GBS in T2DM patients using a large, multicenter, real-world cohort. The model incorporates routinely available demographic, metabolic, and diabetic complication-related variables—including diabetic peripheral neuropathy—and was rigorously evaluated through assessments of discrimination, calibration, and decision curve analysis. Our goal was to provide a reliable, user-friendly tool to support early risk stratification in outpatient diabetes management.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective multicenter study included patients with T2DM from two tertiary hospitals in southern China. The target population was patients with T2DM and an intact gallbladder who underwent abdominal ultrasonography in routine clinical practice and were not part of a universal screening program for all patients with T2DM. Abdominal ultrasonography was requested at the discretion of treating physicians, mainly for routine metabolic or hepatobiliary assessment (e.g., fatty liver evaluation) or for evaluation of symptoms or abnormal liver function tests; specific indications were not systematically coded. One center provided data for model development and internal validation, while the other contributed an independent cohort for external validation during the same study period (2021–2024). For each participant, the index date was defined as the outpatient visit or hospitalization at which abdominal ultrasonography was performed to evaluate gallbladder status. When multiple ultrasound examinations were available within the study period, only the first eligible examination was considered as the index visit. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) adult patients (≥ 18 years); (2) diagnosis of T2DM based on the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria [12]; (3) availability of abdominal ultrasonography to evaluate gallbladder status at the index visit; and (4) complete clinical and laboratory data obtained from the same clinical encounter as the index ultrasonography. Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of cholecystectomy or hepatobiliary surgery; (2) diagnosis of type 1 diabetes or gestational diabetes; and (3) concurrent malignancy, acute infection, or severe systemic disease. Patients with a history of cholecystectomy were excluded because the gallbladder was no longer present and the primary outcome (ultrasound-detected gallbladder stone) could not be assessed in a comparable way. Patients with and without GBS at the index visit were included, and GBS status at that visit served as the binary outcome for model development and validation. Symptom status was not uniformly documented; therefore, this analysis does not distinguish asymptomatic from symptomatic stones. No longitudinal follow-up was performed.

Data collection

Relevant data were extracted from electronic medical records corresponding to the same outpatient visit or hospitalization episode as the index abdominal ultrasonography, including demographics (age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol use), metabolic indicators (HbA1c, FPG, PBG, TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C), liver function tests (ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, TBil, DBil, TBA), renal function (BUN, SCr, eGFR), and diabetes duration. Smoking status was defined as current smoking (≥ 1 cigarette per day for ≥ 6 months) versus non-smoking (never smoked or former smoker who quit ≥ 1 year prior). Alcohol use was defined as current drinking (≥ 1 standard drink per week for ≥ 6 months) versus non-drinking (never drank or former drinker who abstained ≥ 1 year). Diabetic complications were also recorded, including diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), lower extremity arterial disease (LEAD), diabetic kidney disease (DKD), and diabetic retinopathy (DR). All laboratory tests were performed in the accredited central laboratories of the two participating hospitals (ISO 15189 certified), with no changes in analytical methods or instrumentation during the study period (2021–2024). When more than one laboratory measurement was available during the same clinical encounter, the value closest in time to the ultrasound examination was used for analysis. Patients without complete information on key predictors (age, diabetes duration, HbA1c, FPG, TG, or DPN) at the index visit were excluded from the final analysis (n = 157, 7.0% of screened cohort). For all other variables, missing data were minimal and handled by complete-case analysis without imputation. Given this low proportion of missingness and the lack of systematic differences in available demographic and clinical characteristics between included and excluded cases, complete-case analysis was considered appropriate and multiple imputation was not pursued. Data extraction was performed by two trained investigators and discrepancies—primarily involving ambiguous laboratory values, unclear complication documentation, or equivocal ultrasound descriptions— were resolved by consensus to minimize transcription errors. DPN was defined as a documented diagnosis of distal symmetric diabetic neuropathy by an endocrinologist or neurologist, based on typical distal sensory symptoms and at least one abnormal sign on neurological examination (using standardized tools including 10-g monofilament, vibration perception testing, or ankle reflex assessment), after exclusion of other causes of neuropathy, in line with contemporary guideline recommendations [13].

GBS was defined by ultrasonographic identification of mobile echogenic foci with posterior acoustic shadowing within the gallbladder lumen [14]. Information on GBS was obtained by manual review of formal abdominal ultrasonography reports in the electronic medical records. All examinations were performed by experienced sonographers and interpreted by radiologists according to standardized departmental protocols; reports with ambiguous descriptions were jointly reviewed and classified by two investigators by consensus. Inter-observer variability was not formally quantified.

Dataset allocation

Using the R software (version 4.2.2), the primary dataset (n = 1,306) from the First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University was randomly divided into a training set (n = 914, 70%) and an internal validation set (n = 392, 30%) through stratified sampling based on GBS status. The external validation set (n = 456) from the Fifth People’s Hospital of Ganzhou was used to assess the model’s generalizability.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using means ± standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Group comparisons were performed using the t-test, ANOVA, chi-square, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate logistic regression was used to identify potential predictors of GBS, and variables with p < 0.05 were entered into a multivariate logistic model. No automated stepwise selection was applied; variables were retained based on their independent association in the fully adjusted model. Collinearity among the predictors in the final multivariate model was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with values < 5 considered acceptable. Independent predictors were incorporated into a nomogram using the “rms” package in R. Model performance was assessed by the area under the ROC curve (AUC), calibration plots, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) test. The Brier score was calculated to assess overall model calibration (range 0–1, with lower values indicating better calibration). An optimal nomogram cut-off score was determined using the Youden index in the training set, and corresponding sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio (LR+), and negative likelihood ratio (LR–) were reported. Clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA), which calculates the net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities by weighting true-positive and false-positive classifications. Internal validation was performed with 1,000 bootstrap resamples, in which repeated samples of equal size were drawn with replacement from the training set to estimate optimism-corrected performance and assess model stability, and external validation was conducted in an independent cohort. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2), with a two-sided p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University and the Fifth People’s Hospital of Ganzhou. Informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University and the Ethics Committee of the Fifth People’s Hospital of Ganzhou due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 2,234 patients with T2DM who underwent abdominal ultrasonography were initially screened. After applying exclusion criteria, 472 patients were excluded: prior cholecystectomy or hepatobiliary surgery (n = 215), incomplete data on key predictors (n = 157), age < 18 years (n = 8), type 1 or gestational diabetes (n = 22), missing abdominal ultrasound data (n = 15), and malignancy, acute infection, or severe systemic disease (n = 55). The remaining 1,762 patients were included in the final analysis. Among these, 1,306 were from The First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University and randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 914) and an internal validation cohort (n = 392) using R software. The remaining 456 patients were from the Fifth People’s Hospital of Ganzhou and served as the external validation cohort (Fig. 1). The overall prevalence of GBS was 13.2% (232/1762), with comparable rates across the three subsets: 13.1% in the training set, 13.3% in the internal validation set, and 13.2% in the external validation set.

Patients excluded due to incomplete key data (n = 157) did not differ significantly from included patients in available demographic or clinical characteristics. Specifically, excluded vs. included patients had similar age (61.28 ± 12.03 vs. 60.75 ± 11.92 years, P = 0.573), sex (52.9% male vs. 53.1%, P = 0.892), diabetes duration (9.16 ± 8.07 vs. 9.43 ± 8.31 years, P = 0.684), HbA1c (7.79 ± 1.88% vs. 7.92 ± 1.96%, P = 0.426), FPG (8.48 ± 3.15 vs. 8.63 ± 3.08 mmol/L, P = 0.537), TG (1.91 ± 1.09 vs. 2.02 ± 1.18 mmol/L, P = 0.298), and DPN prevalence (24.8% vs. 25.1%, P = 0.761).

Independent predictive factors for GBS

In the derivation cohort (n = 1,306), univariate analysis identified multiple factors significantly associated with GBS (Table 2). Briefly, patients with GBS were older, had longer diabetes duration, and exhibited higher HbA1c, FPG, TG, TC, and HDL-C levels (all P < 0.05). The prevalence of DPN and LEAD was also greater in the GBS group (P < 0.001 and P = 0.011, respectively).

Development and validation of the predictive nomogram

Based on the six independent predictors, a nomogram was constructed (Fig. 2). Point assignments and corresponding β coefficients are provided in Supplementary Table S1. For illustration, a patient aged > 60 years (13.5 points) with 20-year diabetes duration (20 points), HbA1c 9% (55 points), FPG 10 mmol/L (22.5 points), TG 7 mmol/L (22 points), and DPN (22.5 points) would accumulate 155.5 points, corresponding to an estimated 50% probability of having GBS at the index visit.

The nomogram demonstrated robust and consistent performance across all three cohorts (Fig. 3). In the derivation cohort, there were 232 GBS events and 6 predictors, yielding an EPV of 38.7, well above the recommended threshold of 10, indicating low risk of overfitting. In the training set, the AUC was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.79–0.84). Internal validation yielded an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.77–0.85), while external validation confirmed generalizability with an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.77–0.88). Calibration curves showed excellent agreement between predicted and observed probabilities (HL P = 0.594, 0.106, and 0.459 for training, internal, and external sets, respectively). Brier scores were 0.089 (training), 0.092 (internal), and 0.094 (external), further supporting good calibration.

A nomogram score of 120 points (≈ 25% predicted risk) was identified as the optimal cut-off (sensitivity 76.2%, specificity 74.5%). For clinical convenience, patients may be stratified as low risk (< 100 points, ≈<10% GBS probability), intermediate risk (100–140 points, ≈ 10%–40% probability), and high risk (> 140 points, ≈>40% probability).

DCA showed a broad range of threshold probabilities—6–50% in the training set (Fig. 3G), 5–70% in the internal validation set (Fig. 3H), and 5–55% in the external validation set (Fig. 3I)—highlighting the clinical utility of the nomogram in risk stratification across commonly encountered decision thresholds. At higher thresholds (approximately > 50% predicted risk), the model curve intersected with the ‘treat-none’ strategy, indicating limited additional net benefit for decisions requiring very high certainty.

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective study, we developed and externally validated a clinical nomogram to estimate the prevalent risk of GBS in patients with T2DM, based on six routinely available clinical variables: age ≥ 60 years, duration of diabetes, HbA1c, FPG, TG levels, and the presence of DPN. The model demonstrated robust discriminatory capacity and calibration across training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, and showed promising clinical utility as evidenced by decision curve analysis. Compared with a previously published T2DM-specific nomogram—which was single-center and lacked external validation [9]—the present model benefits from a multicenter design, independent external validation, a larger sample size, and the inclusion of diabetic peripheral neuropathy as a diabetes-specific predictor.

This study reaffirmed several well-recognized risk factors for GBS in populations with diabetes. Older age remained a significant unmodifiable risk factor, consistent with previous studies demonstrating an age-related decline in gallbladder motility and increased hepatic cholesterol secretion, both of which contribute to bile lithogenicity and gallstone formation [6, 15, 16]. Notably, even within the framework of modern diabetes management, aging appears to exert an independent effect on biliary risk [17]. These findings underscore the importance of heightened clinical vigilance in elderly patients with T2DM, who may be predisposed to gallbladder dysfunction despite adequate glycemic control. Given the elevated prevalence of gallstones in this population and the often atypical or silent clinical presentation [15, 17], clinicians may consider abdominal ultrasonography on a case-by-case basis as part of individualized risk assessment. However, no specific surveillance interval can be recommended from this cross-sectional study.

This study reaffirms that longer duration of T2DM is independently associated with increased risk of GBS formation. Prior studies have shown that patients with diabetes duration over 10 years are significantly more likely to develop cholelithiasis [7, 18, 19]. A key mechanism involves chronic hyperglycemia-induced autonomic neuropathy, which impairs the gallbladder’s contractile response to cholecystokinin (CCK), leading to bile stasis [20]. Ultrasound studies confirm reduced gallbladder emptying in patients with diabetes and autonomic dysfunction [21]. Moreover, prolonged hyperglycemia may suppress CCK receptor expression and related signaling pathways, further reducing gallbladder motility and promoting cholesterol supersaturation [22]. Our findings highlight the importance of incorporating diabetes duration into GBS risk assessment, providing clinicians with a practical, quantifiable indicator for risk awareness and clinical vigilance.

Elevated HbA1c levels emerged as one of the most powerful predictors in our model. Poor long‑term glycemic control has been implicated in oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and dyslipidemia—factors that collectively impair gallbladder smooth muscle response and promote cholesterol crystallization [15]. Experimental and clinical studies confirm that hyperglycemia inhibits gallbladder motility, induces cholestasis, and leads to oxidative damage in the gallbladder wall, creating conditions favorable for gallstone formation [6, 23]. Consistent with these mechanisms, our findings underscore that elevated HbA1c is not merely a metabolic marker, but also a robust predictive biomarker for biliary complications in T2DM care.

FPG, reflecting short-term glycemic fluctuations, was identified as another independent predictor. Unlike HbA1c, FPG may capture transient hyperglycemia-induced effects such as osmotic shifts and autonomic instability [24]. Previous reports have shown that elevated fasting glucose increases bile cholesterol saturation, particularly in insulin-resistant individuals [25]. By including both HbA1c and FPG, our model accounts for both chronic and acute glycemic disturbances, offering a more nuanced evaluation of metabolic impact on GBS development.

Hypertriglyceridemia was also significantly associated with increased GBS risk. Elevated TG levels drive hepatic very-low-density lipoprotein overproduction and enhance biliary cholesterol excretion, facilitating lithogenesis [26, 27]. Furthermore, TG-rich lipoproteins may directly impair gallbladder contractility. This finding aligns with prior reports in populations without diabetes, but our study confirms the independent role of TG in diabetic gallstone pathophysiology after adjusting for glycemic parameters.

DPN emerged as the most impactful categorical variable in our model. Autonomic neuropathy impairs gallbladder innervation and delays emptying, increasing the risk of bile stasis and stone formation [20, 28]. Importantly, DPN often progresses subclinically and may be underrecognized in outpatient settings. Its integration into the nomogram emphasizes its value not only as a marker of systemic microvascular damage, but also as a practical indicator of biliary dysfunction in patients with diabetes.

It is important to emphasize that while these factors are independently associated with GBS in this cross-sectional analysis, the present study does not establish causality. Whether therapeutic improvements in glycemic control or triglyceride levels directly reduce gallstone risk remains plausible but unproven and requires prospective interventional evaluation.

Although several variables—such as TC, HDL-C, and LEAD—showed statistical significance in univariate analysis, they lost significance in multivariate regression. This suggests potential confounding or mediation through stronger predictors like TG and HbA1c. For example, while HDL-C is inversely associated with cardiovascular risk, its role in gallstone formation is less direct. Similarly, LEAD reflects macrovascular disease burden but does not directly impair gallbladder physiology. Other clinical and biochemical variables, including sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol use, liver and renal function, and complications such as DKD and DR, were not independently associated with GBS in our adjusted analysis. These findings suggest that while these factors reflect overall disease burden, they may lack a specific mechanistic link to gallstone pathogenesis.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it is derived from a large, multicenter outpatient cohort of patients with T2DM, reflecting real-world clinical settings and enhancing the generalizability of the findings. The sample size and multicenter design increase the statistical power and robustness of the model. Second, the inclusion of both internal and external validation using independent cohorts strengthens the credibility and reproducibility of the nomogram. This two-step validation approach is essential for demonstrating model stability and applicability across diverse clinical populations. Third, all included predictors—age, diabetes duration, HbA1c, FPG, TG, and DPN—are routinely measured in clinical practice and do not require additional testing or cost, making the tool easily implementable in outpatient settings. Furthermore, DCA demonstrated that the model offers meaningful net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, suggesting potential clinical utility in supporting individualized risk assessment, though prospective studies are required to determine whether model-guided decision making improves patient outcomes or provides incremental benefit over usual care. In practice, patients identified as high risk by the nomogram may merit closer clinical follow-up, a lower threshold for repeat ultrasound when biliary symptoms or liver test abnormalities occur, and more targeted lifestyle and metabolic counseling, rather than prophylactic cholecystectomy. Based on the Youden index, a nomogram score of 120 points (≈ 25% predicted risk) may serve as a practical reference to identify patients who could benefit from closer monitoring or repeat imaging. For instance, patients with scores < 100 points (~ 10% risk) might continue routine annual ultrasound if clinically indicated, whereas those with scores > 140 points (~ 40% risk) might be considered for closer clinical follow-up and optimization of glycemic and lipid control, although the benefit of such intensification specifically for reducing gallstone risk remains unproven and requires prospective evaluation. However, these thresholds are suggested for exploratory clinical use only and have not been prospectively validated; their impact on patient outcomes requires evaluation in future studies. Of note, all proposed clinical actions based on the nomogram are exploratory and hypothesis-generating; they should not be interpreted as evidence-based recommendations pending prospective validation.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be noted. First, as a retrospective study based on electronic medical records, information and selection bias cannot be excluded, even though baseline variables were restricted to the index visit when gallbladder ultrasonography was performed; GBS status also relied on routine ultrasound reports without formal assessment of inter-observer variability. Although operator-dependent, abdominal ultrasound has high sensitivity (84%–95%) and specificity (> 95%) for gallstone detection and remains the first-line modality in clinical practice, whereas CT may miss non-calcified stones and MRCP is reserved primarily for bile duct evaluation. Second, external validation was conducted in a single region in southern China and only in patients with T2DM and an intact gallbladder, which may limit the generalizability of the model to other populations, healthcare systems, or patients with a history of cholecystectomy, particularly in settings with different ethnic compositions and dietary patterns. Moreover, because abdominal ultrasonography was requested at the discretion of treating physicians and indications were not systematically recorded, indication bias may have influenced which patients were included, and the nomogram reflects risk in a clinically referred population rather than a universally screened T2DM cohort. Third, lifestyle factors and detailed bile acid or gallbladder motility measures were not available. Importantly, the lack of data on antidiabetic medications is a major concern, particularly regarding incretin-based therapies. The use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) has been associated with an increased risk of biliary events. Potential underlying mechanisms include GLP-1 RA–mediated weight loss leading to cholesterol mobilization into bile, direct inhibition of gallbladder smooth muscle contraction, and delayed gallbladder emptying. Because medication data were not available, we could not perform sensitivity analyses to isolate drug-specific effects; future prospective studies should incorporate detailed treatment histories to address this question. Furthermore, we could not reliably distinguish symptomatic from asymptomatic GBS. Accordingly, the nomogram estimates the probability of ultrasound-detected GBS at the time of assessment, not the risk of developing symptomatic disease or requiring intervention. A small proportion of hospitalized patients were included, which may introduce heterogeneity; future studies in purely outpatient cohorts are warranted. Fourth, we did not explore alternative lipid parameters such as non-HDL-C, which may offer complementary predictive value in future studies. Future studies may benefit from more liberal variable selection thresholds to comprehensively evaluate clinically plausible predictors. Future work with larger samples may also benefit from penalized regression (e.g., LASSO) to further confirm variable selection stability, as well as subgroup analyses excluding patients with macrovascular or renal complications to assess model performance in lower-risk populations. Finally, the cross-sectional design captures GBS status at a single time point and cannot assess future incident stone formation or establish causality; and prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate the nomogram and determine whether its use can improve clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, this study presents a validated, clinically practical nomogram to estimate the baseline risk of GBS in patients with T2DM. Incorporating routinely available clinical indicators—age, diabetes duration, HbA1c, FPG, triglycerides, and DPN—the model demonstrated strong discriminatory power and external applicability. This tool estimates the probability of ultrasound detected GBS at the time of ultrasound, regardless of the indication for the examination, and may assist in risk stratification for research or exploratory clinical purposes. However, its impact on clinical outcomes has not been evaluated, and prospective validation in diverse populations is required before routine use can be recommended.
