Accessibility of telomeric overhangs to stabilizing small-molecule ligands
Authors: Janan Alfehaid, Vidsara Surasinghe, Sineth G Kodikara, Mohammad L Kabir, Sajad Shiekh, Masayuki Tera, Kazuo Nagasawa, John J Portman, Hamza Balci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman chromosomes terminate in 50–300 nucleotide (nt) long single-stranded telomeric overhangs composed of repeating d(TTAGGG) sequences, which can fold into tandem G-quadruplex (GQ) structures protecting chromosome ends. Stabilizing GQs by small-molecules inhibits telomerase, motivating development of GQ-targeting anti-cancer agents. However, how interactions among successive GQs and bound ligands affect small-molecule accessibility remain unclear. We used single-molecule fluorescence microscopy and stepwise photobleaching to quantify binding stoichiometry of fluorescent oxazole telomestatin derivative L1Cy5-7OTD to telomeric overhangs capable of forming 1–6 GQs (30–162 nt long), spanning much of the physiologically relevant range. Longer overhangs bound more ligands on average but consistently below the theoretical maximum, saturating at six ligands even when 12 binding sites were available. Increasing the inter-GQ spacer from 3-nt to 9-nt enhanced ligand binding, a result independently confirmed by ensemble fluorescence enhancement experiments using N-methyl mesoporphyrin IX as a ligand. A one-dimensional lattice model, describing equilibrium ligand binding to partially ordered telomeric overhangs, revealed positive cooperativity between folding of successive GQs, negative cooperativity between ligands bound opposing (top and bottom) faces of successive GQs, and reduced affinity at duplex-single strand junction. These results show how telomeric architecture governs ligand accessibility and inform rational GQ-targeted drug design.

Introduction

The ends of linear chromosomes resemble DNA double strand breaks (DSB) and must be protected to avoid deleterious repair and fusion. Telomeres, specialized nucleoprotein structures composed of d(TTAGGG) DNA repeat sequences and associated proteins, play an important role in this process [1–3]. The d(TTAGGG) sequences, each called a telomeric repeat or a G-Tract, extend over 9–15 kb and terminate with a 50–300-nucleotide (nt) long single stranded region, called the G-tail or G-overhang [4]. This G-overhang acts as the key loading site for telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein reverse transcriptase, which uses the free 3′-end as a primer to extend telomeres [5–8]. Significantly, the G-overhang can fold into tandem secondary structures known as G-quadruplexes (GQs) [9–12]. The GQ structure consists of stacked guanine tetrads, in which guanines at the tetrad corners form Hoogsteen hydrogen bonds, stabilized by monovalent cations located within or between tetrad planes [13]. GQ formation is dynamic and can be influenced by sequence, loop length, ion concentration, and the presence of specific proteins or small-molecules [14, 15]. Depending on environmental conditions, the telomeric sequences can fold into parallel, antiparallel, or hybrid conformations [16, 17]. By sequestering the 3′-end of the telomere into compact structures, GQs influence telomere length regulation [18–21]. Telomeric GQs also contribute to end protection by masking chromosome ends and preventing their misrecognition as DSB, thereby suppressing erroneous DNA damage response [21].

As telomerase is upregulated in most cancers and GQs can inhibit its access and catalytic activity, GQ stabilizing small-molecule ligands have emerged as potential anti-cancer therapeutics [22–25]. Stabilization of telomeric GQs by small-molecules inhibits telomerase activity [26–29] and thereby reduces the proliferative capacity of cancer cells [30, 31]. Many GQ ligands feature planar aromatic scaffolds that enable strong π–π stacking onto terminal G-tetrad surfaces [32]. Among the most potent ligands, telomestatin (from Streptomyces anulatus) is the first natural product shown to be a telomerase inhibitor at nanomolar concentrations by facilitating the formation or stabilization of GQ structures [33, 34]. Oxazole telomestatin derivatives (OTDs) have been designed as simplified analogs of telomestatin and provide greater potential as drug candidates in terms of solubility and cytotoxicity while maintaining the GQ specificity and strong affinity [35, 36]. Collectively, OTDs serve as versatile chemical tools for probing GQ biology, and their strong and selective recognition of GQs also underscores their potential as scaffolds for drug development, particularly in telomerase inhibition.

NMR studies investigating binding of L2H2-6OTD [37], which is less planar than L1H1-7OTD, to a single telomeric GQ suggested that it preferentially binds to the top G-tetrad face (closer to 5′-end) of the GQ at 1:1 ligand:GQ concentration ratio in both parallel and hybrid conformations [38]. Binding to the second face (closer to 3′-end) was observed at higher ligand concentrations only for ligands with cationic amine side chains. These observations suggest an asymmetric binding affinity for L2H2-6OTD between the two G-tetrads of the GQ.

The heptaoxazole core of 7OTD is nearly planar (dihedral angle ∼175.5°), enabling strong π–π stacking interactions with the G-quartet surface and resulting in high GQ affinity, surpassing that of related non-planar derivatives [39]. To couple strong GQ recognition with direct optical detection, L1H1-7OTD was conjugated with a Cy5 fluorophore, making L1Cy5-7OTD uniquely suited for quantifying binding stoichiometry and dynamics. Using single-molecule Förster resonance energy transfer (smFRET), we previously reported that L1Cy5-7OTD binds to a single telomeric GQ in primarily a 1:1 stoichiometry with high affinity and selectivity [38]. In another study, we demonstrated that BODIPY-labeled 7OTD (L1BOD-7OTD) exhibits low-nanomolar affinity for different GQ structures and increases the thermal melting temperature (ΔTm) of telomeric GQ at a comparable level to the unlabeled ligand [40]. These results suggest the fluorophore modification does not substantially alter the ligand’s specificity and affinity for GQ. The studies on L1BOD-7OTD also showed a mix of one and two ligand binding to a single GQ even at 4:1 ligand:DNA concentration ratios. These results collectively suggest that two 7OTD molecules can feasibly bind a subset of GQ molecules at elevated ligand concentrations; although, one ligand binding is the dominant stoichiometry.

In addition to such fluorophore-conjugated synthetic ligands, several naturally fluorescent molecules have been employed to monitor GQ folding. Among them, N-methyl mesoporphyrin IX (NMM) displays remarkable selectivity for GQs, particularly for the parallel conformation [41–43]. The fluorescence of NMM is strongly enhanced upon stacking onto the G-tetrads [44], and it preferentially binds to GQ over duplex DNA [45]. These characteristics make NMM a sensitive probe for detecting GQ structures and interrogating their accessibility.

Despite recent advances [46–52], how structural features of long telomeric overhangs, such as number of G-Tracts or inter-GQ spacing, affect ligand binding and accessibility remain largely unexplored. Most prior structural studies have focused on short telomeric repeats (4–7 G-Tracts), which fold into a single intramolecular GQ. However, human telomeric overhangs are substantially longer, 50–300 nt or 8–50 G-Tracts. Considering each GQ requires 24 nt [(TTAGGG)4], 50–300-nt long telomeres may form 2–12 GQs. To address this gap, we employed single-molecule total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy to directly measure the binding stoichiometry of L1Cy5-7OTD on telomeric overhangs of varying lengths and structural features. In addition, we used ensemble fluorescence enhancement assay utilizing NMM to independently validate the single-molecule findings. We also performed computational studies using a lattice model [53, 54] in which the folding stability of different regions of the overhang, stacking interactions between neighboring GQs, and cooperative binding between opposing faces of successive GQs were parametrized to gain insights about the underlying structural features and interactions.

Materials and methods

DNA constructs

We employed a total of 26 different DNA constructs in this study: (i) 12 wild-type telomeric constructs with 4–26 G-tracts; (ii) 7 mutant telomeric sequences that either had longer spacers between GQs or destabilized GQs, and (iii) 7 non-telomeric constructs with more stable GQs and varying length of spacers between GQs.

All DNA oligonucleotides (sequences are given in Supplementary Table S1) were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (Iowa, USA) and further purified by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis prior to use. Partial duplex DNA (pdDNA) constructs (Fig. 1A) were prepared by annealing a short strand (which includes a 5′-biotin and a Cy3) and a long strand, which includes complementary sequences to the short strand, in addition to an overhang of repeating sequences: GG(TTAGGG)nTTAG, where n = 4, 6, 8, …, 26, allowing formation of 1–6 GQs. The short and long strands were mixed in 1:5 molar ratio in the presence of 150 mM KCl and 10 mM MgCl₂ for annealing. MgCl₂ concentration was later reduced to 2 mM for the single-molecule experiments. A higher concentration of longer strand was used to ensure all Cy3-labeled and biotinylated short strands have a long strand partner. The mixture was heated to 95°C for 3 min, followed by controlled cooling to 30°C in 1°C steps with 3 min incubation per step using a PCR (polymerase chain reaction) thermal cycler. Various technical aspects of working with GQ structures using single-molecule fluorescence were described in earlier work [55]. Hybridized constructs form a pdDNA construct with a 28-bp duplex region (which includes two double-stranded telomeric repeats at the junction between double- and single-stranded region) and a free 3′ telomeric overhang (with varying lengths) that is available for ligand binding.

Single-molecule fluorescence microscopy

All single-molecule fluorescence microscopy measurements were carried out using a prism-type TIRF setup equipped with an Olympus IX-71 microscope and an Andor IXON EMCCD camera (IXON DV-887 EMCCD, Oxford Instruments). The IXON camera has 512 × 512 pixels and a pixel size of 16 μm. An Olympus water objective (60x, 1.20 NA) was used to collect the fluorescence signal. The total magnification in our setup is 90x, which results in an effective pixel size of 178 nm. A software (smCamera) developed by the Ha Lab was used for image acquisition and generation of the single-molecule time traces [56]. This software is written in C++ and is available in Github: https://github.com/eerun00/smCamera. Movies of 1000–1500 frames were acquired at 100 ms per frame (10–15 movies per construct).

Imaging chambers were constructed using laser-drilled quartz slides and coverslips. The slides and coverslips were passivated with a mixture of mPEG (PEG-5000, Laysan Bio, Inc.) and biotin PEG (biotin–PEG-5000, Laysan Bio, Inc.), bonded with double-sided tape and sealed with epoxy. Before introducing the pdDNA samples, the microfluidic chamber was treated with 2% (v/v) Tween-20 to reduce non-specific binding. After 5 min incubation, the excess detergent was removed from the chamber with several successive washings and 0.01 mg/ml streptavidin was incubated for 2 min before L1Cy5-7OTD-bound DNA molecules were introduced into the chamber.

Photobleaching step counting assay

The pdDNA constructs were diluted to 400 pM in the presence of 150 mM KCl and 2 mM MgCl₂ and mixed with 1 μM L1Cy5-7OTD and 10 nM quencher strand. The quencher strand is added to suppress the fluorescence of unhybridized short strands. Samples were incubated in microcentrifuge tubes for 15 min at room temperature, then introduced into microfluidic chambers and equilibrated for an additional 10 min (Fig. 1B). Excess or unbound DNA and L1Cy5-7OTD were removed by washing the channel with 20 μl of imaging buffer. This washing step also removes L1Cy5-7OTD molecules that might have unstable binding to the DNA molecules. The imaging buffer contains Tris–HCl (50 mM, pH 7.5), 2 mM Trolox, 0.8 mg/ml glucose, 0.1 mg/ml bovine serum albumin, 150 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and gloxy (glucose oxidase and catalase). In a typical smFRET experiment where highest photostability is desired, gloxy is used at 0.1%–1% concentration (1% corresponds to 0.1 mg/ml glucose oxidase, 0.02 mg/ml catalase). However, in the photobleaching step counting assay, this was reduced to 0.01% (1.0 μg/ml glucose oxidase, 0.2 μg/ml catalase) to facilitate photobleaching of fluorophores while keeping them photostable enough to resolve several consecutive photobleaching steps. The single-molecule traces were analyzed using a custom MATLAB code. Statistical analysis and plots were made in Origin.

The photobleaching assay showed a concentration-dependent binding stoichiometry (Supplementary Fig. S1) and the optimal concentration of L1Cy5-7OTD was determined to be 1 μM, beyond which background levels due to non-specific binding to the surface were significant. The 1 μM L1Cy5-7OTD concentration is more than an order of magnitude greater than the dissociation constant of L1Cy5-7OTD [35, 57]. While challenging to estimate experimentally, computational analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1C) suggests reaching 50% occupancy of binding sites with L1Cy5-7OTD would require ∼10 μM L1Cy5-7OTD, which is not feasible with this single-molecule assay due to high background noise, and unlikely to be of physiological relevance.

The pdDNA constructs are labeled with a Cy3 fluorophore, which enabled determining the locations of the DNA molecules on the surface with a green laser beam excitation (532 nm) during the first few seconds of data acquisition (Fig. 1). This was followed by turning off the green laser beam and turning on the red laser beam to image L1Cy5-7OTD molecules until all molecules photobleach (Fig. 1D and E). Only the Cy5-molecules that co-localize with a Cy3 (the surface immobilized DNA molecules) were included in analysis.

The number of L1Cy5-7OTD molecules bound to a telomeric construct was determined by counting discrete photobleaching steps, with each step corresponding to the loss of fluorescence from a single L1Cy5-7OTD molecule. A software called AutoStepFinder was used in this analysis [58]. This software is available on Github: https://github.com/jacobkers/Loeff-Kerssemakers-et-al-AutoStepFinder. Near-complete Cy5 labeling efficiency of L1Cy5-7OTD is ensured during chemical synthesis and purification. To minimize false positives from unhybridized short strands carrying Cy3, a 12-nt long quencher strand—complementary to part of the short strand and containing a broadband quencher at its 3′-end—was added. This quenching step eliminates Cy3 emission from unhybridized molecules, thereby ensuring more reliable quantification of DNA constructs that do not show L1Cy5-7OTD binding, i.e. DNA molecules with zero Cy5 photobleaching steps. Gel electrophoresis experiments suggest ∼15%–30% of the short strands (with Cy3) remain unhybridized after annealing (Supplementary Fig. S2). A large majority of these false-positives are eliminated by inclusion of the quencher strand [59], which minimally disturbs the pdDNA constructs due to already formed 28-bp long duplex segment.

The resulting distributions of photobleaching step analysis are plotted as histograms for each DNA construct (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs S3–S5, and Supplementary Table S5). Each histogram contains data acquired from ~250 DNA molecules. To test the robustness of the results, we accumulated ∼2-fold higher number of photobleaching events on 8GTr, 14GTr, and 24GTr constructs (Supplementary Fig. S6 and Supplementary Table S6). These two sets of the data yielded very similar distributions, supporting the statistical robustness of the results.

Another potential issue associated with the photobleaching step assay is the maximum number of photobleaching steps that can be detected. To establish a range, we studied binding of a short complementary strand to the 16GTr construct. To enhance binding stability, the short strand included three locked nucleic acid (LNA) modifications: TAA +C+C+C TT-Cy5, where +C refers to cytosine with LNA modification. These data (Supplementary Fig. S7 and Supplementary Table S7) show that at least nine consecutive photobleaching events can be detected using this assay.

Circular dichroism measurements

Circular dichroism (CD) measurements (Supplementary Fig. S8) were performed using a Jasco J-1500 CD Spectrophotometer. The wavelength scan was performed with a 1.00-nm bandwidth. The CD melting data were based on the signal at 292 nm and data were acquired after a ±0.1°C temperature stability is attained. A ramp rate of 1 C/min was used. The DNA and small molecule concentrations were 10 µM and 50 µM, respectively. To amplify the effect of the small molecule-induced stabilization and attain maximum resolution between the effects of labeled and unlabeled ligands, the measurements were performed in 50 mM KCl and 1 mM MgCl2 (instead of the 150 mM KCl and 2 mM MgCl2 used in single-molecule experiments).

Impact of fluorophore attachment on small molecule binding

To demonstrate that attaching a fluorophore does not fundamentally alter specific binding of the small molecule to GQ with high affinity, we performed two sets of experiments using L1H1-7OTD (unlabeled) and L1Cy5-7OTD (Cy5-labeled). In the first set of experiments (Supplementary Fig. S8), we demonstrated that thermal melting temperatures of a telomeric GQ shift by similar amounts when incubated with L1H1-7OTD or L1Cy5-7OTD. These CD-based experiments also showed that telomeric GQ is primarily in the hybrid conformation before and after incubation with small molecules; although a shift toward parallel conformation is present after small molecules are introduced. In the second set of experiments (Supplementary Fig. S9 and Supplementary Table S8), we demonstrated that preincubating the DNA molecules with 1 µM unlabeled L1H1-7OTD before introducing 1 µM L1Cy5-7OTD resulted in significant number of photobleaching steps; although fewer than that of the case where DNA was directly mixed with L1Cy5-7OTD. These results demonstrate feasibility of partially displacing L1H1-7OTD by L1Cy5-7OTD, further supporting the comparable affinity and specificity of the two ligands. The results are also consistent with negative cooperativity between successive ligand-binding events as binding of L1H1-7OTD inhibits binding of L1Cy5-7OTD, despite the availability of unoccupied binding sites.

Fluorescence displacement assay

This assay (Supplementary Fig. S9 and Supplementary Table S8) followed the same protocol as photobleaching step counting assay except that the DNA constructs were preincubated with 1 µM unlabeled L1H1-7OTD for 20 min before introducing 1 µM L1Cy5-7OTD.

Fluorescence enhancement assay using NMM

NMM was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific and diluted to 1 mM concentration in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), followed by further dilution in an appropriate buffer. DNA constructs used for NMM studies were identical in sequence and design to those used for the L1Cy5-7OTD assay, except that the short strand lacked the Cy3 to ensure NMM is the only source of fluorescence emission. The pdDNA constructs were diluted to a concentration of 50 nM in an assay buffer containing 150 mM KCl and 2 mM MgCl₂ and mixed with 150 nM NMM in a 96-well plate with black walls and transparent bottom at room temperature. The plate was incubated in dark for 30 min to allow steady-state binding of NMM to the DNA constructs. Fluorescence spectra were acquired using an Agilent BioTek Synergy Neo2 plate reader with an excitation wavelength at 399 nm and emission collected from 580–700 nm (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figs S10 and S11, and Supplementary Tables S9 and S10). Data were analyzed by comparing the fluorescence emission of NMM alone to that of DNA–NMM complexes to quantify the fluorescence enhancement as a function of overhang or spacer length, with increased fluorescence intensity corresponding to greater NMM binding. Due to limited quantities of long telomeric constructs, we were not able to use excess quantities of DNA to maximize NMM fluorescence enhancement. This resulted in relatively smaller fold-increase in NMM fluorescence in the presence of telomeric DNA compared to reports in the literature. To illustrate, we used 1:3 concentration ratio for DNA:NMM in our studies, while reports in the literature suggest NMM fluorescence enhancement continues to increase until ~5:1 DNA:NMM ratio and ~25% of the maximum enhancement is attained at 1:3 ratio [60]. Therefore, the relatively modest enhancement we observe is consistent with these reports.

Computational model

In the absence of small molecules, the equilibrium distribution of folded GQs is described by a simple one-dimensional lattice model, which describes the overhang as a sequence of N TTAGGG repeats. Each repeat can either be unfolded with a statistical weight of 1 or be one of the four consecutive repeats in a folded GQ with overall statistical weight of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{K}_{\mathrm{f}}}$\end{document}. Cooperativity due to interactions between neigboring GQs is modeled with a statisical weight of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{K}_{\mathrm{c}}}$\end{document}. This model of telomeric GQ folding was introduced by Carrino and Mittermaier et al. [61]. By fitting thermal melting data for single stranded DNA with 8–12 repeats (without a double stranded segment on one end), Mittermaier and coworkers reported values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{K}_{\mathrm{f}}} = 59$\end{document} for interior repeats, and enhanced stabilities when GQ’s start or end (from the free ends of the ssDNA) on the last repeat, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{K}_{{\mathrm{f}}1}} = 229$\end{document}, or the next to last repeat, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{K}_{{\mathrm{f}}2}} = 80$\end{document}. They also reported negative cooperativity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{K}_{\mathrm{c}}} \,\lt\, 1)$\end{document} between adjacent GQs, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${{K}_{\mathrm{c}}} = 0.64$\end{document}. In our earlier work [62], this model was used to understand single-molecule data and we found that to match localization patterns of accessible repeats, the stability for GQs at the junction region between double stranded and single stranded DNA (which were not present in Mittermaier study) needed to be significantly reduced compared to the free 3′-end, and that the cooperativity is positive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $({{K}_{\mathrm{c}}}\,\gt\, 1)$\end{document}. Also, there was a range of parameter values consistent with the experimentally observed accessibility patterns.

While preserving the parameters that describe folding and cooperatively of GQs, this model was expanded to include small-molecule binding to GQs in the current study. As suggested by NMR studies [37], the model accommodated binding of two OTD molecules to each GQ, with a weight of t or b for binding to the top or bottom face of the GQ, respectively. The top and bottom nomenclature we use just reflects the face with higher and lower binding affinity, respectively, and follows the aforementioned NMR convention only this respect. As our study uses pdDNA constructs while the NMR study was performed on single stranded telomeres, the naming should not be generalized to structural details. This naming is also visually appealing in reference to the junction between single and double stranded telomere, which creates an asymmetry between the two faces. The face that is close to the junction is called the bottom and the other face is called top, as highlighted in Fig. 4A.

With the inclusion of small molecule binding, the new model fits within a class of lattice models used to describe cooperative ligand binding to one-dimensional double-stranded DNA [63–65]. Due to combinatorial frustration associated with binding at the boundaries of DNA, patterns of binding probabilities develop with a period determined by the ligand’s footprint [66].

Results

A single-molecule fluorescence assay (Fig. 1) was used to determine the stoichiometry of small-molecule binding to telomeric overhangs (see Supplementary Table S1 for sequences). In this assay, stepwise photobleaching of the Cy5 fluorophores within L1Cy5-7OTD molecules was monitored in order to quantify the number of ligand molecules bound to each DNA construct.

To demonstrate that Cy5 attachment does not fundamentally alter specific binding of the small molecule to GQ with high affinity, we performed two sets of experiments using L1H1-7OTD (unlabeled) and L1Cy5-7OTD (Cy5-labeled). In the first set of experiments (Supplementary Fig. S8), we demonstrated that thermal melting temperatures of a telomeric GQ shift by similar amounts when incubated with L1H1-7OTD or L1Cy5-7OTD. These CD-based experiments also showed that telomeric GQ is primarily in the hybrid conformation before and after incubation with small molecules.

In the second set of experiments (Supplementary Fig. S9 and Supplementary Table S8), we demonstrated that preincubating the DNA molecules with 1 µM unlabeled L1H1-7OTD before introducing 1 µM L1Cy5-7OTD resulted in significant number of photobleaching steps; although fewer than that of the case where DNA was directly mixed with L1Cy5-7OTD. These results demonstrate feasibility of partially displacing L1H1-7OTD by L1Cy5-7OTD, further supporting the comparable affinity and specificity of the two ligands. The results are also consistent with negative cooperativity between successive ligand-binding events as binding of L1H1-7OTD inhibits binding of L1Cy5-7OTD, despite the availability of unoccupied binding sites.

Figure 2A shows a compilation of the photobleaching step analysis for 12 DNA constructs with varying telomeric overhang length as a 3D histogram. 2D histograms for each DNA construct are shown in Supplementary Fig. S3. Only DNA constructs that showed at least one L1Cy5-7OTD-binding event were included in this analysis. A similar analysis that includes the DNA constructs that did not show any binding events (zero photobleaching steps) is shown in Supplementary Fig. S4. Data illustrating a potential source for the zero binding events due to imperfect hybridization of DNA strands are presented in Supplementary Fig. S2. Figure 2B and Supplementary Fig. S4B show the average number of L1Cy5-7OTD molecules bound to each DNA construct. In general, the average number of bound molecules increases with overhang length, rising by ∼2.5-fold between 4GTr and 26GTr constructs. Considering that the number of GQs (1–6) increases by 6-fold within this length range, the observed 2.5-fold increase is modest and implies that the tandem GQs do not act as independent (non-interacting) units in terms of their accessibility to L1Cy5-7OTD.

To further explore this issue, we designed mutants of telomeric constructs and non-telomeric constructs in which the unfolded (spacer) regions between consecutive GQs were varied in a controlled manner. In these constructs, GQs were separated by either 3-nt d(TTA) spacers (wild-type sequence) or 9-nt d(TTATTATTA) spacers, which were implemented by replacing every fourth d(TTA) with d(TTATTATTA). Photobleaching step analysis on these constructs revealed that constructs with 9-nt spacers consistently accommodated binding of ∼30% more L1Cy5-7OTD molecules than their 3-nt spacer counterparts (Fig. 3A and B). Similarly, we designed non-telomeric (3L1L) constructs that contained more stable GQs formed by (GGGT) repeats. Two groups of constructs were created in which we (i) increased the number of GGGT repeats (n = 14, 18, 22, or 26 repeats) while keeping 3-nt spacer between successive GQs; or (ii) kept n = 18 repeats but varied the separation between GQs with 3-nt, 6-nt, or 9-nt spacers (1–3 repeats of TTA). Data on these constructs show a systematic increase of L1Cy5-7OTD binding as the spacer length (Supplementary Fig. S10A and Supplementary Table S9) or the number of GGGT repeats (Supplementary Fig. S11A and Supplementary Table S10) is increased. Overall, these results demonstrate that increasing the distance between GQs alleviates steric hindrance and enhances ligand accessibility.

In another experiment, we created less stable mutant telomeric sequences for the 14GTr and 24GTr constructs in which the middle guanines in the first four and last four G-tracts were changed to adenines, i.e. GGG mutated to GAG (Supplementary Fig. S5 and Supplementary Table S5). These constructs effectively reduce the number of G-tracts by eight and result in reduced binding of L1Cy5-7OTD. These results demonstrate the specific binding of L1Cy5-7OTD to the GQ structures and the correlation between binding stoichiometry and number of stable GQ structures.

To independently verify GQ formation and accessibility, we employed NMM, a selective GQ probe whose fluorescence is enhanced upon binding to GQ (Fig. 3C) [41]. Therefore, the fluorescence emission intensity of NMM can be used as a qualitative gauge for small molecule binding to telomeric overhangs. NMM emission spectra showed markedly higher fluorescence intensity in the presence of telomeric GQs compared to NMM alone (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, the NMM fluorescence emission signal was consistently greater (∼2-fold based on the peak amplitude at 610 nm) in constructs with the 9-nt spacers compared to respective constructs with 3-nt spacers (Fig. 3D). Similar experiments were performed with the non-telomeric 3L1L constructs and showed increasing NMM signal with increasing spacer length between GQs (Supplementary Fig. S10B) and with increasing number of GQs separated by a fixed 3-nt spacer (Supplementary Fig. S11B).

In order to better understand the implications of these experimental results in terms of underlying interactions and structural details, we performed computational studies using a transfer matrix model. Discussion

Our experimental data and computational studies demonstrate that both the length of the overhang and the spacer length between GQs are critical determinants of the GQ accessibility. In particular, Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S10 highlight the role of spacer length. Constructs with 3-nt spacers between GQs likely adopt compact, stacked structures that sterically restrict access to the GQ surfaces. By contrast, longer spacers reduce these stacking interactions and expose additional binding interfaces for small-molecules. Together, these findings highlight the structural complexity of telomeric overhangs: whereas repeat number dictates the maximum number of GQs, spacer length (unfolded regions) and stacking interactions modulate accessibility. Such structural effects are likely to be critical in vivo, where overhang folding heterogeneity and protein interactions may similarly control the binding efficiency of GQ ligands.

The distribution of binding stoichiometries in Figs 2A and 4B can be further analyzed to deduce structural information beyond average number of bound ligands (average molecular occupancy in the computational model terminology). The 4GTr construct (one GQ) displayed a maximum of two photobleaching steps, the 8GTr construct (two GQs) showed up to four photobleaching steps and the 12GTr construct (three GQs) reached six photobleaching steps. These results align with NMR and ESI–MS studies [37, 40] indicating that two OTD molecules can bind to a single GQ, one to each of the top and bottom faces, with a significant preference for one of these tetrads. Interestingly, we did not observe more than six photobleaching steps for any of the constructs we studied, even though the fraction of molecules exhibiting six photobleaching steps continued to increase with overhang length. To illustrate, the fraction of six photobleaching steps was six-fold higher in the 20GTr compared to the 12GTr (shortest overhang that can accommodate six L1Cy5-7OTD molecules). However, the increase in the fraction of six-steps between 20GTr to 26GTr was within uncertainties of our measurements. While six may not be an absolute maximum and restricted by experimental limitations, we propose that stacking of adjacent GQs in longer overhangs makes it increasingly challenging for more small molecules to access their binding sites. This results in the number of bound molecules being at a level that is significantly lower than the theoretical maximum.

Our study argues against telomeric overhangs being described by a beads-on-a-string type model where GQs are treated as non-interacting units. In such a model, the average number of bound ligands would increase linearly with increasing number of GQs and all binding sites would be occupied by a ligand until maximum occupancy is reached. Our data suggest interactions between neighboring GQs and spacer length restrict ligand accessibility and prevent them from reaching maximum occupancy. Another factor in this context is two binding sites on each GQ for the OTD ligands. The beads-on-a-string model would require each bead having two binding sites that are independent of each other. Our computational model suggests there is negative cooperativity between binding to the two faces and that there is significant asymmetry between binding to the top face compared to the bottom face (also supported by NMR studies) [37]. This is supported by our data on 4GTr and 6GTr constructs in which single step photobleaching is significantly more dominant than two step photobleaching even though the small molecule concentration is more than an order of magnitude greater than the dissociation constant. Similar conclusions can be made based on BODIPY-labeled of L1H1-7OTD (L1BOD-7OTD), which showed a mix of 1:1 and 2:1 binding stoichiometry to telomeric GQ even when ligand:GQ concentration ratios were 4:1 [40]. Therefore, in addition to interactions between neighboring GQs, interactions between the two faces of each GQ also argue against beads-on-a-string type model. We propose interactions between GQs and asymmetry between binding to the two faces of each GQ to be the main reasons for not reaching maximum ligand occupancy under our assay conditions. It is possible that we would reach two-ligands per GQ at very high ligand concentrations; however, such concentrations are not accessible in our measurements due to elevated background noise from non-specific binding of the fluorescent molecules to the surface. Also, such ligand concentrations are probably too high to be of physiological relevance for potential drugs. To obtain an estimate for relevant ligand concentrations, we performed a computational analysis for the 24GTr and 26GTr constructs (Supplementary Fig. S1C). This analysis suggests the ligand concentration has to be ∼10 µM to reach 50% occupancy and >100 µM to reach 80% occupancy.

Another interesting outcome of the photobleaching step analysis is the consistently higher average number of bound molecules for [\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $4n + 2$\end{document}]GTr constructs compared to those for [\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $4n$\end{document}]GTr, where n represents the maximum number of GQs the construct can form. For example, both 12GTr and 14GTr can form up to 3 GQs (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n = 3$\end{document}), but the average number of bound L1Cy5-7OTD molecules (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\langle {{{n}_b}} \rangle$\end{document}) is greater in 14GTr (2.1 ± 0.1 versus 1.5 ± 0.1). This pattern is consistent across all constructs and could be due to (i) on average more GQ molecules forming in the [\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $4n + 2$\end{document}]GTr constructs; or (ii) the two additional repeats in [\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $4n + 2$\end{document}]GTr constructs functioning as flexible spacers and enhancing ligand access to otherwise low-accessibility GQ surfaces. Interestingly, a supporting evidence for this mechanism can be deduced from a comparison of the 4GTr and 6GTr data in which only one- or two-step photobleaching is possible and the 6GTr construct demonstrates ∼2-fold higher fraction of two-step events compared to the 4GTr. This particular observation cannot be explained with a greater fraction of overhangs folding into GQ, since that would have impacted the 1-step and 2-step events similarly. Instead, we propose this to be due to localization of the unfolded G-Tracts between the duplex stem and the GQ [67] and thereby enhancing accessibility of the GQ to small-molecules. Our computational studies support this second mechanism and argue against formation of a greater number of GQs in [\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $4n + 2$\end{document}]GTr constructs being the dominant mechanism.

This study establishes stepwise photobleaching analysis as a single-molecule approach for quantifying the stoichiometry of small-molecule binding to telomeric GQs and introduces transfer matrix calculations as a versatile computational framework for modeling these interactions. Using L1Cy5-7OTD as a small molecule ligand, we demonstrate that binding scales with the number of GQs but remains consistently below the theoretical maximum, saturating at six molecules under our assay conditions. Based on supporting evidence from our computational model, we attribute this behavior primarily to structural compaction of the overhang, asymmetry between binding to top and bottom faces of GQ, and negative cooperativity between ligands bound to top and bottom faces of successive GQs. Furthermore, we show that additional G-tracts, beyond complete GQs (e.g. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $[ {4n + 2} ]$\end{document}GTr) act as flexible spacers, especially near the junction region, enhancing binding probability and potentially alleviating steric hindrance. Spacer-length variation supports this principle: constructs with 9-nt spacers between successive GQs consistently accommodated more L1Cy5-7OTD and NMM molecules than those with 3-nt spacers. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that telomeric overhang architecture governs ligand accessibility, providing mechanistic insight that may guide the rational design of GQ-targeting anticancer agents and telomerase inhibitors.
