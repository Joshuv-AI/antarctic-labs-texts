Polymorphism of the dihydropteroate synthase resistance gene of Plasmodium falciparum in three hospitals in Mbouda, Western Cameroon
Authors: Noumedem Anangmo Christelle Nadia, Yamssi Cedric, Guemegne Anaiss Patricia Line, Tchuenkam Kom Pacome, Gamago Nkadeu Guy Armand, Noumedem Kenfack Jaurès Anaud, Djeussi Doriane Esther, Guela Djoukou Mba Edna, Kana Tsague Yval, Mpoame Mbida, Zhong Fuyou, Haibo Hu

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria, is an endemic disease which causes significant mortality worldwide, particularly in Africa, including Cameroon. This study aimed to characterize the Pfdhps resistance gene of Plasmodium falciparum in three hospitals in Mbouda Cameroon.MethodTwo hundred and ninety-six samples were collected from consenting patients for rapid diagnostic tests (RDT) and thick blood smear (TBS) to determine parasite intensity. Positive samples were spotted on Whatman paper for molecular analysis. Plasmodial DNA was extracted using the Chelex-100 method, and amplification was performed via nested PCR. The A437G and A581G mutations were identified using restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) with AvaII and BstUI enzymes, followed by 1.3% agarose gel electrophoresis. Data were analyzed using SPSS 21 software.ResultsThe prevalence of Plasmodium falciparum was 25.34%. The Pfdhps resistance gene prevalence was 74.66%, with A437G 56% and A581G 64% mutations. the age group [6–35] are the majority carriers of the gene and females (77.8%) have a higher prevalence of the gene than males (71.8%). Mutant alleles showed higher frequencies (57.14% for A437G and 91.66% for A581G) compared to wild-type alleles (42.85% for A437G and 8.33% for A581G). The mutant alleles of the two mutations are more predominant in the age group [6–35] and in females for A437G and in males for A581G.ConclusionThe Pfdhps resistance gene was prevalent in Mbouda, with high rates of A437G and A581G mutations. These findings highlight the need for enhanced epidemiological surveillance of antimalarial resistance, particularly in Mbouda.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40794-025-00278-9.Keywords: Malaria, Resistance, Pfdhps, Mbouda, Cameroon

Introduction

Malaria is a disease caused by a blood parasite of the genus Plasmodium. This disease remains a major public health challenge worldwide, particularly in Africa [1]. The parasite is transmitted by the bite of a mosquito of the genus Anopheles. According to the World Health Organization, in 2021 there were approximately 241 million cases of malaria and 627,000 deaths worldwide [2]. In 2020, 95% of malaria cases and 96% of malaria-related deaths were recorded in Africa, with 80% of deaths affecting children under the age of 5 [3]. Antimalarial drug has been developed to eradicate this disease, but Plasmodium parasites have developed mechanisms of resistance to escape the action of these synthetic molecules. The rapid development of resistance to numerous antimalarial drugs (chloroquine, proguanil, or mefloquine), including more recent ones atovaquone-proguanil combination or artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) and sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine, underscores the need for continued research efforts. Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine is the molecule recommended by the WHO for malaria prevention and is widely used in pregnant women starting from the second trimester of pregnancy [4]. ACT, introduced to the market to solve this resistance problem, is currently facing the emergence of P. falciparum strains that are resistant to it. This resistance was first observed in western Cambodia in 2009 [5].

Certain genetic mutations, particularly those in P. falciparum dihydrofolate reductase (Pfdhfr) and dihydropteroate synthase (Pfdhps), have been associated with resistance to pyrimethamine and sulfadoxine. It should also be noted that a combination of Pfdhps and Pfdhfr mutant alleles confers clinical resistance to Sulfadoxine-Pyrimethamine (SP) [6]. Similarly, mutations in the Pfdhps codons (S436A/F, A437G, K540E, A581G and A613S/T) and Pfdhfr codons (C50R, N51I, C59R, S108N, I164L) have been associated with resistance to SP [7]. When the prevalence of Pfdhps K540E mutations is greater than 95% and that of Pfdhps A581G is greater than 10%, the World Health Organization recommends discontinuing the marketing of IPTp-SP [8]. Despite the efforts made by the WHO in the fight against malaria through the development of various drugs, including sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine, which is the most effective in Africa, several studies have reported its ineffectiveness in combating this disease.

Mutations occurring at codons 437 and 581 within the dihydropteroate synthase (Pfdhps) gene of Plasmodium falciparum are recognized as pivotal indicators of sulfadoxine resistance. The A437G substitution constitutes an early and prevalent alteration that imparts moderate resistance to sulfadoxine [9], whereas the A581G mutation is correlated with elevated levels of resistance and therapeutic failure, particularly when its frequency surpasses 5% within a population [10]. Consequently, the ongoing surveillance of the prevalence of these mutations is imperative to assess the sustained efficacy of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) in malaria prevention initiatives such as intermittent preventive treatment during pregnancy (IPTp) and in infancy (IPTi). In certain P. falciparum populations, this mutation is observed either concomitantly or as an alternative to A581G, suggesting that it may assume a similar role in contributing to SP resistance [11]. The increasing identification of A431V underscores the dynamic genetic diversity of P. falciparum in response to pharmacological pressure and emphasizes the necessity for regional monitoring of Pfdhps polymorphisms to inform malaria control strategies across Africa.

Few studies have demonstrated resistance of Plasmodium falciparum to sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine in Africa, despite increasing resistance in Cameroon [12–14]. The objective of this study was to determine the polymorphism of Pfdhps gene in order to improve malaria management and develop strategies to combat this disease.

Materials and methods

Study design and study site

This study was a cross-sectional and analytic study.This study was conducted in three hospitals in the district (Fiela, the district hospital and the Protestant hospital in Montchio) of Mbouda, a municipality in Cameroon and capital of the Bamboutos department in the West Region (Fig. 1), approximately twenty kilometres from Bafoussam.

Map locating Mbouda in Cameroon [15]

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study participants were individuals of all age groups and both sexes who presented to the laboratory for malaria diagnosis during the study period. After providing free and informed consent (or parental/guardian consent for minors), they were enrolled in the study. Participants were recruited consecutively as they reported for malaria testing, regardless of the reason for testing, provided they met the inclusion criteria. Individuals with a known history or clinical evidence of haemolytic disease were excluded from participation.

Sampling

The minimum sample size was calculated based on an expected prevalence of 26.75% among urinary schistosomiasis–malaria coinfected children in the West Region of Cameroon [16], using standard epidemiological formulas for cross-sectional studies [17]. Considering a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, the required sample size was estimated to be 296 participants.

Parasitological analysis

Venous blood samples were collected from each participant by trained laboratory personnel under aseptic conditions. A portion of the blood sample was promptly utilized for parasitological examination using Plasmodium falciparum-specific rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) (SD Bioline Malaria Ag P.f, Standard Diagnostics Inc., Republic of Korea) and light microscopy. The remaining blood was spotted onto Whatman® filter paper to obtain dried blood spots (DBS) for molecular analysis. The DBS samples were air-dried, individually packed in sealed plastic bags with desiccant, and stored at room temperature until DNA extraction. Three drops of buffer were added to the buffer area. Finally, after fifteen minutes, the results were read. RDTs that showed two lines, one for the test and one for the control, were considered positive, while those that showed only one line were considered negative. When the control line did not appear, the test was considered invalid and had to be repeated using another RDT cassette. Molecular analysis

DNA extraction

DNA extraction was performed using the chelex method described by Musapa et al. [19]. Initially, the Whatman paper on which the blood had been spotted was cut with sterile scissors and placed in tubes containing a 20% Tween solution. It was then shaken for 30 s and placed in an incubator at 4 °C for a minimum of 16 h. The tubes were vortexed for 15 s, after which the Tween solution was removed using a pipette. Each tube was then filled with 1 ml of PBS, followed by incubation at 4 °C for 15 min before the PBS was removed. Finally, a 20% chelex solution was prepared and added to each tube. The tubes were vortexed for approximately 15 s and heated in a water bath for 10 min on three occasions. After this, the tubes were centrifuged for 5 min, and the supernatant was transferred to another tube, which was then centrifuged again. The supernatant was transferred to a final labelled tube.

DNA amplification by nested PCR

The Pfdhps gene was amplify according to the method described by Ali et al. [20]. The total reaction volume included 5 µl of nuclease-free water (NFW), 3.8 µl of 2X Master Mix with standard buffer (New England Biolabs, MA, USA), 0.2 µl of each primer (R2, R/), and 0.8 µl of DNA extract. Agarose gel electrophoresis

DNA was observed using 1.3% agarose gel prepared using 1X TBE. It was then cooled to approximately 60 °C, and 8.8 µl of SafeView was added, stirred, and the gel was poured to a thickness of 5 mm. The migration was performed at 150 V during 30 min. At the end of the electrophoresis the gel was visualized under UV light using a transilluminator.

Restriction fragment length polymorphism of the Pfdhps gene

The method described by Ali et al. [20] was used for the A437G fragment, the enzyme used was Ava II, and for A581G, it was BstUI (Biolabs) as seen on Table 2. For a total volume of 10 µl, the solution consisted of 5 µl of nuclease-free water, 3.9 µl (A437G) or 3.8 µl (A581G) of Nest 2 amplicons, and 0.1 µl (BstUI) or 0.2 µl (Ava II) of enzyme, incubated at 37 °C for A437G and 60 °C for A581G for 1 h.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance for this study was obtained from the Regional Ethics Committee for Human Health Research of the West Region, under registration number No: 452/26/02/2025/CE/CRERSH-OU/VP. Prior to sample collection, the objectives, benefits, and potential risks of the study were clearly explained to all participants in a language they understood. Written informed consent was obtained from each adult participant. For minors, assent was obtained from the children when possible, and written consent was provided by their parents or legal guardians. Confidentiality of participants’ data was strictly maintained throughout the study, and all samples were coded to ensure anonymity. Participants who were diagnosed positive for parasitic infections were referred to appropriate healthcare facilities for medical follow-up and treatment according to the national guidelines.

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed by estimating the proportions and frequencies of variables using Microsoft Excel 2013 and SPSS 23 software. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the main risk factors, and the chi-square independence test was used to assess associations between risk factors. A p-value less than 5% (p < 0.05) with an Odds Ratio > 1 was considered statistically significant at a 95% confidence interval.

Allelic frequencies of A437G and A581G mutations according to knowledge, attitudes, and practices

Table 11 shows the allelic frequencies of the A437G mutation according to knowledge, attitudes, and practices. It appears that the mutant allele was more frequent among individuals with knowledge of malaria, its modes of transmission, and its signs and symptoms (53.28%), as well as among those using insecticide-treated mosquito nets (38%), those not using insecticides (47.6%), and those not wearing protective clothing (45.2%). The A581G mutant allele was more frequent among individuals with knowledge of malaria (85.4%), its modes of transmission (83.3%), and its signs and symptoms (85.4%), as well as among those using insecticide-treated mosquito nets, those not using insecticides (66.7%), and those not wearing protective clothing (25%). The same applied to the wild-type allele, which was more frequent among individuals with the same knowledge and practices mentioned above but at different percentages.

Discussion

The prevalence of Plasmodium falciparum infection was 25.34%. This rate contrasts with the findings of Laure et al. [24], who reported a malaria prevalence of 33.1% in Yaoundé, Cameroon. However, it aligns with the study by Nyasa et al. [21]. conducted in 2021 in Nkongo-Mbeng (Southwest Cameroon), which analyzed malaria prevalence trends and associated risk factors. In our study, children aged 0–5 years had the highest infection rate (37%), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.27). This high rate among young children contradicts the findings of Dosoo et al. [22]. in the Accra Region of Ghana, who reported a prevalence of only 3.3% in children aged 6–10 years. This disparity may be due to the heightened vulnerability of children under five, attributed to their immature immune systems and greater exposure in endemic areas. According to the World Malaria Report [23], children under five accounted for approximately 76% of malaria-related deaths in 2022 in Africa, the region with the most intense transmission.

Males had a slightly higher prevalence rate (33.3%) than females, though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.17). This aligns with findings from Doutoum et al. [24]. in Chad, where the male infection rate (38.6%) was higher than that of females (35%) without statistical significance. This slight male predominance may be due to behavioral factors, such as greater outdoor exposure during agricultural or night-time activities, or outdoor play for children. However, the lack of statistical significance suggests that sex is not a determining factor in this population. These observations are consistent with WHO conclusions (2023), which indicate that malaria affects both sexes similarly in high-transmission areas, although contextual differences may arise from socioeconomic roles or local behaviors.

A statistically significant difference (p = 0.01) was found in education level, with a higher infection positivity rate among individuals with no formal education (42.4%). This highlights the impact of education on malaria prevention behaviors. Uneducated individuals may have limited access to information about preventive measures such as ITN use, early diagnosis, or access to ACT (artemisinin-based combination therapies).

In a study conducted by INwaneli et al. [25] in Nigeria, the prevalence rates of malaria were documented as 16.7%, 26.7%, 29.9%, and 46.2% among children aged less than 5 years, from 5 to less than 10 years, from 10 to less than 15 years, and from 15 to 17 years, respectively. This high rate among the uneducated suggests that health education campaigns should target this population to reduce disparities in malaria prevalence. A statistically significant difference (p = 0.000) was found in insecticide use, with higher infection rates among those not using insecticides (31.8%). N’Guessang et al. [31] confirmed that the absence of insecticide use increases Anopheles mosquito numbers, thereby increasing malaria risk. This high prevalence rate among non-users reflects the effectiveness of insecticides in reducing malaria transmission. Insecticides decrease mosquito density and their capacity to transmit Plasmodium. According to WHO (2023), indoor residual spraying (IRS) with long-lasting insecticides is among the most effective interventions in high-transmission areas, although growing resistance poses challenges.

A significant difference (p = 0.001) was also observed with respect to wearing protective clothing, with a much higher infection rate (54.2%) among individuals not wearing protective garments. A study by Tchouakui et al. [26]. emphasized the importance of long clothing (e.g., long sleeves and pants) in reducing exposure to mosquito bites, especially during periods of peak mosquito activity at night.

This study established a frequency Pfdhps resistance marker at 74.66%. The high prevalence of A437G (56%) and A581G (64%) mutations aligns with reports from Nigeria [27], Ghana [28], and Sierra Leone [29], the mutant allele in the Pfdhps inducing resistance to SP. While the I431V mutation has only recently been characterized, it is increasingly recognized as an important emerging Pfdhps variant in West and Central Africa. This mutation often occurs together with A437G and A581G, forming multi-mutant haplotypes that may contribute to higher-level SP resistance [9]. Recent molecular surveillance has reported rising frequencies of I431V in several parts of Cameroon and neighboring countries, suggesting regional spread of this allele [30, 31]. Although its direct effect on SP resistance is still under investigation, its consistent association with known resistance markers indicates that it may enhance or stabilize resistant haplotypes [31]. The growing prevalence of I431V therefore warrants close monitoring, as its expansion could signal the evolution of new, more resistant P. falciparum lineages with implications for the continued use of SP in IPTp and seasonal chemoprevention programs.

However, our findings are lower than those reported by Tuedom et al. [12]. in Cameroon, who found a 90% prevalence of the A437G mutation and a low prevalence of A581G. In contrast, Jian et al. [32]. in Guinea found a 90.51% prevalence for A437G and only 0.63% for A581G. The high prevalence of A437G compromises the efficacy of SP for IPTp and treatment, while A581G is associated with prophylaxis failure. However, low A581G levels may suggest that SP could still be effective for IPTp in certain areas despite resistance. The high resistance gene prevalence may be due to misuse or prolonged use of SP by patients. Individuals aged 6–35 had the highest prevalence of the Pfdhps resistance gene (86%), with statistical significance (p = 0.001). This finding aligns with Madkhali et al. [6], who found a higher prevalence of Pfdhfr and Pfdhps mutations in individuals under 30 years. The high prevalence among 6–35-year-olds may reflect more frequent exposure to SP-based antimalarial treatments, especially among children and young adults, suggesting a selective pressure favoring resistant strains. No statistically significant difference (p = 0.552) was observed between sexes, although females (77.8%) had a higher prevalence than males. A 2022 study by Ocan et al. [33]. found a higher prevalence of the Pfcrt gene in males, contrasting our results. This female predominance may be due to increased SP exposure through IPTp (intermittent preventive treatment during pregnancy). While the trend observed in our study could reflect such exposure, the lack of significance indicates only a weak association. Likewise, no significant difference (p = 0.309) was found based on place of residence, though urban residents showed a higher gene prevalence (79.1%). This may be due to increased antimalarial drug use and better treatment access in urban areas. High population density may also promote transmission of resistant strains, despite the lack of statistical significance. Modern-house residents (76.2%) had higher resistance gene prevalence than those in non-modern homes (72.7%), but this difference was not significant (p = 0.732). This contradicts findings by Nundu et al. [34], who reported a 32.8% prevalence of the Pfcrt gene in rural residents in a study on malaria epidemiology in children under five in Kinshasa, DRC. The difference may be due to urban population growth and more frequent SP use over time.

Mutations are represented by two types of alleles: wild-type and mutant. In our study, for the A437G mutation, mutant alleles had a frequency of 57.14%, the highest, while wild-type alleles had a lower frequency of 42.85%. For A581G, the most frequent allele was the mutant at 91.66%, while wild-types were at 8.33%. The mutant allele frequency for Pfdhps A437G is similar to that found by Madkhali et al. [6], who studied the increasing prevalence of Pfdhfr and Pfdhps mutations linked to SP resistance in P. falciparum isolates from Jazan, Saudi Arabia, and found 43.7% wild-type and 56.3% mutant. For A581G, our findings contradict their study, which reported 100% wild-type and 0% mutant. They attributed differences in allele frequency to variations in isolate origin.

The mutant Pfdhps A437G allele frequency may be due to frequent genetic recombination during mitosis in the P. falciparum life cycle. The A581G mutant frequency could result from fewer mitoses among parasite clones. The mutant allele was most prevalent in the 6–35 age group for both mutations, while wild-types were lowest (2.7% for 6–35 and >35 age groups). Bohissou et al. [8] noted increased Pfdhps mutations after seasonal chemoprevention, often targeted at young people, which may explain these frequencies.

In accordance with the World Health Organization’s Guidelines for Malaria [35], the therapeutic efficacy of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) for intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp-SP) is preserved even in regions where resistance markers are prevalent, provided that the prevalence of Pfdhps A581G remains below 10%. Conversely, when the prevalence of A581G surpasses this threshold, particularly in combination with elevated frequencies of Pfdhps K540E, there is a significant reduction in the prophylactic effectiveness of SP. Our investigation reveals that the prevalence of A581G (64%) greatly exceeds this critical threshold, indicating that the efficacy of SP for IPTp and other preventive strategies may be considerably compromised in Mbouda. According to current WHO recommendations, an A581G prevalence exceeding 5% signals a substantial decline in SP’s preventive efficacy. In our setting, where A581G prevalence far exceeds this limit, the protective benefit of IPTp-SP is likely to be significantly reduced. This suggests that pregnant women in Mbouda may receive limited protection against malaria despite adherence to IPTp-SP schedules. Exceeding this threshold also implies a heightened risk of malaria-related maternal anemia, low birth weight, and neonatal complications. From a policy standpoint, these findings underscore the urgent need to re-evaluate the continued reliance on SP as a single preventive regimen. Strengthening pharmacovigilance, expanding molecular surveillance of resistance markers, and piloting alternative or complementary interventions such as the use of dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine for IPTp or integrating vector control strategies should be considered to maintain effective protection for pregnant women. In addition, health authorities should ensure timely dissemination of updated guidance to clinicians and maternal health programs to adapt preventive strategies in response to rising SP resistance.

This observation underscores the pressing requirement for the ongoing surveillance of molecular resistance markers and the assessment of alternative preventive strategies for pregnant women and other susceptible populations.

Limitation of the study

No investigation was carried out on other molecular markers, which would have helped deepen the understanding of resistance mechanisms and support the development of more suitable treatments.

Conclusion

This study highlights a significant presence of mutations associated with resistance to sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP). They underscore the importance of continuous monitoring of Plasmodium falciparum resistance to antimalarials in order to anticipate changes in drug sensitivity.
