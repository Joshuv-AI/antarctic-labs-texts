Alternative oncogenic drivers and sensitivity to BTK inhibition in CRISPR/Cas gene-edited human DLBCL cell models
Authors: Janneke A Eken, Fleur R M Havenaar, Ruben A L de Groen, Edwin Quinten, Hailiang Mei, Julieta H Sepúlveda-Yáñez, Marcelo A Navarrete, Hans G Drexler, Joost S P Vermaat, Cornelis A M van Bergen, Hendrik Veelken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Only a potent CARD11 mutation is an alternative oncogenic driver to autonomous BCR signaling in ABC-DLBCL.•Prediction of responsiveness to BTK inhibition requires exact knowledge of the molecular mechanisms of BCR pathway activation in ABC-DLBCL.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most frequently diagnosed hematologic malignancy and follows an aggressive clinical course.1, 2, 3 Standard immunochemotherapy with the R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, Oncovin [vincristine], and prednisone) regimen cures a substantial fraction of patients with DLBCL, but ∼35% of patients eventually succumb to their disease. DLBCL is a highly heterogeneous disease and can be divided based on gene expression profiling into 2 subtypes according to the putative cell of origin: activated B-cell–like (ABC) and germinal center B-cell–like (GCB) DLBCL.4, 5, 6 Genomic profiling permits distinguishing 5 to 7 DLBCL clusters, with variable reliability of assignment of an individual case to a particular cluster.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12

The various DLBCL subtypes depend on different growth and survival signals caused by genetic events that activate distinct cellular pathways constitutively. In particular, chronic active B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling causes constitutive NF-κB activation in ABC-DLBCL, particularly in the genetic clusters MCD/C5B.4,8,10,12,13 The BCR pathway can be activated in ABC-DLBCL through binding to autoantigens or by autonomous BCR signaling through atypical homodimeric interactions of the BCR immunoglobulin chains without requirement for external antigens.14, 15, 16 However, signaling originating at the BCR can currently not directly be incorporated in DLBCL subtyping based on genetic clustering. Numerous gain-of-function mutations in activators or loss-of-function alterations in members of the BCR signaling cascade contribute to chronic active BCR signaling.13,17 In addition, BCR signaling merges with Toll-like receptor(TLR) signaling activated by the recurrent MYD88L265P mutation in the endolysosome at the central My-T–BCR signaling hub.13,17,18

However, these insights into ABC-DLBCL pathogenesis are insufficient to accurately predict the efficacy of targeted therapeutic agents in individual patients with DLBCL. An important example of insufficient predictive power of genetic clustering concerns the use of Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors. In relapsed ABC-DLBCL, monotherapy with the BTK inhibitor ibrutinib resulted in an objective response rate of 37%.19 Even archetypical MCD/C5B cases with co-occurrence of canonical MYD88 and CD79B mutations can fail to respond, and DLBCL expressing MYD88L265P but wild-type (WT) CD79 appear resistant to BTK inhibition.17,19

Gain-of-function mutations in BCR pathway members acting downstream of BTK are obvious candidates to cause resistance to BTK inhibition. A conspicuous candidate is CARD11, member of the CARD11-BCL10-MALT1 (CBM) signalosome complex in the BCR signaling cascade.17 Gain-of-function mutations in CARD11 occur recurrently in DLBCL. However, individual CARD11 mutations have quite different oncogenic potency as indicated by activation of NF-κB reporter constructs and the ability to replace each other.20 Furthermore, DLBCL with robust autonomous BCR signaling can carry gain-of-function mutations in CARD11, as well as in MYD88 and CD79B, raising the question of the contribution of each of these drivers in individual cases as the basis for predicting the efficacy of BTK inhibitors.15

Here, we developed cellular DLBCL systems based on accurate CRIPR/Cas-mediated gene editing to clarify the quantitative contribution of mechanistically defined drivers and to develop tools to accurately predict sensitivity to BTK inhibitors. As a first and paradigmatic aim, we aimed to directly demonstrate autonomous BCR signaling as a “paraphysiological” mechanism with equal oncogenic potency as strong gain-of-function mutations by reciprocal exchange.

Methods

Experimental model

Karpas 422 (Research Resource Identifier [RRID]: CVCL_2210), OCI-Ly3 (RRID: CVCL_8800), and TMD8 (RRID: CVCL_A442) were obtained from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures and cultured in Iscove modified Dulbecco medium (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Bodinco, Alkmaar, The Netherlands), 3mM l-glutamine (Lonza), and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Lonza). For OCI-Ly3, medium was substituted with 10% human serum (Sanquin). Cell line authentication was performed by whole-exome sequencing (supplemental Methods), and we monitored for mycoplasma contamination. Fresh-frozen biopsies of histologically confirmed DLBCL samples originate from the Leiden University Medical Center pathology archive. This was approved by the ethics committee (B16.048) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Gene editing

For homology-directed repair (HDR), CARD11 exons were targeted with the CRISPR/cas9 ribonuclear protein (RNP) nickase (IDT, Coralville, IA) and single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide template DNA (supplemental Table 1). For gene knockout (KO) by nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ), CARD11 exon 5 and BCR were targeted with WT cas9 nuclease RNPs. RNPs (and single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide) were (co)transfected by electroporation (NEON transfection system; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA; supplemental Methods). For retroviral transduction, Phoenix-GALV were transfected with vectors (supplemental Table 2) by lipofection (FuGeneHD; Promega, Madison, WI). DLBCL cell lines were transduced on fibronectin-coated (RN; Takara, Shiga, Japan) culture plates (Greiner Bio, Gremsmünster, Austria) with supernatant for 48 hours.21, 22, 23

Flow cytometry

For extracellular staining, 1 × 105 cells were stained with Zombie NIR (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) and antibodies specific for immunoglobulin M (IgM), IgG, IgK, IgL, Nerve Growth Factor Receptor (NGFR), and/or CD79B at 4°C for 20 minutes (supplemental Table 3).

For phosphoflow analysis, 1 × 105 cells were stained with Zombie NIR for 10 minutes at 37°C. After cross-linking with anti-IgM, anti-IgG, or phosphate-buffered saline for 5 minutes, cells were fixed, permeabilized (Foxp3 transcription factor staining buffer set; eBioscience, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and stained with anti-pPI3K or anti-pCD79A at room temperature for 20 minutes.

For NF-κB activation analysis with a reporter assay, 1 × 105 cells transduced with cyan fluorescent protein (CFP) reporter under control of the NF-κB response element24 and were stained with Sytox Blue (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before measurement. Data were analyzed using FlowJo V.10 software (Ashland, OR).

Pharmacokinetic sensitivity assay

A total of 1.5 × 104 cells per well were cultured in a flat-bottom 96-well plate with titrated concentrations of acalabrutinib for 92 hours, or the MALT1 inhibitor MI225 for 48 hours, followed by addition of 20 μL of MTS dye (Promega, Mannheim, Germany) for 4 hours at 37°C. Absorbance at 490 nm was normalized by untreated controls. For measurement of NF-κB activation, cells were treated with 10μM acalabrutinib for 24 hours before analysis of CFP expression.

Competitive growth assay

Cells were seeded at equal proportions at 2.5 × 105/mL with weekly dilution. Monitoring was performed by polymerase chain reaction of CARD11 exon 5 (supplemental Table 1) and HindIII (NEB, Ipswich, MA) digestion to distinguish HDR-edited from nonedited alleles by electrophoresis (BioAnalyzer; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA).

RNA sequencing

Poly-A RNA (AllPrep DNA/RNA kit; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was paired-end sequenced (150 bp; Illumina NovaSeq Xplus; GenomeScan, Leiden, The Netherlands). Reads were processed using FastQC and aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) using STAR aligner. Counts were generated using featureCounts, normalized by variance stabilizing transformation, and after excluding genes used for modification (supplemental Table 4), we performed variance partitioning. Genes with reads of R2 >0.75 are plotted. Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis was performed with DEseq2. DGE genes were used for gene set enrichment analysis using Molecular signatures database (MSigDB)26 (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb) and the Signature database (SignatureDB)27 (https://lymphochip.nih.gov/signaturedb/) gene signatures. Data output is reported in supplemental tables 6-10.

Results

Distinct oncogenic potential of CARD11 variants

As a paradigmatic ABC-DLBCL, TMD8 cells are MYD88L265P and CD79BY196H mutated, homozygous for WT CARD11, and express a clonal IgM κ with robust autonomous signaling activity (supplemental Figure 1).13,15 Accordingly, TMD8 cells are highly sensitive to BTK inhibition with acalabrutinib (50% inhibitory concentration [IC50] = 0.047nM) and other BTK inhibitors.13,15 To assess the effects of CARD11 mutations with high predicted pathogenicity scores in an ABC-DLBCL driven by autonomous BCR signaling, we edited CARD11 in TMD8 cells by CRISPR/cas9 HDR into variants derived from the triploid CARD11L251P/L251P/L251P ABC-DLBCL cell line OCI-Ly3 and the CARD11D230N/WT GCB-DLBCL cell line Karpas 422 (Figure 1A; Table 1).20 OCI-Ly3 and Karpas 422 express a clonal BCR without detectable autonomous signaling activity (supplemental Figure 1) and are highly resistant to BTK inhibition (IC50 acalabrutinib > 50μM; Figure 1B).13 In addition, we selected a CARD11K215N mutation from a primary DLBCL case with autonomous BCR signaling (4609) and a CARD11R337Q mutation found in a case of transformed follicular lymphoma without autonomous BCR signaling (12099; supplemental Figure 1; supplemental Table 5).15

Acalabrutinib sensitivity was determined for established CARD11 gene–edited TMD8 clones (supplemental Figure 2). Clones carrying a silent CARD11K215K mutation remained equally sensitive as unedited parental TMD8 cells (Figure 1B; supplemental Figure 2B). In accordance with previous data, introduction of a hemizygous CARD11L251P/- allele converted TMD8 cells to a fully acalabrutinib-resistant phenotype (IC50 > 50μM).15 Homozygous CARD11R337Q/R337Q alleles induced only marginally decreased sensitivity to BTK inhibition (IC50 = 0.07nM to 1.3nM). The heterozygous CARD11R337Q/WT (IC50 = 0.02nM), homozygous CARD11K215N/K215N (IC50 = 0.025nM), and homozygous CARD11D230N/D230N (IC50 = 0.14nM) genotypes did not reduce sensitivity to acalabrutinib (Figure 1C). In conclusion, only CARD11L251P conferred complete independence from autonomous BCR signaling, as indicated by absolute resistance to acalabrutinib in this model. We formally tested differential dependency of CARD11 gene–edited TMD8 clones on autonomous BCR signaling by KO of IgM and IgK BCR chains through CRISPR/cas9-mediated NHEJ. The initial efficiency of BCR KO as measured by flow cytometry after 3 days consistently demonstrated 36% to 82% of BCR-negative cells (Figure 1D). After BCR KO of unedited parental TMD8 cells, the fraction of BCR-negative cells progressively declined in cell culture and practically disappeared by day 15 (Figure 1E). Decline of BCR-negative cells occurred with similar kinetics in CARD11D230N/D230N TMD8 cells. BCR-negative populations of CARD11K215N/K215N and CARD11R337Q/R337Q TMD8 cells declined marginally slower. In contrast, CARD11L251P/- TMD8 cells were able to survive BCR KO, as indicated by stable persistence of an IgM-negative population of 43% to 58% after 21 days (Figure 1D).

To confirm that the loss of BCR-negative TMD8 cells was due to the absence of autonomous BCR signaling rather than loss of BCR expression, we also knocked out IgM BCR in CARD11-edited TMD8 clones after introducing the IgG heavy chain from OCI-Ly3 (Figure 1F). Forced expression of that IgG chain did not increase the persistence of IgM KO cells in any CARD11-edited TMD8 clone (Figure 1G). Overall, the decline of CARD11-edited TMD8 cells after IgM KO matched their measured sensitivity to BTK inhibition. Together, these results confirm that CARD11 coiled-coil domain mutations differ in their ability to compensate for loss of autonomous BCR signaling by activating downstream pathways. Only CARD11L251P exhibited sufficient survival signals without any BCR or with a nonsignaling BCR, indicating full oncogenicity in this context.

Direct comparison of autonomous BCR signaling and the CARD11L251P allele as oncogenic drivers

To investigate the proposed functional equivalence between autonomous BCR signaling and the CARD11L251P mutant, we established BCR KO (“TCko”) clones from TMD8 CARD11L251P/- (“TC8”) clone 2C11 (Figure 2A). Genetic modifications of all clones were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (supplemental Figure 3) and flow cytometry (Figure 2B). NF-κB activation was measured in parental cell lines (supplemental Figure 4) and TMD8 clones transduced with a reporter construct encoding CFP under control of an NF-κB–responsive element. Overall, NF-κB was highly activated in all untreated TMD8 clones (Figure 2C-D). However, only CARD11WT TW8 clones showed decreased NF-κB activation after 24-hour treatment with acalabrutinib (10μM). Editing of CARD11 into CARD11L251P reduced both CD79B expression and constitutive phosphorylation of CD79A Y182. Subsequent abolishment of BCR expression fully abrogated CD79B expression and CD79A phosphorylation. Conversely, the CARD11L251P-encoding allele enhanced PI3K phosphorylation, with a more pronounced effect after BCR KO (Figure 2E; supplemental Figure 5). In 3 independent clones of each genotype, high sensitivity to acalabrutinib was confirmed for WT TMD8 (TW8) cells, and absolute resistance was demonstrated for TC8 and TCko mutants (Figure 2F). The genotype-specific effect on BTK inhibition was contrasted by unchanged sensitivity to the MALT1 inhibitor MI2 for all investigated clones (supplemental Figure 6).

Direct comparison of the CARD11L251P allele and autonomous BCR signaling as oncogenic drivers in OCI-Ly3 cells

To reciprocally compare autonomous BCR signaling and the CARD11L251P mutation as oncogenic drivers, we exchanged these mechanisms in OCI-Ly3 (Figure 4A). OCI-Ly3 cells are a particularly well-suitable comparator to TMD8 for these experiments because they also carry the MYD88L265P mutation. In addition to acalabrutinib resistance, expression of WT CD79B and survival of CD79A and SYK knockdown collectively indicate independence of OCI-Ly3 from proximal BCR activation.13 The endogenous OCI-Ly3 BCR was knocked out by targeting both Immunoglobulin heavy chain variable region (IGHV) and Immunoglobuling light chain constant region (IGLC) (supplemental Figure 3B). BCR KO OCI-Ly3 (OCko) cells were viable and could be clonally expanded. As the next step, the TMD8 BCR was introduced with an NGFR reporter gene into OCko clone 11B4. Due to the triploidy of the mutant CARD11L251P allele in OCI-Ly3,28 we did not attempt to revert all 3 alleles to WT by gene editing. Instead, we transduced TMD8-expressing OCko cells with a retroviral vector carrying a codon-optimized and CRISPR-resistant CARD11WT gene on a dicistronic expression cassette with green fluorescent protein. CARD11WT-expressing OCko8 clones were selected by green fluorescent protein expression, followed by CRISPR/cas9 NHEJ KO of all 3 mutant CARD11L251P alleles to generate OWko8 clones (supplemental Figure 7A-C). It proved impossible to establish viable CARD11WT-transduced OCI-Ly3 cells lacking the CARD11L251P alleles (“OWko” cells) without prior transduction of the TMD8 BCR.

BCR surface expression was assessed in viable OCI-Ly3 clones by flow cytometry (Figure 4B). Cross-linking with isotype-specific antibodies induced strong calcium release and confirmed functionality of the corresponding BCR (supplemental Figure 8A). NF-κB activation was detected in all OCI-Ly3 clones. Only OWko8 clones showed potential sensitivity to inhibition of BTK by acalabrutinib (Figure 4C-D). Analysis of key BCR signaling elements revealed abrogation of CD79B expression and constitutive phosphorylation of CD79A by BCR KO and reinstitution by transduction with the TMD8 BCR (Figure 4E). In addition, cells driven by the TMD8 BCR (OCko8 and OWko8) showed slightly decreased phosphorylation of PI3K (Figure 4E). Furthermore, all OCI-Ly3 clones that retained the CARD11L251P alleles remained completely resistant to acalabrutinib (Figure 4F). After KO of the CARD11L251P alleles, CARD11WT-expressing OWko8 clones showed increased sensitivity to acalabrutinib (IC50 = 0.039μM-0.668μM), although they did not become as highly sensitive as WT TMD8 cells. Sensitivity to the MALT1 inhibitor MI2 (IC50 = 0.15μM-0.54μM) was unaltered by stepwise genetic modifications (supplemental Figure 8B).

In cocultures of established clones of OCI-Ly3, OWko8 clones expressing the autonomously signaling TMD8 BCR and WT CARD11 outcompeted clones driven by the triploid CARD11L251P allele (unmanipulated OC3 and BCR-KO OCko clones) after 3 weeks (Figure 5A). The proliferative advantage of OCI-Ly3 cells carrying both oncogenic drivers (OCko8 clones) was consistently evident after 1 week of coculture (Figure 5B). OCI-Ly3 clones retaining their original BCR (OC3) had a marginal proliferative advantage over OCI-Ly3 clones driven only by CARD11L251P alleles (OCko). These experiments confirmed the TMD8 BCR as a stronger proliferative driver than the CARD11L251P allele even when present in triploidy. Elimination of the endogenous, nonsignaling OCI-Ly3 BCR gave only a marginal proliferative disadvantage.

Gene expression profiling with alternative oncogenic drivers

To identify possible alterations of gene expression profiles after exchange of oncogenic drivers by gene editing and retroviral transduction, we performed transcriptomic analysis of individual clones. Genetically modified and unmodified clones clustered with their respective original cell line and at a large distance from the GCB-DLBCL cell line Karpas 422 (Figure 6A-B). Tight coclustering of clones with bona fide identical genotypes indicated absence of potentially perturbing founder effects in the clones used for functional testing. Gene expression profiling therefore shows that parental cellular identity is largely preserved despite genetic changes, and major transcriptional shifts do not occur after swapping the TMD8 BCR and activating CARD11 allele.

Subtle driver-dependent transcriptional changes were revealed by DGE analysis and identified 3904 differentially expressed genes after exchange of the autonomous BCR signal with the oncogenic CARD11 allele in gene-modified TMD8 clones (Figure 6C; supplemental Figure 9A). In OCI-Ly3 clones lacking their endogenous BCR, these oncogenic drivers differentially regulated 1081 genes. Only 216 dysregulated genes were found in both ABC-DLBCL cell line systems. One-hundred genes were concordantly upregulated in TMD8 and OCI-Ly3 derivative clones that were driven by autonomous BCR signaling; higher expression of 68 genes was concordantly associated with dependence on the CARD11L251P variant (supplemental Figure 9B). Among the upregulated genes associated with autonomous BCR signaling in both ABC-DLBCL cell lines were CD69, an early activation marker for T and B cells,29 and the chemokine receptor CXCR4. CARD11L251P induced the upregulation of CCND2 in both cell line systems.

Gene set enrichment analysis of all differentially expressed genes among the range of clone sets based on gene expression signatures (SignatureDB)27 and pathway enrichment (MSigDB)26 databases revealed only marginal overlap between the 2 cell line systems. Decreased activity of IRF4 and the IRF4/SPIB complex in CARD11L251P-driven cells in both ABC-DLBCL cell line systems was suggested by enrichment of IRF4-repressed target genes in SignatureDB transcription factor target gene sets “IRF4_ABC_repressed_all” and “IRF4_SPIB_ABC_repressed_all”30 (Figure 6D). Concordant enrichment in MSigDB module 75 (Immune response), encompassing enriched clinical annotations of DLBCL, was noted for CARD11L251P-driven cells.

Overall, gene expression profiling confirmed that autonomous BCR signaling and CARD11L251P act as alternative, mutually exchangeable oncogenic drivers without appreciable alteration of the original ABC-DLBCL phenotype in these ABC-DLBCL models.

Discussion

Extensive genetic and transcriptomic analyses of large patient cohorts have provided systematic insight into the substantial heterogeneity of DLBCL and have opened avenues to improve pharmacological therapy beyond the long-established standard of R-CHOP–like immunochemotherapy.5,7,10 An actionable feature of the ABC-DLBCL subtype is its consistent dependency on BCR pathway signaling.13 BTK inhibitor monotherapy benefits only a minority of patients with ABC-DLBCL, but adding a BTK inhibitor to R-CHOP improves outcomes, especially in the MCD and N1 genetic clusters dominated by the ABC subtype.19,31,32

The MCD cluster is defined by a high percentage of cases with activating mutations in genes that influence BCR signaling and NF-κB activation. Among these, mutations in CD79B are thought to enhance BCR signaling.13 Mutated MYD88 interacts with the CBM signalosome in the endolysosome within the so-called My-T–BCR complex.17 In addition, several genes affecting the BCR pathway are recurrently mutated in DLBCL, but no single gene is mutated in all patients with ABC-DLBCL. Activating mutations of the CARD11 gene, a central component of the CBM signalosome, have been shown to cause NF-κB activation to varying degrees. The CARD11 L251P, L232LI, and S250P variants appear equivalently potent in maintaining OCI-Ly3 survival, whereas the D230N and D387V variants are insufficiently oncogenic to replace L251P.20,33 This example highlights the need to quantify the roles of all genes of the BCR pathway in transforming GCB- to ABC-DLBCL to better predict responses to targeted therapies such as BTK inhibitors. We have recently demonstrated that antigen-independent, autonomous signaling of the BCR expressed by the malignant B-cell clone is detectable in most patients with ABC-DLBCL and provides a plausible origin of chronic active BCR signaling.15 This paraphysiological yet oncogenic BCR function is associated with the IgM isotype and is acquired by somatic hypermutation during germinal center reactions. However, the true contribution of autonomous BCR signaling to the malignant phenotype and aggressive behavior of ABC-DLBCL in the context of a multitude of coexisting genetic aberrations remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate full functional equivalence of autonomous BCR signaling and a strong activating CARD11 mutation in reciprocal ABC-DLBCL models that differ in CARD11 and CD79B genotypes but are matched for the canonical activating MYD88 mutation. Specifically, autonomous BCR signaling can fully replace the potent oncogenic CARD11L251P mutation for survival and proliferation of OCI-Ly3 ABC-DLBCL cells. Conversely, introducing a single CARD11L251P allele by accurate gene editing compensates TMD8 cells for loss of autonomous BCR signaling and enables TMD8 cells to survive without any BCR expression, an uncommon feature in DLBCL and typically seen only in Hodgkin lymphoma and, occasionally, Burkitt lymphoma.34,35 Similarly, ablation of the BCR induces apoptosis in mature murine B cells, but activating PI3K signaling can compensate for the loss of BCR expression.36,37 Our observation of increased PI3K phosphorylation in the presence of CARD11L251P therefore offers a plausible explanation for the survival of BCRko TMD8 and OCI-Ly3 cells. In contrast, the CARD11D230N variant expressed by Karpas 422 cells was not associated with measurable phosphorylation of PI3K, and BCRko Karpas 422 cells are not viable (data not shown).38 Activation of the mTORC1 complex by CARD11L251P has been observed in murine B cells and may be induced by phosphorylated PI3K via AKT.33

Despite the high oncogenic potency of the CARD11L251P allele and activation of PI3K, ABC-DLBCL cells driven by the autonomous BCR signal retained a slight proliferative advantage. This observation could potentially be explained by additional effects of proximal BCR signaling, such as calcium release via PLCγ2,39,40 suggesting that autonomous BCR signaling, acquired by somatic hypermutation in germinal center reactions in ABC-DLBCL,15 exerts a “paraphysiological” and hence optimal growth and survival signal. In contrast, activating CARD11 mutations are truly pathologic and therefore perhaps not optimally able to replace BCR-originating signals despite higher PI3K phosphorylation. The combined presence of autonomous BCR signaling and CARD11L251P even modestly slowed growth. This effect could be related to the “Goldilocks” principle of B-cell selection, that is, a selective disadvantage for B cells receiving strong autoantigen-induced BCR signals mediated by metabolic gatekeepers.41,42 This effect might suggest that the intermediate strength of the autonomous BCR signal compared to the stronger effect of external BCR cross-linking is more lymphomagenic than autoantigen recognition.

Nonetheless, proximal and distal activation of the BCR signaling cascade barely changes gene expression profile of ABC-DLBCL cells in culture. In both the analyzed cell line systems, CARD11L251P-driven cells had decreased repression of known IRF4 target genes. Increased activity of IRF4 in ABC-DLBCL cells driven by autonomous BCR signaling is in accordance with the downregulation of IRF4 through pharmacological BTK inhibition.30 Differential expression of IRF4-repressed targets therefore suggests subtle differences in NF-κB activation by CARD11L251P compared to an autonomously signaling BCR. Of note, we did not detect any significant driver-dependent difference in expression of gene sets that are induced by IRF4/SPIB, including CARD11 itself in both cell line systems.30

As predicted, exchange of proximal with distal BCR pathway activation via the BCR or CARD11, respectively, dramatically alters sensitivity of ABC-DLBCL cells to BTK inhibition. Despite high predicted oncogenicity scores and gain-of-function effects observed for CARD11K215N and CARD11D230N upon artificial overexpression in Jurkat T or HEK293 cells,20,43 but in accordance with the failure of CARD11D230N to compensate suppression of CARD11L251P,20 these recurrent mutations conferred only marginally reduced to completely unaltered sensitivity to acalabrutinib. The lack of detectable oncogenic activity of CARD11K215N explains why one case with DLBCL (4609) carrying this mutation expressed a BCR with strong autonomous BCR activity.15 A gene-edited homozygous CARD11R337Q/R337Q has gain of function as indicated by reduced sensitivity to BTK inhibition. This variant was present in a DLBCL transformed from follicular lymphoma and may have contributed to a more aggressive phenotype in a lymphoma usually not driven by activated BCR signaling.

In conclusion, our results emphasize the causal role of autonomous BCR signaling in ABC-DLBCL by demonstrating its functional equivalency to the strongest known oncogenic CARD11 variant. Furthermore, we demonstrate the need to understand the precise mechanism of NF-κB activation through BCR signaling to accurately predict effects of proximal BCR blockade with pharmacological BTK inhibitors. In this context, it must be noted that both tested cell lines carry the canonical activating MYD88 mutation that is known to physically interact with the CBM signalosome in the endolysosome.17 Therefore, it remains to be clarified how reversion of MYD88 to WT would affect the exchange of oncogenic drivers in our experimental systems. Autonomous BCR signaling, however, cannot be readily added as classifying feature to genetic DLBCL clustering because autonomously signaling BCR remain hitherto structurally indistinguishable from mutated IgM lacking this particular function. The transcriptional changes associated with the exchange of BCR and CARD11 mutants as oncogenic drivers appear also insufficiently pronounced to differentiate reliably between ABC-DLBCL with sensitivity or intrinsic resistance to BTK inhibition, either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy or other targeted agents.19,31,44 Therefore, there remains a considerable need to develop modeling tools to recognize atypical BCR-BCR interactions that form the structural basis for autonomous BCR signaling in individual patients with DLBCL.16,45 Our gene-edited cell line models lend themselves to quantifying the relative contributions of any mutation that enhances BCR pathway signaling, in particular canonical CD79B and MYD88 variants and any other mutation found in the MCD and BN2 LymphGen and C1/C5 consensus clusters, respectively, to identify optimal combinations of targeted therapeutic agents.7,10,12
