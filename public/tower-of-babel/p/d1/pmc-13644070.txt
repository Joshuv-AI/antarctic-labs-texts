Longitudinal dream-content shift and void-like dissociative phenomenology around outpatient ketamine infusions in chronic low back pain: a case report
Authors: Kei Torii, Ryo Nishitani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAltered-state phenomenology around ketamine may relate to longer-term changes in imaginal processes, but observations across routine care are scarce. We report an educational case focusing on pre-defined dream motifs over time, without making treatment efficacy claims.Case presentationA 48-year-old Japanese man with severe chronic primary low back pain reported dreams during insight-oriented psychotherapy from 2009 to 2025; over 360 narratives were contemporaneously documented. In 2014, he received five low-dose outpatient intravenous ketamine infusions (15 mg; 0.23 mg/kg; first 20 min, then 30 min; no premedication) and described a void-like dissociative state during the first session. Using a pre-specified codebook, two raters (treating clinician and external psychiatrist) independently coded an ID-based, period-stratified random subset of 50 dreams after re-ordering and masking of period labels for three primary motifs (obstruction, fear, anger) plus a descriptive social-interaction/role-completion motif. Obstruction decreased from pre- to post-ketamine periods in both raters (treating clinician: 6/9 vs 4/30; external psychiatrist: 6/9 vs 7/30). In the double-labelled subset, the social-interaction/role-completion motif was labelled more often post- than pre-ketamine by both raters (2/9 pre; 10/30 and 11/30 post).ConclusionsAcross the observation window that included outpatient ketamine infusions in 2014, dream content shifted from recurrent obstruction toward imagery of movement, interpersonal engagement, and everyday role completion (illustrated by selected de-identified dream narratives; Table 5). These single-patient, hypothesis-generating observations describe ketamine-associated void-like phenomenology and longitudinal dream-content change without making efficacy claims. Background

Ketamine is an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist used as an anesthetic and, at subanesthetic doses, as a treatment modality in various neuropsychiatric and pain contexts. Clinical trials in treatment-resistant depression demonstrated rapid antidepressant effects [1, 2], while experimental studies documented acute dissociative-like and perceptual alterations at subanesthetic doses [3–5].

Dreaming has been reported around ketamine exposure. In healthy volunteers, subanesthetic ketamine increased unpleasant dream incidence compared with placebo over subsequent nights [6]. Earlier anesthesia literature described postoperative ‘ketamine dreams’ [7], and a randomized trial suggested that positive suggestion might reduce unpleasant dream recall when ketamine is used for sedation [8]. However, longitudinal descriptions of dream-content changes across routine-care ketamine courses are scarce, particularly in chronic pain. Ketamine has also been reported to influence sleep architecture in some contexts [9].

We, therefore, report a CARE-guided case documenting longitudinal dream-content change across an observation window that included outpatient ketamine infusions in chronic pain, using a pre-defined codebook and two-rater labelling after masking of period labels. Clinically, it illustrates a practical stance for outpatient ketamine care: orient patients that dream-like or dissociative phenomenology—including vivid imagery or a void-like state—may occur and can be explored; invite brief phenomenological documentation; and—within an insight-oriented framework—leave room to consider subjective meaning rather than immediately pathologizing the content while maintaining standard physiological monitoring and the option to adjust administration parameters to match intensity to tolerance and clinical goals. This report is framed as an unexpected medication-associated phenomenon in routine care, rather than an intervention-efficacy case report. The completed CARE checklist is provided as Additional file 1.

Case presentation

Patient information and context

A 48-year-old Japanese man (body weight 65 kg) had severe chronic primary low back pain (ICD-11 MG30.02). Pain began in his early 30 s after occupational physical strain and progressed to persistent daily pain described as “as if after being hit hard”, most intense in the morning and exacerbated by movement and vibration. He reported partial transient relief with heat and topical measures.

Functional limitation was profound. From approximately age 37 until the initial home-visit consultation in 2009 (age 44), he was almost entirely homebound (leaving home only rarely for medical visits). Travelling by car was difficult, because road vibration aggravated pain, and even sitting in the back seat could exacerbate symptoms.

He reported no leg radicular pain, numbness, weakness, or bowel/bladder dysfunction. Psychiatric diagnoses were not assigned; he did not report prominent depressive symptoms and had no history of psychosis. He engaged in insight-oriented psychotherapy, including dream work, initiated during home visits in late 2009. Across sessions, he repeatedly reported dreams; the clinician documented the dream narratives contemporaneously in clinical notes and used them for collaborative dream analysis as part of ongoing insight-oriented psychotherapy.

Past interventions for pain included multiple interventional procedures (e.g., epidural and nerve root blocks, intradiscal injection, and trigger-point injections) without clinically meaningful benefit. Concomitant medications during the ketamine year (2014) included pregabalin (Lyrica) 150 mg/day, sulpiride (Dogmatyl) 200 mg/day, transdermal buprenorphine 5 µg/h (Norspan), zolpidem (Myslee) 10 mg at bedtime (intermittent before June 2014; nightly thereafter), and etizolam (Depas) 1 mg/day until May 2014, when it was discontinued after the patient reported minimal use. This timing may reflect a shift in bedtime hypnotic use from etizolam to zolpidem. Other concurrent medications in 2014 included alfacalcidol (One-alpha), Kampo herbal medicines (Hachimijiogan or Shimbuto), a topical NSAID patch (ketoprofen; Moras Tape), and sennosides (Pursennid).

Past medical history included unrelated surgery in his 20 s and chronic laboratory abnormalities under internal medicine follow-up. Family history was unremarkable for chronic pain. Social history: he lived with a family member, was unmarried, and had stopped working due to pain after periods of clerical/office employment and later self-employment. A longstanding community affiliation and outreach practices were noted and were de-identified in dream texts.

Clinical findings.

Orthopedic evaluations over several years, including neurological examination (lower-extremity motor strength, sensation, and deep tendon reflexes), did not identify objective neurological deficits. At our clinic, he consistently reported no red-flag symptoms or history suggestive of serious spinal pathology, including fever, unintentional weight loss, history of malignancy, major trauma, progressive neurological deficit, worsening nocturnal pain with systemic features, or bowel/bladder dysfunction.

Diagnostic assessment and analytic approach

Lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and plain radiographs performed during prior evaluations at a regional tertiary orthopedic service showed no clinically meaningful structural abnormalities (no disc herniation, spinal canal stenosis, fracture, tumor, or infection), and no radiculopathy or other neurological syndrome was identified. These findings were documented in an orthopedic referral letter and were also reviewed by the treating clinician. Given the chronicity, repeated absence of red-flag features, and prominent functional impact, the working diagnosis was chronic primary low back pain (ICD-11 MG30.02).

Dream records were analysed using a pre-specified codebook (Additional file 2) and double-labelling after masking of period labels on a period-stratified, ID-selected subset, consistent with established dream-content analysis principles [10–12]. For the double-labelled subset, each record in the deduplicated 362-dream corpus was assigned a de-identified study ID and classified into pre-ketamine (2009–2013), ketamine year (2014), or post-ketamine (2015–2025) strata. Before motif coding, a period-stratified random selection without replacement was performed from study-ID lists only, targeting 10 pre-ketamine, 10 ketamine year, and 30 post-ketamine dreams; after date-based period assignment and reclassification, the realised subset was 9, 11, and 30 dreams, respectively. Selected dream texts were then stripped of dates and period labels, pooled, re-ordered, and independently coded by the treating clinician and the external psychiatrist. Selection was based on study ID and period stratum only, not on dream content, motif labels, affective tone, or clinical outcome. Inter-rater reliability was summarised using Cohen's kappa [13], with values reported in Additional file 2: Supplementary Table S2. Additional exploratory counts were generated on the full de-identified corpus using pre-specified dictionary-based pattern rules (Additional file 2: Supplementary Table S1).

A language/cultural challenge was that the dream corpus was originally documented in Japanese and included culturally specific interpersonal and community references. Coding was performed on the original Japanese texts; English translations were prepared only for manuscript presentation. Potentially identifying cultural or group-specific details were generalised or redacted, which may have reduced narrative specificity.

To improve readability and transparency of manuscript preparation, a large language model (ChatGPT, OpenAI) was used for English-language editing and formatting. In addition, before motif coding, ChatGPT was used only to assist with administrative random selection and re-ordering of de-identified dream-study IDs from period-specific ID lists for the double-coded subset. No dream narrative text was provided to ChatGPT. ChatGPT was not used to generate dream narratives, translate dream narratives, assign motif labels, adjudicate ratings, calculate motif frequencies or inter-rater reliability, or interpret the findings. The authors verified the selected IDs, ratings, counts, and kappa calculations and take full responsibility for the final manuscript.

Therapeutic interventions

These infusions were administered in 2014 as historical routine pain-clinic care, not as part of a contemporary dedicated ketamine-clinic programme or research protocol. At our clinic at that time, low total doses of intravenous ketamine, typically 10–15 mg, had been used as a last-line adjunct for refractory pain when standard pain-clinic interventions (e.g., nerve blocks and pharmacotherapy) provided insufficient control. For this patient, five outpatient intravenous ketamine infusions were given across 2014. Each infusion used 15 mg ketamine (0.23 mg/kg; body weight 65 kg). The first infusion was delivered over 20 min; infusions 2–5 were delivered over 30 min using a standard intravenous set by gravity drip with manual adjustment of drip rate. No premedication was used.

The 15 mg dose was selected as a cautious, low-total-dose outpatient trial in a ketamine-naive patient receiving concomitant central-nervous-system-active medications. Although the estimated infusion rates were approximately 0.69 mg/kg/h for the first 20-min infusion and 0.46 mg/kg/h for the subsequent 30-min infusions, the total dose per session was low and the exposure duration was shorter than many chronic-pain ketamine protocols. Therefore, this regimen should not be interpreted as an evidence-based analgesic protocol for chronic low back pain. Gravity-drip delivery with manual rate adjustment also had lower dose-delivery precision than pump-based protocols.

Monitoring consisted of continuous clinical observation and pulse oximetry with displayed pulse rate. No oxygen desaturation below 90% was observed, and the patient maintained spontaneous ventilation throughout. No clinically evident cardiorespiratory instability, need for rescue intervention, unplanned transfer, or serious medical complication occurred. However, fixed-interval vital-sign documentation was not available: capnography was not used, non-invasive blood pressure was not documented at predetermined intervals, and no formal sedation scale was recorded.

During the first infusion, the patient reported a particularly intense, dream-like "void" phenomenology characterised by perceived complete darkness/absence and a strong existential tone (sense of irreversibility) while remaining consciously aware. Because this possibility had not been explicitly discussed in advance, the experience was initially frightening to the patient. After the first infusion and the void-like dissociative episode, he was observed at rest until the acute experience had resolved and he had returned near baseline. In routine clinic practice, discharge was allowed after rest only when the patient could stand and walk briefly without difficulty and showed no evident distress or cardiorespiratory instability; no formal discharge score was used or documented. For subsequent sessions, the planned infusion duration was increased to 30 min to modulate experiential intensity and align administration with tolerance and clinical goals; the same void-like experience did not recur. In follow-up, the patient later framed the experience as meaningful subjective material within psychotherapy.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient continued insight-oriented psychotherapy with regular follow-up throughout the observation window. Dream narratives were elicited during sessions and documented by the clinician from 2009 to 2025; dream texts were de-identified prior to analysis and dates were coarsened to reduce re-identification risk. Despite persistent pain, he gradually increased activity from being largely homebound to engaging in short outings and brief daily walks, with gradual re-engagement in practical and social activities over subsequent years. Over time he resumed reading and writing, became able to use a computer/smartphone, and could travel short distances by car for brief errands.

Patient perspective: During the first infusion, the patient entered a completely dark, “void-like” state—described as a “world of nothingness”—and initially felt as if he might not be able to return. Because this possibility had not been discussed beforehand, he reported fear at the time. After the clinician later explained that such dream-like dissociative phenomena can occur with ketamine and can be approached as meaningful subjective material within psychotherapy, he felt better able to contextualise the experience. For subsequent sessions, the planned infusion duration was lengthened to 30 min; the patient did not report recurrence of the same void-like state. He recalled short-lived symptomatic relief for 1–2 days after the first infusion, while the immediate subjective effects varied across later sessions. Later in 2014 he described increased ability to attend community activities and engage in short outings despite persistent pain.

Dream corpus overview and qualitative trajectory

Across longitudinal insight-oriented psychotherapy sessions, the clinician elicited, documented, and collaboratively analysed dream narratives from 2009 to 2025 (over 360 documented dream reports). For transparency and interpretability, we summarise the qualitative trajectory alongside the quantitative motif counts and provide representative de-identified dream narratives (translated) from each period (Table 5). In the pre-ketamine period, dream narratives repeatedly depicted attempted movement or task completion that was thwarted by external constraints. During the ketamine year, dreams became more vivid and scene-like, including constriction/threat and rapid transformations (index dream). In the post-ketamine period, dreams increasingly depicted ordinary public-life scenes with reciprocal interaction and concrete completion of small roles or tasks (e.g., completing a bank transaction followed by grocery shopping with a sense of reassurance), consistent with the observed reduction in ‘obstruction’ and descriptive increase in the manually double-labelled social-interaction/role-completion motif.

Conclusions and discussion

This CARE-guided case describes a longitudinal shift in dream content across an observation window that included five low-dose outpatient ketamine infusions in 2014, ongoing insight-oriented psychotherapy with dream work, medication changes during 2014, and gradual functional recovery. We pre-specified motif definitions, used double-labelling after masking of period labels, and observed a descriptive reduction of the 'obstruction' motif from the pre-ketamine to the post-ketamine period, alongside a descriptive increase in the manually double-labelled social-interaction/role-completion motif. These observations should be read as single-case, hypothesis-generating phenomenology, not as evidence that ketamine drove a 16-year clinical or dream-content trajectory.

Dreaming around ketamine exposure has been described in anesthesia and sedation, and ketamine has been associated with vivid or unpleasant dream experiences. In healthy volunteers, subanesthetic ketamine increased the incidence of unpleasant dreams compared with placebo over the three post-administration nights [6]. Earlier anesthesia literature discussed postoperative ‘ketamine dreams’ [7], and a randomized trial suggested that positive suggestion might reduce unpleasant dream recall when ketamine is used for sedation [8]. These reports support the plausibility that ketamine can influence dream phenomenology, although they primarily address short-term effects.

Ketamine can also modulate sleep physiology. A review of ketamine, sleep, and depression summarized evidence that ketamine can affect early-night slow-wave activity and sleep consolidation in some contexts [9]. Such sleep-architecture effects provide a plausible short-term pathway through which ketamine could influence dream phenomenology or memory/affective processing, but they do not establish ketamine as the driver of this patient's longer-term dream trajectory.

Alternative explanations should be considered before any ketamine-centred interpretation. The observed longitudinal shift may reflect concurrent dream-focused psychotherapy and meaning-making, naturalistic functional recovery over time, the May–June 2014 change in bedtime hypnotic regimen, other concomitant medications, or reporting/documentation biases inherent to clinician-documented dream narratives. Accordingly, we treat the temporal association as descriptive and hypothesis-generating, and we avoid causal attribution to ketamine.

Interpretive hypothesis (unconscious/imaginal processing). Beyond descriptive motif counts, one possible, strictly hypothesis-generating interpretation is that the acute ketamine-associated dissociative experience and later dream reports may both reflect changes in imaginal or meaning-making processes occurring within ongoing psychotherapy. This interpretation cannot distinguish the contribution of ketamine from dream-focused psychotherapy, medication changes during 2014, gradual functional recovery, or documentation effects. From this limited perspective, ketamine-associated dissociative experiences and dreaming can be viewed as phenomenologically related altered states, but not as evidence of a single causal process. Within an insight-oriented framework, such experiences may be approached as potentially meaning-bearing subjective material; in this case, the patient and clinician explicitly treated the ketamine-associated experience as "dream-like" material to be reflected upon rather than as noise to be dismissed. Although such experiences can be intense and are variably appraised (including frightening when unanticipated), they should be managed with anticipatory preparation, appropriate physiological monitoring, and post-session clinical support. Future phenomenological work may further characterise extreme "void-like" experiences and evaluate whether specific experiential dimensions relate to longer-term psychological or behavioural change.

In this case, the ketamine year did not coincide with a sustained immediate step-change in pain-related functioning; the patient reported transient symptomatic relief after early infusions with variable subjective effects thereafter. The observed dream-content shift occurred alongside later gradual behavioral re-engagement and changes in disability measures, and the patient and clinician explicitly used ketamine-associated experiences and evolving dream themes as material for psychotherapy and meaning-making. Given the single-case design and concurrent psychotherapy, causal attribution to ketamine is not possible. Nonetheless, the case suggests that systematic dream-content monitoring and integration-oriented reflection may provide clinically salient phenomenological information when exploring longer-term change around altered-state interventions.

Clinically, the first infusion was accompanied by a void-like dissociative, dream-like phenomenology that the patient initially experienced as fearful, because it occurred without prior preparation or psychoeducation. In outpatient ketamine care, particularly when embedded in an insight-oriented framework, it may be helpful to approach such experiences not only as events to be managed, but also as phenomenological material that can be named, narrated, and integrated. Practical bedside steps include anticipatory guidance (informing patients that vivid imagery, a sense of emptiness/void, and shifts in self-boundaries can occur), inviting the patient to describe what happened in their own words, and offering brief post-session time to reflect and integrate the experience. This meaning-oriented stance does not replace safety monitoring; it complements it. Clinicians should titrate infusion parameters to tolerance and clinical goals, maintain physiological monitoring and recovery procedures appropriate to the sedation/dissociation risk, and have clear protocols to slow or stop administration if physiological instability emerges or if the patient requests that the session be paused.

Limitations include the single-case design, potential reporting and documentation bias in verbally reported dreams (clinician-documented narratives), non-independence of serial dreams (possible autocorrelation), lack of polysomnography or objective sleep measures, and concurrent psychotherapeutic intervention. Period-label masking may have been imperfect for the treating clinician, who documented the dreams and may have recognized some narratives. The small pre-period double-labelled sample also made obstruction proportions imprecise, as reflected by the descriptive Wilson confidence intervals. We mitigated re-identification risk by de-identifying dream text and coarsening dates, but residual risk remains.

The ketamine infusions were performed in 2014 as historical routine pain-clinic care rather than under a contemporary dedicated ketamine-clinic or research protocol. Gravity-drip administration lacked pump-level dose-delivery precision, and monitoring/recovery documentation was limited relative to current moderate-sedation expectations [22]: capnography, fixed-interval non-invasive blood-pressure recording, a formal sedation scale, and a formal discharge score were not used or documented. These limitations restrict interpretation of dose-delivery precision and acute safety monitoring, and the regimen should not be presented as a model for current outpatient ketamine practice.

Concomitant medications may also have influenced sleep continuity, dream recall, dream affect, and motif expression. Psychotropic medications can affect dream recall and dream content; sedative effects that reduce intra-sleep awakenings may reduce dream recall, and sulpiride has been reported to make recalled dreams less clear and more emotionally neutral without major sleep-architecture change [18]. Pregabalin may increase slow-wave sleep and sleep depth, potentially altering awakenings and recall [19]. Buprenorphine and opioids may disrupt sleep architecture, including wakefulness, non-rapid-eye-movement sleep, and sleep latency [20]. Zolpidem and the May–June 2014 shift from etizolam to nightly zolpidem may have affected sleep continuity, awakenings, recall, and abnormal-dream or parasomnia-like phenomena [21]. These medication effects cannot be separated from ketamine exposure, psychotherapy, or recovery in this single case.

Overall, this case adds a longitudinal perspective to the literature on ketamine-associated altered experiences by documenting a structured dream-content shift across an observation window that included outpatient ketamine infusions, ongoing psychotherapy, medication changes, and gradual recovery in chronic pain. It raises, rather than tests, a hypothesis of phenomenological continuity between acute ketamine-associated dissociative experiences and later dreaming. Prospective studies combining ketamine dosing records, contemporary monitoring and recovery documentation, standardised dream/sleep measures, phenomenological assessments of acute experiences, and clinical outcomes are warranted. Clinical takeaway: (1) prepare patients for potentially intense dream-like or void-like dissociative experiences even at low dose; (2) document and integrate narratives when clinically appropriate; and (3) maintain contemporary physiological monitoring and recovery/discharge procedures.
