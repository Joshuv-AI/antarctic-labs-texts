Dynamic transitions in falls and physical activity among older adults in Malaysia: a longitudinal multi-state analysis
Authors: Theng Choon Ooi, Nurul Fatin Malek Rivan, Suzana Shahar, Devinder Kaur Ajit Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn this study, we examined the longitudinal trajectories of falls and physical activity statuses among older adults and identified factors associated with state transitions.MethodsA total of 1,327 community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 60 years (mean age = 68.72 ± 6.02 years; 673 females, 50.7%) who participated in the second, third, and fourth waves of data collection and had complete falls and physical activity data were analysed using a multi-state Markov model.ResultsAmong participants with one fall, 63% remained fall-free in subsequent assessments. Those with ≥ 2 falls had a 25% probability of returning to no-fall state and a 21% probability of transitioning to a 1-fall state. Physical activity levels remained relatively stable over time, with comparable probabilities of improvement or decline. Key factors influencing transitions included age, education, skeletal muscle mass, depression, cognitive function, and physical fitness.ConclusionThis study highlights the dynamic nature of falls and physical activity patterns among older adults, emphasizing the crucial role of modifiable factors including cognitive function, muscle mass, and physical fitness. Our findings suggest that comprehensive, targeted interventions addressing these key determinants could effectively improve mobility, reduce fall incidence, and enhance overall quality of life in ageing populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25080-2.Keywords: Falls, Longitudinal, Older adults, Physical activity, Trajectory

Background

The global population is ageing at an unprecedented rate, with a substantial increase in the number of older adults expected in the coming decades [1]. This demographic shift has far-reaching implications for public health and healthcare systems worldwide [2, 3]. Among the significant health concerns facing older adults are increased risk of falls and reduced physical activity levels, both of which have profound implications for independence and quality of life.

Falls represent a prevalent and serious health issue among older adults, with consequences including fractures, hospitalizations, and decline in functional independence [4]. The risk of falls typically increases with age due to multiple factors including muscle weakness, balance impairments, cognitive decline, chronic health conditions, and medication use [5–7]. Beyond the immediate physical harm, falls also carry significant psychological consequences. Older adults who experience falls often develop a fear of falling, which is associated with reduced independence, diminished quality of life, poorer rehabilitation outcomes, increased risk of frailty and disability, higher rates of institutionalisation, and a heightened likelihood of recurrent falls [8]. Furthermore, falls impose a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems through costs associated with fall-related injuries, including acute hospitalizations, rehabilitation services, and long-term care requirements [9].

Physical activity is defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure, including activities undertaken during leisure, transport, work, or domestic tasks, and encompassing both moderate- and vigorous-intensity movements [10]. It represents a vital component of maintaining health throughout the lifespan and preventing adverse outcomes such as falls, with its importance becoming increasingly pronounced with advancing age. While structured exercise programs have been consistently demonstrated to reduce fall risk among older adults, emerging evidence indicates that regular engagement in light- to moderate-intensity physical activity also contributes to preserving muscle strength, balance, and mobility, thereby lowering the likelihood of falls and other adverse health outcomes [11]. Beyond fall prevention, physical activity contributes significantly to overall health maintenance by improving cardiovascular function, preventing metabolic disorders, and reducing the risk of cognitive impairment [12]. Furthermore, regular physical activity plays a crucial role in preserving functional independence among older adults [13].

Despite these well-documented benefits, there is a concerning tendency for individuals to become progressively less active as they age, leading to accelerated declines in physical function. Previous studies have demonstrated that physical inactivity affects approximately 21%, with 48.8% of older adults in Malaysia specifically classified as physically inactive [14, 15]. This high prevalence of inactivity can be attributed to various factors, including functional limitations, mobility impairments, chronic health conditions, and increasingly sedentary lifestyle behaviours. Understanding the longitudinal patterns of physical activity in older adults and identifying factors that influence these patterns is essential for developing effective strategies to promote healthy ageing.

Falls and physical activity in older adults demonstrate a reciprocal relationship, whereby reduced activity levels contribute to greater fall risk through declines in strength and balance [11], while experiencing a fall often triggers fear-related activity restriction, thereby perpetuating a detrimental cycle [16]. Previous studies have demonstrated that both falls status and physical activity patterns undergo significant transitions over time rather than following deterministic trajectories [17–19]. These transitions appear responsive to interventions targeting modifiable factors including muscle strength, balance training, and cognitive function [2, 20]. Understanding the temporal dynamics between these interrelated factors is essential for developing effective strategies to interrupt transition patterns and promote functional mobility in ageing populations [21].

Given the global ageing trend and its associated health challenges, investigating the longitudinal trajectories of falls and physical activity among community-dwelling older adults represents a research priority with significant public health implications [1, 22]. Such research can provide critical insights into the natural progression of these interrelated factors over time and illuminate their complex interactions. Moreover, identifying associated risk and protective factors will facilitate the development of targeted interventions and evidence-based policies aimed at reducing fall burden and promoting sustained physical activity among older adults. Therefore, in this study, we aim to investigate the longitudinal trajectories of falls and physical activity, as well as their associated factors, in community-dwelling older adults in Malaysia.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was part of the LRGS-TUA study, a population-based longitudinal study of community-dwelling older adults in Malaysia. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Medical Research and Ethics Committee of Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM 1.5.3.5/244/NN-060–2013), and it was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Eligible participants who agreed to participate were required to sign the informed consent form. The first wave of data collection took place from May 2012 to February 2013, while the second, third, and fourth waves of data collection occurred 18, 36, and 60 months after the first wave, respectively. The study design, sampling method, and inclusion and exclusion criteria were described in detail previously [23, 24]. In summary, community-dwelling individuals aged 60 years and above with no documented major psychiatric illness or mental disorders were included in the present study. As physical activity data were not collected during the first wave, it was excluded from the present analysis. Consequently, only data from the second, third, and fourth waves were included, with the second wave serving as the baseline for this analysis. A total of 1,327 participant who had completed the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE) questionnaire and with complete falls data were included in this study.

Data collection

The detailed methodology, including the interview protocol employed for data collection, has been documented in the LRGS-TUA longitudinal study as previously described [20]. In brief, trained enumerators were recruited to assist in data collection. The detailed methodology and data collection procedures for each domain are elaborated in the following sections.

Socio-demography data and medical history

Participants were interviewed face-to-face to collect socio-demographic data, including age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, living arrangement, smoking habits, and education level. Additionally, participants’ self-reported medical histories were recorded in this study. Multimorbidity was defined as the presence of two or more co-existing diseases in an individual, which may include conditions such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, stroke, osteoarthritis, heart diseases, cataract/glaucoma, renal failure, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, tuberculosis, gout, hip fracture, thyroid disorders, and cancer [25].

History of falls

Before recording the falls history, participants were provided with a clear definition of falls, following the WHO Global Report on Falls Prevention in Older Age as ‘an event whereby a person inadvertently coming to rest on the ground or lower level, excluding intentional change in position to rest on other objects’, as reported previously [26]. At baseline (second wave of data collection), participants were asked if they had experienced any falls in the past 18 months. For the following follow-up interviews, any falls occurring between the two visits were recorded.

Physical activity measurements

The Malay version of the PASE questionnaire assessed the physical activity of older adults over seven days in three different domains, namely leisure, household, and work-related activities [27]. It assigned a score based on the frequency, duration, and intensity level of activity over the previous week, with higher scores indicating greater physical activity. Participants were ranked according to their PASE scores and stratified into tertiles. The highest 33.3% were classified as the first tertile, representing high physical activity; the middle 33.3% as the second tertile, representing moderate physical activity; and the lowest 33.3% as the third tertile, representing low physical activity.

Mortality status

The mortality status of the participants was identified through the Mortality Data Matching Service provided by the National Registration Department (NRD), Malaysia, as reported previously [28].

Physical measurements and body composition

Participants’ body height and weight were measured using the Leicester Height Measure (CMS Weighing Equipment, UK) and the Tanita HD319 weighing scale (Tanita Corporation of America, IL, USA), respectively, to the nearest 0.1 cm and 0.1 kg. Body mass index (BMI) was then calculated using the formula ‘body weight (kg)/height (m)²’. Body composition was analyzed using Bio-electrical Impedance Analysis with the Inbody S10 device (Biospace, Seoul, Korea), following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Physical performance assessments

Participants’ physical performance was assessed through a series of tests, including a 2-minute step test, chair stand test, dominant handgrip strength test, and timed up and go (TUG) test. These assessments were used to evaluate the participants’ aerobic endurance, lower limb muscle strength, upper limb muscle strength, as well as mobility and balance status, respectively [24].

Cognitive status, psychosocial and functional status

The Malay versions of the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) were employed to assess the participants’ global cognitive function. The detailed methodology for conducting the neurocognitive assessment was previously described [24]. Additionally, the level of depression among participants was evaluated using the 15-item Malay version of the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15), while their disability level was assessed using the World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0 (WHODAS 2.0).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA), and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Univariate comparisons between groups were performed using one-way ANOVA for continuous data and the Chi-square test for categorical data. Then, the longitudinal transitions of falls and physical activity categories were analyzed using the multi-state Markov (MSM) model from the msm package in R statistical software version 4.2.3 [29]. Missing data on falls status and PASE scores were considered missing at random and were censored in the analysis. For falls trajectories, we defined ‘no fall’ as state 1, ‘1 fall’ as state 2, ‘2 or more falls’ as state 3, and ‘death’ as state 4. Meanwhile, for physical activity trajectories, ‘high physical activity,’ ‘medium physical activity,’ ‘low physical activity,’ and ‘death’ were defined as states 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. The origin states of falls and physical activity trajectories were set based on data collected at the second wave. Transitions in falls or physical activity categories occurred when there was a change in state in the subsequent wave. State 4 (all-cause mortality) is an absorbing state in the model because no transitions can emerge from it. The matrices of estimated transitions from wave X to wave X + 1 for each fall or physical activity state were calculated using the MSM model. Additionally, we included covariates in the MSM models to identify potential variables associated with the longitudinal transitions of falls or physical activity states. Since data from the first wave were excluded from the present analysis, all covariates were derived from the second wave and were treated as constant across subsequent waves. Covariates were considered associated with the longitudinal transitions of falls or physical activity states based on the calculated hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Statistical significance was determined if the 95% CI did not include 1.

Table 2 presents potential risk factors associated with transitions in falls states. Lower skeletal muscle mass and poorer performance in the TUG test were associated with an increased risk of transitioning from no falls to 1 fall and from 1 fall to ≥ 2 falls states. Participants with increasing age, lower years of education, lower scores in the 2-minute step test and chair stand test, poorer performance in MMSE and MoCA cognitive tests, higher depression scale score, and lower levels of physical activity were associated with a direct transition from no falls to ≥ 2 falls states. Meanwhile, being older, male, a smoker, and having lower MoCA test scores were associated with an increased risk of death among those in the ≥ 2 falls state. Additionally, individuals with higher skeletal muscle mass had a higher likelihood of reverting from the unfavourable ≥ 2 falls state to the more favourable no-fall state, while increasing age was associated with lower probabilities of transitioning from the ≥ 2 falls to 1 fall state.

Table 4 depicts the factors associated with the longitudinal transitions of physical activity statuses. Individuals with higher years of education had lower probabilities of transitioning from the high to medium physical activity. Higher BMI, lower scores in the chair stand test, and fewer lifestyle activities were associated with a higher likelihood of a direct shift from high to medium physical activity. Participants with hypertension and multimorbidity were less likely to transition from medium to high physical activity. Additionally, participants with low physical activity had lower chances of reverting to high physical activity if they were older, had lower performance in the 2-minute step test, MMSE, and MoCA test, and experienced higher levels of depressive symptoms and WHODAS 2.0 disability scores. The probabilities of reverting from low to medium physical activity were also associated with advancing age, decreased skeletal muscle mass, lifestyle activities, and performance in the chair stand test and TUG test. The risk of transitioning from low physical activity to death increased with advancing age, being male, having slower TUG test performance, and decreased with higher MMSE and MoCA cognitive scores.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the dynamic trajectories of falls and physical activity among community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years and over, with particular emphasis on identifying factors associated with bidirectional transitions between states. By employing a Multi-State Markov (MSM) model, we were able to characterize not only the risk factors for transitioning to unfavourable circumstances which is a common focus in existing literature, but also the protective factors facilitating improvement from unfavourable to favourable states. This bidirectional analytical approach represents a significant methodological advancement in falls and physical activity research, as it provides a more comprehensive understanding of the complex, non-linear nature of these health trajectories in older populations. This study findings contribute novel insights into the reversibility of falls risk and physical inactivity, offering evidence-based guidance for developing targeted interventions to prevent adverse outcomes and promote recovery among vulnerable older adults. Such information is crucial for informing clinical practice and public health strategies aimed at enhancing the health and wellbeing of an ageing population.

Our findings challenge the assumption that falls represent an irreversible downward trajectory for older adults, revealing significant potential for recovery. Individuals with a single fall showed a 63% probability of returning to a no falls state within a year, while even those with multiple falls maintained a 46% chance of transitioning to fewer falls. The relatively high probability of reversal observed, particularly among individuals with more than two falls, is noteworthy, as such patterns are less frequently reported in older populations where recurrent falls are typically viewed as markers of progressive decline. A plausible explanation is that some falls may have been precipitated by transient or situational factors, such as acute illness, medication side effects, or environmental hazards, rather than reflecting persistent frailty. Furthermore, older adults may have subsequently modified their behaviour, engaged in rehabilitation, or adapted their living environments following a fall, thereby reducing their likelihood of recurrence. These patterns align remarkably with Hartley et al.‘s (2023) TILDA cohort findings, strengthening evidence for falls reversibility as a generalizable phenomenon. However, the elevated mortality risk among frequent fallers (23% compared to 1–2% in non-fallers) highlights the serious consequences of recurrent falls and highlights the importance of intervention for this vulnerable group.

In contrast to the dynamic trajectories observed for falls, physical activity patterns exhibited considerable stability, with the majority of participants maintaining their baseline levels throughout the study period (57% high, 45% moderate, 61% low). However, nearly symmetrical transitions were also observed between adjacent categories, such as the 27% probability of moving from high to moderate activity and a comparable probability of transitioning from moderate to high. This indicates that while physical activity behaviours in later life often remain relatively stable, meaningful bidirectional shifts do occur, particularly between high and moderate activity groups. Such transitions may reflect short-term fluctuations in health status, lifestyle circumstances, or motivation rather than sustained behavioural change. Importantly, these findings suggest that although large-scale shifts in activity levels may be uncommon, even modest increases are achievable and, once established, may be maintained over time. This highlight the importance of early and ongoing promotion of physical activity, as relatively small gains could have lasting benefits for mobility and fall prevention in older adults.

Our findings revealed several shared factors influencing the longitudinal trajectories of both falls and physical activity among community-dwelling older adults. Age, education, physical fitness, cognitive function, and depressive symptoms emerged as significant determinants of transition probabilities between different states. This interrelationship is expected given the established bidirectional relationship between falls and physical activity documented in previous research, where higher physical activity levels are associated with reduced falls risk through improved muscle strength, balance, and functional ability [30, 31].

Advancing age demonstrated complex associations with both domains. Older age was associated with a lower probability of transitioning from lower to higher levels of physical activity, a pattern that may reflect age-related declines in physiological function, functional capacity, and overall health status [32]. Simultaneously, increasing age was associated with direct transitions from no falls to recurrent falls, from multiple falls to death, and reduced likelihood of recovery from multiple falls to single fall status, aligning with findings reported by Hartley et al. (2023) [33]. These age-related transitions likely reflect progressive physiological decline, increased frailty, and greater vulnerability to falls-related complications with advancing age [30, 34, 35].

Education level emerged as a protective factor, with higher education associated with lower probability of transitioning from no falls to frequent falls. This protective effect likely stems from greater health literacy, awareness of prevention strategies, and propensity for health-seeking behaviours among more educated individuals [36]. Similarly, higher education was associated with maintained physical activity levels, potentially due to better understanding of physical activity benefits and greater access to resources enabling active lifestyle maintenance [37].

Physical fitness indicators, including skeletal muscle mass and performance on measures such as chair stand test, 2-minute step test, and Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, consistently predicted favourable transitions in both domains. Greater muscle mass and better physical performance were associated with lower probability of declining to less favourable states and higher likelihood of improvement. This aligns with established understanding of sarcopenia and frailty as significant contributors to functional decline [38], where preserved muscle mass and strength enable greater mobility and activity engagement [39]. Conversely, higher BMI predicted transitions from high to low physical activity, likely reflecting limitations in physical capacity and performance associated with excess weight [40, 41].

The TUG test specifically emerged as a valuable predictor of falls transitions, with longer completion times predicting progression from no falls to single falls and from single to recurrent falls. Although gait speed has been identified as a predictor of falls in several studies, the predictive utility of the TUG test in community-dwelling populations has been questioned [42, 43]. However, our previous research demonstrated its effectiveness when applying modified cut-off values [44]. This aligns with the World Falls Guidelines (WFG), which emphasize the importance of individualized falls risk stratification over a universal approach [45, 46]. Emerging evidence suggests that muscle strength, alongside balance [5, 7], plays a crucial role in fall risk, highlighting the need for tailored assessment thresholds [47, 48]. Incorporating multidimensional evaluations can improve the accuracy of risk identification and enhance the effectiveness of targeted interventions. These findings collectively emphasize the importance of lifestyle interventions targeting muscle preservation to maintain physical activity and reduce falls risk [31, 49].

Cognitive function demonstrated significant associations with both falls and physical activity trajectories. Better cognitive performance, as measured using MMSE and MoCA, predicted lower probability of transitioning from no falls to recurrent falls and higher likelihood of transitioning from low to high physical activity. This bidirectional relationship reflects the complex interplay between cognition and physical function, where cognitive impairment can increase falls risk through disrupted executive function and sensory-motor processing [50, 51], while also limiting physical activity engagement through difficulties in planning and executing activities [52, 53]. Recent evidence from Muurling et al. (2023) confirms that cognitively normal older adults maintain higher physical activity levels than cognitively impaired counterparts [54]. The reciprocal relationship between physical activity and cognition [55] suggests potential synergistic benefits from interventions targeting both domains [56, 57].

Depressive symptoms demonstrated consistently negative impacts, with higher depression scores predicting reduced likelihood of transitioning to more active states and increased risk of transitioning from no falls to recurrent falls. These associations likely reflect depression’s multifaceted effects, including reduced motivation, social withdrawal, psychomotor retardation, impaired sleep, and attention deficits [58, 59]. Additionally, antidepressant medications may independently increase falls risk through side effects such as orthostatic hypotension, dizziness, and mental confusion [59].

Beyond these shared factors, our analysis identified domain-specific determinants. Hypertension and multimorbidity predicted reduced likelihood of transitioning from medium to high physical activity, reflecting how chronic conditions can impose physical limitations and medication side effects that constrain activity [60, 61]. Smoking specifically increased mortality risk by approximately 2.2 times among recurrent fallers, potentially due to compromised overall health status and impaired recovery from fall-related injuries [62, 63].

This study has notable limitations, particularly its reliance on self-reported fall events, which may introduce recall bias. Older adults may inaccurately recall fall incidents over time, a concern that is especially pronounced among individuals experiencing cognitive decline. This affects the precision of fall data and the validity of associated findings. Additionally, physical activity levels were assessed using the PASE questionnaire, which, despite being a widely used tool, may not fully capture variations in activity intensity and duration, potentially leading to misclassification. Moreover, while current evidence indicates that physically active or fit older adults generally have a lower risk of falling compared to their inactive or less fit counterparts, this does not preclude the possibility that fall risk may still be elevated among those who are active or fit. Future research should incorporate objective measures such as wearable sensors or fall diaries to enhance accuracy in tracking both falls and physical activity patterns.

Despite these limitations, this study offers important contributions as the first large-scale longitudinal analysis in Malaysia to examine falls and physical activity across consecutive survey waves. By identifying key predictors of fall recovery and activity transitions, the findings support the development of targeted interventions to improve mobility and reduce fall risks in ageing populations. Given the observed stability in activity levels, early interventions focusing on muscle mass preservation, cognitive health, and physical function are essential. Future research should explore the integration of multidomain strategies including strength training, cognitive stimulation, and behavioural interventions to enhance mobility and promote healthy ageing at both individual and community levels.

Conclusion

This longitudinal analysis of falls and physical activity trajectories among community-dwelling older adults highlights the dynamic interplay between these two domains. Our findings emphasize the role of modifiable factors such as muscle mass, functional performance, cognitive health, and psychological wellbeing in determining transitions within both falls and physical activity statuses. The bidirectional relationship between falls and physical activity highlights the need for integrated intervention approaches to prevent functional decline and promote active ageing. Regular assessment and multidimensional interventions addressing physical, cognitive, and psychological factors are essential to reducing falls burden, sustaining physical activity, and enhancing mobility in older populations.
