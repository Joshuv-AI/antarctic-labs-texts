Arterial stiffness and incident chronic kidney disease risk: a two-phase retrospective cohort study
Authors: Guang Yang, Xin Shen, Ying Ding, Bokai Cheng, Yansong Zheng, Jiahui Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe effect of arterial stiffness (AS) on incident chronic kidney disease (CKD) in healthy individuals remains unclear. In this study, we examined the relationship between AS, measured using brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV), and cardiovascular-metabolic comorbidities in predicting CKD development.MethodsThis two-phase retrospective cohort study used data from the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital (2009–2021). The cross-sectional phase included 83,354 participants (77,711 without CKD and 5,643 with CKD), whereas the longitudinal phase included 13,738 participants. The association between baPWV and incident CKD was assessed using Cox regression analysis with restricted cubic splines, stratification, and mediation.ResultsCKD exhibited a prevalence of 6.77%. Participants with CKD had significantly higher baPWV than those without CKD. Compared to the reference category (< 1,400 cm/s), participants with baPWV ≥ 1,800 cm/s showed markedly increased odds of CKD development. Longitudinal analysis identified 403 incident CKD cases (2.93%) over a mean follow-up of 2.95 ± 2.02 years. A dose–response relationship was observed between baPWV and CKD development (p < 0.001), with the highest baPWV category conferring a 65% greater risk (p < 0.05). Mediation analysis showed significant indirect effects through systolic blood pressure (52.9%), diastolic blood pressure (45.1%), hyperlipidaemia (27.5%), and glycated haemoglobin (15.3%) (all p < 0.05).ConclusionsElevated baPWV values are associated with greater incident CKD risk, with blood pressure and glucose-lipid metabolism dysfunction potentially mediating this association. Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a significant public health concern worldwide, marked by high incidence, various complications, and poor prognosis. The 2023 Sixth China Chronic Disease and Risk Factor Surveillance study revealed that approximately 82 million adults in China developed CKD between 2018 and 2019, with prevalence rates of 8.2% for CKD and 2.2% for kidney function impairment [1]. Previous studies have indicated that cardiovascular events are the most important cause of death among patients with CKD [2]. The 2024 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines recommend predicting cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in the CKD population to guide CVD prevention [3]. Arterial stiffness (AS) is prevalent among patients with CKD and serves as a significant risk factor for CKD development and renal function deterioration, and may also contribute to an increase in cardiovascular events among these patients [4–6]. Therefore, early identification of AS in patients with CKD is essential for preventing cardiovascular events and slowing CKD progression.

AS in patients with CKD is characterised by progressive vascular damage and decreased arterial compliance. AS assessment serves as a valuable tool for detecting early pathological changes in vascular structure and arterial wall dynamics, providing important insights into CVD risk in the CKD population [7]. Brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) is a widely used non-invasive method for assessing vascular function in clinical practice. This technique provides several distinct advantages, including operational simplicity and excellent reproducibility, establishing it as an important indicator for AS evaluation [8]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that baPWV is significantly correlated with declining renal function and proteinuria [9, 10]. Furthermore, elevated baPWV values are associated with an accelerated decline in the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [11].

Existing research on the CKD–AS relationship has primarily focused on patients with underlying comorbidities, with large-sample studies in healthy populations remaining scarce. Given the clinical need for early intervention strategies, this study examined the relationship between AS (assessed using baPWV) and CKD in a health screening cohort using both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. We focused on the ability of AS to predict CKD onset and its mediating effects, aiming to provide crucial insights for the early detection and primary prevention of CKD.

Methods

Study participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using a two-phase design. The initial phase comprised a cross-sectional analysis of individuals attending the Chinese People’s Liberation Army (PLA) General Hospital for routine health examinations from December 2009 to May 2021. The primary analysis included 83,354 participants, comprising 77,711 individuals with no prior CKD history and 5,643 with established CKD. Comparative analyses were performed to evaluate demographic features, metabolic comorbidities, and baPWV levels between participants with and without CKD. The subsequent phase constituted a longitudinal investigation encompassing 14,468 individuals from the initial phase who made multiple hospital visits. Each consecutive visit was separated by a minimum interval of 6 months. Our analyses focused on examining potential relationships among incident CKD, baPWV, and metabolic comorbidities through data collection and statistical evaluation. Baseline baPWV was measured at the initial health examination, and participants were followed for incident CKD development. The participant selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of study population. CKD: chronic kidney disease, baPWV: brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity, UACR: urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, HbA1c: glycated haemoglobin A1c

This research complied with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Medical Ethics Committee at the Chinese PLA General Hospital (reference number S2023-542-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data collection and study variables

Information was gathered across four domains: (1) baseline demographic and behavioural characteristics encompassing age, sex, smoking habits, and alcohol consumption; (2) medical history records, comprising coronary heart disease (CHD), hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus (DM), hyperuricemia, and hyperlipidaemia; (3) evaluation of physiological metrics, including body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), heart rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP); and (4) biochemical parameters, encompassing serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), serum uric acid (Ua), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and C-reactive protein (CRP).

Comprehensive methods for medical history collection and laboratory testing are detailed in the supplementary data.

Exposure variable

The exposure of interest was AS, quantified using baPWV. Measurements were obtained using an automated VP1000 PWV analyser (Omron, Kyoto, Japan) with cuffs placed on all four limbs to capture brachial and post-tibial pressure waveforms through plethysmographic and oscillometric sensors. The baPWV was calculated using the formula (La-Lb)/ΔTba, where ΔTba represented the transit time between waveforms and (La-Lb) represented the brachium-to-ankle distance based on participant height.

Based on established standards, participants were categorised into three groups: normal AS (baPWV < 1,400 cm/s), moderately elevated AS (1,400 ≤ baPWV < 1,800 cm/s), and severely elevated AS (baPWV ≥ 1,800 cm/s) [12]. In both the cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, the normal AS group served as the reference category.

Study outcomes

The principal outcome measure in this retrospective cohort study was incident CKD, identified as the initial documented CKD diagnosis throughout the observation period, coinciding with the return visit time-point. Therefore, the observed endpoint for all analysed participants was either (1) the identification of new-onset CKD based on exam metrics or (2) their last recorded examination (for those without incident CKD). CKD diagnosis required either the presence of albuminuria or an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² (the prevalence estimate based on single measurements). Albuminuria was established when the UACR exceeded 30 mg/g [1]. The 2021 CKD-EPI equation utilising serum creatinine values was employed for eGFR determination [13]. The earliest CKD diagnosis was also determined based on single measurements.

Participants underwent continuous surveillance from baseline assessment (2009–2021) through either CKD development or final follow-up encounter, with censoring at the earliest occurrence.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical data are reported as counts and proportions. Group comparisons for continuous measures were performed using Welch’s t-test or analysis of variance, whereas categorical variables were analysed using chi-square test.

To examine potential nonlinear associations between baPWV variations and study outcomes (CKD, eGFR, and UACR in the cross-sectional analysis; incident CKD in the longitudinal follow-up), we employed linear and Cox proportional hazards regression analyses incorporating restricted cubic splines (RCS). RCS modelling utilised four knots positioned at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles to capture flexible dose–response relationships.

Time-to-event analysis for CKD development was performed using Kaplan–Meier estimation, with survival curve comparisons performed using the log-rank method.

We employed Cox proportional hazards regression to assess the relationship between baPWV and CKD incidence. Sequential modelling approaches with progressive covariate adjustment were implemented to examine the influence of confounding variables on the observed associations. Model performance was evaluated across different adjustment levels to assess the stability of effect estimates, incorporating covariates with variance inflation factors < 10 to ensure reliable assessment of the effect of baPWV on cumulative CKD occurrence.

Stratified analyses utilising Cox regression were conducted across predefined subgroups defined by sex, age, HTN status, CHD presence, DM diagnosis, BMI categories, and HbA1c levels to identify potential interaction effects and confirm the consistency of primary findings across diverse patient subsets.

Causal mediation analysis was conducted using a quasi-Bayesian framework incorporating 100 simulation iterations. Time-to-CKD diagnosis was modelled using Weibull distribution parameters within R’s “survreg” function. The “mediation” package facilitated decomposition of total effects into direct and indirect pathways. Cox regression models incorporated observation time and event occurrence (CKD diagnosis), with confidence intervals (CIs) and significance testing derived using a simulation-based methodology.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and/or MSTATA software (www.mstata.com). Bidirectional hypothesis testing was used to establish statistical significance at p < 0.05.

A complete-case analysis was adopted without applying data imputation techniques, as the completeness of the dataset was high, and the occurrence of missing values for any primary analytical variable was < 1%.

Results

Cross-sectional analysis: study population demographics

The initial cross-sectional phase enrolled 83,354 participants, among whom 5,643 (6.77%) reported prior CKD. Baseline characteristics of the study population differed significantly across baPWV groups for all variables (all p < 0.01). Age increased progressively with increasing baPWV levels. Categorical variables, including sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, and comorbidities, showed significant inter-group variations. Continuous variables, including BMI, WC, blood pressure, and metabolic markers, consistently increased with increasing baPWV levels. Renal function markers indicated progressive deterioration, with declining eGFR and elevated ACR in higher baPWV groups (Table 1).

Cross-sectional analysis: baPWV distribution in the non-CKD and CKD groups

The mean baPWV was significantly higher in individuals with CKD (1,575.97 ± 255.42 cm/s) than in those without CKD (1,439.26 ± 214.82 cm/s, p < 0.001). Similarly, the distribution of baPWV categories differed markedly between the two groups (Supplementary Table S1). The violin plot shows the central tendency, variability, and data density of each group and further indicates that baPWV values were generally higher in the CKD group than in the non-CKD group (Supplementary Figure S1).

Cross-sectional analysis: relationships between baPWV, CKD, eGFR, and UACR

Supplementary Figure S2 shows the dose–response relationships between baPWV and renal function parameters using RCS analysis. In the adjusted models, the analysis revealed several distinct patterns: a progressive increase in the CKD odds ratio (OR) with increasing baPWV levels, showing a near-linear positive association (p-overall < 0.001, p-nonlinear = 0.315, Supplementary Figure S2A); a U-shaped relationship between baPWV and UACR, with the lowest values observed around 1,400–1,500 cm/s, followed by a sharp increase at higher baPWV levels (p-overall < 0.001, p-nonlinear < 0.001, Supplementary Figure S2B); a monotonic increase in SCr with increasing baPWV, displaying both significant overall and nonlinear associations (p-overall < 0.001, p-nonlinear = 0.001, Supplementary Figure S2C); and an inverse relationship between baPWV and eGFR, with a steeper decline observed at higher baPWV values (p-overall < 0.001, p-nonlinear = 0.145, Supplementary Figure S2D).

Logistic regression analysis revealed that higher baPWV was significantly associated with increased odds of CKD (Table 2). In the unadjusted Model 1, each SD increase in standardised baPWV was associated with a 67% increased risk of CKD (OR: 1.67, 95% CI: 1.63–1.71, p < 0.001). This association remained statistically significant after adjusting for covariates in Model 2 (OR: 1.35, 95% CI: 1.32–1.39, p < 0.001) and Model 3 (OR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.14–1.21, p < 0.001). When baPWV was categorised into predefined groups, participants with baPWV ≥ 1,800 cm/s demonstrated substantially elevated odds compared to the reference group (< 1,400 cm/s), with an OR of 5.19 (95% CI: 4.77–5.66, p < 0.001). A significant dose–response relationship was consistently observed across all models (p < 0.001).

Longitudinal cohort analysis: patient demographics and baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of all participants categorised by baPWV levels in the longitudinal cohort study phase are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Compared to the baPWV < 1,400 cm/s group, participants with higher baPWV (≥ 1800 cm/s) were older (53.16 vs. 44.86 years), predominantly male (75.14% vs. 64.71%), and demonstrated greater prevalence of HTN (51.14% vs. 12.43%), DM (21.57% vs. 5.99%), and CHD (4.43% vs. 1.01%). This group also exhibited poorer metabolic status, as evidenced by elevated BMI (25.77 vs. 24.37 kg/m²), WC (89.65 vs. 84.84 cm), and SBP (131.56 vs. 114.17 mmHg, all p < 0.001). Lipid profiles, including TC, TG, HDL-c, and LDL-c, also demonstrated significant deterioration with higher baPWV levels (p < 0.001). In addition, markers of renal function, including UACR (9.05 ± 5.74 mg/g) and eGFR (101.55 ± 10.47 mL/min/1.73 m²), were significantly impaired in the baPWV ≥ 1,800 cm/s group (p < 0.001).

Longitudinal cohort analysis: association between baPWV and incident CKD

This cohort study enrolled 13,738 participants who had no prior history of CKD and completed a minimum of two hospital visits. The duration of follow-up between initial and final assessments varied from 1 to 11 years. Throughout the observation period, 403 individuals (2.93%) experienced incident CKD development, with a mean time to event occurrence of 2.95 ± 2.02 years. Participants with elevated baPWV (≥ 1,800 cm/s) demonstrated a significantly greater incidence of new-onset CKD compared to those with lower values (6.43% vs. 2.15%) (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3).

Figure 1 shows the associations between baPWV values and new-onset CKD using multivariate Cox regression models with RCS. After adjusting for selected covariates, higher baPWV values remained consistently associated with increased hazard ratios (HR) for new-onset CKD (p < 0.001 in Fig. 2A and p = 0.005 in Fig. 2B). As shown in Fig. 1C, following adjustment for all covariates, the association was attenuated (p = 0.066), although the RCS curve demonstrated a similar trend. The p-nonlinear values across all three panels indicated no significant evidence of nonlinearity in the dose–response relationship (Fig. 2A–C).

Figure 2D presents the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis stratified by baPWV categories, demonstrating that participants in the highest baPWV group (≥ 1,800 cm/s) experienced the greatest cumulative incidence of CKD over the follow-up period, whereas those with lower baPWV values exhibited correspondingly lower event rates (log-rank p < 0.001; Fig. 2D).

The multiple Cox regression analysis revealed a trend toward higher baPWV being associated with increased HR for new-onset CKD; however, after adjusting for covariates, some models did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Table S4). The continuous form of baPWV showed minimal effect sizes across all models, with significant associations in Models 1 and 2 (both p < 0.01) but not in Model 3 (p = 0.056). The standardised form paralleled this finding (Models 1 and 2: p < 0.01, Model 3: p = 0.056). Significant associations between baPWV categories and new-onset CKD were observed across the three models. In Model 1 (unadjusted), individuals with baPWV levels ≥ 1,800 had a 220% increased risk of new-onset CKD (HR: 3.20, 95% CI: 2.29–4.47, p < 0.001). After adjusting for demographic and clinical factors in Model 2, the association was attenuated but remained significant for the highest baPWV category (HR: 1.84, 95% CI: 1.29–2.63, p < 0.001). In Model 3 (fully adjusted), the highest baPWV category remained associated with a 65% increased risk of new-onset CKD (HR: 1.65, 95% CI: 1.13–2.41, p = 0.010). A significant trend was observed across all models (p < 0.05).

Longitudinal cohort analysis: subgroup analysis of the association between baPWV and new-onset CKD

Subgroup analyses using Cox regression revealed significant associations between baPWV and new-onset CKD across various subgroups. In the overall population (n = 13,738), the HR for incident CKD was 1.26 (95% CI: 1.16–1.38, p < 0.001). When stratified by sex, both female (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.07–1.51, p = 0.006) and male (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.14–1.41, p < 0.001) participants demonstrated significant associations, with no significant interaction by sex (p for interaction = 0.985).

Age subgroups showed varying effects, with the strongest association observed in individuals aged ≥ 60 years (HR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.18–1.92, p = 0.001), whereas no significant association was observed in those aged < 40 years (HR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.74–1.45, p = 0.838). The interactions by age, CHD, HTN, DM, BMI, and HbA1c levels were not significant (all p-interaction > 0.05). However, within these subgroups, significant associations were observed in participants without CHD (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.16–1.39, p < 0.001), without HTN (HR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.01–1.33, p = 0.031), with HTN (HR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.02–1.33, p = 0.029), without DM (HR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.13–1.40, p < 0.001), with BMI < 24 kg/m² (HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.14–1.62, p = 0.001), with BMI ≥ 24 kg/m² (HR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.11–1.37, p < 0.001), and with HbA1c < 7% (HR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.13–1.37, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that baPWV is consistently associated with increased CKD incidence across most subgroups, with no significant effect modification by the examined variables (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S5).

Mediation analysis

The total effect of baPWV on new-onset CKD risk was statistically significant for most mediators, with coefficients ranging from − 0.0402 to -0.1310 (all p < 0.05), except for alcohol consumption, hyperuricaemia, and hyperlipidaemia (p > 0.05). The direct effect of baPWV on new-onset CKD risk remained significant across all mediators, with coefficients ranging from − 0.0402 to -0.1128 (all p < 0.05). Significant indirect effects were observed for age (coefficient: -0.0167, p < 0.001), BMI (coefficient: -0.0182, p < 0.001), WC (coefficient: -0.0178, p < 0.001), SBP (coefficient: -0.0588, p < 0.001), DBP (coefficient: -0.0527, p < 0.001), Ua (coefficient: -0.0071, p < 0.001), TG (coefficient: -0.0086, p < 0.001), HDL-c (coefficient: -0.0078, p < 0.001), FBG (coefficient: -0.0161, p < 0.001), HbA1c (coefficient: -0.0160, p < 0.001), and CRP (coefficient: -0.0009, p < 0.001). The proportion of the total effect mediated by these factors ranged from 0.8% to 52.9%. Notably, SBP and DBP exhibited the highest proportions of mediation, accounting for 52.9% and 45.1% of the total effect, respectively. Other mediators, such as age, BMI, and WC, also contributed significantly, with proportions ranging from 13.5% to 14.1% (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

This study revealed that individuals with CKD exhibited significantly elevated mean baPWV values compared to their non-CKD counterparts, reflecting increased AS among the CKD population. Through subsequent follow-up analysis, we identified a notable association between increased AS and CKD development, with the strongest relationship observed in individuals aged ≥ 60 years, those without CHD or DM, and those with HbA1c levels < 7.0%. Mediation analysis demonstrated that hyperuricaemia, hyperlipidaemia, hyperglycaemia, HbA1c, and FBG were potential mediators linking baPWV to CKD onset. These findings establish a clear dose–response relationship between elevated baPWV levels and increased CKD burden. Among patients with pre-existing CKD, we observed a linear correlation between AS and CKD. Regarding the relationship between AS and new-onset CKD, the correlation between baPWV and incident CKD was attenuated after adjusting for covariates, suggesting that both vascular pressure dynamics and metabolic processes governing glucose and lipid homeostasis are associated with disease progression.

Hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, and DM constitute the core components of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome, serving as pivotal pathogenic factors for both AS and CKD. This study elucidates a complex bidirectional crosstalk within CKM. AS may amplify metabolic disturbances, thereby accelerating renal parenchymal injury through haemodynamic perturbations and oxidative stress. Conversely, metabolic disorders may exacerbate vascular stiffness via endothelial dysfunction and inflammatory pathways, establishing a deleterious cycle. Consistent with the American Heart Association’s recent CKM definition, our findings substantiate a holistic management paradigm by identifying AS, quantified via baPWV, as a viable therapeutic target for CKD prevention in high-risk cohorts. This not only validates baPWV’s predictive utility for incident CKD but also delineates the intricate network of vascular, renal, and metabolic interactions, offering a comprehensive framework for clinical practice.

The positive association of baPWV with both prevalent CKD and incident CKD populations in our study was generally consistent with findings from previous investigations. The Health ABC Study, focusing on 2,129 older adults, demonstrated that AS markers were significantly associated with kidney disease development. Aortic pulse wave velocity predicted incident CKD, whereas pulse pressure predicted both rapid kidney function decline and incident CKD, suggesting that arterial rigidity was associated with renal disease progression [14]. A Rotterdam study including participants aged ≥ 55 years demonstrated that each SD increase in PWV was associated with a 7% higher risk of incident CKD, and confirmed that other AS indicators, including pulse pressure and carotid stiffness, were associated with accelerated decline in kidney function. Each SD increase in carotid stiffness was associated with a 0.08 mL/min/1.73 m² steeper annual eGFR decline [15]. An analysis of a community-based cohort in China demonstrated a clear inverse linear relationship between baseline baPWV and the subsequent rate of eGFR decline: after full adjustment, each 100 cm/s increase in baseline baPWV was associated with an extra annual decrease of 0.14 mL/min/1.73 m² in eGFR [16]. The Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study reached similar conclusions by expanding the assessment to include intercorrelated segments of PWV parameters, encompassing carotid-femoral PWV, heart-femoral PWV, heart-ankle PWV, baPWV, heart-carotid PWV, and femoral-ankle PWV. This multidimensional approach validated the association between AS and subsequent incidence of CKD and kidney function decline in a cohort of community-dwelling older adults [11]. Additional studies carried out in diverse regions and populations have further substantiated the pathophysiological role of AS in incident CKD and shown that AS parameters can predict declines in eGFR [17, 18]. Several investigators have examined the effect of AS on renal function decline in patients with pre-existing CKD. Yun et al. reported that female participants with an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² exhibited significantly higher baPWV than those with eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m² and that baPWV increased in a stepwise, inverse relationship with eGFR decline [19]. McIntyre et al. conducted a prospective cohort study of 1,741 patients with early-stage CKD (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m²) followed over 1–5 years that demonstrated that PWV values increased progressively as eGFR levels declined, indicating worsening AS concurrent with CKD progression [20]. Likewise, Chen et al. followed 145 patients with CKD stages 3–5 and demonstrated that an elevated baPWV (> 1,964 cm/s) was independently associated with progression to dialysis or death [21]. Unlike previous research, our findings comprehensively demonstrates the close relationship between AS severity and CKD development using both cross-sectional and longitudinal cohort study designs. Cross-sectional analysis confirmed that AS is more severe in populations with existing CKD, whereas longitudinal cohort analysis further validated the predictive role of AS in incident CKD, thereby providing multidimensional evidence for the association between AS and CKD. Moreover, our study population comprised healthy individuals undergoing routine health examinations, instead of focusing solely on populations with specific underlying diseases or older adults, thereby emphasising the importance of early CKD screening and risk stratification. In our Cox regression analysis, the association between continuously scaled baPWV and CKD risk was attenuated to borderline significance (p = 0.056) in the fully adjusted model, whereas the categorical analysis remained statistically significant. This pattern suggests that AS may be more useful for stratifying high-risk populations than for demonstrating a strict linear dose–response relationship with CKD risk. Alternatively, it may indicate that continuous measures are more susceptible to the effects of multicollinearity following extensive covariate adjustment.

Although AS and CKD exhibit a bidirectional relationship, evidence primarily indicates that AS is temporally associated with CKD development. Tian et al. used cross-lagged path analysis to elucidate the temporal relationship between baPWV and eGFR. Their analyses confirmed a unidirectional relationship from baseline AS to follow-up eGFR decline, indicating that increased AS may be temporally associated with eGFR decline [18]. The presence of AS impairs vascular compliance, creating elevated flow velocities that adversely affect the vulnerable microcirculation within the kidneys. These haemodynamic alterations promote endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress, eventually leading to ischaemic microvascular injury and deterioration of renal function [17]. In this study, we employed RCS to examine both linear and nonlinear associations between baPWV and CKD across cross-sectional and longitudinal study designs. Our findings demonstrated a statistically significant linear correlation in the cross-sectional analysis, whereas non-linearity testing yielded non-significant p-values in both study phases. However, within the cohort investigation, covariate adjustment resulted in the attenuation of the linear association between baPWV and incident CKD, a finding corroborated through Cox proportional hazards modelling. To further elucidate these relationships, we conducted mediation and stratified analyses, which revealed that the baPWV–new-onset CKD association operates through intermediary pathways involving blood pressure, hyperuricaemia, and glucose-lipid metabolism. Therefore, in the association between AS and incident CKD, CVD and multiple metabolic disorders may accelerate this process [22–24]. Recently, the American Heart Association has established the concept of CKM syndrome, which provides enhanced recognition of the intricate pathophysiological interactions between CVD, kidney disease, and metabolic disorders [25]. Recent studies demonstrate a remarkably high prevalence of metabolic syndrome comorbidity (54%) among patients with newly diagnosed CKD, with individuals with both hypertension and diabetes representing the highest-risk population for CKD development [26]. Our prior research also validated that AS serves as a critical mediator in the pathophysiological pathway linking insulin resistance and metabolic comorbidities to elevated CKD risk [27], with moderate to severe AS exhibiting a significant positive association with CKD development in older populations (data under review for publication). Hyperglycaemia induces endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses through activation of the advanced glycation end products pathway, thereby accelerating AS and glomerular sclerosis [28, 29]. The triglyceride-glucose index, derived from TG and FBG measurements, exhibits a significant independent association with AS in patients with DM [30]. Furthermore, cumulative blood pressure load serves as an effective predictor for both AS progression and diabetic kidney disease incidence [31]. Sustained hypertension exacerbates cardiac afterload and promotes glomerular hyperfiltration, resulting in renal haemodynamic characterised by high perfusion and low vascular resistance, which renders the kidneys particularly susceptible to pulsatile pressure-induced damage and ultimately establishes a deleterious cycle of cardiorenal dysfunction [32]. Based on literature review and our findings, we speculate that AS severity directly correlates with CKD incidence, with concurrent hypertension and glucose-lipid metabolic disorders synergistically accelerating disease progression in this high-risk population. This highlights the clinical benefit of early intervention targeting modifiable risk factors, particularly in individuals with established AS. Therefore, clinical practice should prioritise comprehensive assessment and monitoring of AS parameters, baPWV, as fundamental components of patient management.

This study has some limitations. First, our cross-sectional classification of CKD was based on single measurements and may not reflect the true prevalence of CKD as defined by KDIGO guidelines (which require persistence of abnormalities for ≥ 3 months). The relatively short follow-up period restricted our ability to observe the long-term progression trajectory of CKD. In addition, the predominance of microalbuminuria among incident CKD cases resulted in reduced statistical power and precluded the presentation of CKD staging data (Supplementary Tables S7 and S8). Second, we did not collect data on the use of medications, such as RAS inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists during follow-up, which may have influenced CKD incidence, especially in participants with longer follow-up durations. Third, as an observational study, we cannot establish causal relationships between baPWV and CKD development. Despite comprehensive covariate adjustment, residual confounding from unmeasured variables remains possible. Therefore, the associations observed should not be interpreted as evidence of causation without confirmation from randomised controlled trials or other causal inference approaches. Lastly, the mediation analysis should be interpreted with caution as potential mediators were measured at baseline concurrently with baPWV exposure, limiting causal inference regarding temporal mediation pathways. Thus, the findings require confirmation through longitudinal measurement of mediator variables. Future studies incorporating prospective designs, larger sample sizes, and extended follow-up periods to provide more comprehensive evidence are also warranted.

Conclusions

This retrospective cohort investigation revealed a significant relationship between baPWV as an AS marker and CKD development, showing that elevated baPWV values were associated with greater susceptibility to incident CKD. In addition, mediation analyses identified hypertension and glucose-lipid metabolism dysfunction as potential mediators in this association. Further research is needed to determine whether interventions targeting AS and metabolic parameters reduce the incidence of CKD.
