Factors associated with HIV infection among adolescents and young people in Mozambique: sub-analysis of immunization, malaria and HIV/AIDS indicator survey in Mozambique-IMASIDA 2015
Authors: Eduardo Mangue Chicanequisso, Acácio Sabonete, Jahit Sacarlal, Érika Valeska Rossetto, Muemed Nury Cassimo, Cynthia Semá Baltazar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHIV infection is of concern, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa which is home to two thirds (67%) of the total number of people living with HIV (PLHIV) in the world. In Southern Africa, Mozambique ranks second, with about 2.2 million PLHIV. Adolescents and young people represent priority groups in the fight against HIV. We set out to assess factors associated with HIV infection among youth which are priority groups for HIV prevention, due to their increased vulnerability to infection.MethodsThe 2015 Immunization, Malaria and HIV/AIDS Indicators Survey in Mozambique was used to identify factors associated with HIV infection among 4,247 participants aged 15–24 years. In bivariate analyses, these variables were included in multiple logistic regression models that were run separately for male and female. Modeled adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for variables independently associated with HIV infection.ResultsIn 2015, 295/4,247 (6.9%) of youth were HIV-infected (females, 237/2,427 [9.8%]). Factors associated with HIV infection in males included being a widower (AOR = 52.69, 95%CI: 2.07-1340.33, p = 0.02). Living in Niassa or Sofala was a protective factor in relation to the reference province (Nampula). In females, being aged 20–24 years (AOR = 2.06, 95%CI: 1.41-3.00, p < 0.001), widowed (AOR = 15.59, 95%CI: 3.99–60.77, p < 0.001) or separated (OR = 2.11, 95%CI: 1.09–4.08, p = 0.03), having age of first cohabitation below 18 years (AOR = 1.71, 95%1.14–2.59, p = 0.01), residing in Zambézia (AOR = 3.52, 95%CI:1.06–11.65, p = 0.04) and being wealthier (AOR = 2.20, 95%CI: 1.11–4.36, p = 0.02) were associated with HIV infection. Having completed higher education was a protective factor (AOR = 0.07, 95%CI:0.01–0.58, p = 0.02) in comparison to those with no school education.ConclusionThe factors associated with HIV in females are biological and sociocultural. Strategies tailored to girls and young women with the aim of reducing gender inequalities, combating harmful traditional practices and increasing educational opportunities are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24122-z.Keywords: Mozambique, HIV seroprevalence, Adolescents and young people, Youth, Epidemiologic factors

Background

After four decades, the fight against the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) epidemic remains one of the greatest challenges of global and national public health policies [1], due to the continuous increase of the population of people living with HIV (PLHIV) [2].

Globally, about 37.7 million people were living with HIV at the end of 2020. Sub-Saharan Africa is home to two thirds (67%) of all people living with HIV (PLHIV) [3]. The number of new infections is highest in youth aged between 15 and 24, with two out of every seven new HIV infections worldwide in 2019 occurring in people in this age group [4]. Cases tend to be more prevalent in females compared to males. UNAIDS data show that in sub-Saharan Africa, six out of seven new HIV infections in adolescents (15–19 years) occur among girls. Females aged 15–24 are twice as likely to be living with HIV as males [3].

In Southern Africa, Mozambique ranks second with around 2.1 million PLHIV. It is also estimated that there were about 98,000 adults and children newly infected with HIV and 38,000 deaths due to AIDS in 2020 in Mozambique [5].

In 2015, the Survey on Immunization, Malaria and HIV/AIDS Indicators (IMASIDA) was conducted in Mozambique. The prevalence of HIV in people aged 15 to 49 years was 13.2%, with the two extremes of 5.2% in Tete and 24.4% in Gaza provinces, respectively. Urban areas had a prevalence of 16.8%, compared to 11.0% in rural areas [6].

The reported prevalence of HIV in adolescents and young people aged 15–24 years (designated “youth”, according to the UNAIDS definitions [4]) in IMASIDA was 6.9% [7], which is considered very high when compared to other countries in the region [8]. In some of Mozambique’s neighboring countries, such as Tanzania, Malawi and Zambia, the average HIV prevalence among adolescents of both sexes was 2.5%, 2.1% and 3.5%, respectively [9].

The high prevalence of HIV among youth has been attributed to a variety of factors, including biological, socioeconomic, cultural, and behavioral. Several studies on HIV infection among adolescents have shown that sex, age, place of residence, inconsistent condom use and multiple lifetime sexual partners and intergenerational sex were associated with HIV infection [10, 11].

The factors that contribute to high HIV prevalence in young people were not sufficiently explored or comprehensively addressed broadly in the IMASIDA final report. While certain variables are recognized as risk factors for HIV infection among Mozambican youth, the specific contribution of each factor remains unclear. This knowledge gap underscores the importance of investigating the factors associated with high HIV infection rates in this population.

Methods

Study design and data collection

This analysis is based on data collected during the 2015 IMASIDA, a multi-stage cross-sectional national population-based household survey representative of the population of Mozambique. The survey sample frame was based on the 2007 Mozambique population census, and the sample was stratified by province and urban and rural area [6]. Survey results were weighted for non-response and to reflect the population distribution of the country. Respondents were asked to provide informed consent for the interview and to conduct rapid HIV testing. For each participant who agreed to participate in HIV testing, the rapid HIV testing algorithm outlined by the Ministry of Health of Mozambique was followed, as described in the survey report [6]. This algorithm consists of initially performing the “Determine” HIV-1/2 test, following the manufacturer’s instructions. For reactive samples, a second rapid test (Uni-gold HIV) was performed.

For the purpose of prevalence calculation, four assays were used in series, namely, two enzyme immunoassays (EIA), Geenius™ HIV 1/2 Rapid Confirmation test (Bio-Rad, France) (rapid confirmatory HIV test) and the polymerization chain reaction (PCR). Inconclusive and indeterminate samples were treated as negative. Samples reactive on the two EIA and Geenius were considered to be HIV-positive. Samples reactive on the EIA, but non-reactive or indeterminate on Geenius, were further analyzed by PCR. Samples with a negative PCR result were considered HIV-negative for the purpose of calculating HIV prevalence. Samples with a positive PCR result were considered HIV-positive [6].

This secondary analysis included youth aged 15–24 years. All those who did not belong to the age group of interest were excluded from the secondary analysis. aligning with the UNAIDS definition of youth as a critical age group in the context of HIV prevention and intervention. Individuals outside this age range were excluded from the analysis to maintain a clear focus on the factors influencing HIV infection among youth [12].

To better understand and study the factors associated with HIV infection, we categorized the variables into three main groups: biological factors, which are non-controllable or fixed variables (such as age group and sex [13]); behavioral factors, which depend on individual choices and actions (including condom use during last sexual encounter, number of sexual partners, duration of cohabitation, number of sex partners in the last 12 months, whether the husband/partner is circumcised, and age at first cohabitation); and finally, socioeconomic determinants, which are not entirely within the individual’s control (such as marital status, religion, educational level, place of residence, occupational status, province, and wealth index), also referred to as underlying determinants [14].

Statistical procedures and analysis

The analysis for this study was conducted using data from the 2015 IMASIDA survey. The IMASIDA questionnaire database was merged with the HIV results database, for both male and female respondents. This merge was based on three key variables common to both databases: cluster number, household number, and respondent’s line number. Subsequently, a subset of the database containing the variables of interest was used for the analysis. The outcome of interest was serological status, categorized as positive or negative, as described earlier. All participants without HIV test results (915 females and 847 males) were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1). These participants completed the interview but did not provide consent for HIV testing, and therefore, no blood sample was collected as this procedure was not included in the IMASIDA research protocol.

Flowchart of the selection of participants included in this analysis

To ensure the representativeness of the results, all data were weighted during the analysis. Weighting was applied to account for the complex sampling design, allowing the findings to be generalizable to the national and provincial levels. This approach aligns with the IMASIDA reports, where only the weighted figures are considered representative of the broader Mozambican population [7].

The analyses were performed using the STATA statistical package, version 16.1. The data were weighted to account for the complex sample design, ensuring the representativeness of the findings. The survey weights and sample design were incorporated into the regression models using the “svyset” and “svy”: logistic commands in STATA. These commands adjust for the complex sampling structure by accounting for clustering, stratification, and unequal probabilities of selection, resulting in unbiased estimates and accurate standard errors that are representative of the Mozambican population.

To identify factors associated with HIV infection, a binary logistic regression model was initially applied, with HIV status as the dependent variable. Odds ratios (OR) were calculated to assess the associations between variables. Variables that were statistically significant (p < 0.05) in the bivariate analysis were then included in a multivariate model. Multiple logistic regression was used to identify factors independently associated with HIV status. The regression models incorporated survey weights and accounted for the complex sample design, ensuring that the findings were representative of the Mozambican population.

Results

Prevalence of HIV by sociodemographic characteristics

The sample consisted of 4,247 youth aged 15 to 24 years, 1,820 (42.9%) males and 2,427 (57.2%) females. The prevalence of HIV among this population was 6.9%. HIV prevalence was higher among females than males (9.8% versus 3.2%), with the 20–24 age group being the most affected, showing a prevalence of 13.3% among females and 5.5% among males, as illustrated in Table 1. Participants with primary and secondary education levels were among the most likely to be HIV-infected, with prevalence rates of 6.9% and 6.8%, respectively.

Analysis of factors of exposure to HIV

Table 2 presents the results of the bivariate analysis, where six variables (age group, province, marital status, number of unions, cohabitation time, and condom use at last sex) showed significant associations with HIV status. However, in the multivariate analysis, only province and marital status remained statistically significant for male participants. Specifically, residing in Niassa (AOR = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.01–0.64, p = 0.02) or Sofala (AOR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.03–0.57, p = 0.01) was found to be a protective factor against HIV infection compared to the reference province, Nampula. Additionally, being widowed was strongly associated with a higher risk of HIV infection (AOR = 52.69, 95% CI: 2.07-1340.33, p = 0.02) compared to those who were never married. The remaining variables did not show statistically significant associations with HIV status in males.

The analysis further revealed that widowed females were fifteen times more likely to be HIV-positive (AOR = 15.59, 95% CI: 3.99–60.77, p < 0.001), and separated females were twice as likely (AOR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.09–4.08, p = 0.03) compared to females who had never been in a union. Household wealth also played a significant role, with women in wealthier households being over twice as likely to be infected with HIV (AOR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.11–4.36, p < 0.02) compared to those in the poorest households.

Additionally, early cohabitation, defined as being under 18 years of age at first cohabitation, increased the likelihood of HIV infection by 70% (AOR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.14–2.59, p = 0.01) compared to those who began cohabitation at 18 years or older.

While other factors, such as the number of sexual partners in the last 12 months (OR = 2.64, 95% CI: 1.19–3.60, p = 0.01) and the age of first sexual intercourse (OR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.12–2.29, p < 0.01), were significant in the crude models, they were not significantly associated with HIV status after adjusting for other variables.

Discussion

We found several individual and socioeconomic factors to be associated with HIV status. To ensure representativeness we used weighted data to closely approximate the true population distribution in Mozambique. While behavioral factors, identified in the literature as key to HIV infection, did not prove statistically significant in our adjusted models, they should not be disregarded. Some effects may become more evident in later age groups, following a greater cumulative exposure over time.

The prevalence of HIV was particularly high among young females, where it is up to three to four times higher than in males of the same age group [14–16]. Although some associations of factors with HIV infection in males had large odds ratios, they were not statistically significant, except age group. This may be due to the lower prevalence of HIV in males in this study. research is needed to explore variables such as marital status, length of cohabitation, number of unions throughout life, and condom use during the last sexual intercourse, to better understand the factors that increase HIV risk among young males.

Our results revealed a strong association between HIV and marital status, particularly among widows This aligns with previous research conducted in South Africa, which reported that widows have up to twice the risk of HIV infection compared to their married counterparts [17]. The increased vulnerability of females to HIV infection, as observed in our study, is consistent with broader epidemiological trends indicating that women are generally more susceptible to HIV than men [17, 18]. It is plausible that, in many instances, HIV transmission to the widow occurred prior to the death of her spouse, highlighting the complex interplay between marital status and HIV risk.

The prevalence of HIV in females in Mozambique was high during the course of several HIV surveys [6, 19]. This heightened vulnerability is partly attributed to biological factors, as female anatomy inherently increases the risk of HIV acquisition [20]. Research on African females has highlighted that vaginal microbial diversity and specific bacterial concentrations are associated with an elevated risk of HIV infection [21]. Additionally, socio-behavioral factors, such as engaging in sex in exchange for favors, contribute to this increased risk [22]. The gender gap in HIV prevalence is further exacerbated by age-related vulnerabilities among young females, which are compounded by pervasive gender inequalities [22, 23]. For instance, cultural practices like of “maranza1” in Mozambique and “sugar daddy” in Tanzania, where young females engage in sexual relationships with older males in exchange for material goods or social advancement, are significant drivers of HIV transmission among females. These older partners often belong to age groups with higher HIV prevalence and may have multiple sexual partners. Consequently, young females frequently find themselves unable to negotiate condom use due to the power imbalances inherent in these relationships [8].

Extensive research indicates that sexual relationships with older males significantly elevate the risk of HIV infection among young females [10, 24]. Our study’s findings align with this evidence, demonstrating that young females in such relationships are at a heightened risk of HIV, particularly when these relationships are initiated at an early age or within the context of premature marriages. This early initiation of sexual activity, combined with limited power to negotiate condom use, severely compromises a young woman’s ability to protect herself from HIV infection, thereby increasing her vulnerability. The strong association observed between age, marital status, and HIV infection in our analysis underscores the critical need for targeted interventions that address these risk factors.

In this analysis, condom used during last sex with most recent partner was not statistically significant, even in the face of numerous studies that prove the effectiveness of condom use in combating the transmission of HIV. A large proportion of young people aged 15–25 persist in not using condoms in Mozambique and other southern African countries [19, 25, 26]. Many factors play a role in this, including: early onset of sexual activity, women’s limited bargaining power, low educational level, omitting the use of condoms during sexual interactions (the lack of a condom or a desire not to interrupt pleasure) and having a steady partner [25, 27]. On the other hand, condom use can only be an effective method when used consistently. Therefore, sexual health programmes for youth (known as SAAJ in Mozambique) should be strengthened, guaranteeing easy access to free condoms, especially in low-income contexts. The Ministry of Health’s Health at School Programme plays an important role in increasing access to information about the risks of early sexual activity and the need for correct and consistent condom use.

Our results indicate that males who are in one or more unions or marriages have a higher risk of HIV infection, primarily due to increased sexual exposure. This finding is consistent with research across Africa, which shows that sexual transmission is the predominant route of HIV infection [28–30]. Despite the clear association, we excluded this factor from the final regression model for males due to collinearity with other variables. This suggests that while multiple unions are an important risk factor, their effects may overlap with other critical variables, such as age and sexual behavior, complicating their independent assessment in our analysis.

In our study, we were unable to verify the association between HIV status and the practice of voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) among the partners of female participants. Despite this limitation, VMMC remains an important and cost-effective intervention, offering significant protection against HIV transmission from females to males. UNAIDS recommends VMMC as part of a comprehensive HIV prevention package in several countries across eastern and southern Africa [31]. Studies indicate that circumcision can reduce the risk of HIV infection by up to 60% compared to uncircumcised individuals [32, 33].

Our findings underscore the significant influence of sociodemographic, economic, and behavioral factors on the transmission of HIV among the young population and their partners. These results suggest that if Mozambique is to drastically reduce HIV prevalence, it is essential to place young females at the center of the response. This strategy should involve addressing gender inequality, combating harmful traditional practices such as initiation rites (which are prevalent in the northern and central regions of Mozambique) [34–36], preventing child marriage, increasing educational opportunities, and discouraging transgenerational sexual relationship.

However, this study has several limitations. The data used may not fully reflect the current situation, as it has been several years since the IMASIDA survey was conducted. Additionally, the use of secondary data presents challenges, as the survey did not capture all potential risk factors, such as drug use, smoking, and sexual orientation. Another limitation is the cross-sectional design of the survey, which limits our ability to establish a temporal relationship between exposure to risk factors and the outcome (HIV infection). Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data for variables such as sexual behavior and condom use introduces the possibility of recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially leading to an underestimation or overestimation of the associations observed. While other important risk and protective factors were collected in the original survey, they were not addressed in this secondary analysis.

Conclusion

The prevalence of HIV among youth in Mozambique is notably high, particularly among females. This high prevalence is influenced by individual factors that reduce women’s bargaining and decision-making power regarding their sexual and reproductive health. To gain a better understanding of HIV risk among young males, there is a need for more diversified studies that explore the specific associations and factors contributing to their HIV infection rates.

The findings of this study highlight the importance of considering factors such as age at first cohabitation, education level, wealth index, and marital status when addressing youth HIV prevalence. These factors are also linked to the broader HIV epidemic in Mozambique. Targeted health interventions that focus on these determinants, particularly those aimed at empowering young females, could lead to more effective HIV prevention and resource optimization.
