Advanced hybrid-green ultrasound–infrared–microwave Trifolium repens essential oil isolation with multi-target ethnomedicine bioactivity against neuropathy, inflammation and multidrug-resistant infection
Authors: Sara Al Ayoubi, Ibrahim Abdelwahab, Maha Aboul-Ela, Abdalla El-Lakany, Karim Raafat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Trifolium repens (white clover) is rich in bioactive volatiles with therapeutic potential. This study developed an advanced hybrid ultrasound–infrared–microwave hydro-distillation (UIMHD) method for efficient essential oil (EO) isolation and investigated its active sesquiterpene, pseudoionone, for multi-mechanistic neuroprotection in diabetic neuropathy (DN). The optimized UIMHD system synergistically combined ultrasound cavitation, infrared heating, and microwave hydro-distillation, enhancing yield and reducing time versus conventional extraction. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) profiling identified pseudoionone as the dominant constituent. Pharmacological evaluations were performed in alloxan-induced diabetic mice to assess antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and anticonvulsant activities. Cytokine modulation (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10), oxidative stress markers (CAT, GSH, TBARS), and strychnine-induced seizure assays were conducted to elucidate mechanisms. The UIMHD technique increased T. repens essential oil (TR-EO) yield by 62.5% and shortened isolation time by 50%. Pseudoionone (49.16%) exhibited potent hypoglycemic and insulin-restorative effects, alleviated thermal hyperalgesia and tactile allodynia, and improved antioxidant defenses. Both the TR-EO and pseudoionone significantly down-regulated TNF-α and IL-6 while up-regulating IL-10, indicating anti-inflammatory cytokine balance restoration. Additionally, pseudoionone delayed strychnine-induced tonic seizures, suggesting functional involvement of glycinergic pathways. Strong antibacterial and antibiofilm activities were also observed against multidrug-resistant Shigella dysenteriae. The advanced UIMHD method provides a sustainable platform for isolating bioactive volatiles from T. repens. Pseudoionone emerges as a multi-target natural agent mitigating diabetic neuropathy via TNF-α/IL-6/IL-10 modulation, oxidative- stress restoration, and strychnine-sensitive GlyR potentiation.

1. Introduction

Trifolium repens (T. repens), commonly known as white clover, is a widely distributed perennial herb belonging to the Fabaceae family [1]. Known for its broad range of applications in traditional medicine, T. repens has garnered attention in modern pharmacological research due to its diverse bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenoids, and phenolic compounds [2]. These constituents have been reported to possess several beneficial pharmacological properties, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial effects [1,3]. Despite its traditional use, limited scientific evidence exists regarding the full therapeutic potential of T. repens essential oil, particularly in the context of modern isolation techniques and in-depth pharmacological investigations [2]. The increasing demand for green, efficient, and sustainable isolation methods presents an exciting opportunity to explore the therapeutic potential of T. repens essential oil more thoroughly [4].

Recent advances in isolation technologies have revolutionized the ability to isolate high-quality bioactive compounds from plant material [5]. Conventional isolation techniques such as steam distillation and solvent isolation are often associated with significant energy consumption, long isolation times, and the potential for degradation of heat-sensitive compounds [3]. Thus, the development of novel, more efficient and environmentally friendly isolation methods is crucial. One such promising approach is the integration of ultrasound, infrared, and microwave energy sources, with the aim of overcoming the limitations of traditional hydro-distillation and maintaining the integrity of sensitive bioactive molecules.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic serious metabolic-disorder characterized by persistent-hyperglycemia resulting from defects in insulin-secretion, and/or insulin-action [6]. One of the critical pathophysiological mechanisms implicated in the onset and progression of DM is oxidativestress, which arises from a disorder between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and the body’s defense antioxidant mechanisms [7]. This oxidative stress contributes significantly to cellular damage and is engaged with a pivotal role in the development of long-term DM complications [8].

Among these complications, diabetic neuropathy (DN) affecting a substantial proportion of diabetic individuals [9]. The interplay between sustained hyperglycemia and oxidative stress leads to structural and functional neuronal alterations, giving rise to symptoms such as hyperalgesia and allodynia [10]. These manifestations severely impair quality of life and remain a clinical challenge due to the limited efficacy and side effects of current therapeutic options [11].

Growing evidence highlights the link between diabetic neuropathy, oxidative stress, and impaired glucose metabolism, underscoring the importance of targeting both antidiabetic and antioxidant pathways in therapeutic strategies [2]. Natural products with dual antidiabetic and neuroprotective actions are gaining attention for their potential to mitigate neuropathic pain by restoring redox balance and improving glucose regulation [12].

Furthermore, inflammation is increasingly recognized as a central mediator in the pathogenesis of diabetic neuropathy [13]. Elevated proinflammatory cytokines levels, for instance TNF-α and IL-6, contribute to neuronal damage and pain hypersensitivity. Compounds with anti-inflammatory properties not only attenuate neuroinflammation but also exert beneficial effects on oxidative stress and glucose homeostasis, suggesting a multifaceted approach in alleviating diabetic neuropathy [14]. Additionally, certain phytochemicals possess anticonvulsant properties, which may further aid in dampening abnormal neuronal excitability associated with neuropathic pain [15].

Although hybrid extraction strategies combining two energy sources (e.g., ultrasound–microwave or infrared-assisted extraction) have been previously reported, the present work introduces a distinct, sequentially integrated ultrasound–infrared–microwave hydro-distillation (UIMHD) platform specifically optimized for volatile oil isolation. Unlike previously described hybrid systems, the UIMHD approach employs short-duration ultrasound pre-treatment for micro-channel formation, controlled infrared pre-heating to soften plant matrices below degradation thresholds, and rapid microwave hydro-distillation within a closed system, thereby enhancing extraction efficiency while preserving thermolabile constituents.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report integrating ultrasound, infrared, and microwave energies in a single green workflow for Trifolium repens essential oil isolation, coupled with comprehensive bio-guided fractionation and multi-target in vivo pharmacological validation.

Thus, this study aimed to develop and evaluate a novel, environmentally friendly hybrid isolation technique that integrates ultrasound, infrared, and microwave technologies for the efficient isolation of essential oils from Trifolium repens. The study also assessed the biological properties of the resulting EO and its most active constituent, focusing on their anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, neuroprotective, anticonvulsant, and multi-drug resistant antimicrobial activities, and possible-mechanisms of action.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Chemicals

All solvents, standards, and chemicals utilized in this study were of analytical grade, unless stated, and were commercially-obtained from Merck (Germany).

2.2. Plant material

Trifolium repens (white clover) aerial parts were harvested during the flowering phase (Spring, 2017) from a local farm in Moukhtara (Lebanon, 33.6588°N, 35.6141°E). The fresh aerial parts were authenticated by the Department of Pharmaceutical Sciences, BAU. A representative Trifolium repens sample was kept in the faculty-herbarium with voucher sample (PS-17-48). After collection and authentication, the fresh aerial parts were directly used in the isolation processes. The plant material was collected in 2017, authenticated at the time of collection, and immediately processed for essential oil isolation. All subsequent phytochemical analyses and bio-guided fractionation were conducted on oils obtained from this authenticated material.

2.3. Essential oil isolation

2.3.1. Advanced novel ultrasound-infrared assisted microwave hydro-distillation (UIMHD)

A novel green isolation protocol, Ultrasound- Infrared Assisted Microwave Hydro-Distillation (UIMHD), was developed and optimized for efficient recovery of volatile constituents from Trifolium repens (Fig. 1A). This integrated method synergistically combines ultrasound pre-treatment, infrared-assisted heating, and microwave hydro-distillation into a single workflow to enhance isolation efficiency with the aim of minimizing thermal degradation.

A total of 100 g of the prepared plant material was used for each isolation cycle. The sample was immersed in 600 mL of distilled water within a glass isolation vessel and placed in an ultrasonic-bath working at a frequency of 40 kHz and a power output of 200 W. The sonication process was maintained for 2.5 min at room temperature to facilitate micro-channel formation and pre-rupture of cell walls, promoting easier release of volatile compounds during subsequent steps [16]. Following ultrasound pre-treatment, the isolation vessel was exposed to an external infrared (IR) heat source to accelerate pre-distillation thermal softening of plant tissues. A near-infrared emitter (wavelength range: 0.78–1.4 μm) was positioned at a varying distance of 10–30 cm above the vessel. The best yield was found with the 1.2 μm and 15 cm distance above the isolation-vessel. The IR heating was applied for 2.5 min, with surface temperatures monitored to not exceed 60 ± 2 °C to prevent degradation of heat-sensitive compounds [17,18]. After IR exposure, the vessel was immediately transferred to a closed microwave- assisted hydro-distillation system. The distillation was conducted at a microwave power of 600 W, maintaining internal temperatures around 100 ± 3 °C. The apparatus included a vertical Clevenger- type condenser to facilitate continuous condensation and separation of the volatile phase. The distillation was run for 10 min, during which the essential oil began to accumulate in the calibrated receiver. Upon completion of the distillation, the collected distillate was allowed to cool, and the volatile oil layer was carefully separated using a separatory funnel. The oil was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 to remove moisture traces and filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane. The purified essential oil was stored in amber glass vials at 4 °C until further chemical analysis. The essential oil yield was calculated as volume per weight (V/W,%) based on the dry weight of the plant material, following complete moisture removal prior to extraction. All EO isolations have been performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility, and results are reported as mean ± SD [19].

The isolated essential oils were analyzed using GC–MS to determine both their qualitative and quantitative volatile profiles. Chemical characterization was carried out on a GC-system connected to a mass selective detector, utilizing a capillary column (HP-5MS; 30 m × 0.25 mm internal diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness). Helium served as the carrier gas, maintained at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. A 1 μL volume of the oil, previously diluted in hexane at a 1:100 ratio (v/v), was introduced in split mode with a 1:20 split ratio. The injector was held at 250 °C. The oven temperature program began at 50 °C (held for 2 min), followed by a gradual ramp of 4 °C/min up to 250 °C, where it was maintained for an additional 5 min to allow for complete compound elution. The mass spectrometer operated under electron ionization (EI) at 70 eV, with the ion source and quadrupole temperatures set at 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively. The system scanned a mass range of m/z 40–550 in full-scan mode. Identification of volatile constituents was accomplished by comparing the obtained mass spectra to those in the NIST and Wiley databases, with further confirmation based on retention indices aligned with published data [20,21].

The UIMHD parameters employed in this study were selected based on a yield- and reproducibility-driven optimization strategy, with the primary objective of maximizing essential oil recovery while preserving volatile integrity. Preliminary trials were conducted to identify operational ranges for ultrasound exposure, infrared pre-heating, and microwave- assisted hydro-distillation that consistently produced stable yields without evidence of thermal degradation.

All UIMHD extractions were performed in triplicate, and extraction yields showed minimal inter-batch variation, confirming the reproducibility of the selected conditions. Formal full-factorial design or response-surface methodology was not pursued, as the focus of the present work was on biological validation of the optimized extraction output rather than exhaustive process engineering optimization.

2.4. Microwave-assisted extraction method (MAE)

In order to compare the novel method results, MAE method was performed. MAE was carried out using a closed-vessel laboratory-grade microwave extraction system. Approximately 100 g of freshly powdered Trifolium repens aerial parts were placed into a 1000 mL round-bottom flask containing 600 mL of distilled water. The plant-to-solvent ratio was maintained consistently throughout the process.

The isolation was performed at a controlled microwave power of 600 W, with the temperature maintained at approximately 100 ± 2 °C. The system was equipped with a condenser to allow for continuous distillation and condensation of the volatile fraction. The process was sustained for 30 min to ensure sufficient cell wall rupture and release of volatile phytoconstituents.

Upon completion, the condensate was collected and the essential oil layer was separated using a separatory funnel. The oil was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate to eliminate residual moisture, then filtered and stored in amber vials at 4 °C until further analysis. The obtained extracts were subsequently subjected to GC–MS for qualitative and quantitative profiling of the volatile constituents, utilizing the same technique as the novel method.

2.5. Phytopharmacological evaluations and bio-guided fractionation and isolation of the most active compound(s)

The isolated essential oil was loaded onto a silica gel column (60–120 mesh; 80 × 3 cm i.d.) for fractionation. A gradient elution system was employed using non-polar to polar solvents as follows: Hexane: Ethyl acetate (100:0 to 0:100) with incremental polarity (10% steps). Each fraction collected by time into about 100 fractions was obtained. All eluates were concentrated under reduced pressure and stored at 4 °C until further analysis. Each of the 100 fractions was monitored by thin layer chromatography (TLC; Silica gel 60 F254, hexane: ethyl acetate 7:3). Spots were visualized under UV light (254 and 366 nm) and by vanillin-sulfuric acid staining. Fractions with similar TLC profiles were pooled, reducing the number to 26 major fractions. GC–MS (Agilent 7890B GC coupled with a 5977A MSD) was used to analyze the 26 pooled fractions. The capillary column used was HP-5MS (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness). The temperature program was: 50 °C (hold 2 min) to 280 °C at 5 °C/min (hold 5 min). Carrier gas was helium at 1 mL/min. Identification of components was carried out by comparing mass spectra with NIST 17 library and retention indices. All 26 pooled fractions were screened for acute antidiabetic activity [22].

2.6. Experimental animals and housing conditions

Male Swiss albino mice (8–10 weeks-old; body weight 25–31 g) were utilized for all experimental protocols. Animals were obtained from an accredited animal facility and housed under standard laboratory conditions: a 12-h light–dark cycle, room-temperature of 22 ± 2 °C. Animals were given ad libitum access to a standard pellet diet and water throughout the study.

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee under approval number IRB 2014A-003-P-R-0005 and were conducted under the supervision of the responsible institutional authority. All experiments were performed in accordance with international guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and are reported in compliance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines [23].

2.7. Diabetes-induction

Experimental diabetes mellitus has been induced by intraperitoneal injection (ip) in three every other days of alloxan monohydrate at a total dose of 180 mg/kg body weight, freshly dissolved in sterile normal saline. Prior to alloxan administration, animals were fasted overnight with free access to distilled-water. To prevent acute hypoglycemia, animals were given 5% glucose solution for 24 h post-injection. Blood glucose levels (BGL) were measured 72 h after alloxan administration using a glucometers (tail vein sampling). Animals with fasting BGL ≥200 mg/dL were considered diabetic and were included in this research [24,25].

2.8. Anti-diabetic monitoring

Acute (for 6 h post-dose) and subchronic (for 8 days post-dose) phases were monitored utilizing blood glucose levels (BGL) were measured utilizing Sigma-glucometers (Germany) [26]. Chronic Phase (8 weeks post-dose) was monitored, chronic hyperglycemia was confirmed by quantifying glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) using a HbA1c Analyticon micro-tube assay kit (Germany) [27]. Trifolium repens essential oil (TR-EO) was administered orally by gavage at doses of 25, 50, and 100 mg/kg body weight, while the most active compound, pseudoionone (PSI), was administered orally at doses of 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/kg body weight. Treatments were given once daily.

Acute antidiabetic effects were evaluated over 6 h post-administration, subchronic effects were assessed over 8 consecutive days, and chronic antidiabetic effects were evaluated following 8 weeks of continuous daily treatment in alloxan-induced diabetic mice. Serum insulin was also monitored. Blood-samples have been collected via retro-orbital plexus, and serum insulin levels have been determined using HPLC-DAD following validated isolation protocols described before [28]. Briefly, serum was dissolved in acetonitrile (ACN), and the supernatant has been filtered through a membrane filter. The analysis was carried out on a C18-column using a solvent mobile-phase consisting of ACN and double-distilled water (60:40, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The detection was performed at 214 nm, and insulin quantification based on calibration with standard solutions [29,30].

2.9. Anti-diabetic neuropathy monitoring

Following 8 weeks of confirmed diabetes and continuous daily oral treatment with TR-EO or PSI, as described in the previous section, animals were assessed for peripheral neuropathy using multiple behavioral models. Hot-plate hyperalgesic thresholds were monitored. Animals were placed on a heated-surface maintained at 52 ± 1 °C, and latency to nociceptive-response (paw licking or jumping) has been recorded. Tail flick latency tests were also monitored. The distal portion of the tail was exposed to radiant heat, and the withdrawal latency was measured [31]. To monitor the allodynia, Von Frey filament test was employed. Tactile allodynia was assessed using calibrated von-Frey filaments tested to the plantar-surface of the hind-paw, recording the force required to elicit a withdrawal response [32–34].

2.10. Anti-inflammatory activity

Acute Inflammation was monitored utilizing the paw edema model. The hind-paw edema was induced using carrageenan-injection (0.1 mL, 1% w/v) into the subplantar-region of the right hind paw. Paw volume has been measured using a plethysmometer at baseline and at regular intervals postinjection [25]. Moreover, Chronic Inflammatory Pain was also investigated. Mechanical hyperalgesia due to persistent inflammation was evaluated using the von Frey test over a 7-day period following repeated inflammatory stimulus [33].

2.11. In-vivo monitoring of oxidative stress

Serum catalase (CAT) activity has been measured spectrophotometrically by monitoring the deterioration of hydrogen-peroxide at 240 nm [35]. Moreover, lipid peroxidation (TBARS Assay) was also employed to monitor the in-vivo oxidative-stress. Thiobarbituric-acid reactive-substances have been quantified as an index of malondialdehyde (MDA) formation [36]. Nevertheless, reduced glutathione (GSH) levels was also quantified using Ellman’s reagent and spectrophotometric detection at 412 nm. Catalase activity was expressed as kU/L of serum, reduced glutathione (GSH) levels as μg/mg protein, and lipid peroxidation (TBARS) as nmol malondialdehyde (MDA)/100 g tissue. All measurements were performed in triplicate and expressed as mean ± SD for statistical analysis [24,37–39].

2.12. Anti-inflammatory cytokine evaluation

Post-anti-inflammatory assays, blood samples were collected, and serum-levels of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines were quantified using ELISA kits. The focus was on key mediators including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10. Cytokine modulation was used to establish the anti-inflammatory profile of the extracts and PSI, correlating with both behavioral and biochemical outcomes. Serum concentrations of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10 were quantified using ELISA and expressed as pg/mg protein, according to the manufacturers’ instructions [26].

2.13. Anticonvulsant activity

To evaluate potential anticonvulsant effects, mice were pre-treated with test TR-EO, followed by administration of strychnine nitrate (2 mg/kg, i.p.), a glycine receptor antagonist that induces convulsions. The latency to onset of tonic-clonic seizures and the survival time were recorded. Protective efficacy was compared with vehicle treated group (n = 6/group). The most active isolate, Pseudoionone (PSI), was further evaluated across dose gradients to support its seizure-modulating activity [40].

2.14. Multidrug-resistant antibacterial assay

The antimicrobial efficacy of Trifolium repens essential oil (TR) and its principal active compound, PSI, was evaluated against multidrug-resistant Shigella dysenteriae isolates using the disc diffusion and microdilution methods. Essential oils were extracted via a novel hybrid green isolation approach combining ultrasound, infrared, and microwave- assisted techniques. The isolates were initially screened for resistance to antibiotics including ampicillin, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, tetracycline, and co-trimoxazole. Biofilm-forming capacity was assessed and categorized into non-, moderate-, and strong-biofilm formers. TR and PSI were subsequently tested against selected strong biofilm-forming isolates to determine minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) and their capacity to inhibit biofilm development. The reduction in biofilm biomass was quantified to assess the antibiofilm potential of both agents.

2.15. Statistical analysis

Experimental-data have been expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical evaluations were performed using OriginPro (USA). Statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc multiple-comparison test. For time-course experiments, repeated-measures ANOVA was applied where appropriate. Exact p-values are reported where applicable, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Group sizes (n = 6 animals per group) were selected based on prior validated in vivo diabetic and neuropathic pain models, which consistently demonstrate sufficient statistical power to detect biologically relevant differences in behavioral, biochemical, and inflammatory endpoints. Effect sizes were evaluated based on the magnitude and consistency of treatment effects relative to diabetic controls [25,27]. Formal effect size metrics were not calculated due to the exploratory nature of the study.

3. Results

The aim of the current study is the exploration of Trifolium repens (white clover), a medicinal plant rich in TR-EO, is of particular interest. The present study employs a novel hybrid-green isolation method combining ultrasound, infrared, and microwave technologies to isolate volatile constituents of T. repens. Comprehensive GC–MS analysis and subsequent phytopharmacological evaluations aim to elucidate the therapeutic potential of this species in managing diabetes-associated oxidative stress, inflammation, and neuropathic complications, anticonvulsant, and multi-drug resistant antimicrobial activities, and possible-mechanisms of action.

3.1. Comparative efficiency of isolation methods

The novel Hybrid-Green Ultrasound-Infrared Assisted Microwave Hydro-Distillation (UIMHD) significantly improved isolation efficiency compared to traditional microwave-assisted isolation (Table 1). The UIMHD method halved the isolation time (0.15 ± 0.01 h vs. 0.30 ± 0.01 h) and yielded a substantially greater volume of essential oil (6.50 ± 0.01 mL vs. 4.00 ± 0.01 mL), with statistically significant differences ( p < 0.05). This enhanced performance is attributed to the synergistic action of ultrasound pre-treatment, infrared heating, and microwave hydro-distillation, which collectively improve cell wall rupture, facilitate volatile diffusion, and minimize thermal degradation.

3.2. GC–MS profiling and phytochemical differentiation

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) had shown distinct phytochemical profiles between the two isolation methods. As shown in Tables 2A and 2B, the hybrid method resulted in a broader and more abundant spectrum of volatile constituents. Notably, Pseudoionone, a sesquiterpenoid with known biological activity, was identified as the major compound in both extracts, but its relative abundance was significantly higher in the hybrid extract (49.16%) compared to the microwave extract (46.22%).

Moreover, the total volatile composition was enhanced by the hybrid method (98.02%) compared to the conventional approach (89.62%). The hybrid method also yielded significantly higher percentages of oxygenated monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes (24.28% vs. 20.4%) and unsaturated terpenoids (19.04% vs. 16.00%), which are known to contribute to anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities (Table 2C). These results underscore the phytochemical superiority of the hybrid technique and validate its relevance for pharmaceutical applications.

3.3. Bio-guided fractionation and isolation of the most active compound

Fraction 14 exhibited the most significant activity. GC–MS analysis revealed a major component, tentatively identified as pseudoionone (PSI), with >85% purity in this fraction. Fraction 14 was re-chromatographed on silica gel using a solvent system of hexane: ethyl acetate (9:1). The major eluate was collected and crystallized using cold methanol. The isolated compound was confirmed as pseudoionone using GC–MS(purity >98%), and 1H-NMRand 13C-NMR (CDCl3, 400 MHz) [2,9,10,16,18,22,27, 28,36,41–48] (Fig. 1B and Table 3A).

3.4. Antidiabetic effects

Therapeutic evaluation suggested the antidiabetic potential of both the crude essential oil (TR) and the purified compound PSI. In acute and subchronic models, both treatments resulted in significant and progressive reductions in BGL compared to the diabetic-control group (Figs. 2A and B). Furthermore, long-term glycemic regulation assessed by HbA1c levels after 8 weeks showed that both TR and PSI significantly reduced glycated hemoglobin concentrations (Fig. 2C), indicating effective control of chronic hyperglycemia.

Serum insulin levels, quantified using a validated HPLC-DAD method, TR and PSI significantly increased circulating serum insulin levels toward normoglycemic values (Fig. 2D).

3.5. Neuroprotective and antinociceptive effects in diabetic neuropathy

Behavioral assessments confirmed the development of diabetic neuropathy 8 weeks post-alloxan injection. Diabetic mice showed reduced latency in both the hot plate and tail flick tests, indicating thermal hyperalgesia (Figs. 3A and B). Treatment with TR and PSI significantly increased latency times in both tests, suggesting a restoration of pain thresholds and attenuation of peripheral nerve sensitization.

Additionally, mechanical allodynia, assessed by von Frey filament testing, revealed a marked decrease in paw withdrawal thresholds in diabetic animals. Both TR and PSI reversed these effects in a dose-responsive manner (Fig. 3C), highlighting their efficacy in alleviating mechanical hypersensitivity associated with chronic diabetic neuropathy.

3.6. Anti-inflammatory activity

The TR and PSI anti-inflammatory potentials were evaluated utilizing both acute and chronic-models. In the acute carrageenan-induced paw edema test, TR and PSI significantly inhibited paw swelling at all tested doses, indicating strong peripheral anti-inflammatory activity (Fig. 4A).

In the chronic inflammatory pain model, repeated administration of TR and PSI improved mechanical pain thresholds as assessed by von Frey filaments (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that the treatments not only reduce acute inflammation but also modulate long-term inflammatory pain signaling, potentially through central and peripheral mechanisms.

3.7. Modulating oxidative stress

Oxidative stress parameters revealed that chronic diabetes has been correlated with a significant decrease in catalase and reduced glutathione (GSH) levels, alongside elevated TBARS, a marker of lipid peroxidation. Treatment with TR and PSI restored catalase activity and GSH levels, and significantly reduced TBARS concentrations in a dose-dependent manner (Table 3B). The antioxidant-activity has been more pronounced at higher doses, particularly for PSI, indicating its potential role in redox regulation and neuroprotection in diabetic models.

3.8. Anticonvulsant activity via glycine receptor potentiation

In the strychnine-induced seizure model, both TR and PSI delayed the onset of tonic-extensor convulsions and increased survival time. Interestingly, PSI at 50 mg/kg provided complete protection in several animals, suggesting a possible interaction with glycine receptors (Fig. 4C). These results propose a novel anticonvulsant mechanism possibly related to glycinergic modulation, which may also contribute to the antinociceptive effects observed in diabetic neuropathy.

3.9. Modulation of inflammatory cytokines

The anti-inflammatory mechanism was further validated by ELISA quantification of serum cytokines. Diabetic mice exhibited elevated TNF-α and IL-6 levels, accompanied by suppressed IL-10, reflecting a pro-inflammatory state. TR and PSI significantly decreased TNF-α and IL-6 while markedly elevating IL-10 in a dose-dependent manner (Table 3C). Notably, the most elevated doses of TR (100 mg/kg) and PSI (50 mg/kg) demonstrated cytokine profiles comparable to ibuprofen, indicating robust immunomodulatory effects.

3.10. Multidrug-resistance against Shigella dysenteriae

Among 30 clinical isolates of Shigella dysenteriae, resistance rates ranged from 10% to 13.3% across multiple antibiotics, including ciprofloxacin and ceftriaxone (Tables 4A and 4B). A majority (60%) of isolates were classified as strong biofilm producers. The TR oil showed MIC values averaging around 625 μg/mL, achieving up to 98.56% reduction in biofilm formation. Pseudoionone exhibited lower MIC values (approximately 312 μg/mL), with biofilm reduction reaching 97.33% (Table 4C). These results affirm both TR and PSI’s potent antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities, with PSI demonstrating slightly superior efficacy.

4. Discussion

The present study introduces a novel hybrid-green isolation method—ultrasound-infrared-assisted microwave hydro-distillation (UIMHD)— for isolating essential oils (EOs) from Trifolium repens (white clover). This technique synergistically combines ultrasound, infrared, and microwave technologies to enhance isolation efficiency, reduce processing time, and improve the quality of the extracted oils.

The UIMHD method demonstrated a significant improvement in isolation yield and time efficiency compared to conventional microwave-assisted isolation. Specifically, it achieved a 62.5% increase in TR-EO yield and halved the isolation time. These enhancements are attributed to the combined effects of ultrasound-induced cavitation, infrared heating, and microwave energy, which collectively disrupt plant cell walls and facilitate the release of volatile compounds.

GC–MS analyses have revealed that the EOs extracted via the novel UIMHD contained a higher concentration of bioactive compounds, notably pseudoionone, which constituted 49.16% of the total volatiles. The method also yielded greater amounts of oxygenated monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes, compounds known for their therapeutic properties, compared to similar Trifolium species [1,21].

The efficacy of the UIMHD method aligns with findings from previous studies that have explored hybrid isolation techniques. For instance, the combination of ultrasound and microwave-assisted hydrodistillation has been shown to enhance the isolation of clove essential oil, resulting in higher yields and increased concentrations of eugenol, a compound with strong antioxidant properties. Similarly, studies on the isolation of anthraquinones from Heterophyllaea pustulata have suggested that combining ultrasound and microwave techniques can improve isolation efficiency and reduce solvent usage [49].

These comparisons underscore the potential of hybrid isolation methods in optimizing the yield and quality of plant-derived compounds. The UIMHD technique, in particular, offers a green and efficient alternative for TR-EO isolation, minimizing thermal degradation and preserving the integrity of thermolabile constituents, as concluded with analogues natural products [34].

This study demonstrates the efficacy of a novel hybrid-green ultrasound-infrared assisted microwave method in isolating bioactive essential oils from Trifolium repens. The optimized multi-modal technique resulted in efficient isolation with preservation of thermolabile constituents.

Bio-guided fractionation, employing column chromatography, TLC, and GC–MS, led to the isolation of pseudoionone (PSI) as the major bioactive compound. The chromatographic behavior, spectral identity, and biological activity confirm the successful isolation and characterization of PSI, as done before with similar phy-to-active compounds [26].

This integrated phytochemical and pharmacological approach validates Trifolium repens as a potential source of antidiabetic agents and highlights the power of green isolation and bio-guided isolation in drug discovery from natural sources.

It is important to interpret the observed elevation in serum insulin levels in the context of the alloxan-induced diabetic model. Alloxan is known to cause selective but incomplete destruction of pancreatic β-cells, and residual functional β-cells may remain viable following induction of diabetes. Therefore, the increase in circulating insulin observed after 8 weeks of treatment with T. repens essential oil and pseudoionone is unlikely to reflect complete β-cell regeneration.

Rather, these findings more plausibly suggest partial preservation of surviving β-cells, enhanced β-cell functional recovery, and/or improved peripheral insulin sensitivity, resulting in more efficient insulin secretion and utilization. Similar insulin-restorative patterns have been reported for phytochemicals with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, where attenuation of oxidative stress and cytokine-mediated damage supports residual β-cell function rather than de novo cell regeneration.

Accordingly, the present results should be interpreted as indicating functional improvement of the insulin axis, rather than structural regeneration of pancreatic β-cells.

Both the novel method essential oil (TR) and the most active compound isolated, pseudoionone (PSI), displayed robust antidiabetic effects in acute and subchronic models. They significantly reduced blood glucose levels and HbA1c, indicating both immediate and sustained glycemic control. PSI suggested a dose-dependent restoration of serum insulin levels, suggesting pancreatic β-cell preservation or stimulation of insulin secretion. These findings suggest that PSI may act via insulinotropic mechanisms or improve insulin sensitivity, as observed before with analogues phytochemicals [50].

Behavioral studies using thermal and mechanical stimuli supported the neuroprotective efficacy of both TR and PSI in diabetic neuropathy. They restored nociceptive thresholds in hot plate, tail flick, and von Frey filament assays. The reversal of hyperalgesia and allodynia implies peripheral nerve protection and central pain modulation, possibly mediated by anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant pathways, as concluded priorly [29].

Acute and chronic inflammatory pain models suggested the significant anti-inflammatory capacity of TR and PSI treatments. Carrageenan-induced paw edema was notably reduced, while chronic mechanical hypersensitivity was attenuated. ELISA assays supported that TR and PSI decreased proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and elevated the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, thereby restoring immunological homeostasis. These results reinforce the idea that pseudoionone exerts systemic anti-inflammatory effects with both local and central action components, as found before with comparable metabolites [51].

Similarly, the observed modulation of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10 supports an anti-inflammatory profile; however, these cytokine changes should be interpreted as downstream correlates of biological activity, rather than definitive proof of direct pathway-specific targeting.

Oxidative stress, a core factor in diabetes pathology, was modulated by TR and PSI. Both agents restored catalase activity and glutathione (GSH) levels while significantly reducing TBARS, a lipid peroxidation marker. This redox balance restoration suggests that pseudoionone confers cytoprotective effects by enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses.

In the strychnine-induced seizure model, TR and PSI delayed the onset of convulsions and increased survival time. This finding, alongside glycine receptor potentiation hypotheses, points to a novel CNS activity of pseudoionone, potentially relevant for neuropathic pain and seizure-related comorbidities in diabetes.

While the anticonvulsant and antinociceptive effects observed for pseudoionone are consistent with modulation of inhibitory neurotransmission, including glycinergic pathways, the present findings are based on functional in vivo models and do not constitute direct molecular evidence of receptor binding or activation. Accordingly, glycine receptor involvement should be interpreted as a functional association rather than a confirmed molecular mechanism.

The inclusion of antimicrobial evaluation in the present study was intended to provide pathophysiological context rather than to introduce a separate therapeutic indication. Patients with diabetes, particularly those with long-standing disease and peripheral neuropathy, are known to exhibit increased susceptibility to bacterial infections, including those caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, due to impaired immune responses and compromised tissue integrity [52,53].

Within this framework, the observed antimicrobial and antibiofilm effects of Trifolium repens essential oil and pseudoionone are presented as adjunctive biological properties that may complement their primary antidiabetic and neuroprotective activities, rather than as standalone antimicrobial interventions.

The novel method essential oil, TR, and PSI also showed potent antimicrobial and anti-biofilm effects against multidrug-resistant Shigella dysenteriae. PSI exhibited lower MIC values (ca. 312 μg/mL) and reduced biofilm formation by over 97%, supporting its potential as a natural antimicrobial agent against multi-drug resistant pathogens. This expands the therapeutic relevance of T. repens essential oil beyond metabolic diseases, highlighting its versatility in managing infectious threats in immunocompromised conditions such as diabetes. Accordingly, antimicrobial findings are discussed in a supportive context and should be interpreted as secondary observations that enhance the translational relevance of the study in diabetic settings.

The enriched phytochemical profile of the EOs obtained via UIMHD translates into enhanced pharmacological activities. Both the crude TR-EO and isolated pseudoionone exhibited significant antidiabetic effects, including the regulation of blood glucose levels and improvement of insulin sensitivity. Additionally, they have shown neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties, as evidenced by behavioral assessments and cytokine modulation, compared to other phyto-active metabolites [9]. The antimicrobial efficacy against multidrug-resistant Shigella dysenteriae further highlights the therapeutic potential of these phyto-active compounds.

The sample sizes employed in this study are consistent with established in vivo diabetic and neuropathic pain models and have been previously demonstrated to provide adequate statistical power for detecting meaningful biological and behavioral effects [25,54].

Regarding the study timeline, the aerial parts of Trifolium repens were collected in 2017, and all in vivo experiments were conducted between 2018 and 2019 under a valid institutional ethical approval. The interval between data generation and manuscript submission reflects the extended scope of the work, which included optimization of a novel hybrid-green extraction platform, comprehensive bio-guided isolation and structural characterization of the major active constituent, and additional multidrug-resistant antimicrobial and antibiofilm investigations. Importantly, all experimental procedures and analytical methods employed remain consistent with current international standards [55].

5. Conclusions

The integration of ultrasound, infrared, and microwave technologies in the UIMHD method represents an efficient and sustainable approach for the isolation of essential oils from Trifolium repens. This hybrid-green technique enhances extraction efficiency and phytochemical richness while preserving volatile constituents, supporting its potential applicability in natural product research. Future studies should focus on method scale-up and evaluation across other medicinal plant species.

This study introduces a novel hybrid-green isolation strategy for volatile oils and provides experimental evidence supporting the broad-spectrum biological potential of Trifolium repens essential oil and its major constituent, pseudoionone. The biological activities observed for pseudoionone are consistent with modulation of inflammatory, oxidative, and neuronal pathways, as demonstrated through in vivo functional models. Through a synergistic approach combining optimized extraction, rigorous phytochemical characterization, and bio-guided pharmacological evaluation, pseudoionone is highlighted as a promising multifunctional natural candidate warranting further investigation.

Collectively, these findings support the need for deeper mechanistic studies and future translational validation, underscoring the value of integrating green chemistry platforms with modern biological evaluation in the discovery of bioactive agents from Eastern Mediterranean medicinal plants.
