Defining physician acceptance of radiosensitivity testing for guiding therapeutic interventions in head and neck cancer: A Delphi consensus study
Authors: Juliette Thariat, Arnaud Beddok, Sébastien Salas, Philippe Gorphe, Mélanie Doré, Séverine Racadot, Aurore Vozy, Yoann Pointreau, Charles Dupin, Sophie Renard, François Mouawad, Sandrine Pereira, Sophie Deneuve, Pierre Blanchard

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Delphi consensus assessed clinical maturity of radiosensitivity testing in HNC.•Experts agreed that biomarkers are not ready for routine treatment adaptation.•Conditional use was supported only within prospective clinical trials.•Supportive care practices showed high expert agreement.•Study defines a translational pathway for biomarker integration.Keywords: Head and neck cancer, Radiotherapy, Radiosensitivity testing, Biomarker, Consensus, Clinical trial

Introduction

Despite advances in radiation therapy (RT) planning, delivery, systemic combinations, and supportive care, radiation-induced toxicities remain a major challenge/concern in head and neck cancer (HNC) [1]. Acute toxicities can disrupt RT delivery, leading to unscheduled interruptions, which facilitates tumor repopulation and worsens local control [2]. Long-term sequelae, including dysphagia, xerostomia, fibrosis, osteoradionecrosis, or chronic pain, may compromise essential functions, markedly impair quality of life, and impose substantial societal costs. These toxicities are only partly explained by dosimetric parameters. Clinical observations suggest that severe over-reactions reflect substantial inter-individual variability in normal tissue radiosensitivity, which current radiobiological models poorly capture [3].

Over recent decades, numerous assays have attempted to measure individual radiosensitivity. Building on these advances, precision radiotherapy seeks to incorporate biological variability into treatment planning, with the dual goal of reducing toxicity and enabling tumoricidal dose delivery in situations where conventional radiotherapy would be limited [4]. In this context, pre-treatment biomarkers of radiosensitivity could inform individualized risk-adapted care pathways, including modifications in dose or fractionation, modifications in volumes, selection of radiation modality, prioritization of proton therapy, or reinforcement of supportive interventions [5]. However, clinical adoption requires robust evidence of predictive value and a clear framework on how such test results should guide treatment adaptation.

Historically, several assays explored the biological basis of radiosensitivity [6]. Early fibroblast-based assays (clonogenic survival, micronuclei, comet assay) suggested inter-patient differences but suffered from limited reproducibility and scalability, precluding clinical implementation [7]. Molecular assays have gained traction [8]. Decades of research have demonstrated biological plausibility, from early fibroblast survival assays [9] to more recent molecular approaches based on DNA damage response pathways [5]. The translational COPERNIC program demonstrated that impaired nucleoschuttling of phosphorylated ATM (Ataxia Telangiectasia Mutating) after irradiation correlated with clinical over-reactions [10]. The Radiation-Induced Lymphocyte Apoptosis (RILA) assay quantifies CD8 T-cell apoptosis after ex vivo irradiation [11]. In a large multicenter breast cancer cohort (>500 patients), higher CD8-RILA predicted a lower incidence of late breast fibrosis [12]. Genomic approaches have identified single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with toxicity. In HNC, rs861639, rs1800469, and rs1801321 were associated with acute dermatitis in a recent meta-analysis, although effect sizes were modest [13]. Other SNPs of genes involved in DNA repair (ERCC1 and ERCC5) and apoptosis (MDM2 and TP53) were associated with the severity of radiation-related side-effects (dermatitis, fibrosis, xerostomia, osteoradionecrosis) in HNC patients (Borchiellini et al., 2017). Proteomic and cytokine-based biomarkers, such as AK2 for fibrosis or TGF-β1 trajectories in lung RT, also show biological plausibility but remain insufficiently validated for routine practice [5].

Recently, phospho-ATM quantification emerged as a candidate marker [14], and translational development led to RadioDtect®, a blood-based assay derived from the ATM pathway [14]. Early studies suggested that when combined with Normal Tissue Complication Probability (NTCP) models, RadioDtect® improved prediction of acute and late toxicity in HNC, representing one of the first attempts to combine dosimetry and biology in clinical practice [15].

For radiosensitivity testing to be clinically meaningful, an actionable alternative must exist. In RT, actionable alternatives include modifying the dose or fractionation schedule, substituting concurrent agents, or offering proton therapy when NTCP models show a demonstrable dosimetric advantage and potential side-effect avoidance [16]. This question is particularly relevant in the current context of clinical equipoise regarding proton therapy. While a recent U.S. randomized trial reported encouraging outcomes, suggesting clinically meaningful benefits and renewed interest in broader implementation, a contemporaneous British trial failed to demonstrate a significant advantage, thereby reinforcing ongoing uncertainty [17], [18]. Together, these contrasting findings highlight the need for careful patient selection, rigorous trial design, and continued comparative research before definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding the role of proton therapy. They also support the rationale for ongoing and future trials investigating toxicity reduction strategies with proton therapy compared with IMRT.

Supportive interventions can also be intensified according to radiosensitivity profiles, including proactive swallowing therapy, prophylactic gastrostomy or nasogastric tube, dietary optimization, and photobiomodulation. The challenge is not only to establish predictive accuracy but also to define how clinicians should respond to test results in real-world settings.

To clarify these questions, consensus-based methods provide a structured approach to explore expert opinion. The Delphi process, by anonymizing responses and iteratively refining statements across multiple rounds, provides a robust framework to identify areas of agreement, clarify controversial practices, and set research priorities [19]. The present Delphi study aimed to determine the perceived clinical value and appropriate indications of the use of radiosensitivity testing in patients with HNC, focusing on situations where testing could meaningfully guide treatment adaptation or supportive care.

Methods

Delphi design and objectives

A modified Delphi consensus was conducted to identify clinical situations in which an ATM-related radiosensitivity test could justify adapting treatment in patients at increased risk of radiation-induced toxicities. The process followed established Delphi methodology, based on iterative anonymous scoring with controlled feedback [19], and applied the 2016 Haute Autorité de Santé (HAS) consensus definitions for agreement, disagreement, and absence of consensus, supplemented by the proportion of raters showing strong agreement (https://www.has-sante.fr/upload/docs/application/pdf/2011–11/guide_methodologique_cf_40_pages_2011-11-03_15-40-2_278.pdf). The primary outcome was the set of statements reaching consensus in favor. Secondary outcomes included mapping results to potential clinical actions—such as proton referral, systemic therapy adjustment, and supportive interventions—and identifying research priorities for prospective validation in test-positive patients.

Panel composition

According to HAS guidance, Delphi processes do not require formal power calculations; a steering committee of 5–8 members and a panel of approximately 15 raters are considered sufficient for content validity.

A multidisciplinary panel was purposively recruited from French centers with expertise in HNC and normal-tissue toxicity, including radiation oncologists, medical oncologists, and medical physicists/dosimetrists. Eligibility required at least 5 years of post-training experience and current clinical practice in HNC. Thirteen experts participated in both rounds, representing diverse institutions (university hospitals, cancer centers, private clinics and proton centers) and balanced gender distribution.

To ensure broader validation and representativeness, a second independent panel of 30 radiation oncologists and head and neck surgeon was later invited to review and score the final consensus statements anonymously.

Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and all participants gave informed agreement for data use.

Development of statements

Using a scoping review and structured discussion, the steering committee examined a wide range of potential interventions. Some options were judged unethical or impractical (for example, omission of image guidance or selection among IMRT techniques) and were excluded. Decision statements and clinical vignettes were then formulated across four domains: (1) modality selection and plan adaptation, including photon IMRT versus proton therapy, consideration of MR-Linac, and robust/NTCP-driven plan adaptation; (2) concurrent systemic therapy, including switching from high-dose cisplatin to weekly or split-dose schedules, or to non-cisplatin regimens in radiosensitive patients; (3) supportive and preventive measures, including swallowing therapy, psychological support, and nutritional follow-up; (4) enteral access strategies, including prophylactic gastrostomy versus nasogastric tube. Statements were worded precisely, with paired versions where appropriate (for example, “if reimbursed” versus “even if not reimbursed”) to explore potential barriers to implementation. Certain statements explicitly referred to an ATM-related radiosensitivity test (RadioDtect®) with pre-specified sensitivity, specificity, and AUC values derived from published validation studies [14], [15].

Delphi procedure

Panelists rated the appropriateness of test-guided interventions on a 9-point Likert scale (1 = strongly inappropriate, 9 = strongly appropriate). Free-text fields captured rationale, feasibility, and implementation caveats. Round 1 consisted of independent online scoring. Between rounds, anonymized feedback was provided, including medians, interquartile ranges, and synthesized comments. Round 2 invited re-rating of all items without consensus in Round 1. Based on feedback from Round 1, several statements, presented side by side with their Round 1 versions in Table 2, were rephrased before Round 2 to improve clarity, reduce ambiguity, and better specify clinical situations. These modifications primarily involved wording refinement and contextual clarification, without altering the underlying clinical intent of the statements. For each statement, median scores, proportions of ratings in the 7–9 and 1–3 ranges, and consensus categories were calculated according to HAS methodology. Consensus in favor was defined as a median ≥ 7 with no rating in the 1–3 range. Rejection consensus was defined as a median ≤ 3 with no rating in the 7–9 range. Lack of consensus was defined as a median of 4–6 or dispersed scores. Provisional consensus (median ≥ 7 with some votes in 1–3) and provisional negative consensus (median ≤ 3 with some votes in 7–9) were also recorded.

Qualitative analysis

Free-text comments were analyzed independently by two investigators using inductive thematic analysis to identify barriers (such as reimbursement or logistics) and enablers (such as NTCP model automation). Ambiguities were clarified in subsequent rounds without altering clinical intent.

Stopping rules and external validation

Stopping rules were as follows: (1) at least 80% of items reached either positive or negative consensus, or (2) two rounds were completed with stability of medians (absolute change < 1 point for ≥ 80% of items). Following completion of the two Delphi rounds, according to HAS guideline, a broader group of 30 independent reviewers was invited to evaluate the final consensus statements to ensure external validation and broader representativeness. Responses were anonymized; conflicts of interest were declared and managed a priori. Data were hosted on a secure institutional server, and only aggregated results were shared during feedback. Reporting followed HAS recommendations for Delphi consensus studies.

Results

Panel participation and external validation

Thirteen experts participated in both Delphi rounds, representing a diverse mix of public (53.9%) and private (38.5%) institutions, with one participant from an associative or research structure (7.7%). The panel included mainly radiation oncologists (61.5%), along with medical oncologists (23.1%) and head and neck surgeons (23.1%). Nearly all reported treating over 100 head and neck cancer (HNC) patients annually, most frequently with oropharyngeal (28.6%) and oral cavity (27.8%) primaries.

The external validation panel included 30 independent head and neck specialists, predominantly radiation oncologists (96.7%) and one head and neck surgeon (3.3%), working in public institutions (60.0%) or private clinics (40.0%). Their professional experience ranged from 5 to over 30 years, and half of them managed more than 40 HNC patients per year. Characteristics of both groups are summarized in Table 1.

Consensus outcomes

Across the two Delphi rounds, twenty-six statements were evaluated.

In the first round, seven statements reached consensus in favor, primarily related to supportive and organizational measures intended to mitigate radiation-induced toxicities. These included the systematic use of nutritional and physiotherapy support, photobiomodulation for toxicity management, and long-term follow-up after pharyngeal irradiation, as well as the adoption of IMRT as the reference technique and dose optimization in cases at risk of osteoradionecrosis. The perceived strength of evidence was deemed sufficient to warrant their routine use, obviating the need for a clinical trial in view of the lack of equipoise concerning their omission (Table 2).

Thirteen additional statements achieved provisional consensus (either positive or negative), mainly concerning test-guided therapeutic adaptations such as proton referral and fractionated or weekly-dose cisplatin for frail patients, while others addressed supportive care (e.g., smoking and alcohol cessation).

The remaining items showed no consensus according to predefined HAS criteria. After controlled feedback and re-rating in the second round, four additional statements reached definitive consensus in favor, including the use of a radiosensitivity test in genetically radiosensitive syndromes, its integration within therapeutic trials, proton referral in case of predicted high toxicity risk, and systematic prophylactic gastrostomy in nutritionally frail patients.

Conversely, one statement concerning the anticipated widespread adoption of MR-Linac within five years achieved rejection consensus. In total, ten statements reached final consensus in favor and one was rejected after two rounds. The detailed distribution of median scores, agreement proportions, and consensus categories across both Delphi rounds is presented in Table 2.

Overall, the results revealed a clear separation between interventions already integrated into routine clinical practice, mainly supportive-care measures, and those requiring further validation, particularly radiosensitivity test-guided treatment adaptations.

External validation

Nine statements that had reached full or near-full consensus were submitted for external validation. Among the nine items, full consensus in favor was achieved for individualized nutritional follow-up (median = 9, IQR = 9–9, 100% ratings 7–9) and long-term post-treatment follow-up (median = 9, IQR = 9–9, 93.3% ratings 7–9). Photobiomodulation also reached borderline consensus (median = 7, IQR = 6–9, 63.3% ratings 7–9). Four additional statements obtained provisional consensus in favor, including the prescription of radiosensitivity testing in complex cases (median = 7.5), in osteoradionecrosis-risk situations (median = 7), in genetically radiosensitive syndromes (median = 7), and for proton-therapy referral when a high toxicity risk is predicted (median = 7).

Adaptation of treatment modality by either IMRT or proton therapy within a therapeutic trial based on test results reached strong consensus (median = 8, 86.7% ratings 7–9), whereas preventive gastrostomy in nutritionally frail patients showed no clear agreement (median = 6). Discussion

This national Delphi consensus highlights a clear distinction between supportive-care interventions, which are widely accepted in routine practice, and radiosensitivity test-guided treatment adaptations, which remain investigational. The strongest consensus was observed for supportive and organizational measures, particularly individualized nutritional follow-up and long-term surveillance, whereas treatment decisions based on radiosensitivity testing elicited more cautious endorsement. This pattern reflects both the importance of toxicity prevention and the current limitations in the validation of radiosensitivity biomarkers for clinical use.

Several statements that reached consensus, including IMRT as the reference technique, individualized nutritional follow-up, and long-term post-treatment surveillance, reflect established good clinical practice rather than the specific contribution of radiosensitivity testing. Their inclusion in the Delphi process nevertheless provided a useful benchmark to contrast interventions already integrated into routine care with biomarker-driven decisions, which remain less mature and more cautiously endorsed.

The highest consensus levels were achieved for individualized nutritional management, systematic long-term surveillance and photobiomodulation. This confirms the central role of multidisciplinary supportive care in mitigating radiation-induced sequelae, in line with recommendations from MASCC [20]. Persistent treatment-related sequelae remain a major source of morbidity, with unmet supportive needs reported in over 40% of patients up to several years after treatment completion [21]. These findings reinforce the consensus emerging from the present Delphi process, emphasizing the importance of long-term survivorship programs integrated into routine RT follow-up. Photobiomodulation also received favorable ratings, though with greater variability, likely reflecting heterogeneous experience and the lack of evidence for preventive photobiomodulation.

By contrast, test-guided therapeutic adaptation, including the use of a radiosensitivity biomarker to decide proton-therapy referral or systemic therapy modification, achieved only conditional agreement. The current maturity level of radiosensitivity biomarkers was deemed insufficient to justify major primary treatment modifications outside a clinical trial setting. Statements supporting test-guided primary treatment adaptations, such as proton therapy referral versus IMRT, were nevertheless considered relevant. This perception likely reflects, on the one hand, the recent communication of a first positive phase III trial in favor of protons for oropharyngeal cancer (Frank SJ et al., 2025), and, on the other hand, the limited performance of currently available radiosensitivity blood tests and the absence of prospective data supporting their use for treatment guidance. Enhanced predictive performance has been reported for ATM testing when combined with NTCP models [15], representing one of the earliest attempts to integrate biological and dosimetric risk stratification in head and neck cancer. However, available evidence remains restricted to small cohorts, with only moderately discriminative accuracy (AUC typically < 0.8). Consequently, modifying standard treatment pathways based solely on biomarker results appears justifiable primarily within a clinical trial context. The use of blood-based and NTCP modeling has several preliminary demonstrators, including the RadioDtect ATM-test [15] in patients with HNC, or the RILA test [12] in non-HNC populations and candidate SNP panels [13] (Borchiellini et al., 2017), underscoring that these tools, though promising, remain insufficiently validated for clinical decision-making. The present Delphi exercise confirms that radiosensitivity testing and NTCP modeling are perceived as conditionally acceptable, with their use being justified only within therapeutic trials employing validated assays and supported by established reimbursement frameworks.

This study has limitations. The expert panel was limited in numbers, yet Delphi methodology prioritizes expertise diversity over sample size, and participants represented all major institutional categories involved in HNC care. The external validation round strengthened the robustness of the results but did not weight responses according to professional experience, which may explain the dispersion observed for some items. Moreover, the statements were anchored to an ATM-related assay (RadioDtect®), which does not capture the full diversity of radiosensitivity biomarkers under investigation, including genomic and proteomic candidates. It will be critical in future years to yield harmonized reporting of assay performance, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC across the different methods.

Despite these limitations, this Delphi study delineates a pragmatic roadmap for the clinical integration of radiosensitivity testing in patients with HNC. It identifies clinical scenarios in which treatment-guidance based on radiosensitivity testing in patients with HNC would be most acceptable. It also clarifies the conditions for ethical and regulatory implementation, and defines realistic priorities for future research. Next steps include multicenter prospective validation of biological assays combined with NTCP modelling; the development of standardized performance-metrics and reporting guidelines for radiosensitivity tests, and the incorporation of biomarker-based stratification within randomized radiotherapy trials- for example comparing photon and proton therapies. Through these, radiosensitivity testing may evolve from an exploratory research tool into a key component of personalized radiotherapy planning.

Conclusion

This Delphi study highlights a clear hierarchy of clinical acceptability in HNC radiotherapy: supportive-care measures are already part of standard practice, whereas radiosensitivity-based treatment adaptations remain investigational and should currently be evaluated within clinical trials. These findings provide a pragmatic framework for the stepwise clinical integration of radiosensitivity biomarkers.
