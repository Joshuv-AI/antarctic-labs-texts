Creating change through short-term pilots and rapid cycle testing: a case of cardiovascular risk reduction
Authors: Laura Ellen Ashcraft, Katy Mahraj, Laurie A Norton, Marguerite Balasta, Kayla Clark, Karen Glanz, John Wood, Wendell Kellum, Carolina Garzon Mrad, Jordan Sigler, Yaphet Getachew, Alexander C Fanaroff, Shivan J Mehta, David A Asch, Kevin G Volpp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the availability of safe, effective and low-cost diagnosis and treatment options for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and a significant decrease in death rates in the past several decades, ASCVD remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the USA. Scalable, evidence-based strategies have not achieved a level of success that optimally lowers cardiovascular risk. In this case study, we describe the first three years of the ASCVD Risk Reduction Initiative at a large academic health system, an effort to develop, evaluate and scale a heart disease prevention programme for patients whose modifiable risk remains high despite having access to care. The Initiative models a learning health system, combining traditional randomised controlled trials with quality improvement projects, iterative rapid cycle testing, data analyses, incorporation of learnings from concurrent projects at other institutions and user research to test a variety of approaches to cardiovascular risk reduction. This approach could be generalised to other important population health improvement challenges. In this article, we detail our pilot study process, results to date and challenges experienced in this example of care delivery innovation in action.

Background

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), the underlying cause of myocardial infarction and stroke, remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the USA. Important risk factors for ASCVD such as hypertension and hyperlipidaemia are easy to diagnose and have safe, effective and low-cost treatments yet these treatments have limited adoption.1 2 Many high-risk patients do not receive treatments due to low prescribing rates3 and medication adherence among patients who are prescribed therapies is as low as 40–45%—even among patients who have already had heart attacks.4 healthcare financing in the USA has tended to incentivise care being reactive and not proactive in improving health. Further, the clinical workforce continues to experience staffing shortages, agenda overload and burnout.5

The combination of these factors suggests that a new approach is needed to reduce ASCVD risk in the USA. We brought together traditional research methods, quality improvement, rapid cycle testing and human-centred design to address this challenge. We briefly describe each of these before describing our approach.

Traditional methods

The randomised controlled trial (RCT) is viewed as the gold standard in healthcare research due to the high degree of internal validity resulting from random assignment and blindness to treatment.6 7 However, interventions implemented in RCTs may not mirror real-world practice (external validity) and results may reflect the context within which they were tested.6 8 Innovations in evaluation such as observational and pragmatic trial designs seek to address these limitations while maintaining a similar level of rigour.6 Efficacy and effectiveness trials require significant investment of time and money, with a high opportunity cost inherent in intervention selection and design.

Quality improvement

QI is a systematic approach to addressing problems that arise in healthcare9 10 and is often anchored in metrics first introduced by Donabedian.11 QI work is often framed within continuous QI or the idea that every process is an opportunity for improvement.10 12

Rapid cycle testing

Rapid cycle testing uses iterative cycles of planning, testing and refinement to evaluate the desirability, feasibility and/or viability of a proposed solution at a small scale before investing in larger evaluation or implementation.13 Rapid cycle testing integrates proximal outcomes, existing data sources14 and qualitative insights13 through shareholder engagement to improve clinical care.14 This approach can identify the best ways to reduce barriers to access, improve care delivery and reduce health disparities15 and has been shown to be effective in QI initiatives, comparative effectiveness trials, tests of healthcare delivery and in the adoption and application of learning health systems.16

Human-centred design

HCD has three key elements, including assessing user needs, incorporating stakeholder feedback early and through the process and using systems thinking about interactions across individuals, groups and organisations and is well-suited to address complex problems in healthcare.17 18

In 2021, we identified a significant ASCVD evidence-practice gap in our health system. At that time, a retrospective analysis identified 133 070 primary care patients that had both elevated ASCVD risk with at least two elevated blood pressures in the prior year. To address this gap, we sought to create effective, scalable population health strategies. We envisioned a future state wherein the healthcare system (1) uses data to risk-stratify patients and identify those at high risk for cardiovascular events, (2) enrols high-risk patients into evidence-based care pathways that increase appropriate prescribing of anti-hypertensive and lipid-lowering medications using proactive outreach, (3) enhances patient engagement and (4) relieves overburdened primary care clinicians through new staffing models and automation.

We recognised that the aforementioned goals could not be realised through a singular trial, but rather a larger programme of research which leveraged the strengths of traditional scientific methods, QI, rapid cycle testing and HCD. We launched the ASCVD Risk Reduction Initiative (‘the Initiative’) in July 2021 to develop and evaluate innovative care models to prevent ASCVD events. The following describes how we developed the initiative and lessons learnt and proposes a roadmap for others who seek to address complex chronic disease in their health system.

The ASCVD Risk Reduction Initiative

The initiative included four phases: (1) planning, (2) pilot studies, (3) a hybrid type I effectiveness-implementation trial and (4) broader implementation and dissemination (figure 1). This manuscript focuses on the first three phases where we combined traditional RCTs with QI projects, rapid cycle testing and data analyses. As an interdisciplinary team, we conducted multiple pilot studies in parallel to test more hypotheses over a shorter period of time than would have been possible if conducted sequentially. This allowed us to develop and rigorously test a novel intervention to improve care delivery while building evidence for local improvement. Phase 4, currently in development, builds on the effectiveness and implementation results from phase three to expand the ASCVD intervention.

Phase 1: planning

We used four criteria to select a portfolio of pilot studies to launch the initiative. First, we focused on modifiable risk factors directly related to ASCVD: primarily BP and Low-Density Lipoprotein- Cholesterol (LDL-C). Second, we selected two regions within our health system with high disease burden which included an academic medical centre serving a primarily urban population and a community health system serving a mix of urban, suburban and rural populations in Pennsylvania. Third, we tested interventions on both patient and clinician behaviour.1922 Fourth, we leveraged behavioural economic approaches shown to be effective in other contexts using the framework of the ‘nudge ladder’ to map strategies on a continuum of likely effectiveness and degree of likely receptivity.23 Strategies at the bottom of the ladder (providing information) are generally seen as non-objectionable by recipients yet not very effective, whereas strategies at the top (eliminating or restricting choice) can be highly effective but difficult to implement (see figure 2).23 We prioritised approaches in the middle of the ladder such as defaults, incentives and reducing friction, because eliminating or restricting choices is not likely to be an option in this context.

During our planning phase, we also solicited participation from multidisciplinary collaborators and structured our team into workgroups and advisory groups. A leadership team consisting of the principal investigator and leads for research operations, innovation, clinical oversight and project management made major decisions and maintained accountability. We invited collaborators across research, clinical and operational roles to participate in a series of touchpoints for information sharing and decision making. Workgroups moved the initiative forward and shared insights across pilot studies on a regular basis, while advisory groups raised the initiative’s profile, maintained alignment with organisational priorities, surfaced new perspectives and validated major design decisions.

Phase 2: short-term pilot studies

We ultimately supported 10 pragmatic RCTs, six QI studies and four rapid cycle tests with a cumulative sample size of 75 149 patients and 122 clinicians, summarised in online supplemental table 1. As part of these efforts, we collected qualitative data from 109 semistructured interviews and 15 group feedback sessions with patients, clinicians, community members and other subject matter experts. The results of these trials are reported elsewhere.192224 In addition, we conducted simulations to examine how much reduction in cardiovascular risk could be expected from interventions to improve modifiable risk factors.

While several pilot studies focus on the effectiveness trial design, from patient messaging to staff and clinical workflows to technology build, participation rate and techniques to increase participation were one of the elements we followed most closely. Over the years, we have been able to triple participation in low-risk, clinically grounded interventions ranging from remote monitoring of blood sugars for patients with poorly controlled diabetes to colorectal cancer screening by shifting from conventional opt-in (ie, ‘Would you like to participate?’25) to opt-out in clinical contexts in which the benefits are well documented and far greater than the risks (‘We will sign you up for this clinically beneficial intervention unless you decline’26). Using a similar approach, one of our early pilot studies achieved an initial uptake rate of 35.7% in remote BP management using opt-out framing.27 A subsequent pilot study almost doubled that rate to 64.7% by adding text message outreach and removing friction for participants through a waiver of consent from our institutional review board, approved due to the intervention’s low risk level.21

Clinical process and outcome metrics

In addition to participation, we closely followed engagement in BP monitoring and lipid screening and impact on BP control and statin prescribing across pilot projects. While several pilot studies validated successful intervention ideas, others demonstrated which promising ideas were unsuccessful and provided insights as to why they were unsuccessful. Generally, interventions that required busy clinicians to do more—through nudges to prescribe anti-hypertensive and statin medications during visits, peer comparison feedback reports or a default schedule for return BP check visits in the practice—were less acceptable and/or effective than interventions that provided support virtually and asynchronously and that involved others besides the primary care clinicians providing that support.2831

Rapid cycle testing and HCD

To integrate the most promising approaches from across our RCT and QI pilots in preparation for the effectiveness trial, we ran four cycles of rapid testing with 6–15 patients across eight primary care clinicians in both urban and suburban/rural regions with a focus on maximising patient participation. The first two cycles offered a 10-week, text-based programme to lower BP and cholesterol; the second two cycles extended the programme to 16 weeks to develop, test and refine messaging, outreach timing and workflows to improve patient participation and experience. Through rapid cycle testing, we also gained insight into staff workload and documentation requirements, developed training and job aids and automated workflow where possible to increase efficiency. We assessed user needs and experience by incorporating feedback questions into staff-patient touchpoints throughout the process and conducting semistructured interviews with patients and referring primary care providers at the end of each cycle.

Fake back end

To facilitate rapid cycle testing, we used a ‘fake back end’ that presents as a real service to users but depends heavily on manual inputs. This is intended to enable testing of alternative approaches before committing the resources necessary to automate for scale. The fake back end consisted of a research coordinator serving as a patient navigator and the patient’s own primary care clinician standing in for whom we envisioned would be a centralised advanced practice provider in the effectiveness trial.

Iterative adaptation

By iteratively adapting our approach, we increased initial enrolment from 57% in our first cycle to 83% in our fourth cycle. While we did not anticipate that we would replicate this rate at larger scale, the consistent improvement of this metric in each cycle gave us confidence that our outreach strategies were acceptable for the effectiveness trial. The proportion of patients completing self-enrolment into the programme via text message versus requiring a staff phone call increased from 0% in our first cycle to 60% in our fourth cycle, a valuable finding that supported the possibility that the enrolment process could be scalable with less personnel time. Important modifications included reducing the number of text messages, adding the primary care clinician’s name into the message, framing that called on the patient to take charge of their health while conveying encouragement and empathy and language that supported lifestyle changes as well as medication.

Automation and remote care

In many of our pilot studies, we tested the application of automated text messaging, remote monitoring and telemedicine to population health to increase patient engagement while reducing provider burden. We incorporated these technologies using a Penn-based software platform (Way to Health) to bring care into our patients’ lives outside traditional healthcare settings while increasing efficiency for our providers and staff. Way to Health is a platform developed and tested to support behavioural change interventions now used in more than 300 programmes at more than 15 institutions.32 We leveraged the Way to Health platform and our Epic electronic health record (EHR) to automate text messaging, receive BP and step count data, send patient-reported data into the EHR and trigger clinical and navigator workflows. Automated texting was also an important vehicle in combination with study team phone calls for delivering behaviourally informed messaging to nudge engagement.33

Phase 3: a hybrid type I effectiveness-implementation trial

Through the pilot study process, we developed Penn Medicine Healthy Heart (PMHH), a 6-month, text message-based programme to help patients lower BP and cholesterol with support for remote monitoring from home (figure 3). The primary endpoints are systolic BP and LDL-C at 6 months post randomisation. A full programme description and trial protocol can be found here.34 An important input from outside of our pilot studies was a successful programme developed at Mass General Brigham that used remote monitoring and centralised non-clinical navigators and demonstrated significant improvements in BP and cholesterol in two cohort studies published during our development phase.3537 We built on and further refined this approach by layering in behavioural science strategies to increase uptake and ongoing engagement and software tools integrated into the EHR, developed and tested through our pilot studies, and designing an RCT to test this more rigorously.

Penn Medicine Healthy Heart. The Penn Medicine Health Heart programme was the culmination of the work in the first two phases of the initiative and is described in detail elsewhere. ASCVD, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease; EMR, electronic medical record; PCP, Primary Care Provider.

We designed PMHH as a randomised effectiveness trial of 2420 patients across 33 primary care practices split between Lancaster County and Philadelphia. A description of the design of this trial has previously been published.34 We are also collecting contextual factors around the implementation of PMHH using an effectiveness-implementation hybrid design (type I).3840 In parallel with the effectiveness study, we have scaled components of the programme into clinical operations and continued to optimise programme participation, efficiency and outcomes. Two of our pilot studies, centralised programmes for BP management and statin-prescribing, generated Penn Medicine leadership interest following strong published results and media attention. Both programmes have been implemented in Penn Medicine primary care practices that are not participating in the trial. In addition, we chartered new workgroups to explore emerging areas for programme impact and scalability to lay the groundwork for phase 4 (broader implementation and dissemination). Focal points include developing a payment model to support scaling PMHH or its components depending on trial results41 planning and conducting an implementation science evaluation of the trial and applying advanced data science methods to identify high-risk individuals who would benefit most from future similar interventions.

Making it happen in three steps

We encourage teams interested in applying this approach to start with three foundational steps: define the problem with clear metrics of success, prioritise hypotheses to test and foster accountable learning cycles (see figure 4). These steps are iterative and provide a mental framework to guide a project. This iterative process occurs across all stages of the four-phase programme strategy. Using directional signals of effectiveness and being comfortable with the uncertainty—along with more robust evidence when available—is critical in facilitating rapid progress.

Discussion

ASCVD affects millions of people, yet rates of BP and cholesterol control among individuals at elevated risk remain far from optimal. In response to this need in our health system, our team developed the ASCVD Risk Reduction Initiative and embedded a series of learning cycles. These cycles sought to understand how to develop interventions that are responsive to the needs of patients and build on the strengths of our investigative team. We employed a four-phase strategy including: (1) planning, (2) short-term pilots, (3) local implementation and (4) broader implementation and dissemination of the developed intervention.

A crucial component to advancing this work is ensuring its long-term sustainability. We leveraged significant investment of health system operations and research dollars in addition to the ongoing social investment of human capital as part of the socialisation process. We continue to use ongoing implementation evaluations about the ways in which the PMHH intervention is adopted with fidelity. We are also using economic evaluations to understand how to ensure financial stability through reimbursement of the programme long-term. These insights (publications forthcoming) support the potential for continued and sustained implementation.

Limitations

Our proposed process was not without limitations. Preparing PMHH required significant investment in research operations, clinician socialisation and staff training. This investment limits potential generalisability to healthcare settings without such infrastructure. We faced challenges including separate EHRs between the multiple Penn Medicine sites and differences in hiring, credentialing, research billing and referral workflows. Our navigators and nurse practitioners were cross used in research and clinical tasks which necessitated learning and documenting in multiple systems including our EHR, research database and remote monitoring platform to fulfil programme delivery and evaluation roles. Our ability to compare directly across pilot studies was limited by differences in eligibility criteria, study measures and intervention design. We experienced challenges with obtaining exit interviews with a fully representative sample of patients. Given time constraints, we planned the hybrid type 1 trial while several pilot studies were still underway.

Conclusion

Applying and combining the strengths of rapid cycle testing, QI, traditional research methods and HCD is a powerful way to address a clinical problem and may align with existing efforts toward a learning health system.16 Our combined and additive approach to care innovation in practice requires significant effort but can help bring institutional focus to addressing high-value care delivery challenges. We demonstrate that an approach for a problem as substantial as optimising ASCVD prevention requires relentless commitment from a variety of shareholders, significant resources, collaborator development and management and systems thinking.
