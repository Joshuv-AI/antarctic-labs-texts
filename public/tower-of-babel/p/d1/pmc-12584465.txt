Trends in oesophageal cancer surgery in Australia & Aotearoa New Zealand
Authors: Thomas A. Eldredge, Arsalan Bakhtiar, Manjunath Siddaiah-Subramanya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOesophagectomy is a complex procedure, demanding a high degree of surgeon and institutional expertise. Many health systems globally have centralised oesophagectomy services to optimise patient outcomes. Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand have relatively small populations and face unique geographical and economical challenges for centralisation of care, resulting in multiple centres performing fewer procedures. This has implications for training and maintenance of skill among surgeons. The aim of this study is to assess training, workload and current practices among Australian and Aotearoa New Zealand oesophageal surgeons, for comparison to international data.Methods and resultsAn online survey was sent to surgeons through multiple means - A total of 59 complete responses were received. Majority of respondents performed < 10 oesophagectomies in 2023 (n = 35/59; 59%), while 90% of hospitals amongst respondents performed < 30 oesophagectomies (n = 53/59). Similar rates of minimally invasive and robotic-assisted surgery were observed; however minimally invasive surgery was more common among surgeons with < 10yrs practice compared to those with > 10yrs (p = 0.016). Robot-assisted surgery was only performed by 16% of respondents; access to robotic platforms and training the largest reported barrier. Intraoperative surgical techniques and preferences were comparable to international data.ConclusionOverall, the study demonstrated contemporary oesophagectomy practices among Australian and Aotearoa New Zealand surgeons when compared with international practices. With continuous growth of minimally invasive and robotic-assisted surgery, early-career oesophageal surgeons must remain up to date with emerging technologies and practices to maintain optimal patient care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03280-2.

Introduction

Oesophagectomy remains the only curative treatment for oesophageal cancer; a morbid disease with 5-year survival of only 25% [1]. Surgical treatment options vary depending on the type and location of the tumour. Similarly, variation in operative technique exists among countries, hospitals and individual surgeons [2]. Oesophageal surgeons in Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand (AANZ) face geographical and population density challenges for patient care. Centralisation of care is difficult due to long travel distances, often at great expense, for patients to receive appropriate care. The current non-centralised model for oesophagectomy in AANZ has multiple centres providing care, albeit at lower volumes. Consequently, many centres perform low volumes of oesophageal resections when compared to global figures, albeit with comparable outcomes [3, 4]. Low case volume generates fewer opportunities for early-career oesophageal surgeons to learn and established surgeons to maintain proficiency. This can impact adoption of new and progressive treatment practices, such as minimally invasive oesophagectomy (MIO) and robot-assisted MIO (RAMIO). The global differences in surgical technique and decision making have been assessed in a large survey-based study [2]. However, involvement of AANZ surgeons was limited to 37 participants (14% of respondents) and may not provide an accurate representation of current practices. This study aims to comprehensively evaluate oesophagectomy training and practices among AANZ surgeons, enabling comparison with international data.

Methods

A standardised survey (see supplementary material) of AANZ oesophageal surgeons was conducted over a 3-month period between November 2024 and January 2025. The web-based survey was designed and distributed through Survey King (www.surveyking.com, Survey King, Southfield, USA), a subscription-based online survey tool. Links to the survey were distributed to participants through multiple means: (1) At the Australian & Aotearoa New Zealand Gastric and Oesophageal Surgery Association (AANZGOSA) 2024 Annual Scientific Meeting; (2) Via email to AANZGOSA members (n = 238), and (3) direct contact with individual surgeons. It is unclear how many AANZGOSA members perform oesophagectomy, as the membership base includes retired surgeons, trainees and surgeons performing solely benign and metabolic/obesity surgery. As such, a target sample size of 60 was assigned a-priori by the investigators, felt to represent approximately 50% of surgeons currently performing oesophagectomy in AANZ. Extracted data included demographics, prior oesophageal surgery training, surgical approach details, perioperative management and attitudes towards MIO and RAMIO. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics and Chi-squared test where appropriate. There was no remuneration for involvement. The project was approved by the South-Western Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee (study reference 2024/ETH01281).

Results

Demographics

A total of 59 complete responses were received. Majority of responses were from Australian surgeons (95%) and surgeons in metropolitan tertiary hospitals (79%). Surgeons at all stages of their careers (early, mid and late) participated in similar proportions (Table 1).

Training and case volume

Majority of participants (91%) had undertaken two or more years of specialist upper gastrointestinal (UGI) surgery fellowship (median 2 years, IQR 2–3), after attaining fellowship of the Royal Australasian College of Surgeons (FRACS) (Table 1). Further, more than 50% of participants have undertaken fellowships outside of AANZ; majority of those in the United Kingdom (87%). Majority of respondents performed less than 10 oesophagectomies in 2023 (n = 35/59; 59%) (Fig. 1), while 90% of hospitals amongst respondents in AANZ performed less than 30 oesophagectomies (n = 53/59) and 63% performed less than 20 oesophagectomies (n = 37/59) (Fig. 1).

Operative and post-operative management

A transthoracic approach was favoured by 97% of respondents (n = 57/59) over a transhiatal approach. A clear majority of respondents did not perform routine pyloromyotomy (n = 42/59; 71%), intra-operative anastomotic leak test (n = 46/59; 78%), or routinely use intra-operative adjuncts to assess vascularity of anastomosis (n = 52/59; 88%). Routine use of a feeding jejunostomy tube was favoured by 56% of respondents (n = 33/59). The thoracic duct was most commonly identified visually (n = 48, 81%), with peri-operative administration of a high-fat meal (n = 10/59, 17%) and indocyanine green (ICG) assistance (n = 1/59, 2%) less frequently utilised. Routine formal ligation of the thoracic duct was only performed by 27% of respondents (n = 16/59). Variation in anastomotic technique was observed. For MIO procedures, three respondents reported using cervical anastomoses only, however stapled or handsewn technique was not specified. For intrathoracic anastomoses, stapled techniques (circular and linear) were preferred for both open (n = 44/59, 75%) and MIO (n = 45/59, 77%) procedures compared to handsewn techniques. Respondents using handsewn techniques preferred 1-layer closure over 2-layer closure for both open (n = 12/59 vs. 3/59) and MIO (n = 3/32 vs. 1/32) procedures. Table 2 summarises the above data.

A formal ‘enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS)’ programme was regularly utilised by 54% of respondents (n = 32/59). Multi-modal analgesia was utilised by all respondents (Fig. 2), with the most used modalities being patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (n = 43/59) and regional anaesthesia placed by the surgeon (e.g. subpleural catheter) (n = 34/59). Approximately half of respondents (53%; n = 31/59) performed routine contrast swallow (or similar) prior to commencing oral intake; the timing of which demonstrated a bi-modal distribution with day 1 and day 5 being the most common responses (n = 18/59 and n = 15/59 respectively).

Minimally invasive and robotic-assisted surgery

Forty-nine respondents (83%) reported performing MIO (Table 2). This was more frequent among surgeons < 10years post-FRACS compared with those ≥ 10 years post-FRACS (n = 31/33 vs. n = 18/26), reaching statistical significance (p = 0.016). Around half of respondents performed MIO for both thoracic and abdominal phases (n = 27/49; 55%) with 35% (n = 17) using MIO for the abdominal phase only and 10% (n = 5) for the thoracic phase only. Majority of respondents (n = 37/49;76%) had been performing MIO since commencement of consultant practice, seven (14%) changed from open to MIO during early consultant practice, three (6%) in their mid-career, and two (4%) in their late-career. Of the respondents not performing MIO (n = 10), reasons included: active decision due to technical factors (n = 4/10), disease-related factors (n = 1), and patient factors (n = 1); not being trained in MIO (n = 3), and open surgery having shorter duration (n = 1). Four of the respondents not performing MIO stated they would like to undergo training, two respondents were unsure, and four did not want to train in MIO.

Robot-assisted MIO was not commonly performed among respondents (16%, n = 8/49), with Da Vinci (Intuitive Surgical, California, USA) being the only platform used. Of the eight respondents, six utilised the platform for both the abdominal and thoracic phases, while two utilised it for the thoracic phase alone. The largest reported barrier to training in, or performing, RAMIO was access to a robotic platform (n = 33/59; 56%), followed by access to training and proctors (n = 6/59; 10%). The time required to train in RAMIO was a reported barrier, due to impact on existing clinical practice and family life (n = 1), especially in mid-late career surgeons (n = 2). Fifteen respondents (25%) reported disinterest in undertaking training in RAMIO, including three respondents stating a lack of benefit from the robotic platform over traditional open/laparoscopic/thoracoscopic techniques.

Discussion

Oesophagectomy is associated with significant morbidity; up to 60% of patients experiencing a complication [5]. To achieve good patient outcomes, the procedure demands exceptional technical skill, backed by surgeon training and experience.

Procedure volume

Volume-outcome relationships are frequently observed for complex procedures, with multiple meta-analyses demonstrating decreased mortality post-oesophagectomy in centres with high case volume [6, 7]. The complex multi-disciplinary care required post-oesophagectomy demands knowledge and experience across allied health, nursing and medical staff. The literature supports this, demonstrating that institutional volume may impact patient outcomes more than individual surgeon volume [8]. Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand face geographical and population density challenges for care of patients with oesophageal cancer. Centralisation of care is difficult, as patients would need to travel long distances, at large expense, to receive appropriate care. The current model for oesophagectomy in AANZ is a non-centralised system, resulting in multiple low volume centres. In a global study by De Groot et al. ‘low-volume’ was defined as < 50 oesophagectomies [2]. In the present study, only one respondent reported >40 oesophagectomies at their hospital in 2023, while six respondents reported >30 at their hospitals. In fact, almost two-thirds (63%, n = 37/59) of respondents reported their centres performed < 20 oesophagectomies in 2023. A database-based study from AANZ in 2019 also reported very low average annual oesophagectomy numbers across involved hospitals, with only 4 centres (6.2%) reporting >12 procedures [9]. However, this study only captured ~ 50% of the total oesophagectomy procedures performed in Australia, which may account for the discrepancy in volume compared to the current study. This same study also assessed the volume-outcome relationship among the included hospitals, concluding that low and medium volume centres (1–5 and 6–11 procedures, respectively) have higher in-hospital mortality, albeit still meeting international benchmarks. Another 2018 study from AANZ, using a different data source, reported similar overall mortality rates to the 2019 study, again being comparable to international data [3]. The discussion around centralising oesophagectomy care in Australia continues. Some data suggest a benefit; however, this must be considered alongside the unique geographical context and financial structure of the AANZ health systems.

Operative technique and management

Considering the low case volume in AANZ, the challenge exists of providing adequate training to early-career oesophageal surgeons, while also ensuring skill maintenance among practicing surgeons. In this study, more than half of respondents undertook subspecialist oesophagogastric surgery fellowships internationally as part of their training. Most were undertaken in the UK, where oesophageal cancer services are centralised by the National Health Service (NHS), resulting in high-volume exposure to oesophagectomy procedures. This training model enables AANZ surgeons to attain high training standards internationally, while still having exposure to sufficient cases upon return to AANZ to maintain proficiency and achieve good patient outcomes. Various aspects of intra-operative and post-oesophagectomy care have been investigated to determine optimal practice. A systematic review from the United Kingdom concluded stapled oesophago-gastric anastomosis techniques result in fewer anastomotic leaks/strictures compared with handsewn methods [10]. Another recent systematic review evaluating routine intraoperative pyloric drainage procedures observed no difference in mortality, anastomotic leak, respiratory morbidity or vomiting [11]. Although heterogeneity in gastric emptying time among studies was noted, the authors still concluded that routine pyloric drainage is not necessary. No consensus recommendation exists for routine use of jejunal feeding tubes, use of adjuncts for thoracic duct identification, or routine post-operative radiographic leak test [12, 13]. In the current study, variance in surgical technique and management was observed, in keeping with existing recommendations (or absence thereof) and very comparable to international practices [2]. The only notable difference was utilisation of ICG for determination of anastomotic site, based on perfusion, with only 12% of respondents in the current study using ICG compared with 29% in international data.

Minimally invasive and robotic-assisted surgery

Advancement in oesophagectomy practices is predominantly driven by European and Asian centres, where high-quality clinical trials are performed with large participant numbers. Minimally invasive oesophagectomy is a significant step forward for oesophageal surgery, with large studies demonstrating improved morbidity and quality of life, without compromising oncologic outcomes, compared with open oesophagectomy [14–16]. Australians, on an individual basis, are often early adopters of new health technology, however at a health system level, adoption of new practices often trails Europe, Asia and America [17]. The present study demonstrates similar patterns of technology use and patient management among AANZ surgeons, when compared to international data. Globally approximately 80% of surgeons perform MIO, of which two-thirds perform totally MIO and one-third perform hybrid MIO [2]. Among respondents in this study, a similar proportion perform MIO, albeit fewer performing totally MIO (55% of MIO procedures). Notably, a significant difference exists in the proportion of surgeons performing MIO among those < 10 years post-FRACS compared with surgeons ≥ 10 years post-FRACS. This is consistent with modern general surgery training, with minimally invasive surgery now standard of care for many general surgery and oesophagogastric procedures. Transitioning from open surgery to MIO is increasingly unlikely as surgeons are more established in their practice. This study demonstrated that most surgeons performing MIO had done so since commencement of consultant practice (n = 37/49), with transition from open surgery to MIO occurring for seven surgeons early in their career, three in their mid-career and two late in their career. These data show that most AANZ surgeons are contemporary in their oesophagectomy practice, with timely adoption of new technology and practices to benefit the AANZ population.

Robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) is experiencing exponential growth globally, with AANZ no exception [18]. The number of General Surgery RAS cases performed in Australia almost doubled between 2018 and 2022 [19]. Further, General Surgery is outpacing both Urology and Gynaecology (the two other highest-volume RAS specialties) for growth of RAS, rising from 1% of all RAS cases in AANZ in 2008 to 17% in 2023 [19]. Even with such growth, access to RAS systems in AANZ is limited, especially in the publicly funded health system, as reported by many respondents in this study. Despite this, the present study demonstrated the proportion of respondents performing RAMIO is comparable to international figures reported in the study by de Groot et al. [2]. A significant limitation of de Groot’s study is the under-representation of surgeons in North America, who account for more than 50% of global RAS procedures [20]. Unlike the data for MIO in the present study, there was no difference noted between early-career and mid-late career surgeons regarding RAMIO; lack of exposure to RAMIO during training being a likely explanation. Few hospitals in the AANZ public health system have RAS platforms, and those that do, experience high demand from multiple surgeons in multiple specialties, significantly limiting access. A survey of UK and Irish surgical trainees in the NHS (a similar, publicly-funded health system) revealed almost three-quarters (73.4%) of trainees were interested in developing robotic skills, however very few had received any training opportunities (12%) [21]. As such, surgeons seeking RAS training often need to pursue international fellowships or private training courses, often at significant personal cost. Without prior RAS experience during training, however, AANZ surgeons seeking international post-fellowship robotic positions risk being disadvantaged compared with international applicants with foundational RAS training. Use of RAS has already been included in the core curriculum for Urological training in the United States of America and it can be expected General Surgery will follow [19]. Progress has been made in AANZ to develop RAS training pathways, with formation of the RACS Robot-Assisted Surgery Working Party. Robot-assisted surgery will continue to grow in AANZ and is anticipated to form a key part of the future of oesophagectomy, especially as technology and artificial intelligence systems evolve [22]. Future oesophageal surgeons in AANZ need to be cognizant of RAS training pathways and opportunities if seeking to incorporate this technology in their practice.

This study builds on the existing international data surrounding oesophagectomy practices and preferences. It forms the largest study of oesophageal surgeons in AANZ on this topic, providing a clear snapshot of practice that can be compared with in future studies. To note, the study design was to assess surgical preferences and opinions, rather than clinical outcomes. Previous recent studies have investigated oesophagectomy clinical outcomes, concluding comparability to international standards [3, 9]. As such, this study provides further context about the comparability of AANZ practices with international data. The study has some notable limitations. Selection bias exists for all opt-in survey-based studies, and low response rates from surgeons in Aotearoa New Zealand may further bias results and misrepresent practices. The relatively low response rate increases the risk of Type II error, however given the observational nature of this study, trend identification still holds some value in the context of non-significance. Regarding procedure numbers, this study relies on respondent data, which is subject to recall bias, rather than objective data such as databases or hospital auditing data. The data collected also doesn’t enable determination of the number of hospitals or whether these were privately- or publicly funded, as participants were not asked to state their hospital to maintain anonymity.

Conclusions

This study reflects the contemporary practices among AANZ surgeons performing oesophagectomy and demonstrates comparability to international preferences. Minimally invasive oesophagectomy (hybrid or totally MIO) is performed by majority of surgeons globally and thus should be incorporated into existing training programmes to ensure up-to-date practice. Although RAMIO is being utilised by some AANZ surgeons, current trends suggest ongoing growth and must be taken into consideration by up-and-coming oesophageal surgeons and training programmes.
