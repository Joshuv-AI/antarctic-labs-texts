Personalised screening intensity based on existing lung cancer risk and spirometry
Authors: Patrick Goodley, Hana Zahed, Haval Balata, Alberto Alonso, Anna Sharman, Zoe Merchant, Kath Hewitt, Richard Booton, Matthew Sperrin, Hilary A. Robbins, Philip A.J. Crosbie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExisting targeted lung cancer screening programmes are either annual or biennial. We tested the hypothesis that existing risk models can inform personalised screening intervals to improve cost-effectiveness and reduce interval cancers. Spirometric airflow obstruction is a biomarker of lung cancer risk available in some cohorts, so we also explored whether this could add predictive value for stratification.MethodsWe tested the performance of risk models to predict next-round risk among baseline-negative participants in annual screening in Manchester and National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) cohorts. Models were adapted in the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group and American College of Radiology Imaging Network (ECOG-ACRIN) sub-study of the NLST to investigate whether incorporating the spirometric percentage of predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1 % pred) improved discrimination and screening efficiency at next-round screens.ResultsIn Manchester, where a Prostate, Lung, Colorectal and Ovarian Cancer Screening Trial model 2012 (PLCOm2012) score of >1.51% was used to determine eligibility, cancer was detected in 1.1% of those with a negative baseline scan after a 1-year interval (45 out of 4136), and in 2.5% of those with a risk of ≥6% (27 out of 1082). The same trend was seen in the NLST, but with lower risk overall. The PLCOm2012 model discriminated next-round risk with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.72 (95% CI 0.65–0.79), whereas the Lung Cancer Risk Assessment Tool + CT model for negative CT results (LCRAT+CTneg) was potentially superior, with an AUC of 0.75 (95% CI 0.68–0.82) on external validation. The addition of FEV1 % pred as a predictor variable modestly improved discrimination (ΔAUC 0.01–0.04).DiscussionBaseline risk could be used to extend intervals for substantial numbers of lower-risk participants, thereby reducing cost and screening harms, while focusing resources on those at highest risk to avoid delayed detection. Adding FEV1 % pred is unlikely to meaningfully contribute to next-round risk prediction.

Introduction

Lung cancer screening using low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) reduces lung cancer mortality [1–3]. Uncertainties remain regarding aspects such as optimal screening frequency, participant selection and the extent of screening harms, as highlighted by a Cochrane meta-analysis of clinical trials [4]. There are ongoing efforts to implement and improve targeted screening in a range of countries [5, 6]. Most programmes offer either annual (e.g. USA, China, Poland) or biennial (England, Taiwan, South Korea) screening [7]. However, tailored screening intervals based on lung cancer risk could improve efficiency, lower costs and reduce harm [8]. Such an approach would provide more intensive screening schedules to those at highest risk and longer screening intervals to lower-risk participants. This is most pertinent to individuals with negative baseline scans who have a lower 12-month cancer risk than those with indeterminate nodules [9–11]. As most participants have negative scans, extending their screening intervals could have a substantial beneficial impact, although this would need to be balanced against the risk of delayed cancer detection. Risk-based criteria could provide a refined, personalised approach to balancing these risks and benefits.

Increasingly, risk prediction models are being used in routine clinical practice to determine screening eligibility, for example the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian Cancer Screening Trial model 2012 (PLCOm2012) and Liverpool Lung Project model version 2 (LLPv2) in the English lung cancer screening programme [12, 13]. There is an opportunity for such risk models to inform personalised screening intervals, as each participant has an individual score calculated at baseline. Bespoke models have also been developed that predict next-round risk of lung cancer, such as those by Schreuder et al. (patient characteristics, polynomial) and Robbins et al. (LCRAT+CT), but these are not currently recommended for clinical use [14–16]. In addition to standard clinical variables such as age and smoking exposure, biomarkers may offer predictive value. For example, the presence of airflow obstruction, as measured by spirometry, is an objective biomarker of smoking damage that is associated with lung cancer risk. The use of spirometry has therefore been proposed to aid risk-based eligibility for screening [17–19].

In this study, we investigated the potential use of risk models to predict incident lung cancers and tailor the length of screening intervals after a negative LDCT result. Specifically, we 1) evaluated the performance of risk models currently used to determine screening eligibility (PLCOm2012 and LLPv2), 2) evaluated the performance of models specifically designed to predict next-round lung cancer risk (Schreuder and LCRAT+CTneg) and 3) assessed whether measures of lung function using spirometry could improve risk model performance.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective analysis of two cohorts, detailed below, to investigate the association between lung cancer risk and incident lung cancer detection in the next screening round (defined as “next-round cancer”) following a negative baseline LDCT result. In addition, we performed an exploratory examination of spirometry and the risk of incident lung cancer development.

Study cohorts

National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) LDCT screening arm

In brief, the NLST [1] provided three rounds of annual LDCT screening to participants aged 55–74 years with a ≥30 pack-year smoking history who, if former smokers, had quit within 15 years. A negative LDCT result was defined as one with no non-calcified nodule ≥4 mm in diameter. The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group and American College of Radiology Imaging Network (ECOG-ACRIN) NLST sub-study incorporated pre-bronchodilator spirometry, which included 9413 participants [20, 21].

Manchester Lung Health Check (MLHC) pilot and clinical programme

The MLHC pilot (2016–18) [22] provided annual targeted LDCT screening over two rounds to ever-smokers aged 55–74 years in socioeconomically deprived parts of Manchester, with screening eligibility defined by a PLCOm2012 score of ≥1.51%. In 2019, the service expanded across north and east Manchester [23]. This was similar to the pilot except the upper age limit was 80 and the PLCOm2012 model with no ethnicity predictor (PLCOm2012NoRace) was used to define eligibility (threshold ≥1.5%); this excluded the effects of US racial/ethnic categories. A negative LDCT was defined as no non-calcified pulmonary nodule ≥5 mm in diameter, as per British Thoracic Society pulmonary nodule guidelines [24]. Spirometry without bronchodilation was performed to screen for airflow obstruction [25]. Data for this analysis were extracted from prospective clinical records.

Risk stratification by PLCOm2012

First, we evaluated the association between baseline PLCOm2012 risk and next-round cancer incidence. Where PLCOm2012NoRace risks had been used, PLCOm2012 scores were calculated at participant level. Ethnicity was assigned the referent category (white) for all participants. Multiple imputation with chained equations was performed within each cohort to impute missing values in predictor variables (see the methods section in the supplementary material). Baseline-negative screenees who attended the second round were stratified by PLCOm2012 score. The number of next-round cancers in each risk stratum was used to calculate incidence per next-round screenee and the number needed to screen to detect one cancer after 1 year. It was envisaged that, in practice, screening intervals would be based on a threshold risk, above which a shorter screening interval would be offered. Therefore, next-round risk was also calculated based on a range of thresholds.

Next-round risk prediction by existing models

Baseline assessment findings were used to calculate risk scores for PLCOm2012 and LCRAT using the lcmodels R package. LLPv2, LCRAT+CTneg and Schreuder's model were calculated from published coefficients [14, 15]. The predictive performance of these models for next-round risk was evaluated in terms of discrimination (area under the curve (AUC), with 95% confidence intervals). Calibration was not reported because of differences in the time periods over which each model predicts, such that expected and observed case numbers would differ for each mostly due to differences in the time horizon, rather than differences in predictive performance. AUCs are presented separately for the NLST and MLHC cohorts due to their differing screening eligibility criteria and risk distributions [26]. The Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis + artificial intelligence (TRIPOD+AI) checklist for prediction model development and validation was followed [27].

Spirometry and next-round lung cancer risk

Associations between spirometric measurements and next-round cancer were explored in baseline-negative participants who underwent spirometry, termed the MLHCspirometry cohort, and those in the ECOG-ACRIN sub-study of the NLST, termed the NLSTspirometry cohort, using univariable logistic regression. Continuous variables comprised absolute forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), percentage of predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1 % pred), absolute forced vital capacity (FVC), the FEV1:FVC ratio, and dichotomous criteria used to define airflow obstruction (FEV1:FVC <0.7 and FEV1 % pred <80%).

Development and validation of next-round risk models including spirometry

Next, we explored whether adding spirometry to existing risk models improved their predictive performance. FEV1 % pred was selected a priori as the most informative measure of airflow limitation because it is adjusted for age, sex, height, weight and ethnicity, and is a continuous variable used clinically to define the severity of airflow obstruction [28]. Its relationship with lung cancer risk has been repeatedly demonstrated [29, 30]. “Adapted models” were developed in the larger ECOG-ACRIN baseline-negative cohort, which included spirometry (NLSTspirometry), with versions incorporating FEV1 % pred as a predictor. Model discrimination was compared using AUC in the external MLHCspirometry cohort, as detailed in the supplementary material.

Screening efficiency

Lastly, screening efficiency of the existing and novel models was examined by calculating how many next-round cancer diagnoses may have been delayed according to the proportion of baseline-negative screenees selected for longer screening intervals by a range of risk thresholds. The relationship between putative thresholds for extending screening intervals and the expected impact of delayed next-round cancer diagnoses at each threshold was displayed as Lorenz curves.

Results

The majority of baseline screening scans were categorised negative in the NLST (72% (19 118 out of 26 455)) and MLHC (87% (5065 out of 5852)). Overall, 22 572 of these participants attended a second screening round and were therefore included in the study, 18 167 in the NLST cohort and 4136 in the MLHC cohort (see figure 1). Cohort characteristics are described in table 1, with further details in table S2. Next-round lung cancer incidence among baseline-negative participants was 0.34% (62 out of 18 167) in the NLST and 1.09% (45 out of 4136) in the MLHC cohorts. These cancers represented 37% (62 out of 168) and 63% (45 out of 71) of all those detected in the second round in the NLST and MLHC cohorts, respectively. In a biennial screening programme, these patients may not have received a cancer diagnosis for an additional 12 months, risking stage progression and worse outcomes.

Risk stratification by PLCOm2012

Calculated lung cancer risk was significantly higher in the MLHC cohort than in the NLST cohort (median PLCOm2012 3.49% (IQR 2.19–6.15) versus 2.28% (IQR 1.43–3.84); p<0.001), consistent with the risk-based eligibility applied in MLHC. Irrespective of the model used, patients diagnosed with lung cancer had significantly higher calculated risk scores than cancer-free screening participants in both the NLST and MLHC cohorts. In the NLST, the median PLCOm2012 risk was 4.63% (IQR 2.50–6.85%) in those diagnosed with cancer, compared with 2.28% (IQR 1.43–3.84%) in those without cancer. MLHC risks were higher overall, with median PLCOm2012 risks of 7.67% (IQR 3.59–10.7%) for next-round cancer cases and 3.47% (IQR 2.18–6.11) for non-cancer cases. Next-round cancer incidence among baseline-negative participants increased with PLCOm2012 score (figure 2, table S3). In the MLHC, there was a 15-fold difference in next-round lung cancer incidence between the lowest- (1.51–2%, incidence 0.14%, 1 out of 700) and the highest-risk stratum (≥10%, incidence 2.9%, 13 out of 450).

Next-round risk prediction by existing models

Discrimination of existing models to predict next-round risk was similar in both cohorts. PLCOm2012 had an AUC of 0.72 (95% CI 0.65–0.69). Other models ranged from an AUC of 0.68 (95% CI 0.60–0.76) for LLPv2 to 0.75 (95% CI 0.68–0.82) for LCRAT+CTneg in MLHC (see figure 3).

Spirometry and next-round lung cancer risk

Overall, 9885 study participants performed spirometry. The NLSTspirometry cohort comprised 6578 NLST participants, and 3307 participants formed the MLHCspirometry cohort (figure 1). A total of 58 cancers were detected in the second screening round, 20 in the NLSTspirometry cohort (incidence 0.30%) and 38 in the MLHCspirometry cohort (incidence 1.15%). Univariable logistic regression showed spirometric measures of lung function to have significant associations with next-round lung cancer, including FEV1 % pred, with OR 0.97 (95% CI 0.96–0.99; p<0.001) per percentage point increase in the MLHCspirometry cohort (table S4).

Addition of FEV1 % pred to existing models

Adapted models were trained in the NLSTspirometry cohort and discrimination was externally tested in MLHCspirometry. Internal model performance is described in table S5. External validation of these adapted models in the MLHCspirometry cohort found that discrimination ranged from AUC 0.68 (95% CI 0.60–0.76) with Schreuder to 0.75 (95% CI 0.67–0.81) with LCRAT+CTneg+FEV1 % pred (table 2). Adding FEV1 % pred modestly increased the AUC of all models, with changes ranging 0.01 (95% CI −0.02–0.04) in AUC units for LCRAT+CTneg to 0.04 (95% CI −0.01–0.10) for LLPv2. Compared with PLCOm2012, the only adapted models to have statistically significantly superior discrimination were LCRAT+CTneg (AUC 0.73, 95% CI 0.65–0.80; one-sided p=0.037) and LCRAT+CTneg with FEV1 % pred (AUC 0.75, 95% CI 0.67–0.81; one-sided p=0.031).

Screening efficiency at different thresholds

The impact on efficiency of screening fewer people at annual intervals in the baseline-negative study cohorts combined is shown in figure 4 and table S6. If the lowest-risk 40% of participants according to PLCOm2012 (risk <2.24%) had extended screening intervals, there would probably have been a delay in detecting 10% of next-round cancers (6 out of 58). Using a PLCOm2012 model incorporating FEV1 % pred, 7% of diagnoses would have been delayed (4 out of 58). There was little difference between models, with Lorenz curves largely overlapping across the lower half of the risk distribution. Using the lowest-risk 40% of screenees, according to the model variant with the best discrimination, LCRAT+CTneg with FEV1 % pred, would not have improved efficiency at this threshold, with delayed diagnosis for 12% (7 out of 58).

Discussion

Key findings

In two large independent lung cancer screening cohorts, the observed incidence of screen-detected lung cancer 12 months after a negative scan varied from 0.34% (NLST, 62 out of 18 167) to 1.09% (MLHC, 45 out of 4136). Irrespective of cohort, baseline risk score as calculated by the PLCOm2012 risk model was highly predictive of next-round cancer incidence after a negative baseline LDCT. Of the models designed for use after a negative screen, LCRAT+CTneg may perform best, but differences between models were small. Spirometric measures of airflow obstruction, including FEV1 % pred, were strongly associated with next-round cancer risk. However, they provided only modest additional predictive value to existing risk models. Had screening intervals been extended from annual to biennial for lower-risk participants, a large proportion of LDCT scans could have been avoided, with relatively low numbers of cancer diagnoses delayed.

Comparison to existing literature

There is accumulating evidence that risk prediction models can identify screening participants at higher risk of next-round cancer. However, tailored screening intervals have not yet been applied clinically. Different approaches to risk stratification for this purpose have been explored, such as the presence versus absence of nodules ≥4 mm (Patz criterion), risk models using baseline participant characteristics (Schreuder's patient characteristics model) and risk models combining participant characteristics with radiological findings (Schreuder's polynomial model, LCRAT+CT). Risk prediction model approaches have shown predictive ability superior to binary approaches. For example, external validation in the German Lung Cancer Screening Intervention (LUSI) trial cohort found the binary Patz criterion to have poor discrimination, with an AUC of 0.56 (95% CI 0.53–0.72) [16]. The multivariable LCRAT+CTneg and polynomial models were superior, with AUCs of 0.73 (95% CI 0.63–0.82) and 0.75 (95% CI 0.67–0.83), respectively.

Next-round incidence after a baseline negative result in the MLHC cohort was high (1.1%). It was dramatically higher than the analogous group in the NLST, yet this group is referred to a 2-year interval under the current UK protocol. The reason for the high risk is presumably related to the socioeconomic characteristics of the MLHC cohorts, as explored previously [26]. This finding highlights the potential benefit of tailored screening intervals in a UK setting. We found that baseline risk scores were highly predictive of next-round risk. This was observed for “pre-screening models” as well as dedicated next-round risk models. PLCOm2012, which has undergone extensive external validation and is in current clinical use alongside LLPv2 in the UK to determine screening eligibility, performed similarly to LCRAT+CTneg (AUCs of 0.72 and 0.75, respectively).

Screening efficiency has been investigated in other cohorts, with similar findings. In the LUSI trial, the 50% of the cohort with the lowest LCRAT+CTneg risks contained 20% of next-round cancers [16]. This study showed even greater efficiency, with 14% of next-round cancers occurring among the 50% of the cohort with the lowest LCRAT+CTneg risks. Using the simpler pre-screening PLCOm2012 with a marginally more cautious approach of extending the screening interval for 40% of participants would likely have delayed 10% of cancer diagnoses; an absolute risk of only 0.15% among all baseline-negative screenees (6 out of 3950).

Compared with annual screening for all, risk-stratified intervals could bring substantial reductions in cost, healthcare resource use and screening harms. The potential harms of lung cancer screening ought to be considered carefully; investigation of positive, indeterminate and incidental findings often lead to further exposure to ionising radiation and sometimes invasive investigations, and overdiagnosis can lead to distress and overtreatment. These costs and risks may be greater in lung cancer screening than programmes for other cancer types (breast, bowel, etc.) and challenge the feasibility of implementing further lung cancer screening programmes [31, 32]. Therefore, it is imperative that measures to reduce costs and risks are explored, including lengthened screening intervals and risk stratification. Conversely, it is possible for biennial programmes to identify participants at higher risk of interval cancers. In the highest-risk quarter of the MLHC cohort with PLCOm2012 ≥6% (26% (1082 out of 4136)), one in 40 were diagnosed with lung cancer 1 year after a negative screen. As pre-screening risks are already calculated in the UK setting, the only requirement for implementation would be the scheduling of the next screening round, subject to the LDCT result being negative. Indeterminate and positive findings already have surveillance and investigation protocols, such as the Lung Imaging Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS) or England's national screening protocol [33, 34].

There has been interest in the potential for spirometry to detect undiagnosed airflow obstruction in the high-risk populations targeted for screening [25, 35], although the downstream impact of this on health outcomes remains uncertain [36]. The potential of spirometry to contribute to next-round cancer risk prediction is novel. It represents an objective biomarker of smoking-related lung damage, which could plausibly add value in risk prediction models that otherwise rely largely on self-reported tobacco consumption and medical histories. In univariate analysis, a number of measures of lung function were significantly associated with next-round lung cancer incidence. However, FEV1 % pred added only modest discriminatory power to prediction models, and is therefore unlikely to offer a major improvement in prediction of risk after a negative LDCT. A prospective study may be able to establish added predictive value of spirometry with more certainty, although it would have to be very large to include more next-round cancer cases among relatively low-risk baseline-negative participants. Other types of information, such as blood-based biomarkers or radiomic analysis of screen-negative LDCT images, may hold more promise for producing large improvements in risk discrimination for individuals with a negative baseline screen.

Strengths and limitations

Although data were drawn from a large clinical trial (the NLST) and clinical programme (the MLHC), confidence intervals were wide due to the relatively low numbers of next-round cancers in the selected cohorts. Only two screening rounds were evaluated due to the MLHC programmes being limited to two annual rounds. Interval cancers and false positives were not included. By replicating findings in cohorts from different settings, their generalisability is strengthened. The cohorts had differing risk profiles, with the NLST representing more educated individuals with lower smoking exposure, and the MLHC reflecting more socioeconomically deprived populations with higher tobacco exposure [20, 26]. In addition, the findings were similar despite differences in screening eligibility criteria and nodule management protocols.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate that existing tools used to calculate lung cancer risk, and thereby determine screening eligibility, such as the PLCOm2012 model, can also predict incident cancers 1 year after a negative screen. This could enable stratification into a higher-risk group for annual screening and a lower-risk group that could more safely have longer screening intervals. Personalising screening intervals in this way could substantially increase cost-effectiveness and reduce harms, compared with annual screening for all, and improve early-stage lung cancer detection, compared with biennial screening alone. Dedicated next-round risk models, such as LCRAT+CTneg, may discriminate better, but differences are small. FEV1 % pred may add modest predictive value to existing risk models, although the resource implications of large-scale implementation of spirometry for this purpose may outweigh the gains in efficiency.
