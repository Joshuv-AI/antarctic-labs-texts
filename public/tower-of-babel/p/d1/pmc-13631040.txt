The correlation between yttria content and thermal–mechanical properties of GRX-810
Authors: Yehong Chen, Huan Ding, Selami Emanet, Ehsan Bagheri, Michael M Talachian, Paul R Gradl, Shengmin Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) prepared GRX-810 samples with different yttria contents (from 0.3 to 1 wt%) were systematically examined. The mechanical properties and thermal properties were measured under as-built and Hot Isostatic Pressing (HIP) heat treated conditions. The results indicate that yttria content significantly affects the thermal properties of as-built L-PBF GRX-810. Higher yttria reduces thermal diffusivity and conductivity by up to 6% in of as-built samples. HIP restores thermal properties to low-yttria levels, indicating that L-PBF-induced microdefects and porosity, rather than composition, primarily cause the thermal degradation. Based on the mechanical testing results, yttria content up to 0.45% provides the most favorable strength–ductility balance in the present dataset. Higher yttria content (from 0.5 to 1%) leads to the reduction of the elongation from 27% to around 4% in the as-built samples. Following HIP, the yield strength decreases from ~ 785 MPa in the as-built state to ~ 685 MPa. The elongation increases from 4% in the as-built state to ~ 42%. HIP samples with yttria content less than 0.45% exhibit a high ductility at approximately 40% maximum elongation, while HIP samples with yttria content higher than 0.5% exhibit a moderate ductility of approximately 18% maximum elongation at failure. These thermal and mechanical performance studies provide valuable guidance for GRX-810 applications and provide a reference for further AM-ODS alloy optimization.Keywords: L-PBF, GRX810, Thermal-property, HIP, Mechanical-property

Introduction

NASA recently developed a new oxide-dispersion-strengthened (ODS) NiCoCr-based alloy, employing a model-driven alloy design methodology and utilizing laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) based additive manufacturing (AM) technologies. This innovative alloy is designated GRX-810. NiCoCr alloys have attracted significant attention in recent years due to their excellent mechanical properties, thermal stability, and corrosion resistance [1–5]. Ni-based alloys’ stable mechanical strength at high temperatures and superb creep resistance make them the perfect candidate materials for energy generation applications, aerospace applications, heat exchangers, and high temperature industrial processing and petrochemical applications. Current research topics on NiCoCr alloys include the mechanisms of nanoparticle dispersion strengthening, and how grain refinement and compositional adjustments would impact mechanical and thermal performance, etc. Studies on AM of novel NiCoCr alloys are also popular topics, aiming to overcome the limitations of traditional manufacturing methods for fabrication of complex and precisely engineered components [6–13].

GRX-810 alloy is mainly composed of Ni, Co, and Cr and further enhanced by yttria nanoparticles, which was first disclosed in the 2020s [1]. NASA researchers revealed how AM could be leveraged to produce ODS alloy GRX-810 without the use of traditional resource-intensive processing steps such as mechanical alloying or chemical reactions. The GRX-810 alloy was specifically designed for aerospace applications, including liquid rocket engine injectors, preburners, turbines, and hot-section components, capable of withstanding temperatures up to 1,316 °C. Consequentially, GRX-810 represents a class of alloys that can bridge the temperature gap between traditional nickel-based superalloys and refractory alloys. Resonant acoustic mixing was employed to coat NiCoCr based powders with nano-scale yttrium oxides. Then, through the L-PBF AM process, the coated powders were successfully consolidated into 99.9% dense parts. Due to the high temperature and extremely strong mixing in the laser molten tracks, and fast cooling-rate of the L-PBF process (106–107 K/s), nano-scale yttrium oxides can be dispersed into the NiCoCr matrix. Taking the benefit of dispersing yttria nanoparticles into the alloy matrix, GRX-810 alloy is effectively strengthened by yttria for hindering the movement of dislocations and enhancing grain boundary stability at high temperatures. NASA researchers demonstrated that the mechanical results of GRX-810 had a twofold improvement in strength, over 1,000-fold better creep performance, and twofold improvement in oxidation resistance compared with the traditional polycrystalline wrought Ni-based alloys at 1093 °C [1].

Yttria is well known for its high melting point (above 2400 °C) and exceptional stability, which makes it an ideal candidate for high temperature strengthening in metal alloys. The incorporation of yttria nanoparticles uniformly in the base alloy matrix is the crucial factor for improving the performance of GRX-810. Element yttrium is widely known for refining the microstructures and enhancing the extreme temperature stability of alloys [14, 15]. The yttria nanoparticles can inhibit the movement of dislocations which is usually the primary reason for material failure under high stress [16–22]. Creep resistance is significantly crucial to the materials utilized under high temperature and large stress environments, where the conventional materials might deform slowly over time. The integration of yttria nanoparticles can enhance an alloys’ creep resistance. This dispersion strengthening mechanism could significantly extend the service life and durability of GRX-810, particularly in the applications that require long term performance in harsh environments.

Hot Isostatic Pressing (HIP) is a critical thermomechanical process used to optimize the alloy performance. HIP exposes parts simultaneously to high temperatures and uniform gas pressure (often exceeding 100 MPa) in an inert gas environment, a process effectively removing porosities and micro-defects. HIP can improve thermal conductivity, ductility, and structural consistency by enhancing interparticle bonding [23–26]. Although HIP provides these advantages, it may also result in yield strength reduction due to grain coarsening and detrimental phase formation and aging. For example, Kreitcberg et al. [27] systematically investigated the mechanical behavior of L-PBF processed Inconel 625 in the non-heat treated (NHT) state and after different post-processing routes, including HIP. Compared with the NHT, HIP effectively eliminated process-induced micro-porosity and homogenized the microstructure through recrystallization, which also led to grain coarsening and the formation of MC carbides. More importantly, HIP resulted in a pronounced improvement in tensile ductility: at 760 °C, the elongation to failure of horizontally built samples increased from ~ 5 to 7% in the NHT condition to ~ 25% after HIP, while the elongation of vertically built samples was maintained at ~ 22–25%, thereby largely suppressing the build-orientation dependence of tensile properties. Poulin et al. [28]. reported that a HIP treatment of L-PBF Inconel 625 for 4 h at 1120 °C under a 100 MPa argon atmosphere significantly reduced the porosity from (0.06, 0.25, 0.87, and 2.67%) down to (0.03, 0.06, 0.08, and 0.38%). Xu et al. [29] examined the impact of HIP (1200 °C and 150 MPa for 4 h) on mechanical properties of L-PBF Inconel 738 alloy. HIP reduced the defect densities in the XY and YZ planes of the as-built Inconel 738 alloy from (0.53% and 0.292%) to (0.251 and 0.123%). The HIP treatment also reduced the room temperature tensile properties of the as-built Inconel 738 alloy because of grain growth, low dislocation density, and coarser precipitates. However, HIP treatments significantly improved the ductility under room temperature tensile testing.

Currently, the effect of yttria loading in GRX-810 is not well understood. A uniform dispersion of an appropriate amount of yttria in the base alloy matrix is necessary for achieving desirable mechanical performance in GRX-810. However, under the same L-PBF laser melting strategies, different amounts of yttria powders may behave differently with the NiCoCr powders in terms of melting, mixing, and redistribution. As a result, different yttria contents could lead to distinct thermal, electrical, and mechanical properties in NHT L-PBF components. Therefore, a natural research question is how yttria content affects the properties and microdefect formation in L-PBF GRX-810 components.

In this study, GRX-810 alloys with different yttria contents were examined under both L-PBF AM NHT condition and after-HIP condition. Specifically, by varying the yttria content over the range from 0.3 to 1.0 wt%, this experimental study evaluated the mechanical and thermal properties of L-PBF GRX-810 alloys. The yttria composition matrix used in this study was treated as a literature-informed screening matrix within the GRX-810 alloy development framework, rather than a uniformly spaced statistical design-of-experiments matrix. The selected compositions cover both higher yttria contents comparable to previously reported GRX-810/AM-ODS compositions and a more densely sampled low-yttria region (0.30–0.45 wt% Y2O3), where the present results reveal a more favorable strength–ductility balance. Therefore, the current dataset should be interpreted as a guided exploratory screening of available yttria compositions, rather than as a statistically optimized composition design. More specifically, the objective of this work is to evaluate how yttria content and HIP treatment influence the thermal and mechanical properties of L-PBF GRX-810 alloys and to establish a preliminary processing–microstructure–property correlation based on measured property trends and representative microstructural observations. Representative microstructural analyses were also performed to support the interpretation of the observed property trends, while a fully quantitative microstructural investigation is reserved for a dedicated follow-up study.

The apparatus and theory for thermal diffusivity/conductivity measurements

Thermal diffusivity and conductivity measurements were conducted using a Netzsch LFA 467 HT apparatus, Fig. 2. Netzsch LFA 467 HT operates in a vertical orientation, with a pulsed energy (xenon light pulse) introduced through the bottom window toward the sample. The sample is centrally positioned, while an infrared (IR) detector is located above the top window. A xenon lamp serves as the flash source, controlled by software to deliver pulses ranging from 20 to 1200 µs. After the irradiation of the sample’s lower surface by the xenon lamp flash, the IR detector measures the temperature increase on the opposite side as a function of time [31]. The thermal diffusivity of the sample is calculated from the measured temperature-time curve using Eq. 1 [32].

Thermal diffusivity and conductivity data

As shown in Fig. 4, the thermal diffusivity of GRX-810 alloys increases monotonically with temperature for both NHT and HIP-processed conditions, with substantial overlap among different yttria contents. To quantitatively assess the significance of these variations, 600 °C is selected as a representative temperature, as it lies within the mid–high temperature regime relevant to service conditions and captures the characteristic behavior observed across the entire temperature range. At this temperature, the relative variation in thermal diffusivity across different yttria contents is ~ 2.0% for NHT samples and further reduced to ~ 1.1–1.5% after HIP, both of which remain within the ~ 3% instrumental uncertainty of the measurement technique. A comparison between low yttria contents (< 0.5 wt%) and high yttria contents (> 0.5 wt%) shows that increasing yttria by ~ 0.2 wt% leads to an average reduction in thermal diffusivity of ~ 2% in the NHT condition. Thus, the observed slight reduction in thermal diffusivity with an increasing yttria content was based on the evaluation of the highest accuracy data sets (3,4,6,7 for the NHT). The corresponding variation after HIP is below 1% and not statistically distinguishable from experimental uncertainty. These results indicate that yttria content has a small to negligible influence on thermal diffusivity, particularly after HIP processing.

Thermal diffusivity of a–b NHT GRX-810 sample and c–d HIP GRX-810 samples

In contrast, Fig. 5 reveals a more pronounced dependence of thermal conductivity on yttria content, especially at elevated temperatures. Using 600 °C as a representative reference point, the relative standard deviation of thermal conductivity reaches ~ 4.8% for NHT samples, which is close to the ~ 5% instrumental uncertainty. Therefore, the conductivity differences should be interpreted conservatively as a modest but physically consistent trend rather than a strong composition dependence. At the same temperature, HIP processing reduces this variation to ~ 2.5%, within the instrumental uncertainty limit. This behavior is consistent with the trends observed throughout the temperature range. At higher temperatures, such as 900 °C, the separation becomes more evident, where a low-yttria sample (#7, 0.40 wt% Y2O3) exhibits a thermal conductivity of ~ 27.1 W m−1 K−1 compared with ~ 24.9 W m−1 K−1 for a high-yttria sample (#3, 0.70 wt% Y2O3), corresponding to an ~ 8% difference. Overall, while yttria content introduces measurable variations in thermal conductivity in the NHT state, the thermal transport behavior should not be attributed to yttria content alone. Because porosity content, grain boundary density, and dispersoid distribution were not quantitatively measured for each composition before and after HIP in the present study, these factors are discussed here as possible microstructural contributors based on established thermal-transport mechanisms rather than as directly quantified variables. Therefore, the observed thermal conductivity variation should not be attributed to yttria content alone. Instead, nominal yttria content, defect population, grain-boundary scattering, and dispersoid distribution may collectively influence electron and phonon transport in L-PBF GRX-810 alloys, as reported for AM Ni-based superalloys and oxide-dispersion-strengthened alloys [1, 12, 24, 26, 35]. The reduced conductivity scatter observed after HIP is consistent with the possible effects of defect reduction and improved microstructural homogeneity commonly associated with HIP treatment in L-PBF Ni-based alloys [27–29]. However, because quantitative porosity, grain-boundary density, and dispersoid-distribution measurements were not performed for each composition before and after HIP, the relative contributions of these factors cannot be independently separated in the present study. Future work will combine quantitative porosity analysis, EBSD-based grain-boundary characterization, and high-resolution oxide/dispersoid mapping to determine the individual contributions of these microstructural factors to thermal transport in GRX-810.

To further quantify the temperature dependence, cubic polynomial functions were employed to fit both thermal diffusivity and thermal conductivity data over the temperature range of 25–900 °C. These fitted functions are intended only as empirical descriptions of the experimental data to provide continuous interpolation and facilitate quantitative comparison between different compositions and processing conditions, rather than as mechanistic models of thermal transport behavior. As shown in the Eqs. 12–14, the fitted curves show excellent agreement with the experimental measurements (R² ≈ 0.99), providing a continuous description of thermal transport behavior. Notably, the fitted diffusivity curves for different yttria contents largely overlap, consistent with the uncertainty analysis indicating negligible composition dependence. In contrast, the fitted conductivity curves display clearer separation for the NHT condition, reinforcing that the observed conductivity variations represent a small but physically consistent effect of composition and processing. These empirical fits therefore summarize the experimental observations and facilitate quantitative comparison between HIP and non-HIP samples, but do not provide independent mechanistic validation.

Tensile test of GRX-810

Tensile testing was conducted at a constant strain rate of 0.4 mm/min and at ambient temperature. A small 6 mm mechanical extensometer was implemented for strain measurements. Due to the small spring configuration of the miniature mechanical extensometer, occasional sudden slippage was observed during a few tests. However, this occurred only during part of the deformation process and did not affect the final elongation values or the overall strength–ductility trends reported in this study.

Figure 6 presents the tensile stress–strain responses of GRX-810 samples (along the L-PBF horizontal direction) with different yttria contents in the NHT and HIP-processed conditions, while the corresponding yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and elongation are summarized in Table 5. Clear composition- and processing-dependent trends are observed.

Engineering stress-strain curves of NHT GRX-810 samples and HIP GRX-810 samples

For the NHT samples, lower yttria content generally leads to high yield strength and high ductility. All samples exhibit yield strengths in the range of 732–837 MPa, with alloys containing higher yttria contents (> 0.5 wt%) showing consistently lower yield strengths of ~ 730–797 MPa, and limited elongation of only 4–11%. In contrast, the yield strengths for lower-yttria alloys (< 0.5 wt%) are high (813–837 MPa) while exhibiting substantially improved ductility, with elongations increasing to 25–27%. Ultimate tensile strength (UTS) follows a similar trend: high-yttria samples exhibit UTS values of 801–840 MPa, whereas low-yttria samples reach higher UTS values of 874–892 MPa, indicating a more favorable strength–ductility balance at reduced yttria contents.

HIP processing induces a pronounced shift in tensile behavior across all compositions. In addition to possible pore closure and microcrack healing, HIP-induced grain coarsening, recrystallization, reduced dislocation density, and residual stress relaxation may also contribute to the observed mechanical response under the present processing condition (1163 °C, 3 h, 104 MPa), as commonly reported for HIP-treated L-PBF Ni-based alloys [27–29]. However, direct quantification of these microstructural changes is beyond the scope of the present study and will be addressed in an ongoing microstructure-focused follow-up investigation. Although the yield strength is reduced after HIP, typically by 20–160 MPa depending on yttria content, the elongation increases dramatically. For high-yttria alloys (> 0.5 wt%), HIP increases elongation from 4 to 11% to 10–23%, while low-yttria alloys (< 0.5 wt%) exhibit a much more significant enhancement, reaching elongations of 39–42%. Notably, samples with 0.30–0.40 wt% Y2O3 (samples #5, #7, and #8) retain relatively high ultimate tensile strengths of ~ 822–825 MPa after HIP while achieving elongations exceeding 40%, highlighting the strong synergy between reduced yttria content and HIP treatment in improving the mechanical properties. Therefore, the improved ductility and reduced yield strength after HIP are interpreted as a combined consequence of defect-related improvement and thermally induced microstructural recovery, rather than crack elimination alone. This interpretation is consistent with the present tensile trends and prior HIP studies, while detailed verification of pore evolution, crack healing, grain coarsening, recrystallization, and residual stress relaxation will be presented in a subsequent study.

These tensile results demonstrate that yttria content plays a critical role in governing the strength–ductility trade-off of GRX-810 alloys. Higher yttria additions promote strength (specifically at high temperatures) but severely limit ductility, whereas lower yttria contents enable both high strength and exceptional plasticity. HIP processing further amplifies this effect by significantly enhancing ductility while maintaining competitive strength levels, making low-yttria GRX-810 alloys (< 0.5 wt% Y2O3) particularly attractive for applications requiring a balanced combination of strength and deformation capability.

A similar trend was also reported by NASA researchers [1], where HIP-treated GRX-810 samples exhibit consistently lower ultimate tensile strength than their as-built counterparts at low temperatures, but converge toward comparable strength levels as temperature increases [1]. Notably, the difference between HIP and as-built conditions diminishes above ~ 600 °C, indicating that defect elimination and microstructural homogenization induced by HIP primarily influence room- and intermediate-temperature mechanical behavior [1]. This consistency between the present results and NASA’s high-temperature tensile data further supports the role of HIP in redistributing the strength–ductility balance without compromising high-temperature load-bearing capability.

To better understand the impact of yttria content on tensile properties, the fracture surfaces of tensile samples were examined. Tensile data in Table 5 indicate that yttria contents less than 0.45% lead to higher elongation.

Figure 7 presents a direct comparison of the tensile curves of HIP and Non-HIP #1 samples, along with their respective fracture morphologies. Sample #1 has the highest yttria content (1%) among all samples. It can be observed that the fracture surface of the Non-HIP sample contains numerous cracks, which significantly reduce the sample’s ductility. After HIP treatment, these cracks disappear, leading to improved ductility. Meanwhile, compared to the NHT sample, the yield strength of the HIP sample decreases by 2.9%, while its elongation to failure increases by 260%. Microcracks are observed on most of the fractured surfaces, particularly in the L-PBF horizontal specimens, which are more sensitive to microcracks forming in parallel to the build direction. The reduced ductility in the high-oxide specimens is consistent with microcrack formation and possible oxide agglomeration; however, a fully quantitative fracture-mechanism analysis, including particle size distribution and interparticle spacing evaluation, is beyond the scope of the present study. Therefore, the present observations should be interpreted as evidence-consistent rather than definitive proof of the ductility reduction mechanism. Notably, this effect is no longer evident below 0.5% oxide content. To further examine whether these cracks were formed during tensile testing or in existence inside the original samples, MicroCT scanning was performed on samples that had not undergone tensile testing, as shown in Fig. 16. The results indicate that, except for the #1 Non-HIP sample, where pores were detected, no pores were found in the #1 HIP and #6 samples with the MicroCT resolution of 1 micron. This suggests that large pre-existing pores are not the dominant feature in most samples. However, due to the MicroCT resolution limit (~ 1 μm), smaller submicron defects and nanoscale pores cannot be excluded. Therefore, the observed cracks may be associated with tensile deformation, but their complete absence before testing cannot be definitively confirmed. Further research is necessary to understand the effects of both high and low yttria concentrations on phase formation and dislocation slip mechanisms in the matrix.

GRX-810 is an ODS alloy formed in the L-PBF AM process using nanoscale oxide particles (such as yttria) coated L-PBF NiCoCr-base metal powders. The typical metal powder size for L-PBF usage is from 10 to 45 microns and the typical diameter of oxide particles is 50 nm or less. Therefore, the diameter ratio between the metal powder to ceramic powder is around 1000 or more. Let’s assume both yttria and NiCoCr-base metal particles are spherical shape and have uniform size. As surface area of particles is inversely proportional to their diameter, although only small quantity of yttria was used in this study, from 0.3% to 1%, yttria nano particles result in a large total surface area and can effectively cover the entire surface of the NiCoCr-base metal powders even with a low yttria content of 0.3%. For high yttria content, such as above 0.5%, many yttria powders are likely not be able to make direct contact with NiCoCr-base-metal powders. This could increase the difficulty of achieving uniform yttria dispersion into the molten tracks during the laser melting process and may contribute to microdefects and pore formation in the L-PBF NHT parts. In addition, melt pool dynamics during L-PBF rapid solidification and the wetting behavior between yttria particles and the NiCoCr molten matrix may strongly influence particle redistribution, oxide dispersion stability, and local defect formation. These AM-specific phenomena are expected to play an important role in determining the final microstructure and mechanical response of GRX-810, but their direct evaluation requires dedicated high-resolution characterization and is beyond the scope of the present study. This surface-area-based interpretation provides a qualitative explanation for why lower yttria contents may achieve more effective particle dispersion, while excessive yttria additions may increase the likelihood of particle agglomeration and defect formation during L-PBF processing. However, direct quantitative evidence of particle distribution uniformity, agglomeration thresholds, and interparticle spacing is beyond the scope of the present study and requires dedicated high-resolution microstructural characterization. A more detailed investigation of these mechanisms will be presented in a follow-up study.

Conclusion

This work elucidates the coupled roles of yttria addition and HIP treatment in governing the thermal and mechanical behavior of L-PBF-fabricated NiCoCr based GRX-810 alloys. Across eight compositions (0.3–1 wt% Y2O3), thermal diffusivity exhibits negligible sensitivity to yttria content, with all measured variations remaining within the ~ 3% instrumental uncertainty of thermal analysis, indicating that heat diffusion is largely insensitive to yttria concentration in this regime.

Thermal conductivity, however, shows a measurable dependence on both composition and post-processing. Quantitative analysis of the thermal conductivity reveals that the standard deviation among different yttria concentrations is ~ 0.6–1.4 W m−1 K−1, corresponding to ~ 4.5–5.3% for NHT samples and ~ 1.8–3.2% for HIP-processed samples over most of the investigated temperature range. The larger scatter in NHT samples exceeds the nominal instrumental uncertainty and is attributed not only to uncertainty propagation from specific heat measurements, but also to microstructural heterogeneity including porosity, grain boundary density, and dispersoid distribution, which strongly influence electron and phonon scattering during thermal transport. HIP significantly suppresses this variability by reducing porosity and improving microstructural homogeneity.

Despite this measurement-related dispersion, a consistent compositional trend is resolved: increasing yttria content leads to a modest but systematic reduction in thermal conductivity, while a non-monotonic dependence is also present, with the alloy containing ~ 0.35 wt% yttria exhibiting the highest conductivity. Thermal conductivity increases monotonically with temperature for all compositions, and HIP further enhances heat transport at elevated temperatures. At 900 °C, HIP treatment results in an average increase in thermal conductivity of ~ 1.45 W m−1 K−1 (~ 5.5%) compared with NHT samples.

Mechanical testing reveals that yttria content exerts a strong and non-linear influence on ductility, whereas yield strength is markedly reduced by HIP but remains largely independent of yttria concentration. HIP reduces the yield strength from ~ 785 MPa in the NHT state to ~ 685 MPa, mainly due to grain coarsening, recrystallization, reduced dislocation density, and residual stress relaxation. At the same time, pore closure, microcrack healing, and improved microstructural homogeneity significantly enhance tensile ductility. The ultimate tensile strength of NHT GRX-810 samples with an yttria content less than 0.45% is around ~ 880 MPa and the ultimate tensile strength of HIP samples with an yttria content higher than 0.5% is around ~ 825 MPa. HIP samples with yttria content less than 0.45% exhibit a high ductility at approximately 40% maximum elongation, while HIP samples with yttria content higher than 0.5% exhibit a moderate ductility of approximately 18% maximum elongation at failure.

These results reveal a coupled structure–property relationship in GRX-810 alloys. Yttria content affects particle dispersion during L-PBF processing, which influences oxide agglomeration, defect formation, and local porosity evolution. These microstructural features govern both thermal transport and tensile behavior by altering electron/phonon scattering, crack sensitivity, and deformation compatibility. HIP further modifies this relationship by reducing defect sensitivity and improving microstructural stability; possible contributions from pore closure, microcrack healing, grain coarsening, recrystallization, and residual stress relaxation are consistent with prior studies on HIP-treated L-PBF Ni-based alloys, but their quantitative verification will be the focus of an ongoing follow-up microstructural study.

Together, these results demonstrate that yttria concentration has limited impact on thermal diffusivity but plays a decisive role in tuning thermal conductivity and ductility, while HIP serves as a critical post-processing step to stabilize thermal transport and significantly improve mechanical reliability. This integrated understanding provides quantitative guidance for optimizing GRX-810 alloys through composition–processing co-design for high-temperature, high-performance applications. In the present dataset, the low-yttria regime (0.30–0.45 wt% Y2O3) provides the most favorable strength–ductility balance. Future work should further investigate the effects of melt pool dynamics, particle wetting behavior, and oxide redistribution during rapid solidification to better establish the AM-specific mechanisms governing oxide dispersion stability and defect formation in GRX-810 alloys.
