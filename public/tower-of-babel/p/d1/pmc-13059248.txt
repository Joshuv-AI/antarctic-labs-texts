Potassium fertilization affects nutritional quality and aroma in Haruka apple fruits: insights from multi-omics analysis
Authors: Jiangtao Zhou, Yanhui Chen, Guodong Kang, Jiali He, Cungang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApples are important cultivated fruit crops grown worldwide and represent one of the most nutritious foods in a healthy diet. They are rich in nutrients, such as sugars, organic acids, vitamins, and amino acids, and possess a pleasant aroma. Spraying different types of potash fertilizers on apples can affect the fruit quality at maturity. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms of this effect remain unclear. In this study, two types of potassium (K) fertilizers (K2SO4 and KH2PO4) were applied to the leaves of Haruka apples to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of different K fertilizer types of on fruit nutritional quality and aroma, construct ceRNA network, and identify the most effective K fertilizer type.ResultsSeventy-seven and 45 differentially altered compounds (DCCs) were identified in the nutritional and volatile compounds of fruit flesh of apples under K2SO4 treatment, whereas 25 and 8 DCCs were identified under the KH2PO4 treatment, respectively. Both K2SO4 and KH2PO4 increased the content of tyrosine, vitamin B2, and lipids in mature apples. Additionally, K2SO4 enhanced the nicotinamide content. KH2PO4 improved the aroma of apples during ripening only through the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway, whereas K2SO4 improved the aroma during ripening through the LOX, MEP, and shikimate pathways. K fertilizer treatment increased the expression of LOX and alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) genes by regulating the LOX pathway, whereas the expression of LOX and ADH genes was downregulated in the KH2PO4 treatment compared with that in the K2SO4 treatment. ceRNA networks associated with apples sprayed with potash fertilizer were also established, showing that mdm-miR159a and PC-3p-45634_116 are involved in apple aroma and lipid synthesis.ConclusionsThe application of K2SO4 was more effective than that of KH2PO4 at improving fruit nutritional quality and aroma, offering valuable guidance for production in orchards.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08460-7.

Introduction

Apples (Malus domestica Borkh.) are one of the most important cultivated fruit crops grown worldwide and represent one of the most nutritious foods in a healthy diet. Applies contain water, sugar, organic acids, vitamins, minerals, and high polyphenol antioxidant content (second only to cranberries); they contain 10–30% of the recommended daily consumption of fiber and potassium; and they have a pleasant aroma [1]. As the standard of living has improved, consumers have become increasingly concerned regarding the aroma and nutrient content of apples, implying that research on these aspects has garnered considerable interest in the apple industry.

Potassium (K) is an essential mineral for plant development and plays a role in physiological functions, including carbohydrate metabolism, enzyme activity, osmotic regulation, water-use efficiency, nitrogen uptake, protein synthesis, and assimilate translocation [2]. According to Mirande-Ney et al. [3], alterations in lipid metabolism of the mesocarp of the oil palm fruit occur under K deficiency, and Gil et al. [4] showed that K fertilization is related to the amino acid, myo-inositol, fatty acid, and putrescine contents of tomato fruits. Appropriate K fertilizer treatment can also increase the weight and firmness of apples [5]. Chen et al. [6] found that the application of K2SO4 increased the diameter of fruits but had no effect on fruit firmness. The application of K can also improve the flavor of tomato fruits [7]; Lin et al. [8] found that the vitamin C, soluble solid, glutamic acid, aspartic acid, alanine, and volatile acetic acid fractions of melons significantly increased after the application of K2SO4. K fertilizer affected the titratable acidity of apple fruits [9], and the vitamin C contents increased under K fertilizer treatment in apples [10]. Similarly, foliar application of K2SO4 and KH2PO4 to grapes increased the total soluble solids (TSS) and total anthocyanin contents, with KH2PO4 proving the most efficient [11]. K levels have different effects on the organic acid metabolism in different plant species [12, 13]; although studies have shown that potash fertilizers can affect the fruit quality of apples, no studies have reported how different types of potash fertilizers affect their nutritional and volatile organic components.

In addition to protein-coding RNAs (mRNAs), noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) play essential regulatory roles in nutritional components and aroma pathways of fruits. For example, circRNAs are related to the biosynthesis of amino acids in apples [14], with 29 differentially expressed miRNAs thought to regulate aroma compound biosynthesis in 'Binzi' apple peels during storage periods [15]. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis has indicated that differentially expressed lncRNAs are involved in the phospholipase D signaling pathway and glycerophospholipid metabolism linked to fruit cracking in pomegranates [16]. During the three ripening stages of peach fruits, 575 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, and enrichment analysis indicated their potential contribution to the physiological and metabolic changes associated with fruit ripening, particularly aroma compound accumulation [17]. Compared with these existing studies, no reports exist on how different types of potash fertilizers affect ncRNA expression in apples.

Haruka apples offer a crisp and refreshing taste, an intense fragrance, and are rich in nutrients, such as vitamin C, K, and dietary fiber [18], and have a relatively high demand for K [19]. Therefore, K deficiency may lead to a decline in the nutritional content and flavor of fruits. To date, research on Haruka apples has focused on aspects related to harvesting and storage. For instance, the postharvest storage characteristics and regulating factors of Haruka apples have been analyzed previously, [20] including the optimal harvesting period [21]. However, no studies have investigated how different types of potash fertilizers affect the nutritional and volatile organic components of Haruka apples and ncRNAs. To address this gap, in this study, we analyze the effects of foliar spraying of K2SO4 and KH2PO4 on the nutritional and volatile organic metabolites in Haruka apple fruits. Multilevel assessments are conducted at the mRNA, non-coding RNA (lncRNA, miRNA, and circRNA), and metabolic levels to explore the altered patterns and underlying molecular mechanisms. We also construct a ceRNA network to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms through which the different K fertilizers influence the fruit quality, and identify the type that can more effectively enhance the nutritional and volatile organic profiles of these apples. Our results provide a valuable reference for the application of K fertilizers during apple production.

Materials and methods

Fruit material and treatments

The experiment was conducted from August to October, 2023 at an apple cultivation demonstration base of the Institute of Fruit Tree Research, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS). The orchard is situated on flat terrain with brown loam soil characterized by a pH of 5.6. The soil nutrient contents were as follows: alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen, available phosphorus, and available K at 49.4, 13.8, and 98.4 mg/kg, respectively. Two-year-old ‘Haruka’ apple trees, trained in a slender spindle system and planted at a spacing of 3.5 m × 1.0 m (row × tree), were used in this study. Three foliar spray treatments were applied: (1) 0.3% K2SO4 (T1), (2) 0.3% KH2PO4 (T2), and (3) clean water as the control (CK). A total of 18 uniformly growing trees were selected, with six replicates per treatment. Foliar sprays were administered on August 10 and August 30, 2023. Applications were conducted until the leaf surfaces were thoroughly wetted without the formation of dripping droplets. On October 30, six mature fruits were randomly collected from each tree and combined to form one composite sample per biological replicate. Fruit flesh tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Fruit quality parameters

Single fruit weight (g) was determined using an electronic balance with mean values calculated. The fruit shape index was calculated as the ratio of the longitudinal diameter to horizontal diameter (mm), both of which were measured using a vernier scale. Soluble solids content (SSC; %) was measured with a PAL-1 digital Brix meter (Atago Refractometer, Tokyo, Japan). The vitamin C (Vc) content (mg/100 g) was determined using the 2,6-dichloroindophenol titration method.

Amino acid, vitamin, lipid, and metabolite extraction

For the extraction of amino acids, vitamins, and lipid metabolites, biological samples were first freeze-dried under vacuum using a lyophilizer (Scientz-100F) and ground into a fine powder. Approximately 50 mg of the resulting powder was accurately weighed with an electronic balance (MS105DΜ) and mixed with 1200 μL of a pre-chilled (–20 °C) methanol–water solution (70:30, v/v) containing internal standards. The mixture was subjected to vortex mixing for 30 s at 30-min intervals and repeated six times. Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was carefully collected, passed through a 0.22-μm microporous membrane, and transferred into an injection vial for subsequent analysis. For volatile organic compounds, 500 mg (equivalent to 1 mL) of powdered sample was transferred into a 20 mL headspace vial (Agilent, CA, USA) containing a NaCl-saturated solution to inhibit enzymatic activity. The vials were hermetically sealed with crimp caps fitted with TFE-silicone septa (Agilent Technologies) and incubated at 60 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, volatile compounds were adsorbed using a 120 μm DVB/CWR/PDMS solid-phase microextraction (SPME) fiber (Agilent) exposed to the headspace for 15 min at 60 °C. All extractions were conducted in triplicate.

UPLC and GC–MS analyses

Analysis of amino acids, vitamins, and lipid metabolites was conducted on an ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (ExionLC™ AD, Sciex) coupled with an electrospray ionization (ESI) tandem mass spectrometer operating in both positive and negative ion modes. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an Agilent SB-C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 µm) with a mobile phase comprising solvents A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The injection volume was 2 µL. The effluent was directed into a quadrupole ion trap (QTRAP) mass spectrometer equipped with an ESI source maintained at 550 °C, with ion spray voltages of 5500 (positive) and –4500 V (negative). Gas settings were as follows: ion source gas I (GSI) at 50 psi; gas II (GSII) at 60 psi; curtain gas (CUR) at 25 psi; and collision-activated dissociation (CAD) was set to high. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) was employed using nitrogen as the collision gas at medium pressure. Specific MRM transitions were monitored for each metabolite based on their respective elution windows. The desorption of volatile organic compounds from the SPME fiber was conducted in the gas chromatography (GC) injection port at 250 °C for 5 min under the splitless mode using an Agilent 8890 GC system coupled with a 7000D triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. Separation was achieved with a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) using helium as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. The oven temperature program was initiated at 40 °C (held for 3.5 min), increased to 100 °C at 10 °C/min, subsequently increased to 180 °C at 7 °C/min, and finally increased to 280 °C at 25 °C/min, with a final hold time of 5 min. The injector temperature was maintained at 250 °C. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted in electron impact mode at 70 eV, with the quadrupole, ion source, and transfer line temperatures set to 150, 230, and 280 °C, respectively. Data acquisition was conducted in the selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode. All analytical procedures were conducted by Maiwei Metabolic Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Differential metabolites

Differential metabolites were identified by applying a variable importance in projection (VIP) threshold greater than 1 from orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and a |Log2FC| value of at least 1.0. The OPLS-DA model was constructed using MetaboAnalystR with mean-centered and log-transformed data, and validated through 200 permutation tests to mitigate overfitting. Relevant score and permutation plots were generated to support model evaluation.

RNA extraction, RNA sequencing, and library preparation

Total RNA was extracted from fruit flesh tissues of ‘Haruka’ apples subjected to CK, T1, and T2 treatments using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher). After ribosomal RNA depletion, the enriched RNA fraction, comprising mainly mRNA and non-coding RNA, was used for library construction. Second-strand cDNA synthesis involved incorporation of dUTP for marking, followed by digestion with heat-labile Uracil-Specific Excision Reagent (USER) enzyme (NEB, cat. m0280, USA). Amplification of adapter-ligated products was carried out through PCR, and the resulting libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The experiment included three biological replicates per treatment group, totaling nine constructed libraries. All sequencing procedures were conducted by Maiwei Metabolic Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Transcriptomic analyses

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality assessment with FastQC (v0.11.9). High-quality reads were aligned to the Malus × domestica 'Golden Delicious' GDT2T Genome v1.0 Assembly & Annotation (https://www.rosaceae.org/Analysis/20432016) reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Transcript assembly was subsequently performed with StringTie (v2.1.6) based on the aligned reads.

DEmRNA identification and functional enrichment analysis

Gene expression levels were quantified using FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped fragments), and differentially expressed mRNAs were identified with the DESeq2 package (v1.22.2) applying a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Functional annotation of these genes was conducted through Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses using the OmicStudio online platform.

MiRNA, lncRNA and circRNA identification and target gene prediction

High-quality small RNA reads obtained after filtering out low-quality sequences, adapters (< 18 nt), and poly(A) tails were utilized for miRNA identification. These reads were further processed by aligning against the Rfam (v12.0) and Repbase (v22.07) databases to exclude non-miRNA sequences. The remaining reads were mapped to known miRNAs in miRBase (v22.1), whereas novel miRNAs were predicted based on characteristic hairpin structures using RNAfold. Differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) were determined using normalized expression levels with a Student’s t-test (p < 0.05). Putative target genes of DEmiRNAs were predicted using Gstar (v1.0). Putative lncRNAs were identified as transcripts overlapping in predictions by both CNCI (v2.0) and CPC (v0.9-r2), with a length of ≥ 200 bp and at least one exon. Expression levels of lncRNAs were quantified by FPKM, and differential expression (DElncRNAs) was assessed with DESeq2 (v1.22.2). Cis-regulatory target mRNAs were predicted as protein-coding genes within 10 kb upstream or downstream of each lncRNA, whereas trans-target genes were inferred based on expression correlation between lncRNAs and potential target genes [22, 23]. CircRNA identification was performed by first excluding rRNA-aligned reads using Bowtie2, with all unaligned reads retained for subsequent analysis. Anchor sequences, comprising 20-bp segments from both ends of each unmapped read, were aligned to the reference genome using both CIRCExplorer2 (v2.2.6) and CIRI (v2.0.2) under default parameters [24]. CircRNA expression levels were quantified using the metric of circular junction reads normalized by total mapped reads (per billion) and read length. Differential expression of circRNAs (DEcircRNAs) was identified using the EdgeR package (v3.22.5).

Construction of mRNA, ceRNA, and metabolite regulatory networks

Interactions between metabolomic and transcriptomic profiles were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients (PCCs), with significant associations defined as |PCC|> 0.9 and p < 0.05. Integrated gene-metabolite networks were constructed and visualized with Cytoscape (v3.7.1) [25]. Putative regulatory pairs involving miRNA-lncRNA, miRNA-mRNA, and miRNA-circRNA were predicted using Target Finder and Ssearch36 (v36.3.6), and the resulting interaction networks were also visualized using Cytoscape.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statgraphics Centurion XIX (STN, St. Louis, MO, USA). Prior to analysis, data were assessed for normality and homogeneity of variances. Significant differences among groups were determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test at a significance level of p < 0.05. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All figures were generated using Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Among these, three DElncRNAs overlapped between the three comparisons (Fig. 4G). Across the analyzed samples, 1349 circRNAs were detected, among which 10.8, 84.7, and 4.5% were intergenic, exonic, and intronic, respectively (Fig. 4H). Most of these circRNAs were either > 1000 or ≤ 300 bp (Fig. 4I). The highest number of circRNAs was found on chromosome 2 (chr10), followed by chr5 and chr11 (Fig. 4J). KEGG analysis of DEGs

A total of 102 pathways were involved in T1 vs CK, belonging to six first-level KEGG categories, and the number of upregulated genes was considerably larger than that of downregulated genes annotated to the KEGG pathway. Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis had highest number of DEGs, whereas vitamin B6 metabolism, ABC transporters, flavonoid biosynthesis, fatty acid elongation, and cutin, suberine and wax biosynthesis were significantly enriched in the T1 vs CK comparison (Fig. 5A). In the T2 vs CK comparison, 49 pathways were involved belonging to five first-level KEGG categories, and the number of upregulated genes was considerably larger than that of the downregulated genes annotated to the KEGG pathway. Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, linoleic acid metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, and fatty acid elongation were significantly enriched in T2 compared with those in CK (Fig. 5B). For T2 vs T1, 89 pathways were enriched, involved in six first-level KEGG categories, and the number of downregulated genes was considerably larger than that of the upregulated genes annotated to the KEGG pathway. Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis had the highest number of DEGs and the most significant gene enrichment, and stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid, gingerol biosynthesis, ABC transporters, and flavonoid biosynthesis were significantly enriched in T2 vs T1 (Fig. 5C). Amino acid, vitamin, and lipid component analyses of apples treated with different types of K fertilizers

The L-tyrosine contents were upregulated by factors of 2.91 and 2.84 in T1 vs CK and T2 vs CK, respectively, and the expression of tyrosine biosynthase (ko00400) genes was upregulated in T2 vs CK and T1 vs CK (Figs. 2B and 6A, and Table S3). Discussion

Effect of DCCs and DEGs on nutritional quality and aroma of apple fruits under different types of potassium fertilizers treatments

Amino acids are important organic molecules in plants that are involved in the synthesis and metabolism of many substances. Shen et al. [26] demonstrated that foliar potassium fertilization influences tyrosine concentration in pear fruits, with KNO3 treatments decreasing and KH2PO4 treatments increasing the tyrosine concentration. In our study, both K2SO4 and KH2PO4 treatments increased the tyrosine content in apple fruits, and the upregulated expression of genes associated with tyrosine biosynthesis (ko00400) may have contributed to elevated L-tyrosine levels. Vitamins and their derivatives are important for improving fruit quality; apples are a good source of vitamins C, B2, B3, B5, and B6 [27]. Applying potassium fertilizer can increase vitamin C content in tomato fruits [28], however, in our study, applying K fertilizer also had no significant effect. This might be caused by different potassium concentrations and different types of fruits. Acid phosphatase involved in vitamin B2 synthesis was significantly upregulated in the K2SO4 and KH2PO4 treatments, whereas it was downregulated in the KH2PO4 treatment. This may explain why K2SO4 fertilizer increases the content of vitamin B2 content and why the content was higher under the K2SO4 treatment than that under the KH2PO4 treatment.

Fatty acids are essential cell membrane components, constituting a selectively permeable barrier that represents an accessible fluid medium for lipophilic molecule complexes and a matrix for enzymes that catalyze different metabolic reactions and affect changes in lipids in fruit flesh [29]. Previous studies have indicated LOXs catalyze the dioxygenation of polyunsaturated fatty acids, and the resulting hydroperoxides are further metabolized into biologically active oxylipins, including C6-aldehydes and C6-alcohols [30]. LOXs also affect apple fruit aroma by acting on fatty acids [31]. In our study, the expression level of linoleate 9S-lipoxygenase and lipoxygenase in the K2SO4 treatment was higher than that in the KH2PO4 treatment, potentially participating in the oxygenation of polyunsaturated fatty acids into unsaturated oxylipins with linoleic acid as a substrate, thereby affecting the aroma of apples under different types of K fertilizer treatment. Aroma is one of the most important indicators of apple quality. K fertilizer improved the apple fruit aroma at maturity, and K2SO4 was better than KH2PO4 in this regard, which is consistent with previous research showing improved volatile composition in grapes following foliar fertilization with K [32]. ADH genes are involved in the formation of alcohols and esters by participating in the lipoxygenase pathway, which affects the aroma of apples [33]. The activity of ADH in the treatment with high-K fertilizer was significantly higher than that in the control, which may have promoted the aroma metabolism of bananas [34]. Consistently, in this study, we found that K fertilizer treatment increased the expression of ADH genes by regulating the LOX pathway, which may enhance the content of volatile metabolites, such as esters, aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones, improving apple aroma. The expression of ADH genes was downregulated in the KH2PO4 treatment compared with that in the K2SO4 treatment, which may explain the differences in volatile metabolites, such as esters, aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones, under the different treatments. Terpenes are the major aroma compounds responsible for floral flavors [35, 36], and TPS, DXR, MVK, and FPPS influence the aroma of apples by participating in the synthesis of terpenoid volatile compounds [37]. In our study, TPS, DXR, MECPS, and FPPS influenced the aroma of apples treated with K2SO4 by participating in the synthesis of terpenoid volatile compounds, which is partially consistent with the results of previous studies. CCR genes play important roles in the production and accumulation of aromatic compounds in grapes [38], and CCR was involved in changes in the aroma and quality of strawberry fruits under the application of high-K fertilizers [39]. In our study, the expression of CCR was upregulated at maturity in the K2SO4-fertilized apples, suggesting its potential role in promoting the production and accumulation of aromatic compounds through the shikimate pathway.

The effect of changes in ncRNA on the nutritional quality and aroma of apple fruits under different types of K fertilizer treatments

Similar competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks have been identified as essential regulators of many physiological and pathological processes. For example, miRNAs can suppress the translation of complementary target mRNAs, while ceRNAs can competitively bind to these miRNAs to abrogate their function. Classes of ceRNAs include certain mRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs [40]. In our study, the number of lncRNAs was larger than that of circRNAs in ceRNAs, indicating that lncRNAs are another important component of ceRNAs; similar results have also been observed in maize development [41]. miRNAs, namely, the most important components of ceRNAs, play an important role in many physiological and pathological processes. In our study, CCR in the shikimate pathway and CYP450 in the MEP pathway were targeted by mdmmiR159a, indicating that miR159a is involved in apple aroma synthesis. In contrast, the targets of miR159a were lipoxygenases related to the LOX pathway in pears, which affect aroma formation [42], but the target genes were not different. This result may have been caused by the different species. Currently, there is little information regarding the function of miR535b, although previous studies have shown that miR535b is mainly involved in dormancy progression in sweet cherry floral buds [43] and in our study lipid metabolism. Moreover, there is little information regarding the function of PC-3p-45634_116, for which the target gene was acid phosphatase, which may be involved in vitamin B2 synthesis based on our results.

Conclusion

Spraying with K fertilizer can improve the nutritional metabolism and aroma of apple fruits at maturity, with the K2SO4 treatment yielding better results than the KH2PO4 treatment. Both treatments increased the tyrosine content of apple fruits, which did not differ between the treatments. K fertilizer increased the vitamin B2 content of apples, with K2SO4 showing a stronger effect than KH2PO4. The nicotinamide content increased only after K2SO4 treatment. K2SO4 treatment increased the terpene, ester, aldehyde, alcohol, and ketone contents, whereas the KH2PO4 treatment increased the terpene, ester, and aldehyde contents. The expression of eight genes related to nicotinamide synthase was upregulated following K2SO4 treatment. K fertilizer treatment increased the expression of ADH genes by regulating the LOX pathway, which affected the content of volatile metabolites. K2SO4 also upregulated the expression of AAAT, CCR, MECPS, FPPS, TPS, and DXR genes by regulating the shikimate and MEP pathways, which affected the content of volatile metabolites. Overall, 24 miRNAs, 28 circRNAs, and 147 lncRNAs were involved in nutritional metabolism and aroma in K2SO4 and KH2PO4 treatments. Our results provide a valuable reference for the application of K fertilizers during apple production.
