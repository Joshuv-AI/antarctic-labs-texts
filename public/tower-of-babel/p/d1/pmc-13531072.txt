Transcatheter Mitral Valve-in-Valve Replacement With Microaxial Flow-Pump Support for Bioprosthetic Valve Failure
Authors: Daniel Schwarzkopf, Hristian Hinkov, Anika Nagel, Chong Bin Lee, Christoph Klein, Marian Kukucka, Evgenij Potapov, Axel Unbehaun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStructural deterioration of bioprosthetic mitral valves is a common cause of late valve failure, requiring surgical or transcatheter replacement of the failed prosthesis. In rare cases, hemodynamic compromise may lead to cardiogenic shock, requiring mechanical circulatory support and making treatment particularly challenging.Case SummaryA 76-year-old man with prior bioprosthetic mitral valve replacement presented with cardiogenic shock. A microaxial flow pump was implanted. Echocardiography revealed severe transvalvular mitral regurgitation due to leaflet tear. Because of prohibitive surgical risk, the heart team opted for transcatheter mitral valve-in-valve replacement. The procedure resulted in immediate hemodynamic stabilization and weaning from mechanical support within 24 hours.DiscussionThis case demonstrates the feasibility of transcatheter mitral valve replacement combined with microaxial flow-pump support in cardiogenic shock as a potential rescue therapy in selected patients.Take-Home MessageTimely transcatheter intervention may be life-saving in cardiogenic shock due to bioprosthetic mitral valve failure.

History of Presentation

A 76-year-old man presented to the emergency department with acute worsening dyspnea and angina pectoris. Functional capacity was severely reduced, corresponding to NYHA functional class IV.

After admission, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit because of acute respiratory insufficiency. Intravenous diuretic therapy and noninvasive ventilation resulted in transient clinical improvement.

On hospital day 2, the patient developed progressive hemodynamic deterioration requiring escalating doses of vasopressors, with a peak norepinephrine dose of 0.31 μg/kg/min. This was accompanied by worsening respiratory failure and the development of lactic acidosis (pH: 7.2 and serum lactate: 42 mg/dL).

Consequently, an Impella 5.5 device (Abiomed) was implanted via a surgical right axillary approach. The device, capable of providing up to 5.5 L/min of cardiac output, was operated at a stable flow of 3.1 L/min, resulting in rapid hemodynamic stabilization and a marked reduction in vasoactive support.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of arterial hypertension. Ten years earlier, a 31-mm Epic bioprosthetic mitral valve (Abbott Laboratories) had been implanted for severe mitral stenosis. The medical history was notable for treated pulmonary tuberculosis diagnosed 30 years ago.

Differential Diagnosis

Prosthetic mitral valve endocarditis was considered as the primary differential diagnosis but was ruled out by the absence of clinical signs of infection, repeatedly negative inflammatory markers (procalcitonin: <0.05 μg/L, C-reactive protein: <5 mg/L), and echocardiographic findings consistent with a localized rupture of the prosthetic leaflet and absence of vegetations.

Investigations

Chest radiography on admission demonstrated marked pulmonary edema (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe transvalvular mitral regurgitation. The left ventricle was hyperdynamic, with tachycardia (heart rate: 130 beats/min) and reduced stroke volume, as reflected by a velocity-time integral of 10.4 cm. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed degeneration of the bioprosthetic mitral valve prosthesis with prolapse of the anterior prosthetic leaflet and severe mitral regurgitation (Figure 2, Figure 3). No valvular vegetations or further infective endocarditis-related findings were observed. Management

The patient was discussed by the institutional heart team to determine the optimal treatment strategy. Given advanced age, critical clinical condition, and prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE II: 11.9%; Society of Thoracic Surgeons predicted risk of mortality/morbidity: 35.1%), redo surgical mitral valve replacement was deemed unsuitable. Consequently, the heart team decided to proceed with transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR) valve-in-valve procedure.

A 4-dimensional computed tomography (4D-CT) scan was performed for further procedural planning. The internal diameters of the bioprosthesis were measured (mean diameter of 26.4 mm, an area of 551.7 mm2, and a perimeter of 83.4 mm). A transseptal approach was considered feasible. The predicted neo–left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) area was 383 mm2 (Figure 5). The risk of periprocedural interference with the microaxial flow-pump (mAFP) was considered low.Figure 5Preprocedural 4-Dimensional, Full–Cardiac Cycle Computed Tomography(A) Measurements of the degenerated valvular area. As soon as hemodynamic stability was achieved, TMVR was performed on day 3 after admission. The right femoral vein was accessed percutaneously, and transseptal puncture was performed under TEE guidance at a height of 15 mm above the mitral annular plane. After crossing the mitral prosthesis, 2 super-stiff guidewires were positioned in the left ventricle to facilitate septostomy and enhance delivery system support. Balloon atrial septostomy was performed using a standard 14 × 40 mm balloon. A 29-mm balloon-expandable transcatheter heart valve (THV) prosthesis (SAPIEN 3 mounted on the Commander catheter system; Edwards Lifesciences) was positioned within the degenerated surgical mitral valve and deployed under ongoing mAFP support and rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats/min using slow, controlled inflation. The aim was to position the THV precisely deep to the left ventricle, but inside the ring of the mitral valve prosthesis. TEE demonstrated no residual mitral regurgitation and a mean gradient of 2 mm Hg. No relevant LVOT acceleration was observed. A trace iatrogenic atrial septal defect was noted. Fluoroscopy time was 14 minutes, with a dose area product of 4,405 cGy·cm2 (Video 2).

The patient was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit and was extubated on the same day. No neurological events occurred. Inotropic and vasoactive support was discontinued within 12 hours. Given sustained hemodynamic stability, the mAFP was explanted at the bedside on postprocedural day 1, and the patient was transferred to the regular ward later that day.

Given the need for comprehensive dynamic postprocedural assessment, a 4D-CT and transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated the transcatheter mitral valve prosthesis without transvalvular or paravalvular regurgitation, a mean gradient of 5.4 mm Hg, and an LVOT area of 370.8 mm2 (Figure 7), with complete stent expansion and no evidence of leaflet pinwheeling.Figure 7Postprocedural Computed Tomography Scan(A) Measurements after TMVR. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged home on postprocedural day 15 with markedly improved functional capacity, corresponding to NYHA functional class I, following initiation of vitamin K antagonist therapy.

At the 3-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable, without interim hospitalizations. Discussion

Mitral valve prosthesis failure due to structural valve deterioration accounts for 49% of biological prosthesis failures.1 Valve deterioration requires reintervention in up to 30% of patients at 10 years and over 50% at 15 years.2,3 Deterioration is more common in the mitral than in the aortic position.4

Mitral regurgitation affects 0.6% of the global population.5 In an aging population, challenging presentations of biological prosthesis failure, including rare cases of acute cardiogenic shock, are expected to become more frequent.

Emerging evidence suggests that TMVR can be performed under mAFP support, although data, particularly in the setting of profound cardiogenic shock, remain limited. A recent report demonstrated TMVR valve-in-ring under mAFP support in a high-risk patient with severe mitral regurgitation after failed mitral repair.6 During that procedure, the mAFP flow was temporarily interrupted during valve deployment, and device weaning was achieved after several days.6

The present case adds to the literature demonstrating the feasibility of antegrade TMVR in acute non-native mitral valve pathology, reporting a valve-in-valve implantation performed under continuous mAFP support, and it highlights the use of microaxial flow-pump as a bridge to urgent transseptal intervention in profound cardiogenic shock. Timely transcatheter treatment of structural cardiogenic shock caused by mitral valve degeneration appears crucial, as successful TMVR directly enabled weaning from mAFP support and resolution of cardiogenic shock within 24 hours.

The presence of mAFP support may introduce technical considerations during TMVR. In this case, no relevant interaction with the delivery system was observed, allowing continuous support, while rapid pacing, apnea, slow inflation, and TEE-guided monitoring of left ventricular cavity size were key to procedural success.

Previous reports have described mAFP support as a bridge to urgent mitral valve surgery in severe mitral regurgitation.7 Available data remain limited to retrospective series of transapical mitral valve implantation as valve-in-valve or valve-in-ring procedures in patients with cardiogenic shock, without the use of mAFP support.8

Current guidelines provide criteria to distinguish structural from nonstructural bioprosthetic valve deterioration and endorse TMVR valve-in-valve as an alternative to redo surgery in selected patients with failed mitral bioprostheses.9 However, long-term durability of TMVR appears limited, with freedom from death or reintervention decreasing to 20% at 8 years.10 Outcomes are significantly worse in mitral valve-in-ring and valve-in-mitral annular calcification procedures, suggesting that TMVR may currently be better suited as a bridge strategy rather than a definitive solution.10

Careful exclusion of LVOT obstruction remains mandatory, underscoring the importance of patient selection based on detailed anatomical assessment apart from the medical rationale for treatment modality selection. Surgical redo procedures remain a valid option for carefully selected patients with acceptable surgical risk, mitral valve prosthesis endocarditis, or anatomy unfavorable for transcatheter intervention, such as a high risk of LVOT obstruction or unsuitable annuloplasty rings.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates the procedural feasibility of transcatheter mitral valve-in-valve replacement in bioprosthetic valve failure under microaxial flow-pump support and may represent a feasible definitive treatment strategy for selected high-risk patients with cardiogenic shock.
