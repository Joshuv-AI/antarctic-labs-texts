Ion-triggered reconfigurable hydrogels with salt-enhanced mechanical and swelling properties via network topological adaptation
Authors: Lingling Ren, Guoxuan Ma, Zheng Wang, Shuang Liu, Haoran Cheng, David A. Weitz, Liyuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrogels typically deteriorate under high-salinity conditions because electrostatic screening and reduced polymer-solvent affinity suppress swelling and weaken load-bearing network connectivity. Here, we report a double-network hydrogel that strengthens while swelling in brine. Compared with its behavior in water, the network undergoes an ion-triggered topological reconfiguration upon salt exposure. The intrachain zwitterionic ion pairs open and reform as inter-network SBVI+-AMPS− bridges, increasing effective bridge density at lower polymer fraction. The hydrogel exhibits 1.63-fold tensile strength and 1.21-fold swelling ratio in 200 g/L NaCl compared to deionized water. SAXS and XPS confirm salt-induced structural homogenization and charge redistribution. Density functional theory calculations support strengthened ionic association under saline conditions between SBVI and AMPS. Free energy analysis reveals that reduced loop fraction and increased connectivity enable associative stabilization to compensate for elastic swelling penalties. Core-flooding demonstrates robust injectability in high-salinity porous rock. This mechanism provides design rules for salt-adaptive hydrogels.

Introduction

Hydrogels are water-swollen polymer networks with tunable hydration, transport, and mechanical properties, making them attractive for applications ranging from water treatment1,2 and enhanced oil recovery (EOR)3,4 to implantable biomedical devices5,6. Yet under high-ionic-strength conditions, conventional hydrogels commonly undergo dehydration, loss of swelling capacity, and mechanical deterioration7–9. This instability arises primarily from ion-mediated screening of charged segments and the concomitant reduction in effective polymer-solvent affinity10,11, which disrupts hydration, alters chain conformation and mesh structure, and diminishes load-bearing network connectivity12,13.

From a thermodynamic perspective, this behavior is classically described by Flory-Rehner theory, in which equilibrium swelling is governed by the balance between mixing and elastic free energies14,15. In brine, hydrated ions weaken polymer-solvent interactions and suppress electrostatic contributions to swelling16,17, often driving excessive shrinkage, structural collapse, or uncontrolled deformation, particularly in networks with high ionic content or limited cohesive stabilization18. Substantial efforts have been devoted to improving hydrogel performance in saline environments. Increasing the density of ionic groups can enhance hydration under moderate salinity19–21; however, at higher salt concentrations, this strategy may intensify ion-water competition and accelerate charge screening. Zwitterionic polymers provide an important alternative because their strong hydration and internal charge neutrality can produce anti-polyelectrolyte behavior22,23. However, many zwitterionic hydrogels still show either mechanical softening or collapse of dynamic ionic associations in salt-rich media24, so that swelling and mechanical robustness cannot be improved simultaneously. Nonionic networks are less directly affected by electrostatic screening, but they often remain limited by reduced solvent quality, phase separation, or insufficient adaptability under concentrated brine25,26. More broadly, although salt-responsive and ion-enhanced behaviors have been reported in charged, zwitterionic, and nonionic polymer systems, current studies have focused on preserving structural integrity or improving a single property in saline environments27–29. By contrast, hydrogels that become both more swollen and mechanically stronger in concentrated brine remain comparatively rare, and the molecular basis of this co-enhancement has not been quantitatively resolved. This problem is fundamentally challenging because swelling and stiffness are ordinarily coupled in opposite directions. Rubber elasticity relates mechanical stiffness to the density of load-bearing network connections, whereas swelling lowers the polymer volume fraction and generally dilutes effective connectivity. Accordingly, conventional theory predicts that stronger swelling should be accompanied by weaker mechanics unless the network reorganizes to generate new mechanically effective junctions during swelling. The central question of this work is therefore not how to maintain hydrogel properties in brine relative to water, but how to design a saline-responsive network that undergoes structural adaptation such that swelling is coupled to an increase in load-bearing connectivity rather than to its loss.

Here, we report a double-network hydrogel, denoted DAMPS/AM-SBVI, that exhibits simultaneous enhancement of swelling and mechanical performance under high-salinity conditions compared to that in water. The first network is poly(2-acrylamido-2-methylpropane sulfonic acid) (poly(AMPS)), while the second network is composed of acrylamide (AM) and zwitterionic monomer, sulfobetaine vinylimidazole (SBVI). We show that salt exposure induces a topological reconfiguration of the swollen network, in which zwitterionic intrachain ion-pair loops are disrupted and reorganized into reversible load-bearing bridges between the two networks. To quantify this process, we introduce three experimentally accessible descriptors: the effective bridge density (νe), the effective Flory-Huggins parameter (χ), and the absolute loop fraction (floop). We further resolve the free energy into mixing (Fmix), elastic (Fel), associative (Fassoc), and ionic (Fion) contributions to define the energetic basis of the saline response. Small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and density functional theory (DFT) analyses reveal the associated structural rearrangement and charge redistribution. Moreover, core-flooding experiments demonstrate the utility of this hydrogel in high-salinity porous media. Together, these results establish a quantitative framework for understanding how ion-triggered topological adaptation converts swelling into mechanically effective connectivity and provide a design principle for hydrogels required to operate in harsh saline environments.

Results and discussion

Design and synthesis of DAMPS/AM-SBVI

Double-network (DN) hydrogels are widely recognized for their strong mechanical properties, typically constructed by integrating two interpenetrating polymer networks with highly asymmetric structures: a tightly cross-linked, rigid, and brittle first network, and a loosely cross-linked, soft, and ductile second network30. However, conventional DN designs often suffer from limited mechanical enhancement after swelling, especially in saline water, as weak physical interactions between the two networks hinder effective stress transfer31,32. To overcome this challenge, we develop a DN hydrogel system that harnesses inter-network ionic interactions to enhance mechanical performance under high-salinity conditions. We choose AMPS crosslinked by Poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (Mw=700, PEGDA), denoted as poly(AMPS), as the first network due to its high osmotic pressure33, which facilitates efficient uptake of the second network monomer solution. The second network prepolymer comprises neutral monomer AM and a zwitterionic monomer SBVI, crosslinked using PEGDA (Fig. 1A). The nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) characterization of SBVI is shown in Fig. S1. We choose SBVI (a copolymer of propanesultone and vinyl imidazole, i.e., sulfobetaine vinylimidazole) as the zwitterionic monomer, which is a sulfobetaine monomer containing an imidazolium cation structure. This design combines the advantages of sulfobetaine’s inherent anti-polyelectrolyte effect with the enhanced physical interactions provided by the imidazolium cation34. This is validated by experimental comparisons with SBMA, as shown in Fig. S2. DAMPS-SBVI hydrogel appears opaque in water due to strong inter-chain associations, whereas the DAMPS-SBMA hydrogel becomes transparent after swelling, indicating weaker self-association. Additionally, DAMPS-SBVI exhibits strong mechanical properties, with both storage modulus and loss modulus higher than those of DAMPS-SBMA (Fig. S3).

The chemical structure of each hydrogel formulation is confirmed via Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 1B). The presence of sulfonate groups is validated by symmetric and asymmetric S = O stretching bands at 1225 cm−1and 1038 cm−1, respectively35. The characteristic C-N stretching at 1396 cm−1 and S-O bending at 623 cm−1 further verify the AMPS backbone. Notably, the appearance of a C = N stretching vibration peak at 1452 cm−1 corresponds to the imidazole ring in SBVI, confirming its successful incorporation. Compared with SAMPS and SAM-SBVI, DAMPS/AM-SBVI exhibit redshifted S = O bands, indicating reduced electron density caused by ionic complexation between sulfonate anions and quaternary ammonium cations. These spectroscopic signatures provide strong evidence of successful structural integration and inter-ionic interactions.

To systematically enhance both mechanical strength and swelling performance, we optimize the synthesis parameters of the DN system (Table S1 and S2). The monomer concentration of AMPS is chosen with rationale, as the main function of the first network is bearing toughness for the whole hydrogel36,37. We mainly tune the content of the second network to investigate its interaction with the first network for investigating ion density with its hydrogel mechanics. We first investigate the total monomer mole ratio between the first and second networks by varying AM-SBVI concentration. Hydrogels are fabricated using dumbbell-shaped molds to ensure geometric uniformity. After immersing in the AM-SBVI precursor solution and crosslinking, we measure their mechanical properties using a tensimeter. The mechanical properties reach a maximum at an molar ratio of 1:40, yielding a tensile stress of 1.17 MPa and strain of 208% as shown in Fig. 1C. The plateau observed in Fig. 1C is an intrinsic deformation feature of the double-network hydrogel rather than an experimental artifact such as grip slippage38,39. Control experiment shows no detectable relative motion between the specimen and the grips during tensile testing (Fig. S4), while supplementary composition-dependent measurements reveal that the plateau appears only in samples with sufficiently high second-network content (Fig. S5). Combined with direct observation of the stretching process, this behavior is attributed to the onset of damage in the brittle first network, followed by stable load-bearing, necking, and progressive propagation sustained by the loosely crosslinked and highly entangled second network (Fig. S6, Fig. S7, and Supplementary Movie). We then explore the role of cross-linker concentration in the second network, Fig. 1D. Decreasing PEGDA content below 6.1 × 10−4 M achieves mechanical performance with stress and strain exceeding 0.86 MPa and 157%, respectively, indicating a more physically entangled network. When the crosslinker concentration is raised to 1.2 × 10−3 M, DAMPS/AM-SBVI becomes overly rigid and brittle, and both parameters fall sharply below 0.33 MPa and 89%. By fine-tuning the molar content between 0.03 M and 0.09 M, we find that increasing monomer concentration has little effect on tensile stress, but enhances strain, likely due to longer chain flexibility and higher entanglement, as shown in Fig. 1E. Furthermore, we tune the SBVI: AM ratio in the second network formulation. The maximum toughness, both in water and in brine, occurs at a 28:50 ratio, which is used for subsequent experiments, as indicated in Fig. S8. These results underscore the importance of maintaining an asymmetric and deformable secondary network for energy dissipation.

To evaluate the salt-resistant benefits arising from the synergistic effect of the double-network design, we fabricate single-network AMPS hydrogel (SAMPS), single-network AM and SBVI hydrogel SAM-SBVI, and double-network hydrogel DAMPS/AM-SBVI, and systematically characterize their morphological changes, swelling behavior, and mechanical properties. As shown in Fig. S9, SAMPS remain fully transparent in both water and saline. SAM-SBVI show slight opacity in water but became transparent in saline, exhibiting partial anti-polyelectrolyte behavior. In contrast, DAMPS/AM-SBVI hydrogels exhibit a pronounced macroscopic optical transition, changing from opaque in water to transparent in brine and returning to opacity after transfer back to water, as shown in Fig. S10. This behavior suggests that salt-triggered structural reorganization is significantly architecture-dependent, rather than being a simple superposition of the two isolated single-networks. Swelling ratio measurements in saline solution (100 g/L NaCl) reveal that SAMPS exhibit the lowest swelling ratio due to the ionic shielding effect suppressing the osmotic pressure of the polyelectrolyte network (Fig. 1F). Benefiting from the anti-polyelectrolyte effect of SBVI, SAM-SBVI shows an increased swelling ratio. Notably, DAMPS/AM-SBVI display the highest swelling ratio, highlighting the advantage of the double-network structure for enhancing swelling performance. Rheological characterization further confirms this trend, with DAMPS/AM-SBVI exhibiting the highest storage modulus (G′ ) and loss modulus (G′′ ) compared to SAMPS and SAM-SBVI (Fig. 1G). In summary, these results confirm that the cooperative interactions between the brittle AMPS and flexible AM-SBVI networks result in exceptional mechanical performance, which supports the central mechanism of the present work.

Swelling and mechanical properties

In high-salinity environments, hydrogels face significant challenges due to the shielding of electrostatic repulsion by salt ions, which typically leads to reduced swelling and deteriorated mechanical properties40,41. However, the DAMPS/AM-SBVI hydrogel, with its double-network design incorporating zwitterionic monomer SBVI, offers an intriguing solution42,43. We investigate the swelling behavior of the hydrogels in deionized water and various saline solutions. The swelling ratio (Sr) is employed for the quantitative characterization of swelling property. Sr reflects the volumetric changes of the hydrogels, and the experimental results under different solvents are presented in Fig. 2A. The results demonstrate that during the initial 131 min, the hydrogel exhibits a faster swelling rate in water, with Sr values exceeding those in saline solutions. This swelling is mainly driven by the osmotic pressure from the AMPS network, which is being highly charged, facilitating water absorption. Specifically, as illustrated in Fig. S11, the Sr of the single-network SAMPS is 2.43 times that of SAM-SBVI in water, confirming the contribution of AMPS to swelling performance in aqueous environments. From 131 to 300 min, the Sr in water gradually stabilizes, reaching equilibrium at ~17.84, while the swelling rate in saline solutions increases and eventually surpasses that in water. After 300 min, all saline Sr values exceed those in water, gradually stabilizing toward equilibrium by 400 min. Generally, salt ions can shield the electrostatic interactions between polymer chains, typically resulting in reduced swelling performance in saline solutions, with higher salt concentrations leading to more pronounced swelling suppression. Consequently, conventional hydrogels usually exhibit varying degrees of swelling reduction in saline environments compared to pure water. Remarkably, however, the Sr values of our hydrogels DAMPS/AM-SBVI in water, 20 g/L NaCl, 100 g/L NaCl, 200 g/L NaCl, and formation brine (Table S3) are 17.81, 19.13, 19.53, 21.24, and 19.42, respectively. DAMPS/AM-SBVI demonstrate high swelling in saline solutions compared to pure water, achieving an anomalous salt-enhanced swelling property. To further substantiate this anomalous salt-enhanced swelling behavior, the water content (Wr) of the hydrogels is evaluated under different solvent environments. Wr reflects the water retention capacity of the hydrogels, as shown in Fig. 2B. The trend of Wr is closely consistent with that of Sr. Initially, Wr in water exceeded that in saline solutions, while at later stages of swelling, Wr in saline solutions progressively surpassed that in pure water. Furthermore, our hydrogels DAMPS/AM-SBVI exhibit an abnormal swelling enhancement with increasing salt concentration. The Sr in 200 g/L NaCl exhibits an 11.03% increase compared to that in 20 g/L NaCl. The maximum Wr of 7.87 is observed in 200 g/L NaCl, representing a 19.24% increase compared to pure water. This enhanced swelling in brine suggests that the AM-SBVI network undergoes a conformational transition, where the antipolyelectrolyte effect dominates, allowing the hydrogel to stretch and absorb more water in the presence of salt22,24,44,45. Ion concentration plays a significant role in determining the mechanical properties of hydrogels. Most hydrogels experience a decline in mechanical strength as swelling increases, due to the dilution of polymer chains and the weakening of the crosslinked network48,49. However, DAMPS/AM-SBVI demonstrates an exceptional ability to enhance mechanical stability in saline environments. After swelling (Fig. 2C), the hydrogels exhibit stress/strain values of 0.19 MPa/87.76% in water, 0.31 MPa/122.30% in 20 g/L NaCl, 0.31 MPa/100.20% in 100 g/L NaCl, 0.31 MPa/78.91% in 200 g/L NaCl, and 0.31 MPa/91.44% in formation brine. Notably, the DAMPS/AM-SBVI hydrogels show 63.16% higher stress values across all saline solutions compared to water, with the strain values in saline solutions showed a maximum increase of 39.36% compared to water, as shown in Fig. 2D and 2E. The toughness values after swelling in water, 20 g/L NaCl, 100 g/L NaCl, 200 g/L NaCl, and formation brine are 5.98, 26.02, 20.34, 15.19, and 18.59 MJ/m3, respectively. Notably, the toughness of hydrogels swollen in 20 g/L NaCl exhibits a 335.12% increase compared to those in pure water, surpassing previously reported systems with comparable swelling characteristics50–52. As shown in Fig. S12, the Young’s modulus of hydrogels swollen in water, 20 g/L NaCl, 100 g/L NaCl, 200 g/L NaCl, and formation brine are 608.32 Pa, 3321.86 Pa, 5084.69 Pa, 6509.25 Pa, and 6121.68 Pa, respectively. These results show that brine swelling markedly increases the tensile stiffness of the hydrogel relative to water. Rather than simply indicating an increase in permanent cross-linking density, this behavior is more appropriately interpreted as an increase in effective load-bearing connectivity arising from salt-induced ionic association between the AMPS and SBVI networks. Such reversible ionic bridges enable more efficient stress transfer and help maintain mechanical integrity even in the swollen state. Furthermore, the storage modulus (G′) increases from 7197 Pa in water to 17570 Pa in 200 g/L NaCl (Fig. 2F). Even at high salt concentrations, DAMPS/AM-SBVI exhibits G′ > G″ across all frequencies, demonstrating a salt-dependent enhancement in the hydrogel’s elasticity (Fig. S13). Corresponding loss tangent (tan δ) measurements (Fig. 2G) demonstrate systematically higher values for brine-swollen hydrogels, with all samples showing characteristic monotonic decay as frequency increased from 0.1 to 100 Hz. The increased G″ and tan δ values at low frequency suggest additional energy dissipation pathways, possibly arising from reversible inter-network interactions triggered by the salt environment. This elastic-viscous balance reflects the structural rearrangement within the network architecture. To further compare the tensile and shear responses, we also determine the shear modulus from dynamic strain sweep measurements in the linear viscoelastic region (Fig. S14-Fig. S17). The shear modulus of hydrogels swollen in water, 20 g/L NaCl, 100 g/L NaCl, 200 g/L NaCl, and formation brine is 78.12 Pa, 251.47 Pa, 305.53 Pa, 127.48 Pa, and 253.69 Pa, respectively. Although the detailed salt dependence of the shear modulus is not identical to the Young’s modulus, both measurements consistently show that the hydrogel is mechanically stiffer in brine than in water. This difference is attributed to the deformation mode dependent viscoelastic response of the swollen double network hydrogel, since Young’s modulus is obtained from uniaxial tension, whereas shear modulus is derived from small amplitude oscillatory shear. Therefore, these two parameters should be regarded as complementary descriptors of mechanical reinforcement rather than direct equivalent quantities.

Interestingly, when immersed in formation brine, which contains Ca2+ and Mg2+, the swelling and mechanical performance of DAMPS/AM-SBVI are slightly reduced compared to NaCl solutions. This suggests that the ionic composition of the solution, rather than just the ionic strength, plays a crucial role in influencing the hydrogel’s swelling and mechanical stability. The presence of divalent ions, such as Ca2+ and Mg2+, can interact well with the sulfonate group on AMPS or the SBVI group, making the network more compact, limiting the extent of swelling, and thereby reducing the mechanical reinforcement provided by the ionic bridges53. This highlights the critical role of ion specificity in the salt-responsive behavior of the hydrogel, suggesting that the ionic strength alone is not sufficient to explain the hydrogel’s performance in brine. Rather, the ion-specific interactions between the hydrogel and the solution components must be considered when designing hydrogels for use in complex ionic environments.

To contextualize these findings, we compare the swelling behavior and mechanical properties of DAMPS/AM-SBVI hydrogels to those of other materials reported in the literature (Table S4). Comparing strength vs swelling and salt concentration vs swelling in Fig. 2H and 2I, DAMPS/AM-SBVI exhibits high swelling and mechanical strength in brine when compared to other polyelectrolyte-based hydrogels54. For example, hydrogels from other studies typically show much lower swelling ratios, around 2 at 58.5 g/L NaCl due to charge shielding effects13. In contrast, DAMPS/AM-SBVI shows a significant increase in both swelling and mechanical performance in salt solutions, highlighting its salt resistance and robustness. The enhanced mechanical properties of DAMPS/AM-SBVI in saline environments are also reflected in its toughness, which is 4.35-fold greater than that of water-swollen samples (Fig. 2E), and its improved storage modulus (Fig. 2F), confirming its elasticity in brine. The enhanced mechanical performance and swelling behavior in salt solutions can be explained through the salt-induced topological reconfiguration behavior. In pure water, the SBVI chains form intrachain loops due to electrostatic attractions between the zwitterionic groups, limiting their extension. However, when exposed to salt, the ionic screening effect weakens these attractions, causing the SBVI chains to extend and form ionic bridges with the AMPS network. This reconfiguration of the network topology enhances both swelling and mechanical properties, as the extended chains allow for more efficient stress transfer and structural reinforcement. This topological reconfiguration contributes to the improved performance of DAMPS/AM-SBVI hydrogels in saline environments.

Interestingly, contact angle measurements offer direct support for the formation of weak hydrophobic associations. After swelling in distilled water, the hydrogel displays a contact angle of 17.75° (Fig. S18), indicating strong hydrophilicity. However, after brine swelling, the contact angle increases significantly to 59.41° (Fig. S19), suggesting the emergence of hydrophobic microdomains. This is consistent with the loop-opening and bridge-forming transition of zwitterionic SBVI chains under ionic screening, exposing hydrophobic imidazole moieties and facilitating secondary non-covalent associations. These interactions likely act as dynamic junctions, contributing to network stability and salt-enhanced elasticity. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DAMPS/AM-SBVI hydrogels not only retain high swelling capability in brine but also exhibit mechanically adaptive behavior through ion-triggered reconfiguration, ionic complexation, and hydrophobic association, features rarely achieved simultaneously in conventional systems.

Microstructure and ion-triggered topological reconfiguration mechanism

To explore the underlying mechanism of the unusual combination of enhanced swelling and mechanical properties of DAMPS/AM-SBVI hydrogels, we systematically characterize the network morphology and structure of hydrogels swollen in both water and brine. To investigate the changes in transparency of hydrogels swollen in water and brine (Fig. S10), we perform SEM images to characterize the network structure. The water-swollen hydrogel displays a heterogeneous polymer matrix with constricted pores and reduced porosity, typical of phase-separated structures, as shown in Fig. 3A. In contrast, the brine-swollen hydrogel exhibits a more homogeneous structure, characterized by expanded pores and a looser network topology, as shown in Fig. 3B. These structural differences indicate that salt exposure induces pronounced network reorganization. Further confirmation of the structural reorganization in response to salt exposure is provided by small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements (Fig. 3C). The SAXS pattern of brine-swollen hydrogels shows a weak and diffuse scattering halo, indicative of less-ordered structures, while the water-swollen hydrogel exhibits significantly enhanced scattering intensity and a distinct scattering ring. This difference highlights the formation of phase-separated domains in the water-swollen hydrogel, where the peak intensity is directly related to phase contrast55. The high phase contrast within the water-swollen network explains the opaque appearance observed macroscopically, while the brine-swollen hydrogel, which lacks well-defined scattering entities, appears transparent. To further elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the changes in network morphology and structure, we perform Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses. The FTIR spectra (Fig. 3E) reveal that the water-swollen hydrogel exhibits characteristic peaks at ~1175 cm−¹ and ~1036 cm−¹, corresponding to the -S = O stretching vibration of the -SO₃− groups. Upon swelling in brine, these peaks shift to ~1180 cm−1 and ~1040 cm−1, indicating the formation of electrostatic attractions between the -SO3− groups and other groups in the brine-swollen hydrogels31. This suggests that salt ions promote ionic interactions within the network, particularly between the anionic sulfonate groups and cationic quaternary ammonium groups from the SBVI segment. XPS data further support these findings, showing a shift in the S 2p spin-orbit doublet from 168.57 eV and 167.42 eV in water to 168.07 eV and 167.35 eV in brine (Fig. 3F). Similarly, the N 1s peak shifts towards lower binding energy in brine, from 401.58 eV and 399.63 eV in water to 401.36 eV and 399.40 eV in brine. These shifts confirm that electrostatic interactions between the anionic and cationic groups are enhanced in brine, further facilitating the inter-network ionic complex formation. Quantum chemistry calculations are further performed to study the interaction of the imidazolium-sulfonate pairs. The electrostatic potential (ESP) mapping, as shown in Fig. 3G, reveals that the distribution of internal zwitterionic ion-pairs by hydrated salt ions not only reduces intramolecular electrostatic attraction but also exposes highly polarized regions on the polymer chains. These regions serve as secondary hydration traps, promoting additional water coordination and enhancing local water retention. This redistribution of electrostatic potential contributes to the unexpected increase in both swelling capacity and mechanical resilience under saline conditions. DFT simulation results reveal that the electrostatic interaction energy between imidazolium and sulfonate ion pairs in water is −5.38 eV. Notably, this interaction becomes significantly stronger (−12.19 eV) in the presence of hydrated counterions, which mediate ion-bridged micro-solvated clusters, as shown in Fig. 3H. These quantitative data support the antipolyelectrolyte effect-induced formation of robust inter-network ionic associations in AMPS-SBVI under saline conditions, which directly correlates with the observed enhancement in mechanical modulus.

As the AM content in the second network is relatively high, AM-related local interactions should also be considered. FTIR analysis reveals that the C = O stretching band shifts from 1652 to 1646 cm−1 (Fig. S20) after swelling in brine, indicating the carbonyl environment is altered in the presence of salt. To further probe this possibility, we apply DFT calculations to further quantify the interaction between Na+ and carbonyl oxygen of AM (Fig. S21). The calculated binding energy for the O-Na+ complex is −0.02345 eV, supporting the existence of the cation-dipole interactions, but also indicating that this interaction is weak. Therefore, we do not regard the AM-Na+ interaction as the principle of load-bearing interaction in the hydrogel. Instead, it is more appropriately considered as a secondary factor that may assist local chain relaxation and reduce AM rich phase association under brine exposure. Besides, other AM-related weak local associations may also be present, including nonspecific amide-amide dipole association, weak van der Waals contacts, and salt-modulated local aggregation within the AM-containing second network. These effects may influence local morphology and baseline viscoelasticity, but they do not by themselves account for the anomalous co-enhancement observed in saline media.

In high AM content systems, hydrogen bonding is the most probable noncovalent inter- and intramolecular interactions. The strengths of hydrogen bonds vary widely, spanning from weak van der Waals interactions to strong, partially covalent bonds, and even to hydrogen bonds with typical covalent characteristics60. However, in our system, hydrogen bonding is not the primary contributor to mechanical enhancement. Instead, the disruption of hydrogen bonds facilitates the formation of intra-network ionic associations, which have been demonstrated in the Supplementary Information Fig. S22– S25.

The decomposition (Fig. 3L) shows a consistent trade-off: Fel increases as the network connectivity rises, while Fassoc becomes more negative as loops convert into load-bearing bridges. Fmix shifts only slightly with NaCl (small rise in χ), and Fion is much smaller than elastic and associative terms, which can be neglected. Consequently, the principal balance is between the elastic energy cost Fel and the associative stabilization Fassoc. Altogether, salt repositions the energy minimum toward states with higher connectivity and fewer loops, thereby explaining the unusual co-enhancement of swelling and modulus in hypersaline media. The coexistence of great swelling (smaller \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi$$\end{document}ϕ) with high modulus (larger νe) does not arise from a better solvent, but from salt-driven topology control. Screening unlocks SBVI intrachain ion-loops and exposes hydrated sites, which re-link across AMPS to form inter-network, reversible bridges that bear load on mechanical timescales. The network thus homogenizes and increases connectivity with decreased floop and increased νe. In formation brine, divalent competition reduces sulfonate availability (θs < 1), lowering absolute Fassoc and νe value relative to 200 g/L NaCl, while the generated bridges in the system maintain the very large Fel and keep the system near a new minimum state.

Altogether, the co-enhancement of swelling and mechanical strength in brine is governed primarily by salt-induced topological reconfiguration of the double network hydrogel. In brine, salt screening weakens the intrachain zwitterionic ion-pair loops associated with SBVI and exposes additional ionic sites, which subsequently reorganize into inter-network reversible bridges with AMPS. This loop to bridge conversion decreases the fraction of non load bearing loops and increases the effective load-bearing connectivity of the swollen network, as reflected by the decrease in floop and the increase in ve. Meanwhile, hydrogel bonding associated with AM is not the dominant source of mechanical reinforcement, as demonstrated by the urea perturbation experiments. Weak Na+-carbonyl cation-dipole interactions may exist and assist local structural relaxation or morphological homogenization, but they are secondary to the topology-driven network reorganization. Moreover, other AM related weak local associations may also be present in brine, such as nonspecific amide-amide dipole association, weak van der Waals interaction, and salt-modulated local aggregation within the second network. These effects may contribute to local structural adaptation, but they do not dominate the anomalous co-enhancement of swelling and mechanical strength. Therefore, the unusual coexistence of large swelling and high modulus in brine does not arise from a better solvent environment or from AM-centered noncovalent interactions alone, but from salt-driven topology control that shifts the free energy minimum toward a highly connected and mechanically effective network hydrogel state.

Coreflooding plugging and degradation behavior of DAMPS/AM-SBVI hydrogels

Before evaluating the plugging performance of DAMPS/AM-SBVI in coreflooding experiments, we investigate the hydrogel’s degradation behavior under various reservoir-relevant conditions to assess its temporal stability and self-degradability. As shown in Fig. 4A, hydrogels are immersed in different media and incubated at 80°C to simulate high-temperature reservoir conditions. We observe rapid degradation in deionized water, with nearly complete mass loss within 7 days. In contrast, under saline environments, including 20 g/L NaCl, 100 g/L NaCl, 200 g/L NaCl, and actual formation brine, the hydrogel demonstrates significantly slower degradation, with more than 5% of the mass remaining after 60 days. This delayed degradation suggests that salinity inhibits hydrolytic cleavage, thereby enhancing the hydrogel’s lifespan under brine conditions. Considering that reservoir temperatures vary widely, we further examine the degradation behavior in formation brine at 50 °C and 80 °C (Fig. 4B). To probe the structural integrity of the hydrogel throughout degradation, we monitor its viscoelastic properties over time in formation brine. As shown in Fig. 4C and 4D, both the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′) decline progressively over a 30-day period at 80 °C. Within 7 days at 80°C, G′ drops from 8669 Pa to 334 Pa. However, even after 30 days, G′ remains higher than G″ across all frequencies, indicating that the hydrogel retains its solid-like character despite mass loss. These results highlight that the hydrogel network undergoes a gradual softening rather than abrupt failure, ensuring effective plugging throughout its functional lifespan. To further explore the temperature-dependent mechanical resilience of degraded hydrogels, we examine their rheological profiles after 7 days of aging in formation brine at various temperatures. As shown in Fig. 4E and 4F, higher temperatures accelerate mechanical degradation, with G′ values decreasing from 480 Pa at 50 °C to 322 Pa at 80 °C. Nonetheless, G′ remains consistently higher than G″, affirming that the gels retain sufficient integrity to perform short-term plugging prior to complete degradation. Moreover, the hydrogel undergoes visible degradation, transitioning from a swollen, cohesive network to a fragmented or dissolved state (Fig. 4G), a process mirrored in its rheological evolution, from a solid-like response to a more fluid-like behavior, thereby supporting the proposed degradable profile control mechanism.

We next assess the in situ plugging performance through coreflooding experiments using carbonate rock cores with a 1 cm diameter channel as simulated high-permeability fractures (Fig. 4H). Core samples used in the gel flooding tests exhibit a porosity of 29.1% and an initial permeability, K, of 120.6 mD, representative of medium-permeability sandstone formation. During the initial water flooding phase, minimal injection pressure, around ~0.01 MPa, is observed due to the open fracture, resulting in a recovery factor of 28.7% as illustrated in Fig. 4I. Upon gel injection, the pressure steadily increases, peaking at 4.4 MPa after two pore volumes, with the permeability decreasing to 0.962 mD, indicating successful plugging of the high-permeability path and gel propagation within the porous media. This pressure buildup validates successful in situ plugging and supports the salt-triggered hydrogel’s applicability for water shutoff in heterogeneous reservoirs. The second water flooding stage requires a breakthrough pressure of 5.3 MPa, after which the pressure stabilizes at 2.5 MPa. The oil recovery factor improves to 61.1%, reflecting a 32.5% enhancement attributed to fluid diversion toward lower-permeability zones. After degradation, a third round of water flooding is conducted, during which the injection pressure drops back to 0.08 MPa, only marginally above the initial pre-gel baseline with permeability back to 40.2 mD. This substantial decrease confirms that the hydrogel undergoes controlled degradation, leading to the reopening of blocked fractures and minimal long-term permeability damage. Overall, DAMPS/AM-SBVI hydrogels exhibit a rare combination of reconfigurable mechanical strength, salt-resistance, and controlled degradability. The plugging efficiency (Pe) reaches 99.2%, and the core damage ratio (Dr) is limited to 66.7%, confirming that the material provides reliable conformance control while minimizing irreversible formation damage (Table S7). These characteristics make it a promising candidate for temporary profile control in heterogeneous reservoirs, particularly under high-salinity and high-temperature conditions.

In conclusion, we have established a salt-driven adaptive topological reconfiguration as the governing mechanism that reconciles swelling with mechanical strengthening in the DAMPS/AM-SBVI hydrogel. Across water, NaCl brine, and formation brine, the hydrogel evolves toward states with higher effective bridge density and lower loop fraction, indicating progressive conversion of non-load-bearing intrachain loops into mechanically effective reversible bridges. Free-energy analysis shows that this response is governed primarily by the competition between elastic cost, Fel, and associative stabilization, Fassoc, whereas the ionic contribution, Fion, remains comparatively small and the mixing term, Fmix, changes only modestly through χ. This energy landscape shifts the free-energy minimum toward states of higher connectivity and fewer inner loops, explaining the unusual enhancement of modulus and swelling at high salinity. By designing chemistries that accelerate loop-to-bridge conversion and preserve sulfonate availability in mixed brines, while keeping χ changes modest and setting the permanent crosslink density to place Fel in a compensable window, one can steer the three descriptors, ve, floop and χ toward the desired operating point. Together with the coreflooding results, these insights provide rational rules for engineering salt-adaptive hydrogels that remain highly swollen yet mechanically robust in subsurface and other ion-rich environments.

Methods

Materials

Acrylamide (AM, 99%),2-Acrylamide-2-methylpropanesulfonic acid (AMPS, 98%), 1-vinylimidazole (99%), 1,3-propane sultone (99%), acetonitrile (99%), Poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate 700 (PEGDA700), mineral oil (25 cSt), methyl blue, and Sudan III are purchased from Aladdin. α-Ketoglutaric acid (KA, 98%) is obtained from Macklin. All chemicals and reagents are used as received.

Preparation of zwitterionic monomers

We select SBVI (a copolymer of propanesultone and vinyl imidazole, i.e., sulfobetaine vinyl imidazole) as the zwitterionic monomer, which is a sulfobetaine monomer containing an imidazolium cation structure. This design combines the advantages of sulfobetaine’s inherent anti-polyelectrolyte effect with the enhanced physical interactions provided by the imidazolium cation. The zwitterionic monomers (sulfobetaine vinylimidazole, SBVI) are synthesized by quaternization reaction following the modified procedures reported in the literature62. Vinylimidazole (0.2 M) and 1,3-propane sultone (0.25 M) are dissolved in acetonitrile. The mixture is thoroughly stirred, followed by purging with nitrogen gas to remove oxygen from the solution. Then, the flask is sealed and allowed to react at 40 °C for 24 h. After the reaction, the resulting precipitate is collected, washed with alcohol, and dried at 45 °C in the oven.

Preparation of the single-network hydrogels

Taking the single-network AMPS (SAMPS) hydrogel as an example, appropriate amounts of AMPS are dissolved in deionized water, followed by the addition of the crosslinker PEGDA700 and the initiator KA. The mixture is thoroughly mixed to obtain the prepolymer solution of the single-network hydrogels. This prepolymer solution is then injected into a custom-made glass mold and irradiated under a 365 nm ultraviolet lamp for 30 minutes to complete the polymerization reaction. The detailed preparation formula of the single-network hydrogels is shown in Table S1. According to the design principles of DN hydrogels, the first network should maintain as high crosslinking density as possible, but it must also ensure that the first network is not too brittle to proceed with the subsequent network absorption experiment. Other single-network hydrogels (SAM-SBVI and SAM-AMPS-SBVI) are prepared following the same procedure.

Preparation of double-network DAMPS/AM-SBVI hydrogels

The double-network hydrogels are fabricated through a two-step soaking method involving free radical polymerization. In brief, the preparation of the first network follows the same procedure as that of the single-network hydrogels, with the composition listed as No.5 in Table S1. Subsequently, the first network hydrogel is precisely cut into gel pieces of uniform shape and mass using a dumbbell-shaped spline. These pieces are then immersed in a specific amount of the second network prepolymer solution, which consists of monomers AM and SBVI, minimal crosslinker PEGDA700, and initiator KA. The detailed preparation formula for the second network is provided in Table S2. After the hydrogels have fully absorbed the second network prepolymer, they are irradiated under a 365 nm ultraviolet lamp for 40 minutes to obtain the DAMPS/AM-SBVI. The detailed preparation formula of the DN hydrogels is shown in Table S2.

Preparation of DAMPS-SBVI and DAMPS-SBMA

In the DAMPS-SBVI system, the first network AMPS is synthesized and fully swollen in a predetermined amount of SBVI, PEGDA700, and KA solution prior to polymerization. Similarly, in the DAMPS-SBMA system, the first network AMPS is synthesized and fully swollen in a predetermined amount of SBMA, PEGDA700, and KA solution prior to polymerization. After the hydrogels have fully absorbed the second network prepolymer, they are irradiated under a 365 nm ultraviolet lamp for 40 minutes to obtain the DAMPS-SBVI and DAMPS-SBMA.

Chemical and structural characterizations

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) is used to analyze molecular composition, functional groups, and chemical bonding within the hydrogel. FTIR spectra of the dry hydrogel samples are recorded using IR Tracer 100. The measured specimens are prepared by the KBr-disk method, and the spectra in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 are collected with a resolution of 4 cm−1. All the experiments are carried out at ambient temperature. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is used to provide information on the chemical states of the elements. The hydrogel samples are firstly vacuum-dried for the XPS using a Thermo Fisher Escalab Xi + . The analysis chamber is maintained at a vacuum level of 3 × 10−9 mbar, and the excitation source is Al Kα radiation (hv = 1486.68 eV). The operating voltage is 15 kV, with a filament current of 10 mA, and signal accumulation is performed over 6 cycles. The pass energy is set to 50 eV, with a step size of 0.05 eV. Charging correction is applied using the C 1 s binding energy of 284.80 eV as the reference. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is utilized to investigate the surface network structure of the hydrogels. The cross-sectional morphologies of the freeze-dried bulk hydrogel are sputtered with a layer of gold and then observed by ThermoFisher Scientific Apreo 2S + . The accelerating voltage is 5 kV. NMR measurement is performed using deuterated dimethyl sulfoxideas the solvent at 25 °C on a Bruker AVANCE III HD superconducting Fourier transform nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (400 MHz).

Rheological test

Rheological measurements are performed using a HAKKE MARS III to analyze the viscoelastic behavior of the hydrogels. Disc-shaped hydrogel samples are tested at 25 °C using a parallel-plate geometry with a 2 mm gap. The storage modulus (G′ ) and loss modulus (G′′ ) are measured as a function of frequency, ranging from 0.1 to 100 rad/s, at a constant strain amplitude of 1%.

Mechanical property test

Dumbbell-shaped hydrogel specimens are fabricated to assess their tensile properties. A universal testing machine (CTS-E05) is utilized to measure the mechanical strength of the hydrogels. Tensile tests are performed at a constant crosshead speed of 20 mm/min, with the tensile strength and tensile strain recorded at the point of fracture. The modulus (stiffness) is determined as the slope of the stress-strain curve at a strain of 5%, while the toughness is calculated as the integral of the stress-strain curve.

Laboratory core-flooding test

The dried bulk hydrogels are ground into particles of ~50 meshes for the laboratory core-flooding test to evaluate the plugging performance of the hydrogels. The cores were drilled to a depth of 5 cm with a hole diameter of 1 cm, simulating the channels created during water flooding. The procedure begins with the core saturation using formation oil (Sudan III-stained mineral oil) by a vacuum pressure saturation device, followed by the injection of simulated formation brine supporting Table S3 at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min, referred to as the first water flooding stage. The water permeability K1 is calculated by Darcy’s law at this time. Then, hydrogel particles of two pore volumes are injected into the core at a constant flow rate of 0.5 mL/min until the hydrogels flow through the outlet and the injection pressure is stable. Finally, simulated formation brine was injected into the core again at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min until the replacement pressure was stabilized for a period of time. The water permeability K2 is calculated by Darcy’s law at this time. During the displacement process, the pressure changes during the experiment are recorded in real time. Then, the core holders are kept in an oven at 80°C for one month. Density functional theory calculations

The electronic structure DMol3 package of Materials Studio 2023 is utilized to perform all of the Density Functional Theory (DFT) computations. The exchange-correlation energies are calculated using the generalized gradient approximation with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerh of functional. The basis set used is the double numerical plus polarization function. In all calculations, the van der Waals forces are accounted for using the DFT-D within TS’s scheme. Where Et is the total energy of the whole system, E1 is the total energy of component 1, and E2 is the total energy of component 2.

Young’s modulus

The Young’s modulus of the hydrogels swollen under different conditions is calculated from the slope of the linear region (strain range: 10–20%) of the tensile stress-strain curve.

Shear modulus

The shear modulus is determined through a dynamic strain sweep test. A strain sweep is conducted in the range of 0.1% to 10% to evaluate the linear viscoelastic region (LVR). The storage modulus G′ of hydrogels after aging in the different solutions is plotted as a function of strain. The LVR is identified as the strain region where G′ remained essentially constant. Within this linear region, G′ is independent of strain and equals the shear modulus. Therefore, the shear modulus is calculated by averaging all G′ values obtained within the LVR.

It should be noted that the shear modulus and Yound’s modulus are obtained from different deformation testing modes, and therefore should not be directly equated numerically. Young’s modulus reflects the response under uniaxial tension, whereas shear modulus reflects the response under small amplitude oscillatory shear. Accordingly, the two parameters provide complementary information on the mechanical reinforcement of the hydrogel network under brine-swollen conditions.

Degradation kinetics

The hydrogel specimens are cut into uniform shapes and volumes using an 8 mm punch and then incubated in either distilled water or a specific brine solution until they reach full swelling. The G′, G′, and the remained mass fraction (Fr) are used as measures to evaluate the degradation efficiency at each time point.
