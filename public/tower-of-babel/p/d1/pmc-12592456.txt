Red cell distribution width and cerebral white matter hyperintensity
Authors: Ki-Woong Nam, Hyung-Min Kwon, Han-Yeong Jeong, Jin-Ho Park, Kyungha Min
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRed cell distribution width (RDW) is an indicator of red blood cell size heterogeneity and may reflect underlying chronic hypoxemia or inflammation. While RDW has been associated with the prognosis of various pulmonary and cardiovascular diseases, its relevance in cerebrovascular disease remains underexplored. In this study, we evaluated the association between RDW and cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD). We evaluated consecutive health check-up participants who underwent brain MRI between January 2006 and December 2013. As subtypes of cSVD, white matter hyperintensity (WMH) was quantitatively assessed, while lacunes and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) were evaluated qualitatively. RDW was measured from blood samples as part of the complete blood cell count. A total of 3,040 health check-up participants were evaluated (mean age: 56.3 years, male sex: 53.6%, mean body mass index: 24.16 kg/m2). In multivariable linear regression analysis, RDW was significantly associated with WMH volume after adjusting for confounders [β = 1.789, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.115 to 3.463]. Age, systolic blood pressure, white blood cell and red blood cell counts were also associated with WMH volume, independent of RDW. On the other hand, lacunes and CMBs were not significantly associated with RDW after adjusting for confounders. RDW was associated with cSVD in health check-up participants. This association was observed exclusively with the WMH, while no significant relationship was found with lacunes or CMBs. Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD) is a chronic cerebrovascular condition whose prevalence is steadily increasing due to population aging and advancements in brain imaging technology1. Although cSVD can cause clinical symptoms such as cognitive impairment, dysphagia, and gait disturbances, it is often a subclinical pathology without overt manifestations2,3. However, because cSVD increases the risk of both stroke and vascular dementia, early identification and appropriate management before the onset of severe disability are essential4,5. cSVD encompasses several subtypes with distinct morphological features [e.g., white matter hyperintensity (WMH), lacunes, cerebral microbleeds (CMBs)]5,6. Despite these differences, they are frequently observed together, prompting numerous studies aimed at identifying common pathological mechanisms and shared risk factors5,7–9.

Red cell distribution width (RDW), a component of the complete blood cell count, reflects the heterogeneity in circulating red blood cell (RBC) volume10. An elevated RDW indicates increased variability in RBC size, which may result from ineffective erythropoiesis or excessive RBC destruction10. Traditionally, RDW has been used to evaluate the etiology of anemia and various hematological conditions10–13. However, growing evidence suggests that RDW may also serve as a prognostic marker for cardiovascular outcomes14–17. In addition, a few studies have reported associations between RDW and cSVD, particularly WMH18,19. Nevertheless, these studies were limited by small or poorly defined study populations and relatively imprecise measurements of WMH volume.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the association between RDW and cSVD in health check-up participants. Furthermore, we aim to investigate the potential mechanisms underlying the association between RDW and cSVD subtypes by incorporating laboratory results that support the major pathological processes reflected by RDW (e.g., hypoxemia and inflammation) in previous studies20.

Methods

Study population

We included consecutive health check-up participants who underwent brain MRI between January 2006 and December 2013 at the Health Promotion Center of Seoul National University Hospital. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) missing data on blood cell counts; (2) a history of active cancer, hematologic disease, or use of immunosuppressants; (3) presence of severe hepatic (e.g., liver cirrhosis) or renal disease (e.g., end stage renal disease, dialysis); (4) recent major trauma or surgery; or (5) a history of stroke or transient ischemic attack. After applying these exclusion criteria, a total of 3,040 participants were included in the final analysis.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (No. 1502-026-647). Given the retrospective design and use of anonymized, de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines.

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory factors

As part of a comprehensive health check-up program, we conducted a broad assessment encompassing demographic, clinical, and laboratory factors. This included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, ischemic heart disease, current smoking status, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure (BP). Laboratory evaluations were performed after an overnight fast of at least 12 h and included measurements of hemoglobin A1c and total cholesterol levels, white blood cell (WBC) and RBC counts, hemoglobin level, RDW, platelet counts, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) level21. To ensure the accuracy of blood cell counts, samples were immediately collected into calcium EDTA tubes following venipuncture and centrifuged at 2,000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min. Cell counts were then analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer (XE-2100, Sysmex, Kobe, Japan)21. Of the total 3,040 participants, 2,902 underwent pulmonary function testing, from which forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) values were obtained, allowing for the calculation of the FEV1/FVC ratio.

Radiological factors

All participants included in the study underwent brain MRI and MRA using 1.5-T scanners (Signa, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA or Magnetom SONATA, Siemens, Munich, Germany). MRI acquisition parameters included a basic slice thickness of 5 mm, T1-weighted images [repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE): 500/11 ms], T2-weighted images (TR/TE: 5,000/127 ms), T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images (TR/TE: 8,800/127 ms), T2-gradient echo images (TR/TE: 57/20 ms), and three-dimensional time-of-flight MRA images (TR/TE: 24/3.5 ms)22.

As in our previous studies, we evaluated three subtypes of cSVD22. WMH volume was quantitatively assessed using the Medical Imaging Processing, Analysis, and Visualization (MIPAV) software (version 11.0.0, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For this analysis, each participant’s T2-FLAIR images were extracted as DICOM files and uploaded into the software. The boundaries of WMH lesions were delineated, and lesion volumes were calculated using a semi-automated method by summing the lesion areas across all slices22. Lacunes were defined as well-defined, asymptomatic lesions measuring 3–15 mm in diameter, located in the territory of perforating arterioles, and exhibiting signal characteristics similar to cerebrospinal fluid on T1- or T2-weighted images23. CMBs were defined as focal, round lesions less than 10 mm in diameter with low-signal intensity on T2-gradient echo images23. All radiological parameters were rated by two well-trained neurologists (K.-W.N. and H.-Y.J.) who were blinded to the clinical and laboratory data. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third investigator (H.-M.K.).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and as median [interquartile range] for non-normally distributed data. Skewed variables were log-transformed. However, for WMH volume, a large proportion of participants had zero values; therefore, a small constant of 0.001 was uniformly added prior to transformation to allow log conversion while minimizing its influence on the overall values.

To identify potential predictors of cSVD, both univariate and multivariable analyses were conducted. For WMH volume, simple linear regression analysis was performed. Variables with a P-value < 0.05 in the univariate analysis, along with RDW, were included as covariates in the multivariable linear regression model. In cases of suspected multicollinearity, the clinically more relevant variable was selected for inclusion (e.g., systolic vs. diastolic BP). For lacunes and CMBs, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess associations with continuous variables in the univariate analysis. Subsequently, variables with a P-value < 0.05 were considered potential confounders and were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model along with RDW.

Given that RDW may be unfamiliar to many neurologists, we also explored its associations with a range of demographic, clinical, and laboratory factors. To illustrate this, we stratified participants into tertile groups according to RDW values and compared their baseline characteristics. The Kruskal–Wallis test and linear-by-linear association analysis were used for continuous variables, whereas the chi-squared test was applied for categorical variables. In addition, to visually illustrate RDW’s relationship with hypoxemia and inflammation—key pathological mechanisms potentially linking RDW to cSVD—we generated cubic spline curves depicting the associations between RDW and pulmonary function test parameters, as well as inflammatory markers.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 3,040 health check-up participants were included in the analysis (mean age: 56.3 ± 9.4 years; male sex: 53.6%; mean BMI: 24.16 ± 3.09). The mean WMH volume was 2.65 ± 6.40 mL, and lacunes and CMBs were identified in 222 participants (7.3%) and 124 participants (4.1%), respectively. The median value of RDW was 13.0% [12.6–13.4]. In the comparison of baseline characteristics across RDW tertiles, RDW showed a positive linear association with age, hypertension, diabetes, current smoking, hemoglobin A1c level, WBC and platelet counts, hs-CRP level, WMH volume, and the presence of lacunes and CMBs. In contrast, RDW were negatively associated with hemoglobin level, FEV1, FVC, and the FEV1/FVC ratio (Table 1). Summarizing these findings, RDW demonstrated a negative correlation with pulmonary function test results (Supplementary Figure I) and a positive correlation with inflammatory markers (Supplementary Figure II).

In our data, WMH volume was associated with age, hypertension, diabetes, current smoking, systolic and diastolic BP, hemoglobin A1c and fasting glucose levels, WBC and RBC counts, hemoglobin level, and RDW. In the subsequent multivariable linear regression analysis, RDW remained significantly associated with WMH volume after adjusting for confounders [β = 3.134, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.350 to 4.918]. Age (β = 0.106, 95% CI: 0.094 to 0.117), systolic BP (β = 0.018, 95% CI: 0.011 to 0.025), WBC (β = 0.080, 95% CI: 0.014 to 0.145) and RBC counts (β = −0.286, 95% CI: −0.530 to −0.042) were also associated with WMH volume, independent of RDW (Table 2). On the other hand, lacunes and CMBs were not significantly associated with RDW after adjusting for confounders (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we found that RDW was associated with WMH volume in health check-up participants. Since RDW was not associated with lacunes or CMBs, the pathological mechanism reflected by RDW appears to be involved in the development of a specific pathology rather than a common pathology that permeates all subtypes of cSVD.

The exact pathological mechanism underlying the close association between RDW and WMH volume remains unclear. However, several hypotheses may explain this relationship: First of all, we can think of it in relation to hypoxemia. RDW is an indicator of how widely RBC sizes are distributed11. In states of hypoxemia, erythropoiesis is upregulated due to inadequate oxygen supply, leading to the premature release of immature RBCs into the circulation, thereby elevating RDW24. Based on this mechanism, RDW has been used as a prognostic marker in patients with COPD or congestive heart failure25–28. In fact, also in our data, RDW showed a negative correlation with FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC ratio, suggesting that it may be associated with lung conditions that may induce hypoxemia. Chronic diffuse hypoxemia is one of the well-known pathological mechanisms of WMH, and RDW may be an indicator of high-risk patients exposed to this environment5. However, this hypoxemia does not appear to reduce perfusion of the perforating artery enough to cause lacune formation.

Second, systemic inflammation may be the pathological mechanism linking the two. In patients with pulmonary disease, including COPD, persistent hypoxemia can lead to an inflammatory response, which can promote abnormal erythropoiesis in the bone marrow and increase RDW24,29,30. Of course, there are many pathological conditions other than chronic hypoxemia that can cause systemic inflammation. Previous studies have shown that RDW also increases in situations of systemic inflammation that are not accompanied by anemia or hypoxemia (e.g., rheumatoid disease, cancer, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease)31–35. Similarly, in our data, RDW showed a significant positive correlation with WBC count and hs-CRP level. Chronic systemic inflammation may directly contribute to the well-known pathological mechanisms of WMH, such as atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction, and lipohyalinosis5. Therefore, RDW may reflect the high-risk group of WMH as an indicator of the underlying chronic inflammation. Lastly, the association may be coincidental, arising from sharing vascular risk factors. As demonstrated in Table 1, RDW was significantly associated with various cardio- and cerebrovascular risk factors, many of which are also well-established contributors to WMH development.

The subjects in our study were relatively healthy individuals undergoing routine health check-ups. Among the 2,902 participants who completed pulmonary function testing, only 4.2% had an FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.70. Notably, even after adjusting for or excluding these participants from the analysis, RDW remained significantly associated with WMH volume. Additionally, both WBC counts and hs-CRP levels in our study population were within subclinical ranges. Our findings suggest that WMH development may occur even under near-normal, subclinical levels of hypoxemia or inflammation, and that RDW may serve as a marker reflecting such subtle pathological conditions.

Also in our relatively healthy cohort, RDW values were largely within the normal reference range and lower than the cut-off levels reported in previous cardiovascular and stroke studies, suggesting that their association with WMH may reflect underlying chronic pathological processes. Importantly, RDW remained independently associated with WMH even after adjusting for established risk factors, underscoring its potential pathophysiological relevance. Further research is needed to determine clinically meaningful RDW thresholds.

When interpreting our findings, several limitations should be considered. First, this is a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study; therefore, our results demonstrate associations rather than causality. Second, the study population was relatively young and had few comorbidities, which may have led to an underestimation of the influence of established vascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular disease. Third, RDW was assessed using a single measurement obtained during the health check-up. Since WMH develops over a prolonged period, longitudinal RDW measurements—analyzing either their average or temporal changes—might have provided stronger evidence of a causal relationship. Fourth, the WMH volume used in our study was not normalized by intracranial volume, which should be taken into accounted when interpreting the results. However, as no studies have demonstrated a direct association between intracranial volume and RDW, consideration of a strong biological interaction does not appear to be necessary. Lastly, although we applied exclusion criteria, the potential influence of unmeasured medical conditions and medications that may affect RDW should also be acknowledged.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that elevated RDW is positively associated with WMH volume among health check-up participants. Given that RDW is a simple, inexpensive parameter routinely obtained from standard blood tests, it may be a useful marker for identifying individuals at higher risk for WMH in clinical settings. Nonetheless, our findings should be validated in future prospective studies.
