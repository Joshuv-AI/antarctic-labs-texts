Modeling age-related methylation changes in vitro with intestinal organoids
Authors: Himani Vaidya, Gennaro Calendo, Kelsey Keith, Anthony Pompetti, Curt Balch, Jozef Madzo, Jaroslav Jelinek, Jean-Pierre J Issa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntestinal organoids, three-dimensional cultures derived from intestinal stem cells, serve as a promising model for studying aging. DNA methylation is recognized as a biological clock of aging, and we hypothesized the value of organoid DNA methylation for aging research. To test this, we compared DNA methylation patterns in organoids to those observed in primary aging intestinal epithelium.ResultsOur genome-wide analysis revealed significant DNA methylation changes during organoid establishment and culture. Specifically, 27% of CpG sites exhibited hypomethylation, while 11% gained methylation (hypermethylation). The observed hypermethylation primarily affected CpG islands (14–25% of CpG sites) and corresponded with sites susceptible to age-related methylation in vivo (p < 0.001). Early-passage (P0 and P2) organoids derived from 4- and 24-month-old mice retained aging-specific methylation patterns, with a correlation coefficient of 0.48 (p < 0.001) between in vivo and ex vivo methylation differences. Furthermore, age-related hypermethylation continued to change with passage, with linear modeling using CpG sites that strongly associated with age from a previous dataset indicating that organoids age at an approximate rate of 2 weeks per week in culture. In contrast, hypomethylation primarily (30–36% of CpG sites) occurred in non-aging-associated genomic regions, such as non-promoter, non-CpG island regions (including transposable elements), and showed no correlation with differentiation or aging in vivo.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that DNA methylation changes in organoids reflect two distinct biological processes: aging, as marked by CpG island hypermethylation, and an unexplained stochastic hypomethylation defect. Additionally, treatment with the DNA methylation inhibitor decitabine reduced the methylation age of organoids from 22 to 10 months, demonstrating the potential of organoids as a model for studying extrinsic influences on age-related DNA hypermethylation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-026-02558-y.Keywords: Organoids, Aging, DNA methylation, Epigenetics, Intestinal Epithelial cells

Background

The study of aging could reveal novel pathways and mechanisms that increase lifespan and delay the onset and progression of age-related chronic diseases. Animal models of aging mostly consist of yeast, C. elegans, Drosophila, and rodents [1]. However, these animal models are not always applicable to the complexities of human aging. One avenue for creating cellular and molecular aging models would be to employ organoids, three-dimensional structures that recapitulate the cellular composition and architecture of an organ in miniature [2]. Such 3D organoids derived from patient/animal organs could be used for personalized medicine and drug discovery [3], in addition to the study and modeling of various age-related diseases [4]. The first organoid model was established from adult intestinal stem cells in 2007 [5], followed by numerous organoid model systems of other organs [6–11]. Organoids have been used to study hallmarks of aging such as epigenetic changes, stem cell exhaustion, genomic instability, loss of proteostasis, deregulated nutrient sensing, and altered cellular communication, in various organs such as the intestine, pancreas, brain, liver, and lung [12–17]. However, organoid models are not immune to artifacts of cell culturing, and it remains unclear how closely they reflect in vivo aging. To explore organoids as models of aging, we compared DNA methylation changes in in vitro and in vivo aging, where in vivo refers to primary intestinal epithelial cells isolated from mice aged 4, 12, 18, and 24 months, and in vitro refers to organoids derived from mice aged 4 and 24 months. We hypothesize that passaging intestinal organoids in vitro progressively over time recapitulates in vivo aging, with culture time inducing DNA methylation changes that mirror age-related patterns in intestinal epithelium. And treating the organoids derived from 24-month mice with decitabine reverses age-related DNA methylation in intestinal organoids and lowers epigenetic (DNAm) age toward a more youthful state.

Results

Differential methylation analysis between intestinal organoids and primary cells

We investigated DNA methylation alterations in intestinal organoids by isolating crypts from distinct intestinal regions, including the upper small intestine (USI), lower small intestine (LSI), and colon (COL), in mice aged 4 and 24 months, as grown in a 3D organoid culture in Matrigel (to maintain their structure). During the first 1–2 days of culture, crypts were observed to close, expand, and bud, forming spherical structures (Additional file1: Fig. S1A). By days 3–7, these spheres developed crypt-like invaginations, and the organoids acquired a more complex, differentiated morphology, resembling the crypt-villus architecture of the intestine (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B–C). Crypts were passaged every 7 days, with DNA isolated from each organoid passage subjected to reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) [18] to investigate DNA methylation patterns that change during organoid culture. A schematic diagram of the experimental procedure is included in Additional file 2: Fig. 2 and a list of samples is included in Additional file 3: S. Table 3. S2. While RRBS has some limitations such as bias toward CpG islands, and cannot discern 5-methyl-dC from other cytosine modifications, it is widely accepted in aging methylation studies [18]. We categorized the organoid samples into three groups: (1) early-passage (organoids from passages 0, 2, 3, or 4); (2) middle-passage (organoids from passages 10, 11, 12, or 13); and (3) late-passage (organoids from passages 24 or 27). In total, RRBS yielded analyzable data from 64 organoids. We compared these to DNA methylation in primary upper and lower intestinal and colonic epithelial cells isolated from 4-, 12-, 18-, and 24-month-old mice studied previously [19] (a total of 44 distinct samples). From these DNA methylation data, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that cluster PC1 attributed 63% of the variance to differences between primary cells and organoid cultures (Fig. 1A). Most organoids clustered according to early-, middle-, or late-passage. Within the early-passage group, two subclusters were identified, with one clustering more closely with primary cells, while the other organoids clustered mostly with middle-passage organoids (Fig. 1A). Late-passage organoids formed two clusters, including a distinct subgroup at the extreme end. In contrast, PC2 accounted for merely 6% of the variance, and was driven by differences between small intestinal and colonic primary cells (Fig. 1A) (a detailed PCA is included in Additional file 4: Fig. S3). These results suggested profound DNA methylation changes in organoids compared to uncultured cells.

Differential methylation analysis, performed on 52,369 CpG sites that had a sequencing depth of at least 20 reads in ≥ 75% of the samples (comparing primary cells with organoids), revealed that 11% of CpG sites gained DNA methylation, while 27% lost methylation in organoid samples, compared to age-matched primary cells (Fig. 1B). Previously [19], we identified distinct age-associated DNA methylation patterns in intestinal cells, with non-promoter CpG islands showing the strongest association with age, while non-promoter non-CpG island regions were primarily associated with differentiation, consistent with prior studies [20]. Based on these findings, we focused on these genomic regions in this dataset. In organoids, CpG sites in promoter-CpG islands predominantly gained methylation, with 12% increasing in methylation, and only 1% decreasing (Additional file 5: Fig. S4A). In promoter non-CpG islands, 14% of CpG sites gained and 5% lost methylation (Additional file 5: Fig. S4B). Similarly, non-promoter CpG islands gained methylation in 25% of CpG sites, with 5% losing it (Additional file 5: Fig. S4C). By contrast, non-promoter non-CpG islands and transposable elements displayed a net loss of methylation. Specifically, non-promoter non-CpG islands lost methylation in 30% of their CpG sites and gained it in 11%, while transposable elements lost methylation in 36% of their CpG sites, and gained it in 6% (Additional file 5: Fig. S4D–E). We used odds ratios to quantitate these patterns (Fig. 1C). Promoter-CpG islands, non-promoter CpG islands, and promoter non-CpG islands, were 2 to 3 times more likely to gain methylation in organoid cultures than other genomic regions (Fig. 1C). In contrast, non-promoter, non-CpG islands lost methylation 18 times more frequently than other regions, while transposable elements had a two-fold greater propensity for methylation loss, compared to other genomic regions (Fig. 1D). Thus, there appears to be two distinct patterns of DNA methylation drift in organoids – hypomethylation that nearly exclusively affects non-promoter, non-CpG island sites (i.e., the vast majority, 47,265 out 52,369 detectable CpG sites in the genome), and an opposite hypermethylation effect on CpG islands, which are the primary targets of methylation aging in the intestine [19]. A quantitative description of the odds ratios and confidence intervals is provided in Additional file 6: Fig. 5, Additional file 7: S.Table 2).

We next asked directly whether CpG sites differentially methylated between organoids and primary cells are also affected by age in vivo. For this, we used age-related correlation values for each CpG site in the small intestine and colon from our previous study [19]. We derived Spearman’s correlations between methylation and age for each detected CpG site across USI, LSI, and COL samples from mice aged 4, 12, 18, and 24 months. Figure 1E shows that CpG sites unchanged in organoids had a median R value of 0.09 ± 0.002 (SEM). In comparison, hypermethylated sites in organoids exhibited a significantly higher median R value of 0.18 ± 0.005 (SEM; p ≤ 0.0001 vs. unchanged sites). Hypomethylated sites, however, showed no meaningful correlation with age, with a median R value of 0.06 ± 0.0012 (SEM).

Other than aging, the main determinant of methylation differences is differentiation. To assess whether altered differentiation explained the methylation changes in organoids, we looked for overlap between organoid-specific changes and those seen when comparing the colon and small intestine (9% hypomethylated and 4% hypermethylated sites) (Additional file 8: Fig. S6A), as well as those that were significantly different between the gastrointestinal (GI) tract (small intestine and colon) and other tissues, including blood, spleen, lungs, and liver (14% hypomethylated and 14% hypermethylated sites) (Additional file 8: Fig. S6B). There was a minimal overlap between the three datasets (Fig. 1F–G) indicating that most CpG sites affected by organoid culture do not link to differentiation. To confirm this, a correlation analysis of significantly differentially methylated CpG sites in organoids versus primary cells, performed across all available samples (i.e., different organoid passages, primary intestinal epithelial cells, blood, liver, lung, kidney, heart, skeletal muscle, and spleen), revealed that all passages of organoids more strongly correlated with primary intestinal epithelial cells compared to other organs (Additional file 9: Fig. S7A–B). These results show that organoid hypomethylation cannot be attributed to aging or differentiation and it remains unexplained, perhaps analogous to changes seen in cell culture [21]. However, by focusing on hypermethylated CpG sites linked to aging in the intestinal epithelium, organoids could still potentially be used to model aging if these sites escape from the global hypomethylation observed.

Early-passage organoids maintain aging-specific methylation changes seen in vivo

Given the massive (27% of total CpG sites) hypomethylation observed above, we asked whether organoids exhibit age-related DNA methylation changes similar to those observed in primary samples. DNA methylation at aging-associated CpG sites was compared between primary intestinal epithelial cells isolated from 4- versus 24-month-old mice and organoids at passages 0 and 3, derived from crypt cells of 4- and 24-month-old mice. This analysis revealed a significant correlation between changes in vivo and changes in vitro at early passages (r = 0.48, p < 0.001, Fig. 2A). Nevertheless, PCA (Fig. 1A) revealed differences in organoids isolated from different mice of the same age (4 or 24 months), even at the same passage number. To investigate this further, we examined the correlation between each organoid and its corresponding primary cells. Studied organoids were derived from 4 different mice. In two cases, the earliest passage (P0) correlated almost exactly with its corresponding age-matched primary sample. For instance, P0 organoids, from 24-month-old mouse 283, had a correlation value of r = 0.98, while P0 organoids from another 24-month-old mouse (285) had an r value of 0.96 (Fig. 2B and D and Additional file 10: S.Table 1). In two other organoid cell line cases, one derived from younger mouse 202 and the other from older mouse 196, the correlation for P0 was strong but weaker (r = 0.84 and 0.85, Fig. 2D and F and Additional file 10: S.Table 1), suggesting that initial organoid establishment can involve substantial selection and/or stochastic changes. For all four organoids, as passage number increased, the correlation with primary age-matched samples decreased progressively (Fig. 2C, E, G, Additional file 10: S.Table 1). At the latest passage examined, the r values ranged from 0.62 to 0.75, indicating that epigenetic drift [22, 23] continues in vitro.

Long-term passaging of organoids dysregulates DNA methylation

Since we established that organoids differ in DNA methylation compared to primary cells (Fig. 1A), we next focused solely on organoids to determine how prolonged passaging alters their methylation. Our PCA analysis showed that PC1, accounting for 41% of the variance, clustered by passage stage (Fig. 3A). Early-passage organoids (P0, P2, P3, P4) formed two distinct clusters, which we designated as early 1-passage and early 2-passage, with early 2-passage clustering with middle-passage organoids (P10, P11, P12, P13). Analogously, late-passage organoids formed two separate clusters, with the far-right cluster, late-passage organoids labeled as “crisis,” due to their state of profound hypomethylation, inability to culture further, and weakest correlation to primary cells (Figs. 3A and 1A). The term “crisis” was originally used to describe cultured cells that reach a phase where the rates of cell division and death are balanced, maintaining a constant population size [24]. It is also used to describe replicative senescence, driven by telomere shortening [25].

To understand DNA methylation dynamics in organoid passaging, we examined all 52,369 CpG sites that met the threshold of coverage greater than 20 in 75% of the organoid samples. We initially focused on samples at two extremes: 4-month-old primary samples and crisis organoids, and we plotted methylation differences using a waterfall plot ranked in descending order (Fig. 3B, far right). As expected, based on Fig. 1, we observed a very high degree of epigenetic drift, with predominant hypomethylation (yellow), but also a substantial degree of hypermethylation (green). We then plotted differences between each organoid passage stage and the 4-month primary samples, in the order of early 1-, early 2-, middle, and late passages from left to right. Each line in the plot represents the same CpG site across all stages, allowing us to track how DNA methylation changes with passage number (Fig. 3B). To compare these findings with aging, we plotted DNA methylation differences between primary samples from 24- and 4-month-old mice (Fig. 3B, far left). We found that for both hypo- and hypermethylation, DNA methylation changed with surprising regularity across all organoid stages, affecting a shared set of CpG sites. Strikingly, a subset of CpG sites hypermethylated with age in primary samples (green) became progressively hypermethylated across organoid passage stages (indicated as below the dotted red line). However, most of the other CpG sites (above the dotted line) were unchanged or became hypomethylated with organoid passaging (yellow), seemingly in a stochastic manner unrelated to initial aging patterns observed in the 24-month versus 4-month comparison.

These data suggest that organoids maintain and extend age-related hypermethylation, but only for a subset of CpG sites. To understand this further, we selected 7569 hypermethylated CpG sites that correlated with primary intestinal epithelial cell aging (r > 0.5), from our previous dataset [19] and filtered them for CpG sites that were hypermethylated in crisis organoids compared to 4-month-old primary cells, with a methylation difference greater than 10% as observed in the waterfall plot (CpG sites below red dotted line in Fig. 3B). This analysis yielded 1388 CpG sites. To characterize these sites, we conducted an odds ratio analysis comparing them to the 7569 hypermethylated CpG sites associated with primary intestinal epithelial cell aging. The 1388 CpG sites (i.e., those that changed in both in vivo and in vitro aging) were 1.9 times more likely to be in promoter regions and 2.3 times more likely to be within CpG islands. Genes not expressed in the intestine were found to be 1.6 times more likely to be represented, and Polycomb-associated genes were 1.9 times more likely to be represented among the 2194 CpG sites (Fig. 3C, Additional file 11: S. Table 4). By contrast, non-promoter, non-CpG island sites, as well as transposable elements, were less likely to show conserved aging patterns in vivo and ex vivo, most likely because they were affected by the global hypomethylation defect described earlier.

To visualize methylation patterns, we created a bee-swarm plot showing the methylation status of the 1388 CpG sites across various sample types: primary young (4-month), primary old (24-month), early passage (1 and 2), middle passage, late passage, and crisis organoids. The results revealed that in primary young samples, the majority of these CpG sites exhibited less than 25% methylation. This methylation level increased significantly with age in primary old samples and continued to rise in organoids. The crisis organoids showed the highest methylation levels (Fig. 3D). These findings suggest that while organoids exhibit global hypomethylation compared to primary cells, a subset of aging-associated sites still progressively gain methylation with passage in the organoids.

As previous studies showed age-related hypomethylation in transposable elements (reviewed in [26]), we also explored this phenomenon in our primary and organoid passaging datasets. We analyzed the methylation differences in transposable elements with age by comparing young and old primary cells. Our results showed that only 1% of CpG sites within transposable elements lost methylation, while 3% gained methylation with age (Additional file 12: Fig. S8A). Violin plots of methylation levels in CpG sites across various transposable element types, SINE, LTR, LINE, and DNA repeats, were generated for different sample types, including primary young (4-month), primary old (24-month), early passage (1 and 2), middle passage, late passage, and crisis organoids. The analysis revealed minimal differences in DNA methylation between young and old primary samples. However, in organoids, DNA methylation decreased progressively with passage, with crisis organoids exhibiting the most significant loss of methylation (Additional file 12: Fig. S8B). These results demonstrate that organoid passaging induces hypomethylation of CpG sites in transposable elements.

Aging trajectories in organoids

To determine the rate of aging with organoid culture passage, we employed linear modeling to estimate the age of studied organoids. We used the 1388 aging-CpG sites described earlier that had a coverage of greater than 20 in at least 75% of the samples. For each primary sample, we calculated the average methylation percentage across these 1,388 CpG sites, and a linear model of this average was fit to predict age. This model was then applied to organoid methylation values to calculate methylation age. We first examined the methylation age of the earliest passage organoids studied. As shown in Fig. 4A, organoids derived from young (4-month-old) mice show accelerated aging at the earliest passage, ranging from 5 to 25 months (mean 14, ± 3.2 SEM). This was not found in organoids derived from older (24-month-old) mice (Fig. 4B), where the age acceleration ranged from -8 to + 5 months (mean − 0.3, ± 1.2 SEM), p < 0.001 when compared to organoids derived from young mice). These data suggest that an increased methylation age may be selected for during organoid establishment from young mouse stem cells but not from old stem cells. We next examined the effect of passage on methylation age. All organoids studied gained age with passaging (Fig. 4C and Additional file 13: Fig. S9). Four distinct organoids had data from at least 5 different time points, allowing us to estimate the correlation between passage number and methylation age; R2 varied from 0.6 to 0.9, suggesting a strong correlation (p < 0.001 for all four). Across these four lines, the aging rate varied from 0.31 to 0.53 (average 0.44), meaning they gained 1–2 weeks of methylation age per week in culture (average age gain 2 week/per week in culture). We then estimated the average rate of methylation age change across all 10 organoid cell lines. On average, the organoids gained approximately 2 weeks of methylation age (i.e., 0.45 months) for each week in passage (Fig. 4D).

The data so far suggest that, using selected sites, one can derive reproducible methylation ages for organoids and demonstrate a linear, passage-dependent increase in age that could be used to model aging interventions in vitro. Given the predominance of hypermethylation with age in vivo [19, 26] and in the current in vitro data, we tested the potential of DNA methylation inhibitors in reversing the methylation age increase. For this, we treated organoids, isolated from the upper small intestine of 24-month-old mice, with decitabine, a cytosine analogue that incorporates into DNA during replication [27]. Beginning 24 h after crypts were seeded in Matrigel, we treated the organoids with 50 nM decitabine (DAC) every 48 h for three doses, followed by a resting period (no DAC treatment) of 2 weeks. This treatment was repeated for four cycles, with DNA isolated for reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) [18] at the end of each passage. To estimate methylation age, we applied the same linear model as above. Those results, shown in Fig. 4E, revealed that organoids derived from 24-month-old mice initially had a methylation age of 22 months at Passage 0 of DAC treatment (P0). After 1 week of DAC treatment, their methylation age decreased to 10 months at Passage 2 (P2). During the two resting passages (P3, P4), without DAC treatment, their predicted methylation age increased to 12 and 14 months, respectively. At resting passage P10, during the third treatment cycle, the methylation age was predicted to be around 13 months. Following the fourth DAC treatment cycle (P11), the predicted age dropped to 9.5 months, with a slight increase to 10 and 11 months during subsequent rest periods at Passages 12 and 13, respectively. Taken together, these findings suggest that organoids age predictably in vitro at a rate almost twice as fast as primary cells, and that they can be used to model interventions that modulate methylation age.

Overall, our findings indicate that organoids age in vivo at a predictable rate nearly twice as fast as primary cells and can serve as useful models for testing interventions that influence methylation age. However, the rate of organoid aging varies for several reasons. During establishment, organoids can diverge from primary tissue due to selective pressures, stochastic changes, and variable epigenetic drift, further influenced by the artificial culture environment. Therefore, studying aging with organoids requires multiple independent clones to capture this heterogeneity.

Organoid gene expression changes

We next performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq, which measures the average gene expression across all cells in a sample) on RNA isolated from organoids at early (P2, P3, P4) and middle (P11, P12, P13) passages and related the expression changes to those observed in primary large intestinal samples. Differential expression analysis showed massive changes in organoids, affecting almost 60% of the expressed transcriptome (Fig. 5A). Pathway analysis of the altered genes suggested in part the expected altered differentiation and metabolic programs associated with switching from in vivo to ex vivo function (Additional file 14: Fig. S10C). Indeed, large differences in gene expression were also seen when comparing small intestine with colon in vivo (Additional file 14: Fig. S10A), and there was a substantial overlap between the gene sets altered in organoids and those altered in differentiation in vivo (Fig. 5D). Thus, altered differentiation is a prime driver of the gene expression changes in organoids. To further differential expression analysis, we looked for genes involved in DNA methylation, histone modifications, and senescence (Additional file 15: Fig. S11 A–C). While a few genes showed significant differences, the log-fold changes were modest (around 2–2.5 LogFC), and overall, there were no substantial alterations across these pathways.

To assess whether DNA methylation impacts gene expression in organoids, we categorized CpG site methylation percentages into five groups: 0–10%, 10–20%, 20–50%, 50–80%, and 80–100%, based on previous studies [19, 28]. Overall (Fig. 5B–C), there were large and significant differences in average expression between promoters that displayed low levels of methylation (0–20%) and those that had higher levels of methylation (> 20%). When we analyzed gene expression levels in individual organoids at different passages, we observed similar trends (Additional file 16: Fig. S12A–B). The higher expression in the 0–10% methylation category aligns with the established link between low promoter methylation and active transcription [29].

Finally, we looked for overlap between differential methylation and differential expression between organoids and primary intestine, controlling for tissue type. In the upper small intestine (USI), there were 2061 genes with detectable methylation levels in promoters (> 20 reads in 75% of samples, i.e.) in both organoids and primary tissues. Of these, 419 showed significant differential methylation, 1054 showed significant differential expression, and there was partial but significant overlap (165 genes) between these two groups (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

Understanding the biology and mechanisms of aging is essential for preventing age-related diseases and discovering potential anti-aging strategies. Recently, organoids from various organs have been developed to study tissue development and disease, and in this study, we aimed to evaluate whether intestinal organoids, grown in 3D culture, could serve as a model for DNA methylation changes during aging. To model aging, we used intestinal crypts, known to possess colon adult stem cells (ASCs), rather than induced-pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), used primarily to model tissue-specific differentiation. Our principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering patterns between primary cells and cultured organoids. Differential methylation analysis showed significant hypomethylation in organoids, compared to primary cells, affecting 27% of CpG sites, with the most pronounced changes occurring in non-promoter, non-CpG island regions, which can affect tissue differentiation sites or the noncoding genome. By contrast, hypermethylated CpG sites correlated with age, and differentially methylated sites in organoids, compared to primary cells, did not associate with differentiation. With a focus on CpG island hypermethylation, one can use organoids to model age-related changes in the GI tract. Potential uses include studying how one-carbon metabolism, which provides essential methyl groups for DNA methylation, influences age-related epigenetic changes. Genetic variants affecting DNA methyltransferases (e.g., DNMT1, DNMT3A) could be investigated to determine their role in accelerated or delayed epigenetic aging. Metabolite profiling in aging organoids could reveal how metabolic shifts contribute to hypermethylation patterns, potentially identifying biomarkers for early detection of GI aging or disease. Additionally, organoids could serve as a platform to test pharmacologic agents and dietary interventions such as folate, SAM (S-adenosylmethionine), or demethylating agents to determine their effects on methylation maintenance and reversal. Such models may also be useful in predicting how cancer drugs targeting epigenetic regulators (e.g., EZH2, DNMT) influence DNA methylation and stem cell function in the aging gut.

Early passage (P0, P2) organoids, from 4- and 24-month-old mice, retain age-related changes with primary cells; however, as passage number increased, the organoid samples extended this age-related methylation at a rate twice faster than in vivo. Overall, our results show that while organoids undergo hypomethylation compared to primary cells, they can still be an effective tool to model aging, in vitro. Several studies have shown that prolonged passage of 2D-cultured cells can alter DNA methylation patterns, a phenomenon known as epigenetic drift [22, 23]. Epigenetic drift can be further defined as a gradual decline in the stability of epigenetic patterns, leading to a loss of epigenetic information and change in the epigenomic landscape [30]. For example, organoid hypomethylation may be a consequence of epigenetic drift, during passaging, where repeated cell divisions lead to a significant loss of DNA methylation, over time, and create cell culture artifacts and could impact the cellular functions of the organoids [21]. These culture-related modifications can influence cellular processes, highlighting the importance of considering such alterations when using organoids for research. The exact causes of this massive hypomethylation (27% of total CpG sites) drift remain unclear. Possible explanations include missing cofactors essential for methylation, errors introduced during DNA replication, or secondary adaptations due to gene expression changes. One possibility is that essential cofactors for DNA methylation, such as S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the primary methyl donor, are depleted or inefficiently utilized in organoid culture systems. This could be due to altered one-carbon metabolism, which is responsible for generating SAM from folate and methionine. Additionally, deficiencies in folate or other methyl cycle components might impair DNA methylation maintenance over successive passages, leading to progressive hypomethylation. Such widespread hypomethylation can disrupt cellular homeostasis by altering chromatin structure, deregulating gene expression, and activating transposable elements. Hypomethylation of CpG sites in transposable elements, in particular, may have important implications for cellular homeostasis [31]. While a detailed functional analysis is beyond the scope of the present study, these findings highlight a potentially important avenue for future investigation.

Another potential explanation is one-sided errors during DNA replication, particularly during the maintenance of CpG methylation. DNA methylation patterns are typically copied by DNMT1, which recognizes hemi-methylated DNA following replication and restores full methylation [32]. However, if this process becomes inefficient or asymmetric, daughter cells may progressively lose methylation, leading to a drift toward hypomethylation. This could be exacerbated by the absence or reduced activity of DNMT1 or its cofactor UHRF1, both of which are critical for maintaining methylation during cell division [33]. Additionally, gene expression adaptation in culture conditions could contribute to drift. The absence of in vivo microenvironmental signals including immune cells, microbiota, and extracellular matrix components may alter epigenetic regulation, leading to the downregulation of chromatin remodelers, DNA methyltransferases, and repair enzymes that are crucial for maintaining methylation patterns. Another possibility is that passaging and prolonged culture select for cells with hypomethylation drift, favoring those that adapt to artificial conditions by relaxing epigenetic constraints. This resembles epigenetic reprogramming in cancer, where hypomethylation contributes to genomic instability and transcriptional changes [34].

A technical reason for the observed hypomethylation in the organoids could be due to the limitations of using reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS), a method that primarily focuses on CpG-rich regions of the genome. While this approach is well-suited for detecting age-related hypermethylation in CpG islands, it may underrepresent hypomethylation in other regions, potentially contributing to the lack of correlation observed in organoids. Nonetheless, our conclusions on CpG island hypermethylation remain robust. Also, it is worth noting, that out of 52,369 detectable sites in our analyses, 47,265 were outside CpG islands, suggesting that RRBS still provides sufficient coverage for a preliminary assessment of non-CGI sites. Future studies should examine global methylation patterns across multiple passages, assess DNMT activity, and explore whether metabolic supplementation (e.g., SAM and folate) can stabilize methylation patterns in long-term organoid cultures. Another limitation of RRBS is that it cannot distinguish 5-methylcytosine (5mC) from other cytosine modifications such as 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC). These marks are biologically distinct; one study showed that 5hmc is strongest in the differentiated villus cells and the signal is depleted in the crypt region [35], Another study also showed that 5hmC is low in the proliferating gut crypt progenitors and increased in the differentiated villus epithelium of the mouse small intestine (SI) [36]. Thus, 5hmC is enriched in intestinal epithelium, in differentiated cells, and is known to decline in rapidly dividing cells that make up the crypt of the intestines, which are used to derive the cultured organoids. Therefore, some of the observed methylation loss could, in part, reflect changes in 5hmC rather than true demethylation, however these are small changes and does not diminish the validity of our comparative analyses between primary cells and organoids. Future studies employing oxidative bisulfite sequencing or other modification-specific methods will be important to dissect the relative contribution of 5mC versus 5hmC to aging- and culture-associated signatures.

Our results show that organoids from young (4-month) intestine appear older than 4-month primary epithelial cells, whereas organoids from old (24-month) intestine vary by organoid line, with some older and some younger than the corresponding primary epithelium. This suggests that in young mice, subsets of stem cells with survival advantages, driven by intrinsic epigenetic or metabolic traits, may preferentially expand in culture, whereas in older mice such advantages are limited by senescence, accumulated damage, or epigenetic rigidity. This balance between survival and senescence may act as a natural check on unchecked proliferation. The linear model used to assign a “methylation age” to the organoids is based on primary (non-organoid) epithelia and therefore can directly be used to answer this comparative question (with the caveat that this applies only to the selected CpG sites). Thus, when we find that organoids derived from 4-month-old mice are 15–20 months old, this implies that they look as “old” as uncultured epithelia from 15 to 20 month-old mice. With in vitro aging, they generally gain methylation age and, within 4 months in culture, they already look as old as the 24-month-old epithelium; this could be a sign of accelerated aging. Further research is needed to determine the molecular drivers of this selection process and its implications for organoid-based tissue regeneration and disease models.

Physiologically, the striking drifts in gene expression and in hypomethylation may limit the physiologic usefulness of organoids. Studies have also demonstrated that the microenvironment significantly influences DNA methylation changes [37]. While ex vivo organoids offer advantages over 2D cell culture systems, such as cellular heterogeneity and a 3D architecture that better mimics tissue structure, organoids still lack key components of the in vivo environment, including immune cells, blood vessels, microbes, and a fully representative extracellular matrix [38]. This absence can disrupt the cellular microenvironment, potentially contributing to alterations in DNA methylation patterns and potentially distorting the epigenetic landscape within the organoid model.

Furthermore, the formation of a necrotic core in long-term organoid cultures, particularly composed of senescent cells, could be another contributing factor to DNA methylation changes. These senescent cells secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and proteases, a phenomenon collectively known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [39]. SASP can create a local inflammatory environment, further driving epigenetic alterations, including DNA methylation changes, which may impact the stability and aging characteristics of organoids in long-term culture [40]. This phenomenon is particularly concerning for the long-term use of organoids in aging studies, as it may skew the results and reduce the model’s ability to accurately reflect physiological processes.

Additionally, as organoid cultures are often maintained for extended periods, the accumulation of genetic mutations and epigenetic drift over successive passages may introduce variability that limits their predictability and reproducibility. The effects of cell culture-induced changes, such as epigenetic drift, are well documented in other model systems [22, 23], with implications for cellular identity and function, further complicating the use of organoids for accurate disease modeling. To improve the utility of organoids in aging and disease research, further advancements are needed to enhance the mimicking of in vivo conditions, such as incorporating immune cells, improving ECM components, and refining culture systems to minimize drift and better preserve DNA methylation patterns. By addressing these challenges, organoids may become a more accurate and reliable model for studying aging, tissue development, and disease processes.

Conclusion

Despite these challenges, our linear modeling demonstrates that aging can be effectively recapitulated, ex vivo, by focusing on methylation of age-associated CpG sites. Analogously, organoid treatment with the DNMT inhibitor decitabine (DAC) could reverse DNA methylation age gain with passage. In summary, in this study, we extensively characterized intestinal organoids as a model for aging, based on methylation age. In our model, we demonstrated that these organized structures age roughly twice as fast as primary tissues, thus overcoming the numerous limitations (time, cost) of model organisms, such as intact mice and various invertebrates. Further work is needed to demonstrate the relevance of mouse organoid aging to the same biological process in humans.

Methods

Organoid culture

Intestinal crypts, from Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-creERT2 (JAX Labs) mice aged 4 months and 24 months, were isolated by dissecting the small intestine (upper small intestine and lower small intestine) and colon, following euthanasia. The intestine was cut longitudinally and washed with cold PBS, to remove debris. The tissue was then cut into 2–5 mm pieces and incubated in Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (STEMCELL Technologies, #100–1077), at room temperature for 30 min, on an orbital shaker, at 60 rpm. After incubation, the tissue was vortexed gently to release the crypts. The supernatant was filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer (Thermo Fisher, #22,363,548), and crypts were collected by centrifugation at 250 g, for 5 min, at room temperature. The crypt pellet was resuspended in Complete Mouse IntestiCult™ Organoid Growth Media (STEMCELL Technologies, # 06005), and Matrigel (Corning, #356,231) was added to a 1:1 ratio. The organoids were added as 50 µL droplets into a pre-warmed 24-well plate (CellTreat, #229,123) and incubated at 37 °C for 10–15 min, to solidify the Matrigel. Once the Matrigel solidified, 7500 µL of complete IntestiCult™ Organoid Growth Medium (Mouse) (STEMCELL) was added to each well, with 1% Primocin (InvivoGen, # ant-pm-1). Organoids were cultured in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator at 37 °C, with medium changes every 48 h. Organoids were passaged every 7 days, when reaching 200–500 µm in diameter, by mechanically disrupting the Matrigel and organoids using room temperature Gentle Dissociation Reagent, followed by resuspension of the fragments in fresh IntestiCult organoid growth media and Matrigel (1:1 ratio), and re-plating. Organoids were treated with 50 ng/mL decitabine (DAC) beginning 24 h after plating. DAC was replenished every 48 h during media changes, and organoids were passaged on day 7. After each cycle of DAC treatment, organoids were passaged twice without DAC exposure, allowing for a rest period. This treatment and rest cycle were repeated for a total of 21 days.

DNA extraction

Cell pools from all organoids were lysed in 2% SDS and 25 mM EDTA, overnight. Ammonium acetate (10 M) was added to precipitate SDS with proteins, which were removed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm. DNA was then precipitated with isopropanol, washed in 70% ethanol, and dissolved in LTET (10 mM Tris, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1% Tween 20). DNA concentration was measured using the Qubit dsDNA Broad Range kit (Thermo Fisher, #Q32850).

Reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS)

Two micrograms of DNA were digested with 100 U of MspI endonuclease (NEB, #R0106), at 37 °C, for 3.5 h. The Klenow fragment (3′ > 5′ exo-) DNA polymerase (NEB, #M0212) was used to fill in 3′ recesses and generate 3′ dA overhangs. Size selection was performed using AMPure XP beads (Beckman, #A63881), with a 2X bead-to-DNA volume ratio. Methylated sequencing adapters (NEB, #E7535) were ligated to DNA fragments with T4 DNA ligase (NEB #M0202), at 16 °C overnight. Adapters were cleaved by incubation with USER enzyme (NEB #M5505), at 37 °C, for 15 min. Dual size selection with AMPure XP beads removed DNA fragments greater than 500 bp and less than 150 bp. Purified DNA fragments were bisulfite-converted using the EpiTect Bisulfite Conversion Kit (Qiagen, #59,104). The bisulfite-converted fragments were cleaned using a 3X AMPure bead-to-DNA ratio. NEBNext Index i7 primers (NEB #7335, #7500) were used to amplify libraries with EpiMark Hot Start Taq (NEB, #M0490), for 15 cycles. Library concentrations were measured using the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity kit (Thermo Fisher, #Q32851). The Agilent Bioanalyzer determined size distribution and molarity using the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA kit (Agilent, #5067–4626). Libraries were pooled to a 10 nM concentration and spiked with 25% phiX (Illumina, #FC1103001) for sequence diversity. Sequencing occurred over several years at multiple facilities.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Organoids were collected, pelleted, and stored in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher, #AM7020) at -20 °C. Pellets were lysed in 1 mL TRIzol (Thermo Fisher, #15,596,026), with the addition of 100 µL bromo-chlorophenol (Fisher, #AC173530050). Phase separation was achieved by centrifugation at 12,000 g, for 15 min, at 4 °C, and the RNA-containing supernatant was collected. RNA was precipitated with an equal volume of isopropanol, followed by a wash with 1 mL 75% ethanol. The RNA pellet was collected by centrifugation at 7500 g, for 5 min, and dissolved in 30 µL nuclease-free water. RNA concentrations were measured using the Qubit RNA Broad Range kit (Thermo Fisher, #Q10210), and RNA integrity (RIN) was determined using an Agilent RNA 6000 Nano kit (Agilent, #56,067–1511). RNA was sent to BGI Genomics for library preparation and sequencing.

Data processing and analysis

Sequenced RRBS libraries were trimmed with TrimGalore, and reads aligned to the mm9 mouse genome using the Bismark Bisulfite Read Mapper and Methylation Caller, to call CpG methylation. Data were filtered for a minimum coverage of 20 reads per CpG. Differential methylation analysis was performed using the limma package in R. First, a design matrix was constructed to account for experimental conditions and potential confounders. The lmFit function was then applied to fit a linear model on the M-values for each CpG site, followed by the application of empirical Bayes moderation using the eBayes function to obtain moderated t-statistics and associated p-values.

RNA libraries were trimmed using Trimmomatic, aligned to the mm9 genome, and gene counts obtained using STAR.

Raw count data was imported into edgeR for downstream differential expression analysis. Normalization factors were estimated for each library using TMM normalization. Differentially expressed genes, between conditions, were determined by applying the glmTreat function to test genes, relative to a minimum required fold-change.

Linear modeling

To investigate age-related DNA methylation changes, we analyzed the average methylation levels of CpG sites from 4-, 12-, 18-, and 24-month-old mice. For each sample, the average methylation level across all CpG sites was calculated. A linear model was constructed using these average methylation values as a function of the known ages of the mice. The fitted equation of the linear model was then used to predict the age of organoids at different passage numbers. Intestinal organoids were isolated from 4- and 24-month-old mice, and the average CpG methylation level was calculated for each passage. These average methylation values were used as input into the linear model to estimate the “methylation age” of organoids at various passages. All statistical analyses and model fitting were conducted in R using the lm() function.
