Dispatch of locum doctors to rural clinics on remote islands in Japan
Authors: Miki Yamauchi, Akihisa Nakamura, Daisuke Matsubara, Kazuhiko Kotani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: The dispatch of a locum doctor (DLD) system is an important aspect of the clinical settings in remote islands, where securing doctors is a major challenge. This study investigated the conditions associated with DLD requests and observed an association between DLD requests and several regional factors in rural clinics on remote islands in Japan.Materials and Methods: Questionnaires regarding DLD requests were mailed to all rural clinics on remote islands managed under the rural plans of the prefectural governments of Japan in December 2022. The regional factors assessed included population size (large remote islands: population ≥1,000 people), doctor staffing on islands (single or multiple doctors within an island), and distance between rural clinics and the nearest core hospitals, which are designated at the prefectural level of Japan to support and collaborate with rural clinics (long distance, ≥25 km). The association between DLD requests and regional characteristics was analyzed with and without DLD requests.Results: Of the 72 rural clinics (32%) that responded completely to the questionnaire, 27 (38%) had made DLD requests. In the logistic regression analysis, DLD requests were positively associated with long distances from core hospitals (odds ratio [OR]=3.82, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.26–11.6) and the presence of a single doctor on the island (OR=5.24, 95% CI: 1.73–15.9).Conclusion: These results indicate that DLD requests may be more frequent from remote islands, which were far from core hospitals, and those staffed by a single doctor. Measures based on these results may contribute to the development of efficient medical support systems for remote islands in Japan.Keywords: island medicine, locum doctors, medical care system, rural clinic, rural medicine

Introduction

Owing to their geographical characteristics, remote islands can face unresolved issues, including limited medical resources and difficulties in securing doctors1,2,3). Remote islands are often staffed by a small number of doctors, with only a single doctor available for providing medical care on some remote islands4). As a result, these doctors are often unable to go on personal or sick leaves or participate in specialized training1, 5).

To address these issues, some remote islands have implemented systems for dispatch of a locum doctor (DLD). The DLD system ensures a consistent supply of medical care by covering both predictable absences, such as professional training and personal events, and unpredictable absences such as sick leaves. Additionally, it provides extra doctors to meet surges in medical demand5,6,7,8,9,10,11). In Japan, core hospitals are designated at the prefectural level to support and collaborate with rural clinics (RCs), including through DLD7, 12). Because of its role in providing appropriate and flexible staffing, the DLD system is important in the clinical settings of remote islands, where securing doctors is a major challenge.

Although DLD can support medical care on remote islands, few studies have been conducted on DLD in these areas. On remote islands, factors such as single practice and distance from core hospitals may affect DLD requests13, 14). Thus, this study investigated the conditions associated with DLD requests and observed an association between DLD requests and several regional factors in RCs on remote islands in Japan.

Materials and Methods

Study design and materials

In December 2022, self-administered questionnaires were mailed to all RCs (n=224) on remote islands managed under the rural plan of the prefectural governments of Japan. The questionnaire was collaboratively designed by experts and doctors working on remote islands to ensure the relevance and clarity of the questions. The survey collected information from medical personnel regarding the presence of DLD requests. The population of each island was obtained from the 2020 Population Census of Japan15), since an island’s population size can affect medical care systems16, 17). Data on the doctors on the islands, i.e., whether the island had multiple doctors or only a single doctor, were obtained from the records of the local health bureau18). In this study, remote islands with population ≥1,000 people were classified as large remote islands, whereas those with population <1,000 people were classified as small remote islands17, 19). On the basis of the findings of previous studies, direct distances of less than 25 km between clinics and the nearest core hospitals were classified as short distances, whereas those of 25 km or more were classified as long distances20, 21). The distances between the RCs on the remote islands and the core hospitals nearest to the RC were calculated using the ArcGIS Pro 3.0 software program (ESRI, Redlands, CA, USA).

Statistical analyses

SPSS Version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the statistical analyses. Categorical variables were presented as percentages, whereas continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Factors such as island population (small vs. large), distance from core hospitals (short vs. long), and staffing of doctors on islands (single vs. multiple) were compared between RCs with and without DLD requests by using Pearson’s χ2 test. A multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted using these factors as independent variables and DLD requests as the dependent variable. Island population was excluded as a covariate owing to its high coefficient of association with the staffing of doctors on the islands (φ coefficient: 0.742, P<0.001). In all analyses, statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Results

A total of 72 RCs (32%) that completely responded to the survey were included in the analyses; their characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median population of the remote islands where the RCs were located was 704 (IQR: 210–2,300). The median distance from the nearest core hospital to the RCs was 23 km (IQR: 13–55 km). Thirty RCs (42%) were staffed by a single doctor on the island. A total of 27 RCs (38%) had made DLD requests. A significant association was observed between DLD requests and the island population (small remote islands [59%] vs. large remote islands [41%], P=0.01), distance from core hospitals (short distance [41%] vs. long distance [59%], P=0.03), and staffing of doctors on the islands (single [63%] vs. multiple [37%], P<0.01) (Table 2).

In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, both the distance from core hospitals and staffing of doctors on the islands were associated with DLD requests (Table 3). Long distance from core hospitals was associated with significantly higher DLD requests than short distance (odds ratio [OR]=3.82, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.26–11.6). Additionally, the presence of a single doctor on an island was associated with significantly higher DLD requests than the presence of multiple doctors on an island (OR=5.24, 95% CI: 1.73–15.9).

Discussion

This study investigated DLD requests and observed an association between several regional factors and DLD requests by RCs on remote islands of Japan. Approximately 40% of the RCs on remote islands made DLD requests. A previous study conducted in one prefecture in Japan reported that 75% of RCs in the mainland made DLD requests22). In comparison with the results of this previous study, our findings showing that 40% of the RCs made DLD requests seems rather low. We assume that several factors may contribute to this result, including the significant burden of travel to remote islands, restrictions on accommodation and living conditions, and the financial costs associated with a DLD request6, 10). In addition, the potential for DLD requests to be refused must be acknowledged. A previous study indicated that core hospitals in Japan have fewer doctors per 100 beds than general hospitals, with only 30% of the core hospitals providing DLDs. Thus, core hospitals may struggle to meet all DLD requests from RCs7).

In this study, long distances from core hospitals showed a significant positive association with DLD requests. This is similar to the findings of previous studies reporting that greater rurality is linked to a higher demand for DLDs13, 14). Clinics situated far from core hospitals face challenges in obtaining direct support, leading to a higher risk of medical care interruptions due to doctors’ absences23). Consequently, DLDs may be requested more proactively to prevent such interruptions, despite the travel burdens involved.

In this study, a significant positive association was observed between having a single doctor on the island and DLD requests. Having multiple doctors on an island may facilitate cooperation and shared responsibilities within an island, potentially reducing the number of DLD requests. Conversely, when only one doctor is available, the need for DLDs increases to avoid disruptions in medical care, particularly when the doctor becomes ill or has to go on a leave. Furthermore, studies indicate that doctors in rural areas, including remote islands, often perform a wider range of medical procedures than their urban counterparts4, 24). These responsibilities may include emergency medical services, chronic disease management, after-hours care, and basic surgical or obstetric procedures. Therefore, the workload of a single doctor on an island tends to be heavier and more complex, which may further increase the need for external support such as DLD.

By enabling doctors to go on necessary leaves and provide additional staffing during peak medical demands, the DLD system can alleviate the burden on doctors and serve as a strategy for securing and retaining them on remote islands5). This study highlights the importance of building a more robust and responsive DLD network on remote islands, particularly for RCs that face long distances and staffing challenges. Developing infrastructure, allocating budgets for DLDs, ensuring adequate staffing in core hospitals, and implementing regular DLDs may be crucial.

One strength of this study is its nationwide survey of remote islands in Japan, which potentially offers representative data for the country. However, this study had several limitations. First, the questionnaire had a low response rate (32%), since mail surveys often yield low responses. When the characteristics of RCs that responded to the survey (responding RCs) and RCs that did not respond to the survey (non-responding RCs) were compared, significant differences were observed in relation to the island population (responding RCs vs. non-responding RCs; median: 704 [interquartile range {IQR}: 210–2300] vs. 245 [IQR: 104–613], P<0.001) and the proportion of islands with only a single doctor (responding RCs vs. non-responding RCs; 42% [number: 30/72] vs. 59% [89/152], P=0.018), whereas the analyses based on the distance from the nearest core hospital did not show any differences (responding RCs vs. non-responding RCs; median: 23 km [IQR: 13–55 km] vs. 21 km [IQR: 12–39 km], P=0.276). Given the differences in the island population and the staffing of doctors on the islands, a potential non-response bias can be considered. The fact that the proportion of islands with a single doctor in the responding RCs was lower than that in the non-responding RCs may indicate that RCs with an interest in DLD requests participated in this survey; thus, caution is required when interpreting and generalizing the findings. Second, the survey used self-reported data. Third, we did not acquire information regarding the characteristics of doctors, such as age, sex, or specialty, or detailed information on the DLD requests, such as frequency, periods, and acceptance rates, when RCs made DLD requests from core hospitals. Furthermore, we were unable to gather information regarding the core hospitals that dispatched physicians, such as their staffing levels and response rates to DLD requests. Fourth, due to the challenges in measuring actual travel distances, we used direct distance values to assess the proximity of RCs to core hospitals. Because the direct distance and actual travel distance are generally correlated, the impact of using the direct distance on the results is likely minimal25). Nevertheless, these limitations should be carefully considered when generalizing the results of this study.

Conclusion

Approximately 40% of the RCs made DLD requests. A significant association was observed between the DLD requests and long distances from core hospitals as well as staffing with a single doctor. Measures based on these results may contribute to the development of efficient medical care support systems for the remote islands in Japan. However, considering the need for more data and the limitations of this study, the generalizability of these findings must be interpreted with caution. These findings warrant further investigation.
