Familial Searching utilising the National Forensic DNA Database of South Africa (NFDD-ZA) to aid in the identification of Missing Persons and Unidentified Human Remains
Authors: M. Singh, J.H. Smith, K. Ehlers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The identification of missing persons and unidentified human remains (MPUHR) remains a significant challenge in South Africa. While primary and secondary identification methods, such as DNA kinship analysis, are routinely used, these approaches often depend on the availability of close biological relatives and do not always result in positive identifications. This has prompted the need to explore alternative identification strategies. The Criminal Law (Forensic Procedures) Amendment Act, 2013 provides for the use of DNA analysis not only in criminal investigations but also in the identification of MPUHR. Notably, the Act permits familial searching within the National Forensic DNA Database of South Africa (NFDD-ZA), yet this capability remains underutilized for humanitarian identification purposes (1). This study investigates the feasibility of employing familial searching using the Combined DNA Index System (CODIS) platform to support the identification of MPUHR. Case studies were used to evaluate key variables such as DNA typing chemistries, the number and type of biological relatives required, and the effect of genetic anomalies (e.g., tri-allelic patterns) on match accuracy. Findings from this study indicate that familial searching in NFDD-ZA can be successfully conducted with Global Filer™ STR profiles in accordance with the DNA Act and DNA regulations thus familial searching can be used as a routine tool for the identification of missing persons and unidentified human remains by the South African Police Service (SAPS).

Introduction

The identification of missing persons and unidentified human remains is a global humanitarian and forensic challenge. In South Africa, this issue is particularly acute, with approximately 10,000 unidentified individuals buried each year without proper identification [1]. In accordance with the National Health Act, 2003, the Forensic Pathology Services (FPS) are responsible for investigating unnatural deaths, while the South African Police Service (SAPS) bears the responsibility of identifying unidentified decedents [2].

Several factors contribute to the persistent backlog of unidentified persons, including undocumented migration, lack of personal identification, social marginalization, and unresolved missing person cases [3]. There are several factors specifically contributing to South Africa's problem with identification of deceased individuals, include a combination of the high level of crime, South African citizens with no identification documents and large number of undocumented individuals [4]. Traditional human identification forensic techniques such as dactyloscopy, anthropology, and odontology are commonly used to identify decedents or human remains because they are relatively rapid and cost-effective. However, these methods rely on the availability and condition of specific anatomical features, which may not always be present or intact due to decomposition or incomplete records of these specific features. [5]. In these cases, DNA has proven to be useful for the identification of MPUHR due to the fact that biological relatives share genetic material. When there is no known biological relative for kinship analysis, familial searching in a DNA database is an alternative solution.

Although DNA kinship analysis using STR analysis has proven to be a powerful tool, its effectiveness is limited when only direct relatives are available for comparison [6]. In the UK when a match is obtained on the National DNA Database (NDNAD), it is communicated to the investigating officer in the form of a report. Kinship analysis is when profiles of known relatives are utilised to assist in identifying a body or the donor of a crime sample [7]. Familial searching is done utilising the database to identify biological relatives of the unknown donor of a crime sample or aid in the identification of an unidentified body. Familial searches were conducted and prioritized ranked results by leveraging the capabilities of the CODIS software, specifically its functionality for Unidentified Human Remains (UHR) searches. Traditionally, a CODIS's pedigree search is employed by adding DNA profiles from relatives of the missing person into a constructed pedigree to identify potential matches with UHR. This strategy diverges from this conventional method by implanting the “target DNA profile,” derived from the forensic sample or a crime scene into a hypothetical pedigree. This allows for assessing potential familial relationships by comparing the forensic sample or a crime scene profile against existing profiles in the database. The results are quantified using the Joint Pedigree Likelihood Ratio (JPLR), an extension of the traditional Likelihood Ratio (LR) that incorporates allele frequency data to provide a statistical measure of relatedness. This application of CODIS's pedigree search functionality enhances the prioritization of familial outcome investigative leads in cases where direct matches are absent [8].

The Criminal Law (Forensic Procedures) Amendment Act, 2013 provides a legislative foundation for the broader use of forensic DNA technologies in South Africa, including the identification of MPUHR through familial searching (SAPS, 2014). Despite this legal provision, familial searching using the National Forensic DNA Database of South Africa (NFDD-ZA) has not yet been applied in missing person investigations and identification of decedents. Within NFDD-ZA is the CODIS module, which was integrated into NFFD-ZA in October 2024 [9]. The CODIS module contains DNA profiles obtained from various types of commercial kits including the AmpFLSTR™ Profiler Plus Kits, AmpFLSTR™ Identifiler™ Plus Kit and the Global Filer™ STR kit. The CODIS Module is used to do daily automated high stringency searches, which is configured in such a way that it produces candidate forensic investigative leads based on a full DNA profile match. These leads are then verified and utilised to solve crimes committed by serial offenders. In the case of familial searching in a forensic DNA database, the search however involves conducting a medium stringency searching process, thus it is a partial match. By using a known DNA profile (MPUHR/biological relative) to identify a partial match with a candidate DNA profile (MPUHR/biological relative), investigative leads can be generated.

This study aims to assess the implementation, operational feasibility and effectiveness of familial searching using CODIS in the South African context. Specific objectives include: i) Conducting case studies of familial searches using CODIS, ii) Evaluating STR typing chemistries (15 STRs vs 22 STRs) iii) Determining the minimum number and type of close relatives needed for informative searches, and iv) the impact of tri-allelic STR variants on search accuracy.

Material and methods

This study employed a qualitative research design that integrates both primary and secondary data sources. Primary data were obtained through purposive sampling of relevant profiles from the National Forensic DNA Database of South Africa (NFDD-ZA), specifically focusing on cases where DNA samples from biological relatives of missing persons and unidentified human remains were available. The purposive sample selection contained DNA profiles from AmpFLSTR™ Identifiler™ Plus Kit and Global Filer™ STR kit data sets, obtained from both solved and unsolved biological relationship determination cases, were sampled from the NFDD-ZA. In this study, a total of 135 known DNA profiles (consisting of 123 pedigrees) were obtained during a three-month period.

The CODIS module, as implemented by SAPS as part of the NFDD-ZA system solution, was used to build pedigrees and conduct familial searches. Pedigree trees were generated using the CODIS Pedigree Manager. The “Single Node” configuration was applied when only one known DNA-typed individual was available, while the “Missing Persons” configuration was used when multiple relatives were present.

The pedigree structures were subjected to internal consistency checks to confirm biological plausibility before being saved for search execution. Using the Pedigree Searcher function, the validated pedigrees were used to search the CODIS module for potential familial matches. CODIS generated ranked candidate lists based on calculated likelihood ratios. Candidate matches were stored and ranked using the Rank Manager. The POPSTATS module of CODIS was used to interpret the likelihood ratios and assign kinship categories (e.g., parent-offspring, full sibling, half sibling).

Results and discussion

One hundred and twenty-three case studies were analysed in CODIS module, the first set of case studies comprised of 44 identified (solved) UHR case studies, was analysed using the AmpFLSTR™ Identifiler™ Plus Kit. The second set of case studies comprised of 32 unidentified (unsolved) UHR case studies analysed using both AmpFLSTR™ Identifiler™ Plus Kit and Global Filer™ STR kit. The third set of case studies comprised of 46 unidentified (unsolved) UHR case studies was analysed with Global Filer™ STR kit.

The case studies were then subjected to familial candidate match generation and ranking using the CODIS module. The resulting outcomes were ranked according to their likelihood ratio. It is noteworthy that the CODIS can generate a maximum of 1000 potential familial candidate matches [10]. These candidate matches were then confirmed through statistical kinship analysis to determine the likelihood of a true biological relationship. In this process, a joint pedigree likelihood ratio (LR) threshold is applied—configured at 1.00E+00 (or 1), which indicates that the likelihood of a relationship is at least as probable as unrelatedness. Only those candidate matches that meet or exceed this threshold are retained, ranked, and reported as potential familial matches. Notably, the parentage kinship cases were the only ones that produced a first-rank result when using profiles generated using the AmpFLSTR™ Identifiler™ Plus Kit, meaning only one possible familial match was identified (see Table 1). In contrast, the other kinship cases, namely paternity, maternity, and sibling, each produced over 100 ranked results using only AmpFLSTR™ Identifiler™ Plus Kit generated DNA profiles, indicating multiple potential familial matches as indicated in Table 1.

Parentage case study

In the parentage case study, the DNA profiles of both parents and the child were available. This case was subjected to a familial search using the CODIS to generate a missing person category pedigree. If for example the parents have reported a missing child and their DNA profiles were loaded into the Investigative Index of the CODIS module, and the DNA profile derived from the child's human remains were loaded into the MPUHR Index of the CODIS module, a similar search would have been done as in this example. There are two familial searching scenario configurations possible. In the first scenario, the parents are associated to the identified nodes in the pedigree tree, and the child is an unidentified node (See Fig. 1). In this scenario, the pedigree familial search uses the known DNA profiles associated with parents to search against in the CODIS module for any familial DNA profile matches. The CODIS module returned one familial candidate match for this case study. Thus, in this scenario outcome the familial candidate matches correctly associated the parents to their child.Fig. 1Parentage case study scenario one pedigree tree.Fig. 1

In the second scenario, the two parents are considered as the unidentified nodes, and the child is associated to an identified node in the pedigree tree. (See Fig. 2). In this scenario, the pedigree familial search uses the known DNA profiles associated with the child to search against in the CODIS module for any familial DNA profile matches. The CODIS module returned familial candidate matches (ranks) one and two for the parents, respectively. In this scenario, the familial candidate match outcome was also correct and associated the parents to their child.Fig. 2Parentage case study scenario two pedigree tree.Fig. 2

Sibling case studies 1 and 2.

In terms of sibling case study 1, at locus D2S1338 the DNA profiles shared no alleles between the siblings. Similarly, at locus D19S433 for sibling case study 2, no alleles are shared. Table 5 below illustrates the DNA comparison for the two case studies. Furthermore, the two loci in the DNA profile where the sibling does not share alleles for both case studies are highlighted. In a sibling relationship it is possible that there may be no alleles shared in a particular locus. In a case study done it was concluded that limited information can be obtained when siblings do not share alleles at polymorphic heterozygote locus [12].

In both case studies it was observed that there are three and six homozygote loci occurring in the DNA profiles of the two UHRs, respectively (see Table 5). This could have influenced the familial searching outcome because the UHRs inherited the same allele from each parent, which indicates population substructure meaning the parents could also be genetically related. It is noteworthy that in both case studies, the actual biological relatives were ranked as potential familial matches at positions 813 and 470, respectively. These results illustrate that, if these been true familial searching cases, identifying the correct biological relatives would have been highly challenging due to the large number of higher-ranked potential candidate matches. Based on our findings, we postulate that familial searching using the NFDD-ZA may not be effective when both the target and candidate profiles represent siblings. Furthermore, the samples in these cases studies were typed with a STR kit that contains only 15 STR's, which could have also reduced the effectivity of the familial searching outcome.

Sibling case study 3, which include a tri-allele at TPOX

A solved identity sibling case study includes a sibling who has a tri-allele at TPOX locus genotype 9:10:11 (see Table 6). When the familial search in CODIS was done utilising the sibling as the known, no familial matches were generated. When the UHR profile was utilised, it resulted in five hundred and twenty-three familial candidate matches, but the known sibling was not one of the familial matches generated. Parent offspring kinship LR were obtained when the heterozygote genotypes 9:10/9:11/10:11 and homozygote genotypes 9 and 10 was used for TPOX loci in statistics calculations. A sibling kinship LR was obtained when a homozygote genotype 11 for TPOX was used in calculations. Based on our findings, we postulate that if a biological relative has a tri-allele in their DNA profile, the likelihood of identifying a missing person or unidentified human remains in the DNA database will most likely not yield a positive familial searching outcome in CODIS. The occurrence of tri-alleles at a single locus can be observed in phenotypically normal individuals. There are basically two types; first, when there is an unbalanced peak-height ratio of the alleles; and second, when there is a balanced peak-height ratio of the alleles [13]. Sibling case study 4, which includes non-sharing of alleles at two loci

In this case study, the sibling does not share alleles at two loci namely D21S11 and D2S1338 (see Table 7). When this occurs limited information can be obtained, furthermore when the sibling DNA profile was utilised as the known identity during the familial search, more than a thousand ranks were generated, and the UHR was ranked 343rd, which makes it not suitable for familial searches Utilising the UHR as the known resulted in the sibling being ranked 96th. In 15 cases, the only known DNA profile was the UHR and in 18 cases, only the biological relatives were contained. These individuals were DNA typed with both AmpFlSTR Identifiler Plus® Kit and Global Filer™ STR kits. The total number of case studies used for this study was 33. From the analysis of these cases, it produced both parent-offspring and sibling familial candidate matches, and there were more than a hundred possibilities for each case study. Even familial candidates that match ranked 10th had likelihood ratios that were reportable. None of the case studies identified a familial candidate match in just one province. The occurrence of familial candidates in more than one province is operationally significant. First, geographic dispersion of candidates suggests true kinship signals rather than localised database artefacts, reinforcing that the investigative lead is not confined to a single jurisdiction. Secondly, multi-province appearance is consistent with internal migration, dispersed family networks, or offender mobility, all, which widen the plausible search radius for the missing individual and any putative relatives. Thirdly, cross-provincial concordance provides an immediate cue for inter-jurisdictional coordination (e.g., aligning case records, travel histories, arrest/conviction data, and biometric holdings) and prioritization of resources where candidate densities are highest. Finally, the fact that even rank-10 candidates yielded reportable likelihood ratios indicates a deep ‘signal tail’ in the candidate list, supporting expanded, rather than narrowed, follow-up when candidates are distributed across provinces.

From a technical perspective, the pedigree search was configured to report matches at fifteen loci, ensuring backward compatibility with the AmpFlSTR Identifiler Plus® STR Kit (15 autosomal STRs) while the target reference profiles were typed with Global Filer™ STR Kit (21 autosomal STRs). This harmonisation reduces kit-related false negatives: profiles originally generated on AmpFlSTR Identifiler Plus® STR Kit remain eligible for reporting thresholds, yet any subsequent re-typing on Global Filer™ STR Kit can increase informativeness and refine likelihood ratios. Together, cross-province concordance and kit-compatible thresholds provide stronger, geographically actionable leads than a scenario in, which candidates arise from a single province only.

Use of additional markers

Consistent with international experience, our findings show that casework limited to AmpFlSTR Identifiler Plus® (15-STRs) profiles is suboptimal for familial searching: candidate lists inflate, and the ranks of both the UHR and putative relative are unstable, limiting investigative utility [[17], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. This insufficiency of the limited STR kit chemistries (e.g., 15-STRs kit) arises from the large number of familial candidate matches generated and the unstable ranking of both the UHR and the putative biological relative within those candidate lists. The number of familial candidate matches generated in our case studies that had profiles with only fifteen STRs, were in the order of several hundred. This high number of candidate familial matches is not a practical and feasible to communicate to an investigating officer to perform elaborate investigative work. For each candidate match that has an acceptable likelihood ratio the investigating officer will have to investigate even if it is a false positive match as proven by paternity case study 1. Secondly, when a UHR has a familial match with a known individual's DNA profile in the database, a confirmation buccal sample needs to be obtained from the biological relative, which could be a tedious process for the investigating officer due to the several familial candidate matches identified. In addition, the findings demonstrated that a high prevalence of homozygote genotypes in an individual's DNA profile can result in false positive familial matches as well as no matches due to limited amount of genetic information available. In addition, the findings demonstrated that a high prevalence of homozygous genotypes in an individual's DNA profile can result in false positive familial matches or, conversely, no matches at all due to the limited discriminatory power of such profiles. The occurrence of three and six homozygous loci observed in two of the UHRs, respectively, may reflect reduced genetic diversity or population substructure within the reference population, which can influence allele frequency distributions and matching probabilities [25,26].

Jurisdictions that routinely employ familial searching mitigate these issues by using larger autosomal STR panels and, where appropriate, lineage markers (20.22). In the UK, the NDNAD familial-search service operates under formal protocols and guidance; similar, codified procedures exist in parts of the USA (e.g., California SDIS familial-search MOU) and in Australia via ANZPAA-NIFS guidance, each emphasising expanded marker sets and staged follow-up rather than reliance on legacy 15-locus overlaps. Operationally, moving from 15 to 21–24 autosomal STRs (e.g., GlobalFiler™) reduces false positives and shortens candidate lists; adding Y-STRs further refines paternal-line assessments. Where resources permit, NGS panels (e.g., ForenSeq MainstAY or DNA Signature Prep) enable simultaneous analysis of 27 A-STRs plus 25 Y-STRs (and optional X-STRs/SNPs), improving separation of relatives from unrelated while maintaining database compatibility.

Homozygosity

Although moderate levels of homozygosity are expected in small or endogamous populations, unusually high homozygosity across multiple loci can complicate kinship analyses, familial searching and affect the statistical confidence of the forensic DNA match results. The inheritance of identical haplotypes from a common ancestor creates long tracts of homozygous genotypes known as runs of homozygosity (ROH). ROH patterns show the degree of autozygosity and kinship, which are both diminished by globalisation and human mobility. In many Southern African groups, admixture between ancestries reduces long ROH, while recent cousin marriages increase them; large South African and sub-Saharan studies show this pattern [27]. All populations, including admixed or outbred populations, have ROH, which are caused by two distinct processes: consanguineous unions (mating between relatives) and a limited effective population size [28]. While longer ROHs indicate higher levels of consanguinity and more recent parental relatedness, short ROHs are typical of admixed populations that resemble ancient parental relatedness [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. ROHs can result via inbreeding [32,33], the founder effect [34], or consanguineous marriages, which are thought to affect about 10 % of people worldwide [35]. ROHs can also be “runs of hemizygosity,” which occur when one chromosome is deleted, resulting in a loss of heterozygosity [36].

Several mitigation strategies can be implemented to manage the impact of elevated homozygosity on familial searching: (i) compute familial likelihood ratios using population-structure-aware genotype probabilities by incorporating a co-ancestry parameter θ [37] and, where warranted, an individual inbreeding coefficient F; this reduces LR inflation under substructure and inbred backgrounds [[37], [38], [39]]; (ii) Expand autosomal marker sets and, where policy permits, add microhaplotypes and SNP panels selected for high aggregate heterozygosity and robust laboratory performance; microhaplotypes exhibit high polymorphism and no stutter artefacts, thereby increasing information content for relationship tests [[18], [40], [41], [42]]. Well-balanced, high-information panels improve discrimination between close relatives (e.g., siblings) and unrelated individuals compared with traditional STR-only sets [42,43]; (iii) Maintain population-stratified allele-frequency databases aligned to the NFDD-ZA user base and avoid undifferentiated “global” pools, with regular review against observed database patterns [23,44,45,46].

Tri-alleles

The presence of tri-alleles can result in zero familial candidate matches being identified if this information is not available prior to search being conducted in the database. Tri-allelic genotypes can drive familial likelihoods to zero under strict bi-allelic matching [[47], [48], [49]]. We therefore recommend a mitigation protocol: profiles with >2 called alleles at any locus should be automatically flagged for review; tri-allelic loci should be masked from the initial familial search and likelihood computation, with the combined likelihood renormalised over the remaining loci; and a dual-pass strategy should be implemented comprising a standard masked search and a permissive pass that allows a single-locus incompatibility or an extra allele at loci known to be tri-allele-prone [[44], [50], [51]]. Putative tri-allelism should be confirmed by replicate testing and, where feasible, retyping on an expanded autosomal STR kit, with confirmed events recorded in the database metadata [52]. Shortlisted candidates can then be resolved using adjunct marker systems (Y-STRs, mtDNA, or additional autosomal loci). This workflow minimises avoidable missed matches while preserving auditability and scientific defensibility

False familial candidate matches

Familial searching case studies that contained pedigree with only trees with Global Filer™ STR kit profiles resulted in fewer familial candidate matches, thereby reducing the number of false positives [22]. This will also assist the investigating officer when performing follow-up investigations. In single parent pedigrees (i.e., maternity or paternity kinship the biological relative was identified as the first familial candidate match). Familial matches identified resulted in various generation of relatives being identified such as half siblings (2nd) and cousins (3rd). Thus, it can be postulated, the additional six STR's available in this kit will have a positive impact when familial searching is conducted in NFDD-ZA through the CODIS module.

To maximise the discriminatory power of familial searching and to reduce the number of candidates matches generated, it is essential to use DNA marker kit chemistry that contains a minimum of 22 STR markers (21 autosomal STRs and 1 Y-STR). However, to further enhance the effectiveness of familial searching within the NFDD-ZA, a DNA marker kit incorporating a greater number of autosomal STRs together with additional Y-STRs should be validated and verified within the Forensic Science Laboratory. The inclusion of more autosomal STRs increases the power of discrimination across the population, thereby reducing false positive outcomes, while additional Y-STRs provide greater resolution in paternal lineage analysis. For example, the Promega's PowerPlex® Fusion 6C System has two additional autosomal STRs namely Penta D and Penta E and two additional Y STRs namely DYS576 and DYS570 that may be used classical capillary electrophoresis workflow. Alternatively, a single Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) workflow can be utilised. For example, the Verogen ForenSeq MainstAY Kit amplifies 27 autosomal STRs and 25 Y-STR markers, while the ForenSeq DNA Signature Prep Kit includes 27 autosomal STRs, 24 Y-STRs, X-STRs, as well as identity-informative, phenotype-informative, and ancestry-informative SNPs within a unified workflow. These kits provide comprehensive panels covering CODIS, ESS, and other internationally standardised loci, offering powerful discriminatory capabilities for forensic and genealogical/familial searching. NGS therefore delivers multilayered forensic intelligence that extends well beyond the capacity of standard CE-based kits.

Conclusion

Familial searching in NFDD-ZA is permitted only for the identification of missing persons and unidentified human remains (MPUHR) under the DNA Act. Known biological relatives are essential for such searches. Although having both biological parents maximises kinship informativeness, this is often infeasible in South Africa; in such cases, a single parent with a sibling can still be highly informative. The degree of relatedness between the MPUHR and the reference(s) governs discriminatory power and the size of candidate familial lists. Sibling-focused searches typically yield more candidate matches, increasing downstream investigative workload and triage demands.

Strategies to support familial searching should prioritise reference and target profiles typed at ≥21 autosomal STRs, with Y-STR support where patrilineal information is relevant. Where appropriate, genealogical leads and adjunct marker systems (e.g., additional autosomal loci, Y-STRs/X-STRs, or SNP-based panels) can further refine relationship inference and reduce non-informative leads.
