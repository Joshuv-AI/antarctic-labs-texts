Placental pathology and its association with duration of abnormal fetal heart tracing in near‐term and term infants with hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy: A retrospective study
Authors: Áine Fox, Emma Doyle, Adam Reynolds, Maria Farren, Rocco Cuzzilla, Michael Geary, Breda Hayes, Claire M McCarthy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSpecific fetal heart rate (FHR) patterns are associated with fetal compromise. Several placental histopathological findings are associated with neonatal morbidity and adverse neurodevelopment. The relationships between intrapartum FHR patterns and placental histopathology in infants with hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) remain uncertain. This study hypothesized that in infants with HIE, placental histopathology findings are related to abnormal FHR patterns.Material and MethodsThis was an observational study performed on a historical cohort from a single tertiary neonatal intensive care unit. Infants were born at ≥36 weeks' gestation with moderate or severe HIE between September 2006 and December 2021. Placental histopathology was assessed by a perinatal pathologist using the Amsterdam Working Group Criteria. FHR, recorded from intrapartum cardiotocography (CTG) was assessed by an obstetrician using a structured proforma. Assessors were blinded to clinical course and outcomes. Duration and type of FHR abnormality were compared across categories of placental histopathology findings.ResultsFifty infants with moderate or severe HIE (with complete data relating to intrapartum CTG and placental histopathology) were included. Increased duration of FHR abnormality was associated with the presence of histological chorioamnionitis (HCA) (p = 0.013) but not with other placental histopathological lesions. There was no evidence of associations between placental histopathology findings and specific FHR features on intrapartum CTG.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates an association between HCA and increased duration of CTG abnormality in infants with moderate or severe HIE. Intrapartum CTG alone is limited in its ability to identify the presence or absence of placental histopathology. Multimodal intrapartum assessment, such as CTG with placental NIRs or Doppler assessment, may improve the maternal–placental–fetal triad assessment. This could lead to improved intrapartum risk stratification and infant outcomes. Future research should investigate the utility of multimodal assessment to improve risk stratification in labor.Keywords: hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, intrapartum cardiotocography, neonatal encephalopathy, placental pathology

Key message

Histological chorioamnionitis is associated with a longer duration of non‐reassuring and abnormal fetal heart rate patterns for infants with moderate and severe hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy. The assessment of fetal heart rate during labor shows limited capability in identifying placental abnormalities in this cohort.

INTRODUCTION

Hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) continues to be a leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality, with a significant proportion of survivors having adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. Most infants affected by HIE present with encephalopathy in the immediate postnatal period. Of those with moderate or severe encephalopathy, approximately one‐third have an intrapartum sentinel event identified as the cause. The cause remains largely unknown for the remainder. 1

The maternal–placental–fetal (MPF) triad is the foundation for fetal growth and development. Fetal development can be impaired, with the potential to cause injury if there is an issue with one of these elements or how they interact together. 2

HIE is a consequence of hypoxia–ischemia from impaired perfusion or gas exchange in the antenatal and/or intrapartum period. This is affected by the placenta's ability to increase perfusion and gas exchange in the setting of hypoxia–ischemia. 3 When perfusion or gas exchange is insufficient, a fetus will attempt to compensate by reducing energy expenditure and increasing cardiac output. Ultimately, if hypoxia–ischemia persists or is profound, a fetus will decompensate, leading to brain injury and/or death. This process can be observed through fetal heart rate (FHR) patterns on cardiotocography (CTG) monitoring. 4 The evidence to support the use of intrapartum continuous CTG to assess fetal well‐being is sparse. 5 Research has demonstrated its use to be associated with reduced neonatal seizures, without improvement in neonatal neurodevelopmental outcomes, risk of death, or incidence of cerebral palsy. On the other hand, CTG is associated with increased rates of intrapartum intervention.

There have been many studies in recent years demonstrating an association between an increased incidence of placental histopathology in infants with HIE. 6 , 7 However, there is limited research investigating the relationships between intrapartum CTG and placental histopathology. Two studies demonstrated a potential association between pathological CTG features (including reduced variability and absence of accelerations) and various placental histopathologies. 8 , 9 In order to improve the use and interpretation of CTG, further evaluation of these relationships as well as knowledge development, could help further improve intrapartum risk stratification. The objective of this study was to determine if placental histopathology findings are associated with FHR patterns on intrapartum CTG in a population of near‐term and term infants with moderate or severe HIE. This study hypothesized that in near‐term and term infants with moderate or severe HIE, placental histopathology findings are related to abnormal FHR patterns.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

We conducted a single‐center retrospective study of infants born at ≥36 weeks' gestation, admitted with moderate and severe HIE between September 2006 and December 2021. This institution has a birth rate of approximately 8000 deliveries per annum, with an incidence of HIE between 1 and 2.5 per 1000 births per annum over the study period. Infants were diagnosed with HIE by the attending neonatologist based on the clinical history, laboratory results, and neonatal condition at birth. The grade of encephalopathy was determined using the Sarnat score and amplitude‐integrated electroencephalography (aEEG). Infants were eligible for inclusion if they had moderate or severe HIE 10 and had complete data relating to intrapartum CTG and placental histopathology. Infants were excluded if they were found to have an alternative cause for encephalopathy other than hypoxia–ischemia (for example, structural brain anomaly, genetic abnormality, and/or metabolic disease).

Infants' parents consented to the retrospective collection and analysis of data but were not otherwise involved in this study.

Main outcome measures

Rates of FHR abnormality, and duration of FHR abnormality, were compared across categories of placental histopathology findings, including normal and abnormal. Abnormal placental histopathology categories included: (1) histological chorioamnionitis (HCA); (2) fetal vascular malperfusion (FVM); (3) maternal vascular malperfusion (MVM); (4) villitis of unknown etiology (VUE); and (5) delayed villous maturation (DVM).

Cardiotocography assessment

Software for CTG recording changed during the period of study. Infants born prior to November 2017 had their CTG recorded on Athena Health Record software (K2 Medical Systems Ltd., Plymouth, UK). In November 2017, the hospital changed to an electronic health care record and a new CTG software programme, Fetalink software (Cerner Corporation, Kansas City, MO, USA; https://healthinnovationnetwork.com/wp‐content/uploads/2019/04/Fetalink‐user‐guide.pdf).

CTG was assessed by one reviewer (Consultant Obstetrician, CMcC) using a structured proforma. CTG was anonymized and assigned a random study number. The reviewer was blinded to clinical course, stage of labor, and interventions in labor. Time of birth was made available to the reviewer so that they could determine time blocks for assessment.

CTG was assessed using the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline, “Fetal monitoring during labour,” updated in 2022. This classification is in line with the Irish guideline, “National Clinical Practice Guideline: Fetal Heart Rate Monitoring”. 11 Assessment of contraction frequency was performed over 15 min. Where a CTG had a contraction frequency of more than 7 in 15 min, an additional assessment of contraction frequency over 10 min was performed. Where this contraction frequency exceeded 5 in 10 min, it was classified as hyperstimulation.

Where CTG was available, it was examined up to 12 h prior to delivery. CTG was assessed in 1‐h blocks with the exception of the hour before delivery, which was assessed in 15‐min blocks. The structured proforma included assessment of CTG features and a summary assessment of each time block (Figure S1). The summary assessment classified CTG according to the NICE guideline 12 and was assigned by the reviewer based on the assessment of the individual features on CTG. CTG assessment included assessment of baseline FHR, variability, presence of accelerations, presence of decelerations, predominant type of deceleration, presence of prolonged deceleration, presence of bradycardia, contraction frequency, and interpretation of CTG as normal, suspicious, or pathological (Figure S1).

Cardiotocography statistical analysis

CTG was analyzed for each time block. This dataset was heterogeneous. As outlined above, continuous intrapartum CTG is not routinely used in labor. The duration and intervals of CTG recording differed for each maternal‐placental‐fetal (MPF) triad. Each CTG characteristic (baseline FHR (reassuring, non‐reassuring, or abnormal), variability (reassuring, non‐reassuring, or abnormal), accelerations (present or absent), decelerations (present or absent), contractions (normal, hyperstimulation, or not in established labor), conclusion (normal, suspicious, or pathological)) were analyzed for each available time block of 1 h (or 15‐min blocks in the hour prior to delivery). An additional binary variable was created from this analysis, and the conclusion was normal or abnormal (abnormal was defined as suspicious or pathological). A summary conclusion was also assigned to the hour before birth based on the segments of time available (Table S1).

Reliability testing

Inter‐rater and intra‐rater reliability were assessed for CTG assessment. Kappa coefficient, K, was used for assessing reliability. This was tested for CTG conclusion classification “reassuring,” “suspicious” or “pathological,” and “normal or abnormal” for each time period that was scored. This was done as part of a larger study looking at infants with HIE and a group of healthy control infants.

Inter‐rater reliability testing

A second reviewer (Consultant Obstetrician, MF) scored 43 CTGs for inter‐observer reliability assessment, resulting in 313 time blocks being assessed by two reviewers.

Intra‐rater reliability testing

The primary reviewer scored 21 CTGs a second time, resulting in 158 time blocks being re‐assessed by one reviewer. These CTGs were given a new random study number and were mixed with other CTGs for assessment. The reviewer was unaware that they were rescoring previously scored CTGs.

Placental analysis

Placentas were collected at the time of delivery, and slides were archived after clinical histological analysis. They were retrieved from storage for review in this study and reviewed by a perinatal histopathologist (ED). The reviewer was blinded to the clinical course of infants, including the grade of encephalopathy. Birthweight and gestation were made available to the reviewer for the purpose of accurate placental assessment. Macroscopic placental findings were collected from historical histology reports after the slides were reviewed.

Placental histology

A standardized proforma for placental assessment was used for analysis, including the following macroscopic parameters (Table S2): placental weight (PW), birthweight to placental weight ratio (BW:PW), and coiling index (CI). Placental histopathology assessment included the presence and severity of HCA, FVM, MVM, VUE, and DVM. Placental histopathology was classified as per the Amsterdam Placental Workshop Group Consensus Statement 2016. 13

Due to small numbers of placentas with each grade and stage of placental lesions, grade and stage were recorded but not used for statistical analysis. For statistical analysis, each placental pathology was classified as a binary variable (present or absent). In those placentas with HCA, the presence or absence of fetal response (FR) was also assessed. An additional binary variable was created from the above analysis, namely, the presence or absence of abnormal placental pathology. 6 This variable does not reflect the number of pathologies present or the severity of each pathology.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata SE Version 17 (StatCorp, College Station, TX, USA). For summary statistics, continuous normally distributed variables were reported as means and standard deviation (SD), and continuous non‐normally distributed variables were reported as medians and interquartile range.

A chi‐squared test was used to compare categorical variables where the cell count was greater than 20. Fisher's exact test (two‐sided test) was used for categorical variables where the cell counts were less than 20. A two‐sided T‐test was used to assess the relationships between continuous normally distributed variables and a binary variable. The Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used to assess the relationship between continuous non‐normally distributed variables and a binary variable. Statistical significance was set at a p‐value < 0.05.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that there are associations between abnormal FHR on intrapartum CTG and placental histopathology findings in near‐term and term infants with moderate or severe HIE.

There was a relationship between increased duration of abnormal CTG and HCA. This was also apparent for infants with any placental pathology; however, this was due to the influence of HCA on this variable (Table 3). It is important to note that the group with HCA had a higher median duration of CTG recording compared to the full cohort. Therefore, this difference in the duration of CTG may have had an impact on this result. Abnormal CTG has been found to be linked to HCA in other studies. Kovo et al. 14 studied 213 women who delivered their infants by emergency cesarean section due to non‐reassuring CTG and found those with lower cord pH (pH 7.2) had a higher incidence of HCA.

Tarvonen et al. 15 examined a retrospective 20‐year birth cohort study including 314 cases of HIE. They found a higher prevalence of HCA in cases with a normal admission CTG; however, they overall demonstrated that placental histopathology was more common in “non‐avoidable” HIE cases. In contrast to the current study, they demonstrated a higher frequency of FVP/MFM in cases with abnormal CTG tracing.

In the current study, there was no evidence of other relationships between summary CTG characteristics or specific CTG features (decelerations, accelerations, variability, and baseline heart rate) and placental histopathology findings.

This study also found no relationship between the presence of placental pathology and mode of delivery (Table 4). Mode of delivery is influenced by many factors (including maternal and fetal factors, as well as intrapartum events); thus, this finding is not unexpected. However, this does differ from associations found with mode of delivery and chorioamnionitis in other studies. The differences may be explained by the differences in each cohort group. This study reports on a cohort of infants with moderate and severe HIE who did not have evidence of clinical chorioamnionitis, whereas other studies reporting increased rates of cesarean section with chorioamnionitis reported on a group with clinical chorioamnionitis and were not isolated to infants with HIE. 16

Of note, the group in this study had a high incidence of both abnormal CTG (76%) and placental pathology (86%); however, these rates are similar to those in other studies. 17 , 18

The results of this study demonstrate that CTG abnormalities for infants with moderate or severe HIE are not associated with specific categories of placental pathology (FVM, MVM, HCA, VUE, or DVM) and, in isolation, are not useful for the prediction of placental histopathology. Consecutive abnormality (>1 h) on CTG may be a potential indicator of placental histopathology in fetuses who later present with moderate or severe HIE. Where consecutively abnormal intrapartum FHR characteristics are recognized, adjunctive measures (including fetal blood sampling (FBS) or fetal scalp stimulation) may be beneficial. The absence of a statistically significant relationship between abnormal intrapartum FHR and placental histopathology, particularly FVM and DVM, highlights the ongoing concerns regarding the limitations of CTG in the assessment of at‐risk MPF triads. This study demonstrates that the presence or absence of placental histopathology cannot be routinely identified on intrapartum CTG using the NICE guidelines.

The relationship between placental histopathology and prolonged CTG abnormality must be interpreted with caution, as this study has exclusively examined a group of infants with moderate or severe HIE. A study by Kühnert and Schmidt 19 demonstrated differences in antenatal CTG features between MPF triads with placental insufficiency compared with normal pregnancies, including a reduction in the number of accelerations and an increase in episodes of fetal tachycardia and bradycardia. This would suggest that the hypoxic intrapartum period can place additional stress on an already compromised MPF triad, leading to more exaggerated CTG features. This is supported by our finding that an increased number of abnormal hours of CTG and consecutively abnormal CTG (>1 h) increases the risk of finding any positive placental histopathology. There was no statistically significant relationship between a single placental histopathology and CTG abnormality. This could be due to the relatively small number of each placental histopathology represented in this study.

CTG monitoring is the main modality used for intrapartum fetal surveillance and remains at the center of many intrapartum fetal assessment programs, including the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (RCOG) new “Avoiding Brain Injury in Childbirth (ABC)” program. This program aims to detect fetal compromise earlier in labor through the use of meticulous intrapartum fetal surveillance, including the use of CTG. 20

As clinicians strive for improved outcomes for infants, new modalities for assessment are also being considered. Through the utilization of other modalities with CTG (for example, biomarkers of placental function), algorithms could be created to aid the identification of MPF triads with an increased risk of placental impairment.

Ultrasound Doppler analysis is commonly used to monitor the progress of MPF triads that are identified as at‐risk 21 , 22 through suboptimal fetal growth or disorders of placental implantation, including pre‐eclampsia. Doppler assessment of the umbilical artery (UA), middle cerebral artery, ductus venosus, aortic isthmus, and cerebroplacental ratio has been shown to be a useful measure for assessing compromise in fetuses with IUGR. 21 UA Doppler surveillance has been shown to reduce perinatal death by 34% in high‐risk pregnancies 22 ; in particular, the antenatal ductus venosus Doppler waveform has been shown to be a surrogate marker of fetal acidemia in high‐risk pregnancies. 21 However, the use of these parameters in labor is not established. Kauppinen et al. 23 conducted a study of 160 post‐term (>41 weeks) MPF triads assessing Doppler measurements of the placenta and CTG pre‐induction. They found that the combination of these two pre‐induction assessments could not determine which fetus would have an unfavorable outcome. A fetus exposed to intrapartum hypoxic–ischemia often develops changes on its CTG. Changes may also be present on intrapartum umbilical vessel Doppler measurement. 24 , 25 The implementation of Doppler assessment in these scenarios is challenging but may provide additional information on fetal status.

In the last decade, the use of non‐invasive near‐infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) monitoring has increased. 26 There is some evidence to support its use as a mode of assessing placental oxygenation. 27 Ražem et al. conducted a study investigating the effectiveness of placental NIRS for identifying intrapartum fetal hypoxia and subsequent acidosis. 28 Their study of 43 healthy pregnancies found intrapartum placental deoxygenations were associated with acidosis (pH < 7.2). They found that placental NIRS was better at predicting neonatal acidosis than intrapartum CTG. Nguyen et al. 29 used NIRS to investigate placental oxygenation (n = 12) and found a relationship between placental pathology and lower placental oxygen levels. This provides further evidence that placental pathology likely leads to suboptimal oxygenation of the fetus, potentially resulting in poor tolerance of labor. Like fetal blood sampling and fetal scalp stimulation, new modalities as described above are worthy of further study and may enhance the interrelation of intrapartum CTG, facilitating improved identification of an at‐risk MPF triad.

Detailed analysis of placenta histopathology and intrapartum CTG (up to 12 h before delivery) has not been performed before. Reviewers for each modality were blinded to the clinical course. This study adds valuable knowledge to the relationship between placenta and CTG in the setting of moderate and severe HIE.

The retrospective design of this study led to heterogeneity in the data available with missing data, likely limiting the study's ability to find relationships between specific CTG abnormalities and placental histopathology. Continuous intrapartum CTG monitoring is not standard for all pregnancies in this institution. This led to variable durations of monitoring and potential selection bias, as continuous intrapartum CTG monitoring is reserved for those with an identified risk factor.

Although there appears to be a relationship between some CTG features and placental pathology, it is limited. This could be due to small numbers in each group, making it difficult to find an association.

The low incidence of HIE (1–3/1000 births) in High Income Countries (HIC) creates difficulties performing studies on HIE and often necessitates the inclusion of longer study periods. In the current study, infants born over a prolonged period of time (i.e., 15 years) were included, during which clinical practice changes could have influenced clinical management and outcomes. The addition of a matched control would have been beneficial in the study. As placental histology is not routinely performed on healthy infants, it was not possible to obtain a matched control group. Another arm of this study looked at placental histopathology for infants with HIE and a healthy control group. 6 This control group was recruited for this study and was born at a different time period from the infants with HIE, leading to potential bias in reviewers' assessment of both CTG and placentas.

CONCLUSION

The findings of this study demonstrate an association between placental pathology and the duration of CTG abnormality, specifically consecutive periods of CTG abnormality, in infants with moderate or severe HIE. This adds to the limited knowledge of the relationships between these entities. Placental histopathology has the potential to increase a fetus's risk for HIE. CTG alone is limited in its ability to identify placental histopathology; however, using it with other modes of MPF assessment may improve the identification of high‐risk pregnancies. This could lead to improved intrapartum risk stratification and infant outcomes.
