CRISPR Technology Applied to the Analysis of Antibiotic Resistance Genes in the Environment
Authors: Jiayin Yan, Xiaoxian Yuan, Zijun Zhu, Zhuang Li, Minghuo Wu, Jing Liu, Lizhong Zhu, Wei Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Environmental transmission of antibiotic resistance poses a risk to human health. Understanding the behavior and health risks of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in the environment requires accurate nucleic acid analyses. Technical challenges remain in the sequencing of ARGs in low abundance relative to total environmental DNA and in on-site analysis. CRISPR technology has been applied to the characterization and monitoring of ARGs in various environmental media. For sequencing of ARGs, CRISPR technology has enabled the enrichment of ARGs present in the environment at a very low abundance. Using CRISPR/Cas9 systems with a pool of guide RNAs (gRNAs) could simultaneously enrich a large panel of ARGs in one environmental sample before sequencing for good coverage and reading depth. CRISPR/Cas9 also enabled targeted long-read sequencing for understanding the genetic context of low-abundance ARGs. CRISPR-based rapid detection has the potential for on-site analysis. Current applications include the analysis of antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) and ARGs in the air and the urban water cycle. CRISPR technology is also useful for differentiating single-nucleotide mutations between antibiotic-susceptible and -resistant variants. Rapid detection of antibiotics together with ARGs is also possible with the use of aptamers that specifically bind to antibiotics.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance is one of the major global public health threats in the 21st century, with an estimated global burden of millions of associated annual deaths in 2050. Antimicrobial resistance existed in the environment before the clinical application of antibiotics. The overuse of antibiotics on humans and animals provides selection pressure and thus increases the relative abundance of resistant bacteria. Meanwhile, Antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) spread and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) horizontally transfer within the “One Health” framework. Municipal wastewater treatment plant (WWTP), pharmaceutical manufacturing effluents, animal farms, aquaculture facilities, and manure-amended soils are hotspots of ARB and ARGs. , Human exposure to and infection of antibiotic resistant pathogens causes adverse clinical outcomes.

Antibiotic resistance can be categorized as intrinsic resistance and acquired resistance. Intrinsic resistance is an inherent trait of a bacterial species, arising from structural or functional characteristics, such as impermeable cell walls or innate efflux pumps. Acquired resistance, which is the primary driver for the spread of resistance in the environment, results from genetic alterations and includes two main mechanisms: (1) chromosomal gene mutations (e.g., induced by antibiotic selection pressure or oxidative stress) and (2) the horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of antibiotic resistance genes from other organisms. , Understanding the environmental behavior of ARGs, reducing their generation and replication, and preventing their transfer to clinically significant pathogens require the qualitative and quantitative study of ARB and ARGs in the environment, distinguishing single-nucleotide polymorphisms, and obtaining the genetic context and hosts of the ARGs. Such characterization of the ARGs and ARB in the environment primarily relies on sequencing techniques and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) techniques. Rapid sample preparation and isothermal nucleic acid amplification techniques are promising alternatives for on-site analysis because they could be conducted by using portable devices and do not rely on a thermal cycler. The limitations and challenges of isothermal amplification techniques include false positives, inhibitors, and amplicon contamination.

CRISPR techniques have recently been applied to advancing both rapid detection and metagenomic sequencing (by enriching low-abundance genes) of ARGs in the environment, on the basis of their previous success in diagnostics. CRISPR techniques used in combination with isothermal amplification overcome the limitation of isothermal amplification in specificity, allow different types of readouts, and are field-deployable. − CRISPR-based target nicking before sequencing increased the depth of sequencing target genes in samples containing higher amounts of nontarget nucleic acids. These techniques leverage the important properties and functions of CRISPR/Cas systems, including the specific recognition of target nucleic acid sequence with a guide RNA and the nuclease activity of the CRISPR-Cas ribonucleoprotein after its binding to the target. ,, Analyzing different targets only requires altering the spacer region (∼20 nt) within the guide RNA to be complementary to the target sequence. However, challenges remain in accurately analyzing low-abundance ARGs in complex environmental matrices. In this focused review, we aim to highlight what traits of CRISPR technology are adaptable to meet the special needs of environmental ARG analyses. We summarize the fundamental principles of different CRISPR-based techniques for the analysis of antibiotic resistance and demonstrate the role of CRISPR-Cas systems in different techniques. We compare the technical performance required for analyzing ARGs in different environmental matrices and the capabilities of available techniques, to highlight the need to advance preanalytical and analytical techniques.

CRISPR-Mediated Enrichment of Low-Abundance DNA in Environmental Samples for Sequencing

Metagenomics enables the characterization of the antibiotic resistome (the collection of all ARGs) in the environment, and provides information on the source, host organism, transfer, and health risk of ARGs. ,− However, ARGs only account for a small portion of the total DNA extracted from environmental samples. , Thus, it is challenging to accurately characterize the resistome using the available sequencing techniques. A meta-analysis of data from 150 publications has demonstrated global ARG baselines in the environment. qPCR quantification of ARGs in 1594 samples across 30 countries and 12 sample types shows that a relative abundance of ARGs to the 16S rRNA gene is 10–5–10–3, and that some ARGs in the first quartile have relative abundance as low as 10–8 relative to the 16S rRNA gene. Analysis of ARGs at such a low abundance requires high sensitivity and reading depth.

CRISPR techniques allow the enrichment of the DNA of interest before sequencing and have been used for clinical analysis of rare variants related to disease and antimicrobial resistance sequences. − Schultzhaus et al. have discussed different CRISPR enrichment strategies, including the depletion of abundant and nontarget DNA through cleavage, the sequence-specific cleavage and gel purification, target nucleic acid concentration through the sequence-specific binding of CRISPR systems to the target and separation, and the locus-specific cleavage for the fusion of adapters to primarily the DNA of interest.

The environmental analysis of resistance from both point mutations and horizontal gene transfer could benefit from adopting such sequencing strategies. The diverse background DNA in high concentrations in environmental samples makes targeted sequencing of ARGs useful but also challenging. Although the sequencing with CRISPR treatment is targeted, the sequencing adapter can also be added to nontarget sequences if there is random DNA breakage, resulting in a low fraction of on-target reads. Off-target effects on genomes of environmental microbes should be considered when designing gRNA. Meanwhile, proper DNA extraction techniques should be used according to the sample type, considering the high complexity of the environmental matrices. An additional step of inhibitor removal was also included in environmental analyses.

Target-specific cleavage for adding adapters to the DNA of interest has been applied to the analyses of ARGs and integrons in wastewater, sediment, and soil. ,, The enrichment of ARGs before Illumina or nanopore sequencing is to first block all the sequences from sequencing adapter ligation by removing the 5′ phosphate. ,,, Therefore, sequencing of nontarget DNA is restricted. Then, CRISPR/Cas9 systems introduce double-strand breaks at ARGs, and only the freshly generated ends with 5′ phosphate can add to the sequencing adapter for sequencing (Figure ). The CRISPR/Cas9 system consists of a Cas9 protein and a guide RNA. , The guide RNA is the complex of a tracrRNA and a crRNA, and could also be engineered and connected to be a single guide RNA (sgRNA) molecule. The 5′ end of the guide RNA is a single-stranded region, i.e., spacer region, of 20 nt that should be designed according to the target DNA site. A PAM region (e.g., NGG) next to the 20-nt target site is required for target binding. The CRISPR/Cas9 system targets the DNA through the recognition of PAM and the base pairing between the guide RNA and the target DNA. After binding to its target, the CRISPR/Cas9 system cleaves both strands of target DNA. The cleavage generates two blunt ends. After the cleavage, CRISPR/Cas9 remains on the PAM-adjacent end and releases the PAM-distal end.

Enriching Amplification of ARGs before Illumina Sequencing

The strategy of enriching a large panel of ARG sequences in the sample before Illumina sequencing is to design a large set (thousands) of guide RNAs based on the ARG databases. After the CRISPR/Cas9 cleavage at ARGs and adapter ligation to ARGs, the ARGs with adapters are amplified using PCR, achieving target enrichment before metagenomic next-generation sequencing. This strategy is termed finding low abundance sequences by hybridization (FLASH). FLASH provides up to 5 orders of magnitude target enrichment and a subattomolar limit of detection. The first demonstration of its application is for the detection of low-abundance ARGs in respiratory fluid and dried blood spots.

Such CRISPR-based target enrichment increased both the number of ARGs detected in wastewater and the read depth (Figure A). DNA was extracted from filtered wastewater influents. DNA extracts were further purified to remove the PCR inhibitors. The initial dephosphorylation of DNA prevents DNA from connecting to sequencing adapters, reducing the interference from nontarget DNA in the wastewater sample. A total of 6010 gRNA FLASHit-generated targets all 4641 ARGs in the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) protein homologue model (over 6000 ARGs listed as of December 2025). Each gRNA contains a 20-nt fragment that is fully paired with the target sequence. The gRNA-Cas9 complexes target ARGs in massive genome DNA and form a stable complex through base-complementary pairing. This binding activates the nucleic acid endonuclease activity of Cas9 and cleaves the target DNA at the target sites. The fresh-cut target DNA fragments carry the phosphate group at the end for adapter ligation and amplification and are sequenced. Such CRISPR-Cas9-modified next-generation sequencing detects up to 61 more ARG families or 1189 more ARGs in wastewater compared to the regular NGS method. The strategy has a higher sensitivity in the detection of low-abundance target genes, reducing the detection limit of target ARGs from 10–4 to 10–5. Data from CRISPR-based target-enrichment metagenomic sequencing also guide the design of qPCR primers and probes for ARG surveillance.

Enrichment of target ARGs before Nanopore Sequencing

Nanopore Cas9-targeted sequencing (nCATS) uses Cas9-guided adapter ligation for targeted nanopore sequencing and achieves an enhancement of sequencing coverage by dozens to hundreds of folds. The technique allows for the study of nucleic acid sequences and epigenetic modifications. One demonstration of its environmental application is the sequencing of integrons and the genetic context in oil-contaminated coastal sediments.

On the basis of the Nanopore Cas9-targeted sequencing for cell and tissue samples, and Fuhrmeister et al. developed a strategy of CRISPR/Cas9 targeted, long-reading sequencing of ARGs and genomic context, Context-Seq (Figure B), for the analysis of blaCTX-M and blaTEM containing sequence in fecal samples. They first spiked blaCTX-M-55 and blaTEM-1 genes into a composite sample of DNA extracted from Kenyan soil, and used this sample together with DNA extracted from E. coli to optimize the method. Phosphates at the 5′ end of all DNA are first removed, so the sequencing adapter cannot be connected to the DNA. CRISPR/Cas9 cleaves blaCTX-M and blaTEM genes at positions close to either end of each gene. The Cas9 cleavage generates a fresh end with 5′ phosphate for adapter ligation. Target sequences were directly sequenced. Sequences upstream and downstream of blaCTX-M and blaTEM were analyzed. Different design of the guide RNA pairs resulted in different enrichment performance. Digesting Cas9 after its cleavage at target gene sites with thermolabile proteinase K enhanced the assay performance. The optimized Cas9 cleavage during the sample preparation of nanopore sequencing allowed an enrichment of 7 – 15-fold coverage over nontargeted method. Such targeted sequencing allowed the study of bla CTX‑M and bla TEM genetic context and transmission dynamics. Sequences of ARG-surrounding regions were clustered and polished to generate one consensus sequence per cluster. Mobile elements, co-occurring genes, and host bacteria were analyzed using the consensus sequences. Fuhrmeister et al. envision further research using Context-Seq to analyze soil and water samples for the investigation of antimicrobial resistance. This method requires relatively high DNA input (1.5–3.0 μg). Therefore, the analysis of ARGs in low-biomass matrices may require extensive concentration of DNA.

CRISPR Technology for Rapid Detection of Environmental ARGs

Rapid and portable methods for on-site ARG monitoring are crucial to safeguard public and environmental health. Although qPCR provides accurate concentration measurements, the need for thermal cycling limits its applicability for on-site detection. Isothermal nucleic acid techniques and CRISPR-based analytical techniques have demonstrated their potential for on-site analysis and contribute to the screening of infectious diseases in resource-limited areas. ,

Absolute concentrations of representative environmental ARGs in the air, water, and soil obtained from real-time quantitative PCR or digital PCR provide guidance on the choice of preanalytical techniques and analytical techniques for efficient sensitivity in rapid analysis. When the target gene copy number is low, e.g., several to dozens of copies per sample, or aM level in a typical 20–100 μL detection reaction, isothermal nucleic acid amplification is combined with CRISPR for accurate detection. The amplification ensures the sensitivity. The use of CRISPR-Cas systems allows signal generation and high specificity. For example, isothermal amplification first generates millions of amplicons. CRISPR/Cas13 , recognizes RNA amplicons. Cas12 , systems recognize DNA amplicons. Distinct from CRISPR/Cas9 systems, after binding to their target, Cas13 and Cas12 also possess nonspecific nuclease activity, i.e., trans-cleavage activity. After target recognition, Cas13 and Cas12 cleave multiple reporters (e.g., fluorophore and quencher labeled single-stranded nucleic acid) and generate amplified signals. The efficiency of signal amplification is limited by the kinetic parameters of the Cas enzymes. The typical limit of detection when using CRISPR/Cas13 and Cas12 systems and regular fluorescence detection without nucleic acid amplification is around high fM to low pM of target in the CRISPR/Cas reaction. ,

Application of CRISPR Technology to the Rapid Detection of ARGs in Various Environmental Compartments

Integrated isothermal nucleic acid amplification and CRISPR techniques allow sensitive analysis of ARB and ARGs in air, water systems, − and potentially contaminated surfaces , (Table ). Sensitive detection requires a proper choice of sampling volume, sampling techniques, target concentration, extraction techniques, and nucleic acid assays according to environmental concentration and matrices. From CRISPR-based diagnostics to CRISPR-based environmental analysis, although established nucleic acid detection techniques can be adapted to ARB and ARG analyses by designing primers and gRNA to be specific to target ARB and ARGs, preanalytical procedures are different for diverse matrices. Advances in both preanalytical and analytical techniques are necessary for potential on-site analysis.

Comparison of CRISPR-Based ARG Detection Techniques

LOD refers to the concentration of target in the detection reaction, if not specified.

Toward on-site analysis of airborne ARB, a CRISPR technique uses an electrostatic sampler, a portable isothermal amplification device, and integrated CRISPR/Cas9 nickase (Cas9n) and rolling circle amplification (RCA). Airborne bacteria are collected through the electrostatic sampler into PBS buffer solution and then centrifuged. DNA is extracted with a kit. The single-tube Cas9 nickase-triggered amplification reaction (CN-TAR) starts with the binding of sgRNA-Cas9 nickase complex to the target gene, allowing Cas9n to cleave one strand of the target. The nicked free end then allows the circular RCA probe to bind. RCA reaction extends the 3′ end and generates long repetitive single-stranded DNA. These amplification products are designed to form G-quadruplex structures, which enables the intercalation of Thioflavin T (ThT) for fluorescence generation.

The application of CRISPR technology for rapid ARG detection in water samples has also been reported. One significant example of CRISPR-based water analysis is the detection of sul1, qnrA-1, mcr-1, and class 1 integrons (intI1) in the urban water cycle with one-tube integrating recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA)-CRISPR/Cas12a method (Figure B). Tap water, hospital wastewater, influent and effluent of WWTPs, and rivers are filtered first before nucleic acid extraction. Sediment, sludge, and livestock farm wastewater samples are usually centrifuged first before filtration. The DNA extract is then added to the RPA-CRISPR reaction. RPA is an isothermal amplification technique that amplifies the target gene exponentially at around 40 °C within minutes to dozens of minutes. Sequence-specific CRISPR/Cas12a interacts with the amplicon and trans-cleaves fluorescence reporters. Although the initial RPA-CRISPR assay requires opening the tube after amplification, different strategies have been designed for single-tube RPA-CRISPR assays. In the ARG detection, Cheng et al. used suboptimal PAM when choosing the CRISPR targeting location within the ARGs. PAM recognition is the first process of CRISPR/Cas12a binding to its target. Optimal PAM is the TTTV motif next to the target region, where V stands for non-T nucleotides. CRISPR/Cas12a binding to targets with suboptimal PAM is slow. Therefore, the cleavage of amplicons is slower than the generation of amplicons, allowing the exponential amplification to continue. This integrated one-tube RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a method detects several aM ARGs in 20 min. Rapid analysis of ARGs showed that sul1 and intI1 were present in all types of water samples, while qnrA-1 and mcr-1 were mainly detected in wastewater influents. Such observation was further confirmed by qPCR analysis. The absence of qnrA-1 and mcr-1 in WWTPs effluent, sludge, or rivers may suggest that the two low-abundance genes were degraded during the wastewater treatment.

One-step assays integrating recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) with CRISPR/Cas12a have also been developed for rapid and sensitive detection of blaNDM. Performance was tested using spiked surface water and wastewater samples. Alternatively, loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) is the other often used exponential amplification technique. It operates at temperatures around 60 °C. LAMP-CRISPR/Cas12a with fluorescence and lateral flow readout enabled the detection of ermB in wastewater.

Different strategies have also been developed for the analysis of contaminated surfaces. ,, One example is a stepwise RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a–Au-Fe3O4 nanozyme colorimetric assay for the detection of kanamycin resistance, ampicillin resistance, and chloramphenicol resistance genes in surface swab samples. Another example is an RPA-CRISPR/Cas12b single-tube assay for the detection of mcr-1 and tet­(X4) on different surfaces at meat-selling markets.

Rapid Differentiation of Single-Nucleotide Resistance Mutation

Point mutation is also an important mechanism of antibiotic resistance. Distinct from the analysis of horizontally acquired ARGs, the detection of antibiotic resistance from point mutations requires discrimination at single-nucleotide resolution. It also requires the detection of mutants within a large excess of the wild-type background. Mutation identification relies on sequencing, but the detection of known single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) can also be done with nucleic acid amplification and detection techniques such as PCR with single-mutation-sensitive probes. Nucleic acid hybridization tolerates mutations in the middle. Low reaction temperatures or the use of long primers/probes in isothermal techniques make hybridization stable and difficult to differentiate single mutations. Special design for amplification primers and probes is required. CRISPR systems are able to differentiate SNPs or single-nucleotide variations (SNVs) and have been used for clinical samples. Such differential targeting and activation have allowed the sequencing of rare mutations with the high-abundance wild-type nucleic acid and the differentiation of pathogen variants. Further applying the current discrimination strategy to analyze antibiotic resistance bacteria in the environment requires a high discrimination factor and proper preanalytical techniques.

One application of the CRISPR technique on resistance mutation is NASBA-ARMS-CRISPR-Cas13a assay for the differentiation of quinolone-resistant S. enterica from quinolone-sensitive ones. Quinolone-resistant S. enterica contains a single-nucleotide mutation in the gyrA gene compared to wild-type (Figure ). Bacterial RNA was extracted and then analyzed by AlleRNA assay. Nucleic acid sequence-based amplification (NASBA) is an isothermal RNA amplification technique that proceeds through cyclic enzymatic steps: the binding of reverse primer target mRNA and reverse transcription to produce complementary cDNA; the digestion of RNA on cDNA with RNase H; the binding of forward primer containing a T7 promoter to the cDNA to generate a double-stranded DNA template; and T7 RNA polymerase transcription and production of RNA amplicons. Allele-specific amplification refractory mutation system (ARMS) uses primers that match perfectly with mutant-type gyrA cDNA, but mismatch with wild-type gyrA for one nucleotide at the 3′ end of the primer. Therefore, NASBA amplifies mutant gyrA mRNA from viable quinolone-resistant S. enterica, but not wild-type gyrA mRNA from quinolone-susceptible S. enterica. The resulting mutant RNA amplicons subsequently hybridize with crRNA and activate Cas13a. Cas13a indiscriminately cleaves reporter RNA labeled with a fluorophore and a quencher, producing a detectable fluorescence signal. Such a strategy is able to detect down to 0.5% mutant in wild-type. mRNA only exists in viable bacteria that actively express the target gene. The detection of mRNA shows the drug resistance of bacteria, instead of the total amount of intercellular and extracellular resistance genes obtained in DNA analyses. The detection of mRNA also enabled the study of bacterial viability under the exposure to quaternary ammonium salt, 75% ethanol, and peracetic acid. Such viability information cannot be provided by DNA analyses. Further application of this strategy to environmental analysis should take into account the relative abundance of resistant bacteria in mixed microbial communities containing highly abundant WT bacteria and any potential inhibitors or RNase in the matrices.

Rapid Detection of Antibiotics Together with ARGs

Antibiotics and ARGs coexist in environmental systems. Antibiotics used in humans and animals have expanded the reservoir of ARGs in the environment. Traditional methods usually analyze antibiotics using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The use of aptamers also contributes to the rapid detection of antibiotics in different matrices. , Aptamers are single-stranded DNA or RNA that can bind to a target molecule specifically. Aptamers are available for β-Lactams, aminoglycosides, chloramphenicols, (fluoro) quinolones, lincosamides, tetracyclines, and sulfonamides antibiotics.

Several assays based on nanomaterials aim for the analysis of both antibiotics and ARGs in parallel. − One example is colorimetric and photothermal detection of azithromycin and the ermB gene using Fe–C–N@Au-aptamer, complementary DNA-modified streptavidin magnetic beads (SMB-cDNA), CRISPR/Cas12a, and 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrates (Figure ). Azithromycin binds specifically to its aptamers modified to the surface of Fe–C–N@Au through Au–S bonds (Figure A). Azithromycin binding to aptamers competes with the hybridization of aptamers to SMB-cDNA. Therefore, in the presence of azithromycin, some Fe–C–N@Au-aptamers are free from SMB-cDNA. Fe–C–N@Au-aptamer with azithromycin stays in the supernatant during magnetic separation. Fe–C–N@Au-aptamer in supernatant is then added to TMB and H2O2. The catalytic reaction allows colorimetric detection or photothermal detection under near-infrared. Fe–C–N is also able to degrade azithromycin. For ARG detection, ermB activates CRISPR/Cas12a to trans-cleave the DNA linker of Fe–C–N@Au-DNA-SMB, releasing nanozymes Fe–C–N@Au to catalyze the TMB-H2O2 reaction and produce noticeable colorimetric and photothermal changes. The signal intensity is positively correlated with the abundance of the resistance gene. The reported sensitivity for both azithromycin and ermB is at a low pM level. The authors also analyzed tap water, river, and lake water spiked with azithromycin and ermB.

Likewise, a smartphone-integrated biosensor uses Fe3O4 with polydopamine layer and cDNA (FP@cDNA) nanoparticles, Prussian blue nanoparticles with zirconium 1,4-dicarboxybenzene MOF and MnO2 on the surface (PB@UiO-66@MnO2) nanozyme composites, CRISPR/Cas12a for fluorescence, colorimetric, and photothermal detection of ampicillin and bla TEM . Alternatively, a multimode detection platform uses FP@cDNA for magnetic separation and MOF-818@PtPd (MPP) nanozymes as catalysts for the detection of sulfamethazine and sul1 in spiked environmental samples. ROX-aptamer binds to sulfamethazine. FP@cDNA can remove the free ROX-labeled aptamer. The fluorescence of the ROX-aptamer binding to sulfamethazine reflects the concentration of sulfamethazine. sul1 activates Cas12a to cleave the single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) linking the MOF-818@PtPd nanozymes and Fe3O4@PDA. Therefore, the free MOF-818@PtPd in the supernatant catalyzes the colorimetric and photothermal reaction. Alternatively, sul1-activated Cas12a cleavage of the phosphate-modified single-stranded DNA (P-ssDNA) on the electrode prevents the assembly of MOF-818@PtPd onto the electrode through the P-ssDNA, allowing electrochemical detection of sul1 down to the femtomolar level. The application of these techniques with different nanomaterials for analyzing real-world environmental samples could be limited because of the complexity of material preparation and inadequate sensitivity.

Perspectives

CRISPR techniques have contributed to different aspects of the analysis of the environmental ARGs. CRISPR-based ARG amplification enables the targeted sequencing of thousands of known environmental ARGs with enhanced coverage. CRISPR-based enrichment of ARGs for long-read sequencing also enables studies of the genetic contexts of low-abundance ARGs. CRISPR-based rapid detection techniques have been applied to the environmental analyses of ARB and ARGs. Advanced CRISPR techniques and preanalytical techniques successfully used in other settings could be further adapted for the monitoring of ARGs or understanding the evolution and transmission of antimicrobial resistance.

The integration of isothermal amplification and CRISPR-Cas systems provides sufficient sensitivity for environmental analysis but mostly provides information on ARG presence or possibly semiquantitative measurements of ARG amounts. End point reading or CRISPR detection after the completion of isothermal amplification is qualitative. With standardized workflow and calibration curves, isothermal amplification with real-time fluorescence measurement could be semiquantitative, so could the integrated single-tube isothermal assay with CRISPR. Quantitative isothermal amplification often involves a digital platform. To convert the concentration of ARGs in each reaction to the concentration in the sample and in the environment, it is also necessary to have spiked controls going through the same preanalytical process and to measure the recovery for calibration.

Preanalytical technology matters in the analysis of ARGs in different environmental matrices. Although the discussed application is primarily for water analysis, with proper sample-processing techniques, the current analytical technique could be adapted for soil analysis. Even for conventional qPCR techniques, sample collection, concentration, and nucleic acid extraction impact the accuracy of environmental surveillance. ,− The compatibility of preanalytical techniques with isothermal amplification and CRISPR technologies is still worth investigating. Preanalytical techniques remove inhibitors of nucleic acid amplification and detection. PCR, RPA, LAMP, RCA, and CRISPR techniques operate at different temperatures and use different proteins, probes, and buffering systems. These techniques are distinct in their tolerance of inhibitors in the matrices. − Lysis of microbes during sample preparation is required for the detection of intracellular ARGs. Isothermal nucleic acid techniques operate at temperatures lower than those of PCR, the temperature and reaction buffer may not lyse microbes efficiently. Without lysis, only extracellular ARGs are detected. The concentration of nucleic acid during sample preparation helps with improving the analytical sensitivity. On-site analysis requires rapid preanalytical and analytical techniques. − Simplifying the matrix removal, lysis, and concentration procedure would make the overall detection more feasible for on-site analysis.

The presence and even the quantity of ARGs do not directly reflect the health risk. ,,− Many of the resistances are from horizontal gene transfer between organisms. ARGs in human pathogens pose more challenges to public health than those in nonpathogenic environmental bacteria. Rapid techniques mostly show the presence of a gene without showing its host. Sequencing provides host information on chromosomal genes, but analyzing the host of plasmids is challenging. This is because the plasmids are not on the same DNA molecule as the phylogenetic markers, and the ARGs on plasmids could transfer between different microbes. Assays that allow the identification of the host of ARGs are required. Strategies include imaging, single-cell separation before analysis, and linking the plasmid with the host genome. − We envision the contribution of the CRISPR technology to this end.
