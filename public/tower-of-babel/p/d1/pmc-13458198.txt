High-pressure-resistant totally implantable venous power ports for improving image quality in contrast-enhanced computed tomography
Authors: Hong Deng, Yanjun Xu, Yanli Peng, Yifan Wang, Shuli Feng, Jiale Zeng, Xingping Tang, Yuanfang Liu, Bingjia Lai, Yun Su, Zehong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) is essential for cancer screening, staging, and surveillance, but achieving optimal image quality requires the administration of high-pressure contrast injection via a reliable venous access. Peripheral intravenous catheters (PIVC) have significant limitations in oncology settings, including pain, extravasation risk, and contrast pooling that degrades image quality. Totally implantable venous power ports (TIVPPs) offer a central venous route resistant to high-pressure injection, yet quantitative evidence of their impact on image quality metrics remains scarce. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of TIVPP-based contrast injection on image quality and safety in chest CECT for oncology patients.MethodsIn this retrospective fixed-order sequential study, 195 oncology patients underwent two chest CECT scans at a tertiary medical center between August 1, 2022, and July 31, 2024. Each patient first received contrast injection via a PIVC and then via a TIVPP. Objective image parameters, including signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), and CT attenuation values, were measured. Subjective image quality scores, the presence of artifacts, and complication rates were compared. Statistical analyses were performed via paired t-tests and McNemar or Fisher exact tests.ResultsThe time interval between the PIVC and TIVPP examinations was 131.0±26.2 days (median 128 days; range 71–180 days). Contrast extravasation occurred in 3.1% of examinations performed with PIVCs but in none performed with TIVPPs (P=0.030). Compared to PIVCs, TIVPPs provided significantly higher aortic attenuation (405.50 vs. 363.50 HU; P<0.001), SNR (28.32 vs. 26.45; P=0.005), and CNR (25.83 vs. 21.24; P<0.001). Pulmonary vein image quality was rated as excellent in 100% of TIVPP examinations, higher than the 93.4% of PIVC examinations (P<0.001). Visualization of the subclavian artery was markedly superior with TIVPPs (97.4% excellent) compared with PIVC (0%; P<0.001).ConclusionsTIVPPs provide a safe, reliable, and diagnostically superior route for high-pressure contrast injection in chest CECT for patients with cancer. They maintain significantly superior objective and subjective image quality compared to peripheral venous access, with no contrast extravasation. However, the fixed-order sequential design might have introduced temporal confounding, which should be considered in the interpretation of our findings.

Introduction

The incidence and mortality rates of malignant tumors have increased, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide and constituting a major global health challenge (1). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) is a key imaging modality for the screening, diagnosis, staging, and postoperative surveillance of cancer (2). Achieving optimal vascular and tissue enhancement in CECT requires the high-pressure injection of iodinated contrast medium, making reliable intravenous access critical for diagnostic success (3).

Contrast media are conventionally administered via peripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs). However, PIVCs involve certain critical limitations, particularly for oncology patients who require repeated imaging within short intervals. Repeated venipuncture causes pain and discomfort, and increases the risk of phlebitis, extravasation, and vascular injury (4-6). Furthermore, high-pressure injection via PIVCs may also lead to contrast pooling in peripheral lymphatic regions, especially in patients with chemotherapy-associated lymphadenopathy, such as those with breast or lung cancer. This pooling can produce artifacts that obscure key vascular structures and reduce diagnostic accuracy. In thoracic imaging, beam-hardening artifacts from PIVC-based injection can markedly degrade the visualization of mediastinal or pulmonary vessels (7). In addition to venous access, CECT image quality is strongly influenced by scan timing and patient-tailored contrast administration strategies. Bolus tracking techniques enable real-time triggering of image acquisition based on contrast enhancement in a target vessel, thereby optimizing peak vascular and parenchymal enhancement (8). Furthermore, individualized contrast media dosing—based on body weight, lean body weight, or body mass index (BMI)—has been shown to reduce interpatient variability in hepatic and vascular enhancement, improving diagnostic consistency (9,10). In contrast, central venous access has been shown to optimize contrast distribution, reduce stagnation, and provide more consistent bolus profiles (8,11,12). These limitations related to peripheral intravenous access underscore the need for a safer, more stable, and diagnostically superior contrast injection route in oncology imaging.

Totally implantable venous power ports (TIVPPs) are subcutaneous devices widely used for long-term chemotherapy administration in patients with cancer. Compared to PIVCs, TIVPPs offer reduced trauma, easier maintenance, fewer complications, greater patient comfort, and improved safety (5,13). Crucially, TIVPPs provide a central venous route that is resistant to high-pressure injection, thereby minimizing the risks of extravasation and dislodgement (6,12). This direct access shortens the injection pathway, reduces peripheral contrast retention, and enhances the imaging clarity of the thoracic vessels and lymph nodes. In one study, contrast injection via a central port in treatment-naïve esophageal cancer patients notably reduced pooling in the subclavian vein and produced clearer tumor margins as compared with peripheral routes (10). Additionally, a prospective study involving 98 patients with implanted power ports reported that a substantial proportion of CECT scans were performed via TIVPPs without device malfunction or contrast extravasation (14). These findings affirm the technical feasibility and safety of using TIVPPs for CECT. However, quantitative evidence demonstrating improvements in objective image quality metrics, such as signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), and artifact reduction, remains scarce.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the efficacy of high-pressure-resistant TIVPPs for CECT imaging in patients with malignant tumors. Specifically, we compared TIVPPs with PIVCs in terms of image quality, safety, and injection efficiency, using an intraindividual before-and-after design in which each patient served as their own control. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0241/rc).

Methods

Patients

This study was a retrospective, nonrandomized, fixed-order before-after analysis conducted at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University from August 1, 2022, to July 31, 2024. During the study period, a total of 425 oncology patients underwent CECT via PIVCs at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Of these, 277 patients subsequently underwent a CECT scan via a TIVPP. After the inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied, 82 patients were excluded due to incomplete clinical or imaging data (n=43), different contrast injection protocols between the two examinations (n=25), or poor image quality due to motion artifacts (n=14). The remaining 195 adult patients with malignant tumors who had previously received chemotherapy and underwent two CECT examinations were included. Each patient served as their own control: contrast agent was administered first via a PIVC during the initial examination and then via a TIVPP during the second examination (Figure 1). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) pathologically confirmed malignant tumors; (II) completion of two chest CECT examinations under comparable contrast injection and scanning protocols, with the first examination performed via a PIVC and the subsequent examination performed via a TIVPP; (III) age ≥18 years; and (IV) availability of complete clinical and imaging data. Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) incomplete clinical or imaging data; (II) different contrast injection protocol between the two examinations (e.g., different contrast dose, injection rate, or iodine concentration); and (III) poor image quality due to motion artifacts or other technical factors. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (No. SKSKY-2022-315-03), and is registered in the National Medical Research Registration and Information System of China (No. MR-44-23-017761). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians.

Contrast injection and CECT scanning protocols

For examination via PIVCs, contrast was administered through a 20-gauge PIVC (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Contrast injection was performed via the right upper extremity in most patients (n=183; 93.8%), while the left upper extremity was used in 12 patients (6.2%) due to poor right-sided venous access. Patency was confirmed with a 10-mL saline flush performed prior to injection. Patients were observed for 30 minutes postexamination to monitor for adverse events.

For examination via TIVPPs, patients with an implanted TIVPP (model No. 8806061; 6F ChronoFlex, Bard Access Systems, Salt Lake City, UT, USA) underwent standardized port maintenance before scanning. This power-injectable port has a recommended maximum flow rate of 5 mL/s and a pressure tolerance of 325 psi. In all patients, the TIVPP catheter was inserted via the right internal jugular or right subclavian vein, with the catheter tip positioned at the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium. After blood return was confirmed, the port was flushed with 10 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride, which was followed by the contrast-enhanced scan according to institutional protocols. As with the PIVC protocol, patients were observed for 30 minutes after scanning.

All examinations were performed with a dual-source, dual-energy SOMATOM Force CT device (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a high-pressure injector (MEDRAD, Bayer Healthcare, Berlin, Germany). Scans extended from the thoracic inlet to the diaphragm. Parameters included automatic kilovolt selection, automatic tube current modulation, a detector collimation of 192×0.6 mm, a slice thickness of 1 mm, and a reconstruction interval of 1 mm. Ioversol Injection (350 mgI/mL), a nonionic iodinated contrast agent, was administered at a weight-based dose of 1.0–1.2 mL/kg (total 60–90 mL), corresponding to an iodine dose of 350–420 mgI/kg. The injection rate was determined individually based on the patient’s peripheral venous condition, ranging from 2.5 to 4.0 mL/s. For the majority of patients, the injection rate was 3.0–3.5 mL/s, while for patients with poor venous access, it was 2.5 mL/s. The injection duration varied accordingly. A 30-mL saline flush was administered at the same rate immediately after contrast injection. For each patient, the same contrast dose, iodine dose, and injection rate were used for both the PIVC and TIVPP examinations to ensure comparability. Arterial phase acquisition was triggered by bolus tracking in the thoracic aorta, with scanning initiated 5 seconds after 100 HU was reached.

Image analysis

Complete blinding was not feasible because the venous access type (PIVC vs. TIVPP) could be identified on the images based on the presence of residual contrast in peripheral veins (in PIVCs) or the characteristic catheter tip position (in TIVPPs). To minimize bias, region-of-interest (ROI) measurements were performed with standardized anatomical landmarks, and subjective scoring was conducted independently by two radiologists using predefined criteria without knowledge of the study hypothesis.

Two imaging physicians (Y.S. and B.L., with 10 and 5 years of experience, respectively) performed the ROI measurements. A circular ROI (maximum 200 mm2) was placed in the thoracic aorta and the adjacent muscle on arterial-phase images, with calcifications, cystic changes, or partial volume effects being avoided. ROIs were positioned at similar anatomical locations for each examination and measured twice on the same transverse section, with the average value used for subsequent analysis. Observation indicators

Two experienced chest radiologists (Y.L. and Z.Y., with 10 and 19 years of experience, respectively) independently evaluated the image quality and artifacts according to the predefined criteria. The images of vascular injection of contrast agents in patients with indwelling TIVPPs and those with indwelling peripheral venous high-pressure-resistant needles were compared and analyzed. The leading observation indicators were as follows: (I) changes in heart rate before and after contrast injection; (II) CECT image quality, including overall evaluation, liver evaluation, pulmonary artery, and pulmonary vein (1 point: poor; 2 points: fair; 3 points: good; 4 points: excellent); (III) collateral vessels and reflux, including collateral vessels around the shoulder joint, as well as arterial-phase inferior vena cava and hepatic vein reflux (the evaluation scoring refers to the evaluation of the image artifacts); and (IV) image artifact evaluation standard, with the subclavian artery, superior vena cava, and right atrium serving as indicators (1 point: obvious, unobservable; 2 points: significant artifact affecting observation; 3 points: present but not affecting observation; 4 points: no artifacts). For subclavian artery artifacts, TIVPP examinations consistently evaluated the right subclavian artery (all ports were right-sided), while PIVC examinations evaluated the ipsilateral subclavian artery (same side as the injection site). For artifacts related to the superior vena cava and right atrium, evaluation was consistent regardless of injection side.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed with R version 4.4.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The normality of continuous variables was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data with a normal distribution are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and were compared with paired t-tests. Nonnormally distributed data are expressed as the median and interquartile range and were compared with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To determine the potential effect modification and assess the robustness of the findings, we conducted subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Subgroup analyses were stratified by clinically relevant variables. Sensitivity analyses were conducted without specific patient subgroups. Categorical variables are described as the frequency and percentage (%) and were compared between two groups with the McNemar χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. The intraclass correlation coefficient was used to assess the interobserver consistency for parameter evaluation. The interobserver agreement was defined as poor (<0), slight (0–0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), substantial (0.61–0.80), or almost perfect (0.81–1.00). A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

A total of 195 patients (103 females, 52.8%; 92 males, 47.2%) completed both PIVC and TIVPP examinations. The mean age was 54.39±12.27 years. The time interval between the PIVC and TIVPP examinations was 131.0±26.2 days (median 128 days; range 71–180 days). All patients had an interval of ≤6 months (180 days). The mean BMI was 22.62±4.15 kg/m2 for the PIVC examination and 22.65±4.38 kg/m2 for the TIVPP examination, which did not represent a significant difference (P=0.359). The most common primary malignancies were thoracic tumors (48.2%), followed by abdominal (40.0%), tumors (7.2%), and head and neck (4.6%). The mean actual injection rates were 3.29±0.40 mL/s for PIVC examinations and 3.27±0.34 mL/s for TIVPP examinations (range 2.5–4.0 mL/s for both), which did not represent a significant difference between the two examinations (P=0.291). Extravasation rates

Contrast extravasation occurred in 6 patients (3.1%) during PIVC examinations but in none during TIVPP examinations (0%), representing a significant difference (P=0.030) (Table 1).

Heart rate changes

Both injection routes were associated with stable heart rates. The prescan heart rate was 84.29±12.96 bpm during the PIVC examination and 86.20±16.43 bpm during the TIVPP examination (P=0.408), and the postscan heart rate was 84.49±12.20 bpm and 83.10±15.12 bpm, respectively (P=0.328). Within-examination comparisons revealed no significant change for PIVC (P=0.127), whereas a significant reduction was observed in the TIVPP examination (P<0.001) (Table 2).

Objective image quality parameters

Interobserver agreement was good to excellent across all measurements (intraclass correlation coefficient range 0.74–1.00). For the thoracic aorta (arterial phase), CT attenuation was higher for TIVPP than for PIVC examination (405.50 vs. 363.50 HU; P<0.001). TIVPPs had a significantly higher SNR (28.32 vs. 26.45, P=0.005) and CNR (25.83 vs. 21.24, P<0.001) (Table 3, Figure 2). Meanwhile, no significant differences were observed in attenuation or noise for muscle tissue between the two examination types. Finally, sensitivity analysis excluding the 12 patients who received left-sided PIVC injection (n=183) showed consistent results: TIVPP remained significantly superior to PIVC in terms of aortic attenuation (426.3±111.7 vs. 379.9±98.8 HU; P<0.001), SNR (30.6±11.2 vs. 28.3±11.6, P=0.0012), and CNR (28.3±11.6 vs. 23.8±10.4; P<0.001). Subclavian artery artifacts also remained significantly reduced with TIVPP examination as compared to PIVC examination, with an excellent rating proportion of 97.5% and 0%, respectively (P<0.001).

Collateral vessels and reflux assessment

Collateral vessels (including periprosthetic vascular reflux around the shoulder joint) and reflux phenomena (including arterial-phase inferior vena cava and hepatic vein regurgitation) were evaluated (Table 5). No statistically significant differences were observed between PIVC and TIVPP examinations for any of these parameters (all P values >0.05).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis stratified by median time interval (≤128 days: n=98; >128 days: n=97) showed that TIVPP examination was associated with significantly higher aortic enhancement and lower artifact scores in both subgroups (all P values <0.01), with no significant interaction (P for interaction =0.248). Spearman correlation analysis showed no significant correlation between time interval and improvement in aortic enhancement (r=0.007; P=0.925).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that using TIVPPs for contrast injection significantly enhances both safety and image quality in oncologic chest CECT scanning as compared to scans performed with PIVCs. Contrast extravasation occurred in 3.1% (6/195) of PIVC examinations but was absent in all TIVPP examinations. Intra-individual analysis comparing arterial-phase TIVPP examination and PIVC examination revealed higher attenuation values of the thoracic aorta for TIVPP (405.50 vs. 363.50 HU, P<0.001) and a superior SNR (28.32 vs. 26.45; P=0.005) and CNR (25.83 vs. 21.24; P<0.001). The proportion of excellent ratings for the pulmonary vein was significantly higher for TIVPP examinations (100% vs. 93.4%; P<0.001). For the subclavian artery, the TIVPP examination also showed a significantly higher proportion of excellent ratings (97.4% vs. 0%; P<0.001). No significant differences were observed in the image noise or CT values of the muscle between the two examination types.

Safety profile of TIVPPs for high-pressure contrast injection

In this study, TIVPPs demonstrated a favorable safety profile for high-pressure contrast injection, with no extravasation observed in 195 TIVPP examinations, while the rate of extravasation for PIVC examinations was 3.1%. This finding is clinically significant, as extravasation remains a major concern during CECT, which relies on rapid bolus administration via a power injector (16). The major risk factors for extravasation include patient-related conditions such as vascular fragility resulting from long-term radiotherapy or chemotherapy in malignant tumors (17), treatment-related aspects including the osmotic concentration and viscosity of iodine-based contrast agents (18), and procedural factors, particularly high-pressure injection through fragile peripheral veins (19). TIVPP directly addresses these risks by providing a stable, high-pressure-resistant central venous conduit. The TIVPP system, comprising a subcutaneous port chamber with a self-sealing septum connected to a radiopaque central venous catheter, ensures reliable and secure delivery of contrast. Although rare complications of TIVPP, such as catheter migration or injection-induced intrathoracic pressure changes, have been reported (20), none were observed in this study. However, it should be noted that the absence of observed extravasation in this cohort does not necessarily imply a complete elimination of risk, and larger studies are warranted to further establish the safety profile of TIVPP.

Mechanisms of image quality improvement: vascular enhancement

TIVPP significantly improved quantitative image quality parameters, including aortic attenuation, SNR, and CNR, compared to PIVC. The higher vascular enhancement observed with TIVPP can be attributed to several factors: The first is related to the elimination of peripheral dilution. Peripheral intravenous injection via PIVC requires contrast to traverse the upper extremity veins, where it can be diluted by the venous blood flow from collateral vessels and lymphatic return. Central injection via TIVPP bypasses these routes, delivering a more concentrated bolus directly to the superior vena cava (21). The second involves reduced bolus dispersion. The shorter transit time from the injection site (central vein) to the right heart minimizes temporal dispersion of the contrast bolus, preserving its compact shape and achieving higher peak enhancement. This is particularly important for arterial-phase imaging, for which the timing of peak enhancement is critical (22). The third reason involves the absence of injection-related pain. Patients in the PIVC group often experienced discomfort during high-pressure injection, which could cause involuntary movement or altered breathing patterns, potentially affecting bolus tracking accuracy. TIVPP eliminated this issue, allowing patients to remain motionless during the scanning window. The fourth factor is related to consistent flow dynamics. The high-pressure-resistant design of TIVPP ensures stable catheter position during injection, preventing the flow rate variations that may occur with PIVC due to catheter movement or venous collapse. This stability promotes more uniform bolus geometry and consistent contrast distribution.

Mechanisms of image quality improvement: artifact reduction

In addition to enhancement, use of TIVPPs substantially reduced imaging artifacts, particularly beam-hardening artifacts from the subclavian vein and superior vena cava. In this study, excellent visualization of the subclavian artery was achieved in 97.4% of TIVPP examinations but none of the PIVC examinations (P<0.001). This reduction in artifacts can be attributed to two main processes. First, central venous injection eliminates contrast pooling in peripheral veins, which is a major source of streak artifacts in thoracic imaging. Traditional peripheral injection, especially from the upper extremity, is often compromised by artifacts and reflux from adjacent venous structures, which can obscure critical anatomy and diminish diagnostic efficacy (23). Second, the radiopaque catheter material and optimized tip position (at the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium) minimize local beam-hardening effects. By delivering contrast directly into the central venous system, TIVPP minimizes pooling and reduces associated artifacts that typically occur with peripheral injection. This resulted in clearer delineation of critical vascular structures and improved diagnostic confidence (6). Overall, TIVPPs significantly enhanced image quality in this study, particularly for the visualization of key vascular structures, including the thoracic aorta, pulmonary veins, and subclavian arteries.

Comparison with prior studies

Our findings differ from those reported by Goltz et al., who noted greater enhancement with peripheral versus central venous catheters (24). The following differences in imaging protocol may account for this discrepancy: The first is injection rate. Our results underscore the specific advantages of a high-pressure-resistant central venous route. Goltz et al. used a lower injection rate (approximately 1.5 mL/s) compared to that of our protocol (mean 3.3 mL/s, range 2.5–4.0 mL/s). Higher injection rates amplify the advantages of central injection because the contrast bolus is delivered more rapidly, reducing the opportunity for peripheral dilution and bolus dispersion. The second is port design. The port type may influence flow dynamics and artifact generation. Goltz et al. used forearm-placed ports (X-Port, BD Bioscience), while our study used chest-placed ports (PowerPort, BD Bioscience). The longer catheter length and smaller lumen of forearm ports may increase resistance and reduce flow efficiency. The third is scan timing. Goltz et al. used a fixed trigger threshold (120 HU) with a 5-second delay, while we used bolus tracking triggered at 100 HU. Differences in threshold and delay may affect peak enhancement capture. Finally, the fourth factor is patient population. Both studies focused on oncology patients, but Goltz et al. included patients in whom peripheral access had failed, potentially selecting a population with more compromised venous status.

Our results are consistent with more recent studies demonstrating that power-injectable central venous ports provide comparable or superior enhancement to peripheral access when optimized protocols are used (10,14). These findings support the use of TIVPP as a preferred contrast injection route in oncology patients requiring serial CECT examinations. The specific advantages of a high-pressure-resistant central venous route are particularly relevant for this patient population.

Heart rate observation

We observed a modest but significant reduction in postscan heart rate in TIVPP—but not in PIVC—examinations. Although the between-session difference did not reach statistical significance, this finding suggests that central venous injection via TIVPPs may attenuate patient stress or local vascular irritation during injection, potentially leading to more stable physiological responses. Compared with peripheral venous access, central venous access through TIVPPs may reduce injection-related discomfort and mitigate sympathetically mediated stress reactions (25). The resulting stable hemodynamic milieu may support more consistent cardiovascular parameters throughout the scan. These observations suggest that TIVPP may have potential benefits beyond image quality and safety, and thus further prospective investigation is needed to clarify the underlying physiological mechanisms.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its intra-individual paired design, which effectively controlled for interpatient variability and enhanced the reliability of the direct comparisons. Moreover, the sample size of 195 patients provided adequate statistical power to identify significant differences in both safety and image quality endpoints. Despite this, the study involved several limitations that merit consideration. First, the fixed-order sequential design (all patients underwent PIVC CECT before TIVPP CECT) might have introduced temporal confounding, as disease status, body weight, cardiac output, or treatment regimens could have changed between the two examinations. However, the relatively short and uniform interval (all ≤6 months; mean 131 days) mitigates this concern. Although each patient served as their own control, time-related biases cannot be fully excluded. Second, the retrospective, single-center design restricts the generalizability of the findings. Third, the requirement for both PIVC and TIVPP examinations might have led to a skewed selection of patients with more complex disease courses or venous access issues. This also limits the generalizability of our findings to the broader oncology population. However, this selection is inherent to the intra-individual paired design, which prioritizes control of interpatient variability. Fourth, the exclusion of pediatric patients, owing to more stringent safety protocols, limited the applicability of our conclusions to adult populations. Fifth, complete blinding of image readers was not possible because the type of venous access could be visually identified on the CT images. This might have introduced bias into subjective assessments, although we attempted to mitigate it by using standardized scoring criteria and independent reading. Sixth, long-term outcomes such as device-related infections, thrombosis, and patient satisfaction were not assessed. Future prospective multicenter studies with extended follow-up are needed to address these gaps and confirm our observations. Seventh, only 12 (6.2%) patients received left-sided PIVC injection, which precluded a formal subgroup analysis by injection side. However, a sensitivity analysis excluding these patients yielded consistent results. Future prospective multicenter studies with extended follow-up should be conducted to address these deficiencies and confirm our observations.

Conclusions

TIVPPs provide a safe, reliable, and diagnostically superior route for high-pressure contrast injection in oncology patients. Compared with PIVCs, TIVPPs eliminated extravasation, reduced artifacts, and significantly improved objective and subjective image quality. By enhancing diagnostic accuracy and minimizing injection-related complications, TIVPPs offer a valuable alternative for patients requiring serial CECT examinations. Future multicenter prospective studies with extended follow-up are warranted to further validate the long-term safety, clinical benefits, and patient-reported outcomes of this approach.
