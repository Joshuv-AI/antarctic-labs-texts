Long-term stability of the triglyceride-glucose index in cancer patient Cohort
Authors: Yulei Xie, Xin Chen, Liang Xie, Yinxu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a simple surrogate marker for insulin resistance (IR), but its longitudinal stability and clinical predictive value in patients with cancer remain poorly understood. To evaluate whether cancer and anti-tumor therapies affect the longitudinal stability of the TyG index over 24 months and to identify its core influencing factors. This retrospective study analyzed 538 cancer patients from a single-center database (2015–2020) who completed five TyG index measurements over 24 months. Longitudinal stability was quantified using repeated measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). Binary logistic regression models were employed to explore factors associated with different TyG levels. A longitudinal K-means clustering algorithm was used to identify trajectory differences, and multivariable logistic regression models determined the independent predictive factors. The TyG index demonstrated excellent longitudinal stability over 24 months (F = 5.38, P < 0.001; average ICC = 0.890; Pearson r = 0.54–0.71). Binary logistic regression analysis showed that body mass index (BMI) was the core factor independently associated with a high TyG index (Q4 > 8.79). K-means clustering identified three parallel longitudinal trajectories (Stable-Low, Stable-Medium, and Stable-High risk). Multivariable logistic regression models indicated that the high-risk trajectory was significantly associated with BMI (OR = 1.25, 95% CI 1.10–1.41, P < 0.001) and diabetes mellitus, whereas advanced cancer stage (OR = 0.95, 95% CI 0.53–1.70, P = 0.862) and cachexia (OR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.45–1.80, P = 0.767) showed no significant effect. The TyG index in cancer patients exhibited a long-term stable trend over 24 months. Severe IR may represent a metabolic phenotype closely associated with fat accumulation, and it may be independent of catabolic states such as cancer stage and cachexia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54793-6.

Introduction

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, as a simple and highly sensitive surrogate marker for insulin resistance (IR), has been widely recognized for its predictive value in cardiovascular and metabolic diseases1–3. However, the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp test, considered the gold standard for assessing IR, is difficult to apply rapidly in large-scale clinical cohorts due to its complexity and high cost4,5. As a potential diagnostic tool, the TyG index only requires the calculation of fasting triglycerides (TG) and fasting plasma glucose (FPG), offering the advantages of convenience and low cost6,7. Recent studies have shown that the TyG index is not only associated with systemic IR and hyperinsulinemia but also plays a role in oncogenic pathways via insulin-related receptors (such as IGF-1, PI3K, and Akt)8,9.

However, current research on the TyG index in the cancer field still has significant limitations: it relies mostly on single baseline measurements. In reality, the disease trajectory of cancer is complex and accompanied by abnormal physiological and metabolic fluctuations10. On the one hand, the high-consumption state of cancer (such as cachexia) can induce catabolism and malnutrition; on the other hand, frequent iatrogenic interventions (such as chemoradiotherapy and metabolic drugs) may disrupt lipid homeostasis11. In this environment of physiological stress and cancer burden, exploring the reliability of the TyG index in cancer metabolic monitoring and assessing its long-term longitudinal stability is of great clinical value.

This study longitudinally evaluated the stability of the TyG index in cancer participants over 24 months. It also aimed to analyze the independent driving factors of the TyG index and its longitudinal change patterns, providing new scientific insights and clinical reference recommendations for cancer monitoring.

Materials and methods

Participants

Participants in this study were selected from the database of the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College between 2015 and 2025. A retrospective longitudinal cohort design was adopted. Participants were enrolled continuously from 2015 to 2020 and completed a 24-month follow-up. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) pathologically or radiologically confirmed cancer; (3) complete baseline data and five TyG index measurements over the 24-month follow-up. Exclusion criteria were: (1) non-cancer patients (e.g., benign nodules, inflammatory lesions); (2) severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; (3) missing clinical baseline data. Ultimately, 538 cancer patients were included. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee (2025ER655-1) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

TyG Definition and laboratory measurements

Venous blood was collected from participants after an 8-hour fast. Biomarker analysis was subsequently performed at the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College (precision error range: ≤ 1/3 TEa). The formula for calculating the TyG index was: ln[TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL) / 2]12.

Included variables

This study included 13 variables for statistical analysis: age (continuous variable), sex (male/female), BMI, hypertension, diabetes, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, cancer stage (advanced/non-advanced), cachexia, cancer treatment stage (active treatment period/non-active period), and the use of corticosteroids, statins, and metformin.

Sample size calculation

This study used repeated measures with k = 5, an expected ICC of 0.70, and a null hypothesis ICC of 0.50. Under the conditions of a two-sided α = 0.05 and 80% statistical power, the minimum required sample size was 42. A total of 538 cases were included in this study, far exceeding this threshold13. Following the principle of events per variable (EPV) ≥ 10, the high TyG index (Q4, n = 135) model included 13 variables—demographic characteristics, cancer features (stage, cachexia), and pharmacological factors (corticosteroids, statins, metformin, etc.)—which met the modeling requirements and supported the K-means trajectory prediction and multivariable logistic regression model calculations14.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Python 3.14 software, utilizing the statsmodels, scipy, pingouin, and seaborn libraries. Missing values were imputed using K-nearest neighbors (KNN, k = 5); the missing rate for all variables before imputation was < 5%15. Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Participants were stratified and evaluated by TyG index quartiles (Q1-Q4). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (%). RM-ANOVA was used to evaluate the trend of changes in the TyG index16. A two-way random-effects model was used to calculate the ICC to quantify longitudinal reliability17. A Pearson correlation heatmap was used to evaluate the correlation strength across timepoints18. A binary logistic regression model was applied to the TyG index quartiles to analyze the associated factors at each TyG index level19. A longitudinal K-means clustering machine learning algorithm (K = 3, 10 random initializations, maximum of 300 iterations) was used to identify the dynamic evolution trajectories of the TyG index over 24 months20. Subsequently, a multivariable logistic regression model (internally validated with 5,000 bootstrap resamples) was used to analyze trajectory differences, and adjusted for covariates including cancer stage, cachexia status, and metabolic drugs21.

Results

Participant inclusion and exclusion flow

Participants with TG and FPG tests (n = 369,927) between 2015 and 2025 were initially extracted from the database. During the screening phase, participants without simultaneous TG and FPG tests (n = 171,342) and those with missing follow-up time (n = 2,959) were excluded. The remaining participants (n = 693) completed five TyG index measurements (baseline, 1–3 months, 3–6 months, 6–12 months, and > 12 months). In the subsequent eligibility assessment phase, participants with non-cancer diagnoses, severe liver and kidney dysfunction, and missing clinical data (n = 155) were excluded. Ultimately, 538 cancer patients were included in this study. All inclusion and exclusion criteria were pre-defined and were not adjusted during the process, ensuring the stability and reliability of the study results (see Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 538 cancer participants were included. Among them, 338 (62.8%) were male, with an average age of 56.19 ± 12.04 years. Significant metabolic phenotype differences were observed as the TyG index increased. BMI showed a gradient upward trend, gradually increasing from 21.75 ± 2.42 kg/m² in the Q1 group to 23.01 ± 2.47 kg/m² in the Q4 group. The prevalence of diabetes also increased significantly with higher TyG indices, from 0.7% in Q1 to 21.5% in Q4. Additionally, the proportion of females in the Q4 group (49.6%) was significantly higher than in the Q1 group (25.2%). In contrast, the distributions of age, smoking history, and alcohol history were relatively balanced. Notably, the proportion of cachexia showed no statistical difference between the Q1 group (22.2%) and the Q4 group (27.4%) (P > 0.05), suggesting that the distribution of severe IR may be independent of the cancer progression process (see Table 1).

Longitudinal stability of the TyG index

RM-ANOVA results showed that the main effect of time was statistically significant (F = 5.38, P < 0.001), but the generalized eta-squared (Generalized η²) was only 0.003. Despite the metabolic burden of cancer and the interference of anti-cancer treatments, the metabolic baseline did not experience a significant drift. To quantify its reliability at a micro-level, a two-way random-effects model was used to evaluate longitudinal consistency. The ICC results showed that the TyG index exhibited excellent temporal stability: the single measurement ICC was 0.618 (95% CI: 0.58–0.65), while the average ICC across 5 timepoints reached 0.890 (95% CI: 0.87–0.90), confirming that individuals maintained consolidated metabolic characteristics over two years.

To visually demonstrate the dynamic associations at each timepoint, a Pearson correlation heatmap was constructed. The results revealed that the TyG indices across all timepoints were strongly positively correlated (r = 0.54–0.71). Even across the long-term, complex disease interval (baseline vs. >12 months), the correlation coefficient was maintained at a moderately high level (r = 0.54) (see Fig. 2). Furthermore, in the early or advanced stages, the correlation coefficients between baseline and > 12 months were 0.54 and 0.55, respectively, remaining stable. However, when further stratified by treatment stage, the correlation in patients during the recovery period slightly attenuated (baseline vs. >12 months: r = 0.54; 1–3 months vs. >12 months: r = 0.49) (see Supplementary Documents S1, S2). In summary, the TyG index has the capacity to resist the metabolic load of cancer progression and chemoradiotherapy.

Binary logistic regression model of TyG index quartiles

TyG index quartiles (Q1–Q4) were incorporated into a binary logistic regression model, adjusting for covariates such as age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, advanced cancer, cachexia, and history of corticosteroids, statins, and metformin use. The results demonstrated that cancer-related factors showed heterogeneity across different TyG levels: advanced cancer was an independent risk factor in the Q1 group (OR = 1.58, 95% CI 1.03–2.42, P = 0.038), but no significant association was observed in the Q2–Q4 groups (P > 0.05); cachexia lacked independent correlation across all quartile groups (P > 0.05).

Among basic metabolic factors, BMI and sex showed significant independent, yet opposite, effects. In the Q4 group, for every 1 kg/m² increase in BMI, the risk of a high TyG index increased by 10% (OR = 1.10, 95% CI 1.02–1.20, P = 0.019); males had a higher risk than females in the Q1 group (OR = 2.48, 95% CI 1.49–4.15, P < 0.001) but a lower risk in the Q4 group (OR = 0.49, 95% CI 0.30–0.81, P = 0.005). Lifestyle and pharmacological factors showed independent effects only in specific quartile groups. Metformin reduced the risk in the Q1 group (OR = 0.25, 95% CI 0.10–0.66, P = 0.005), smoking was an independent risk factor in the Q2 group (OR = 1.93, 95% CI 1.06–3.52, P = 0.031), and hypertension was a protective factor in the Q2 group (OR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.31–0.96, P = 0.036). No significant independent predictors were observed in the Q3 group. In conclusion, extremely low TyG levels (Q1, TyG ≤ 8.18) are jointly regulated by cancer progression and basic metabolic factors, while extremely high TyG levels (Q4, TyG > 8.79) are primarily driven independently by metabolic factors and have no significant correlation with the clinical characteristics of cancer (see Fig. 3).

K-means clustering trajectory analysis

Based on the K-means clustering analysis, three evolutionary trajectories of the TyG index were identified: “Stable-Low”, “Stable-Medium”, and “Stable-High”. The internal evaluation showed that k = 2 scored highest in mathematical separation (Calinski-Harabasz index = 396.1, Silhouette score = 0.369). However, when the number of clusters increased to k = 3, the Inertia (within-cluster sum of squares) dropped to 375.8, indicating a robust model. Balancing clinical requirements with model complexity and interpretability, the 3-trajectory model was ultimately established. These three trajectories exhibited smooth and parallel characteristics throughout the follow-up period, without crossing or regression to the mean. This result indicates that the TyG index maintains a stable and consistent temporal robustness even in the context of cancer (see Fig. 4).

Verification of determinants for longitudinal metabolic trajectories

Using the low TyG trajectory as a reference, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed. The results indicated that basic metabolic factors had a significant independent effect on trajectory classification. Elevated BMI significantly increased the risk of entering the medium trajectory (OR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.06–1.26, P = 0.001) and the high trajectory (OR = 1.25, 95% CI 1.10–1.41, P < 0.001). Conversely, sex showed an opposite effect, with males having a lower risk of entering the high trajectory than females (OR = 0.46, 95% CI 0.22–0.94, P = 0.035). Hypertension and diabetes also showed independent effects in specific trajectory groups, where hypertension increased the risk of the medium trajectory (OR = 1.91, 95% CI 1.14–3.21, P = 0.014), and diabetes drastically increased the risk of the high trajectory (OR = 10.92, 95% CI 3.85–30.93, P < 0.001).

Advanced cancer stage, active treatment phase, and cachexia had no significant independent impact on trajectory classification (P > 0.05), suggesting that cancer characteristics have a limited effect on the longitudinal trajectories of the TyG index. Regarding medication use, only corticosteroids provided a protective effect for the medium trajectory (OR = 0.58, 95% CI 0.34–0.99, P = 0.044), while other drugs showed no significant association with either the medium or high trajectories (P > 0.05). Overall, the longitudinal trajectories of the TyG index are primarily driven by underlying metabolic factors, while cancer-related factors and certain medications exert limited independent effects. This provides empirical evidence for understanding the long-term changes of the TyG index within individuals and its potential underlying mechanisms (see Table 2).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the 24-month longitudinal evolution patterns of the TyG index and cross-validated the findings using multivariable logistic regression models and K-means clustering results. First, the TyG index demonstrated excellent longitudinal consistency across multiple follow-up timepoints in cancer (average ICC reaching 0.890), resisting the metabolic interference caused by cancer progression and chemoradiotherapy. Second, the high IR state (high TyG trajectory) observed in cancer patients was highly correlated with fat accumulation and intrinsic metabolism, and it appeared to be unrelated to cancer-associated cachexia (P = 0.767).

For a long time, this has been a prevalent controversy and limitation in the field of cancer metabolism: cancer is highly consumptive, and the widespread use of chemoradiotherapy and corticosteroids easily induces iatrogenic glucose and lipid metabolic disorders22,23. However, most existing studies on the TyG index have been restricted to a single baseline measurement, which profoundly limits its value in long-term disease monitoring23. In this study, both RM-ANOVA and heatmap analyses collaboratively confirmed that although cancer patients undergo complex disease progression over two years, their lipid and glucose metabolic baselines do not experience substantial systemic drift. The exceptional individual stability of the TyG index implies that a high TyG level is not a transient metabolic decompensation triggered by rapid cancer deterioration. Instead, it is an “intrinsic metabolic phenotype” anchored by biological mechanisms, capable of resisting cancer burden and metabolic interference24. After digging into the core underlying associations using the K-means clustering model, we found that BMI and diabetes are the core factors associated with this “consistently high-risk” metabolic trajectory, whereas cachexia, which represents the extreme consumption of cancer, had no predictive power for the trajectories. Given the observational nature of this study, causality cannot be definitively concluded. Nonetheless, these findings suggest that chronic low-grade inflammation induced by visceral fat accumulation and insulin receptor transduction impairment may form a solid “metabolic memory” within the body25. Even in cases where cancer heavily depletes muscle and adipose tissue, the established IR state cannot be reversed. In other words, these associations indicate that the high TyG state in cancer patients is more a continuation of previous metabolic characteristics rather than a direct product of the current cancer burden.

Our research indicates that the TyG index not only possesses the universality of an “immediate” biomarker but can also be measured at any anti-cancer treatment stage without being disturbed by chemoradiotherapy or cachexia. Furthermore, by “decoupling” IR from cancer consumption, this study identified a high-risk subpopulation (the consistently high trajectory group) driven by metabolic dysfunction. This provides a reference target for “metabolism-oncology” interventions: regardless of cancer stage, adopting metabolic regulation strategies (e.g., metformin, SGLT-2 inhibitors)26,27 could potentially reduce continuous stimulation of pathways like IGF-1/PI3K/Akt, serving as one of the adjuvant means to improve cancer prognosis28,29. In addition, the TyG index offers advantages such as rapidity and simplicity, and its cost is far lower than that of the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp test (< 5%)30.

To clarify the impact of potential heterogeneity in a pan-cancer cohort, we extracted subgroups of cancer types with n ≥ 10 to perform independent longitudinal trajectory evaluations (see Supplementary Material S3). The results showed that over the 24-month follow-up, solid tumors (e.g., lung cancer, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, gastric cancer, and liver cancer) exhibited extremely small fluctuation values (|ΔTyG| = 0.05–0.11). This suggests that most solid tumors possess the capacity to resist metabolic interference from the tumor itself and from chemoradiotherapy. Notably, thyroid cancer (|ΔTyG| = 0.29) and ovarian/cervical cancer (|ΔTyG| = 0.31) showed slight fluctuations during the follow-up period, which may be related to lipid redistribution and insulin sensitivity following endocrine interventions31,32. Overall, this post-hoc analysis confirms that the stability of the TyG index holds broad universality across cancer types (see Supplementary Document S4).

Although this study utilized K-means for trajectory classification, this method still has limitations in capturing the precision of longitudinal individual variations and random effects. Future studies should consider using latent class mixed models to improve trajectory fitting accuracy33. Furthermore, while the post-hoc analysis confirmed the macroscopic stability of the TyG index across cancer types, rare cancers or non-solid tumors (such as hematological malignancies) have not yet been verified due to sample size constraints. The heterogeneous impact of the primary cancer site, targeted therapy, and endocrine therapy on long-term intervention effects still needs to be assessed. Finally, due to the lack of molecular biological data (such as circulating adipokines or cancer transcriptomic data) for multi-omics verification of micro-mechanisms, the aforementioned findings are primarily mechanistic inferences34,35. Future prospective, multi-center, and cross-population cohort studies are required to clarify the exact value of TyG index trajectory changes in predicting survival outcomes and the efficacy of immunotherapy in specific cancers.

Conclusion

This study found that the TyG index is extremely stable in the cancer population and may represent an intrinsic metabolic trait associated with autonomous fat accumulation. Its evolutionary trajectory is independent of cancer cachexia-induced depletion. This exploratory finding provides new scientific insights into the potential application value of the TyG index in cancer metabolic monitoring and lays a foundation for future metabolic interventions assessing cancer survival outcomes.
