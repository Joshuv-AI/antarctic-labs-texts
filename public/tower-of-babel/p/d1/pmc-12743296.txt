Proformer: a multimodal proteomics transformer model for multidisease early risk assessment
Authors: Shizheng Qiu, Yang Hu, Jingjing Liu, Yadong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly identification of individuals at high risk for chronic diseases is crucial for prevention and intervention, yet current risk assessment tools are disease-specific, require extensive clinical data collection, and cannot provide multidisease risk profiles from a single measurement. Several protein large language models have been developed for tasks such as protein structure prediction, function prediction, and sequence design. However, none of these models can be directly applied in clinical settings to predict an individual’s future disease risk. Here, we present a multimodal proteomics Transformer (Proformer) model that integrates protein expression, sequence, and function information for multidisease risk assessment. We trained Proformer using real proteomics data from 47 124 individuals from the UK Biobank to evaluate its performance in discriminating the risk of 20 common chronic diseases. Proformer achieved state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance in all 20 diseases compared with five common machine learning and deep learning models. Compared to three common clinical predictors, Proformer’s 10-year discriminative performance outperforms Age + Sex model for 19 diseases, outperforms the ASCVD risk score for 16 diseases, and outperforms the panel composed of 35 clinical variables for 11 diseases. These results were replicated in the Scotland and Wales cohort from UK Biobank. In conclusion, Proformer enabled users to directly obtain a 10-year risk report for common chronic diseases by inputting their individual proteomics data.Keywords: proteomics, transformer, protein expression, chronic disease risk assessment

Introduction

The identification of high-risk individuals of chronic diseases is crucial for disease prevention [1–5]. Early intervention in most chronic diseases can significantly slow disease progression [6]. Current risk scores are typically designed for single diseases, such as the QRISK3 prediction algorithm and the Framingham Risk Score (FRS) for cardiovascular diseases [7–9]. These risk scores often involve a large number of clinical variables that need be manually collected, including demographic characteristics, physiological and metabolic markers, lifestyle factors, and medical history.

Plasma proteins play a critical regulatory role in various biological processes, including signal transduction, inflammation, immunity, metabolism, and vascular function [10, 11]. Due to their ability to integrate the effects of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors, changes in their levels and status can provide real-time monitoring and prediction of disease onset [12]. Recently, proteomics-based risk scores have emerged as strong contenders for multidisease risk prediction [13–19]. For instance, Gadd et al. trained a machine learning model based on Cox proportional hazards (CPHs) elastic net regression, incorporating 1468 Olink protein levels, to predict the incidence and mortality rates of 23 age-related diseases [20]. However, existing machine learning models assume that the protein features considered are entirely independent, failing to account for the complex interactions among these features observed in real-world settings.

In this study, we propose a multimodal proteomics Transformer model (Proformer) that integrates protein sequence, expression, and functional information to accurately predict the risks of multiple chronic diseases (Fig. 1). We employed Proformer to predict the risks of 20 chronic diseases for 42,756 English participants from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP), and replicated the results in Scotland and Wales cohort. Without incorporating any additional clinical predictors, Proformer demonstrated superior discriminative performance relative to three commonly used clinical risk scores.

Methods

UK biobank

The UK Biobank has recruited ~500 000 participants aged between 40 and 69 years from across the United Kingdom, encompassing a diverse demographic spectrum [17]. This expansive cohort is characterized by the collection of detailed phenotypic, genetic, and health-related data, including extensive baseline measurements, medical records, imaging data, and biological samples such as blood and urine. More detailed information can be found at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/.

Diagnoses of T2D (E11), obesity (E66), glaucoma (H40), hypertension (I10), chronic ischemic heart disease (I25), atrial fibrillation and flutter (I48), heart failure (I50), cerebral infarction (I63), rheumatoid arthritis (M05 and M06), and gout (M10), were defined using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes derived from the National Health Service (NHS) Central Registers. The diagnosis date of asthma, dementia, COPD, stroke, and Parkinson’s disease were obtained through algorithmic combinations of coded information from UK Biobank’s baseline assessment data collection along with linkage data, including self-reported medical condition codes (Field 20002) reported at the baseline assessment, Hospital admissions; diagnoses and procedures (Field 2000), and ICD-10 codes in Death Register records (Field 40001, Field 40002). The date of cancer diagnosis was acquired from a central registry, with cancer types classified according to ICD-10 codes. Dates of death were also obtained from the central registry. The time of blood sample collection was defined as the date of participants’ first visit to the UK Biobank assessment center (baseline time). Exclusion criteria for each endpoint were established by excluding individuals who had been diagnosed with the respective disease prior to baseline. The control group consisted of all UKB-PPP participants excluding those with the specific cases. For each type of cancer, the healthy control group did not develop any of the common cancers, either prior to baseline or throughout the follow-up period.

Proteomic profiling

The UKB-PPP encompasses proteomic data from 54 219 UK Biobank participants and analyzed between April 2021 and February 2022, utilizing up to 2923 unique assays per sample via the Olink Explore platform [21, 22]. A random subset of 46 595 individuals was selected from the baseline UK Biobank cohort, with an additional 6376 participants chosen by UKB-PPP consortium members and 1268 individuals engaged in COVID-19 research. As of November 2024, 53 013 UKB-PPP participants consented to continue their participation in the study.

Proteomic analysis was conducted using the Proximity Extension Assay (PEA) technology from Olink, yielding Normalized Protein eXpression (NPX) values on a log₂ scale. Comprehensive details on sample selection, processing, and quality control are documented online (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/label.cgi?id=1839). Proteins absent in over 50% of participants were excluded, resulting in a dataset comprising 2873 proteins. Remaining missing values were imputed using the “miceforest” package in Python, employing all proteins as predictors for each respective protein imputation.

Protein–protein interaction matrix

Protein–protein interactions were defined according to the criteria established by Szklarczyk et al., whereby an association between proteins is recognized when there is evidence of evolutionary and specific functional partnerships between them [23–28]. The STRING database was utilized to evaluate and quantify all evidence of protein–protein interactions based on various types of evidence. The results were generated as numerical “confidence scores” ranging from 0 to 1, representing the estimated probability that a given association is true. Consequently, an association matrix was generated for the proteins analyzed in this study.

Training and validation

Based on the geographic locations of the 22 assessment centers where participants were recruited for the UK Biobank, we divided the proteomics dataset into a subset for discovery cohort and replication cohort. Participants from England were used for discovery cohort, while participants from Scotland and Wales were used for replication cohort. Each dataset was partitioned into training and validation sets using an 80:20 split. Model training used the binary cross-entropy loss, suitable for the binary classification task.

Proformer applied fixed hyperparameters, including attention heads of 8, Transformer layers of 6, a feedforward network dimension of 2048, a dropout rate of 0.1, a learning rate of 2e-05, a batch size of 32. For the validation set, the model’s output probabilities are transformed using the Sigmoid function, and a threshold of 0.5 is set. Specifically, probability values equal to or greater than 0.5 are considered positive samples, while those below 0.5 are considered negative samples. Finally, the predicted results are compared with the true labels of the samples to calculate performance evaluation metrics for the model, including accuracy, precision, F1 score, recall, and AUC.

Compared with common machine learning and deep learning models

We compared Proformer with common machine learning and deep learning models that performed well on tabular data, such as MLP [38, 39], Residual Network (ResNet) [40], Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) [41], Categorical Boosting (CatBoost) [42], and Feature Tokenizer Transformer (FT-Transformer) [29]. MLP and ResNet are the most common baselines, demonstrating stable performance across different datasets. LightGBM and CatBoost are gradient boosting frameworks based on decision trees, specifically designed for efficiently handling large-scale tabular data. GBDT models typically represent the peak performance of traditional machine learning methods for tabular data [42, 43]. FT-Transformer is a lightweight model based on the Transformer architecture, with performance comparable to GBDT models.

Compared with the best models in published literature

We compared Proformer with the best performance for each clinical endpoint reported in previously published proteomic prediction models. We conducted a systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar using terms: “(proteomics OR protein) AND (risk prediction OR risk score OR disease prediction) AND (UK Biobank OR biobank)” for papers published between 2015 and 2025. The inclusion criteria for published studies were as follows: using proteomics data from prospective cohorts such as the UK Biobank to predict diseases using any method or model, and reporting the model’s AUC. We assessed whether Proformer outperforms baseline models and the best results reported in previous publications across 20 diseases using paired t-tests. Statistical significance was assessed at α = 0.05.

Proteomic risk scores

We evaluated whether individuals in these different risk groups exhibited distinct outcomes after follow-up [44, 45]. Participants were stratified into high-risk (ProRS > 0.66), medium -risk (0.33 < ProRS < 0.66), and low-risk groups (ProRS < 0.33) based on their ProRS. For each participant, the follow-up time was calculated based on the baseline enrollment date, the date of death, and the diagnosis dates of each disease. Specifically, if a confirmed diagnosis date for a particular disease exists and occurs before the follow-up end (September 2024) or death, the outcome is considered to have occurred; otherwise, it is treated as censored. Confidence intervals for the disease-free probability were estimated using Greenwood’s formula.

We designed three commonly used clinical predictor sets. The first set consists of age and sex, a simple yet highly effective predictor set. The second set is based on the ASCVD risk equations, which include gender, age, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, a history of antihypertensive medication use, diabetes history, and current smoking status [46, 47]. The third set is a very comprehensive predictor set that comprises 35 clinical variables (Supplementary Table S1). CPH models were used to perform survival analyses for each disease outcome. For each outcome, models were developed using seven distinct sets of predictors: ProRS, Age + Sex, ASCVD, PANEL, ProRS+Age + Sex, ProRS+ASCVD, and ProRS+PANEL. Harrell’s C-index was employed to evaluate the discriminative performance of the models, with 1000 bootstrap iterations performed to calculate the 95% confidence intervals for the C-index.

Calibration curves

Calibration curves compared predicted probabilities of an event with observed event rates, thereby assessing the model’s reliability. We divided the predicted probabilities into 10 quantile-based groups. For each group, we computed the mean predicted risk and observed event rate.

Results

Study population

This study included data on 2873 plasma protein measurements from 53 013 participants in the UKB-PPP to predict 20 incident outcomes, including neurodegenerative diseases, respiratory disorders, autoimmune diseases, endocrine diseases, cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and eye disorders. Among these participants, 47 124 were recruited from 17 assessment centers in England, and 5889 were recruited from five assessment centers in Scotland and Wales. There are obvious differences in the baseline clinical characteristics and mortality between these two cohorts. For instance, the Scottish and Wales cohort has a higher proportion of white (97.6% versus 92.8%), a higher proportion of current smokers (9.1% versus 7.6%), a higher follow-up time (16.54 versus 15.30 years), and a higher mortality rate (13.1% versus 10.6%) compared to the England cohort, which have also been noted in previous publications (Supplementary Table S2) [48, 49]. Thus, the Scottish and Wales cohort could be used for a validation set. To construct a predictive model rather than a diagnostic model, we excluded participants who had already been diagnosed with these incident outcomes prior to the baseline. Statistical information for all diseases was provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Predicting chronic disease risk with Proformer

We introduce Proformer, a Transformer-based multimodal model that leverages proteomics to predict human diseases, without requiring any additional clinical information [32]. In brief, Proformer concurrently integrates information from three distinct levels: protein expression, sequence, and function. Each numerical expression feature in the proteomics is embedded via the Hadamard product, while the sequence and function embeddings are obtained from ProteinBERT—a pretrained proteomics model developed using ~106 million protein sequences and functional annotations from various species. These embeddings are fused through element-wise multiplication and concatenated with a [CLS] token before being fed into a stack of Transformer layers. The [CLS] tokens are concatenated to generate predictions for the probability of disease occurrence for each sample. We develop a predictive model for each incident outcome and integrate these models into a unified framework, enabling users to directly infer an individual’s risk for all diseases.

In the prediction tasks for 20 chronic diseases, Proformer achieved state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance in all these diseases (100%) and surpassed the best results reported in all previously published studies in 19 of them (95%) (Table 1, Supplementary Table S4). Using paired t-tests, Proformer significantly outperformed other baseline models and the best previous results in the literature (Supplementary Table S5) [19, 20, 50, 51]. Proformer demonstrated excellent performance in predicting 13 common chronic diseases (AUC > 0.80), including dementia (AUC = 0.9135), Parkinson disease (AUC = 0.8219), obesity (AUC = 0.8712), type 2 diabetes (T2D, AUC = 0.8916), gout (AUC = 0.9165), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, AUC = 0.9111), heart failure (AUC = 0.9024), atrial fibrillation (AUC = 0.8713), Stroke (AUC = 0.8495), cerebral infarction (AUC = 0.8364), lung cancer (AUC = 0.8863), and prostate cancer (AUC = 0.9274). Notably, Proformer successfully clustered the embeddings of most types of disease samples and healthy samples into two distinct clusters (Fig. 2).

Proteomic risk scores development

The probability generated by Proformer for each individual’s risk for each disease is defined as the ProRS. We investigated the association between ProRS and the incidence of each chronic disease and found that higher ProRS values were almost consistently associated with an elevated risk for all the chronic diseases (Fig. 3A). We subsequently divided the samples into three risk groups based on the tertiles of ProRS: high risk (ProRS > 0.66), medium risk (0.33 < ProRS < 0.66), and low risk (ProRS < 0.33). We compared disease risks between individuals in the high-risk group and those in the low-risk group. For all chronic diseases, individuals in the high-risk group exhibited at least a 5-fold increased risk compared to those in the low-risk group (Supplementary Table S6). For example, for T2D, the high-risk group had a risk of 50.99% versus 0.46% in the low-risk group (odds ratio [OR] = 225.88, 95% CI: 155.30–328.54), and for dementia, the high-risk group had a risk of 23.89% versus 0.06% in the low-risk group (OR = 569.02, 95% CI: 183.15–1767.91). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that, during the follow-up period, individuals in the high-risk group had a significantly higher cumulative probability of developing chronic diseases compared to those in the moderate- and low-risk groups (Fig. 3B).

Clinical discriminative performance of proteomic risk scores

We evaluated whether ProRS could serve as a supplement or alternative to current clinical risk scores. To this end, we designed three sets of clinical predictors of increasing complexity. The simplest set included only age and sex. The second set comprised the ASCVD risk predictors, a well-validated indicator of cardiovascular disease risk i.e. often extended to noncardiovascular diseases [46, 47]. The third set consisted of an extensive panel of clinical predictors, termed PANEL, which includes 35 variables spanning demographics, anthropometrics, lifestyle factors, medical history, medication use, and blood biochemical markers—exceeding the data available in primary care. Using CPH models, we predicted 20 chronic disease outcomes employing seven sets of predictors: ProRS, each of the clinical predictor sets individually, and their combined models with ProRS. Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was used to assess the discriminative performance of ProRS.

We found that ProRS demonstrated excellent discriminative performance for nearly all diseases, with robust performance observed across different time windows (Supplementary Tables S7–S9, Supplementary Figs S1–S2). Specifically, the discriminative performance (C-index) for predicting the 10-year risk exceeded 0.8 for 15 diseases, including: dementia: 0.940 (95% CI: 0.936–0.945), Parkinson’s disease: 0.948 (95% CI: 0.939–0.957), COPD: 0.900 (95% CI: 0.894–0.907), rheumatoid arthritis: 0.819 (95% CI: 0.800–0.839), obesity: 0.854 (95% CI: 0.848–0.859), T2D: 0.904 (95% CI: 0.900–0.909), gout: 0.915 (95% CI: 0.908–0.922), heart failure: 0.907 (95% CI: 0.900–0.914), ischaemic heart disease: 0.835 (95% CI: 0.829–0.841), atrial fibrillation: 0.853 (95% CI: 0.846–0.859), stroke: 0.871 (95% CI: 0.860–0.883), cerebral infarction: 0.898 (95% CI: 0.888–0.908), breast cancer: 0.850 (95% CI: 0.840–0.861), lung cancer: 0.973 (95% CI: 0.962–0.983), and prostate cancer: 0.958 (95% CI: 0.954–0.962). Among the 20 disease endpoints, ProRS provided higher predictive value than Age + Sex score for 19 endpoints, outperformed the ASCVD risk score for 16 endpoints, and outperformed the PANEL for 11 endpoints (Fig. 4A). For most clinical endpoints, adding clinical predictors to ProRS did not improve its discriminative performance (Fig. 4B). Therefore, ProRS can accurately predict disease risk without the need for any clinical information. Additionally, calibration curves were constructed to assess the concordance between predicted outcomes and actual clinical events, thereby evaluating the clinical utility of the models. The models for dementia, Parkinson’s disease, obesity, T2D, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, and cancers showed good calibration, whereas the risks for other diseases were either overestimated or underestimated (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Replication in the Scottish and Welsh cohort

We used proteomics data from the Scotland and Wales cohort as a replication cohort to evaluate the performance of Proformer. In the replication cohort, Proformer continued to demonstrate excellent disease prediction capabilities—with 16 out of 20 disease endpoints achieving an AUC above 0.7 and 12 endpoints surpassing an AUC of 0.8 (Supplementary Table S10). Except for a few diseases with very small sample sizes, Proformer was able to cluster the majority of disease and healthy samples into two distinct groups (Supplementary Fig. S4). We divided the samples into three risk groups based on the tertiles of ProRS, and found that during the follow-up period, the probability of disease in the high-risk group was significantly higher than that in the intermediate- and low-risk groups, with the high-risk group exhibiting at least a fivefold increase in disease risk compared to the low-risk group (Supplementary Fig. S5, Supplementary Table S11).

Furthermore, we employed seven predictors (ProRS, Age + Sex, ProRS+Age + Sex, ASCVD, ProRS+ASCVD, PANEL, and ProRS+PANEL) in the replication cohort to predict 20 chronic disease endpoints. Harrell’s C-index was used to assess the discriminative performance of the models. We observed excellent 10-year predictive performance in the replication cohort, with the C-index for ProRS exceeding 0.8 in 15 disease endpoints, particularly in neurodegenerative and metabolic diseases (Supplementary Table S12). The predictive value of ProRS was higher than that of Age + Sex in 19 diseases, higher than the ASCVD score in 15 diseases, and higher than that of PANEL in 13 diseases (Supplementary Fig. S6). ProRS also maintained robust predictive performance in the replication cohort, whether for more than 10 years after baseline or over the entire follow-up period (Supplementary Figs S7–S8, Supplementary Tables S13–S14).

Discussion

In this study, we proposed a multimodal proteomics model, Proformer, that provides predictive reports on the risk of common chronic diseases by integrating protein expression, sequence, and functional information. Compared with previously published machine learning models based solely on expression proteomics, Proformer exhibited superior predictive performance, and its 10-year discriminative performance for most chronic diseases surpassed that of three widely used clinical risk scores. Moreover, the protein features prioritized by Proformer in disease prediction may represent key hub proteins regulating disease within real-world plasma protein networks, potentially serving as high-priority drug targets in drug development.

Many studies have shown that proteomics data can capture an individual’s health status [12, 52]. Unlike clinical data, genomics, or transcriptomics, the dynamic changes in plasma proteins integrate a range of etiological factors (such as gene expression, aging, behavior, and drug interventions) thus establishing plasma proteins as potential early indicators of complex diseases [12, 53]. Our results indicated that ProRS, without requiring any additional clinical features (including age and sex, which were previously considered crucial for risk stratification), successfully stratified risk for all endpoints. The Proformer model was particularly accurate in predicting cardiovascular events, metabolic diseases, and neurodegenerative diseases, with C-index typically exceeding 0.8. Cardiovascular events and metabolic diseases are often closely associated with metabolic dysregulation, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress—processes that are predominantly mediated or regulated by plasma proteins [54, 55]. Consequently, plasma proteomics can precisely capture the complex biological processes underlying chronic diseases, reflecting early disease changes.

This study has several advantages. First, the UK Biobank is a large-scale prospective cohort study that has extensively collected biological samples and health-related data from participants, along with long-term follow-up. The diversity of participants (including differences in gender, age, ethnicity, lifestyle, etc.) enhances the model’s applicability across different populations, thereby improving the generalizability of the predictive model. Second, proteins are the functional products of gene–environment interactions, and many diseases are directly caused by the abnormal expression of specific proteins. Therefore, compared to other omics data, proteomics data may serve as more direct biomarkers for diseases. Proformer used plasma proteomics as a single source, without requiring any additional clinical information, significantly reducing research costs. The simplified ProRS we provided became an effective alternative to current risk scores. Third, we have integrated multimodal protein information, including expression, sequence, and function, to precisely predict individual-level future disease risk, which distinguishes it from previous protein language models.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the current implementation requires high-dimensional proteomic profiling, which, while increasingly accessible through commercial platforms like Olink, still represents a cost barrier compared to standard clinical tests. Second, Proformer is trained on UK Biobank participants, who are predominantly of European ancestry, which may limit generalizability to other ethnic populations. Another limitation of our study is the lack of validation in independent cohorts outside the UK Biobank. While our Scotland/Wales replication cohort demonstrates geographic generalizability within the UK and shows different demographic characteristics, external validation in international cohorts with different ethnic compositions and proteomic measurement platforms is essential to fully establish Proformer’s generalizability. In addition, the UK Biobank suffers from healthy volunteer bias—participants are generally healthier, more educated, and of higher socioeconomic status than the general UK population. Nevertheless, as proteomics costs continue to decline and multi-ethnic biobanks emerge, we anticipate that these limitations will diminish over time.

In conclusion, the development of Proformer addresses a critical need in precision medicine: the ability to provide comprehensive multidisease risk assessment from a single biological measurement. Unlike disease-specific risk calculators that dominate current clinical practice, Proformer offers simultaneous evaluation of 20 common chronic diseases, changing the paradigm from sequential single-disease screening to holistic health risk profiling.
