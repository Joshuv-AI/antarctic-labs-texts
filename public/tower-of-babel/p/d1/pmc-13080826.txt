High-Output Heart Failure Associated With Arteriovenous Fistula
Authors: Jihee Son, Jihoon Kim, Ki Hong Choi, Jeong Hoon Yang, Eun Kyoung Kim, Sung-A Chang, Sung-Ji Park, Sang-Chol Lee, Seung Woo Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) are the preferred vascular access for hemodialysis, but they can cause high-output heart failure (HOHF) by imposing significant hemodynamic stress. Despite its clinical significance, AVF-related HOHF remains under-recognized in hemodialysis patients.Case SummaryA 73-year-old woman on hemodialysis presented with progressive dyspnea. She had undergone upper-arm AVF surgery 1 year earlier. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed pericardial effusion and left ventricular enlargement. Right heart catheterization with simultaneous echocardiography showed a high cardiac index (5.5 L/min/m2) that promptly decreased to 3.3 L/min/m2 during manual AVF compression. Two AVF flow-reduction procedures resulted in a reduction in left ventricular size and symptomatic improvement.DiscussionThis case highlights the need to consider HOHF in hemodialysis patients presenting with unexplained heart failure. Hemodynamic assessment—echocardiography or cardiac catheterization with AVF compression—can confirm the diagnosis and guide management.Take-Home MessageIn suspected AVF-related HOHF, echocardiography with transient AVF flow reduction during hemodynamic testing can reverse the high-output state and support the diagnosis.

History of Presentation

A 73-year-old woman presented to the outpatient clinic with exertional dyspnea corresponding to NYHA functional class II. On initial evaluation, her blood pressure was 159/73 mm Hg, heart rate was 59 beats/min, and body temperature was 36.6 °C. Physical examination showed grade 1 pretibial pitting edema in both legs, with elevated jugular venous pressure.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis that had progressed to end-stage renal disease, for which she was receiving maintenance hemodialysis. She had undergone upper-arm arteriovenous fistula (AVF) surgery 1 year earlier.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included heart failure with preserved left ventricular (LV) systolic function, hypertensive cardiomyopathy, and volume overload.

Investigations

Cardiac auscultation revealed a regular rhythm without murmurs. The patient had a left brachiocephalic AVF with palpable thrill and audible bruit. Chest radiography demonstrated cardiomegaly with mild pulmonary edema. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with voltage criteria for LV hypertrophy. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed LV dilatation (end-diastolic dimension: 61 mm, end-systolic dimension: 37 mm) with a low-normal LV ejection fraction of 53%, mild mitral regurgitation, and a small amount of pericardial effusion. Transmitral Doppler inflow showed an L wave, representing mid-diastolic forward flow between the E and A waves, consistent with elevated LV filling pressures and impaired diastolic function (Figure 1, Video 1). The E/A ratio was 0.95, E/e′ ratio was 19, estimated right ventricular systolic pressure was 43 mm Hg, and left atrial volume index was markedly increased at 98 mL/m2. Two months later, she returned with aggravated dyspnea. Despite further volume control, follow-up transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated progressive LV and left atrial dilatation with an increased amount of pericardial effusion. She was admitted for further evaluation. Right heart catheterization showed elevated mean pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (21 mm Hg), cardiac output, and LV end-diastolic pressure (16 mm Hg). Thermodilution cardiac output was marked high (up to 7.0 L/min), and cardiac index was 5.5 L/min/m2. Manual compression of the AVF reduced cardiac output to 4.2 L/min and cardiac index to 3.3 L/min/m2—values within the normal range (Table 1). This invasive assessment was consistent with the echocardiographic assessment (Figure 1, Video 2). Doppler ultrasonography of the AVF also confirmed abnormally high access flow (Table 2). Alternative causes of high-output heart failure (HOHF), including hyperthyroidism, severe anemia, and thiamine deficiency, were evaluated. Treatment

A multidisciplinary team concluded that the heart failure was caused by AVF-related high cardiac output and that surgical reduction of the AVF would be beneficial. As the patient had no viable alternative dialysis access, flow modulation was chosen over complete takedown. AVF flow-reduction surgery was performed, but repeated catheterization still showed elevated cardiac index (4.9 L/min/m2). Outcome and Follow-Up

After 2 operations and volume adjustment, there was marked improvement of echocardiographic indices, including reduced LV chamber size, decreased amount of pericardial effusion, and lower right ventricular systolic pressure and heart failure symptoms (Figure 3, Video 3). Discussion

The hemodynamic mechanism of AVF-related HOHF is primarily an increase in preload and a decrease in systemic vascular resistance. This cascade leads to reduced renal perfusion and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, promoting plasma volume expansion and progressive overload. The consequent rise in preload and sustained hyperdynamic circulation may ultimately result in LV dilatation and dysfunction. In such cases, conventional volume management alone is often insufficient, and reduction of access flow through surgical revision, banding, or fistula closure remains the cornerstone of therapy, with proven benefits in symptom relief and reverse remodeling.1, 2, 3

Our case is distinctive in that invasive right heart catheterization was performed, directly confirming the presence of a high-output state. Cardiac output values derived from right heart catheterization closely matched simultaneous transthoracic echocardiography estimates. Moreover, transient manual compression of the AVF immediately reduced cardiac output and increased systemic vascular resistance, thereby strengthening diagnostic certainty. In our case, simultaneous echocardiography showed excellent concordance with invasive hemodynamic parameters, suggesting that noninvasive assessment with transient AVF compression is a reliable tool for diagnostic evaluation in such patients.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies showing that AVF creation acutely increases LV dimension and cardiac output, while worsening diastolic function toward a restrictive pattern with elevated B-type natriuretic peptide levels.4 In contrast, prior studies have indicated symptomatic, echocardiographic improvement after AVF flow-reduction procedures.5,6 In addition, a report has shown that upper-arm AVFs have been associated with higher flow and greater risk of HOHF,7 as illustrated in the present case. For patients with pre-existing heart failure, consideration of lower-arm AVFs may represent a safer alternative.

Furthermore, management options included AVF takedown or surgical flow modulation. Complete takedown would have eliminated shunt-related physiology but caused irreversible loss of dialysis access and carried procedural risks, including limb ischemia and uncertainty regarding the success of a new contralateral fistula. Flow modulation was therefore selected, as it allowed reduction of excessive shunt flow while maintaining the only feasible hemodialysis access. Serial duplex ultrasonography follow-up was planned to ensure that the adjusted flow remained within an appropriate therapeutic range.

Despite its clinical significance, AVF-related HOHF remains under-recognized, and current recommendations are largely based on case-based experiences. Our case underscores the importance of heightened suspicion, routine echocardiographic surveillance, and individualized decision-making in patients undergoing AVF creation. Further prospective studies are warranted to define risk thresholds for AVF flow and to determine whether AVF ligation confers a survival benefit in this population.

Conclusions

Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for AV access–related high-output physiology in hemodialysis patients with otherwise unexplained heart failure symptoms or new/worsening LV dysfunction. Serial surveillance and early identification are pivotal for optimizing the timing of fistula intervention and improving outcomes.
