Effects of orthokeratology on binocular vision and myopia control in adolescents
Authors: Hui Xu, Xishuo Tao, Shuang Liang, Chong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare the efficacy of myopia control in adolescents wearing orthokeratology lenses (OKL) or single-vision spectacles (SVS). This prospective study included 30 myopic adolescents wearing orthokeratology lenses (OKL group) and 30 wearing SVS (SVS group). To prevent pseudo-replication arising from the strong correlation between fellow eyes, only right-eye data were analyzed, except for binocular tests requiring measurements from both eyes. All participants underwent assessments of visual acuity (VA), refraction, axial length (AL), and binocular visual function, including accommodative response, accommodative amplitude, distance and near heterophoria, gradient accommodative convergence/accommodation (AC/A) ratio, and horizontal fusional vergence. Binocular function was evaluated at baseline and at 1, 3, and 6 months, while VA, refraction, and AL were measured at baseline, 1 week, and 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. At 12 months, AL progression was more severe in the SVS group than in the OKL group (P < .05). OKL improved uncorrected visual acuity to about −0.05 within 1 month and maintained stability, whereas SVS vision declined by 6 months. Best-corrected distance visual acuity improved markedly in the OKL group but only modestly in the SVS group. OKL rapidly corrected spherical equivalent refraction and reduced accommodative lag, with no group differences in accommodative amplitude. OKL effectively slowed axial elongation, improved VA, and reduced accommodative lag in myopic adolescents, demonstrating superior myopia-control efficacy compared with SVS.

1. Introduction

Myopia, also known as nearsightedness, is an ocular condition in which parallel light passes through the ocular refractive system, with the focus falling in front of the retina.[1] In mainland China, the prevalence of myopia is approximately 80% among adolescents.[2] This high prevalence, coupled with the rapid progression of myopia during adolescence, contributes to a significantly increased risk of high myopia in adulthood.[3,4]

To date, several effective interventions have been reported to slow myopia progression in adolescents, such as low-dose atropine eye drops, multifocal soft contact lenses (SCL), and specialized spectacle lenses. Orthokeratology lenses (OKLs) are considered one of the most effective interventions for slowing myopia progression in adolescents, reducing axial elongation by approximately 0.19 mm/year.[5] Compared with single-vision spectacles (SVSs), OKLs are approximately 2 to 3 times more effective in reducing axial length (AL) progression in myopic adolescents.[6,7] In addition to slowing AL elongation, OKL use has also been associated with improvements in accommodative lag.[8,9] For example, Ding et al reported that accommodative lag was correlated with myopia progression during the first 6 months of OKL wear, although the changes in accommodative lag were not statistically significant, and the correlation disappeared after 1 year.[10] Song and his colleagues investigated the accommodation and binocular vision changes of patients who were in the age group of myopia development, which revealed enhanced accommodation despite no significant difference after 3 months.[11] Although many studies have examined the effects of OKLs on accommodation and visual function, few have specifically focused on adolescents in the stage of rapid myopia progression, and direct comparisons between OKLs and SVSs remain limited. Therefore, the aim of this study was to follow adolescents undergoing progressive myopia and compare the changes in accommodation and visual function between OKLs and SVSs.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Participants and study design

In this prospective cohort study, we enrolled myopic adolescents who were treated either with OKLs (OKL group) or SVSs (SVS group) at the Ophthalmology Clinic of the First Hospital of Zibo between March 2020 and March 2021. This study followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of the First Hospital of Zibo (Approval No. YXLL2021072214). All the patients had a routine eye examination, and the guardians signed the informed consent; especially, the guardians of OKL were informed and understood the adverse reactions, and they all signed the informed consent.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: adolescents aged between 10 and 14 years; a spherical equivalent refraction (SER) between −2.00 diopter (D) and −4.50 D, with cylindrical correction of ≤1.00 D; and best-corrected distance visual acuity (BCVA) of 0.0 or better in the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution system prior to OKL fitting. The exclusion criteria were those adolescents who: had a history of visual dysfunction and/or received visual training previously; received other interventions for myopia before enrolling in this study; had any contraindications to OKL or SVSs wearing, as well as those with ocular surface disease, ocular disorders, strabismus, amblyopia, and other systemic disorders; had a history of wearing any corneal contact lenses within 4 weeks; and had pathological myopia.

2.2. OKL and SVS wearing

OKL fitting and lens-wear instruction were carried out following the manufacturer’s instructions (Dreamlite, Procornea, Netherlands). Each participant in the OKL group was required to wear the lenses for at least 8 hours/night. In the SVS group, lenses were prescribed based on each participant’s refractive error and corneal curvature measurements.

2.3. Ophthalmic examinations

Only participants in the OKL group who achieved refractive stability, defined as SER between −0.50 D and +0.50 D after 1 month of lens-wear, were included in the visual function analysis, to ensure all measurements were performed under conditions of complete refractive correction. To prevent pseudo-replication arising from the strong correlation between fellow eyes, only right-eye data were analyzed, except for binocular tests requiring measurements from both eyes (i.e., the binocular assessment).

Participants in each group underwent routine ophthalmic examinations, including visual acuity (VA), refraction, AL, as well as binocular vision function assessments. Uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) and BCVA were assessed monocularly using a 5-meter LogMAR chart. Objective refraction was performed with an automatic refractometer (RM-1, Topcon, Japan) in automatic mode. The differences between spherical lens measurements were required to be within ±0.50 D, cylindrical lenses within ±0.25 D, and axis differences within ±10°. For AL measurement, participants were seated comfortably and instructed to fixate on the internal red target. The AL was recorded automatically once proper alignment and sufficient signal quality were achieved.

The binocular assessment included accommodative response, accommodative amplitude, positive and negative relative accommodation (PRA and NRA), horizontal heterophoria at distance and near, gradient accommodative convergence to accommodation (AC/A) ratio, and base-in and base-out fusional reserves (BIFR and BOFR). Accommodative response was measured using the binocular cross cylinders method. The accommodative amplitude was measured using the pull-away method.[11] PRA and NRA were determined using the Phoropter DK500 (Topcon, Japan). Distance and near horizontal phorias were determined using the von Graefe technique.[11] The AC/A was calculated with the gradient methods using ±1.00 D at 40 cm. BIFR and the BOFR were determined using the phoropter (Phorometer DK500, Topcon, Japan), according to the previous description.[12]

2.4. Assessment timeline for visual and binocular function

Assessment of binocular vision function in each group was conducted at 4 time points: baseline (before initiation of OKL or SVS correction), and 1, 3, and 6 months. Assessment for the VA and refraction was conducted at 6 time points: baseline, 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months. As AL shows negligible short-term variability, measurements were obtained only at baseline and at the 12-month follow-up.

2.5. Statistical analysis

All the data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Kolmogorov–Smirnov was utilized to test the normal distribution. A paired t test was performed for the intra-group comparisons, while a 1-way analysis of variance with repeated measures was performed for the inter-group comparisons at different time points. The Bonferroni method was used to adjust the P values after multiple comparisons. IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0 (IBM Corp.) was used for statistical analyses. The difference was considered to be significant in the presence of a P value of <.05.

A total of 30 participants were enrolled in each group; data from the right eye only were analyzed in this section, yielding 30 right eyes in the OKL group and 30 right eyes in the SVS group. 3.2. OKL is superior to SVS in slowing AL

At baseline, there was no statistically significant difference in AL between the OKL group and the SVS group (24.95 ± 0.61 mm vs 24.89 ± 0.47 mm, P > .05, Table 1). At 12 months, AL progression in the OKL group was significantly reduced compared with the SVS group (P < .05, Fig. 1). Given that AL is the primary contributor to myopia progression in adolescents, these findings suggest that OKL is more effective than SVS in controlling myopic progression.

3.3. OKL-wear significantly improved UCVA and BCVA

After 1 week, a significant difference in UCVA was observed between the 2 groups (P < .001). Due to the corneal reshaping effect of OKL, the UCVA of the OKL group improved significantly (P < .05) after 1 week of lens wear compared to baseline (Table 2). Compared with the 1-month visit, no significant differences in UCVA were observed at 3, 6, or 12 months (all P > .05). This indicated that after 1 month of OKL wear, the UCVA of the OKL group reached its optimal level (around −0.05) and remained stable with continued OKL wear. In contrast, the UCVA in the SVS group showed no improvement at 3 months and declined significantly at 6 months (P < .05, Table 2).

3.4. OKL induced rapid SER correction and stabilized refraction

After 1 week of OKL-wear, SER in the OKL group improved significantly, from −3.25 ± 0.81 D at baseline to −0.22 ± 0.41 D (P < .001). At 1 month, refraction improved further to a near-emmetropic level (0.18 ± 0.26 D). Although a slight shift was observed at 12 months, SER remained close to emmetropia (0.10 ± 0.31 D). Compared with the 1-week visit, SER did not differ significantly at 12 months (Table 4). In contrast, in the SVS group, mean SER progressed to −3.57 ± 0.63 D at 6 months, which was significantly more myopic than baseline (P < .001). By 12 months, SER had further decreased by 0.85 D to −4.05 ± 0.58 D (P < .001; Table 4).

3.5. OKL reduced accommodative lag

When comparing accommodative responses between the OKL and SVS groups, significant differences were observed at both 3 months (1.24 ± 0.59 vs 1.80 ± 0.69, P = .026) and 6 months (1.12 ± 0.44 vs 1.74 ± 0.53, P = .012), with the OKL group showing reduced lag. In contrast, no statistical differences were noticed in the accommodative responses between the OKL and SVS groups at baseline and 1 month (all P > .05, Table 5).

There was no significant difference in the accommodative amplitude between the 2 groups at baseline (13.96 ± 2.76 vs 14.31 ± 3.08, P = .872). 3.7. Comparison of fusional vergence

At baseline, the mean BOFR in the OKL group was slightly higher than that in the SVS group, with significant difference in break point and recovery point (P = .016, P = .003). The BOFR of the 2 groups showed significant differences at 1 month (P < .001, P = .003, P < .001). However, there were no significant differences in breakpoint and recovery point at 3 months (P = .125, P = .262). By 6 months, there were no significant differences in blur point and breakpoint (P = .065, P = .152), while the difference in recovery point was significant (P = .044).

At the baseline, there was a significant difference in breakpoint between the 2 groups in BIFR (P = .010), while the differences in blur point and recovery point were not significant (P = .663, P = .157). After 6 months of intervention, there was no significant difference in breakpoint between the 2 groups (P = .870), but significant differences were observed in blur point and recovery point (P = .032, P = .004, Table 7). The PRA, NRA, distance and near horizontal phorias, and AC/A ratio showed slight changes after lens wear in both groups; however, no statistically significant differences were observed at any follow-up time points compared with the baseline (data not shown).

4. Discussion

Consistent with other studies on OKL in the literature,[13] there was a strong linear relation between SER and AL. It provides an alternative to refractive surgery for many patients, as it slowed down the axial elongation.[14] Consistent with a previous study,[15] our data showed that after wearing the lens for 1-year, the myopia refraction in the OKL group decreased significantly compared with the baseline, and reached a stable level (0.18 ± 0.26 D) at 1 month, which was significantly lower than that in the SVS group (−3.20 ± 0.54 D). Additionally, after 1 year of lens wearing, the refraction in the SVS group increased significantly compared to baseline (−0.85 D). The SVS group showed a significant increase in AL, while the OKL group did not show a notable increase compared to baseline, which indicated that OKL could control the AL in adolescents with myopia.

Several studies have investigated the influence of OKL on accommodative function in myopic children. For instance, Zhu et al reported that OKL wear improved accommodative performance,[16] while Gifford et al demonstrated that OKL increased accommodative response and reduced accommodative lag after 1 month of wear.[17] Batres et al further showed that OKL induces an increase in total ocular spherical aberration, accompanied by a reduction in internal spherical aberration; these optical changes may compensate for positive corneal aberrations and consequently alter accommodative behavior.[18] Consistent with these findings, the present study showed that both groups exhibited accommodative lag at baseline, with mean lag values exceeding 1.00 D, suggesting that pronounced accommodative lag may be common in myopic adolescents. In the OKL group, accommodative lag decreased significantly at the 3-month follow-up and continued to improve by 6 months. In contrast, no improvement in accommodative lag was observed in the SVS group throughout the study, aligning with previous research.[8,17,19]

The divergent outcomes between groups may be attributed to the distinct optical characteristics induced by OKL. Unlike SVS, SCL, or other correction modalities that do not modify corneal morphology, OKL produces substantial corneal reshaping. A recent study indicated that SCL wear does not affect horizontal heterophoria or accommodative flexibility, suggesting that SCL-induced changes in ocular aberrations are insufficient to influence binocular vision function.[20] In contrast, OKL has been shown to induce a 4- to 8-fold increase in spherical aberration after just 1 week of wear, with this change remaining stable over a 1-year period.[21,22] Increased positive spherical aberration enhances accommodative accuracy, which in turn reduces accommodative lag and improves PRA.[16] Together, these results suggest that OKL-induced modifications in corneal shape and spherical aberration contribute to improved accommodative accuracy and reduced accommodative lag, whereas SVS wear does not produce comparable effects.

Several studies have examined whether OKL affects binocular accommodative and vergence functions, yet the findings have been inconsistent. Felipe-Marquez et al found that OKL did not lead to significant short-term or long-term changes in binocular accommodative function.[23] Another study found that OKL use was associated with some improvement in binocular accommodation, although the difference did not reach statistical significance.[24] Zhu et al further demonstrated that OKL treatment did not affect the accommodative-convergence function in myopic adolescents.[25] Similarly, Yam and colleagues showed that the combination of 0.01% atropine with either OKL or SVS did not alter accommodative-convergence function in myopic patients.[26] Consistent with these findings, the present study observed no significant changes in accommodative amplitude or AC/A ratio in either the OKL or SVS group throughout the follow-up period. Regarding fusional reserves, no significant differences in BOFR or BIFR were detected after 6 months of OKL wear in myopic adolescents. A possible explanation is that participants in the present study did not exhibit fusional reserve deficits at baseline, and therefore OKL was unlikely to induce measurable changes in either positive or negative fusional vergence. In addition, the relatively short follow-up period may limit the ability to determine the long-term effects of OKL on fusional reserves in myopic adolescents.

OKL can temporarily correct refractive errors such as myopia and astigmatism. Long-term OKL wear has been shown to significantly improve UCVA, although a slight reduction in VA may occur after lens removal. Additionally, OKL increases higher-order aberrations, particularly spherical aberration, which may enhance both the accuracy and flexibility of accommodation. Substantial evidence indicates that OKL can reduce myopia progression by approximately 40% to 60%.[27,28] In the present study, both groups demonstrated accommodative lag at baseline. However, after 6 months of lens wear, adolescents in the OKL group showed significantly reduced accommodative lag compared with those in the SVS group, suggesting that OKL contributes to improved accommodative accuracy. Furthermore, when baseline values were compared with post-wearing time points, no significant within-group differences were observed in distance or near phoria, accommodative amplitude, AC/A ratio, or fusional reserves. These findings indicate that while OKL improves accommodative lag, its overall impact on binocular accommodative and vergence functions appears to be minimal.

In addition, our study had limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, with only 60 participants. Second, only data from the right eye were analyzed to avoid inter-eye correlation. However, binocular accommodation and vergence are inherently interactive processes, and evaluating a single eye may not fully represent the integrated binocular visual response. Future studies should incorporate binocular measurements to better reflect real-world visual function. Third, the improvement of myopia is a lengthy process, and our follow-up time was relatively short. Therefore, further research will investigate the long-term effects. A randomized controlled trial design would have been preferable to simply assigning patients to 2 groups, which represents another limitation of this study.

5. Conclusions

Wearing OKL improved accommodative function and contributed to myopia control in adolescents, particularly by enhancing accommodative response. These findings provide theoretical support for the role of OKL in slowing myopia progression through its influence on accommodative mechanisms, although further large-scale and long-term clinical studies are needed to substantiate this effect.
