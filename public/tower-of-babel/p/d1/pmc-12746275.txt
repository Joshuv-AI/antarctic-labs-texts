The impact of health misinformation and health literacy on the management of dyslipidemia
Authors: Heather M Johnson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobally, cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death. The dissemination of health misinformation on management dyslipidemia, especially statin misinformation, is a significant threat to public health and to the delivery of cardiovascular preventive care. This brief report explores the intersection of health literacy and health misinformation, sources of misinformation, and the disproportionate impact of misinformation across communities, worsening health disparities. Additionally, interdisciplinary strategies are proposed to combat misinformation, and increase the dissemination of accurate, evidence-based information on dyslipidemia, with the goal of improving the delivery of equitable care and reducing cardiovascular events.Keywords: Dyslipidemia, Misinformation, Health literacy, Cardiovascular disease

Health literacy and health misinformation

Health literacy is defined as the ability to access, process, and comprehend health-related information to engage in health decisions [1] Health literacy is based on social, cultural, environmental and individual factors, with limited health literacy being a significant barrier to healthcare access, clinician-patient communication, medication adherence, and overall engagement with the healthcare system [1,2] Health misinformation disproportionately impacts communities with lower health literacy and is a major contributor to poor health outcomes [3,4] Misinformation is defined as “information that is false, inaccurate, or misleading according to the best available evidence at the time” [5] The definition of misinformation varies across content, source, and motivation [[4], [5], [6]] Misinformation can be spread unknowingly, but can also be intentional and deliberately misleading (i.e. disinformation) [[4], [5], [6]]

The rapid spread of health misinformation is a significant public health risk and is facilitated across multiple domains, particularly social media [5,7] Although social media is an integral environment for timely information exchange, it is also an unfortunate source of health misinformation [8] Social media encompasses various formats, including social networking sites supporting chat groups (e.g., Facebook and Instagram), microblogs (e.g., Twitter, now X), and media-sharing sites (e.g., YouTube, TikTok), among others. Other sources of health misinformation pathways include Internet websites, public figures/celebrities, including digital influencers, in addition to print sources (e.g. books, etc.) and news programs [5,[8], [9], [10], [11]] Social media uses algorithms to promote engagement; research has demonstrated greater trafficking of viral or sensationalized content, amplifying the spread of health misinformation [7,9] The dissemination of health-related misinformation on social media has significantly increased since 2017 [9]; this contributes to implementation gaps between evidence-based research and clinical practice, and persistent health disparities [12,13]

As of 2024, over 75 % of the US population and over 60 % of the global population uses social media [9,14] Research has demonstrated that false information tends to spread more rapidly on social media than accurate evidence-based information [9,15] Additionally, misinformation spreads differently across communities due to cultural variations in belief, historical distrust in medical institutions, and socio-economic factors [3] Both health literacy and the distribution of health misinformation are related to social determinants of health and both can be associated with greater prevalence of CVD risk factors (e.g. hypertension, diabetes, cardiometabolic disease) impacting cardiovascular health outcomes [1,2,16,17] There is a growing area of research, including research frameworks assessing the relationship of misinformation and health literacy on health promotion, health disparities, and healthcare delivery [3,16,18]

Misinformation: cholesterol and statins

Throughout social media there is widespread dissemination of misinformation about cholesterol, dyslipidemia, and lipid-lowering therapies – especially statins [8] Other common topics of health misinformation include vaccines, disease outbreaks/response strategies (e.g. COVID-19 pandemic), and health behavior (e.g. various diet programs) [15] Over time, there has been rising negativity, misinformation and distrust surrounding statins on social media [8,10,19] One study assessed statin-related discussions on Twitter (now X) between 2010 and 2022, analyzing over 1.15 million original tweets, using machine learning models [10] During the study period, human-generated tweets, compared to social bot contributions, rose from approximately 26 % to nearly 80 % [10] Despite this shift in authorship, public skepticism, statin hesitancy and negative sentiment toward statins have increased [10,20] There has been greater emphasis on potential adverse effects of statins with underreporting of evidence-based benefits [20]

Statin and cholesterol misinformation adversely affect health through multiple mechanisms contributing to higher cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [7,8,21] Misinformation alters CVD risk perception, promotes myths on the role of cholesterol in cardiovascular health, limits adoption of statins and other lipid-lowering therapies, and supports the use of alternate therapies that may not offer similar cardiovascular risk reduction [7,8,21] Research demonstrates that exposure to predominantly negative statin-related information decreases the potential an individual would start a statin, adhere to statins, and/or recommend statins to a family member. Statin misinformation also promotes the nocebo effect, where patients experience adverse symptoms after starting statins due to negative expectations rather than pharmacological causes [20] In contrast, balanced representation of potential statin-related benefits and side effects support shared decision-making discussions, and greater initiation and adherence to statins [22] Misinformation also decreases patients’ confidence in medical professionals, limiting delivery of other evidence-based healthcare including vaccines and management of other chronic conditions [6]

Despite medical innovations and advances in healthcare delivery, cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death. Statins have demonstrated long-term effectiveness and safety for primary and secondary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [23], with elevated LDL-C increasing risk of ASCVD morbidity and mortality [24] The ongoing spread of medical misinformation contributes to preventable harm[8] and contributes to ongoing health disparities in cholesterol treatment. An analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2007 to 2018, of US lipid treatment and control in over 33,000 individuals highlighted persistent disparities in control of hypercholesterolemia [25] Among US adults receiving statin therapy, women had lower rates of lipid control than men [25] Additionally, individuals self-identifying as Black adults and most Hispanic ethnicity adults (except individuals self-identifying as Mexican American) had significantly lower lipid control rates compared to individuals self-identifying as White adults [25] Prior research has demonstrated that many patients do not take statins as prescribed and there is ongoing suboptimal adherence across both primary and secondary prevention populations [26]

Strategies to mitigate the impact of health misinformation

Proactive, interdisciplinary interventions are needed to limit the dissemination of health misinformation (Table). Strategic communication efforts are needed to counteract misinformation and support informed decision-making in the management of dyslipidemia [20] Potential strategies to mitigate health misinformation include promoting public health awareness campaigns on the dangers of health misinformation, increasing transparency to assess the quality of websites, and improving the readability and applicability of patient-centered health-related content for the lay community [5,[8], [9], [10], [11], [12],19,21]

It is imperative to target educational programs to improve health and digital literacy to support informed decision-making regarding cardiovascular disease prevention and dyslipidemia management [8] Public health initiatives should target readability of consumer medical websites and online patient education materials (OPEMs) to support health literacy [1] A recent analysis demonstrated that the average readability grade level of statin-related OPEMs across government, commercial/industry, lay press and other sources was 10.9, exceeding the recommended 6th-grade level [1] Surpassing readability recommendations make patient education materials less accessible and less applicable to the broader community; unfortunately, more readable OPEMs often lack reliability, contributing to misinformation [1] Another analysis of online printable education materials from US and Canadian national health organizations demonstrated that the readability averaged 11th-grade reading level, with also inadequate information on management of cholesterol [27] There needs to be greater access to high quality, reliable cholesterol and lipid-lowering information at recommended readability level to support health literacy, promote informed medical decision-making, optimize healthcare delivery and support equitable cardiovascular outcomes [1]

However, effective public health strategies must address the social context in which misinformation spreads, not just the content itself [17] Individuals are more likely to share misinformation that aligns with their pre-existing beliefs, which can reinforce misleading narratives [6] Individuals tend to trust and share misinformation from friends or online communities rather than reliable and trustworthy medical sources [6] There needs to be ongoing engagement by medical professionals in shared decision-making discussions to review their patients’ sources of medical information, guide them towards reliable resources, and address misleading narratives [5,[8], [9], [10], [11],19,21] Decision aids should be integrated into clinical workflows to support CVD risk assessment, evidence-based management and shared decision-making [19] Shared decision-making is considered essential in achieving patient-centered care and improving health outcomes while reducing disparities in cardiovascular care [28]

In spite of the benefits of shared decision-making (SDM) discussions, implementation barriers persist including limited clinical training in SDM communication techniques, variability in health literacy among patients, and clinicians’ perception of time constraints [28] The American Heart Association highlighted the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s SHARE model to support SDM training for clinicians (Seek, Help, Assess, Reach, Evaluate) [28] The SHARE model supports patient engagement and participation in the discussion, allows various treatment options to be discussed (e.g. lipid-lowering medication options), allows patients to highlight their goals and concerns, with a decision reached between the patient and clinician that support patient preferences and improve health outcomes. Additionally, close follow-up is needed after shared decision-making discussions to support adherence.

There is growing research on the potential use of artificial intelligence (AI) to assist with filtering misleading content, support broader dissemination of evidence-based information, and strengthen public health efforts to address health disparities [7,13] Finally, engagement is needed to harness social media as an effective educational tool to highlight evidence-based research, review potential benefits and risks of lipid-lowering therapy, and reduce cardiovascular health disparities [11]

Overall, medical misinformation presents a formidable challenge in cardiovascular health, by influencing public perception of evidence-based interventions and cardiovascular risk. Populations with lower health literacy are at greater vulnerability of health misinformation, worsening health outcomes and health disparities. Mitigating misinformation through interdisciplinary collaborations between healthcare professionals, social media platforms, policymakers, and public health efforts can enhance cardiovascular disease prevention and increase equitable health outcomes.

Improve the readability and applicability of consumer health education (e.g., websites, handouts)4. Review patients’ sources of health information; provide alternate sources for accurate, evidence-based information5. Promote shared decision-making discussions in clinical care6.
