Melatonin and circadian regulation of inflammatory–oxidative pathways in pediatric neurological disorders
Authors: Wangwang Chen, Bivek Singh, Huiying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircadian rhythms are intrinsic 24-hour cycles that regulate nearly all aspects of human physiology and play essential roles in brain development, immune regulation, and redox balance. Adult studies have explored the links between circadian rhythms and neurological or metabolic diseases, but research in pediatric populations remains limited. In infants and children, circadian systems undergo rapid maturation, and this developmental window is particularly vulnerable to disruption from maternal, environmental, or endogenous factors. The aim of this review is to examine how circadian mechanisms intersect with inflammatory and oxidative pathways in pediatric neurological disorders, highlighting both mechanistic insights and therapeutic potential. We conducted a narrative review of PubMed/MEDLINE and complementary sources, covering studies published between January 2016 and March 2025, with relevant studies selected for detailed synthesis. Evidence shows that oxidative stress and inflammation are exacerbated by immature circadian control, with glial circadian clocks, clock genes, and cytokine–melatonin interactions playing important roles. Melatonin is frequently identified as a key circadian-regulated mediator in pediatric conditions, including hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy, autism spectrum disorder, metabolic encephalopathies, and sepsis. Preclinical and translational studies demonstrate that melatonin reduces oxidative damage, maintains mitochondrial function, and modulates immune responses, while early clinical data indicate that it is safe and holds promise as an adjunctive therapy. The review further emphasizes that circadian regulation of oxidative stress is shaped by maternal signals, melatonin in breast milk, and environmental exposures in neonates, particularly in preterm infants. Despite growing evidence, major gaps remain, including the lack of pediatric-specific chronotherapy trials, standardized dosing protocols, and time-stamped biospecimen studies. We suggest a feasible roadmap for future melatonin-based pediatric chronotherapy trials, linking mechanistic insights to clinical application. Overall, advancing circadian biology, particularly through melatonin, offers a promising avenue for pediatric neuroprotection and opens new directions for chronotherapy in vulnerable populations.Keywords: Melatonin, Circadian Rhythm, Pediatric Neurological Disorders, Neuroinflammation, Oxidative Stress, Neuroprotection, Chronotherapy, Clock Genes, Neonatal Encephalopathy, Mitochondrial Dysfunction

Introduction

Circadian rhythms are intrinsic 24-hour cycles, derived from the Latin circa diem (“about a day”). They regulate nearly all aspects of human physiology and play essential roles in brain development, immune regulation, and redox balance. A growing body of research has explored the links between circadian rhythms and a wide range of conditions, including neurological, oncological, metabolic, and endocrine disorders such as obesity and diabetes. Disruption of circadian rhythms contributes to disease onset and progression, while many disease states also impair circadian regulation, creating a bidirectional and self-perpetuating cycle [1, 2].

At the center of this system lies the suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN), the brain’s master pacemaker, which synchronizes peripheral clocks distributed across nearly every tissue, including the developing brain. These rhythms are strongly shaped by environmental cues such as light, feeding, and activity.

While circadian biology has been extensively studied in adult neurology, metabolism, and oncology, research specifically addressing its role in pediatric populations remains limited. Developing rhythms in infants and children undergo rapid maturation and are highly sensitive to environmental and maternal signals, making early life uniquely vulnerable to circadian disruption and its downstream effects. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for clarifying the developmental trajectory of neurological disorders.

Despite the expanding evidence linking circadian disruption to diverse diseases, pediatric studies remain scarce. This is largely due to ethical constraints on repeated sampling, methodological challenges in capturing 24-hour rhythms in infants, and the historical research emphasis on adults. There are limited studies and narrative syntheses that integrate melatonin and circadian regulation of inflammatory and oxidative pathways in pediatric neurological disorders. This narrative review seeks to fill the existing gap by examining how circadian mechanisms intersect with inflammatory and oxidative pathways in pediatric neurological disorders, with emphasis on developmental vulnerability, disease susceptibility, and therapeutic potential. Immature circadian systems in early life may heighten oxidative and inflammatory stress, underscoring the urgency of pediatric-focused research. The review synthesizes current evidence on circadian regulation of immune and redox balance, outlining the ontogeny of circadian rhythms and their links to clock-gene regulation, glial circadian clocks, and cytokine–melatonin crosstalk. Despite the pineal gland accounting for the majority of rhythmic endocrine melatonin in circulation, increasing evidence indicates that melatonin is also synthesized locally in multiple tissues, including the brain and immune cells [3]. Accumulating data further suggest mitochondrial synthesis and localization of melatonin within these cells. Unlike pineal-derived melatonin, these extrapineal pools do not substantially contribute to circulating serum levels. Instead, they appear to act primarily in an autocrine and paracrine manner, exerting antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects within their cells of origin. This local production is thought to contribute significantly to the maintenance of redox homeostasis, particularly under conditions of oxidative or inflammatory stress. Accordingly, studies that measure only circulating melatonin concentrations may underestimate the full biological impact of melatonin signaling in pediatric neuroinflammatory contexts [3].

In this review, mechanistic insights are considered within disease-specific contexts such as hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy, autism spectrum disorder, and metabolic encephalopathies. Particular attention is given to the unique vulnerability of neonates and infants, whose immature circadian systems amplify injury risk, a dimension often overlooked in existing literature. Finally, the review highlights the translational implications of circadian-informed therapeutics, particularly melatonin, and identifies knowledge gaps to guide future pediatric research.

Methodology

We conducted a narrative review using PubMed/MEDLINE to identify relevant studies published in English between January 2016 and March 2025. Search terms included combinations of controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords such as circadian rhythm, clock genes, melatonin, inflammation, oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species, paediatrics, neonates, infants, children, and neurological disorders. Studies that explored the intersection of circadian biology with inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways in pediatric populations were considered eligible. Both original research articles and review papers were included to capture mechanistic insights as well as therapeutic perspectives. We included both preclinical and clinical studies.

Publications in languages other than English were excluded. Studies conducted solely in adult populations were excluded, with a few exceptions when they provided mechanistic or translational insights relevant to paediatrics. The initial broad body of literature was screened manually, and relevant studies were selected for detailed analysis as most relevant to the review objectives. Although PubMed was the primary database, we also performed manual searches using Google Scholar where necessary to ensure comprehensive coverage. Findings were organized into major thematic domains, and any uncertainties in study inclusion were resolved through discussion among all authors. Clinical trial registry databases were not systematically searched, as the objective of this narrative review was to synthesize published mechanistic and translational evidence.

Circadian biology and human health

Ontogeny of circadian rhythms

Circadian rhythms are not fully developed at birth. These rhythms are initially immature and progressively mature under the influence of maternal, environmental, and endogenous signals. In utero, the fetus receives its first temporal cues from maternal rhythms, and disturbances during pregnancy or early life may shape neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Prenatal chronodisruption has been linked to impaired fetal brain development. In a prospective human cohort study of 148 mother–infant dyads, Hoyniak et al. demonstrated that irregular maternal sleep timing and circadian misalignment during pregnancy were associated with smaller cortical and subcortical brain volumes in neonates, mediating the effects of prenatal social disadvantage [4]. After birth, infant health continues to be shaped by hormonal rhythms. Delayed synchronization of these rhythms may compromise early neurodevelopment. In a study of infantile colic, İnce et al. showed that melatonin rhythmicity developed later in infants with colic compared to controls, while cortisol slopes were flatter, suggesting delayed maturation of circadian organization [5].

Breastfeeding provides an additional external cue that reinforces maternal–infant synchrony. A systematic review of 83 studies by Italianer et al. reported significant circadian variation in human milk components, including melatonin, cortisol, tryptophan, and fats [6]. These rhythmic signals act as external chronobiological cues that help entrain neonatal circadian rhythms and support optimal growth and neurological outcomes.

Preterm birth further complicates this process. Van Gilst et al. emphasized that preterm birth abruptly interrupts maternal–fetal rhythmic synchrony, exposing infants to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) environment where constant light and noise obscure natural day–night cues [7]. This disruption in environmental signaling can impair the developmental trajectory of preterm infants, with loss of coordination between central and peripheral clocks affecting brain development. Interventions such as cycled lighting and chrono-nutrition are being explored to restore circadian entrainment in this vulnerable population.

These studies highlight the interplay between prenatal maternal cues, endogenous hormonal maturation, postnatal maternal–infant synchrony, and environmental exposures. These human and translational studies collectively suggest that the maturation of circadian rhythms is linked to early neurodevelopmental trajectories, although the extent and causality of these relationships have yet to be completely established [4–7].

Inflammation and oxidative stress

In neonates and infants, neuronal growth is rapid, oxygen consumption is high, and antioxidant defenses are immature. These features, together with underdeveloped circadian rhythms, make them vulnerable. Disruption of circadian timing can further amplify oxidative injury and inflammatory cascades. Inflammation and oxidative stress therefore play a major role in shaping neurodevelopment and may drive neurodegeneration in the pediatric population.

Melatonin is mechanistically relevant to neonatal brain injury because it can modulate oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades that are disproportionately amplified in the immature brain. In preterm infants, fragile germinal matrix vasculature predisposes to haemorrhage, and secondary injury is driven by haemoglobin breakdown products, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), and glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity [8]. These processes activate apoptotic and necrotic pathways, amplify neuroinflammation, and contribute to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. Similarly, in experimental hypoxic–ischaemic brain injury, synaptic disruption, reduced sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) activity, and microglia-driven oxidative imbalance contribute to progressive neuronal loss [9]. Across these convergent pathways, melatonin has been shown to attenuate ROS accumulation, suppress inflammatory signalling, and preserve synaptic integrity in experimental models, positioning it as a circadian-linked regulator of the oxidative–inflammatory axis central to neonatal vulnerability.

Clinical and translational studies also support these findings. Procaccini et al. systematically reviewed melatonin use in hospitalized infants with infection, respiratory distress, hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, and jaundice [10]. Although the studies were small and heterogeneous, melatonin was safe and showed reductions in inflammatory markers. Rashidi et al. reviewed intrauterine growth restriction, a condition marked by oxidative stress and placental dysfunction, and highlighted melatonin’s antioxidant and vasodilatory actions as possible ways to support fetal growth and reduce long-term neurological complications [11].

Other preclinical studies also strengthen this link. Amer et al. showed that melatonin protected against valproic acid–induced oxidative and inflammatory toxicity in rats, pointing to its role as an adjuvant in neurological therapy [12]. Costa et al. reviewed sedation practices in ventilated newborns and noted melatonin’s analgesic and antioxidant properties, suggesting it as a safer adjunct to opioids [13]. Xie et al. demonstrated that melatonin inhibited abnormal angiogenesis in retinopathy of prematurity by regulating the Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1-alpha / Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (HIF-1α/VEGF) pathway, again pointing to oxidative and inflammatory pathways as central therapeutic targets [14].

These findings show that inflammation and oxidative stress are central mechanisms in pediatric neurodevelopment and disease, and they place melatonin, a circadian-regulated hormone, as a potential therapeutic link between circadian biology and pediatric neuroprotection. Developmental Circadian Disruption and Melatonin-Centred Modulation of Pediatric Neuroinflammatory Vulnerability

Circadian regulation of neuroinflammation

Core clock genes and immune regulation

The molecular framework of the circadian clock is built from a core group of transcription factors, including brain and muscle ARNT-like protein 1 (BMAL1), period (PER), cryptochrome (CRY), circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK), and the nuclear receptors REV-ERB and retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor (ROR). The BMAL1:CLOCK heterodimer stimulates the transcription of Per and Cry, whose protein products feed back to inhibit BMAL1:CLOCK activity, forming the primary negative feedback loop. Superimposed on this, REV-ERB and ROR rhythmically repress or activate BMAL1, fine-tuning the amplitude of circadian transcription. This molecular framework regulates not only metabolic cycles but also immune-related gene expression, including cytokines and inflammasome components [15].

The association between clock genes and immune regulation has been supported by several preclinical and translational studies. In their review of Rev-Erb in metabolism and tissue fibrosis, Raza et al. reported that pharmacological activation of Rev-Erb diminishes nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) activity in preclinical models [16]. NF-κB is a key transcription factor involved in the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the propagation of inflammatory responses. On this basis, circadian components such as Rev-Erb may influence inflammatory tone through modulation of NF-κB-dependent pathways. Although primarily demonstrated in metabolic and fibrotic contexts, this regulatory axis provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how circadian transcription factors could constrain excessive inflammation in pediatric conditions characterised by immune and redox vulnerability.

Evidence of circadian–immune interaction in human neonates has been demonstrated in infants with neonatal encephalopathy (NE). In a prospective ex vivo study, Aslam et al. evaluated whole blood responses during the first week of life and observed that lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a bacterial endotoxin that activates innate immune signalling via Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), significantly reduced BMAL1 expression in infants with NE [17]. LPS stimulation also increased neutrophil activation markers, including CD11b, a surface adhesion molecule associated with inflammatory cell recruitment. In this context, melatonin treatment attenuated LPS-induced upregulation of CD11b and TLR4 expression, indicating modulation of innate immune activation. No significant diurnal variation in circadian gene expression or immune markers was observed during the first postnatal week. These findings suggest that inflammatory stimuli can disrupt circadian transcription in neonatal immune cells and that melatonin may partially modulate this interaction during a developmentally sensitive period.

These findings are also supported by glial studies. Chen et al. described how astrocytes and microglia possess intrinsic circadian clocks that regulate phagocytosis, immune surveillance, and cytokine release [18]. Because these rhythms are still developing in early life, pediatric brains are particularly vulnerable to circadian disruption. Disrupted glial clocks may impair synaptic pruning and immune responses, linking circadian dysregulation to neurodevelopmental susceptibility.

This bidirectional interaction becomes particularly important in early life, when oxidative stress and inflammation can themselves alter circadian transcription. Yang et al. demonstrated that oxidative stress and inflammation can directly reprogram circadian machinery in neonatal mice [19]. Hyperoxia increased REV-ERBα transcription through nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2), whereas inflammatory nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling suppressed it. This effect was seen in neonates but not in adults, highlighting the developmental sensitivity of clock–immune interactions.

Overall, these studies demonstrate that circadian genes regulate not only daily rhythms but also directly shape inflammatory pathways. Circadian–immune coupling may be developmentally immature during early life. Circadian disruption may influence neonatal inflammatory and oxidative responses. However, the long-term neurodevelopmental consequences and the extent to which these effects are modifiable in humans remain uncertain [16, 17].

Microglia and astrocyte circadian clocks in paediatrics

Microglia and astrocytes are two well-studied glial cells that display circadian rhythmicity and are influenced by diurnal variation. Microglia function as resident macrophages of the brain, while astrocytes, the most abundant glial cells, provide structural and metabolic support. Both cell types contain intrinsic circadian clocks that regulate inflammation and neuroprotection. Because these clocks are still maturing in infants and young children, the developing brain is particularly vulnerable to oxidative and inflammatory stress. Evidence from preclinical and clinical research suggests that melatonin can modulate these pathways and help safeguard neurodevelopment.

Across diverse neonatal injury models, microglial overactivation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammasome amplification converge as shared drivers of neurodevelopmental injury. In hypoxic–ischaemic models, microglia-driven oxidative imbalance contributes to neuronal death, while melatonin attenuates these cascades and supports synaptic repair [9]. Similar redox–mitophagy disruption has been observed following repeated neonatal anesthetic exposure, where melatonin restores mitochondrial balance and limits glial activation [20]. Ribeiro et al. showed that neonatal exposure to L-2-hydroxyglutaric acid induced oxidative stress, astrocytic reactivity, microglial activation, and demyelination, all of which were prevented by melatonin co-treatment, highlighting its therapeutic potential in neurometabolic disorders [21]. Favrais et al. found that prenatal melatonin prevented lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced GABAergic neuron loss in a chorioamnionitis model, although oligodendrocyte and myelin protection was only partial, indicating cell-type–specific effects [22].

Clinical studies further support these findings. Dumbuya et al. reviewed pediatric sepsis-associated encephalopathy and identified glial activation as a driver of neuroinflammation, pointing to melatonin as a promising adjunct therapy [23]. Häusler et al. likewise noted that in encephalopathy of prematurity, melatonin’s strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions may support microglial and astrocytic development, though pharmacokinetic and efficacy studies are still required for clinical translation [24].

These studies show that immature circadian regulation in glial cells amplifies pediatric vulnerability to injury, while melatonin consistently emerges as a modulator of microglial and astrocytic function across diverse models.

Cytokine-melatonin interaction

A consistent theme across pediatric inflammatory conditions is the dynamic interaction between melatonin signaling and cytokine regulation. In neonatal and pediatric models, melatonin consistently tracks with or suppresses inflammatory signaling. Systematic reviews in hospitalized neonates have shown that melatonin reduces inflammatory markers in conditions such as infection, respiratory distress, hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE), and jaundice [10, 25]. In asthmatic children, higher oxidative stress and pollution exposure were associated with increased urinary melatonin metabolites and reduced airway inflammation, suggesting a compensatory anti-inflammatory effect [26].

Experimental perinatal studies indicate that antenatal melatonin inhibits IL-1β and related pathways [27]. Reviews in autism spectrum disorder (ASD) further link inflammatory cascades and kynurenine metabolism with potential melatonin dysregulation [28, 29]. Although formal studies of 24-hour cytokine rhythmicity remain limited, current evidence supports a role for melatonin–cytokine crosstalk in pediatric populations.

NF-κB, NLRP3 inflammasome, and circadian crosstalk

In neonatal models, melatonin consistently suppresses NF-κB and inflammasome signaling that contribute to tissue injury. In neonatal white-matter damage, melatonin reduced NOD-, leucine-rich repeat-, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) activation, lowered IL-1β and IL-18, decreased ROS, inhibited TLR4–NF-κB, and restored PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)–Parkin mitophagy [30]. In a neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis model, melatonin improved survival and intestinal histology while repressing NLRP3, TLR4, NF-κB, caspase-1, and systemic cytokines [31]. In neonatal asphyxia, described as a High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1)–TLR4–NF-κB–driven inflammatory state, melatonin is considered a potential adjunct to hypothermia [32].

Mechanistic reviews in neonatology also support melatonin’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, showing regulation of cell death and inflammasome activity through NF-κB down-regulation [33]. Broader studies in hypoxic–ischaemic brain injury link melatonin neuroprotection to oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and SIRT1-related pathways [9]. Additional translational data suggest that melatonin suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines while activating cytoprotective programs such as Nrf2/MST1 (mammalian sterile 20-like kinase 1) in systemic neonatal infection models [34].

These findings establish a robust melatonin–NF-κB/NLRP3 inhibitory axis in neonates. However, direct pediatric evidence connecting clock genes to these pathways is still lacking, underscoring a critical gap in circadian–immune research. Circadian control of oxidative stress

Tryptophan, an essential amino acid, serves as a precursor for melatonin synthesis. Breast-milk melatonin plays an important role in shaping the infant gut–brain axis, influencing immune maturation, microbiome composition, and neurodevelopmental trajectories. Acting both as a circadian signal and an antioxidant, it helps stabilize redox tone and modulate inflammatory pathways during critical developmental windows. Disruption of maternal melatonergic pathways may lower melatonin levels in milk, which in turn can affect infant immune balance and brain development [35].

In vitro studies show that human milk–derived tryptophan has a high oxygen radical scavenging capacity and reduces tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 ( IL-6). Its effects on cell proliferation are concentration-dependent, with low doses inhibiting glioma growth and very high concentrations of certain metabolites (including melatonin) promoting proliferation, especially in the presence of IFN-γ [36]. These biochemical properties provide a mechanistic basis for the proposed role of melatonin and its precursors in shaping early-life redox homeostasis.

Clinical evidence supports these observations. Children with severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) exhibit significantly higher nocturnal melatonin peaks compared to critically ill non-TBI controls, reflecting a compensatory neuroprotective response to oxidative and inflammatory stress [37]. In neonatal sepsis, mitochondria-centred redox imbalance and cytokine-driven ROS sustain pro-oxidant and pro-inflammatory loops beyond pathogen clearance. In this setting, melatonin acts as an adjuvant antioxidant, protecting mitochondrial function and supporting restoration of redox balance [38].

A similar effect has been observed in renal pathology: phosphate overload accelerates kidney decline through tubular oxidative stress and inflammation, whereas melatonin suppresses phosphate-induced ROS in renal epithelia [39]. These findings support the concept that melatonergic signaling, shaped by precursor availability and daily rhythms, buffers oxidative load in early life and represents a promising approach for pediatric chronotherapy.

Diseases specific sections

Hypoxic–Ischaemic Encephalopathy (HIE) / Encephalopathy of Prematurity (EoP)

Melatonin, the primary circadian hormone, is closely linked with inflammation and oxidative stress in neonatal HIE models. In preterm fetal sheep, post-insult melatonin reduced apoptosis, microglial activation, and oxidative DNA damage while supporting white-matter repair [40]. In near-term lambs, combining melatonin with therapeutic hypothermia normalised brain metabolic ratios and further reduced neuroinflammation and cell death compared with either therapy alone [41]. On the receptor level, HIE downregulates MT1 receptors; melatonin restores MT1, limits mitochondrial cell-death signaling, and suppresses glial activation. These benefits are lost with MT1 deletion or pharmacologic blockade, indicating a receptor-dependent mechanism of protection [42].

These findings align with a broader inflammatory–redox framework described in Mechanisms. Melatonin stands out in this context because it crosses the blood–brain barrier and protects mitochondrial function. The older concept of “oxygen radical disease of the newborn” has been reframed to highlight mitochondrial vulnerability in both brain and lung, further supporting melatonin’s relevance [43, 44]. Early studies also showed how rapidly inflammatory genes surge after HIE, underscoring the importance of timing and target selection for any antioxidant strategy [45].

White-matter protection is also mechanistically linked to inflammasome control [30]. However, cell-specific sensitivity remains evident. For instance, in a prenatal chorioamnionitis model, melatonin preserved GABAergic neurons but did not rescue oligodendrocyte lineage or myelination [22]. Clinical translation in neonatal HIE and encephalopathy of prematurity remains limited despite robust neuroprotective effects demonstrated in fetal sheep, lamb, and rodent models. Human studies to date are small and heterogeneous in dosing, timing, and outcome measures. A definitive long-term neurodevelopmental benefit has not been established. Although melatonin has a favourable safety profile and strong biological plausibility as an antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent, it has not been incorporated into standard therapeutic recommendations. Interpretation of early clinical findings is complicated by concurrent therapeutic hypothermia and by developmental variability in pharmacokinetics between preterm and term neonates. These translational gaps highlight the need for rigorously designed and adequately powered pediatric trials before routine clinical implementation can be considered [24, 46, 47].

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is characterised by immune activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and nitro-oxidative stress, which closely interact with sleep and circadian disruption. In this context, melatonin offers a clock-anchored approach to stabilize sleep physiology while reducing oxidative stress and inflammatory burden. Mechanistically, ASD cohorts show elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, microglial activation, nitrosative stress (e.g., peroxynitrite), and bioenergetic deficits. Melatonin may counteract these changes through melatonin receptor 1 (MT1) and melatonin receptor 2 (MT2)-mediated circadian entrainment, direct scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), support of mitochondrial defenses, and suppression of cytokine output [28].

On the clinical side, melatonin has been studied as both a chronobiotic and an antioxidant/anti-inflammatory adjunct. Nutraceutical reviews indicate that it consistently improves sleep onset and maintenance in children with ASD. Some studies also report modest benefits on daytime behaviors, although findings are variable and evidence quality remains mixed [48].

Overall, melatonin supports a circadian-based strategy for ASD by restoring sleep and targeting redox and inflammatory pathways, aligning with the immuno-oxidative phenotype described in this disorder [28, 48].

Mitochondrial & metabolic disorders with neurologic involvement

More than 1,500 inherited metabolic disorders have been described, representing a broad and clinically heterogeneous group that includes numerous disorders with neurological involvement [49]. Mitochondrial and bioenergetic dysfunction, oxidative stress, glial activation, and excitotoxic mechanisms may contribute to neurological injury in selected neurometabolic disorders. However, within the literature identified for this review, evidence examining melatonin or chronobiological approaches in this field was limited. Experimental studies involving D-2-hydroxyglutaric aciduria and L-2-hydroxyglutaric aciduria represent notable exceptions: the accumulated metabolites induced oxidative stress, neuronal and glial injury, and neurobehavioral or bioenergetic abnormalities, while melatonin attenuated several of these deleterious effects [21, 50].

Metabolic crises and organic acidurias illustrate how circadian redox regulation intersects with pediatric neuroinjury. In diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), tryptophan–kynurenine and serotonin/melatonin metabolites decline early and then gradually recover over weeks in parallel with oxidative stress markers. This pattern suggests a temporary down-shift of endogenous melatonin defenses at the time when the brain is most vulnerable [50].

In neonatal models of D-2- and L-2-hydroxyglutaric acidurias, accumulated metabolites drive ROS/RNS production, mitochondrial dysfunction, demyelination, and activation of microglia and astrocytes, leading to lasting motor and cognitive impairments. Melatonin co-treatment reduces oxidative damage, preserves neurons and myelin, and improves behavioral outcomes, with additional evidence that N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) -linked excitotoxicity contributes to injury in the D-2-hydroxyglutarate (D-2-HG) model [21, 51].

These findings support a circadian-informed approach to pediatric metabolic encephalopathies, aligning treatment with melatonin’s receptor-mediated and antioxidant actions to restore redox balance and mitigate downstream neuroinflammation. Translational and therapeutic implications

The current evidence remains heterogeneous, even though melatonin has been studied across a wide range of paediatric and experimental settings. Study populations, disease models, dosing strategies, timing of administration, and outcome measures vary considerably. While many studies report significant changes in inflammatory and oxidative markers, consistent long-term clinical benefit has not yet been established. At present, melatonin has not been incorporated into formal therapeutic guidelines for most paediatric neurological conditions. This likely reflects the need for adequately powered, well-designed randomized trials with standardized chronobiological frameworks rather than a lack of biological rationale.

There have been several recent advances in circadian biology, opening new avenues for chronotherapy and chrononutrition. Research on chronotherapy is expanding across all fields of medicine, not just neurology. With the support of newer biomarker guidance, chronotherapy shows strong promise for improving therapeutic outcomes. In late-onset neonatal sepsis, serum melatonin increased and closely correlated with C-reactive protein, suggesting that endogenous melatonin reflects inflammatory burden and may be useful in combination with timing strategies [52]. In adults with multiple sclerosis, serum melatonin correlated with urinary 6-sulfatoxymelatonin across doses, offering a simple exposure–response assay pair that could guide future pediatric trials [53]. Breast milk melatonin also follows a circadian rhythm and likely helps synchronize infant clocks while providing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory support. This foundation is highly relevant for brain-directed chronotherapy in both preterm and term neonates [54].

Preterm and term infants in neonatal intensive care units, especially those on ventilation, are repeatedly exposed to painful stimuli that sustain oxidative and inflammatory loops. Melatonin has a favorable safety profile, and evidence supports its analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective roles. Once dosing is standardized, this could support opioid-sparing and night-preferential protocols [13, 55]. A pediatric inpatient review, mostly neonatal, also reported reductions in inflammatory markers across infection, respiratory distress, neurologic injury, and jaundice, supporting the idea of hospital-based chronotherapy approaches [10].

Another emerging application is perioperative and anesthetic neurotoxicity. In neonatal rodent models, melatonin prevented later learning and memory deficits following repeated sevoflurane or isoflurane exposure. Benefits included restoration of mitophagy and ATP levels, reduced microglial activation, and decreased endoplasmic reticulum stress and apoptosis through sirtuin-1, mitofusin-2, and PERK (protein kinase RNA-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase) signaling. These effects could be timed around planned anesthetic exposures [20, 56].

Infection-driven brain injury, such as sepsis and sepsis-associated encephalopathy, involves oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier disruption, and glial activation. In neonatal sepsis trials, adjunctive melatonin reduced C-reactive protein within 24 h and improved short-term clinical recovery [57]. Mechanistic syntheses further indicate that melatonin interrupts self-sustaining pro-oxidant and pro-inflammatory feedback loops while preserving mitochondrial integrity [25, 38, 58]. In pediatric sepsis-associated encephalopathy, these same injury pathways have been identified as central drivers of neuroinflammation, and melatonin has been proposed as a potential adjunctive strategy, with future trials requiring attention to time-of-day sampling and dosing [23]. Clinically, children with severe traumatic brain injury have been shown to exhibit significantly higher nocturnal melatonin peaks compared to critically ill non-TBI peers, consistent with an endogenous compensatory response to oxidative and neuroinflammatory stress [37].

Clock-linked immunomodulation is also observed in genetic and developmental conditions. In Down syndrome, melatonin inhibited neutrophil and monocyte activation, downregulated toll-like receptor and myeloid differentiation primary response 88 signaling, and reduced IL-1β and NLRP3 inflammasome activity, all of which contribute to downstream neuroinflammation [59]. In early-life immune activation models, postnatal melatonin reduced microglial activation, tumor necrosis factor-α, nitric oxide, and lipid peroxidation, while also lowering anxiety- and depression-like behaviors later in life, suggesting durable neuroimmune benefits when given in the neonatal period [60].

Metabolic and environmental contexts also align with this framework. In intrauterine growth restriction, cord-blood melatonin is reduced, which may compromise fetal antioxidant defenses and increase vulnerability to hypoxic stress. This opens a potential role for maternal or neonatal melatonin strategies guided by time-controlled sampling [11, 61]. In asthmatic children, higher exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and ozone, together with elevated oxidative stress markers, was associated with increased urinary 6-sulfatoxymelatonin, reduced airway inflammation, and improved lung function. While not neurologic, these findings are consistent with the compensatory melatonin responses seen in traumatic brain injury and sepsis [26].

Importantly, melatonin also demonstrates disease-modifying potential. In three children with Charcot–Marie–Tooth 1 A, high-dose melatonin normalized glutathione redox balance, reduced lipid peroxidation, and lowered multiple proinflammatory cytokines over six months [62].

Future pediatric trials should be prioritized, focusing on standardized melatonin dosing and the collection of biospecimens according to clock time. Pairing serum melatonin with urinary 6-sulfatoxymelatonin (aMT6s) can provide reliable measures of exposure and response. These trials should also include mitochondrial and inflammasome endpoints, such as SIRT1 activity, mitophagy markers, and NLRP3 activation. The areas of focus should be periods of heightened neurobiologic vulnerability, including mechanically ventilated neonates (as an opioid-sparing adjunct), peri-anesthetic exposures, sepsis and sepsis-associated encephalopathy, and high-risk neurodevelopmental conditions. Incorporating these elements will help bridge current research gaps and allow researchers to determine whether circadian-aligned melatonin can consistently reduce oxidative and inflammatory injury in pediatric neurological care. Research gaps and future directions

We did not systematically screen clinical trial registries; therefore, unpublished or withdrawn pediatric trials may not have been captured. There is a growing number of studies exploring circadian regulation of inflammation and oxidative stress, but critical gaps remain in the pediatric context. First, there is a lack of comprehensive mapping of circadian–immune–redox interactions in neonates and children, particularly at the level of clock-gene regulation and its downstream impact on NF-κB and inflammasome signaling. Most evidence comes from adult or animal models, leaving pediatric mechanisms underexplored. Second, time-stamped biospecimen studies are urgently needed to capture rhythmic patterns of cytokines, oxidative markers, and melatonin metabolites across the 24-hour cycle in early life, as these data would provide a mechanistic foundation for pediatric chronobiology. Third, pediatric-specific randomized controlled trials of chronotherapy are almost entirely absent. Melatonin has shown promise as a safe adjunct in neonatal and pediatric conditions, but rigorously designed RCTs incorporating circadian timing, dose optimization, and long-term outcomes are still lacking. Finally, the integration of wearable devices and actigraphy-based monitoring offers a practical avenue to assess circadian disruption and recovery in children, yet such tools remain underutilized in pediatric neurology research. Addressing these gaps will open opportunities for future research and support the development of personalized circadian-based therapies for pediatric neurological disorders. We suggest a feasible roadmap for upcoming melatonin-based pediatric chronotherapy trials to address these gaps. Core design components include weight-adjusted pharmacokinetics, paired biomarker assays (serum melatonin with urinary aMT6s), candidate trial archetypes (HIE, NICU ventilation, sepsis, ASD), time-stamped biospecimen collection, and environment/entrainment controls. This framework offers a translational bridge from mechanistic promise to clinical application.

Limitations

Our narrative review has several limitations. Being a narrative review, we did not apply a systematic framework, and manual screening without a formal risk-of-bias assessment may introduce subjectivity. The search relied primarily on PubMed/MEDLINE with limited use of Google Scholar, so important studies in other languages or databases may have been missed. A few studies from adult neurology were included to broaden understanding, but these may not fully represent pediatric physiology. There is also the possibility of publication bias, as positive findings are more likely to be published. Finally, because our review included preclinical, clinical, and review studies with variability in design, endpoints, and outcome measures, direct comparison and synthesis remain challenging.

Conclusion

From the earliest stages of life, when maternal cues and infant circadian rhythms are still maturing, disruption of these processes heightens vulnerability to oxidative and inflammatory injury. Circadian mechanisms play a critical role across fetal and early postnatal neurodevelopment. The immaturity of these systems in early life increases vulnerability to oxidative injury, immune dysregulation, and long-term neurological impairment. Growing evidence highlights melatonin as a central circadian-regulated mediator that not only entrains developing rhythms but also protects against redox imbalance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammasome-driven inflammation. Several pediatric conditions (hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy, autism spectrum disorder, metabolic encephalopathies, and sepsis) intersect with circadian biology, and although current studies remain limited, melatonin consistently emerges as a promising adjunctive therapy with a favorable safety profile.

However, pediatric-specific chronotherapy studies are still lacking. Standardized dosing protocols, time-stamped biospecimen collection, and incorporation of mitochondrial and immune endpoints will be critical to advance the field. Wearable devices and actigraphy also offer opportunities to monitor circadian disruption and personalize interventions. Together, these approaches will allow deeper mechanistic understanding and may establish melatonin as a novel and feasible strategy to mitigate inflammatory and oxidative stress–mediated brain injury in children. Integrating circadian biology could therefore open a new frontier in pediatric neuroprotection, bridging fundamental science with clinical care.
