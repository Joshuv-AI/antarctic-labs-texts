Claudin-4 as a dual regulator of genome stability and immune evasion in high grade serous ovarian cancer
Authors: Benjamin G Bitler, Julie Lang, Daniel Nunez-Avellaneda, Kian Behbakht, Natalie R Davidson, Elizabeth R Woodruff, Fabian R Villagomez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) thrives in an immune-suppressive microenvironment marked by poor T-cell infiltration and blunted anti-tumor responses, driving immune evasion, tumor progression, and therapy resistance. Regulation of genomic instability, which restricts cytoplasmic DNA accumulation, is essential to prevent immune activation. Claudin-4, often overexpressed in HGSC, is strongly linked to therapy resistance and plays a key role in regulating initiation and resolution of genomic instability, shaping the tumor’s potential ability to evade immune surveillance and withstand treatment; however, its role in immune evasion remains unclear. We used in vitro ovarian cancer models with stable claudin-4 overexpression and knockdown. In a humanized mouse model, ovarian tumors were treated with the claudin mimic peptide (CMP) and a PARP inhibitor. Tumor growth and immune infiltration were assessed by IVIS imaging and spectral flow cytometry. Mechanistic studies included autophagy flux and ISRE reporter assays, complemented by immunoblotting, flow cytometry, confocal microscopy, and bioinformatic analyses of public datasets. We identified a novel claudin-4-driven mechanism that promotes immune evasion in HGSC. Claudin-4 regulated type I interferon signaling through a close association with the small GTPase Rab7, modulating tumor-immune interactions. This was linked to TCR-zeta chain suppression in T cells in vivo—a hallmark of immune evasion. Dual targeting of claudin-4-expressing tumors with CMP and niraparib reshaped the tumor immune microenvironment, restoring TCR-zeta expression and promoting CD8 + T-cell infiltration, leading to improved anti-tumor efficacy of the PARP inhibitor niraparib. Our data show that claudin-4 orchestrates tumor immune evasion and survival, positioning it as a dual regulator of genome stability and immune escape, and highlighting it as a promising therapeutic target in ovarian cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23137-1.Keywords: Ovarian cancer, Tumor immune evasion, Rab7, Type I INF response, Claudin-4, PARPiSubject terms: Cancer microenvironment, Gynaecological cancer, Tumour heterogeneity, Tumour immunology

Introduction

High grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) of tubo-ovarian origin is a highly aggressive and heterogeneous malignancy, often diagnosed at an advanced stage due to the lack of early symptoms and effective screening. In the USA, standard first-line treatment includes tumor debulking surgery followed by platinum-based chemotherapy. PARP inhibitors (PARPi) and bevacizumab have been added as maintenance therapies, particularly for patients with BRCA mutations or homologous recombination deficiency, to improve progression-free survival. However, HGSC still has a poor prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate below 50%, mainly due to high recurrence rates and therapy resistance, which severely limit treatment efficacy and underscore the urgent need to address the mechanisms driving therapy resistance1–4.

Genomic instability is a hallmark of cancer5 and a key driver of therapy resistance, enabling tumors to adapt and evade treatment. It fosters intratumoral heterogeneity, accelerates tumor progression, and facilitates immune evasion, compromising therapeutic efficacy and contributing to disease recurrence6–10. This instability arises from alterations in cell cycle progression and DNA damage repair mechanisms, resulting in an increased rate of mutations and generation of multiple chromosomal modifications, such as amplifications, translocations, and micronuclei formation. Tumor cells actively regulate the generation and accumulation of genomic changes, maintaining instability within a tolerable threshold to avoid severe chromosomal disruptions. However, once this critical threshold is exceeded, tumor cells may lose their ability to control the immune microenvironment, potentially becoming more susceptible to immune recognition and vulnerable to cell death5,9–13. Thus, disrupting the tumor’s finely tuned equilibrium between tolerable genomic instability and immune evasion may unveil a therapeutic vulnerability.

Claudin-4 is aberrantly overexpressed in the majority of epithelial-derived HGSC4,14–17, where it is implicated in the development of therapy resistance and poor patient survival1,4. Claudin-4 plays a key role in regulating genomic instability and functions as a multifunctional protein involved in diverse cellular processes, including cell proliferation18, cell cycle control,12 and DNA damage repair4. Although traditionally recognized as a cell-to-cell junction protein,19,20 recent studies, including our own, reveal that claudin-4 exerts a broader role in ovarian cancer cells. It remodels nuclear architecture and regulates cell cycle progression to prevent genomic alterations,4,12,21 while also facilitating their resolution through autophagy when they arise13.

Accordingly, targeting Claudin-4 could disrupt the tumor’s finely balanced control over genomic instability, potentially reshaping the tumor microenvironment and enhancing the therapeutic outcomes4,12,14. However, despite claudin-4’s known role in genomic instability and well-recognized clinical significance in ovarian cancer4,14–16,19,21,22, the impact of targeting claudin-4 in the ovarian tumor immune microenvironment remains poorly defined. Here, we established both in vitro and in vivo systems to modulate and target claudin-4 function. In vitro, we overexpressed claudin-4 in OVCAR8 cells (which lack endogenous claudin-4) and downregulated it in OVCAR3 cells (which endogenously express high levels of claudin-4). In vivo, we utilized patient-derived xenografts in humanized immune system mice to evaluate claudin-4 targeting. We discovered that claudin-4 orchestrates a type I interferon response, empowering tumor cells to evade immune detection and sustain HGSC survival. In vivo targeting of claudin-4 with CMP amplified the efficacy of the FDA-approved PARPi niraparib, not only suppressing tumor progression but also dismantling key immune evasion mechanisms. This intervention fostered a more immunoreactive tumor microenvironment, marked by a surge in intratumoral CD8 + T cell infiltration, highlighting claudin-4 as a powerful modulator of both immune resistance and therapy response.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Human-derived cells, OVCA429 (RRID: CVCL_3936), OVCAR3 (RRID: CVCL_DH37), and OVCAR8 (RRID: CVCL_1629), collected from the Gynecologic Tissue and Fluid Bank, were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #11875) plus 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Phoenix Scientific, Cat. # PS-100, Lot # 20055-01-01) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Corning, Cat. #30-002-CI) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. HEK293FT (RRID: CVCL_6911) were cultured similarly but in DMEM medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #11995040). Cells were only cultured for up to 20 passages and were tested for mycoplasma during experiments (last tested on September 04, 2024) using the Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. # MP0035).

Inhibition of claudin-4 expression by CRISPRi

Stable transfectants of OVCA429-dCas9 and OVCAR3-dCas9 cells were generated by co-transfection with the dCas9 vector (pB-CAGGS-dCas9-KRAB-MeCP2, RRID: Addgene_110824) and the Super PiggyBac Transposase Expression Vector (pCRISPRia-v2; RRID: Addgene_84832) at a DNA ratio of 2.5:1, respectively, using Lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher, cat: 11668-019) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Selection was carried out by antibiotic resistance (blasticidin). A gRNA (forward: GCTGGCTTGCGCATCAGGAC; reverse: GTCCTGATGCGCAAGCCAGC) specific for human claudin-4 was generated in the Broad Institute portal (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/gppx/crispick/public). Subsequently, the pCRISPRia-v2_base (TagBFP) vector was digested (BstXI and BlpI) to insert the gRNA by ligation, followed by cloning using E. coli (Stbl3™, ThermoFisher, cat: C737303). dCas9-expressing cells were transfected with the pCRISPRia-v2_base vector containing gRNA, as indicated above.

Vectors, lentivirus production and transduction

HEK293FT cells (RRID: CVCL_6911) were transfected using lipocomplexes (Lipofectamine 2000, ThermoFisher, cat: 11668-019) containing the viral packaging system of second generation (psPAX2, RRID: Addgene_12260; pMD2.G, RRID: Addgene_12259) as well as the lentiviral construct of interest (ISRE Luciferase Reporter Lentivirus, Catalog #79824; pLenti-Lifeact-tdTomato, RRID: Addgene_64048; STING-miRFP680, RRID: Addgene_200940, and GFP-tagged claudin-4 pLenti-C-mGFP vector; Cat.# RC200490L2, OriGene, Rockville, MD, USA. From this plasmid, the GFP tag was removed using conventional molecular cloning techniques), respectively. Supernatant from transfected HEK293FT cells was collected, filtered (0.45 μm), used, or stored (-80 °C). We also used EGFP-Rab7A (RRID: Addgene_28047) to transfect ovarian cancer cells.

Immunoblot and CMP synthesis

To analyze the protein expression, tumor cells were scraped from culture plates in the presence of lysis buffer (30mmol/L Tris HCl pH7.4, 150mmol/L NaCl, 1% TritonX-100, 10% glycerol, 2mmol/L EDTA, 0.57mmol/L PMSF, 1X cOmplete™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail), placed on a shaker for 10 min and spun at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. Protein was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to the PVDF membrane using the TransBlot Turbo system (BioRad). Membranes were blocked with Intercept Blocking Buffer (LI-COR, #927-60001) for 2 h at RT. The following primary antibodies were used and incubated overnight at 4 °C: Rabbit anti claudin-4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. # 36-4800, RRID: AB_2533262, µg/mL); mouse anti-human claudin-4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. # 32-9400, RRID: AB_2533096, 1:500 dil); rabbit anti-GAPDH (Sigma Cat# HPA040067, RRID: AB_10965903, 1: 1000), mouse anti-β-actin (Abcam Cat# ab8226, RRID: AB_306371, 1: 5,000); mouse anti CD3z (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. # MA5-15608, RRID: AB_10986021, 1:500); rabbit anti-PD-1 (Cell signaling, Cat. # 86163, RRID: AB_2728833, 1:1000); rabbit anti- phospho-STING (Cell signaling, cat. # 50907, RRID: AB_2827656, 1: 1000); rabbit anti-STING (Cell signaling, cat. # 13647, RRID: AB_2732796, 1: 1000); rabbit anti-Beclin-1 (Proteintech, cat. # 11306-1-AP, RRID: AB_2259061, 1: 1000); rabbit anti-Rab7 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. # PA5-52369, RRID: AB_2646252, 1: 200); rabbit anti-PAX8 (Cell signaling, Cat. # 59019, RRID: AB_2799553, 1: 1000); rabbit anti-LAMP1 (Cell signaling, Cat. # 9091, RRID: AB_2687579, 1: 1000). Membranes were washed 3 times for 5 min each in TBST (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, room temperature, followed by secondary antibodies (IRDye 680RD Goat anti-Rabbit IgG; RRID: AB_10956166; dilution, 1:20,000; IRDye 800CW Goat anti-Mouse IgG; RRID: AB_621842; dilution, 1:20,000) for 2 h at RT. Membranes were washed again 5 times for 5 min each in TBST. For fluorescent detection, bands were visualized using the LI-COR Odyssey Imaging System. CMP was synthesized as previously reported,23 and its specificity relative to a control peptide has been previously validated4,12,13,15,21,23.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde at 4% (PBS 1X) for 10 min, followed by permeabilization (30 min, 0.1% Triton X-100, PBS 1X). Blocking was carried out by 2 h incubation with BSA at 5% (PBS 1X, RT, shaking). Primary antibodies (as cited above, claudin-4, 1: 500; LAMP1, 1: 500; Rab7, 1: 500; pSTING, 1: 500; CD3-zeta, 1: 500) were incubated (BSA at 2%, PBS 1X) overnight at 4 °C while shaking. Secondary antibodies (AlexaFluor546 anti-mouse, ThermoFisher, cat: A-11030, at 2 µg/mL; AlexaFluor647 anti-rabbit, ThermoFisher, cat: A32733, at 2 µg/mL) were incubated 2 h/shaking at RT (BSA at 2%, PBS 1X). Nuclei were stained with DAPI at 1 µg/mL (PBS 1X) for 10 min. All microscopy acquisition was done in the Neurotechnology Center, University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus.

Autophagy flux by flow cytometry

2 × 105 HGSOC cells were seed onto six-well plates (2 mL complete RPMI medium) and treated with 2’3’-cGAMP (cat: tlrl-nacga23s), 1 µg/mL (5 h). Subsequently, cells were detached (trypsin 0.25 mmol/L) and fixed (PBS-PFA 1%). All analyses were carried out in the Cancer Center Flow Cytometry Shared Resource, University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus.

ISRE (interferon-stimulated response element) assay

OVCAR8, OVCAR3, and OVCA429 (ISRE; 1 × 105) cells were seeded on 24 well-plate (1 mL complete RPMI medium). Next day, cells were washed with sterile PBS (1X) and treated with 2’3’-cGAMP, 1 µg/mL (positive regulator of ISRE; InvivoGen, cat: tlrl-nacga23s) in 1mL complete RPMI medium for 8 h. The luciferase-luciferin reaction was carried out using the luciferase assay system (Promega, cat: E1501) and bioluminescence was detected by GLOMA MULTI detection system (Promega).

In vivo model

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Generation of HIS-BRGS mice and chimerism evaluation

Stem Cell Isolation. Investigators were blinded from donor identities and the studies were performed in compliance with University of Colorado Institutional Review boards (COMIRB#16–0541). Human stem cells were isolated from PBMCs prepared from clinically-rejected CB (cord blood) units from the University of Colorado Cord Blood bank (Clinimmune Labs, Aurora, CO) using CD34 + magnetic Miltenyi beads, and expanded in short-term cultures with IL6 (10 ng/ml), SCF (40 ng/ml) and FLt3L (20 ng/ml). CD34 + cells, harvested between days 4–6, were frozen in 90% FCS/10%DMSO and stored at -80 °C prior to injection into neonate pups.

HIS-BRGS mice and chimerism evaluation

All mouse work was performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved of by the University of Colorado’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol #283). We included neonatal female to generate human immune system mice, (day 1–3) BRGS (BALB/cRag2nullIL2RgnullSirpaNOD). Pups were irradiated with 300 rads 2–6 hours prior to injection with 0.2-0.6 × 106 expanded then thawed CD34 + cells. The number of cells injected is equivalent to 50,000 fresh CD34 + cells per mouse, i.e. cell count prior to in vitro expansion. Mice were bred and engrafted in the Pre-clinical Human Immune System Mouse model Shared Resource at the University of Colorado Denver Anschutz Medical Campus vivarium with prior Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocol and in a facility accredited by the American Association for Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care. BRGS mice, both breeders and engrafted, were maintained on an alternating bi-weekly Septra-enriched (Uniprim, Harlan) diet. Mice were injected in the facial vein, liver, or both.

HIS-PDX

Humanized Immune System (HIS) mice were i.p. injected with 5 × 106 cells of a developed patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model, PDX GTFB 1016, described previously24. Briefly, this model is derived from a primary tumor collected from a patient diagnosed with stage IIIC, who was described to have a high volume of ascites, peritoneal carcinomatosis, and was chemonaïve at the time of sample collection. TP53 and BRCA2 mutations were identified, and we subsequently transfected the cells with a GFP-luciferase tag, enabling tumor tracking by In Vivo Imaging (IVIS, Perkin Elmer), as described previously25,26. We carried out two independent in vivo experiments. In experiment #1, tumors were allowed to establish for three weeks (average female mouse weight: 17.5 g). After this period, mice were randomized into three groups: control peptide (n = 5), Niraparib (50 mg/kg, orally, five days per week with two consecutive days off; n = 6), and CMP (4 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, every other day; n = 6). In experiment #2, tumors were allowed to establish for two weeks (average female mouse weight: 26.3 g) before treatment initiation. Mice were then randomized into four groups: control (n = 4), Niraparib (n = 4), CMP (n = 4), and a combination of Niraparib and CMP (n = 5), administered as described above and published previously27.

Control treatments were as follows: in Experiment #1, mice received control peptide15,23 and Niraparib vehicle (5% DMSO, 30% PEG300, PO); in Experiment #2, mice received Niraparib vehicle (5% DMSO, 30% PEG300, PO) and CMP vehicle (PBS, i.p.). The control peptide was included only in Experiment #1, as this was the first use of CMP in this in vivo model. Based on previously validated CMP specificity15,23 and consistent treatment effects observed in Experiment #1, the control peptide was omitted in Experiment #2. Treatments were administered for 28 days in Experiment #1 and 26 days in Experiment #2. Mice were imaged weekly using IVIS to monitor tumor development. One day after the final treatment, animals were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, and tissues were harvested for analysis.

Attrition

In Experiment #1, two mice were euthanized—one due to excessive ascites formation and the other due to poor health; an additional mouse was found dead. In Experiment #2, all mice completed the study.

Flow cytometry for in vivo model

Mouse blood, spleen and tumor single cell suspensions were stained, as described previously,28 with fluorescently-labeled Abs as listed in Supplementary Table 1. Blood samples for chimerism assessment were collected on a Biorad Yeti 5-laser flow cytometer, and spleen and tumor harvest samples were collected on the Cytek Aurora 5-laser spectral cytometer at the University of Colorado Cancer Center Flow Cytometry Shared Resource. All data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Statistical considerations

ImageJ (NIH; RRID: SCR_003070), Prism software (v9.0; RRID: SCR_002798), and FlowJo software (RRID: SCR_008520) were used for microscopy, statistical data, and flow cytometry data analysis, respectively. At least three independent experiments were conducted for most experiments. Unpaired t-tests and Mann–Whitney tests; Kruskal–Wallis test and one-way ANOVA with Dunn’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test were employed, based on data normality distributions and the number of variables. For in vivo experiments, we used Brown-Forsythe ANOVA test and Unpaired t with Welch’s correction. The level of significance was p < 0.05.

Results

Claudin-4’s regulation of genomic instability: implications for type I interferon signaling and the tumor immune microenvironment

We previously identified compelling evidence linking claudin-4 to the control of genomic instability,4,12,13 a hallmark of cancer5. Ovarian tumors exhibit markedly reduced levels of various indicators of this feature when claudin-4 is overexpressed,4,12 suggesting that claudin-4 may act as a regulator of genome integrity, either by preventing the emergence of genomic alterations or by facilitating their clearance, ultimately contributing to tumor cell fitness and survival9,11–13.

Since genomic instability and tumor immune evasion are interconnected hallmarks of cancer,5 with type I interferon signaling linking them,29–34 we analyzed the co-expression patterns of common interferon-stimulated genes (ISG15, MX1, OAS1, IFIT1, EIF2AK2, IRF7, STAT1, RSAD2, BST2, and IFI44)35–38 across ovarian tumor samples (The Cancer Genome Atlas) with low and high claudin-4 expression levels. We found a significant association between high claudin-4 expression and elevated levels of IRF7 and OAS1 (Supplementary Table 2). Of note, high IRF7 expression is linked to significantly reduced progression-free survival (PFS) in ovarian cancer patients (Supplementary Fig. 1a). These results suggest that claudin-4 may influence the interferon response, which could have important implications for shaping the tumor immune microenvironment36,39. Consequently, we analyzed a cohort of 303 human ovarian tumors using the TIMER database,40–42 assessing the relationship between claudin-4 expression, patient survival, and tumor immune cell infiltration. Consistent with prior findings,12 elevated claudin-4 expression was strongly associated with reduced patient survival, an effect particularly pronounced between 48 and 96 months post-diagnosis (Fig. 1a-c). Notably, this time frame aligns with the 5-year survival rate for ovarian cancer, which remains below 50%, reinforcing the potential clinical significance of claudin-4 in disease progression and patient prognosis1–4. In addition to survival, we observed a significant correlation between claudin-4’s effect on patient survival and tumor immune cell infiltration at 48 and 96 months post-diagnosis. We identified two groups of patients with high claudin-4 expression, showing a significant difference in CD8 + T cell tumor infiltration, with higher CD8 + T cell infiltration correlating with better outcomes (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, patients with low claudin-4 expression and low M1 macrophage infiltration exhibited the most favorable survival outcomes. Notably, among patients with high claudin-4 expression, those with increased M1 macrophage infiltration demonstrated significantly better survival compared to those with low M1 infiltration (HR = 0.149, p = 0.00403), suggesting that M1 macrophages may partially counteract the negative prognostic impact of claudin-4 (Fig. 1e). These correlative results suggest that claudin-4 overexpression in ovarian cancer may lead to changes in the tumor immune microenvironment.

Claudin-4’s regulation of DNA integrity and micronuclei clearance via autophagy4,12,13 may represent the critical link shaping the tumor immune microenvironment by modulating type I interferon responses32,43,44. Notably, cGAS-STING signaling appears to coordinate autophagy and the type I interferon pathway in a mutually antagonistic manner45–49. To explore this, we investigated the mutational status of key components of the cGAS-STING pathway, including cGAS, STING, LC3, TBK1, and IRF3,49,50 in ovarian tumor samples (The Cancer Genome Atlas). These components exhibited various genetic alterations, such as amplifications and deep deletions. Similar alterations were also observed in claudin-4, and in established regulators of STING activity, such as Beclin-151–53 and Rab7 (Supplementary Fig. 1b)54,55, the latter of which we previously reported to interact with claudin-4.19 Protein-protein interaction network analysis using the STRING database confirmed direct experimental evidence of interactions among several of these proteins (Supplementary Fig. 2a), and enrichment analysis highlighted their critical involvement in processes such as cellular responses to exogenous DNA, regulation of type I interferon signaling, and the assembly of autophagosomes (Supplementary Fig. 2b). These observations suggest potential alterations in the regulation of type I interferon response in ovarian cancer56.

Consequently, we evaluated the balance between autophagy and type I interferon signaling in ovarian cancer cells. To this end, we generated stable cancer cell lines expressing the tandem protein GFP-mCherry-LC3 to assess autophagy flux and interferon-stimulated response element (ISRE) to monitor type I interferon response, as previously described13,57,58. Cells were treated with cGAMP, a STING activator, and autophagy flux was quantified via flow cytometry. This treatment notably reduced autophagy flux across multiple ovarian cancer cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 3a-c), while inducing a significant increase in type I interferon response in the same cells (Supplementary Fig. 3d-f). Thus, cGAMP exerts opposing effects on autophagy and type I interferon signaling in ovarian cancer cells, further supporting the concept of mutual antagonism. These findings suggest that claudin-4 overexpression in ovarian cancer may induce significant changes in the tumor microenvironment, influencing immune cell infiltration and modulating the type I interferon response, further reinforcing the importance of claudin-4 as a key regulator of tumor progression and immune response (Supplementary Fig. 3g).

Loss of Claudin-4 triggers pSTING elevation and alters STING signaling

Autophagy and type I interferon response are closely interrelated processes regulated by STING activity45–49. Building on our previous findings on claudin-4’s role in autophagy,13 we explored its potential influence on STING activation and the regulation of the type I interferon response. We used CRISPR inhibition to knock down (KD) claudin-4 in OVCAR3 cells (which express high levels of endogenous claudin-4) while overexpressing it in OVCAR8 cells (which do not express endogenous claudin-4). Additionally, we knocked down Claudin-4 in OVCA429 cells to investigate potential cell-line-specific effects and enhance the robustness of the experiments (Supplementary Fig. 4a-c). We then evaluated the expression and activation status of key components of the cGAS-STING signaling49 using confocal microscopy and immunoblotting. Modulating claudin-4, either through overexpression or downregulation, resulted in notable changes in STING protein levels in ovarian cancer cells (Supplementary Fig. 4d-f), suggesting potential alterations in STING activation. This was further supported by an apparent decrease in phosphorylated STING (pSTING) during claudin-4 overexpression (Fig. 2a) and a noticeable increase in pSTING levels following claudin-4 KD in OVCAR3 cells, an effect similarly observed in OVCA429 cells with claudin-4 KD (Fig. 2b, c, confocal images).

STING activity has been shown to be regulated by lysosomal encapsulation in mouse embryonic fibroblasts,59 so to further examine the observed phenotype we labeled lysosomes with LAMP1 using immunofluorescence and performed kymograph analysis on confocal z-stack images. This strategy enabled us to explore the role of lysosomal dynamics in regulating STING activation in the context of claudin-4 expression changes. This approach included line scans of both pSTING and LAMP1 in selected regions of interest. Our analysis revealed co-localization of pSTING with LAMP1 across different ovarian cancer cells (Fig. 2, kymograph section), with pSTING seemingly enclosed within lysosomes (Fig. 2, line scan section), suggesting that pSTING may be inactivated through lysosomal sequestration in these cells. Similarly, we stained ovarian cancer cells to label both claudin-4 and LAMP1 and conducted the same analysis as described above. Our findings revealed that claudin-4, independent of its post-translational modifications, co-localizes with LAMP1 (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b), suggesting that the effect of claudin-4 on pSTING is related to its inactivation through lysosomes. Consistently, since claudin-4 regulation occurs in part through lysosomal degradation,60,61 we treated ovarian cancer cells expressing endogenous claudin-4 with chloroquine, a known inhibitor of lysosomal function and autophagy62,63. Under these conditions, we observed a marked accumulation of claudin-4 (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Subsequently, to gain deeper insights into claudin-4’s potential involvement in STING activity, we performed immunoblot analysis to assess the canonical phosphorylation cascade that drives the type I interferon response (Fig. 2d)49,50. Phosphorylated STING levels were apparently elevated in response to Claudin-4 overexpression (Fig. 2e), accompanied by decreased total STING and phosphorylated TBK1 (pTBK1) levels (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Consistent with earlier confocal microscopy observations (Fig. 2b, c), Claudin-4 downregulation in OVCAR3 and OVCA429 cells further increased pSTING levels (Fig. 2e). This was associated with reduced STING and pTBK1 levels in OVCAR3 cells (Supplementary Fig. 6b), whereas OVCA429 cells displayed a distinct profile (Supplementary Fig. 6c), potentially reflecting cell line-specific processes such as differential autophagy activation13,49 or STING inactivation59. Overall, these findings support the idea that reduced Claudin-4 expression modulates STING activity, a key regulator of immune responses. In addition, we employed the claudin mimic peptide (CMP), a rationally designed small peptide that binds to a conserved domain of claudin-4, disrupting its interactions with key protein partners12,13,15,23. After 48 h of treatment, CMP induced subtle but opposing effects on pSTING levels, reducing them in OVCAR8 cells while increasing them in OVCAR3 cells (Fig. 2e). These results suggest that CMP may not only disrupt claudin-4 interactions but also influence STING activity.

Claudin-4 modulates the type I interferon response in HGSC cells through its association with Rab7

Previously, we conducted a BioID assay in OVCAR3 cells, identifying Rab7 as a claudin-4-interacting protein19. The identification of interacting proteins through the BioID method indicates that these proteins are part of the same complex,64 strongly suggesting a functional relationship. To further confirm this, we determined the spatial association of claudin-4 and Rab7 through colocalization in two different ovarian cancer cell lines with endogenous claudin-4 expression (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Rab7 is a regulator of vesicular trafficking, including lysosomes,65,66 and is involved in STING degradation54,59. Also, Rab7 is closely associated with Beclin-1 in regulating autophagy and innate immune responses52,67–70. Given this connection, we next examined how claudin-4 modulation affects Beclin-1 and Rab7 dynamics. Altering claudin-4 expression led to distinct changes in both the intracellular localization and expression levels of these proteins (Supplementary Fig. 7; Supplementary Fig. 8). Specifically, Beclin-1 levels increased upon claudin-4 overexpression, while Rab7 expression appeared to decrease (Supplementary Fig. 7a; Supplementary Fig. 8a). Conversely, in OVCAR3 cells, claudin-4 downregulation was associated with a marked reduction in Rab7 levels (Supplementary Fig. 7b; Supplementary Fig. 8b), a trend further validated in OVCA429 claudin-4 KD cells (Supplementary Fig. 8c). This consistency across models suggests that Rab7 regulation by claudin-4 is not cell line-specific, whereas Beclin-1 modulation may be more context-dependent (Supplementary Fig. 8b, d). Notably, claudin-4 downregulation in OVCAR3 cells also affected Beclin-1 intracellular distribution, leading to its accumulation in an elongated morphology resembling sorting vesicles, hinting at a potential role in vesicular trafficking71,72. These observations provide compelling evidence that claudin-4 may influence regulators of vesicular processes, potentially impacting immune signaling.

To directly assess the potential impact of claudin-4 on the type I interferon response, we performed a functional assay by generating ISRE reporter cells to evaluate claudin-4’s role in this pathway. In OVCAR8 cells, the overexpression of claudin-4 led to a significant (Fig. 3a) and specific (Fig. 3b) increased in type I interferon response, suggesting that the overexpression of claudin-4 controls that innate immune signaling mechanism by inducing changes in the basal type I interferon response. Conversely, claudin-4 downregulation in OVCAR3 resulted in a dramatic reduction of type I interferon response (Fig. 3c). These findings strongly suggest that claudin-4 plays a role in the type I interferon response, potentially as a positive regulator. To further investigate this, we exposed cells to cGAMP, a known inducer of type I interferon. As anticipated, cGAMP treatment resulted in a significant increase in type I interferon response compared to untreated controls (Fig. 3d, e). However, we found that claudin-4 modulation affected the cGAMP-induced type I interferon response in distinct ways. Specifically, overexpressing claudin-4 in OVCAR8 cells did not significantly alter the interferon response compared to wild-type (WT) cells. This suggests that the previously observed upregulation of the response (Fig. 3a) may indicate a pre-existing high level of interferon activity. This implies that claudin-4 overexpression places the cells in a heightened immune state, offering limited potential for further activation by cGAMP. Consistent with this idea, cGAMP treatment in OVCAR3 claudin-4 KD cells failed to upregulate the response to levels seen in WT cells (Fig. 3e). This suggests that decreased claudin-4 levels impair the activation of the type I interferon response, further highlighting the functional role of claudin-4 in this process.

The proper function of protein complexes hinges on the maintenance of balanced levels of their constituent proteins73. Notably, claudin-4 is part of the same protein complex as Rab7, a small GTPase involved in regulating key processes like autophagy and the innate immune response, alongside Beclin-1.52,67–70 To further explore the biological role of claudin-4 in modulating the type I interferon response and its potential interaction with Rab7, we used shRNA to reduce Rab7 levels in ovarian cancer cells, independent of endogenous claudin-4 expression (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). We also observed alterations in claudin-4 expression following Rab7 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 9c), reinforcing the idea that claudin-4 and Rab7 are not only part of the same cellular machinery but may also directly influence the other’s levels and function. These findings align with our previous observations (Supplementary Fig. 8), where changes in one protein seemed to affect the other, highlighting their interdependence in regulating cellular processes. To investigate further, we treated ovarian cancer cells, including OVCAR8 claudin-4 overexpressing cells and OVCAR3 WT and OVCA429 WT cells (both naturally expressing claudin-4), with Rab7-targeting shRNA and assessed the type I interferon response under resting conditions. We found that Rab7 knockdown led to a downregulation of the type I interferon response (Supplementary Fig. 9d), a trend also observed in OVCA429 WT cells but not in OVCAR3 WT cells (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f). This discrepancy suggests a cell-line specific effect, emphasizing that the role of Rab7 in modulating the type I interferon response is influenced by the underlying cellular context. These findings also suggest that Rab7 is essential for regulating an already heightened basal type I interferon response, as previously observed in the context of claudin-4 overexpression (Fig. 3a). To further investigate the role of Rab7 in the claudin-4-associated type I interferon response, we transiently transfected ovarian cancer cells with Rab7 (Supplementary Fig. 9g, h) while modulating claudin-4 expression and assessed its impact on the response. This strategy led to a general decrease in type I interferon signaling across most tested cell lines (OVCAR8 and OVCAR3). Notably, in cells overexpressing claudin-4, where the type I interferon response was initially elevated, Rab7 transfection significantly dampened this response. However, this effect was absent in OVCAR8 WT cells, suggesting that Rab7’s regulatory influence becomes more pronounced when the interferon response is already heightened, further supporting its potential interplay with claudin-4 in this pathway (Fig. 3f). In OVCAR3 WT cells, Rab7 transfection also led to a clear reduction in type I interferon signaling, though it did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.0538). Interestingly, no such effect was observed in OVCAR3 claudin-4 KD cells, indicating that Rab7’s influence on the type I interferon response may be dependent on the presence of claudin-4 (Fig. 3g). Additionally, STING and phosphorylated STING were observed in cells with transient Rab7 WT transfection. In OVCAR8 claudin-4-overexpressing cells with, pSTING levels were similar to those observed in WT cells; however, the intracellular distribution of STING appeared less polarized in aggregates (Supplementary Fig. 10), suggesting decreased activation59,74. In contrast, in OVCAR3 claudin-4 KD cells, pSTING levels appeared reduced compared to WT cells, opposite to the phenotype previously observed (Fig. 2b), and STING displayed a similar intracellular distribution pattern (Supplementary Fig. 11). Together, our results indicate that claudin-4 participates in a type I interferon response in ovarian cancer cells, potentially through a functional association with the small GTPase, Rab7 that may impact STING activation.

The combination of CMP and PARP inhibitor modifies tumor-immune crosstalk in ovarian cancer cells

Interferons are key orchestrators of the tumor microenvironment, shaping interactions between tumor cells and immune cells while actively influencing tumor progression75. Our findings indicate that claudin-4 participates in a type I interferon response, potentially shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. To test this hypothesis, we co-cultured HGSC cells with Jurkat T lymphocytes while modulating claudin-4 expression. We then evaluated the effects of a combined treatment with CMP and olaparib, a PARPi anti-tumor therapy previously shown to possess therapeutic activity in HGSC4,12. Our investigation focused on alterations in the type I interferon response, as well as a key immune evasion mechanism: the downregulation of the T cell receptor (TCR)-zeta chain in lymphocytes. This mechanism is widely recognized as a critical driver of immune escape during cancer progression,76–83 including in HGSC84,85. Jurkat cells were first exposed to phorbol myristate acetate (PMA, 100nM) and ionomycin (iono; 500nM) for 4 h. Following treatment, we performed immunoblotting to assess the expression of PD-1, a protein known to be upregulated during early T cell activation and after PMA/iono treatment86–88. Furthermore, we examined TCR-zeta (also known as CD3-zeta or CD247), a critical signaling molecule in TCR activation, and observed that both PD-1 and TCR-zeta expression were upregulated following PMA/iono treatment (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Additionally, we treated these cells with olaparib, CMP, and a combination of CMP/olaparib, and observed subtle changes in CD3-zeta expression in response to these treatments (Supplementary Fig. 12c), suggesting that the CMP/olaparib combination may influence the interaction between ovarian tumor cells and Jurkat cells. These preliminary findings point to a potential impact on immune response dynamics. To further investigate this phenomenon, we co-cultured OVCAR8 WT cells with Jurkat cells and applied CMP/olaparib treatment (Fig. 4a). This combination significantly activated the type I interferon response in ovarian cancer cells (Fig. 4b). However, this effect was abrogated upon claudin-4 overexpression (Fig. 4c). To complement these findings, we engineered Jurkat cells to express an ISRE reporter and assessed type I interferon signaling in these cells during co-culture with ovarian cancer cells. While a trend toward reduced type I interferon response was observed in Jurkat cells co-cultured with OVCAR8 WT cells, the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 4d). These results suggest that claudin-4 overexpression opposes the CMP/olaparib-induced activation of the type I interferon response effect in OVCAR8 cells in the presence of Jurkat cells. This aligns with our previous observations of elevated basal type I interferon signaling in OVCAR8 cells overexpressing claudin-4 (Fig. 3a), raising the possibility that claudin-4-driven high basal interferon activity may dampen the ability of ovarian cancer cells to further upregulate this response upon treatment, potentially as a mechanism of survival89. In contrast, CMP/olaparib treatment in OVCAR3 cells correlated with a significant downregulation of the type I interferon response, regardless of claudin-4 expression, when co-cultured with Jurkat cells (Fig. 4e, f). Furthermore, ISRE-expressing Jurkat cells exhibited a trend toward decreased type I interferon signaling when co-cultured with OVCAR3 cells, although this effect did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4g). These findings highlight potential context-dependent differences in how ovarian cancer cells, depending on their genetic background and claudin-4 expression, modulate the tumor-immune microenvironment in response to CMP/olaparib treatment. Also, these results highlight that despite a significant deficiency in the type I interferon response in OVCAR3 cells (Fig. 3c), CMP/olaparib treatment can further reduce this response.

Dual targeting of Claudin-4 ovarian tumors with PARP inhibitor and CMP reshapes the tumor microenvironment and inhibits growth

Previously, we have shown that CMP targets claudin-4, affecting cancer cell survival in vitro,4,12,23 an effect that we have also confirmed in vivo in immune deficient mice15. However, the potential effect of targeting claudin-4 with CMP in the tumor immune microenvironment has not yet been explored. To better understand and to expand on the biological consequences of targeting claudin-4 in vivo, we generated humanized, i.e. human immune system-bearing (HIS), mice28, which have greater translational relevance than traditional immunocompromised mice in modeling the human tumor progression93. In these HIS mice, we implanted a patient-derived xenograft (PDX, GTFB1016) with claudin-4 expression,13 whose previous characterization identified it as HGSC as part of the Gynecologic Tissue and Fluid Bank (GTFB) at the University of Colorado (COMIRB #07-935). Also, these tumor cells were engineered to express a luciferase reporter gene to enable repeated IVIS tumor burden tracking,24,25 creating the PDX-HIS-mice system. We first divided these mice into treatment groups receiving either CMP or the FDA-approved PARPi, niraparib which has better pharmacokinetics than olaparib in preclinical tumor models. For example, niraparib shows higher cell membrane permeability, greater volume of distribution, and more sustained tumor exposure94 (Supplementary Fig. 16a). Tumor growth was monitored over four weeks using IVIS imaging.

Both treatments resulted in significant tumor growth inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 16b) and led to a trend of decreased CD4 + T cell infiltration and increased CD8 + T cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment (Supplementary Fig. 16c, d). This approach demonstrated that CMP exhibits anti-ovarian tumor activity in the presence of human immune cells, comparable to niraparib, highlighting CMP’s potential as an in vivo claudin-4-targeting therapeutic.

To gain deeper insights into human immune cell dynamics and the combined effects of CMP and niraparib, we established a new cohort of HIS mice, optimizing the timeline by shortening both tumor development and treatment durations (Supplementary Fig. 16e). By the end of treatment, a clear divergence in tumor growth emerged between the treated groups and the control group. The combination therapy significantly suppressed tumor growth compared to the untreated group and potentiated the effects of niraparib at multiple time points (Day 23: niraparib vs. niraparib/CMP; *, p = 0.0324; Day 27: *, p = 0.0128) (Fig. 6a). Given that ovarian tumors frequently drive ascites formation, a microenvironment enriched with tumor cells, immune cells, stromal components, and fluid, we sought to assess, using flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 17), intratumoral heterogeneity by detecting EpCAM + tumor cells—a hallmark of epithelial ovarian cancer progression (Fig. 6b-f). All drug treatment regimens effectively reduced the viability of EpCAM-expressing tumor cells. Notably, the anti-tumor activity of CMP alone was comparable to that of niraparib, while the combination of CMP and niraparib produced the most pronounced effect (Fig. 6b). Further analysis revealed that although the proportion of MHC-I + tumor cells remained consistent across treatment groups (Fig. 6c), CMP/niraparib therapy led to a significant reduction in the population of MHC-II + tumor cells (Fig. 6d). Although MHC-II expression is typically restricted to professional antigen-presenting cells for the activation of CD4 + T cells, its presence in various cancers—including HGSC— has been associated with improved patient outcomes. This highlights the pivotal role of CD4 + T lymphocytes in shaping anti-tumor immunity, complementing the well-established cytotoxic functions of CD8 + T cells in tumor surveillance and elimination95–100. Our observations suggest that the combination treatment drives selective pressure on tumor cells expressing MHC-II.

To further investigate the immune landscape of treated tumors, we assessed PD-L1 expression in tumor cells, given its well-established role in tumor immune evasion. During lymphocyte activation, PD-1 is upregulated on T cells, where its interaction with PD-L1 on neighboring cells serves as a checkpoint to prevent hyperactivation and mitigate immune responses101. In tumors, PD-L1 overexpression allows cancer cells to evade immune surveillance by interfering with the TCR signaling cascade (via the zeta chain of the TCR), ultimately suppressing T cell activation. 102 Our data reveal a higher proportion of PD-L1 + tumor cells across all treated groups, with the most pronounced increases observed in niraparib and CMP/niraparib-treated tumors, where PD-L1 levels remained comparable (Fig. 6e). Additionally, these PD-L1 + tumor cells also expressed MHC-II, following a pattern similar to that of total live HGSC cells (Supplementary Fig. 18). In HGSC, PD-L1 expression has been linked to a subset of tumor cells with stem-like characteristics, reinforcing its role in maintaining an immune-resistant, aggressive phenotype103. Notably, irrespective of treatment, approximately 50% of PD-L1 + tumor cells co-expressed PD-1 (Fig. 6f). This autonomous PD-1/PD-L1 co-expression within tumor cells has been previously reported and is thought to modulate mTOR signaling, directly promoting tumor growth and survival104–107. This mechanism suggests a potential decoupling of PD-L1 from its immunosuppressive interaction with PD-1 on T cells, thereby altering the expected inhibitory signaling cascade in anti-tumor immunity102. Together, our findings show that CMP/niraparib treatment reshapes the tumor’s cellular composition, particularly by limiting the expansion of MHC-II + tumor cells while increasing the proportion of PD-L1+/PD-1 + tumor cells. This shift may have profound implications for tumor-immune interactions, potentially affecting the broader systemic immunosuppressive environment characteristic of cancer,108 for example by diminishing the tumor’s immunomodulatory capacity and promoting T CD8 + cells infiltration109.

Targeting Claudin-4 tumors with PARP inhibitor and CMP reactivates immune recognition and promotes CD8⁺ T cell infiltration

Consistent with the potential for reshaping the tumor’s cellular composition and immune microenvironment, our data revealed distinct immune alterations within the tumor microenvironment. We observed a reduction in total CD3 + T cells within the tumor following CMP/niraparib treatment (Fig. 7a), with this effect appearing to be predominantly driven by a decrease in CD4 + T cells (Fig. 7b). In contrast, all treatment groups showed a consistent trend of increased CD8 + T cell infiltration within tumors, particularly in the niraparib-treated group, an effect that remained pronounced when CMP was added to the regimen (Fig. 7c). This finding underscores the compatibility of niraparib with CMP in fostering CD8 + T cell infiltration, potentially through distinct alterations in tumor cell properties that ultimately enhance CD8 + T cell recruitment, an effect evidenced when niraparib was used alone (Fig. 6a, d) compared to the CMP/niraparib combination, suggesting that the combination therapy triggers unique immune-modifying mechanisms. These findings correlate with reduced tumor progression (Fig. 6a) and align with the well-established prognostic advantage of tumor-infiltrating CD8 + T cells, reinforcing their critical role in anti-tumor immunity110.

T cell dysfunction is a hallmark of immune suppression in cancer, where inhibitory signals dampen their ability to mount an effective anti-tumor response111. A key component of T-cell activation is the TCR-zeta chain, an essential signaling subunit of the TCR30,112,113. However, in many cancers, the TCR-zeta chain is downregulated, contributing to lymphocyte dysfunction and serving as a major mechanism of tumor immune evasion76–85,114. In line with this, analysis of ovarian tumor samples showed a strong correlation between TCR-zeta expression and better overall survival and disease free survival (Supplementary Fig. 19a, b), and effect also observed when claudin-4 is overexpressed (Supplementary Fig. 19c, d). Intriguingly, our analysis revealed a significant increase in TCR-zeta chain expression in both splenic CD4 + and CD8 + T cells following CMP/niraparib treatment (Fig. 7d, e). Additionally, we observed a marked expansion of CD4 + T cells expressing PD-1, along with higher PD-1 expression levels in this subset (Fig. 7f). This dynamic suggests a potential mechanism of CD4 + T cell priming, which is crucial for supporting CD8 + T cell activation and tumor infiltration84. Given that the TCR-zeta chain is elevated in splenic lymphocytes, it is plausible that this enhancement reinforces CD4 + T cell-mediated support, ultimately promoting CD8 + T cell recruitment to the tumor microenvironment.

The spleen is one the immune organs associated with the known immunosuppressive environment characteristic in cancer. This organ plays a dual role in immune surveillance, serving as a hub where CD4 + T cells support CD8 + T cell activation to mount an adaptive anti-tumor response115–118. Paradoxically, it can also act as a site where T cells develop tolerance to tumor cells, enabling immune evasion119,120. Our analysis of human immune cells within the spleen and tumor microenvironment revealed that CMP/niraparib treatment significantly reduced the number of myeloid cells in the spleen (Fig. 7g). Taken together, our findings indicate that CMP/niraparib treatment alters tumor cell populations, selecting for those less capable of evading immune surveillance. This shift is accompanied by a reduction in immunosuppressive myeloid cells in the spleen, which otherwise hinder T-cell function. Furthermore, the observed increase in TCR-zeta chain levels suggests a restoration of T-cell activation potential, potentially overcoming cancer-induced immune suppression (Fig. 8). Nevertheless, future additional analysis of functional markers of T-cell activity may further clarify the mechanisms by which CMP/niraparib enhances anti-tumor immunity (Supplementary Fig. 20). Altogether, our findings suggest that dual targeting of claudin-4-expressing ovarian tumors with CMP and a PARP inhibitor may influence claudin-4’s established role in genomic instability, thereby modulating type I interferon responses and reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment.

The proposed model unveils a novel mechanism of immune reprogramming achieved through the combined treatment with niraparib and the claudin mimic peptide (CMP). This dual therapy not only reduces the buildup of immunosuppressive myeloid cells in the spleen (1) but also restores the expression of the TCR-zeta chain in lymphocytes (2) — a key molecule whose loss is a hallmark of tumor-driven immune evasion. By relieving T-cell suppression, the treatment potentially fosters a coordinated activation of CD4 + and CD8 + lymphocytes within the spleen (3), ultimately driving the robust infiltration of CD8 + T cells into the tumor microenvironment (4) and inducing changes in the cellular composition of ovarian tumors (5). This synergistic strategy positions claudin-4 targeting as a promising avenue to overcome immune barriers and enhance anti-tumor immunity in ovarian cancer.

Discussion

Despite continuous advances in ovarian cancer treatment, patient survival rates remain dismally low. A main contributor is therapy resistance, which severely limits treatment efficacy, highlighting the urgent need for innovative strategies to dismantle resistance mechanisms1–4. Claudin-4 has emerged as a therapeutic target of interest, as it is strongly associated with therapy resistance and poor prognosis. However, the precise molecular mechanisms driving claudin-4’s role in tumor progression remain largely elusive4,14–16,19,21,22. Our study uncovers a previously unrecognized function of claudin-4 in regulating the type I interferon response (Fig. 3), revealing a critical link between claudin-4 and genomic instability. This discovery positions claudin-4 as a pivotal modulator of the tumor immune microenvironment (Figs. 1 and 8), suggesting that its influence on immune signaling may contribute to therapy resistance in ovarian cancer.

Ovarian cancer is characterized by higher levels of genomic instability than other cancers. Genome integrity is regulated by claudin-4,4,12,13 which prevents the onset of genomic instability through nuclear structure remodeling and cell cycle regulation,12 and by facilitating the clearance of genomic alterations once they arise13. This molecular mechanism aligns with the strong correlation between claudin-4 overexpression, therapy resistance, and poor patient survival (Fig. 1)4,12. Genomic instability is a hallmark of cancer, driving intratumoral heterogeneity and leading to tumor immune evasion (another hallmark of cancer). The presence of genomic instability suggests that cancer cells operate within a threshold of tolerance, where tumor cells tightly regulate the formation and resolution of genomic instability, balancing mutation accumulation for growth while preventing excessive instability that could lead to cell death5,9–13. In this context, claudin-4 overexpression enhances a tumor’s ability to manage genomic instability, reinforcing the delicate equilibrium between mutation accumulation and chromosomal integrity. By expanding the threshold of genomic instability tolerance, claudin-4 may paradoxically drive therapy resistance while simultaneously rendering tumors vulnerable to targeted disruption of this balance. Consequently, targeting claudin-4 could induce a critical breakdown of this regulatory threshold, potentially opening new avenues for therapeutic intervention.

Our study uncovered a novel role for claudin-4 in orchestrating the type I interferon response, where it closely interacts with the small GTPase Rab7 (Fig. 3). This interaction may influence STING activity (Fig. 2) and cytoplasmic DNA sensing, suggesting a previously unrecognized link between claudin-4 and innate immune signaling. For example, in ovarian cancer cells, Claudin-4 maintains genomic stability by regulating the cell cycle12 and promoting the clearance of genomic instability through autophagy, particularly by targeting micronuclei13. Micronuclei are prone to collapse, releasing DNA into the cytoplasm and potentially activating cGAS-STING signaling, a type I interferon response, and an anti-tumor immune reaction32,43. Notably, cGAS-STING signaling coordinates both autophagy and the type I interferon response,47,49,51,121–123 and high claudin-4 expression in ovarian tumors is associated with the upregulation of mTOR signaling, (Supplementary Fig. 21; Supplementary Table 3) which regulates autophagy124. Particularly, upon detecting cytoplasmic DNA, cGAS produces cyclic GAMP-AMP (cGAMP), which activates STING to trigger either type I interferon signaling via TBK1/IRF3 or induce autophagy through LC3, independent of TBK149,50. Consequently, micronuclei collapse may release DNA,125,126 activating STING to either promote autophagy while suppressing interferon signaling or vice versa45–48. Thus, it is feasible that claudin-4’s regulation on genome stability and micronuclei clearance may represent a critical link to the tumor immune microenvironment. However, further investigation is needed to fully elucidate its impact on the cGAS-STING pathway and alternative mechanisms of cytoplasmic DNA detection.

Claudin-4 overexpression in ovarian tumors correlates with significant alterations in the tumor immune microenvironment, reinforcing its potential role in immune modulation. In this study, we confirmed the anti-tumor activity of CMP in the presence of human immune cells (Figs. 6 and 7, Supplementary Fig. 16b), highlighting its potential for clinical translation as a claudin-4-targeted therapy. Notably, CMP alone induced statistically significant alterations in some markers of the tumor immune microenvironment, which were further enhanced when combined with the PARP inhibitor niraparib. These findings underscore the potential of CMP to modulate the tumor immune microenvironment in vivo and support the evaluation of extended CMP treatment durations to determine whether prolonged exposure enhances T-cell infiltration in the humanized ovarian cancer model. The dual approach (CMP/niraparib) not only enhanced tumor sensitivity to niraparib but also disrupted immune evasion pathways, leading to a substantial increase in CD8 + T-cell infiltration, a hallmark of potent anti-tumor immunity. Notably, niraparib has been shown to improve overall survival regardless of BRCA mutation status. Beyond its well-known role in inducing DNA damage, PARPi can also activate the immune system, contributing to tumor suppression through mechanisms that extend beyond direct genotoxicity. These findings suggest that CMP, in synergy with niraparib, may amplify the immune-mediated effects of PARPi, offering a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention127–130.

Conclusion

Collectively, our findings position claudin-4 as a dual regulator of genomic stability and immune evasion, highlighting its potential for overcoming therapy resistance in ovarian cancer. The combination of CMP and PARPi presents a promising strategy to reshape the tumor immune landscape, sensitize resistant tumors, and improve patient outcomes. Future investigations should explore the clinical translation of these findings, optimizing claudin-4-targeting strategies for therapeutic application.
