Development of a risk calculator for acute kidney injury in trauma patients: an experimental study
Authors: Emad Ramadan, Richard Cook, Raman Srinivasan, Alan Babuji, Deborah Latham, Daniel Gunn, Michael Foreman, Saravanan Ramamoorthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a frequent and serious complication in trauma populations, associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Current risk stratification tools, derived from general medical populations, often lack trauma-specific physiological variables and demonstrate limited applicability in these patients. This study aimed to develop and validate a specialized risk prediction model for the development of AKI within 72 hours of hospitalization in trauma patients requiring surgical intervention.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted involving 157 consecutive trauma patients requiring surgical intervention at a level I trauma center (2020–2022). AKI was defined and staged according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes criteria. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors and develop a risk model, which was then internally validated using a prospective cohort of 53 additional trauma patients.ResultsIndependent predictors of AKI included advanced age, decreased admission estimated glomerular filtration rate, higher Injury Severity Scores, and lower hemoglobin levels. While body mass index and diabetes mellitus were not independent predictors, their inclusion in the composite model improved predictive accuracy. The final model demonstrated reliable discriminative ability, with an area under the curve of 0.91. Prospective validation confirmed this performance, achieving a probability threshold of 0.91 with only two observed false negatives.ConclusionsThis novel trauma-specific risk calculator, using an additive weighting methodology, provides clinicians with a reliable tool for early AKI identification. By incorporating both patient characteristics and injury-related variables, the model may facilitate the timely initiation of renal-protective measures in high-risk trauma populations.Level of evidenceLevel II, prognostic/epidemiological.Keywords: Acute Kidney Injury, risk factor, wounds and injuries, postoperative complications

Background

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common and serious complication in trauma patients, associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs.1 2 Trauma-induced AKI arises from a complex interplay of factors, including hemorrhagic shock, rhabdomyolysis, systemic inflammation, and nephrotoxic interventions.2 Despite advances in trauma care, AKI remains a significant challenge, with reported incidences ranging from 12.6% in general trauma populations to 67% in critically injured patients.1 3 4

Current diagnostic criteria for AKI, such as the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, rely on serum creatinine (SCr) and urine output (UO), which are functional markers that often lag behind actual renal injury.5 This delay limits early intervention, highlighting the need for more timely predictive tools. While novel biomarkers (eg, Neutrophil Gelatinase-Associated Lipocalin (NGAL), Tissue Inhibitor of Metalloproteinases-2 (TIMP-2), Insulin-like Growth Factor Binding Protein 7 (IGFBP7)) and imaging techniques (eg, contrast-enhanced ultrasound) show promise, they are not yet widely integrated into clinical practice.2

AKI in trauma patients carries profound clinical implications. Studies have demonstrated that trauma-induced AKI is independently associated with prolonged hospital stays, higher rates of chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression, a threefold increase in mortality, and an almost fivefold increase in mortality in the critically injured.1 2 For example, Perkins et al1 reported a mortality rate of 26.4% in trauma patients with AKI compared with 10.4% in those without, highlighting the lethal consequences of renal dysfunction in this population. Additionally, AKI often complicates the management of polytrauma patients, contributing to multi-organ failure and increased resource utilization.6

The heterogeneity of trauma patients—ranging from young, healthy individuals to elderly patients with comorbidities—further complicates risk stratification. Traditional AKI prediction models, such as those derived from cardiac surgery or sepsis cohorts, may not fully capture the unique pathophysiology of trauma-induced AKI, as they often do not account for the unique clinical variables associated with traumatic injury.7,5 Moreover, while machine learning (ML) has been employed to predict AKI in hospitalized and critically ill patients, its application in trauma-specific settings remains limited.5 This gap underscores the need for a dedicated risk assessment tool that accounts for trauma-specific variables, such as injury severity, shock severity, and resuscitation strategies.

Extensive research has identified numerous risk factors that predispose trauma patients to AKI development. Patient-specific variables such as advanced age, pre-existing CKD, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension consistently emerge as important predictors across studies.1 Injury-related factors, including high Injury Severity Scores (ISS), direct renal trauma, crush injury, and severe chest or abdominal injuries, demonstrate particularly strong associations with AKI risk.2 4 Perhaps most significantly, treatment-related factors such as massive blood transfusion (>4 units packed red blood cells in 24 hours), vasopressor requirements, and the use of hyperchloremic crystalloid solutions have been independently linked to renal dysfunction.8 9 The recognition of these modifiable risk factors presents important opportunities for prevention, yet current clinical practice remains limited in systematically incorporating them into AKI risk assessment.

The development of a trauma-specific AKI risk calculator represents an important need in critical care nephrology. An ideal prediction tool would incorporate both traditional risk factors and dynamic variables that reflect real-time changes in patient status during resuscitation. By integrating injury severity metrics, such a tool could provide clinicians with continuously updated risk assessments to guide preventive strategies. This study aims to address this gap by developing and validating a comprehensive, trauma-specific AKI risk prediction model that leverages both established and emerging risk factors to optimize patient care.

Methods

This study employed a retrospective cohort design to develop and validate a risk calculator for predicting AKI in trauma patients undergoing surgical intervention. AKI was defined and staged according to the KDIGO criteria. The retrospective approach was selected to efficiently evaluate multiple clinical variables while leveraging existing patient data. The study was conducted in two distinct phases to ensure model development and validation. The initial development phase analyzed data from trauma patients treated between 2020 and 2022, while the subsequent validation phase prospectively tested the model’s performance over a 4-month period in 2023. This dual-phase methodology enhances the consistent validity of our model while minimizing potential selection bias.

The study population consisted of adult trauma patients aged 18 years or older who were admitted to our Baylor Scott & White Medical Center—Dallas level 1 trauma center and underwent surgical procedures for injuries associated with trauma within 24 hours of admission. These procedures included exploratory laparotomies, orthopedic interventions, splenectomies, thoracotomies, and other trauma-related surgeries. We included patients with complete SCr and UO data necessary for AKI staging, while excluding those with pre-existing end-stage renal disease or dialysis dependence, missing critical data elements such as ISS, or who passed within 24 hours of admission. Patient identification and data collection were conducted through a comprehensive electronic health record review. The development cohort included 157 patients from 2020 to 2022, which provided sufficient statistical power for our regression analyses, while the validation phase included 53 patients from 2023.

Demographic characteristics included age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Injury-related data comprised ISS and surgical procedure details. We documented comorbidities including diabetes, hypertension, and CKD, with baseline renal function assessed through estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). eGFR was calculated in the Electronic Health Record system developed by Epic Systems Corporation (EPIC) using the age-appropriate formula Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI), based on the most recent lab values prior to the procedure. Other laboratory parameters included preoperative hemoglobin, SCr, and lactate levels, along with postoperative peak creatinine values and UO measurements.

Statistical analysis focused on identifying predictors of AKI development within 72 hours of hospitalization of trauma patients. We employed multivariable logistic regression to identify significant predictors, retaining variables with p values <0.05 in the final model. Model performance was assessed through discrimination using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) with 95% CIs. The model was subsequently validated in our prospective cohort. The multivariable logistic regression approach allowed for the integration of multiple variables, with the regression coefficients providing the relative weight of each predictor in the final model.

Results

Of the 157 trauma patients analyzed, 77 developed AKI within 72 hours of arrival at the trauma center. The multivariate logistic regression model identified several preoperative risk factors, including age (OR 1.06; 95% CI 1.03 to 1.08), eGFR (OR 0.97; 95% CI 0.96 to 0.99), ISS (OR 1.09; 95% CI 1.05 to 1.13), hemoglobin (OR 0.77; 95% CI 0.67 to 0.89), BMI (OR 1.04; 95% CI 0.99 to 1.09), and diabetes (OR 1.33; 95% CI 0.35 to 5.13). The model demonstrated reliable accuracy, with an AUC-ROC of 0.91 (95% CI 0.906 to 0.916), and successfully predicted AKI in a prospective cohort of 53 trauma patients with only two false negatives (see figure 1 for univariate distributions of key variables for both the development and the validation cohorts).

Discussion

The complex pathophysiology of trauma-induced AKI involves several distinct but interrelated mechanisms that collectively contribute to renal injury. The primary insult typically stems from hemorrhagic shock leading to renal hypoperfusion and ischemic tubular damage.2 This ischemic injury is frequently compounded by rhabdomyolysis in patients with significant musculoskeletal trauma, where myoglobin release causes direct tubular toxicity and intraluminal obstruction.10 The systemic inflammatory response to major trauma further exacerbates renal injury through multiple pathways, including cytokine-mediated endothelial dysfunction, neutrophil activation, and microvascular thrombosis.11

This multifactorial pathogenesis explains both the high incidence of AKI in trauma populations and the limitations of traditional diagnostic approaches. SCr, while useful, represents a relatively late marker of renal dysfunction that may not reflect ongoing cellular injury during the critical early phases of trauma resuscitation.5 Our risk prediction model addresses these challenges by incorporating both patient-specific factors (age, baseline renal function) and trauma-specific variables (injury severity, hemoglobin levels) that reflect these underlying pathological processes.

Our findings align with this multifactorial model. The risk factors identified in our study map directly onto these pathophysiological pathways. For instance, the strong association between a higher ISS and AKI risk reflects the cumulative impact of both direct tissue injury and the ensuing systemic inflammatory response on renal vulnerability. Similarly, the protective effect of higher hemoglobin levels underscores the importance of oxygen delivery in preventing renal medullary hypoxia, particularly during resuscitation when anemia or blood loss is common.2 8

Furthermore, our results confirm the well-established role of patient-specific factors. Advanced age was a significant predictor, which aligns with extensive research showing that aging is associated with reduced renal reserve and increased susceptibility to kidney injury. Likewise, a lower baseline eGFR was a strong risk factor, consistent with the understanding that pre-existing renal dysfunction predisposes patients to further AKI. Additionally, the validation cohort shows directionally similar relationships between AKI and individual variables.

While BMI and diabetes did not reach statistical significance as independent predictors in our model, their inclusion improved predictive accuracy. This suggests their clinical relevance may be realized through interaction with other risk factors. Their co-occurrence with elements like age, injury severity, and hemodynamic instability likely creates cumulative renal stress through synergistic metabolic dysfunction, amplified inflammatory responses, and compromised renal reserve capacity. The logistic regression coefficients provide clinically meaningful quantification of each factor’s contribution. For instance, the positive coefficient for ISS (β=0.086 per point) reflects the cumulative impact of both direct tissue injury and systemic inflammation on renal vulnerability. Similarly, the protective effect of higher hemoglobin (β=−0.0261 per g/L) underscores the importance of oxygen delivery in preventing renal medullary hypoxia during resuscitation.2 8 These quantitative relationships could enable clinicians to identify high-risk patients and understand the relative contribution of each risk factor in individual cases.

This enhanced understanding of risk factor contributions has several important clinical implications. First, it allows for more targeted implementation of renal protective strategies, such as careful fluid management in elderly patients or early monitoring in those with high ISS scores. Second, the model’s transparency facilitates shared decision-making by providing clear explanations for risk stratification. Finally, the quantitative nature of the coefficients enables adaptation to different clinical settings while maintaining predictive accuracy. Future refinements could incorporate dynamic variables such as real-time resuscitation parameters or novel biomarkers to further enhance early detection during the evolving clinical course of trauma patients.

While this study developed a trauma-specific AKI risk calculator with reliable discrimination (AUC-ROC 0.91), several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as only patients with complete records were included, potentially excluding those with severe injuries or early transfers. Although internal validation demonstrated robust performance, external validation across diverse trauma populations is needed to confirm generalizability, particularly given variations in patient demographics, injury patterns, and institutional protocols. Additionally, prehospital resuscitation parameters, genetic factors, and specific nephrotoxic exposures were not accounted for, representing opportunities for future model enhancement. Implementation studies are also needed to determine optimal clinical risk thresholds. Another limitation is the reliance on preoperative variables, which may not fully capture the evolving risk factors in trauma patients, such as hemodynamic instability during resuscitation, delayed inflammatory responses, or secondary insults (eg, compartment syndrome, sepsis). Trauma is a dynamic process, and static preoperative measures (eg, initial hemoglobin or eGFR) cannot account for real-time developments like fluid overload from aggressive transfusion, vasopressor-induced renal hypoperfusion, or nephrotoxin exposure (eg, contrast dye, antibiotics). Incorporating real-time data into ML models can improve their predictive accuracy. Future studies should explore the integration of dynamic variables, such as continuous hemodynamic monitoring and biomarkers, to enhance the model’s performance. Finally, model calibration metrics such as calibration slope and intercept were not reported, representing another limitation of the study. Although these measures can technically be calculated in small cohorts, our limited sample size and low number of outcome events would likely produce unstable estimates and potentially misleading calibration measures. Therefore, calibration assessment was deferred to future studies with larger cohorts where model performance can be evaluated more reliably.

In summary, this study provides a practical tool for early identification and management. The developed risk calculator has the potential to significantly improve outcomes in this vulnerable population by enabling timely and targeted interventions.
