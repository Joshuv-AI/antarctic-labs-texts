Response to "Comment on Opioid-based versus opioid-sparing patient-controlled analgesia using ketorolac and nefopam after total knee arthroplasty: a randomized, double-blind, non-inferiority trial"
Authors: Jiwon Han, Seihee Min
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We would like to thank the authors of this letter [1] for their thoughtful and constructive comments on our study [2]. They raised several methodological concerns regarding the statistical analysis, outcome measures, and safety evaluation, which we are pleased to address.

Regarding the statistical analysis and adherence to CONSORT recommendations for non-inferiority trials, the authors suggested that both intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses should be reported [3]. We would like to clarify that the primary endpoint, the visual analog scale pain score at rest on postoperative day (POD) 1, was successfully obtained for all 98 randomized patients (n = 49 per group) prior to any treatment discontinuation. Two patients discontinued the patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) on POD 1; however, this occurred after the primary outcome had already been assessed. Consequently, the ITT and PP analyses yielded identical results for non-inferiority.

With respect to blinding, we acknowledge that the investigator responsible for preparing the dual-chamber PCA device was aware of the treatment allocation, which is unavoidable in trials involving drug preparation. However, this individual had no role in patient care, data collection, or outcome assessment. In addition, the study solutions were indistinguishable. Therefore, we considered the risks of performance and detection bias to be minimal.

Regarding outcome measures, our study was specifically designed to evaluate the non-inferiority of an opioid-sparing PCA regimen with respect to acute pain control during the 48-h postoperative infusion period. We agree that multidimensional patient-reported outcomes, such as the Quality of Recovery-15, as well as functional measures including range of motion and the Timed Up and Go test, would provide valuable additional insights [4,5]. However, these measures assess recovery trajectories over a longer period and are beyond the predefined scope of this focused trial on analgesic efficacy.

Finally, regarding safety, this study was not adequately powered to detect clinically meaningful differences in adverse events; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. In particular, the absence of statistically significant differences or observed adverse events does not constitute evidence of safety. Furthermore, patients at increased risk of NSAID-related complications, including those with renal impairment or significant gastrointestinal disease, were excluded, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to broader clinical populations. Additionally, the study was not specifically designed to comprehensively evaluate adverse events, including rare but clinically important complications or nefopam-related hemodynamic effects. Accordingly, the safety findings of this study should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Larger, adequately powered, multicenter studies are warranted to more reliably establish the safety profile of ketorolac- and nefopam-based regimens, particularly for high-risk populations.

We hope that our findings, together with those of future studies, will contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting opioid-sparing strategies as clinically meaningful alternatives in postoperative pain management, in line with current opioid stewardship principles.
