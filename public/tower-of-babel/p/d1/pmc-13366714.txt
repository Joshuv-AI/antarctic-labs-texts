A clinically feasible framework to estimate tau pathology and clinical–biological discordance in the Alzheimer’s disease spectrum
Authors: Jung-Lung Hsu, Shao-Yi Huang, Hsiu-Chuan Wu, Kun-Ju Lin, Kuo-Lun Huang, Chin-Chang Huang, SangYun Kim, Ing-Tsung Hsiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTau positron emission tomography (PET) is critical for biological staging and treatment stratification in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), particularly in the era of anti-amyloid therapies where lower tau burden predicts greater clinical benefit. However, tau PET remains costly and inaccessible in many clinical settings. We aimed to develop and validate a clinically feasible framework to estimate global and regional tau burden using routinely available amyloid PET and clinical measures, aligned with the 2024 Alzheimer’s Association (AA-2024) diagnostic framework, and to characterize clinical–biological discordance across the AD continuum.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of 229 individuals spanning cognitively unimpaired, mild cognitive impairment, and dementia stages who underwent [18F]florbetapir amyloid PET, [18F]florzolotau tau PET, structural MRI, and cognitive assessment. Amyloid burden was quantified using the Centiloid (CL) scale (A+ defined as CL > 20). Tracer-specific tau thresholds for global and Braak-stage volumes were derived using two-component Gaussian mixture modeling. Logistic regression models incorporating CL, age, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and medial temporal lobe (MTL) volume were developed to classify high global tau burden and neocortical tau involvement. Among amyloid-positive individuals, biological staging was compared with clinical stage to assess discordance patterns.ResultsGlobal and regional tau burden increased stepwise across clinical severity and amyloid strata. High global tau burden was uncommon in individuals with CL 21–60 (6.1%) but increased in CL 61–100 (22.6%) and > 100 (36.2%). A multivariable model integrating CL, age, MMSE, and relative MTL volume demonstrated good discrimination for high global tau burden (AUC = 0.87) and neocortical involvement (AUC = 0.84). Model robustness was confirmed by bootstrap resampling. Among amyloid-positive participants, 53.1% exhibited clinical-predominant AD, characterized by older age and higher cardiovascular risk despite relatively modest tau burden, indicating substantial clinical–biological discordance.ConclusionRoutinely obtainable amyloid PET and clinical measures can approximate global and topographical tau burden with good accuracy and identify frequent clinical–biological discordance within the AD spectrum. This scalable framework provides a practical surrogate for tau PET in resource-limited settings and may support biological staging, therapeutic eligibility assessment, and precision treatment decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-02083-8.

Background

The advent of disease-modifying therapies for Alzheimer’s disease (AD), particularly monoclonal antibodies targeting different amyloid-β (Aβ) forms, represents a major milestone in AD management [1]. Phase 3 trials have shown that these agents slow clinical progression by approximately 27–36% over 18 months, accompanied by substantial reductions in Aβ plaque burden on positron emission tomography (PET) and improvements across multiple secondary cognitive outcomes [2, 3]. Importantly, subgroup analyses reveal that individuals with lower tau pathology derive the greatest therapeutic benefit [4]. For example, in the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ2 study of donanemab, participants within the low-to-intermediate tau PET subgroup experienced a 36% slowing of decline, compared with 28.9% in the overall cohort [2]. Long-term Lecanemab follow-up studies also demonstrated a 51% slowing of clinical decline among participants in the no/low–tau PET subgroup [5]. These findings highlight the pivotal role of tau pathology burden in predicting the efficacy of amyloid targeting treatments.

Although tau PET remains the gold standard for topographical staging, its limited accessibility has driven interest in scalable surrogates. Plasma p-tau217 and structural MRI metrics have shown promise; however, plasma markers are not yet globally routine, and MRI atrophy often fails to capture the significant inter-individual heterogeneity of tau deposition [6]. Evidence from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) shows that amyloid PET measures are moderately associated with tau burden when acquired in close temporal proximity, reflecting biological coupling between amyloid accumulation and downstream tau propagation [7]. Accordingly, amyloid burden combined with key clinical measures may serve as a practical surrogate for estimating tau burden during MCI and early dementia, particularly in temporo-neocortical regions critical to symptom expression [8]. This rationale forms the basis of the present study’s approach to developing a clinically feasible model for tau estimation.

Despite these advances, no standardized or clinically feasible method exists to estimate tau burden using only routinely available clinical and amyloid PET measures. Asian populations differ from Western cohorts in having a lower prevalence of APOE ε4 and a higher burden of vascular comorbidities, which may modify clinical–biological relationships in Alzheimer’s disease [9, 10]. To address these gaps, we enrolled a large, well-characterized Asian cohort spanning the AD continuum and developed a clinically applicable framework capable of estimating both global tau burden and its topographic distribution from amyloid Centiloid (CL) scores and key clinical parameters. In parallel, we applied the AA-2024 biological and clinical staging framework to characterize areas of concordance and discordance between clinical symptoms and underlying AD pathology [11]. We further investigated how demographic factors and vascular comorbidity contribute to these discrepancies. We hypothesized that tau pathology could be reliably inferred from accessible amyloid and clinical features, and that differences in cognitive reserve and vascular burden would help explain divergence between clinical and biological disease stages.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital and included a total of 229 participants: 49 cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals, 82 patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and 98 patients with dementia. Detailed inclusion criteria have been described in our previous publication; here, we briefly summarize the key inclusion and exclusion criteria [12]. Participants in the CU group met the following criteria: absence of cognitive symptoms, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ≥ 27, and a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score of 0 [13, 14]. The MCI group met the MMSE score ≥ 24 and a CDR score > 0. The dementia group met the CDR score ≥ 0.5 and MMSE score < 24. Clinical stages were operationalized following AA-2024 recommendations : CU participants (CDR 0) were mapped to clinical stages 1–2, MCI (CDR 0.5 with preserved daily activities) to stage 3, and dementia (CDR ≥ 0.5 with functional impairment) to stages 4–6. While CDR served to define these functional categories, the MMSE was utilized as a continuous independent variable in our predictive models to capture finer gradations of cognitive performance. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before the study procedure. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board from Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (CGMHIRB No. 201701837A0, 201700982A0, 201800984A0, 201802151A0, 202000067A0).

The clinical parameters and demographic factors were meticulously documented. The measurement of cardiovascular risk was conducted using the Pooled Cohort Equations (PCEs) tool, a comprehensive instrument designed to evaluate the 10-year risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in adults [15]. Genotyping for APOE alleles ε2, ε3, and ε4 was performed by analyzing two single-nucleotide polymorphisms (rs429358 and rs7412) [16]. Each participant completed cognitive evaluation, including MMSE and CDR, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), [18F]florbetapir PET, and [18F]florzolotau PET scans.

Image acquisition and processing

[18F]florbetapir and [18F]florzolotau tracers were prepared and synthesized according to our previous protocols [17, 18]. All participants underwent both amyloid and tau PET imaging within an interval of one month. Amyloid imaging with [18F]florbetapir was performed using a Siemens Biograph mMR PET/MRI scanner and Tau imaging with [18F]florzolotau was conducted using a Siemens Biograph mCT PET/CT system. Image reconstruction and attenuation correction were performed according to our previous work [19]. T1-weighted MRI was acquired on a 3T Skyra scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) using a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence. PET images were processed using PMOD (version 4.2; PMOD Technologies Ltd., Zurich, Switzerland). Processing steps included motion correction, co-registration to individual T1-weighted MRI scans, and MRI-based spatial normalization of the co-registered PET images to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, as previously described [12]. Intensity normalization was then performed to generate the standardized uptake value ratios (SUVr) image, using the whole cerebellum as the reference region for amyloid PET and the inferior cerebellar cortex for tau PET.

Amyloid positivity and tau burden

For amyloid quantification, CL values were derived using the standardized processing pipeline implemented in our in-house software (AmPQ, https://sites.google.com/view/cgu-mipal/ampq) [20–22]. The threshold for amyloid positivity was established through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis in an independent cohort comprising visually amyloid-negative cognitively unimpaired individuals and visually amyloid-positive dementia patients. The optimal cutoff, defined by Youden’s index, was CL = 20. Subjects with CL > 20 were classified as amyloid positive (A+), whereas those with CL ≤ 20 were classified as amyloid negative (A−). The mean cortical SUVr of amyloid PET was calculated from a composite cortical volume of interest including the frontal cortex, temporal cortex, precuneus, parietal cortex, anterior cingulate, and posterior cingulate, based on prior florbetapir PET methodology [20, 23].

Global tau SUVr was calculated within a previously defined data-driven AD-vulnerable volume of interest (VOI), derived from voxel-wise SPM12 contrasts between amyloid-positive AD participants and cognitively unimpaired amyloid-negative individuals [12]. Global tau burden was then stratified into low, intermediate, or high levels using a bi-Gaussian mixture model based on previously established SUVr cutoff values of 1.45 and 2.12 [12]. This data-driven, tracer-specific threshold approach reduces dependence on cohort-specific cognitive characteristics and arbitrary thresholds, thereby improving robustness and facilitating application across independent cohorts and memory centers.

Braak staging system

The Braak staging system was operationalized by fitting a two-component Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) to the SUVr histograms within each Braak stage VOI (I/II, III/IV, V/VI), using data from our previous study comprising 45 CU A−, 50 MCI A+, and 80 dementia A+ participants (Supplementary Fig. 1) [12, 24, 25]. For each VOI, the cutoff value was defined as the intersection point of the two Gaussian probability density functions (PDFs). Based on this cutoff, dichotomous classification was applied to each VOI as negative (−) or positive (+). Subjects were classified as non-tau stage if all Braak stages were negative (–, –, –); transentorhinal stage if only Braak stage I/II was positive (+, –, –); limbic stage if Braak stages I/II and III/IV were positive (+, +, –); and neocortical stage, if all Braak stages were positive (+, +, +) (Fig. 1). The concordant group were individuals who followed the sequential Braak stage from non-tau to neocortical stage. Individuals whose positivity pattern did not conform to the sequential Braak staging scheme were categorized as undetermined (e.g. (-, -, +), (-, +, -), (-, +, +) and (+, -, +)). Based on the AA-2024 diagnostic framework, amyloid-positive participants were additionally classified into biological stages A–D according to Braak-based tau PET topography: Stage A, non-tau stage; Stage B, transentorhinal stage; Stage C, limbic stage; and Stage D, neocortical stage. Cases with non-sequential Braak positivity patterns were categorized as undetermined and excluded from the clinical–biological concordance analysis (Fig. 1) [11, 26].

MRI volumetric measurement

The volumetric analysis of the brain was conducted using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT) [27]. The total intracranial volume (TIV), total gray matter (GM), frontal lobe (FL), parietal lobe (PL), medial temporal lobe (MTL), lateral temporal lobe (LTL), occipital lobe (OL), and precuneus were calculated. Relative regional brain volumes were calculated by dividing each regional volume by TIV.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v9.5.1) and MATLAB (R2021a). Group differences were assessed using ANOVA with Holm–Šídák correction or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Associations between amyloid burden, tau measures, and clinical variables were examined using linear and multivariable regression. Multicollinearity between predictors was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with higher values indicating greater collinearity. Logistic regression models were constructed to classify tau burden, with model discrimination assessed using Tjur’s pseudo-R². Candidate predictors initially considered included age, sex, education, APOE genotype, cardiovascular risk, amyloid CL, MMSE, and regional MRI measures. Final models retained predictors based on clinical relevance and statistical contribution. Discriminative performance of selected clinical measures was assessed separately using ROC curve analyses and the area under the curve (AUC). Optimal cutoff scores for neuropsychological tests discriminating elevated tau burden were determined post hoc using ROC curve analysis, with thresholds selected by maximizing Youden’s index. The diagnostic utility based on the AUC was interpreted with conventional thresholds: AUC ≥ 0.9 (excellent), 0.8–0.9 (good), 0.7–0.8 (moderate), and 0.6–0.7 (poor) [28]. Model robustness was evaluated by bootstrap resampling (2,000 iterations) with re-estimation of coefficients and Nagelkerke’s R² [29]. A two-tailed P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A post-hoc sensitivity power analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7). For the multivariable regression analysis (N = 229, 4 predictors), the study was powered at > 99% to detect a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15) at alpha = 0.05. For the logistic regression classification models among amyloid-positive participants (N = 134), the sample provided > 90% power to detect the observed area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) values exceeding 0.80. These results indicate that the sample size was sufficient to provide robust effect size estimates and minimize the risk of Type II errors. To quantify the magnitude of biological and clinical effects, Cohen’s d was calculated for group differences in MRI volumes. For logistic regression models, individual predictor weights were expressed as Odds Ratios (OR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). Overall model performance and explained variance were assessed using Nagelkerke’s R2 and Tjur’s coefficient of discrimination (Pseudo-R2).

Results

Demographic data

A total of 229 participants were recruited in the current study. Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of 49 participants with CU, 82 with MCI, and 98 with dementia. The CU group was significantly younger than both the MCI and dementia groups (67.7 ± 6.6 vs. 71.6 ± 6.1 and 71.3 ± 7.5 years, respectively; both p < 0.05). The CU group also had a lower proportion of female participants and more years of education (both p < 0.05). APOE ε4 carrier status and amyloid positivity were higher across diagnostic groups CU to MCI to dementia (both p < 0.05). By contrast, PCEs cardiovascular risk scores did not differ significantly across groups (p = 0.26).

Amyloid and tau burden were higher across diagnostic groups from CU to dementia (Table 1). With respect to the categorization of patients according to low, intermediate, and high levels of global tau burden, the distribution in the CU group was 45:4:0, in the MCI group 53:26:3, and in the dementia group 29:41:28. A significant group difference was identified (χ² test, p < 0.05). A comprehensive analysis of brain MRI volumes revealed substantial between-group disparities in several regions, including the total GM, the FL, the MTL, the LTL, OL and the precuneus (all p < 0.05). The standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d) for MTL volume reduction were 1.61 for CU versus dementia and 1.17 for MCI versus dementia, representing very large effects.

Braak staging system and amyloid burden

We identified Braak stage-specific cutoff values of 1.30 (Braak I/II), 1.57 (Braak III/IV), and 1.47 (Braak V/VI) for [18F]florzolotau PET images (Supplementary Fig. 1). As summarized in Table 1, SUVr values demonstrated significant between-group differences, with stepwise differences across diagnostic groups from CU to dementia (all p < 0.05).

We next examined the relationship between global tau burden and amyloid burden in the context of sequential Braak progression. In our A+ participants, the distribution of CL values exhibited a bimodal pattern, with peaks around 60 and 100. Accordingly, participants were stratified into four groups based on CL values: ≤20, 21–60, 61–100, and > 100. Across amyloid CL strata, global tau burden demonstrated a clear stepwise pattern. Among A– participants, 96.8% exhibited a low global tau burden. In the CL 21–60 group, 93.9% of individuals were classified within the low-to-intermediate tau range. In contrast, higher amyloid levels were associated with a greater proportion of high tau burden: 22.6% of those with CL 61–100 and 36.2% of those with CL > 100 fell into the high-tau category (Table 2).

Biological and clinical staging proposed by the AA-2024 diagnostic criteria

Participants with A+ findings were classified according to Braak-based tau PET staging and cognitive status. Biological stage proportions shown in Fig. 1 were calculated after excluding individuals with undetermined Braak-stage patterns. Accordingly, the percentages were based only on participants with determinate biological stages, which explains the slight difference from Supplementary Table 1. As summarized in Fig. 1 and 75.0% of CU participants were in Stage A, indicating minimal or no tau accumulation. Among those with MCI, 44.0% were in Stage A and 52.0% in Stages B–D, reflecting greater tau burden. In the dementia group, 26.3% were categorized as Stage A, while 55.0% reached Stage D, consistent with advanced tau pathology.

To further characterize disease severity across the AD continuum, amyloid-positive participants with determinate Braak-stage patterns were stratified using the AA-2024 biological and clinical staging framework; four participants with undetermined Braak patterns were excluded from this analysis (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Based on the correspondence between biological stage (tau PET–defined Braak stage) and clinical stage (cognitive status), participants were classified into three subgroups: the clinical-biological concordance subgroup, the clinical-predominant AD subgroup (clinical > biological), and the biological-predominant AD subgroup (biological > clinical) (Fig. 1). Of the A+ participants, 51 (39.2%) were categorized as the concordance group, 69 (53.1%) as clinical predominant subgroup, and 10 (7.7%) as biological predominant subgroup.

Factors associated with clinical–biological discordance

Given the skewed distribution across biological–clinical subgroups (Fig. 1), we stratified analyses by cognitive status to compare the concordance, clinical-predominant, and biological-predominant subgroups. Among participants with dementia, the clinical-predominant subgroup was older, had higher cardiovascular risk, and exhibited lower amyloid and tau PET burden despite greater cognitive impairment compared with the concordance subgroup (all p < 0.05). Sex, education, and APOE genotype did not differ, and age and cardiovascular risk were not collinear, with a VIF of 1, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity; anti-hypertensive medication use was more frequent in the clinical-predominant subgroup (p = 0.02). In multivariable logistic regression analyses comparing the clinical-predominant and concordant subgroups among amyloid-positive participants, higher amyloid burden quantified by CL values was independently associated with lower odds of clinical predominance (OR = 0.97, 95% CI 0.94–0.99, p = 0.02). In contrast, cardiovascular risk estimated by PCEs was no longer significant after adjustment for age, sex, education, amyloid burden, and APOE genotype (p = 0.89).

Within the MCI group, participants classified as biological-predominant subgroup did not differ from the concordance subgroup in age, years of education, or amyloid CL values (all p > 0.05). By contrast, they showed higher global tau SUVr (2.6 ± 0.3 vs. 1.6 ± 0.1; p = 0.01), as well as higher Braak III/IV VOI SUVr (2.0 ± 0.2 vs. 1.6 ± 0.1; p < 0.01) and Braak V/VI VOI SUVr (1.7 ± 0.2 vs. 1.3 ± 0.1; p < 0.01), assessed with the nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test due to the small biological-predominant subgroup size (n = 9) and concordance subgroup size (n = 4).

Figure 2A illustrates the association between CL scores and tau SUVr values. Among all participants, there was a significant association between CL scores and tau SUVr values (R² = 0.46, p < 0.01). In a multivariable regression model, age, CL scores, MMSE scores, and relative MTL volume showed significant associations with tau SUVr values (R² = 0.62, all p < 0.05). However, sex, years of education, APOE genotype, and PCEs scores were not significant factors in the statistical model (all p > 0.05).

Clinical characteristics of low tau group in AD participants

We compared clinical and neuroimaging characteristics of AD participants stratified by global and topographical tau burden (Supplementary Table 2). Participants with low global tau burden exhibited significantly better cognitive performance (higher MMSE, lower CDR), larger TIV and GM volumes, and lower amyloid and tau PET burden compared with those with intermediate/high global tau burden (all p < 0.05). Age, sex, and years of education did not differ between groups.

For topographical tau burden, participants with non-tau or transentorhinal Braak stages showed better cognition, lower amyloid and tau PET burden, and higher PCE cardiovascular risk scores than those with limbic or neocortical involvement (all p < 0.01). This low topographical tau group was also older and had a lower proportion of females (both p < 0.05).

Amyloid CL–based classification model of tau burden

Among amyloid-positive participants with MCI or dementia (CL > 20), global tau burden was dichotomized into low/intermediate versus high groups based on global tau SUVr. Logistic regression incorporating age, CL, MMSE, and relative medial temporal lobe (MTL) volume was used to classify tau burden. The full model showed significant contributions from age, CL, and MMSE (Tjur’s pseudo-R² = 0.35; all p < 0.05) and demonstrated good discrimination (AUC = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.81–0.94). A probability cutoff of 0.268 yielded a sensitivity of 0.83 and specificity of 0.82 for identifying low/intermediate versus high tau burden (Fig. 3A). Each one-point decrease in MMSE was associated with a 19.7% increase in the odds of high tau burden (OR = 0.803; 95% CI: 0.73–0.89; p < 0.01). APOE ε4 carrier status and sex were not significant predictors in the overall model (p = 0.65 and p = 0.29, respectively).

Stratification by amyloid burden revealed distinct discriminative patterns. In participants with CL 21–60, 93.9% exhibited low-to-intermediate tau burden, with relative MTL volume serving as the primary discriminator (AUC = 0.73, p = 0.03). In contrast, among those with CL > 60, 29.0% had high global tau burden, and age and MMSE emerged as the strongest discriminators (AUC = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.76–0.93; Fig. 3B). The odds ratios for age and MMSE were 0.89 and 0.77, respectively. MMSE alone also demonstrated moderate-to-good discriminative performance for distinguishing low from intermediate/high tau burden in this group (AUC = 0.80, p < 0.01).

From a topographical perspective, participants with CL > 60 were classified as neocortical (52.5%), transentorhinal/limbic (20.2%), or non-tau stage (26.3%). Logistic regression distinguishing neocortical from non-neocortical tau involvement identified age and MMSE as significant predictors (Tjur’s pseudo-R² = 0.34), achieving good discrimination (AUC = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.76–0.92) with an optimal cutoff probability of 0.554 (Fig. 3C). The odds ratios for age and MMSE were 0.90 and 0.75, respectively. Each one-point decrease in MMSE increased the odds of reaching the neocortical tau stage by 25.2% (OR = 0.748; 95% CI: 0.67–0.84; p < 0.01).

Robustness of the classification model

To evaluate the robustness of the classification model, 2000 bootstrap iterations resampling was performed (Supplementary Table 3). Among amyloid-positive participants with MCI and dementia (CL > 20), using a dichotomized global tau classification (low/intermediate vs. high), the model demonstrated substantial overall fit, with a Nagelkerke’s R² of 0.474. The bootstrap-derived regression coefficients for age, CL, and MMSE were highly concordant with those estimated from the original logistic regression model, indicating stable parameter estimation and robust model performance.

In participants with higher amyloid burden (CL > 60), the global tau–based classification model likewise showed substantial overall fit (Nagelkerke’s R² = 0.438). From a topographical perspective, classification of neocortical versus non-neocortical tau involvement in the CL > 60 subgroup also yielded robust model fit, with a Nagelkerke’s R² of 0.395.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed a large Asian cohort across the AD continuum and developed a clinically practical model that differentiates global and regional tau burden using amyloid CL values and routine clinical measures, achieving robust classification performance (AUC > 0.85). This framework enables estimation of both global and topographical tau burden without direct reliance on tau PET. Clinically, the clinical-predominant subgroup was characterized by older age, higher cardiovascular risk, and lower tau burden, whereas the biological-predominant subgroup exhibited greater global and Braak stage–specific tau pathology. We further derived tracer-specific [18F]florzolotau Braak-VOI cutoffs using a data-driven GMM model. Consistent with biological staging, individuals with low global tau burden demonstrated better cognitive performance, larger brain volumes, and lower amyloid burden. Together, these findings support a clinically feasible framework for estimating tau burden and regional tau stage using routinely available data.

Bridging amyloid and tau for clinically accessible tau estimation

Our results align with and extend previous evidence demonstrating that amyloid PET metrics are closely associated with both concurrent and subsequent tau accumulation. Prior studies—primarily in non-Asian cohorts—have shown that amyloid PET positivity is strongly associated with greater tau deposition and more advanced disease stage, particularly within temporal and neocortical regions, supporting its use as a screening measure in tau-targeted clinical trials [30–32]. Although the biological linkage between amyloid and tau appears largely conserved across ethnicities, prior cross-ethnic studies have suggested differences in amyloid PET positivity rates as well as comorbidity and sociodemographic profiles across populations, which may influence the observed amyloid–tau relationship [32, 33]. By integrating amyloid CL scores with readily available clinical variables, our model addresses this translational gap, providing an accurate and accessible method for estimating tau burden in settings where tau PET imaging is limited. Unlike prior tau prediction approaches that rely on plasma biomarkers or complex modeling, this framework prioritizes routinely available measures—amyloid Centiloid values, age, MMSE, and medial temporal lobe volume—to enhance clinical feasibility and interpretability [34]. This framework offers a practical pathway toward more inclusive, population-adapted disease staging and precision trial enrollment. Because the present study is cross-sectional, the proposed framework estimates contemporaneous tau burden rather than longitudinal tau accumulation or progression.

Co-pathological influences on the clinical–biological divergence

The present study demonstrated that participants in the clinical-predominant subgroup were older, exhibited higher univariate PCE scores, and had significantly lower amyloid CL and global tau SUVr values compared with the concordance subgroup. However, after multivariable adjustment, the association between cardiovascular risk (PCE scores) and clinical predominance was attenuated, whereas age maintained a stronger, albeit borderline association despite relatively modest tau pathology [35]. This finding suggests that the clinical–biological discordance observed in older individuals may be influenced by additional age-related factors beyond measured amyloid and tau burden. Although unmeasured co-pathologies, including cerebrovascular disease and TDP-43, or reduced cognitive reserve may contribute, these possibilities remain speculative in the absence of direct measurement. This observation aligns with prior studies suggesting that while traditional risk factors like hypertension and diabetes increase AD risk, their impact is often mediated by or synergistic with the biological aging process [36, 37].

Clinical–biological discordance and ethnic heterogeneity

In our cohort, 39.2% of participants had concordant biological and clinical stages, 53.1% exhibited more severe clinical impairment than biological stage (clinical- predominant subgroup), and 7.7% showed the opposite pattern (biological- predominant subgroup). The greater proportion of the clinical-predominant subgroup compared with the biological-predominant subgroup aligns with observations in other ethnic cohorts, suggesting that co-pathologies often exacerbate clinical symptom severity even in individuals with relatively modest tau burden [38, 39]. The finding that 53.1% of amyloid-positive participants exhibited clinical-predominant AD is particularly noteworthy when compared to Western cohorts, such as ADNI, where the linkage between tau pathology and clinical symptoms often appears more direct. In our Asian cohort, the lower APOE ε4 prevalence may contribute to a slower biological progression of tau, yet the high prevalence of ‘silent’ vascular injury—common in East Asian populations—likely exacerbates cognitive decline [10]. This pattern may reflect a scenario in which clinical impairment is influenced by both AD pathology and unmeasured comorbid processes [40, 41]. These findings underscore the AA-2024 workgroup’s call for validating staging criteria in diverse populations, where the biological ‘weight’ of amyloid and tau may be modified by a different background of co-pathologies. Our results underscore the importance of incorporating non-AD biomarkers—such as vascular and inflammatory markers—into multimodal diagnostic frameworks to better capture the heterogeneity of cognitive decline within the AD continuum.

Clinical implications

Among participants with AD, both global and topographical low tau groups showed better cognitive performance, lower amyloid burden, and larger brain volumes than high tau groups. Individuals with amyloid CL values of 21–60 had a very low likelihood of advanced tau pathology, with 93.9% exhibiting only low-to-intermediate global tau burden, and relative medial temporal lobe volume serving as the primary discriminator. In contrast, when CL exceeded 60, the probability of high global and neocortical tau burden increased markedly (22–36%), with age and MMSE emerging as the dominant predictors. APOE ε4 did not remain an independent predictor after multivariable adjustment, consistent with prior evidence that its effects are attenuated once downstream amyloid and tau pathology are modeled [42]. Model robustness was confirmed by bootstrap resampling, demonstrating stable performance with substantial overall fit. The CL threshold of 60, derived from the bimodal amyloid distribution in our cohort, aligns with enrichment strategies used in anti-amyloid clinical trials, including lecanemab [5]. Recent Centiloid recommendations from the AMYPAD consortium, as well as other prior studies, suggest that values > 60 CL represent established amyloid pathology and are associated with a high likelihood of tau PET positivity [43, 44]. In addition, sensitivity analyses using alternative CL thresholds from 50 to 70 showed comparable discriminative performance across cutoffs, with CL > 60 providing the highest AUROC for neocortical classification and the highest balanced accuracy for global tau classification (Supplementary Table 4). The standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d) for MRI volume reductions in the MTL of CU versus dementia (d = 1.61) and MCI versus dementia (d = 1.17) represent very large biological effects. These robust structural differences, combined with the substantial odds ratios for MMSE, provide evidence that our framework captures clinically and biologically meaningful variance in the Alzheimer’s disease spectrum. The high Nagelkerke’s R2 of 0.474 for the global tau model further confirms that nearly half of the variance in tau pathology can be accurately estimated using these routinely available clinical and amyloid measures. Building on these observations, we propose a streamlined clinical decision algorithm integrating CL, age, and MMSE to estimate tau burden across clinically relevant thresholds (Fig. 4). Additional clinically relevant performance measures, including calibration metrics, confusion matrices, PPV, and NPV at the selected cutoffs, were also assessed and are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. An important conceptual consideration is that MMSE was included in the predictive models; therefore, the proposed framework should be interpreted as a pragmatic clinical surrogate integrating biological, structural, and cognitive information rather than as a purely biology-based estimate of tau burden. Models excluding MMSE showed lower explanatory performance, supporting its contribution to discrimination (Supplementary Tables 6, 7).

In settings where tau PET is unavailable, this framework provides a practical surrogate for estimating both global severity and topographic tau involvement using routinely accessible clinical and amyloid measures, supporting biological staging, therapeutic eligibility assessment, and precision treatment selection.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, tau PET cutoffs for Braak staging can vary across cohorts and methodological approaches. Tau PET cutoffs vary across cohorts; external validation is required [38, 45]. In the present study, we instead applied a two-component GMM across the AD continuum to derive tracer-specific cut points. Although this strategy is data-driven and reduces dependence on a single reference group, external validation in independent cohorts is needed. Second, although [18F]florzolotau appears to follow Braak-like patterns, it has not yet been directly validated against neuropathological Braak staging in autopsy-confirmed studies, in contrast to [18F]MK-6240, which has demonstrated strong regional correspondence between in vivo PET signal and post-mortem tau pathology according to Braak stages [46]. Third, our sample was derived from a tertiary memory clinic and did not explicitly include individuals at AA-2024 clinical stages 1–2, which may limit generalizability to community or primary care populations. Fourth, we did not incorporate biomarkers of non-AD co-pathologies or resilience factors—including inflammatory markers, α-synuclein indices, and transactive response DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43)—which could have provided further evidence supporting co-pathological processes. Fifth, the classification models were developed in a single cohort. Although internal performance was strong and bootstrap resampling demonstrated model stability, external validation in independent cohorts and across different imaging platforms and clinical settings is required before broader clinical applicability can be established. Finally, amyloid PET was acquired on a PET/MR system and tau PET on a PET/CT system, and we did not apply partial-volume correction; thus, modality-specific biases and partial-volume effects may have influenced quantitative SUVr estimates.

Conclusion

Using the AA-2024 framework, we identified distinct patterns of clinical–biological concordance and discordance shaped by age, vascular risk, and biomarker burden in a large Asian AD cohort. Leveraging routinely available measures, we developed a clinically feasible model that accurately classified global and neocortical tau burden (AUC > 0.85). This scalable framework offers a practical surrogate for tau PET in resource-limited settings and may enhance diagnostic staging, therapeutic selection, and clinical trial enrichment following external validation.
