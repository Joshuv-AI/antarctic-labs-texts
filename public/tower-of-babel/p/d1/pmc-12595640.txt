Factors influencing thrombocytopenia in geriatric patients in the intensive care unit: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Xuping Cheng, Mingxia Ji, Yun Wang, Xuandong Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThrombocytopenia (TP) has been associated with poor clinical outcomes in geriatric patients in the intensive care unit (ICU). However, the factors leading to TP in critically ill older patients are not clear. In this study, we aimed to investigate the primary factors influencing TP in geriatric patients in the ICU, as well as to evaluate the relationship between TP and adverse clinical outcomes.MethodsBetween 2010 and 2020, 5,848 geriatric patients were initially admitted to the ICU at Dongyang People's Hospital, with 3,147 ultimately included in the study. Lasso regression was used to screen for relevant variables, followed by multivariate logistic regression analysis. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to further assess the impact of TP on in-hospital mortality. Validation of findings was conducted using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care database.Results32.6% of geriatric ICU patients developed TP during their stay. Compared with the non-TP group, the TP group showed significant increases in disease severity, hormone use, vasoactive drug use, and sepsis rates, as well as in-hospital mortality, mechanical ventilation use, duration of ICU stay, and hospitalization costs (p < 0.001). Lasso regression screening identified 24 relevant variables, and 32.6% of patients developed TP during their ICU stay. Sensitivity analyses and PSM confirmed a strong association between TP and increased in-hospital mortality.ConclusionsTP is common among geriatric patients in ICU and is associated with poor prognosis. This study identified several important factors influencing TP, highlighting valuable implications for the early identification of high-risk patients and the development of individualized treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06577-2.

Introduction

Platelets, as crucial blood cells in the human body, play essential roles not only in blood coagulation and wound healing but also in inflammatory and immune responses [1–3]. With a prevalence of 21–77%, thrombocytopenia (TP) is one of the most common clinical problems in the intensive care unit (ICU). TP is often predictive of disease exacerbation and poor prognosis, and is associated with increased stay duration, hospitalization costs, and morbidity and mortality among patients in the ICU [4, 5].

Although many studies have examined the prevalence of TP in critically ill patients and explored its relationship with clinical outcomes, relatively few studies have specifically focused on geriatric patients in ICU. With the accelerating global aging trend, the global proportion of older individuals has gradually increased, accompanied by various underlying diseases and health problems, causing an increase in the demand for ICU admissions [6, 7].

Several factors can impact TP in geriatric patients. The aging process results in decreased bone marrow function and diminished hematopoietic stem cell reserve in geriatric patients, leading to reduced thrombopoiesis, i.e., platelet production [8]. Geriatric patients also frequently present with various underlying diseases and comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease, liver dysfunction, and renal insufficiency, which may also contribute to TP [9]. In addition, geriatric patients undergo more invasive therapeutic measures in the ICU, such as deep vein cannulation, mechanical ventilation, and renal replacement therapy, which accelerate platelet destruction. Although many common factors, such as medication use, disease severity, concomitant infections, and liver and kidney disease, are known triggers of TP, whether these risk factors are equally applicable to geriatric patients remains unclear [10–12].

It was hypothesized that the factors influencing the occurrence of TP in critically ill older patients would differ from those in other populations, and that TP would be associated with adverse clinical outcomes. To investigate this, a retrospective approach was applied, utilizing extensive clinical data from the ICUs of two tertiary hospitals. In this study, we aimed to identify factors that influence the development of TP, hoping to contribute to more effective strategies for the management of TP in geriatric ICU patients.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study was conducted in compliance with the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) initiative (Table S1). From 2010 to 2020, the study population consisted of geriatric patients who were initially admitted to Dongyang People's Hospital's ICU. The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) III dataset, a commonly used freely accessible dataset for critical illness, was used for validation [13].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were patients over the age of 64 who were initially admitted to the ICU. Exclusion criteria were patients with pre-existing TP prior to ICU admission; those with ICU stay duration < 24 h; those who had hematologic malignancies, primary TP, heparin-associated TP, thrombocytopenic purpura, cirrhosis, or prior splenectomy; or those with > 30% of clinical data missing.

The study adhered to all relevant local guidelines and regulations and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Dongyang People’s Hospital (DRY-2022-YX-155). Due to the retrospective and observational nature of the study, informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. Before conducting the analysis, the data were anonymized. After completing the online training course, “Protecting Human Research Participants,” offered by the National Institutes of Health (Certification number 7632299), author XJ was granted permission to access the MIMIC III database.

Data collection

Data were collected by Shanghai LE9 Health Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) using medical record data mining software. The variables were collected at specific time points or intervals as follows: baseline information, including age, sex, APACHE II score, smoking history, alcohol use disorder, and comorbidities, was recorded at the time of ICU admission. Medication-related data such as hormone, antiplatelet drug, and vasoactive drug use were documented within the first 48 h following ICU admission. Laboratory test results (blood gas analysis, complete blood count, blood biochemistry, and coagulation panel) were obtained within the first 24 h of ICU admission. Vital signs, including maximum, minimum, and mean values, were measured continuously within the first 24 h of ICU admission, and their daily averages were recorded for further analysis.

Mortality in hospital was the primary outcome, while mechanical ventilation time, ICU stay time, and hospitalization duration and cost were secondary outcomes.

Diagnostic criteria

Geriatric patients were defined as patients over the age of 64 [6, 14]. TP was defined as platelet count < 100 × 109/L during the ICU stay [4, 15].

Data processing

Variables with more than 20% of missing values were removed, whereas those with < 2% of missing values were filled in with the mean value. Variables with 2%–20% of missing values were imputed using multiple imputation techniques. Outliers were treated as missing values.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were analyzed using the CBCgrps R package [16]. Normally distributed continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), while non-normally distributed data are presented as median (P25, P75). For baseline characteristics, analysis of variance was used for comparisons of continuous variables, and the chi-square test was used for comparisons of categorical variables. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Differences with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

To identify potential factors influencing TP, we initially considered 87 variables based on previous literature and clinical relevance [4, 5, 17]. After excluding variables with over 20% missing values, 75 variables remained. Next, the CARET R package was used for data preprocessing to exclude variables with strong correlations with other independent variables, resulting in 69 remaining variables. The lasso regression method was used for screening these risk factors. The cv.glmnet function was used to select the lasso regularization parameter lambda through tenfold cross-validation, and variables were progressively selected by controlling the lambda value. Finally, 24 variables were included [18]. Univariate analysis showed that all these variables had p < 0.05, and these variables were then incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model. Backward stepwise regression was used to build the final model. The multicollinearity of the variables was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), and all VIF < 5.

To clarify the effect of TP on in-hospital mortality, we performed logistic regression analysis. Model 1 was unadjusted, serving as the baseline, while Model 2 adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 included further adjustments for age, sex, baseline platelet count, and other potential confounders. The results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values. In a sensitivity analysis to rule out the impact of sepsis, we constructed three logistic regression models for non-septic patients using the same approach to confirm the association between TP and adverse outcomes.

Additionally, propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to assess the effect of TP on in-hospital mortality. The variables used for matching included sex, age, disease severity, sepsis status, white blood cell count, platelet count, and lactate levels, with 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching. Finally, a similar analysis was conducted on older patients from the MIMIC database.

Results

Comparisons of complications and clinical outcomes

Comorbidities and clinical outcomes between the two groups were compared (Table 2). In the TP group, 45% of patients had hypertension, compared to 58.3% in the non-TP group (p < 0.001). Additionally, 13.4% and 17% of patients in the TP and non-TP groups, respectively, had diabetes, also showing a significant difference (p = 0.01). Furthermore, patients in the TP group had a significantly higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease and myocardial infarction than did those in the non-TP group (p < 0.05). In the TP group, the in-hospital mortality rate was 28.8%, exhibiting a statistically significant increase when compared to that of the non-TP group, which had a rate of 13.1%. In addition, the TP group had longer durations of mechanical ventilation, ICU stay, and hospitalization and greater hospitalization costs than did the non-TP group (p < 0.001). In the MIMIC III dataset, the incidence of TP was 16.8% (2,363/14,025 patients). Moreover, the TP group had lower rates of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and heart failure than the non-TP group (p < 0.05). Analysis of factors influencing TP

Lasso regression was used to screen 69 candidate variables, and tenfold cross-validation was used to select the optimal lambda value. The lasso regularization path and cross-validation error plots are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. Ultimately, 24 variables were screened. Univariate analysis revealed that all these variables had p < 0.05, qualifying them for inclusion in a multivariate logistic regression model executed through backward stepwise regression. Variables such as APACHE II score (1.05 [1.03–1.07], p < 0.001), initial white blood cell (WBC) count (1.04 [1.02–1.07], p < 0.001), platelet count (0.96 [0.96–0.96], p < 0.001), creatinine level (1.01 [1.01–1.01], p < 0.001), prothrombin time (1.27 [1.19–1.37], p < 0.001), use of vasoactive drugs (1.64 [1.30–2.07], p < 0.001), and hormone use (1.44 [1.11–1.87], p = 0.006) were significantly associated with the development of TP. These results are depicted in a forest plot (Fig. 4). Applying the same methodology to the MIMIC III data, factors such as age, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score, maximum diastolic blood pressure, initial WBC count, creatinine level, pH, red blood cell count, bicarbonate level, calcium level, prothrombin time (PT), and hormone use were identified as factors influencing the development of TP in critically ill geriatric patients. Logistic regression and sensitivity analysis for TP and hospital mortality

Logistic regression analysis confirmed that TP was an independent predictor of hospital mortality. In the unadjusted model, TP was associated with more than a twofold increase in-hospital mortality (OR 2.27, 95% CI: 1.85–2.78, p < 0.001). Patients with TP had higher hospital mortality (22.9% vs. 14.4%, p < 0.001), longer ICU stays (3.9 vs. 2.5 days, p < 0.001), and extended hospital stays (10 vs. 7.8 days, p < 0.001). Logistic regression models in this cohort also indicated that TP was associated with increased mortality, even after adjusting for confounders (OR 1.78, 95% CI: 1.55–2.04, p < 0.001) (Tables S3–S4). Sensitivity analyses, conducted after excluding cases of sepsis, continued to demonstrate a robust correlation between TP and elevated mortality rates (Supplementary Tables S5–S6).

Discussion

This study explored primary factors influencing TP in geriatric patients in the ICU and revealed a high prevalence of TP and a strong association with poor clinical outcomes. A large set of variables was considered as a possible influencing factor, and the lasso regression method, which performs well in feature selection with high-dimensional data, was used for screening [18]. Disease severity, WBC count, creatinine level, PT, and 48-h hormone use were ultimately identified as common factors influencing TP in critically ill geriatric patients at both hospitals.

A high degree of disease severity appears to be associated with an increased risk of TP, consistent with the findings of several previous studies [19, 20]. Notably, infection is considered to be the most important cause of TP in ICU [21]. Moreover, high WBC count, an essential indicator of the body's inflammatory response, showed a significant correlation with thrombocytopenia in our study. Elevated creatinine levels are often indicative of renal dysfunction, while thrombopoietin (platelet production factors) synthesized in the kidneys helps regulate platelet production [22, 23]. Therefore, geriatric patients may be more susceptible to TP due to declined renal function. In addition, coagulation is closely associated with platelet count and function. Previous research has shown that systemic activation of the coagulation system leads to the depletion of coagulation proteins, resulting in TP [24]. Hormones are the first-line therapeutic agents for treating immune TP and are not currently indicated for TP of other etiologies [25, 26]. However, in ICU settings, corticosteroid therapies are frequently administered to manage conditions such as septic shock, inflammation, and adrenal insufficiency. These treatments can affect platelet production and function, potentially contributing to the development of TP. In the present study, geriatric patients with 48-h hormone use had a significantly increased risk of TP, suggesting increased caution in the clinical use of hormone treatment, especially in geriatric patients. For older ICU patients with these risk factors, close monitoring of platelet counts is essential. Early intervention and personalized care strategies may help improve the management of TP in this patient population and reduce healthcare costs in ICU settings.

We performed an in-depth analysis of common comorbidities in geriatric patients and found that the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus was lower in the TP group than in the non-TP group. Surprisingly, in the MIMIC dataset, diabetes was even shown to be a protective factor for TP in geriatric patients, which may be associated with abnormal platelet activation and enhanced platelet secretion in diabetic patients [27–29], resulting in increased circulating activated platelets and platelet-leukocyte aggregates [28, 30]. Hyperglycemia, insulin deficiency, and associated metabolic dysfunction may be responsible for these changes [31]. It remains to be investigated whether factors other than those clearly associated with TP, such as cirrhosis, are risk factors for other common comorbidities in geriatric patients.

This study has some limitations. First, due to its retrospective nature, some data were missing, such as immune system indicators. With the declining immune function in the older population and increasing focus on immune-related thrombocytopenia, we may not have been able to comprehensively evaluate the overall impact of the immune system [32, 33]. Prospective or interventional studies focusing on relevant variables should be considered in the future. Second, numerous factors can influence platelet count in the ICU, including medication use. Although heparin-induced TP was ruled out, other drug-induced thrombocytopenia may not have been considered. Fortunately, this accounts for only a small proportion of ICU patients [34]. Finally, the actual prevalence of TP may be higher due to the exclusion of some cases that may explain the development of TP (cancer, cirrhosis, or heparin-induced thrombocytopenia).

Conclusion

This study identified primary factors associated with TP in geriatric patients in the ICU and revealed their close relationship with adverse clinical outcomes. These findings provide a crucial basis for the early identification and intervention of TP by clinicians.
