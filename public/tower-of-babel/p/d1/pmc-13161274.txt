Gut microbiota-dependent protection by Lactobacillus reuteri against renal injury in brain-dead rats
Authors: Tao Liu, Guangyi Zhu, Haibin Li, Jiqiu Wen, Ning Wen, Xuyong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDonation-after-brain-death (DBD) serves as a major source of kidney transplant donors, and brain death (BD) induces systemic inflammation and gut barrier disruption, leading to renal injury and compromised organ quality. While Lactobacillus reuteri (L. reuteri) demonstrates anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties that maintain gut microbiota homeostasis. However, its role in protecting organs against BD-associated injury remains unclear. This study investigated BD-induced renal injury mechanisms linked to gut-kidney axis dysregulation and evaluated L. reuteri’s protective effects through microbiota-immune interactions. BD triggered intestinal barrier dysfunction, elevating gut permeability and promoting translocation of endotoxin lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to kidneys, which activated the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, driving proinflammatory cytokine release, neutrophil infiltration, and renal dysfunction. L. reuteri pretreatment attenuated these effects by remodeling gut microbiota—reducing Proteobacteria while enriching Bacteroidota—and enhancing tight junction integrity, thereby suppressing pro-inflammatory LPS leakage and accumulation to renal tubule. Mechanistically, the increase in Bacteroidota abundance correlated with the downregulation of renal TLR4/NF-κB signaling, a pattern that is hypothesized to be explained by putative competitive inhibition by LPS derived from Bacteroidota. Concurrently, L. reuteri induced the upregulation of CD25⁺ immune cell expression in ileal glands and promoted CXCL2 production in renal and intestinal tissues, which is associated with the establishment of a gut-kidney immune homeostasis network. L. reuteri mitigates BD-induced renal injury through dual mechanisms—structural modulation of microbiota-derived LPS and intestinal immune remodeling—providing a novel microbiota-targeted adjunctive strategy to enhance donor organ viability in transplantation medicine.Keywords: Lactobacillus reuteri, Brain death, Renal injury, Gut microbiota, LPSSubject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology, Nephrology

Introduction

Organ transplantation is the only effective treatment for patients with end-stage organ failure. Global Observatory on Donation and Transplantation 2022 data show that donation-after-brain-death (DBD) donors account for 77% of deceased donors, but the quality of organs from DBD donors is restricted by multiple factors and thus seriously restricts transplantation success rates. Studies have shown that the excellent outcome rate of kidney transplantation from DBD donors (31.7%) is significantly lower than that from living donors (54.1%)1. Brain death (BD) is able to trigger multiorgan ischemic-hypoxic injury via neuroendocrine dysregulation, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), and hemodynamic instability. However, dysregulation of gut microbiota and its mediated “gut-organ axis” imbalance are considered one of the potential mechanistic factors aggravating organ damage2.

It is well known that the synergistic effects of oxidative stress, hemodynamic disorders, inflammatory cascade reactions, immune activation, and microcirculatory disorders generated after BD may cause renal injury3. IL-6 is considered a very important mediator in the acute-phase response4 and has been shown to be negatively correlated with renal transplant survival rates5,6. In addition to elevated IL-6 levels, increased levels of IL-8, IL-10, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) can also be found in the serum of DBD donors7,8. Additionally, increased activation of intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) in the renal interstitium and glomeruli9, as well as increased numbers of CD4-positive and CD8-positive cells, polymorphonuclear cells, ED-1, and ED-29,10. In kidneys from expanded criteria donors (ECD) and those with a high kidney donor profile index (KDPI ≥ 85%), BD-induced damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as High mobility group box-1 protein (HMGB1) activate the TLR4 pathway, triggering MyD88-dependent and -independent (TRIF) signaling cascades to promote the expression of pro-inflammatory factors (IL-1β, TNF-α, IFN-γ) and anti-inflammatory factor IL-1011. Another study found that a TLR4 desensitization protocol significantly inhibited the levels of pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and CXCL1 in serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid after BD and reduced the expression of neutrophil adhesion molecule CD11b12. Additionally, nuclear translocation of NF-κB upregulates the expression of kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), which serves as a specific marker of acute tubular injury and is closely associated with increased serum creatinine (Scr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels13. Drug pretreatments such as the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 and erythropoietin derivatives can effectively reduce inflammation and endothelial activation14, and the heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) inducer cobalt protoporphyrin (CoPP) significantly improves pancreatic graft survival in animal models15. However, there still is a lack of studies on the protective effects of probiotics against organ injury after BD so far.

BD triggers systemic metabolic disorders through sympathetic excitation and catecholamine storms, leading to acidification of the intestinal microenvironment, inhibition of probiotics, and significant expansion of acid-resistant pathogenic bacteria such as enterobacteriaceae16. Dysregulation of gut microbiota further impairs the intestinal mucosal barrier and the expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, occludin), resulting in increase of intestinal permeability as well as the apoptosis of intestinal neutrophils and intestinal epithelial cells14,17. These changes lead to bacterial translocation from the intestine, release of cytokines and endotoxins into the bloodstream, and increased serum endotoxin levels in critically ill DBD donors17. Meanwhile the level of LPS in the serum of DBD donors is positively correlated with the incidence of post-transplant organ dysfunction and sepsis17,18, suggesting that microbiota-derived endotoxin translocation is a recognized contributor to systemic inflammation and organ injury following BD. LPS activates the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, inducing massive release of pro-inflammatory factors and driving a systemic inflammatory storm19. This established pathway links gut-derived endotoxin exposure to the systemic inflammation observed after BD. These findings suggest that organ injury caused by BD may be related to intestinal immunity and intestinal bacterial translocatio20. However, while the association between gut barrier disruption, endotoxemia, and adverse outcomes is established, the mechanistic role of the translocated LPS itself remains incompletely defined.​ Current understanding primarily focuses on the quantity or bioactivity of total LPS.

Probiotics may protect organs by balancing gut bacteria, repairing gut barriers, and reducing inflammation21,22. As a Gram-positive probiotic, L. reuteri shows dual protective effects on the intestine and kidneys in various disease models through its unique metabolites and immunomodulatory properties23. Daily supplementation with L. reuteri increases gut microbiota diversity in infants, reduces Proteobacteria but relatively increases Bacteroidetes24. L. reuteri is able to resist low pH and bile salts and forms biofilms by adhering to intestinal epithelium and mucus via various surface molecules, therefore enhances its colonization in the gut. It can also regulate immune cell development or IgA levels, induce anti-inflammatory Tregs, reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and inhibit inflammation25. These findings suggest that L. reuteri may regulate inflammation, oxidative stress, and gut microbiota balance through multiple targets.

The present study investigated the hypothesis that gut dysbiosis and TLR4/NF-κB pathway hyperactivation contribute to BD-induced renal injury. Using a rat model of BD, we demonstrate that dysbiosis impairs intestinal barrier integrity, promoting translocation of LPS to the kidneys. This triggers TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling, resulting in inflammation, neutrophil infiltration, and renal injury. Critically, we show that pretreatment with Lactobacillus reuteri remodels gut microbiota composition, repairs tight junctions, and suppresses LPS leakage. Mechanistically, L. reuteri is hypothesized to competitively inhibit pathogenic LPS binding to TLR4, downregulates NF-κB activation, and enhances anti-inflammatory responses. We thus hypothesize that L. reuteri confers protection not only by remodeling gut microbiota and repairing the barrier, but also by introducing structurally distinct, less inflammatory LPS that may competitively inhibit TLR4 activation, thereby describing a novel “gut microbiota-barrier-immune-kidney” protective axis.

Materials and methods

Animals and bacterial strains

Twenty-eight male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (SPF grade, body weight: 250 ± 30 g) were purchased from Suzhou Xishan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and housed in the animal facility of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Animal Use License No. SCXK (Jing) 2019-0010). Rats were randomly assigned to one of three experimental groups: Sham-operated (SHAM), BD, and BD with L. reuteri pretreatment (BD + LR).​All procedures strictly adhered to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University, in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health (NIH publication no.85–23, revised 1996). The facility maintained the following conditions: temperature 20–26 °C, humidity 50–60%, 12-h light/dark cycle (< 40 lx light intensity), 8–15 air changes per hour with HEPA filtration, ammonia concentration < 20 ppm, and noise < 85 dB. Rats were group-housed (3 per cage) and acclimated for 1 week prior to experiments. In this randomized controlled study, rats were subjected to either sham surgery or BD induction, with one BD group receiving a 7-day oral pretreatment of Lactobacillus reuteri(BD + LR). Primary endpoints assessed at 6 h post-procedure included renal function, inflammatory markers, intestinal barrier integrity, and gut microbiota composition.

Rat experiments

Rats were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.) and secured on a thermostatic plate after loss of righting reflex. Surgical fields (head, neck, abdomen) were shaved and disinfected. The carotid artery and jugular vein were cannulated using 24G indwelling needles (heparinized saline-flushed) for arterial pressure monitoring and drug infusion, respectively. A tracheostomy was performed via T-shaped incision below the larynx, connected to a ventilator (tidal volume: 8–10 mL/kg; respiratory rate: 40–60 breaths/min). Cystostomy was performed with a silicone tube for continuous urinary drainage, enabling accurate hourly output measurement as standard in this BD model to dynamically monitor renal function and adjust fluid infusion rates accordingly.

For intracranial pressure manipulation, a 3 mm burr hole was drilled adjacent to the sagittal suture, and a 4F Fogarty balloon catheter was inserted into the epidural space. Heart rate (HR) and mean arterial pressure (MAP) were monitored via a bioelectrical acquisition system (2 kHz sampling rate, 0.1–300 Hz filter bandwidth). After 30 min of hemodynamic stability, the balloon was inflated with saline (20 μL/min). Hydroxyethyl starch (0.039 mL/min) maintained MAP between 80 and 120 mmHg in all three groups. Sham controls underwent identical procedures without balloon inflation. Animals that failed to maintain MAP within the target range (60–140 mmHg) for more than 10 min during or after BD induction were excluded. Following exclusions based on hemodynamic instability and other experimental criteria (e.g., sample quality), the final numbers of rats that completed the study protocol and from which tissues were successfully collected for endpoint analyses were: SHAM, n = 4; BD, n = 6; BD + LR, n = 5. The specific sample size (n) for each assay is detailed in the corresponding methods section and figure legends.

Pretreated rats received daily oral L. reuteri ATCC23272 (1 × 108 CFU/mL, 1 mL/rat) for 7 days prior to BD induction. Brain death criteria included apnea (> 60 s), fixed dilated pupils, and absent corneal/light reflexes. Animals with MAP < 60 or > 140 mmHg for > 10 min were excluded. Six hours post-BD, tissues (kidney, ileum, feces) were collected after cardiac perfusion with saline and processed using sterile sharp blades.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from rat renal tissues (n = 4 for SHAM, n = 5 for BD, n = 5 for BD + LR; one BD sample was excluded due to poor RNA quality) by RNAiso Plus (Takara). RNA was reverse transcribed with One-Step gDNA Remover (Servicebio) and customized primers designed to amplify fragments of the target genes with SYBR master mix (Servicebio). Relative amounts of mRNA were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔ CT method, and GAPDH served as the housekeeping gene. Fold change values were calculated for genes expressed in experimental vs control conditions. The primers used in this study are listed in Table 1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Renal and ileum tissue were collected from euthanized rats and then homogenized and centrifuged. The supernatants were stored at − 20 °C until use for ELISA detection. The levels of TNF-α, IL-6 (Invitrogen), IL-10 (Invitrogen), IL-8 (QuantiCyto), IL-1β (Invitrogen), IFN-γ (Servicebio), KIM-1 (mlbio), MDA (Servicebio), GSH (Servicebio), T-SOD (Servicebio), CAT (Servicebio) GPX (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) and LPS (Elabscience) ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A BCA protein quantification kit (Thermo Scientific) was used to determine the protein concentrations in the supernatants. Urine samples were collected via cystostomy during the experimental period. Urinary KIM-1 levels were determined using a commercial ELISA kit (mlbio) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For the detection of serum intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (iFABP) (mlbio) and LPS (Uscn Life Science Inc), blood samples were collected from rats in each group. Serum was collected after centrifugation and stored at − 70 °C until detection with an LPS kit.

Histological analysis

Fresh kidney and ileum tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, subsequently embedded in paraffin and sectioned (4 μm) and then the sections were stained with H&E. Images of the H&E-stained slides were captured using an automated slide scanning system and could be observed and analyzed with CaseViewer digital slide reading software (Servicebio). Histological analysis was performed using Image J software. To minimize bias, histological scoring was performed by an observer blinded to the group allocation of the samples.

Renal function assessment

CRE and BUN levels in the collected blood samples were measured by Wuhan Servicebio Technology CO., LTD.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Fresh kidney or ileum tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (4 μm). The immunohistochemical (IHC) staining were performed according to the standard protocols by Servicebio (Wuhan, China). IHC staining was conducted with primary antibodies of TLR4 (Cat.19811–1-AP, Proteintech, 1:200), NF-κB (Cat.310126, Zenbio, 1:200), LPS-binding protein (LBP) (Cat.23559–1-AP, Proteintech, 1:300), myeloperoxidase (MPO) (Cat.R25062, Zenbio, 1:500), HO-1 (Cat.R24541, Zenbio, 1:400), CXCL2 (Cat.701126, Invitrogen, 1:250), HMGB1 (Cat.R22773, Zenbio, 1:300), KIM-1 (Cat.PAA785Ra01, Cloud-Clone Corp, 1:200), ZO-1 (Cat.164329, Zenbio, 1:500), CD4 (Cat.250201, Zenbio, 1:200), CD8 (Cat.GB15068, Servicebio, 1:500) and CD25 (Cat.222491, Zenbio, 1:200) at 4 °C overnight, then incubated with HRP- labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (Servicebio) for 50 min at room temperature. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used to visualize the antigen–antibody reaction. Quantification of immunohistochemical staining intensity was performed using Image J software by an investigator who was blinded to the experimental groups.

Gut microbial diversity analysis

Fresh fecal samples from different treatment groups of rats (n = 3–5 per group) were collected, and total DNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol of the Magnetic Soil and Stool DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China). High-throughput sequencing of the 16S full-length gene was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq sequencing platform with Primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) targeted the V3-V4 regions of the 16S rRNA genes. The microbiome data analysis was conducted by Servicebio (Wuhan, China).

Transmission electron microscopy

For transmission electron microscopy, rats were euthanized by an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg). Following confirmation of death, transcardial perfusion was performed with PBS, followed by the collection of renal and ileal tissues. Fresh tissue blocks (< 1 mm3) were immediately immersed in TEM fixative at 4 °C for 4 h, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M PBS for 2 h at room temperature, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Samples were subsequently permeated with acetone, gradually infiltrated with Embed-812 resin via a graded acetone-resin mixture (1:1 for 3 h, 2:1 overnight), and embedded in pure Embed-812 at 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin Sects. (60 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome, double-stained with uranyl acetate in ethanol and lead citrate (15 min each), and air-dried overnight. Ultrastructural analysis was performed using a HITACHI HT7700 transmission electron microscope.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (Version 10.0.2).. For comparisons of gut microbiota differences at the species level among the three groups, a two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) was employed to evaluate the effects of different groups and potential interactions on microbial composition. Other experimental datasets were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, with Shapiro–Wilk test applied to verify data normality and Brown-Forsythe test to assess variance homogeneity. Statistically significant results (P < 0.05) were further subjected to Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for post hoc analysis to identify specific group differences. Graphical representations (bar and line charts) display means ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with significance markers (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001) and error bars indicating SEM.

For gut microbiota analysis, within-sample (alpha) diversity was assessed using the Chao1 index calculated in QIIME2 (version 2020.6)26. Differences in the Chao1 index among groups were evaluated for statistical significance using the unpaired Student’s t-test. To explore differences in the relative abundance of specific bacterial species among the three groups, a repeated-measures two-way ANOVA (based on the general linear model) was performed, followed by Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test for multiple comparisons.

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess the associations between specific gut microbiota and pathway-related proteins. All datasets were tested for normality via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Pearson correlation coefficients and two-tailed P-values were calculated, with a significance threshold of α = 0.05. Scatter plots were generated, overlaid with linear regression lines, and annotated with r and P-values.

Results

Acute renal injury induced by BD and the protective effect of L. reuteri

To explore whether a sustained 6-h status of early BD would impair renal function, and L. reuteri could provide functional protection for kidneys after BD, rats were intragastrically administered 1 ml of L. reuteri solution with a concentration of 1 × 10⁸ CFU/ml every day for 7 consecutive days before establishing the BD model. The renal function and the acute kidney injury biomarker, kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), were detected. The results revealed that the BD state significantly damaged renal function, as indicated by notable increases in serum creatinine (Scr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels, and the expression of KIM-1 in renal tissues (p < 0.01). Although no significant differences were found in urinary KIM-1 across groups (P = 0.9), further immunohistochemical analysis confirmed that, after BD, the kidneys exhibited significant KIM-1 expression mainly in the renal tubular epithelial cells (p < 0.01). The injury in the kidneys was then observed under light microscopy with HE staining, which revealed no apparent architectural damage across groups. However, TEM revealed a spectrum of ultrastructural alterations in renal tubular epithelial cells across the experimental groups (Fig. 1G). Cells from SHAM-operated rats maintained normal architecture, with intact tight junctions, abundant microvilli, and mitochondria displaying well-preserved cristae. In contrast, BD induction resulted in severe damage, characterized by marked cellular edema, significant loss and disarray of microvilli, and profoundly swollen mitochondria with disrupted cristae. Notably, pretreatment with L. reuteri (BD + LR group) markedly attenuated these pathological changes. While moderate edema persisted, mitochondrial swelling was substantially reduced, cristae integrity was partially maintained, and the loss of microvilli was less severe compared to the BD group. These ultrastructural findings corroborate the functional data, demonstrating that L. reuteripretreatment provides significant cytoprotection against BD-induced renal tubular injury.

L. reuteri can inhibit the LPS/TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway in the kidneys after BD to exert a protective effect

To verify whether the TLR4 pathways identified in previous studies on organ damage after BD are activated in the kidneys, and whether L. reuteri exerts renal protection through this pathway, the presence of TLR4 pathway-associated proteins and LPS in the kidneys was detected. The results showed that 6 h after BD intervention, immunohistochemical expression of TLR4 (Fig. 2A/E), LBP (Fig. 2B/F), and NF-κB (Fig. 2C/G) in renal tubular epithelial cells increased significantly (P < 0.05). ELISA also revealed a significant increase in renal tissue LPS levels (Fig. 2D), indicating that BD activates the renal TLR4/NF-κB inflammatory pathway by elevating gut-derived pathogenic LPS. However, pretreatment with L. reuteri significantly inhibited the TLR4-mediated pathological process, likely by suppressing gut-derived LPS release and renal accumulation. The TLR4 pathway exerts damaging or reparative effects through downstream anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory factors, though many downstream molecules exhibit temporal and sensitivity variations. RT-PCR analysis of MyD88-dependent and TRIF-dependent pathways (TRIF, p38 MAPK) showed a 1.5-fold increase in MyD88 mRNA expression versus the SHAM group (P < 0.05). Further analysis indicated that L. reuteri had no significant effect on MyD88 expression (P = 0.323), suggesting its inhibitory effect does not involve direct MyD88 transcriptional regulation. Additionally, TRIF mRNA and p38 MAPK expression showed no statistically significant intergroup differences (P > 0.05; Fig. 3B/C), implying that the TLR4 downstream pathway activated in renal tubular epithelial cells is primarily MyD88-dependent.

L. reuteri regulates renal inflammatory response after BD

Previous studies and extensive clinical practice confirm that stress can induce elevated leukocyte levels (primarily neutrophils) even in the absence of disease. Given the early phase of BD involves a paradoxical state of neurohumoral, metabolic, and pathophysiological activation, this experiment aimed to determine whether LPS-TLR4-mediated pro-/anti-inflammatory responses occur in renal tissues within 6 h post-BD and whether HMGB1—an endogenous TLR4 ligand frequently implicated in ischemia–reperfusion injury and programmed cell death—activates TLR4 pathways and inflammatory responses contributing to renal injury (Figs. 4, 5, 6).

Results demonstrated significantly enhanced pro-/anti-inflammatory responses in renal tubular epithelial cells. Compared to the SHAM group, BD significantly upregulated MPO (P < 0.0001) and HO-1 (P < 0.01) protein expression, indicating robust renal pro-inflammatory activation. L. reuteri intervention markedly reduced MPO (P < 0.001) and HO-1 (P < 0.05) expression versus the BD group. No significant intergroup differences in HMGB1 were observed (P > 0.05). Notably, chemokine CXCL2 showed comparable levels between SHAM and BD groups (P > 0.05) but significantly increased in the BD + L. reuteri group (P < 0.05), a change that is associated with the regulation of immune cell recruitment in renal tissues and may contribute to the balance of local inflammatory responses. This finding indicates that L. reuteri partially balances the post-BD renal inflammatory microenvironment by selectively inhibiting pro-inflammatory factors (MPO, HO-1) and regulating CXCL2 expression, with no involvement of HMGB1.

Experiments confirmed that LPS-TLR4 activates NF-κB p65 via the classical MyD88-dependent pathway, where the release of inflammatory cytokines signifies LPS-TLR4 pathway activation and injury. At 6 h post-BD, mRNA expression of TLR4-associated inflammatory cytokines in renal tissues exhibited significant heterogeneity. Versus SHAM, the BD group showed significantly elevated mRNA levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (P < 0.001) and pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 (P < 0.01), IFN-γ (P < 0.05), IL-1β (P < 0.05), and TNF-α (P < 0.05), indicating broad TLR4-dependent renal inflammatory activation. L. reuteri pretreatment reduced IL-10, IL-6, and IFN-γ expression by 62%, 55%, and 48%, respectively (all P < 0.05), versus BD, while IL-1β remained unchanged (P > 0.05). TNF-α showed an increasing trend in the BD + L. reuteri group, and chemokine IL-8 mRNA exhibited no intergroup differences (P > 0.05). These results suggest L. reuteri downregulates TLR4 downstream cytokines (IL-10, IL-6, IFN-γ) but has limited effects on IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-8, implying BD-induced renal inflammation involves multiple pathophysiological processes.

Differences in Serum LPS-TLR4 Related Inflammatory Factors and Renal Expression After BD

It is proposed that the LPS detected in renal tissue after BD originates from translocation from the gut lumen into the circulation and subsequently accumulates in the renal tubules via blood transport. To determine if the serum exhibits inflammatory responses similar to those observed in the kidneys, relevant inflammatory factors in serum were measured using ELISA kits, and whether their inflammatory effects might also be regulated by L. reuteri was assessed.

Results showed that serum concentrations of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 (P < 0.01) and the pro-inflammatory factors IL-8 (P < 0.05) and IFN-γ (P < 0.05) were significantly elevated after BD, demonstrating BD-induced systemic inflammation activation. Pretreatment with L. reuteri reduced IL-10 and IFN-γ expression by 55% and 48% respectively (both P < 0.05) , while IL-8 concentration showed no significant change (P > 0.05). Although TNF-α showed an increasing trend in the BD + L. reuteri group, the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05), partly because protein expression was undetectable in some samples (all SHAM and BD samples tested negative).

Furthermore, unlike the significant differences in renal IL-1β and IL-6 mRNA expression, IL-6 protein was undetectable in serum, and IL-1β levels showed no significant difference between groups (P > 0.05).

Given that serum inflammatory factor expression patterns differed from those in renal tissue, and considering inter-group data heterogeneity, potential structural variations in LPS across different anatomical sites were hypothesized. Experiments showed low and comparable LPS levels in SHAM and BD groups (P > 0.05). However, LPS increased nearly twofold in the BD + L. reuteri group (P < 0.01), and this elevated serum LPS was derived from Bacteroidota enrichment induced by L. reuteri remodeling of gut microbiota, which is distinct from the pro-inflammatory Proteobacteria-derived LPS that accumulates in renal tissue after BD.

Association between L. reuteri-induced gut microbiota modulation and immune responses in rats

Based on 16S rDNA gene sequencing annotations, cumulative relative abundance plots were generated for highly abundant taxa at the phylum and genus levels. At the phylum level, the BD group exhibited reduced relative abundance of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobiota alongside elevated Proteobacteria. L. reuteri intervention shifted Bacteroidota to the predominant differential phylum, with concurrent recovery of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobiota and reduction in Proteobacteria, indicating effective reversal of BD-induced gut microbiota alterations (Fig. 7A). Genus-level analysis revealed increased abundance of unclassified Muribaculaceae, LigiLactobacillus, Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group, and Ruminococcus in the BD + L. reuteri group versus model controls, while Lactobacillus, Akkermansia, and LimosiLactobacillus decreased (Fig. 7B). The dysbiotic signature of BD-characterized by abnormal proliferation of Proteobacteria and Lactobacillus—was mitigated by L. reuteri through targeted regulation of Lactobacillus, suppression of Proteobacteria, and restoration of beneficial bacteria (e.g., LigiLactobacillus), thereby stabilizing low-abundance genera and improving the gut microenvironment.

L. reuteri modulates intestinal immune activation

This investigation evaluated treatment effects on immune marker expression (CD4⁺, CD8⁺, CD25) within the rat ileal mucosa. Comparative analysis revealed that versus SHAM controls, the BD cohort displayed elevated CD4⁺ cell density (Fig. 10A) and CD25 immunoreactivity (Fig. 10C) in mucosal tissues. L. reuteri pretreatment significantly attenuated both markers, indicating its capacity to counterbalance BD-induced immune upregulation in the ileal epithelium. Notably, immunohistochemical assessment identified substantial L. reuteri-mediated enhancement of CD25+ staining at the protein expression level specifically within lamina propria glands. This tissue-specific shift in CD25⁺ immunoreactivity suggests L. reuteri modulates the intestinal immune microenvironment by regulating the spatial expression of CD25⁺ cells, which may contribute to mucosal defense against pathogenic stimulation. Analysis of ileal antioxidant profiles revealed that compared to SHAM controls, the BD group exhibited biologically meaningful—though statistically non-significant—shifts in oxidative status. At 6 h post-BD, ileal tissues demonstrated directional increases in reduced glutathione (GSH), malondialdehyde (MDA), and total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD) relative to SHAM (all P > 0.05; Fig. 11A/E). These findings suggest a compensatory oxidative stress adaptation characterized by parallel elevation of antioxidant reserves (GSH) and lipid peroxidation markers (MDA), while core enzymatic defenses (CAT/GPX/SOD axis) maintained stability. This pattern implies distinct early-phase intestinal oxidative dynamics following BD compared to chronic pathologies, warranting validation through extended temporal assessments or enhanced detection methodologies.

Discussion

Hemodynamic instability, endocrine dysfunction, and inflammatory-immune cascades following BD drive tissue damage, organ impairment, and diminished graft viability8, yet their mechanistic origins remain elusive. Our findings establish gut-kidney axis dysregulation as a fundamental mechanism underlying BD-induced acute kidney injury (AKI), wherein intestinal barrier compromise and microbiota perturbations critically govern post-BD AKI pathogenesis. Crucially, L. reuteri attenuates renal LPS burden through intestinal barrier preservation and microbiota restructuring (Fig. 12), thereby intercepting TLR4-mediated inflammatory signaling at its source. We propose a dual-mechanism model for this protection: i) structural modulation of microbiota-derived LPS, and ii) establishment of a gut-kidney immune homeostasis network. Mechanistically, our data suggest that L. reuteri-induced enrichment of Bacteroidota leads to a shift in the pool of circulating LPS. We hypothesize that Bacteroidota-derived LPS, which is structurally distinct (e.g., penta-acylated, monophosphorylated lipid A) and possesses lower inflammatory potency compared to the hexa-acylated LPS from pathobionts like Proteobacteria, may act as a competitive inhibitor at renal TLR4 receptors, thereby attenuating the downstream NF-κB inflammatory cascade. This concept—that the lipid A structure, particularly its acylation pattern, dictates LPS immunogenicity—is strongly supported by clinical research. In patients with end-stage renal disease, a shift towards circulating LPS with shorter carbon chains (e.g., C14) is associated with heightened monocyte inflammatory responses, whereas the prevalence of longer-chain LPS (e.g., C18) correlates with reduced inflammation, suggesting that specific LPS subtypes can act as weaker agonists or even antagonists of TLR4 signaling27. This hypothesis is supported by the observed divergent distribution of LPS, with levels elevated in serum but reduced in the kidney, and the concomitant downregulation of renal TLR4/NF-κB signaling following L. reuteri pretreatment. However, this central mechanistic claim remains correlative, as the present study lacks direct biochemical characterization of lipid A structures or functional assays demonstrating competitive inhibition.

Reconstruction of Gut Microbiota by Lactobacillus reuteri and Mechanism in Alleviating Renal Injury After BD via the LPS-TLR4 Axis.

While TLR4 hyperactivation is well-documented in BD-associated nephropathy, its proximal triggers remain undefined. Prevailing research emphasizes downstream effectors (NF-κB/MAPK signaling) while neglecting systemic LPS translocation. We propose that BD-induced neuroendocrine cascades disrupt intestinal integrity, facilitating enteric LPS influx into circulation19. The kidney—with its high perfusion and abundant endothelial/proximal tubular TLR4/MD2 complexes—proves particularly vulnerable to LPS sequestration, mirroring sepsis pathophysiology28.LBP concentration-dependently potentiates LPS-TLR4 interactions, enabling low-concentration LPS cytotoxicity in tubular epithelia28, accounting for our observation of TLR4 cascade initiation despite minimal renal LPS. Significantly, L. reuteri reduced renal accumulation of pro-inflammatory LPS and downstream signaling, including a ~ 40% reduction in NF-κB p65 and a ~ 60% reduction in IL-6 mRNA.

TLRs constitute innate immune sensors activated by damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) from stressed cells, independent of infection29. We observed selective MyD88 mRNA upregulation without TRIF/p38 MAPK alterations, indicating MyD88-pathway dominance during early LPS exposure. L. reuteri's lack of MyD88 modulation suggests targeting of upstream LPS-TLR4 binding. p38 MAPK inactivity may reflect pathway hierarchy. HMGB1—a ubiquitous nuclear DNA chaperone that functions extracellularly as a canonical DAMP30—shows elevated expression in marginal donor kidneys (KDPI ≥ 85%)[4]and mediates renal ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) with kinetics proportional to insult severity31.Circulating HMGB1 peaks 16–32 h post-insult in endotoxemia models32. Absent nuclear-to-cytoplasmic HMGB1 translocation at 6 h post-BD (no IHC-detectable extra-nuclear staining) precludes TRIF-mediated IRI involvement. This defines a temporal activation paradigm: LPS-MyD88 signaling predominates ≤ 6 h post-BD, while HMGB1-TRIF engagement likely occurs later33. Given HMGB1’s established IRI role, our data suggest negligible early-phase IRI within 6 h post-BD.

Sepsis research established that LPS activates TLR4-dependent pathways leading to neutrophil activation and the release of granular proteins such as MPO34. Correspondingly, we observed significant renal MPO elevation in BD rats, indicating robust neutrophil infiltration and activation. Concurrently, the cytoprotective enzyme HO-1 was upregulated, suggesting a compensatory antioxidant response35,36. However, the concurrent elevation of both a pro-inflammatory marker (MPO) and an antioxidant enzyme (HO-1) indicates an imbalance and failed compensation against oxidative stress. Notably, pretreatment with L. reuteri coordinately suppressed both MPO and HO-1, demonstrating its dual capacity to mitigate pathological inflammation and modulate the endogenous antioxidant response.

Concurrent with modulating LPS profiles, L. reuteri appears to establish a gut-kidney immune homeostasis network. This is evidenced by: i) the restoration of intestinal tight junction integrity (Fig. 9) and reduced serum iFABP, indicating improved barrier function; ii) the spatial reprogramming of intestinal immune markers, with downregulation of mucosal CD4 + and CD25 + cells but upregulation of CD25 + cells in ileal glands (Fig. 10); and iii) the selective upregulation of chemokine CXCL2 in renal tissue (Fig. 4). We speculate that these coordinated changes help balance local immune surveillance and repair processes, contributing to renal protection independently of, or synergistically with, LPS modulation. The increased serum LPS in the L. reuteri group, despite improved barrier function, may seem paradoxical. However, this can be explained by the altered source and nature of the LPS. The elevated serum LPS likely originates from the enriched Bacteroidota, whose putative anti-inflammatory LPS may be systemically distributed but lacks the potency to drive widespread inflammation. In contrast, the reduced renal LPS reflects decreased translocation and accumulation of pro-inflammatory LPS from pathobionts like Proteobacteria, a process mitigated by the repaired intestinal barrier and the hypothesized competitive inhibition at renal TLR4 sites.

Structural analyses reveal Bacteroides spp. produce penta-acylated monophosphorylated lipid A–contrasting E. coli's hexa-acylated diphosphorylated form37,38. This confers 100–1000-fold lower inflammatory potency than Enterobacteriaceae LPS39,40. Bacteroides vulgatus (phylum Bacteroidota) secretes penta-acylated anti-inflammatory LPS (A-LPS) featuring monophosphorylated lipid A. Relative to Escherichia coli's hexa-acylated pro-inflammatory LPS (P-LPS), A-LPS exhibits markedly attenuated TLR4 activation capacity and can competitively inhibit P-LPS-induced NF-κB activation41. Crystallographic analysis of the TLR4/MD-2 complex bound to E. coli lipid A reveals that acyl chain count and phosphate group quantity/location directly influence receptor binding affinity and dimerization potential42. The E. coli double mutant mg1655, deficient in lipid A’s fifth and sixth acyl chains, synthesizes a tetra-acylated, diphosphorylated molecule termed lipid IVA43. This variant effectively attenuates TLR4 activation by wild-type E. coli lipid A44, a property exploited in the development of TLR4 antagonists for sepsis therapy45. Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron rough-type LPS, containing only monophosphorylated penta-acylated lipid A, competitively inhibits E. coli-derived LPS-induced NF-κB activation by disrupting TLR4/MD-2 binding41, a mechanism that may promote intestinal immune homeostasis. This aligns with our observation that Lactobacillus reuteri elevates serum LPS without inducing renal hyperinflammation, suggesting Bacteroidetes-derived A-LPS exerts “immunologically silent” effects: it prevents TLR4 overactivation..The differential gut microbiota modulation by BD versus L. reuteri, yielding functionally distinct LPS species, likely underlies the observed divergence in renal vs. systemic inflammatory profiles. Unlike the canonical TLR4-driven cytokine storm (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α) in BD kidneys, serum levels of these organ-damaging cytokines showed no significant intergroup differences, supporting two key conclusions: pro-inflammatory LPS (P-LPS) drives target organ injury in the BD state, and tissue-specific accumulation is a prerequisite for P-LPS pathogenicity.

A seemingly paradoxical but crucial finding that reinforces this compartment-specific model is the concurrent improvement in intestinal barrier integrity (Fig. 9) and the elevation of serum LPS levels in L. reuteri-pretreated animals (Fig. 6). This apparent contradiction can be resolved by considering the source, structure, and potential tissue tropism of the translocated LPS. L. reuteri remodels the gut microbiota, significantly enriching Bacteroidota (which produce putatively less inflammatory, penta-acylated A-LPS) while suppressing Proteobacteria (producers of pro-inflammatory, hexa-acylated P-LPS). The repaired intestinal barrier effectively limits the translocation of pathogenic bacteria and their highly inflammatory P-LPS into systemic circulation. Consequently, the elevated serum LPS in the L. reuterigroup likely originates predominantly from the enriched Bacteroidota. This A-LPS, due to its distinct lipid A structure, exhibits low endotoxic potency and may circulate with limited capacity to activate systemic TLR4 signaling, explaining the absence of a corresponding serum cytokine storm. Notably, the systemic impact of translocated LPS is not determined solely by its quantity but critically by the host’s capacity to neutralize it. Studies in humans indicate that higher levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), which facilitates LPS clearance and inactivation, are associated with a lower bioactivity-to-mass ratio of circulating LPS, effectively buffering its inflammatory potential27.Thus, the elevated serum A-LPS in our model may represent a pool that is effectively neutralized or has inherently low bioactivity. In contrast, the kidneys are protected because: i) barrier repair reduces the inflow of pathogenic P-LPS from the gut lumen, thereby decreasing its delivery to the kidney; and ii) any A-LPS that reaches the kidney may act as a partial TLR4 antagonist. Thus, the data collectively indicate that L. reuteri orchestrates a qualitative shift in translocating microbial products—replacing harmful, barrier-disrupting P-LPS with less harmful or even protective A-LPS.

Collectively, L. reuteri conferred renal protection through a multi-pronged strategy that remodeled the gut-kidney axis. First of all, it reshaped the gut microbiota by increasing Bacteroidota and decreasing Proteobacteria, thereby reducing the source of renal P-LPS. The enriched Bacteroidota are hypothesized to produce less inflammatory LPS, which may competitively inhibit TLR4 activation in the kidney. This aligns with the emerging paradigm that gut dysbiosis-associated pathology stems not necessarily from an increase in total bacterial translocation, but from a shift towards the translocation of more pro-inflammatory microbial products27. Second, it restored intestinal barrier integrity, as evidenced by the normalized distribution of ZO-1, improved ultrastructure of intercellular junctions, and reduced serum iFABP levels. This barrier repair is crucial for limiting the translocation of pathogenic components. Third, it modulated the local intestinal immune microenvironment, suggesting the establishment of a gut-kidney immune homeostasis network that may help contain inflammation and promote tissue repair. These coordinated actions—microbial restructuring, barrier fortification, and immune regulation—collectively mitigate the transmission of gut-derived inflammatory insults to the kidney.

While this study delineates a novel gut-kidney axis mechanism in BD-induced injury and identifies L. reuteri as a promising protective agent, several limitations must be acknowledged to contextualize the findings and guide future research. First of all, the central hypothesis—that Bacteroidota-derived, structurally distinct LPS mediates protection via TLR4 competitive inhibition—is supported by strong correlative data but requires direct validation. The absence of biochemical characterization (e.g., mass spectrometry) of lipid A structures from the remodeled microbiota and functional immune assays (e.g., using purified LPS in TLR4 reporter systems) constitutes a key gap. Second, the pretreatment regimen (7-day oral L. reuteri before BD induction) is a preconditioning strategy that limits direct clinical translatability to the unpredictable timeline of deceased donor management. However, this model provides crucial proof-of-concept that targeting the donor gut microbiota is a viable strategy, potentially challenging paradigms that rely solely on broad-spectrum antibiotics. Third, our findings in a rat model may be influenced by species-specific differences in gut microbiota composition and innate immune signaling, necessitating cautious extrapolation to humans. Fourth, the ultimate test of organ quality—transplant outcome—was not assessed. Future studies employing transplant models are essential to confirm that the observed molecular and histological improvements translate into enhanced graft survival and function. Finally, the complexity of the gut-kidney crosstalk implies that other unmeasured factors, such as microbial metabolites beyond LPS, likely contribute to the protective effect.

In conclusion, this study systematically reveals the potential core mechanism by which the imbalance of the "gut-kidney axis" drives acute kidney injury after BD. BD-induced disruption of the intestinal barrier promotes the accumulation of Proteobacteria-derived P-LPS in the kidneys, activating the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling axis in renal tubular epithelial cells and triggering local inflammatory cascades. In contrast, L. reuteri confers protection via a dual strategy: reshaping the gut microbiota to reduce renal accumulation of pro-inflammatory LPS while potentially introducing competitive inhibitory LPS, and reinforcing the intestinal barrier to fortify a gut-kidney immune homeostasis network. Our findings, consistent with observations in human chronic kidney disease, highlight that the inflammatory outcome of gut bacterial translocation is governed more by the qualitative nature of the translocated products and the host’s neutralization capacity than by its quantitative burden27. This injury is initiated primarily by the LPS-MyD88 axis, Our findings highlight the gut microbiota and intestinal barrier as feasible therapeutic targets for improving organ quality in brain-dead donors.
