CXCL13 and IL-33 as Immunomodulatory Adjuvants Combined with Respiratory Syncytial Virus Fusion (RSV F) Protein to Enhance Mucosal RSV Vaccination
Authors: Hadeel Alnajran, Bandar Alosaimi, Maaweya Awadalla, Fahad M Aldakheel, Khalid A Al-Hamad, Ahad Aldhuwayhi, Dalal Alobaid, Joud Almutairi, Zahra Alslaim, Yomna Alkhalifah, Abdulaziz Alshalani, Ayman Mubarak, Wael Alturaiki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a major pathogen responsible for lower respiratory tract infections, particularly in infants, young children, older adults, and immunocompromised individuals. Although several RSV vaccines and monoclonal antibody therapies have recently been approved, their protective efficacy is often short-lived and relies mainly on systemic immunity rather than mucosal immunity. As RSV is a mucosal pathogen, there is a compelling need to develop novel vaccine strategies capable of inducing robust mucosal immune responses. Inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT) is an ectopic lymphoid structure that plays a critical role in orchestrating the immune response to respiratory pathogens. iBALT is an important target for vaccine development, as it can support both innate humoral and cellular immune responses and contributes to the establishment of immune memory in the respiratory tract. The formation of iBALT is regulated by airway epithelial cells and the lung microenvironment through release of cytokines and chemokines, including interleukin 33 (IL-33) and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 13 (CXCL13). In this review, we propose a novel mucosal vaccination strategy against RSV based on the combination of the RSV fusion (F) protein with immunomodulatory molecules, IL-33 and CXCL13, which are involved in the induction and regulation of iBALT. These immunostimulatory molecules may act synergistically to enhance the immune response to RSV infections. IL-33 promotes innate, mucosal, and adaptive immune responses and supports T cell activation, whereas CXCL13 facilitates B cell recruitment and organization within lymphoid structures. Although targeting iBALT represents a promising approach to improving RSV vaccine efficacy, further studies are required to determine the optimal regulation of iBALT in the airways.

Introduction

RSV is an important pathogen that causes respiratory infections, especially in infants and the elderly. Annually, RSV is estimated to cause approximately 33 million episodes of lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI), among which 3.6 million require hospitalization, and approximately 118,200 deaths occur in children under 5 years of age.1 Although efforts to develop vaccines have been successful, currently approved vaccines provide inadequate protection, and their protective efficacy may decline over time. In some cases, the use of live attenuated viruses may increase the risk of respiratory infections.2,3 This highlights the need for innovative approaches to improve vaccine effectiveness and safety against RSV infections. Furthermore, mucosal vaccination is a promising strategy for enhancing RSV vaccine efficacy by overcoming the challenges associated with traditional methods and strengthening immune responses at the mucosal surface, where RSV initially enters and causes infection. iBALT clusters are formed in the airways and play an essential role in supporting airway immune responses against pulmonary infections.2,4 Accordingly, targeting iBALT via mucosal vaccination may improve the lung immune response and provide broad protection against pulmonary infections. Thus, a deep understanding of how airway epithelial cells (AECs) enhance and regulate mucosal immune responses is critical because they serve as the initial line of defense and activate the host immune response through the production of cytokines and chemokines that shape local immunity.5,6 Moreover, AECs produce several cytokines and chemokines that regulate and modulate local inflammation and activate the immune system.7

Additionally, a recent study showed that nasal immunization of rodents with an RSV vaccine enhances protective immune responses by promoting specific IgA responses in the airways, thereby contributing to viral clearance.8 Furthermore, it has been reported that the use of IL-33 as a mucosal vaccine adjuvant enhances immune responses against influenza A virus and increases IgA and IgG levels.9 Moreover, exogenous IL-33 enhances vaccine-mediated protection against influenza viral infections by activating specific CD8+ T cells.10 However, during viral infection, type-I interferon can induce the expression of CXCL13, resulting in the recruitment of B cells and the development of iBALT, which are critical for the activation of local immunity against influenza infection.11 Overall, emerging evidence indicates that cytokines and chemokines derived from airway epithelial cells, including IL-33 and CXCL13, have strong potential as mucosal vaccine adjuvants by stimulating localized immune responses, promoting iBALT formation, and supporting the establishment of long-term immunological memory. This review aimed to explore the connection between iBALT development and effective local immunity to RSV infection, including the potential of targeting iBALT to improve RSV-F vaccine efficacy when conjugated with IL-33 and CXCL13 as immunostimulatory adjuvants, which may enhance vaccine efficacy and induce long-lasting memory cell responses against RSV infection.

The Fundamental Role of the AECs Against RSV Infection

AECs are key players in defense against pulmonary viral infections, specifically RSV. AECs can act as an initial barrier between the respiratory tract and external pathogens, activating the innate immune response following RSV infection.12 RSV initially targets AECs, resulting in the shedding of epithelial cells and airway obstruction.13 The elimination of dead infected epithelial cells can be considered a form of defense mechanism that limits viral spread.14 Additionally, following RSV infection, AECs identify the virus via pattern recognition receptors (PRPs) such as toll-like receptors (TLRs). For example, TLR-3 is expressed by AECs and can detect RSV intermediate double strand RNA (dsRNA).15 Additionally, TLR-4 can identify the RSV component of the RSV envelope, particularly the F protein.16 Activation of TLRs can result in the production of several inflammatory cytokines and chemokines,17 and it has been reported that RSV infection of AECs can lead to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns such as HMGB1, which leads to activation of the inflammatory response.18

Furthermore, the interaction between RSV and AECs induces various immune responses. It has been shown that RSV infection of AECs has resulted in increased expression of thymic stromal lymphopoietin cytokine (TSLP), which in turn can lead to activate myeloid dendritic cells (DCs), thereby playing a role in the immune response.19 In addition, RSV infection of bronchial epithelial cells triggers the production of type-1 interferon, which can further limit viral replication in infected cells, as previously reported.20,21 Additionally, it has been found that inhibition of viral replication can modify NF-κB activation and cytokine expression in AECs.22 In addition, during RSV infection, the interaction between neutrophils and AECs can result in increased epithelial damage and improved viral clearance.23

After RSV infection of AECs, the airways may be susceptible to subsequent bacterial infections, highlighting the complexity of the immune response in the airways.24 Additionally, alterations in the airway barrier caused by RSV, which are mediated by sustained protein Kinase D activation, indicate the importance of maintaining epithelial cell integrity during viral infections.25 Ultimately, the interaction between RSV and host cells is complex, and a better understanding of how epithelial cells overcome the initial infection might contribute to the development of new strategies to combat RSV infection.

RSV-Incomplete Immunity and Reinfection

Natural RSV infection usually results in inadequate immunity, and this incomplete immunity may cause reinfection, which may be attributed to several factors. Myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs) in neonates display inactive stimulation due to immaturity, which can lead to reduced immune responses and incomplete protection against RSV reinfection.26

Furthermore, the decreased activation and proliferation of human lymphocytes when exposed to RSV, compared to influenza virus, indicates that RSV could potentially impede innate and adaptive immune responses, which may lead to incomplete protection against reinfection.27 Moreover, the inability to produce a lifelong immune response against RSV infection in mice, specifically through ineffective nasal antibody responses, emphasizes the important role of local mucosal immunity in avoiding reinfection.

Additionally, it has been shown that serum anti-RSV antibodies only are not sufficient to offer protection against reinfection. This indicates the significance of robust local cellular mucosal immune responses for effective immunity.28 Additionally, RSV infection of epithelial cells can trigger the expression of programmed death ligands, and the influence of cytokines on immune responses can affect the duration and efficiency of immune protection against reinfection.29 An additional factor prompting incomplete immunity to reinfection is the age at the time of RSV infection. Furthermore, it has been established that RSV infection of neonates can cause significant changes in their immune response through consequent infection. This may cause increased airway hyper-responsiveness and severe disease consequences.30

In addition, RSV infection in neonates can increase the expression of IL-4Rα in CD4 + T cells, which can influence the immune response during reinfection. This can affect the balance between Th1 and Th2 responses, and eventually contribute to incomplete immunity.31 Moreover, the genetic diversity among RSV strains indicates that variations in RSV-G protein may play an essential role in reinfection.32 The genetic diversity of RSV can affect the efficacy of immune responses generated during initial infection, possibly leading to a lack of complete protection against future reinfections. Moreover, it has been shown that, RSV infection of neonatal can lead to limited production of type I interferon responses and decrease activity of plasmacytoid dendritic cell during infection, which in turn can allow the immunopathogenesis upon reinfection, that in turn contributes to incomplete immunity.33

Moreover, it has been demonstrated that the expression of IL-2 during RSV infection can restrict the development of T follicular helper cells and delay the development of protective antiviral immunity, which may have implications for long-term immune responses and the ability to avoid reinfection.34 In addition, difficulties in generating neutralizing antibodies and the need for repeated infections to develop short-term immunity in the early life response to RSV underscore the obstacles in establishing strong and long-lasting immunity against reinfection.35 Collectively, incomplete RSV immunity is a complex issue, and a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying this phenomenon may lead to the development of effective strategies for enhancing immunity and preventing RSV reinfections.

Current Approved Treatments Options for RSV

Current approved treatments for RSV infections include antiviral agents and monoclonal antibodies. Ribavirin is an effective broad-spectrum antiviral that inhibits viral modification and transcription through its nucleoside analog action.36 Ribavirin is the only antiviral agent approved for treatment of severe lower respiratory tract infections in hospitalized infants and young children.37 Furthermore, ribavirin is effective in managing RSV infection among high-risk individuals who have recently undergone hematopoietic stem cell transplantation to reduce mortality associated with RSV pneumonia.38 Additionally, ribavirin can be effectively used to treat RSV infections in people with compromised immune systems.39

In contrast, targeted monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) play a significant role in the treatment of RSV infections. Palivizumab is a neutralizing monoclonal antibody that specifically targets the RSV F protein and has received approval for RSV prophylaxis in infants who are at high risk of developing severe RSV infection.40 Moreover, palivizumab has proven effective in preventing the entry and transmission of RSV, establishing it as an essential treatment option.41

Additionally, nirsevimab is a monoclonal antibody approved in 2023 that specifically targets the RSV F protein and plays an important role in preventing severe RSV lower respiratory tract infections in infants and young children susceptible to severe RSV infections.42,43 Additionally, nirsevimab plays an essential role in lowering the incidence of lower respiratory tract infections among hospitalized infants with RSV.44 Although both antibodies have received authorization for the treatment of RSV infection, there are some important differences between them. Nirsevimab has shown greater efficacy than palivizumab, demonstrating approximately 10 times greater potency and efficiently inhibiting viral spread, even at lower concentrations.45 Additionally, nirsevimab can be administered as a single dose owing to its longer half-life, which may improve patient compliance and reduce costs compared with palivizumab, which requires multiple doses.46 Furthermore, nirsevimab has been reported to be more effective than palivizumab in preventing hospitalization and providing extended protection.47,48

Nirsevimab demonstrates high efficacy against RSV across infant risk groups, with a 70.1% relative reduction in medically attended RSV lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) and a 78.4% reduction in RSV-related hospitalization in preterm infants (29–34 weeks) versus placebo over 150 days.49 In healthy late-preterm and term infants, nirsevimab reduced medically attended RSV LRTI by 74.5% and RSV-related hospitalization by 62.1% compared with placebo.50 In high-risk populations, monthly palivizumab reduced RSV hospitalization by 45% in children with hemodynamically significant congenital heart disease.51 Safety profiles are broadly comparable: serious adverse events occurred in 6.8% versus 7.3% (nirsevimab vs placebo) in term/late-preterm infants,50 and in 55.4% versus 63.1% (palivizumab vs placebo) in children with congenital heart disease, with none judged drug related.51 These data support nirsevimab as at least comparable in effectiveness to palivizumab, with a favorable single-dose profile.

In addition, nirsevimab has safety profiles and side effects similar to those of palivizumab, and no deaths have been reported.42 A cohort study showed that nirsevimab is more effective, provides better protection, and is more cost-effective than palivizumab.52 Additionally, nirsevimab has been shown to be more cost-effective than palivizumab in infants with RSV infection relative to palivizumab.53

Despite the ability of current treatments to reduce the pathogenicity of RSV infection, especially in high-risk individuals, developing an effective vaccine to prevent RSV infection remains a higher priority for overcoming disease complications and reducing the burden of RSV. Recently, the FDA approved Clesrovimab in 2025 for the prevention of RSV infections in infants because of its strong neutralizing efficacy and longer half-life, making it suitable for this purpose.54 This novel monoclonal antibody can identify a conserved epitope on the RSV fusion (F) protein, thereby inhibiting viral entry into host cells.55 The drug, with YTE mutations in the Fc region, has been found to have a long half-life in serum, thus allowing single-dose administration during each RSV season.56

In a pivotal phase 2/3 clinical trial involving 3600 infants, administration of a single 105 mg dose reduced lower respiratory tract infections from 6.5% in the placebo group to 2.6% in the drug-treated group, thereby providing nearly 60% efficacy.57 Hospitalizations due to RSV infections also decreased by 84% in the drug-treated group compared with the placebo group (0.4% vs. 2.3%).57 The drug demonstrated an excellent safety profile, with serious adverse event rates comparable to those of the placebo group.57

Furthermore, Clesrovimab offers advantages over conventional prophylactics such as palivizumab, as it can be administered as a single dose, eliminating the requirement for monthly dosing.58 The drug also showed strong clinical utility against all RSV strains.56 However, anti-drug antibodies were detected in approximately 15% of patients by day 150, particularly among those who experienced late-onset RSV infections. Nevertheless, this did not affect drug levels or short-term efficacy.58,59

Recent Approved Vaccinations for RSV

Despite recent advances, the development of effective RSV vaccines continues to face several important challenges. The first RSV vaccine, developed in the 1960s, was a formalin-inactivated vaccine that failed to provide protection against RSV infection and caused an increased incidence of immune-mediated disease when infants were subsequently infected with wild-type RSV.60

This outcome emphasized the difficulty of developing an effective RSV vaccine and highlighted the need to ensure enhanced safety during vaccine development. Nevertheless, recent advancements in RSV vaccination have led to the approval of new vaccines, such as Arexvy and Abrysvo, which play crucial roles in the prevention of RSV infection.61,62

Both vaccines have been approved for use in individuals aged ≥ 60 years to prevent RSV infections. Arexvy, developed by GlaxoSmithKline (GSK), is a highly potent recombinant subunit adjuvanted RSV vaccine that has demonstrated remarkable efficacy against both RSV subtypes A and B in clinical trials. Abrysvo, manufactured by Pfizer, is a bivalent vaccine containing the RSV prefusion F protein from both RSV subtypes. Additionally, both vaccines were developed using stabilized prefusion F proteins and have demonstrated effectiveness in preventing acute lower respiratory tract infections in older adults.61,63,64

The mechanism of action of these vaccines involves targeting the prefusion F protein of RSV to induce an immune response in which specific antibodies are produced to neutralize the virus and inhibit RSV infection upon exposure, thereby reducing the severity of RSV infection in older individuals. Moreover, these vaccines have been shown to reduce the number of clinically attended patients with severe respiratory tract infections caused by RSV.65

Additionally, a recent study showed that Abrysvo can enhance the production of neutralizing antibodies in pregnant women and prevent or reduce the severity of RSV disease in infants during early life.66 Maternal RSV immunization has been suggested as an effective strategy to address the decline in neutralizing RSV antibody levels during late pregnancy, thereby enhancing maternal immunity and providing passive immune protection to newborns.67 In addition, currently approved RSV vaccines are considered safe and effective in pregnant women.68

Despite their clinical benefits, these vaccines still have several limitations. One significant drawback is that they are primarily targeted toward specific age groups, which restricts their universal application and limits their impact on the broader prevention of RSV infections.69 Additionally, current vaccines may not provide complete protection against all RSV strains and may show variable effectiveness among different populations.69 These limitations highlight the need to develop new strategies that are more flexible and broadly applicable to meet the diverse needs of various populations and provide improved protection against RSV infection.

Furthermore, Arexvy and Abrysvo are administered via injection and mainly induce systemic immune responses and RSV-specific IgG production. Growing evidence suggests that mucosal immune responses play an essential role in combating RSV infection.70 Therefore, mucosal vaccines offer the additional advantage of inducing both systemic and mucosal immunity, which is particularly important because RSV primarily enters the host through mucosal surfaces.70

Recent advances in intranasal RSV vaccine development further support the potential of mucosal immunization strategies for inducing local protective immunity in the respiratory tract.71–74 Intranasal RSV vaccine platforms, including nanoemulsion-based formulations, virus-like particles (VLPs), and nanoparticle-based vaccines, have demonstrated the ability to induce robust mucosal IgA responses, tissue-resident memory cells, and enhanced viral clearance in preclinical animal models.71,73,75,76 These findings further emphasize the importance of local mucosal immunity for durable protection against RSV infection and strengthen the rationale for iBALT-targeted mucosal vaccine strategies.72,73,77–79

The selection of appropriate adjuvants has a critical impact on the immunogenicity and effectiveness of mucosal vaccines. For example, the addition of monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL) adjuvant to an RSV virosome vaccine has been shown to improve immunogenicity, induce a Th1 immune response, and reduce lung pathology.80 Administration of an intranasal RSV-F nanoemulsion adjuvant vaccine to mice induces a Administration of an intranasal RSV-F nanoemulsion-adjuvanted vaccine to mice induced strong and rapid mucosal and systemic neutralizing antibody responses against RSV and protected animals against subsequent infection.81 In addition, the use of Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists as adjuvants has been shown to enhance mucosal immune responses against RSV infection.82 A recent study demonstrated that the use of the AS01 adjuvant with a prefusion RSV-F protein vaccine in mice increased vaccine efficacy and induced effective mucosal cellular and humoral immune responses.83 Furthermore, combining soluble RSV-F fusion protein with glycopyranosyl lipid A (GLA) adjuvant in adults enhanced mucosal neutralizing antibody responses (IgA and IgG) in the nasal cavity and serum, as well as memory B cell responses.84 Additionally, certain adjuvants have been shown to induce specific IgA responses and enhance mucosal immunity.85 Therefore, selecting appropriate adjuvants is critical because they strongly influence the quality and characteristics of the immune response.

The mRNA Vaccine Against RSV in Adults and Infants

Recently, mRNA vaccine platforms have been investigated for the prevention of RSV infections in older adults. Among these, Moderna’s mRNA-1345, the brand name (mRESVIA) has shown promising results, with approximately 84% efficacy against RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infections in individuals aged ≥ 60 years.86 However, the duration of protection remains a concern, as it has been recently reported that vaccine-induced immunity declines to approximately 50% within 18 months of immunization.87

Additionally, the mRNA-1345 vaccine has entered the clinical trial phase for immunization of children. This started with phase 1 clinical trials in RSV-seropositive children aged between 12 and 59 months. The mRNA-1345 vaccine showed a good safety profile with increased levels of neutralizing antibodies against RSV-A and RSV-B.88 However, clinical trials of mRNA-based vaccines have shown a number of challenges in immunization of infants.89 According to a regulatory document, there is a high incidence of severe to very severe lower respiratory tract infections in infants aged 5–8 months who received the mRNA-1345 vaccine.89

The FDA has expressed concerns regarding the safety of the mRNA-1365 vaccine in the same group of infants. The safety of mRNA-based vaccines has led to a high incidence of severe to very severe lower respiratory tract infections in infants. This is a major cause of concern.89 The mRNA-based vaccine is yet to be approved for immunization of infants. In this context, preclinical research has shifted towards different strategies to extend the duration of immune responses. One strategy utilizes enveloped virus-like particle (eVLP) as the delivery mechanism for mRNA vaccines. This approach has resulted in greater prolonged neutralizing antibody levels in mouse models compared to standard mRNA-based RSV vaccinations, underscoring its potential for enhanced protection durability.90

The Immunomodulatory Effects of IL-33 Cytokine and CXCL13 Chemokine in Pulmonary Viral Infection

IL-33 is a multifunctional cytokine belonging to the IL-1 family and is primarily recognized for its role as an alarmin in immune responses. It is produced by various cell types, including lung epithelial cells, in response to tissue damage and inflammation. IL-33 mediates its biological effects through binding to its receptor ST2, which subsequently induces the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines.91 Furthermore, IL-33 is associated with Th2 immune responses, activation and differentiation of T helper cells, and enhancement of B-cell responses.92

IL-33 expression is elevated in response to several viral infections, including influenza virus and RSV infections.9,93,94 In addition, IL-33 participates in the regulation of both innate and adaptive immunity. It can enhance the secretion of IFN-γ from T cells and NK cells, thereby promoting Th1 responses while also contributing to Th2-mediated effects.95,96 Consequently, IL-33 may facilitate both protective immune responses and pathological inflammation. For example, IL-33 has been shown to enhance cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell responses, which are important for viral clearance.96,97 Moreover, IL-33 contributes to balancing the inflammatory environment during viral infection and has been shown to influence the expression of other cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α.10,98 Maintaining the balance between these cytokines is critical because excessive Th2 responses may lead to tissue damage and chronic inflammation, whereas robust Th1 responses are essential for effective viral clearance.99–101

Despite its beneficial immunomodulatory properties, dysregulation of IL-33 has been implicated in several pathological conditions, including autoimmune diseases and allergic reactions.102,103 Therefore, owing to its ability to enhance immune responses and regulate inflammation, IL-33 may represent a promising vaccine adjuvant candidate.104,105

Importantly, IL-33 has also been implicated in RSV-associated immunopathology, particularly in neonatal settings. Saravia et al demonstrated that IL-33 contributes to age-dependent RSV disease severity by promoting Th2-skewed inflammation, mucus production, eosinophilia, and airway hyperresponsiveness in neonatal models.106 These findings highlight the potential risk that excessive or dysregulated IL-33 signaling may contribute to vaccine-enhanced respiratory disease (ERD), which remains a major safety concern in RSV vaccine development. Therefore, although IL-33 possesses immunostimulatory properties that may support mucosal immunity and iBALT formation, its application as a vaccine adjuvant requires careful optimization of dose, timing, and administration strategies.107

CXCL13 is an important chemokine involved in B-cell recruitment and organization within lymphoid tissues. CXCL13 binds to its receptor CXCR5, which is primarily expressed on B cells and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, facilitating their migration into germinal centers (GCs) and lymphoid follicles.108,109

The biological significance of the CXCL13–CXCR5 axis in lymphoid tissue organization has been firmly established by both foundational and recent studies.110 CXCR5 is essential for B-cell migration into lymphoid follicles and for proper compartmentalization within secondary lymphoid tissues, whereas CXCL13-mediated signaling is required for the organization and maintenance of follicular architecture and germinal center formation.107 Together, these findings identify the CXCL13–CXCR5 pathway as a central regulator of B-cell localization, germinal center dynamics, and adaptive humoral immunity.111 Moreover, CXCL13 has emerged as a key mediator of ectopic lymphoid tissue formation and inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT) development during chronic inflammation and infection, where it supports local antibody responses and long-term protective immunity in mucosal tissues.112

In addition, CXCL13 expression is regulated by several factors, including lymphotoxin signaling, which is essential for the development and maintenance of peripheral lymph nodes (LNs).113 Furthermore, CXCL13 plays a critical role in orchestrating immune responses by promoting the formation of ectopic lymphoid structures, thereby enhancing B-cell activation and antibody production.114 CXCL13 levels have also been shown to increase during viral infections, contributing to the recruitment of CXCR5+ lymphocytes that are important for efficient antiviral immunity.4,114,115 Moreover, the CXCL13–CXCR5 axis has been demonstrated to play a vital role in modulating immune cell interactions within the lung microenvironment, ultimately influencing the outcome of viral infections.114,116

Development of A Promising Mucosal Vaccine for RSV

RSV remains a major cause of severe lower respiratory tract infections in infants, highlighting the urgent need to develop effective vaccination strategies. In addition, the immaturity of the infant immune system remains a significant barrier to vaccine effectiveness.117 The role of airway epithelial cells (AECs) in regulating mucosal immunity through cytokine and chemokine production has been increasingly recognized.118 AEC-derived GM-CSF, IL-1, and IL-33 stimulate lung dendritic cell maturation, T-cell activation, and antibody production, whereas TSLP and IL-25 promote Th2 immunity, which is important for RSV immune responses.119–121 The potential of AEC-derived cytokines and chemokines, such as TSLP and CXCL13, as adjuvants in vaccine formulations, including RSV and SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, has also been previously proposed.4,17

Among these adjuvants, IL-33 has emerged as a potent immunostimulatory candidate. Previous studies have demonstrated that IL-33 can stimulate T-cell responses and confer immunity against lethal viral challenges when used in DNA vaccine platforms.92,122 The molecular mechanisms underlying IL-33-mediated immune responses involve stimulation of CD8+ T-cell proliferation, enhancement of Th1 immunity, and induction of antibody production, particularly IgG and IgA.9,123,124 Importantly, IL-33 has been shown to enhance antigen-specific immune responses without evidence of increased IL-4 or IgE production in the reported experimental model.125 However, these findings were derived from a non-respiratory tumour immunotherapy setting and do not exclude the possibility of IL-33-associated Th2-skewed inflammation or immunopathology during respiratory viral infections such as RSV.

CXCL13 has also been recognized as a key chemokine involved in the regulation of humoral immunity. Vaccine formulations incorporating CXCL13 have been reported to stimulate T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, germinal center (GC) B cells, and antibody responses.126 Elevated CXCL13 levels have been associated with vaccine responsiveness, and increased CXCL13 expression has been reported following yellow fever vaccination, correlating with plasmablast expansion and the production of high titers of antigen-specific antibodies.127–129 These findings support the potential role of CXCL13 as an immunomodulatory adjuvant associated with protective immunity.

L-33 and CXCL13 may act synergistically to promote the formation of inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT) during viral infection in the respiratory tract. IL-33 regulates the activation and cytokine production of group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), which are further enhanced by thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP).130–133 This cytokine signaling pathway contributes to the regulation of CXCL13 expression and recruitment of B cells, both of which are essential for iBALT formation.79,134,135 Moreover, IL-17 signaling has been shown to play an important role in regulating CXCL13 expression during viral infections.136 Previous studies in non-pulmonary disease models have also suggested that IL-33-mediated NF-κB signaling may influence CXCL13-associated inflammatory pathways.137,138 However, the direct relationship between IL-33-mediated NF-κB signaling and CXCL13 regulation during RSV infection or pulmonary iBALT formation remains to be fully elucidated. Interestingly, IL-33 has also been implicated in germinal center formation and the generation of long-term immune responses in infants.139

Indeed, IL-33 has been demonstrated to induce the differentiation of CD4+ T cells capable of producing CXCL13, suggesting a direct link between IL-33 signaling and CXCL13 expression. During respiratory viral infections, IL-33 released from damaged AECs may promote Th2-associated cytokine production, which has been associated with increased CXCL13 expression.93,106 Collectively, these findings suggest a potential interaction between IL-33-associated immune signaling and CXCL13-mediated lymphoid organization during respiratory viral infection and iBALT formation. IL-33-associated inflammatory pathways may contribute to conditions that support CXCL13 expression and B-cell recruitment, both of which are important for lymphoid tissue formation and local immune responses. Because iBALT-mediated immune responses are localized within the respiratory tract, targeting these pathways may represent a promising mucosal vaccine strategy against RSV.

At sites of chronic infection and inflammation, iBALT forms a specialized microenvironment that promotes B-cell activation and differentiation, which are essential for robust antibody responses against pathogens such as RSV.106 In addition, CXCL13 can recruit B cells to sites of infection, thereby enhancing iBALT formation and promoting B-cell maturation.140 L-33 within the lung microenvironment may further enhance Th2-associated cytokine production and B-cell activation, supporting plasma cell differentiation and the generation of long-lasting immune memory.4,106 Therefore, targeting CXCL13 and IL-33 signaling pathways may enhance vaccine efficacy through improved iBALT development following immunization, resulting in stronger protection against RSV infection, particularly in infants.141

Nevertheless, caution is warranted when considering IL-33 for infant RSV vaccination strategies. Previous studies have demonstrated that IL-33 can mediate enhanced pulmonary immunopathology during neonatal RSV infection, including exaggerated Th2 immune responses and airway inflammation.106 Given the historical concern regarding enhanced respiratory disease (ERD) associated with RSV vaccines, additional preclinical studies are necessary to determine whether IL-33-based adjuvant approaches can induce protective immunity without promoting immunopathological responses.

In contrast, the presence of iBALT has also been associated with pathological conditions. iBALT formation has been reported to exacerbate pulmonary pathology in allergic airway disease models, indicating that although iBALT may enhance immune responses, it may also contribute to inflammation and tissue damage.142 Moreover, iBALT has been implicated in tissue damage during chronic inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis.143 Consequently, although iBALT contributes to protective immunity, its inflammatory properties may also mediate adverse tissue responses. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms regulating iBALT formation and function is essential for developing optimal vaccine strategies that safely exploit this immune structure.

Mucosal vaccine strategies also offer several advantages over systemic vaccines. Mucosal vaccines generally demonstrate favorable safety profiles because they are less likely to induce systemic side effects than conventional vaccines, which is particularly important for high-risk populations such as infants and the elderly.144,145 In addition, mucosal vaccines can induce strong local immune responses, including secretory IgA (sIgA), while minimizing systemic adverse reactions.146,147 This property enhances their potential as effective strategies against RSV and other respiratory infections.81,148,149 Furthermore, intranasal administration of virus-like particles (VLPs) containing RSV F and G proteins has been shown to induce significant levels of neutralizing secretory IgA and effector cellular immune responses at mucosal surfaces in RSV-vaccinated mice, which are critical for protection against RSV infection.145 Similarly, a recent study demonstrated that VLPs containing prefusion-stabilized F protein effectively induced broad immune responses and provided protection against RSV infection in animal models.150

Ultimately, we propose that combining IL-33 and CXCL13 as immunostimulatory adjuvants linked to RSV-F antigens and administered through mucosal routes may induce potent local immune responses against RSV. The immunomodulatory properties of IL-33 and CXCL13 may enhance the initiation and formation of iBALT structures within the airways, thereby strengthening mucosal immunity. IL-33 may promote Th2-associated cytokine production and B-cell activation, supporting plasma cell differentiation and the production of antigen-specific IgA and IgG antibodies that are essential for effective mucosal immunity. In addition, CXCL13 directs the migration of B cells and Tfh cells, thereby promoting lymphoid organization, antibody production, and the establishment of immune memory in the lungs. Together, these mechanisms may cooperatively enhance iBALT formation, strengthen antiviral immune responses, and support CD8+ T-cell activation and proliferation, leading to efficient clearance of infected cells. Collectively, the synergistic interaction between IL-33 and CXCL13 may offer significant advantages for infant vaccination strategies, particularly in promoting mucosal homing and long-lived immune responses (Figure 1). Nevertheless, further studies are required to validate this proposed vaccine strategy and comprehensively evaluate its safety before clinical application. Conclusion and Future Prospective

Currently, approved vaccines and monoclonal antibodies against RSV are administered via injection and provide limited protection, which may decline over time. As RSV primarily enters through the respiratory tract, the development of vaccines capable of inducing strong mucosal immune responses and durable immune memory represents a promising strategy to improve protection against RSV infection. Additionally, iBALT formation in the lungs has been associated with local immune protection, including antibody production and the generation of long-lasting memory responses against pulmonary viral infections. Accordingly, incorporating specific cytokines and chemokines derived from airway epithelial cells (AECs), which contribute to the activation and organization of iBALT in the airways, may have the potential to enhance vaccine efficacy. Accordingly, we propose the investigation of co-administration of RSV-F antigen with immunomodulatory cytokines/chemokines such as CXCL13 and IL-33 via the mucosal route. Based on the previously reported immunomodulatory roles of IL-33 and CXCL13 in respiratory viral infections and iBALT formation, this strategy may contribute to the enhancement of mucosal and systemic immune responses, including antigen-specific antibody production, T-cell activation, and the establishment of long-lasting immune memory. However, no published study has directly evaluated the specific combination of RSV-F, IL-33, and CXCL13, and therefore these proposed effects remain hypothetical and require further experimental validation. Furthermore, although iBALT plays an essential role in local immune responses against respiratory pathogens, its dysregulated activation may also contribute to inflammatory pathology under certain conditions. Therefore, additional in vitro and in vivo studies are required to evaluate the safety, immunogenicity, optimal regulation, and protective efficacy of this proposed mucosal vaccine strategy before clinical application, particularly in infants and high-risk populations. Particular attention should be given to the potential risk of IL-33-mediated Th2-skewed immunopathology and vaccine-enhanced respiratory disease (ERD), which have previously been associated with severe RSV infection in neonatal models.
