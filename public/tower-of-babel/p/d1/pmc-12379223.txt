All polymer ligand strategy enables highly stable spectra-pure green-emitting perovskites
Authors: Akash V Fulari, Sagar E Shirsath, Gaurav M Lohar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStabilizing perovskite nanocrystals (PNCs) has been a hot topic since last decade. To avoid defect formation under ambient conditions it is crucial to protect crystal lattice using enhanced surface passivation. Polymers are one of the most promising materials for protection of PNCs and generally used along with conventional ligands. In this work we demonstrate all polymer ligands-based strategy to achieve highly stable, spectra-pure green-emitting PNCs of CsPbBr3 at room temperature. We employ combination of Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and Polyethylene glycol (PEG) polymers without the use of conventional Oleic acid and Oleylamine ligands. PNCs synthesized with only PVP emit bluish green weak PL intensity. Upon addition of PEG, PNCs exhibit giant PL enhancement and PL spectra shifted towards pure green region. Xray diffraction (XRD) and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) confirms the formation of CsPbBr3 lattice. We also studied the effect of PEG concentration and obtained an optimized sample with peak PL intensity. As an effect of proper passivation, optimized sample delivered 76% of Photoluminescence Quantum Yield (PLQY). It remained stable with up to one year under ambient conditions. It possessed robust thermal and UV stabilities. Sample retained 92.6% PL after 15 cycles of heating and cooling between 27 °C and 85 °C. Under extreme environmental conditions of 80% relative humidity and high intensity UV irradiation, sample retained 96.81% of original PL after 50 h testing. Further, we fabricated a Light Emitting Diode (LED) using optimized sample. The LED delivered outstanding performance with peak luminous efficiency of 104.33 lm.W− 1 (20 mA, 2.54 V) and high spectral stability after 500 h of continuous operation with 94% of PL retention. We believe this all-polymer passivation strategy could be beneficial for stabilization of perovskite materials for the next generation display devices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17017-x.Subject terms: Materials science, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Halide perovskite nanocrystals (PNCs) have emerged as game changer semiconducting materials of the century1. Their versatile properties such as wide spectral tunability, low-cost facile synthesis, photophysical tuning via ligand engineering, near unity photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) made them potential candidates in the next generation optical devices1–9. However, their poor environmental stability has been limiting their practical applicability10. Due to their ionic nature, PNCs possess low defect tolerance11,12. Owing to this, simple environmental factors such as moisture, oxygen, or heat induce the surface defects resulting into PL degradation by hindering radiative recombination and ultimately leading to crystal deformation13–19. PNCs are capped with capping ligands to protect their surfaces from environmental parameters20,21. Conventionally, oleic acid and oleylamine are used for this purpose22. After prolonged storage time, they tend to detach from the crystal surface due to weak interaction leading to crystal defect formation23.

To overcome this, researchers often implement post-synthetic passivation approaches by protecting crystal surfaces with polymers or silanes24,25. Silanes generally improve stability but due to formation of silica shell they tend to lower the luminescence compared to that of naked crystals26. Also, silica shelled particles are challenging to be processed into uniform films. When it comes to polymers wrapping, they provide better transparency and ability to process PNCs into films27. Inert polymers such as polystyrene are one of the promising candidates for superior passivation of PNCs. Due to lack of amino groups, they do not interact with crystal lattice28. In contrast, polymers such as polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) can interact with crystal lattice due to their amino functional group, changing electronic and optical properties of PNCs29–31. They are widely used for device engineering to provide better grain boundaries and filling factors. However, their use as primary ligands to synthesize specifically colloidal PNCs have not been explored to extent.

In this work, we report a simple one step synthesis protocol by using all polymer ligands to obtain highly stable, spectra pure, green emitting PNCs of CsPbBr3 at room temperature. We optimized the ligand concentration for highest luminescence as a function of PEG additive. Our optimized sample exhibited intense PLQY of 76%. It remained stable for up to one year with < 10% of PL degradation. Sample delivered high thermal cycling performance and robust stability against high intensity UV irradiation (350 W/cm2) under humid environment. A LED is fabricated using optimized sample delivered high luminous efficiency 104.33 lm.W− 1 (20 mA, 2.54 V) and exceptional performance after continuous operation for 500 h with negligible PL degradation (94% retention) and robust spectral stability.

Materials and methods

Materials

All chemicals were technical grade and were used without any post purification. DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide, 99%), DMF (N, N-Dimethylformamino, anhydrous, 99.8%), toluene (≥ 99.9%), Ethyl alcohol (> 99%), Cesium bromide (CsBr, 99.9%), lead (II) bromide (PbBr2 > 98%), polyethylene glycol (PEG) and Polyvinylpyrrolidone (K30), UV curing resin CPS 1040 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Precursor preparation

Equimolar quantities of CsBr and PbBr2 were dissolved in 10 ml of DMF/DMSO (50% vol) to form a clear base precursor by vortexing at room temperature for 45 min. 400 mg of PVP was the added into the base precursor and again vortexed until homogenised clear precursor was formed. This solution was named as precursor solution. For PEG added precursor, the specific quantity of PEG was added into the above-mentioned precursor solution at different concentration of 5 mg/ml to 25 mg/ml. These precursor solutions were used for perovskite synthesis.

Synthesis of colloidal polymer capped CsPbBr3 NCs

The NCs were synthesized at room temperature (30 °C). The 0.1 ml of precursor solution was injected into the antisolvent 10 ml of toluene with 5% vol of ethyl alcohol kept in a glass vial vortexing at 3600 RPM. Within 5 s luminescent colloids were observed under UV365 illumination. For PVP only ligand, a weaker bluish green PL was observed. Same procedure was repeated for the PEG ligand additive. With respect to increment in PEG quantity from 5 mg/ml to 25 mg/ml colloids exhibited red shifted PL emission. The sample were named as 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 for PEG quantity of 5 mg/ml, 10 mg/ml, 15 mg/ml, 20 ml/ml, and 25 mg/ml. The sample 4 exhibited highest PL intensity with pure green emission and was taken for further characterisation.

Synthesis of CsPbBr3 NCs using conventional oa/oam ligands

The salts of CsBr and PbBr2 (0.2 mmol each) were dissolved in 5 ml of DMF. O.5 ml of OA and 0.25 ml of OAm were added to form precursor solution. 1 ml of precursor solution is then injected into 10 ml of toluene under vigorous vortexing. A bright green emission was observed under UV illumination within 10 s. Samples were labelled as OA/OAm and characterized further.

LED fabrication

Sample 4 colloidal was centrifuged at 14,000 RPM, precipitates were collected. A UV curing resin CPS 1040 was then added to the sample in ratio of 0.6:1. The mixture was vortexed until homogenised slurry formed and labelled as active material blend. This blend with gel like consistency was then coated on UV450 chip, the thickness of active material was optimized until we got single green emission peak from the LED. It was then cured using UV light and kept for drying at room-temperature for 2 h. LED was then operated at optimal conditions of 20 mA; 2.54 V and performance were tested.

Characterisation

The High-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) analyses were carried out on a JEOL JEM-2200FS TEM. The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis were conducted using a Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray Diffractometer at 40 kV using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra were recorded on Thermo-Scientific Nicolet iS50. PL spectra were recorded using Thor Labs CCS 200 spectrophotometer. The absorption spectra were recorded on Jasco V-770 spectrophotometer. The absolute PLQY measurements were performed using Jasco FP-8500 spectrofluorometer with an integrating sphere. The scanning electron microscope (SEM) analyses were carried out on HITACHI S-3000 N.

This sample is named as sample 0. To obtain the pure green emission we added different concentrations of PEG as an additional ligand into the precursor. The same synthetic procedure is followed. The exact added concentration of PEG is shown in Figure S1. We synthesized samples 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 by varying PEG quantity from 5 mg/ml, 10 mg/ml, 15 mg/ml, 20 mg/ml, and 25 mg/ml. As a function of increased PEG concentration, the samples emitted greener brighter luminescence. The optical properties of samples are shown in Fig. 2.

(a) Digital photographs of samples 1 to 5 prepared with different quantities of PEG, kept under daylight vs. UV 365. Absorption (b) and Photoluminescence spectra (c) of respective samples. A clear red shifted PL peak could be observed with increasing quantity of PEG ligand.

Figure 2a shows the digital photographs of samples 1 to 5 kept under daylight VS illuminated under UV365. It could be visualised that PL has been enhanced and shifted towards pure green region as a function of PEG increment.

We studied the optical properties of the samples using UV-VIS and PL spectroscopy. Absorption spectra showed red shifted peaks corresponding to that of red shifted PL spectra shown in Fig. 2a and b. To be exact, the PL peaks were observed at 504 nm, 505.6 nm, 507 nm, 515 nm, and 515.2 nm for samples 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 respectively. This peak shift could be explained as a function of defect passivation happening due to PEGylation. Owing to its O-H groups, PEG can readily interact with perovskite surface and passivate the defects resulting in suppression of non-radiative recombination and enhancing radiative recombination leading to brighter, pure green, red shifted PL32. Figure S2a shows digital photograph of sample with and without PEG. A clear shift from bluish green to green could be observed to naked eyes. The comparative PL spectra were plotted in Figure S2b. the Upon detailed investigation we observed that along with red shifted PL the intensity was also increased as a function of increment in PEG quantity until it reaches to 20 mg/ml of PEG (sample 4) and degrades a bit with further addition (Figure S3). This could be due to relatively higher polarity of PEG. The excess PEG concentration starting to affect adversely to the PNC lattice. Relative PL intensities are plotted in Figure S3. Sample 4 exhibited highest PL intensity with peak centring at 515 nm, it is then chosen for the further characterisation. XRD analysis of sample 4 (prepared with PVP + PEG) compared to that of sample 0 (prepared with only PVP) are shown in Fig. 3a.

(a) XRD of sample prepared with PVP only (sample 0) vs. that of PVP + PEG (sample 4). PEG additive sample showed all the peaks matching with reference card of CsPbBr3, PVP only sample exhibited additional peaks arising due to cs excess-Cs4PbBr6 phase formation. Confirming PEG added sample possesed purer CsPbBr3 crystal lattice. (b) FTIR spectra of the samples with and without PEG are shown. PVP only sample shown intense C-H assymetric stretch at 1652 cm− 1, whereas PVP + PEG sample shows lowered intensity of this peak and intense C-O-C vibration at 1022 cm− 1 and 956 cm− 1 confirming PEG attachment to crystal lattice.

Samples showed peaks identical to that of reference of cubic CsPbBr3 XRD PDF#54–0752. Detailed analysis revealed that PVP only sample exhibited additional peaks might be arising because of impure Cs4PbBr6 phase formation due to improper crystallization from the capping ligand. Upon addition of PEG, they were observed to be vanished, and all the peaks were exactly matching to the PDF#54–0752 reference card of cubic CsPbBr3 lattice. This could be due to proper passivation and defect free crystal growth of CsPbBr3 as an effect of additional ligand of PEG. Due to higher polarity, PEG might have converted the 0D-Cs4PbBr6 into 3D-cubic CsPbBr3 shown in Scheme 126,33–35.

The illustration of samples synthesized using PVP only and PVP + PEG. The use of PVP-only ligand resulted in Cs excess impurities and defects, forming Cs4PbBr6 phase. Upon addition of polar PEG ligand, the impure phase transformed into purer cubic CsPbBr3 crystal lattice with dual polymer passivation.

The PEG can form Lewis acid-base pair with uncoordinated ions such as Pb2+ hence stabilizing the isolated octahedral frameworks of [PbBr6]4−. Further, PEG can form an organic network that rests on the boundaries between perovskite crystals, effectively cross-linking perovskite particles, and hindering removal of Cs+ ions from perovskite crystal lattice thereby mitigating the degradation and enhancing stability36–40.

Previously, PEG have been used to improve grain boundaries for perovskite device engineering41–43. To confirm the PEGylation, we conducted FTIR analysis of sample 0 and sample 4 (with and without PEG) shown in Fig. 3b. The spectra of sample 0 revealed the intense C = O stretching vibration arising at 1652 cm− 1 due to PVP ligand. Sample with PEG additive showed C-O-C vibrations at 1022 cm− 1 and 956 cm− 1 respectively whereas the 1652 cm− 1 stretch appears less intense compared to that of PVP only sample. FTIR confirmed the PEGylation assisted crystal passivation and existence of PEG. To further understand the physical properties of optimized sample 4, we conducted TEM and HRTEM.

Figure 4a shows the TEM image of sample 4. It revealed formation of cloud like cluster of NCs.

(a) TEM of sample 4 showing cloud like cluster of nanocrystals. (b) HR-TEM of corresponding sample showing crystal lattice fringes with d spacing of 0.29 nm confirming (2 0 0) plane of CsPbBr3. (c) Elemental mapping confirming the presence of Cs, Pb, and Br from the respective sample.

To get detailed insights we conducted HRTEM analysis shown in Fig. 4b. the lattice fringes were clearly seen with the interplanar spacing of 0.29 nm corresponding to the (2 0 0) plane of cubic CsPbBr3. To confirm the elements, we conducted elemental mapping via SEM-EDS of sample 4 (Fig. 4c). The sample revealed similar cloud like clusters as shown in TEM image with existence of elements Cs, Pb, and Br. The results from FTIR, XRD, TEM, HR-TEM, and SEM-EDS together confirmed the existence of CsPbBr3 crystal wrapped with polymeric ligands. PNCs are known for low stability hence their polymeric wrapping is generally employed as a post synthetic approach. Here we have used polymers as main ligands; to know the feasibility of our samples we conducted PL stability testing as shown in Fig. 5.

Figure 5a shows the PL stability of sample 1 to 5 taken for up to one year under ambient conditions. Sample 1, 2, and 3 showed poor stability compared to that of sample 4 and 5. To be exact, Sample 1, 2, and 3 retained 55%, 64%, and 75% respectively. Whereas sample 4 and 5 exhibited PL retention of 92% and 91% respectively. We also compared the PL stability of sample 0 (PVP only)As shown in Fig. 5b, PVP only sample retained 20% of PL intensity just after 180 days this could be due to lack of passivation from absence of PEG. For comparison of our polymer-based method to that of conventional OA/OAm based method we prepared CsPbBr3 using conventional ligands as shown in Figure S4 (please check materials and methods section for the detailed info). The absorption spectra revealed typical peak position, and PL was found to be cantered at 517 nm (Figure S4a). We compared the stabilities of sample OA/OAm with our polymer-based Sample 4. Figure S4b shows the PL stability of OA/OAm sample under ambient conditions. After about 6 days of exposure, it retained only 67.48% of original PL. To assess stability against heat, we conducted thermal cycling test by heating the sample from 27 °C to 85 °C and measured its PL (Fig. 5c). At elevated temperatures we observed PL quenching effect happening due to halogen vacancy traps44,45. The sample showed quenched PL as low as 67% at 85 °C and upon cooling it recovered 97% of PL. Upon 15 consecutive cycles of heating and cooling, sample retained 92.6% of original PL intensity showing excellent thermal performance. Further we conducted UV irradiation testing under harsh humid conditions. At high intensity UV exposure of 350 W.cm− 2 and 80% relative humidity, sample retained 96.81% of original PL. These tests show robust photostability of our polymer capped material. Further we compared photostability’s PVP only sample and OA/OAm sample as shown in Figure S5. Under intense UV irradiation and 80% of relative humidity, the PVP only sample sustained 58% of PL after 10 h of continuous testing (Figure S5a). On the other hand, OA/OAm sample PL was degraded by half within just 5 h of exposure at same conditions retaining only 48.83% of original PL intensity (Figure S5b). These results demonstrate the positive effect of our polymer-based method on the luminescent properties as well as stability of PNCs.

In addition to stability, one of the important parameters for luminescent materials is the PLQY values. Phosphor materials to be used for lighting applications require a high PLQY (> 60%). To check feasibility of our materials to be used for lighting applications, we conducted PLQY analysis of Sample 0 vs. Sample 4 shown in Fig. 6.

(a) Spectra demonstrating PLQY analysis of sample 0 vs. sample 4 under 365 nm excitation. (c) Calculation of PLQY showing PVP only sample exhibiting 35% vs. sample with PEG additive possesing 76% of absolute PLQY.

Sample 0 exhibited higher absorption with relatively lower PL intensity and delivered only 35.01% PLQY (Fig. 6a,b). Sample 4 exhibited vastly lower absorption and relatively higher PL intensity compared to that of sample 0. It exhibited vastly improved PLQY of 76.07% compared to that of sample 0. This indicated that effect of PEGylation helped to improve radiative recombination resulting into higher PLQY. Figure 6c shows the photon counts calculation for the PLQY of sample 0 and sample 4. We also conducted PLQY analysis of conventional OA/OAm sample shown in Figure S6. It delivered only 36.89% of PLQY (Figure S6).

Owing to high environmental stability and high PLQY (> 60%), we believed our material would be ideal for the lighting application. We fabricated a down conversion LED by using sample 4 as an active material. We blended a specific quantity (0.6:1) of CPS 1040 UV curing resin into the antisolvent containing sample 4, vortexed for 30 min and formend a homogenized active blend with a gel like consistency. This blend was then coated on a UV 450 nm chip to obtain a single emission peak from green wavelength. It was then set aside under ambiant conditions to dry out for 2 h. This LED was then illuminated using optimal conditions at 20 mA, 2.54 V and stability tests were conducted. Figure 7a shows the electroluminescence performance of LED prepared using sample 4.

(a) Electroluminescence spectra of LED prepared using sample 4. After prolonged contineous operation for 500 h under optimal conditions (20 mA, 2.54 V) device showed 94% of PL performance retention. (b) CIE 1931 plot of LED spectra showing x, y coordinates as 0.0961, 0.7606. (c) The luminous efficiency of LED device. After 100 h of operation, LED delivered efficency of 104.33 lm.W− 1.

The LED delivered robust photostability of upto 500 h contineous operation with negligible degradation. It retained 94% of original PL intensity with no observable spectral shift. The inset shows operating LED under optimal conditions (20 mA, 2.54 V) with intense green luminescence. The CIE plot of corresponding LED is shown in Fig. 7b. To get detailed insights on spectral shift we analyzed CIE coordinates of LED device for 1 h of operation and 500 h. Figure S7 shows the negligible change in CIE cooedinates of device. For 1 h they were at x:0.0961, y:0.7606 and after 500 h of operation they were found to be at x:0.0955, y: 0.7585. This shows spectral stability of LED device. Figure 7c shows the luminous efficiency of device. Owing to robust photostability of material, LED delivered a high luminous efficiency of 104.33 lm.W− 1 after 100 h of operation.

Conclusion

In conclusion we demonstrated a simple one step strategy to synthesize colloidal PNCs using all polymer ligands without the use of conventional ligands. We employed PEG + PVP to stabilize and passivate the PNCs. As an effect PEGylation, we observed intense, pure green PL spectra. We optimized the ligand concentration and onbained spectra pure, green emitting sample with high PLQY of 76%. The optimized sample remained stable for upto one year under ambiant conditions. It delivered robust stability against heat, moisture and intense UV irradiation. After 15 consecutive cycles of heating and cooling from 27 °C to 85 °C, sample retained 92.6% of original PL. Under high humiditi of 80% and intense UV irradiation of 350 W.cm− 2 sample showed excellent photostability with 96.81% of remnant PL after 50 h of testing. A down conversion LED fabricated using this sample delivered intense green emission with robust photostability. Under operating at optimal conditions (20 mA, 2.54 V) LED exhibited 94% PL retention after contineous operation for 500 h. The CIE 1931 plot revealed negligible shift in x, y point after 500 h, confirming high spectral stability of LED. LED delivered high luminous efficiency of 102.33 lm.W− 1 after 100 h of operation. We believe our all polymer passivation approach is beneficial towards stabilizing perovskites phosphors for implementation in the next generation display technologies.
