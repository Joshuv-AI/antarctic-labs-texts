Monte Carlo calculations of the simulated radiation dose distribution from a dental bitewing X-ray exposure and the usefulness of thyroid protection
Authors: Martin Andersson, Evagelia Maroussi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDental radiology accounts for 26% of all medical radiological examinations worldwide and 0.2% of the overall collective dose. The most common dental exposure is intraoral bitewing radiography, which is a procedure used to examine the interproximal surfaces of the teeth and is particularly useful for the detection of dental caries and the evaluation of alveolar bone levels. In Sweden, regulations require patients to have a 0.25 mm lead-equivalent thyroid shield during intraoral exposures. The aim of this project is to do a Monte Carlo simulation of the absorbed doses from a bitewing exposure and investigate the radiation protection effect of a lead collar. In this study, a Monte Carlo simulation was done of an intraoral bitewing examination, using the latest International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) human adult mesh phantom. A thyroid collar was added to the mesh phantom to generate the exposure situation of a bitewing exposure. A 60 kV bitewing exposure was simulated using the Monte Carlo software MCNP6.3. The radiation source was simulated using RQR60 with 107 simulated X-ray photons. The simulations were performed both with and without thyroid collar. Absorbed doses were calculated for different organs and tissues in the ICRP adult mesh phantom. The results are calculated for a bitewing exposure with a tube voltage of 60 kV and an exposure time of 0.25 s on a phosphor plate receptor and 0.05 s for the digital sensors. The thyroid protection collar examined in this study did not significantly impact the absorbed dose to the thyroid. However, the absorbed dose to several other organs and tissues was reduced. The absorbed dose reduction was dependent on the distance to the X-ray primary beam, where a greater distance resulted in a greater reduction. The difference in effective dose between use of lead apron and without lead apron is, however, neglectable.

Introduction

Dental radiology represents an important component of modern oral healthcare, enabling accurate diagnosis, treatment planning, and monitoring of dental and periodontal diseases. Globally, dental radiological examinations account for an estimated 26% of all medical imaging procedures, although they contribute only about 0.2% of the collective population effective dose from medical exposures [1]. Among the various modalities, intraoral bitewing radiography remains the most frequently employed technique. This method is particularly valuable for detecting interproximal dental caries and assessing alveolar bone levels, making it indispensable for routine preventive and diagnostic care [2].

Despite the relatively low radiation dose associated with intraoral dental imaging compared with other medical procedures, concerns remain regarding the potential stochastic effects of ionizing radiation, particularly given the high frequency and widespread use of such examinations. The principle of radiation protection for patients, as defined by the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP), emphasizes justification and optimization, with the overarching aim of minimizing unnecessary exposure [3]. In dental radiology, optimization of patient protection is commonly achieved through collimation, filtration, receptor sensitivity improvements, and the use of protective shielding [4].

The thyroid gland is of particular concern in dental radiology because of its anatomical proximity to the maxillofacial region and its radiosensitivity, especially in younger patients. Exposure of the thyroid to radiation, even at low levels, has been associated with an increased lifetime risk of thyroid malignancies [5]. Consequently, many national guidelines recommend or mandate the use of protective thyroid collars or shields during intraoral radiographic procedures. For example, Swedish regulations specify the use of a 0.25 mm lead-equivalent thyroid protection collar during intraoral exposures, reflecting a precautionary approach to radiation protection [6].

However, the effectiveness of thyroid shielding in intraoral bitewing radiography remains a subject of debate. A recent European consensus statement on patient contact shielding recommended that, for dental intraoral radiography, a lead thyroid collar may be used [7]. In the USA, the American Dental Association updated its X-ray shielding guidelines in February of 2024, stating that using a lead apron or thyroid shield during X-rays is no longer necessary [8]. In the UK, Public Health England [9] has stated that the thyroid collar should only be considered in cases where it is unavoidable that the main X-ray beam will be directed at the thyroid gland or very close to it, and in these cases, the Medical physics expert should be consulted regarding the use of a suitable thyroid shield.

While some studies have demonstrated meaningful reductions in absorbed thyroid dose with lead collars [10], others report limited benefit, particularly in examinations where the beam path does not directly include the thyroid [11]. Moreover, the application of thyroid protection may introduce potential drawbacks, such as interference with image quality or positioning difficulties, raising questions about its consistent clinical utility [12].

Monte Carlo simulation has emerged as a powerful tool for evaluating radiation dose distributions in medical imaging. By modelling radiation transport and interaction in anatomically realistic computational phantoms, Monte Carlo methods provide accurate estimates of organ doses and effective doses under various exposure scenarios [13]. Recent advances, including the development of the ICRP adult mesh phantoms, have enabled highly detailed dose assessments in dental and maxillofacial imaging contexts [14]. Applying these techniques to bitewing radiography allows for a robust evaluation of thyroid shielding effectiveness, helping to clarify whether mandated protective measures translate into meaningful dose reductions.

In this context, the present study aims to simulate absorbed doses from a dental bitewing exposure using the Monte Carlo code MCNP6.3, incorporating the ICRP adult mesh phantom with and without a 0.25 mm lead-equivalent thyroid collar. By quantifying organ-specific and effective doses under realistic imaging conditions, the study seeks to assess the protective value of thyroid shielding in intraoral radiography. The results are expected to contribute to the evidence base informing clinical practice and radiation protection policies in dental imaging.

Method

This study employed Monte Carlo radiation transport simulations to model patient dose distribution during a standard 1 premolar intraoral bitewing radiographic examination. The objective was to estimate organ- and tissue-specific absorbed doses in an adult reference individual, with and without the application of a thyroid protection collar, under clinically relevant exposure conditions. All simulations were performed using the MCNP6.3 Monte Carlo software package, which allows for high-precision modelling of particle interactions with matter.

Computational phantom

To represent human anatomy, the latest ICRP adult mesh-type reference computational phantom [14] was utilized. The mesh phantom provides a high-fidelity anatomical model of the adult male and female, segmented into reference organs and tissues according to ICRP standards. These phantoms are based on non-uniform rational B-splines and polygon-mesh surfaces, enabling precise representation of organ geometries and tissue boundaries [14].

For the purposes of this study, the adult male mesh phantom was selected as the reference subject. Organ and tissue definitions, as well as mass and density assignments, followed ICRP recommendations to ensure compatibility with effective dose calculations [3].

Thyroid shield modelling

A lead-equivalent thyroid protection collar, with a nominal thickness of 0.25 mm, was incorporated into the phantom geometry, see Fig. 1. The shield was positioned circumferentially around the anterior neck region, consistent with clinical placement during intraoral radiography. The shielding material was modelled as pure lead, scaled to 0.25 mm thickness, providing attenuation characteristics consistent with regulatory specifications [6]. Simulations were conducted under two conditions: (i) without a thyroid collar and (ii) with a thyroid collar in place. This enabled comparative assessment of absorbed dose reduction attributable to thyroid protection.

A lead collar was added to the ICRP adult mesh-type reference computational phantom.

X-ray source and exposure parameters

The radiographic source was modelled according to the RQR60 reference radiation quality, defined by the International Electrotechnical Commission and ICRP for dental radiology. This spectrum corresponds to a tube potential of 60 kV with additional filtration to mimic clinical beam quality [15]. A rectangular beam collimation representative of bitewing imaging was implemented, directed at the posterior maxillary–mandibular interproximal region. The source-to-skin distance was 30.5 cm and the rectangular beam was set to 3.5 × 4.5 mm2 with an angle of +7 degrees.

Exposure parameters were chosen to represent typical clinical practice with digital phosphor plate receptors. A tube voltage of 60 kV and an exposure time of 0.25 s for the phosphor plate receptor and 0.05 s for the digital sensors were simulated, corresponding to common European guidelines for adult bitewing examinations [16].

Monte Carlo simulation setup

Photon transport was modelled using MCNP6.3 with detailed physics options enabled, including coherent and incoherent scattering, photoelectric absorption with fluorescence emission, and electron transport for secondary dose contributions. The source definition employed 107 photon histories per simulation to ensure statistically robust dose estimates, balancing accuracy with computational efficiency. To determine the number of photons in the exposure, one additional Monte Carlo simulation was performed together with a real measurement using an ThinX detector. The ratio between the absorbed dose from the ThinX detector and the Monte Carlo simulation generated the dose between the real exposure and the simulated per photon simulation.

Dose scoring and organ definitions

Absorbed doses were tallied for organs and tissues explicitly defined in the ICRP mesh phantom. These included radiosensitive organs within and outside the primary beam: thyroid, salivary glands, bone marrow (mandible and cervical spine), brain, oesophagus, and remainder tissues. Energy deposition was scored using MCNP *F6 tallies (MeV per g), subsequently converted to absorbed dose in milligrays.

For each organ, mean absorbed dose was calculated by dividing the total deposited energy by the organ mass. The effective dose was determined according to ICRP Publication 103, applying tissue weighting factors to account for stochastic risk [3].

Quality assurance and validation

To validate the simulation model, the generated RQR60 spectrum was benchmarked against published reference spectra, ensuring correct beam quality representation [17].

Results and discussion

The Monte Carlo simulations provided detailed estimates of organ-specific absorbed doses during a standard intraoral bitewing radiographic examination, with and without a 0.25 mm lead-equivalent thyroid collar. Table 1 summarizes the absorbed doses per exposure to selected organs in the ICRP adult mesh phantom under both conditions. The results demonstrated that the thyroid collar did not significantly reduce the absorbed dose to the thyroid itself, with values of ~3.0 μGy without shielding and 2.9 μGy with shielding. The relative error for the thyroid simulations was 0.056 μGy and for detriment-weighted dose 0.046 μSv.

Although the thyroid was only marginally affected, several other organs and tissues exhibited measurable reductions in absorbed dose when the collar was applied. For example, absorbed doses to the lung, stomach wall, liver, and heart showed decreases ranging from 30% to 70%, depending on organ position relative to the X-ray beam. These reductions can be explained by scatter attenuation from the lead collar, which blocked a portion of secondary radiation. The degree of reduction correlated with organ distance.

Despite these localized reductions, the overall impact on effective dose was negligible. The detriment-weighted effective dose decreased only slightly, from ~2.7 μSv without shielding to 2.6 μSv with shielding when using a phosphor plate. For digital sensors, the effective dose decreased from 0.54 μSv to 0.52 μSv. Absorbed doses of zero mean that there is an unmeasurably low statistic in the simulations, meaning that the X-ray beam did not deposit any energy in those structures. In such cases, the absorbed dose to organs with a reported value of zero should be considered negligible within the uncertainty of the simulation.

The absorbed dose was calculated for different organs and tissues in the ICRP adult mesh phantom. The results are calculated for a bitewing exposure of 60 kV and 0.25 s on a phosphor plate and 0.05 s for the digital sensors. The absorbed doses are given in Table 1. The thyroid protection collar examined in this study did not significantly impact the absorbed dose to the thyroid. However, bitewing is just one of several dental projection; for other projections, the lead collar could provide better thyroid protection.

Conclusion

Thyroid shielding had minimal effect for bitewing geometry but may be more relevant for other dental projections. However, the absorbed dose to several other organs and tissues was reduced. The absorbed dose reduction was dependent on the distance to the X-ray primary beam, where a greater distance resulted in a greater reduction. The difference in effective dose between use of lead collar and without lead collar is, however, negligible.
