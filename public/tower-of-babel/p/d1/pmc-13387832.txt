Successful Trifarotene Treatment of a Typical Kyrle's Disease With Overview of Topical Therapies
Authors: Wiktor Kruczek, Aleksandra Frątczak, Karina Polak, Paulina Rutecka, Dawid Wolak, Mirosław Śnietura, Bartosz Miziołek, Beata Bergler‐Czop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTKyrle's disease is a rare skin disorder involving transepidermal keratin elimination, usually linked to systemic illness. We report a unique case—a healthy 26‐year‐old man resistant to standard therapy. Remission was achieved with daily topical trifarotene 0.005% over eight weeks. This is the first reported KD case successfully treated with trifarotene, a selective RAR‐γ retinoid targeting keratinocyte differentiation and inflammation. Its efficacy and safety suggest promise for patients unsuitable for systemic therapy. A summary table of all topical treatment methods for Kyrle's disease, included in the article, provides valuable guidance for managing patients with significant comorbidities.Keywords: Kyrle's disease, topical administration, trifarotene

Case Description

Kyrle's disease (KD), also referred to as hyperkeratosis follicularis et parafollicularis in cutem penetrans, is a rare perforating dermatosis marked by transepidermal elimination of keratotic material. Clinically, it presents as pruritic, hyperkeratotic papules with central plugs, typically on the extensor surfaces of the lower limbs. KD is frequently associated with systemic conditions such as chronic renal failure, particularly in patients undergoing hemodialysis, as well as poorly controlled diabetes mellitus or hepatic dysfunction. It is classified within the spectrum of acquired perforating dermatoses (APD), alongside entities such as perforating folliculitis and elastosis perforans serpiginosa. Accurate diagnosis is essential, as KD may indicate underlying metabolic or systemic disturbances requiring multidisciplinary management. Though its exact pathogenesis remains unclear, KD is believed to result from abnormal responses to epidermal trauma and faulty keratinization, leading to impaired epithelial repair and elimination of necrotic dermal debris through the epidermis [1]. Histologically, it features epidermal invaginations filled with keratin and basophilic debris, accompanied by dermal inflammation and transepidermal elimination [2]. The condition can be exacerbated by scratching or trauma, consistent with the Koebner phenomenon.

We report a rare case of KD in a 26‐year‐old man with no systemic comorbidities—an atypical presentation diverging from the classical phenotype. The patient had a six‐month history of pruritic, violaceous macules on the right thigh and wrist. Laboratory and serological investigations were unremarkable; previous medical history was irrelevant. Treatments, including topical corticosteroids and antifungals, were ineffective. In line with the dermoscopic spectrum of acquired perforating dermatosis described by Papadopoulou et al. [3], lesions showed partial expression of the characteristic concentric pattern, namely a central brown‐to‐gray structureless area with subtle whitish foci and mild peripheral pigmentation, while the white halo and peripheral vascular erythematous zone were not prominently developed. This likely reflects lesion‐stage variation rather than discordance with the diagnosis. Skin biopsy revealed keratotic plug under atrophic epidermis with an accompanying foreign body granulomatous reaction composed of dermal granulocytic, histiocytic and lymphocytic infiltrates. Conducted CD15 and CD68 immunohistochemical stainings were consistent with KD. Figure 1 illustrates the clinical presentation and high‐resolution dermoscopic features. Remarkably, complete remission without residual scarring or post‐inflammatory hyperpigmentation was achieved using topical trifarotene 0.005%, applied once daily for eight weeks. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of KD successfully treated with trifarotene, a fourth‐generation RAR‐γ‐selective topical retinoid.

Trifarotene offers a targeted mechanism of action aligned with KD pathophysiology. It modulates keratinocyte differentiation through selective RAR‐γ activation, reducing hyperkeratosis and inflammation while restoring epidermal homeostasis. Retinoids regulate gene expression involved in keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation, normalize keratinization, reduce inflammatory mediators such as IL‐31—key contributors to KD pathogenesis [4]. Trifarotene's high selectivity for RAR‐γ, the dominant isoform in the epidermis, enhances local efficacy while minimizing systemic absorption and irritation seen with broader‐acting retinoids, particularly those targeting RAR‐β [4]. Moreover, limited RAR‐γ expression in placental tissue suggests lower teratogenic risk compared to older retinoids [5]. Trifarotene also affects newer therapeutic pathways, such as proteolysis, skin hydration, and cellular adhesion. Its combined antiproliferative and anti‐inflammatory properties render it especially suitable for follicular‐based perforating dermatoses like KD. Unlike tretinoin, which in Poland is only available in fixed‐dose antibiotic combinations, trifarotene is accessible as a monotherapy, increasing its clinical utility. Importantly, for patients with renal or metabolic comorbidities who may not tolerate systemic agents, topical trifarotene provides a safer, localized alternative. Its follicular penetration may further enhance efficacy in disorders involving follicular keratin extrusion. Although our patient had no systemic comorbidities, this does not preclude a potential role for trifarotene in patients with systemic disease‐associated KD. In APD, especially in cases linked to diabetes mellitus or chronic kidney disease, optimization of the underlying systemic condition and control of pruritus remain central therapeutic principles. However, because trifarotene acts locally by normalizing keratinocyte differentiation and reducing cutaneous inflammation, its clinical benefit would be expected to target the epidermal hyperkeratotic/perforating component regardless of whether a systemic trigger is present. Therefore, in patients with diabetes, renal failure, or other comorbidities, topical trifarotene should be regarded not as a substitute for systemic disease control, but as a potentially useful adjunctive topical option, particularly when systemic retinoids or other systemic therapies are contraindicated or poorly tolerated. Further reports are needed to determine whether response rates in comorbid patients are comparable to those observed in isolated cases such as ours.
