Enhancing polyreactivity prediction of preclinical antibodies through fine-tuned protein language models
Authors: Yuwei Zhou, Haoxiang Tang, Changchun Wu, Zixuan Zhang, Jinyi Wei, Rong Gong, Samarappuli Mudiyanselage Savini Gunarathne, Changcheng Xiang, Jian Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have garnered significant attention for their efficacy in treating a variety of diseases. However, some candidate antibodies exhibit non-specific binding to off-target proteins or other biomolecules, leading to high polyreactivity, which can compromise therapeutic efficacy and cause other complications, thereby reducing the approval rate of antibody drug candidates. Therefore, predicting the polyreactivity risk of therapeutic mAbs at an early stage of development is crucial. In this study, we fine-tuned six pre-trained protein language models (PLMs) to predict the polyreactivity of antibody sequences. The most effective model, named PolyXpert, demonstrated a sensitivity (SN) of 90.10%, specificity (SP) of 90.08%, accuracy (ACC) of 90.10%, F1-score of 0.9301, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of 0.7654, and an area under curve (AUC) of 0.9672 on the external independent test dataset. These results suggest its potential as a valuable in-silico tool for assessing antibody polyreactivity and for selecting superior therapeutic mAb candidates for clinical development. Furthermore, we demonstrated that fine-tuned language model classifiers exhibit enhanced prediction robustness compared with classifiers trained on pre-trained model embeddings. PolyXpert can be easily available at https://github.com/zzyywww/PolyXpert.

Introduction

Antibodies, which are proteins originally synthesized by B cells, can bind and neutralize diverse antigens, thereby serving a crucial role in the body's defense and protection mechanisms [1]. In particular, the swift advancement of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) in recent years has positioned them as pivotal elements within the clinical development pipelines of the global biopharmaceutical sector. These mAbs have been instrumental in the treatment of a broad spectrum of diseases, ranging from oncological conditions to infectious diseases [2]. Monoclonal antibodies are anticipated to continue playing an essential role in enhancing human health in the foreseeable future [3]. In the pharmaceutical market, mAb therapeutics continue to hold a commanding position as the best-selling drugs worldwide. The global market for antibody drugs reached $267.7 billion USD in 2024, exemplified by the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitor pembrolizumab generating $29 billion USD in global sales. This market dominance is fueled by intense development activity, with approximately 1,400 antibody therapeutics undergoing clinical trials and nearly 200 already approval [4]. However, the path to commercial success faces significant hurdles: mAb development is exceptionally costly, often exceeding one billion dollars [5], and approximately 70% of antibodies entering clinical trials fail to reach the market [6]. Selecting several candidates with favorable biophysical and biochemical properties is the foundational step [[7], [8], [9]]. While the broad application of technologies such as antibody phage display, single B-cell screening and next-generation sequencing have significantly increased the number of preclinical therapeutic antibody candidates, this abundance makes selecting the most promising antibody a critical challenge. Given that the financial repercussions escalate rapidly as a candidate progresses, early assessment of antibody candidates' developability is crucial to reduce the risk of costly late-stage failures in antibody drug development.

The variable regions of antibodies mediate antigen-specific binding, but they can also mediate non-specific interactions with a variety of non-cognate antigens. Studies have shown that the failure of antibodies may be due to this “polyreactivity” characteristic. Due to the presence of a large number of positively charged or hydrophobic amino acids in the variable regions of polyreactive antibodies, this amino acid distribution is closely related to pharmacokinetics [10]. Antibodies with extensive positive charges tend to rapidly accumulate in the liver, leading to significantly increased peripheral and central clearance rates (e.g., through phagocytosis in the liver or spleen) [11], which in turn affects antibody distribution and bioavailability. In terms of immunogenicity, polyreactivity may increase the risk of antibodies being recognized as foreign by the patient's immune system, thereby triggering the formation of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs). ADAs may neutralize the therapeutic efficacy of the antibody or cause adverse reactions such as hypersensitivity [12,13]. Additionally, polyreactive antibodies may pose risks in the development of therapeutic antibodies due to their ability to bind multiple antigens. For example, camrelizumab is an anti-programmed cell PD-1 antibody that has been shown to induce capillary hemangiomas in the majority of patients treated with it. This effect is primarily attributed to its interactions not only with its target PD-1 but also with human vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2), frizzled class receptor 5 (FZD5), and UL16 binding protein 2 (ULBP2) [[14], [15], [16]]. These interactions may trigger immune responses through unintended off-target binding, thereby increasing immunogenicity.

Polyreactivity can be defined as the ability of antibodies to bind with a diverse array of self and foreign antigen molecules, encompassing soluble membrane proteins, cytosolic proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. Assessments of antibody polyreactivity are commonly conducted using a range of experimental techniques, including enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), baculovirus particle enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (BVP), and flow cytometric assays with poly-specificity reagents (PSR). However, these experimental approaches tend to be time-consuming, labor-intensive, and costly. To streamline the process, there is a need for more efficient methods that can accurately assess polyreactivity without the associated high costs and extensive labor. The polyreactivity of antibodies is closely associated with the isoelectric point (PI) of their variable region domains. Mutation studies have demonstrated that reducing the positive charge content in the variable region (V region) can decrease non-specific binding and thereby enhance specific efficacy [11,17].

The PI of the V region is determined by the primary sequence of the antibody's amino acids, which makes it possible to predict the polyreactivity of antibodies from their sequences. In silico approaches, particularly machine learning (ML) methods, offer a quicker and more cost-effective solution for assessing antibody developability [[18], [19], [20]]. Several tools for predicting antibody polyreactivity have been established. Boughter et al. [21] analyzed the physical properties of 1000 polyreactive and non-polyreactive antibody sequences, including hydrophobicity, charge, side-chain size and spin-spin coupling constants et al. Using these defining features, a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) model was built for the classification and achieved the accuracy (ACC) of 76.4%. Harvey et al. [22] trained multiple antibody polyreactivity prediction models using logistic regression, convolutional neural network and recurrent neural network using one-hot and k-mer encodings of amino acids. The one-hot based logistic regression model achieved the highest area under curve (AUC) of 0.85. Chen et al. [23] proposed a random forest model, the ACC of which reached 86.5 when applied to an independent test dataset. The best model in Lim et al.'s study [24] relied on biochemical features based on 3D structure [24]. This approach not only depended on the precision of the structure modeling algorithm but also increased the computational cost. Extensive research has shown that deep protein language models (PLMs) can create useful representations that can greatly improve the accuracy of predicting antibody properties, including polyreactivity [24,25]. This approach not only depended on the precision of the structure modeling algorithm but also increased the computational cost [24]. Recently, the results of Yu et al.’s [25] study further approved the superior performance of the embeddings generated by PLMs, which outperformed a model built using molecular descriptors based on AlphaFold 2 predicted structures in some cases. In summary, the use of deep learning and language models for predicting antibody polyreactivity has demonstrated promising potential. However, the overall AUC of current PLMs-based models approximate 0.85, indicating that there is still room for improvement. Besides employing embeddings derived from language models as feature descriptors, another effective strategy is to fine-tune pre-trained language models [26]. To date, however, there is no such attempt specifically targeting the prediction of polyreactivity.

In this study, we fine-tuned six PLMs from antiBERTy [27], AntiBERTa2 [28], IgBert [29], ESM-2 [30], ProtBert [31] and ProtT5 [31] to act as the end-to-end polyreactivity predictor for preclinical therapeutic mAbs. Next, we trained convolutional neural network (CNN) models based on the embeddings of last hidden states of PLMs. We also demonstrated that the fine-tuned models outperformed than CNN models trained on embeddings. Although the CNN model exhibited slightly lower performance, it did enhance predictive capabilities compared with existing models. The optimal model, named as PolyXpert is freely available at https://github.com/zzyywww/PolyXpert.

Methods

Dataset construction

Polyreactivity dataset

The polyreactivity dataset of single-chain fragment variables was derived from the study of Chen et al. [23]. Two previously reported human single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) libraries (library #1, library #2) were displayed in yeast and sequenced. Next, the yeasts were fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) sorted according to four different multi-specific reagents, namely, ovalbumin, soluble cytoplasmic proteins (SCPs; from CHO cells), soluble membrane proteins (SMPs; from CHO cells) and insulin. Finally, 246,293 unique sequences in library #1 were identified, containing the 115,038 high-polyreactive scFvs and 131,255 low-polyreactive scFvs. In addition, 127,217 sequences in library #2 were identified, containing the 93,080 high-polyreactive scFvs and 34,137 low-polyreactive scFvs. For the supervised classification task, we split library #1 sequence into a 60% training set, a 20% validation set, and a 20 % test set, because library #1 was cloned from 58 samples, which are much more diverse. Therapeutic antibodies

The therapeutic antibodies were extracted from a study by Jain et al. [32], which included 48 sequences from approved antibodies, and 89 sequences from antibodies in clinical phase II/III trials. For each antibody, the corresponding data from 12 biophysical and biochemical assays were also extracted. Subsequently, six antibodies with conflicting sequence records were excluded, resulting in a final collection of 131 antibody sequences for our dataset. We employed poly-specificity reagents (PSR) soluble membrane proteins (SMP) scores from this dataset to ascertain polyreactivity [33]. The sequences were categorized based on a threshold of 0.27, which represents the 10th percentile of the worst-case values [32].

AbNumber is a convenient Python API for antibody numbering and alignment based on ANARCI [34]. We utilized the AbNumber package to assign numbers to each sequence, allowing us to identify the corresponding germline genes and align the complementarity determining regions (CDRs). The sequence logos were generated using WebLogo [35].

Protein language model and fine-tuning

Pre-trained language models have been proven to be highly effective across a wide range of natural language processing tasks. Among the different pre-trained models utilized in natural language processing (NLP), the transformer model excels at capturing context and meaning by analyzing the relationships between elements in sequential data, such as words in a sentence. We fine-tuned several models on whole-sequence classification task, including antiBERTy [27], AntiBERTa2 [28], IgBert [29], ESM-2 [30], ProtBert [31] and ProtT5 [31]. The structures and the size of parameters are listed in Table 2. Considering the relevance to our research field, we selected both general PLMs and antibody-specific language models. For the ESM-2 models, several checkpoints are available with varying sizes. We chose the medium-sized esm2_t30_150M_UR50D model due to computational resource limitations. The “Trainer” class in the transformer library provides a comprehensive API for training in PyTorch. We employed this framework to implement supervised binary classification, with all parameters made trainable during fine-tuning. For model adaptation, we initialized a task-specific architecture using AutoModelForSequenceClassification class. Training leverages a batch size of 16 per device with 2-step gradient accumulation, executed over 10 epochs. The models were trained with an initial learning rate of 2×10−5 using the default linear decay scheduler. Weight decay (wd) values were uniformly sampled from [0.0, 0.1] during hyperparameter optimization. Through epoch-wise validation loss monitoring, we selected the checkpoint with minimal validation loss as the optimal model. Convolutional neural network construction

The 1D convolutional architecture is particularly well-suited for processing sequence data, such as time series or text. In this study, we constructed a 1D-CNN to effectively process such data. The architecture begins with two convolutional layers, with the first layer receiving input defined by embeddings from pre-trained language models. The input channels are 1,024 for ProtT5, ProtBert, AntiBERTa2 and IgBert, 640 for ESM-2, and 512 for antiBERTy, respectively. Each convolutional layer employs a kernel size of 3, followed by a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function. To further condense the output, we introduced a global average pooling layer, which aggregates the feature maps into a single vector. This is followed by a fully connected layer that maps the pooled features to 64 neurons. To mitigate the risk of overfitting, we applied a dropout layer (P = 0.5). The final layer outputs two neurons, corresponding to the binary classification task. The optimization of the network weights of the final model was carried out using the Adam optimizer. After each epoch, the “scheduler” was called to update the learning rate, thereby enhancing the training effectiveness.

Traditional model establishment

The sequence-based representation was obtained by averaging pooling operations over the embeddings of each amino acid and was used to train traditional machine learning models. Various classifiers using Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Gaussian Bayes (GB), KNeighbors (KNN), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Logistic Regression (LR) were constructed. We implemented five-fold cross validation to obtain the best hyperparameters with “GridSearchCV” function in the Scikit-learn package.

Performance evaluation

During the model training process, we selected the cross-entropy loss function to compute the loss on the validation set, which we used to identify our optimal model. For a binary classification problem, the cross-entropy loss can be defined as:Loss=−1N∑i=1N[yilog⁡(pi)+(1−yi)log⁡(1−pi))]Where N is the number of samples. yi is the true label for sample i (0 or 1). pi is the predicted probability that the label is 1 for sample i.

To assess the performance of the predictive models, we employed six widely used metrics: SN, SP, ACC, F1-score, MCC and AUC.

SN, also known as the true positive rate or recall, evaluates the model's capacity to correctly identify positive cases among all actual positive instances. SP measures the model's ability to accurately identify negative cases among all actual negative instances. ACC quantifies the overall correctness of the model's predictions. MCC is a performance metric that considers the balance between true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN). The F1-score is a comprehensive metric that effectively reflects the performance of a model even in cases of class imbalance. These metrics are defined as follows:SN(Recall)=TPTP+FNSP=TNTN+FPACC=TN+TPTP+FN+TN+FPF1=2×Precision×RecallPrecision+Recall(Precision=TPTP+FP)MCC=TN×TP−FP×FN(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)

Specifically, TP represents the count of high polyreactivity antibodies that are correctly predicted, while TN corresponds to the number of accurately predicted low polyreactive antibodies. Meanwhile, FP and FN are the quantity of wrong predictions of high-polyreactive antibodies and low-polyreactive antibodies, respectively.

We also plotted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculated the AUC to showcase the performance of each model. AUC values range from 0 to 1, where 1 signifies a perfect classifier and 0.5 indicates a random or ineffective classifier. AUC is independent of the threshold used in prediction, making it more comparable among different models. The bootstrap resampling method with 1,000 iterations was employed to estimate 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for all evaluation metrics.

Fine-tuning PLMs for antibody polyreactivity prediction

To investigate the impact of supervised fine-tuning on predicting the polyreactivity of antibodies, we fine-tuned the weights of six pre-trained PLMs: antiBERTy, AntiBERTa2, IgBert, ESM-2, ProtBert, and ProtT5 for the classification of binary polyreactivity status. During fine-tuning a model, the training loss, validation loss, and validation accuracy evolve with each epoch. We monitor these metrics to understand the model's performance over time. Our strategy involves saving the model checkpoint from the epoch where the validation loss is minimized. This approach ensures that we retain the model state corresponding to the point of optimal performance on the validation set. The training processes of the six models are shown in Fig. 2. The performance of the antiBERTy, AntiBERTa2, IgBert and ESM-2 models exhibited significantly lower losses compared with ProtBert and ProtT5. Notably, validation loss of ProtBert models exceeded 0.2, resulting in subpar performance on the test set with all metrics falling below those of the other three models. Conversely, the fine-tuned ESM-2 model demonstrated superior SN, SP, ACC, MCC, F1-score, and AUC values across the training dataset and both test datasets, achieving 0.9010, 0.9008, 0.9010, 0.9301, 0.7654, and 0.9672, respectively, on the library #2 external independent test dataset. Convolutional neural network models with NLP-based embeddings

The antiBERTy, and ESM-2 generated L × 512 and L × 640 representations for each sequence, respectively. AntiBERTa2, IgBert, ProtBert, and ProtT5 generated L × 1024 representations for each sequence where L is the sequence length. Base on these features, we performed CNN models using the same parameters settings. Compared PolyXpert with traditional machine learning models

As demonstrated in our comparative analysis, the PolyXpert outperforms the ESM-2-CNN model. Next, we explored the performance of traditional machine learning trained on sequence-level ESM-2 features to predict antibody polyreactivity. SVM, RF, GB, KNN, XGBoost and LR were used to train models and their performances on two test datasets were subsequently evaluated. All traditional models performed well in library #1 test dataset, with SVM, KNN, and logistic regression models all achieving metrics exceeding 0.9 (Fig. 4). However, in library #2 test dataset, the performance of these models sharply declined, indicating poor generalization when applied to unknown data. All scores are reported in Fig. 4. In contrast, the PolyXpert continued to excel in library #2, maintaining a balance between high accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, demonstrating its robustness in predicting antibody polyreactivity. We observed that LR achieved comparable overall performance to PolyXpert, though with ∼5% lower sensitivity. More importantly, the key strength of language models lies in their inherent interpretability through attention mechanisms. In contrast to traditional ML models, which rely on sequence-level features and lack positional resolution, PLMs enable the visualization of attention mechanisms to trace model decision-making processes, thereby pinpointing critical amino acid residues influencing polyreactivity. This capability provides crucial guidance for subsequent antibody engineering. Enhancing the explainability of polyreactivity prediction model through attention mechanisms

The key advantage of the transformer model lies in its capability to visualize internal attention weights, thereby identifying input tokens that significantly influence model predictions. This attention mechanism operates through iterative matrix multiplications on attention weights across transformer layers. Following fine-tuning, attention scores tend to show a preference for specific input tokens. By inputting the amino acid sequence into the model, it becomes feasible to pinpoint the tokens that hold the most significance in the prediction process. We randomly selected 100 of high and low polyreactive sequences with equal lengths from the dataset, respectively, and calculated the average intra-chain attention along the antibody heavy and light sequences for the final layer of the PolyXpert. We observed an increase in average attention towards the CDRL3 and CDRH3 regions. Surprisingly, certain amino acids with notably high attention scores, such as H23, H43, H104, H118, L23, L41, and L104, are not located within CDR regions (Fig. 6). The attention scores at these sites all exceeded the average scores of both the CDR and framework region (FR), with the majority even surpassing the highest scores observed in the CDR (Table 5). Instead, they are situated at the periphery of the CDR regions. We believe that these pivotal amino acids also impact the antibody's polyreactivity. Discussion

Computational techniques have been increasingly popular and useful in drug discovery and development [37]. As an indispensable part of pharmaceutical industry, the discovery and development of therapeutic antibodies is no exception [20,38]. The primary objective of antibody developability research is to identify lead antibodies that adhere to druggability criteria [39]. Notably, polyreactivity plays a pivotal role in antibody developability: antibodies demonstrating high polyreactivity can lead to misinterpreted outcomes, reduced reproducibility of routine experiments, and waste of resources in terms of time and money [40].

Structural modeling and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations can analyze the rigidity and flexibility of antibodies, which are characteristics associated with antibody polyreactivity. These techniques can theoretically be used to assess the binding flexibility and degree of polyreactivity of antibodies. However, there is currently no standardized computational method for calculating antibody polyreactivity using molecular simulations. Traditional studies suggest that antibody polyreactivity is positively correlated with the flexibility of the CDR3 region [41]. In contrast, another published study using MD simulations proposed a different conclusion: polyreactive antibody fragments (Fabs) exhibited higher rigidity in simulations. This rigidity may facilitate the formation of a neutral platform that allows binding to multiple antigens, offering a novel explanation for the molecular mechanism of polyreactivity [42]. Discrepancies in research findings may arise from differences in experimental conditions and antibody types, as well as variations in the metrics and models used across studies. For example, the relative importance of sequence features (e.g., CDR3 length, proportion of charged amino acids) versus structural dynamic properties (e.g., rigidity, and flexibility) remains controversial [43]. Additionally, the high computational cost of MD simulations limits their application to large-scale datasets, making this approach currently unsuitable for calculating polyreactivity for a large number of antibody.

While existing studies have revealed factors influencing antibody polyreactivity [21], the relative importance of many features is still a topic of debate [44], and a practical tool for predicting the polyreactivity of mAbs is lacking. Presently, antibody development research extensively employs data mining and machine learning techniques, demonstrating that the sequence characteristics of the antibody variable region play a pivotal role in determining the physicochemical properties of therapeutic antibodies [45]. Numerous polyreactivity prediction tools, which are built on physicochemical properties or structural features, have been developed [[21], [22], [23]], while the utilization of PLMs in this filed has been progressively investigated [24,25]. However, the existing tools failed to provide friendly-used models or servers. In this work, we employed three approaches to develop a predictive model for antibody polyreactivity: fine-tuning pre-trained language models, training a convolutional neural network based on amino acid-level embeddings, and utilizing traditional machine learning models based on sequence-level features. Both the fine-tuned model and the CNN model proved to be effective in this study. The PolyXpert achieved the best predictive performance, with sensitivity and specificity exceeding 0.90 and an AUC of 0.9672.

Given the demonstrated effectiveness of both pre-trained PLMs and CNNs in predicting polyreactivity, a natural question arises: would directly combining these architectures yield superior results? To investigate this, we trained a composite model integrating ESM-2 with a CNN. On an external independent test set, this hybrid model achieved an AUC of 0.9676, which is only 0.0004 higher than the fine-tuned ESM-2. While hybrid architectures may prove advantageous for tasks requiring multi-scale feature integration, our results indicate that polyreactivity classification can be sufficiently addressed through fine-tuning PLMs alone. The increased complexity of the composite model introduced higher computational costs without commensurate performance gains, likely because ESM-2's pre-trained attention mechanisms already capture sequence patterns critical for polyreactivity prediction, rendering the CNN component redundant. It must be emphasized that PolyXpert is not a replacement for experimental methods but serves as a supplementary tool to prioritize candidates, thereby reducing workload. Additionally, our training data comprised scFvs screened against four polyreactivity reagent, which may introduce biases. Given the diversity of antibody types and antigens reagent, achieving consistent predictive results across all antibodies remains challenging. The availability of diverse polyreactivity antibody datasets may expand the clinical applicability of models in the future.

We attempted to compare this model with other published models but encountered several limitations. For instance, Boughter et al. [21] purported to offer a mature graphical interface tool that distinguishes “polyreactivity” or “non-polyreactivity” in antibody sequences through linear discriminant analysis. However, in practical application, we found that this tool could only analyze CDR sequences and was prone to automatic shutdowns. When we input example data, we obtained a scatter plot without providing an intuitive prediction result of polyreactivity. Interestingly, Harvey et al. [22] trained a machine learning models to accurately assess polyreactivity from nanobody sequence and presented an online service. However, the diversity in antibody and antigens types or mixtures used may yield different features associated with polyreactivity [46], making comparisons with our tool challenging. Furthermore, there are other tools that either rely on structural features, do not furnish post-training models, or provide training code without accompanying training data, significantly diminishing their usability and comparability [[23], [24], [25]]. Here, we offer a command-line version of the sequence-based model exclusively, accompanied by detailed instructions, to streamline researchers' utilization. Several databases such as AACDB [47], SAbDab [48] and Thera-SAbDab [49] indeed provide a robust data foundation for structure-based methods. However, extracting structural features from these databases still requires substantial computational resources and time. Predictors leveraging structural features (e.g., Aggregation propensities and solvent-accessible surface area or hydrodynamic properties) exhibit computational scalability limitations analogous to MD simulations. Compared to structure-based model, PolyXpert's sequence-only framework cannot fully capture conformational dynamics or spatial epitope arrangements critical for certain polyreactivity mechanisms. This limitation is particularly relevant for antibodies where polyreactivity arises from spatial interactions rather than linear sequence motifs. While structure-based tools can resolve such cases, their reliance on experimental or predicted structures introduces practical barriers: computational antibody modeling remains resource-intensive for large-scale screening. We emphasize that PolyXpert prioritizes accessibility and scalability for early-stage discovery, where sequence data is abundant but structural insights are scarce. This sequence-based model provides a higher-throughput and faster approach.

Previous studies have emphasized the influence of hydrophobicity and charge on the polyreactivity of antibody sequences, finding amino acid frequency differences between polyreactive and non-polyreactive antibodies based on small-scale data [21]. We analyzed the most intuitive differences between the two classes of sequences using a larger-scale dataset. Overall, the CDRs of polyreactive sequence have a higher content of positive charge amino acids, which is consistent with previous conclusions. In addition, site-specific aspartic acid substitutions are common in low-polyreactive sequences, perhaps helping to guide sequence design. We conducted an interpretability analysis on the attention mechanisms of the model to explore the critical amino acid positions that the model prioritizes during the decision-making process. We noted an escalation in attention weights on the CDRH3 and CDRL3 regions compared to others. Prior research has also underscored the importance of CDR regions in antibody polyreactivity [[23], [50]]. However, there has been limited focus on the amino acids within the FR region. Surprisingly, we observed attention weights increasing at specific positions in non-CDR regions, surpassing those in the CDRs, such as H23, H43, L23, and L41. These positions are located along the peripheries of the CDRs, indicating their probable involvement in the conformational binding of polyreactive antibodies. Solely concentrating on the impact of CDR regions may result in the omission of valuable information.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed sequence-based fine-tuned ESM-2 model, named PolyXpert, for predicting polyreactivity of therapeutic mAbs candidates. The sensitivity of proposed model was 0.9010, the specificity was 0.9008, and the AUC was up to 0.9672. We have also developed a user-friendly command line tool, which are freely available at https://github.com/zzyywww/PolyXpert. This tool provides a high-throughput and efficient assessment tool for antibody drug developability from the polyreactivity perspective. To bridge computational innovation with practical implementation, PolyXpert offers two critical utilities in antibody development workflows: (1) early-stage screening of large candidate pools generated by next-generation sequencing to exclude high-polyreactivity sequences, significantly accelerating experimental screening. The filtered candidates can then be fed into downstream analytical workflows, such as structure modeling platforms including Schrödinger or Biovia Discovery Studio, for structural prediction, antigen–antibody interaction analysis, and developability assessment; and (2) late-stage engineering guidance, where its interpretable attention scores highlight specific residues contributing to polyreactivity (e.g., charged/hydrophobic patches in CDR regions), enabling targeted mutagenesis for risk mitigation while preserving antigen-binding potency. For instance, when a highly polyreactive antibody is input into PolyXpert, the model provides residue-specific attention weights that pinpoint critical sites. Researchers can then perform single- or multi-site saturation mutagenesis at these high-weight identified as non-contacting positions, followed by iterative PolyXpert screening to systematically explore the mutational space. This enables discovery of low-polyreactive variants while maintaining antigen affinity. By addressing both candidate attrition and sequence optimization, PolyXpert enhances the efficiency and success rate of therapeutic antibody development.
