Dynamic inflammatory trajectories and 28-day mortality in patients in the intensive care unit: An exploratory observational study
Authors: Xinyuan Ding, Shangzhong Chen, Lihong Zhu, Chengcheng Zheng, Hang Mou, Guolong Cai, Yanfei Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Early inflammatory trajectories strongly predict 28-day mortality in ICU patients.•Sustained high inflammation (Traj. 3) is linked to the highest mortality risk.•Multi-indicator trajectory predicts immune stability and 28-day mortality risk.•Vasopressors and steroids benefit high-inflammation states. Steroids worsen outcomes in those who rapidly develop high inflammatory states.•Traj. 3 marks early high-risk; Day 9 is a risk node, needing early intervention.

Introduction

Dysregulated inflammatory responses, characterized by a mixed state of hyperinflammation and immunosuppression, are frequently observed in critically ill patients[1,2] and are closely associated with poor outcomes and increased mortality rates. This uncontrolled immune dysregulation complicates the clinical management of severe illnesses, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the inflammatory dynamics in critical care settings.[3]

Although several studies have explored the role of inflammatory mediators (IFMMs) in patient prognosis, they have mostly focused on isolated variables or static values.[[4], [5], [6]] However, the condition of critically ill patients is highly dynamic, and static values of IFMMs may not sufficiently reflect this dynamic progression. For example, the rate of C-reactive protein (CRP) increase was more predictive of respiratory deterioration than the baseline CRP values in patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19).[7] This observation aligns with broader research demonstrating that the elevation kinetics of IFMMs serve as robust prognostic indicators.[8,9] Moreover, the intricate relationships among IFMMs highlight the importance of analyzing the correlation between the inflammatory status and prognosis based on their interrelationships and dynamic changes over time. In this study, CRP, procalcitonin (PCT), interleukin (IL)-2, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10 were selected as longitudinal inflammatory markers based on their comprehensive coverage of inflammatory pathways to align with clinical practice and to facilitate a more comprehensive exploration of dynamic changes in immune function.[10]

Group-based multiple-trajectory modeling (GBMTM)[11] offers a promising approach to address these challenges by considering both longitudinal changes and individual variability. This method enables the analysis of biomarker trajectories over time by integrating data from multiple mediators at different time points.

This study aimed to investigate the inflammatory dynamics in critical illness using GBMTM to analyze the trajectories of CRP, PCT, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10 during the early stages of intensive care unit (ICU) admission. This approach aims to improve patient outcomes by enabling more precise and personalized treatment strategies.

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective study included patients admitted to the ICU of our hospital between July 4, 2018, and June 24, 2024. The Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Hospital approved this study, and the committee waived the requirement for informed consent (Approval No.: ZJHIRB-2025-038 K). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age <18 years; (2) ICU length of stay <5 days; (3) absence of inflammatory data; (4) pregnancy; (5) presence of a malignant tumor; or (6) history of a malignant tumor.

Data collection

Comprehensive demographic and clinical data were collected within the first 24 h of ICU admission, including: (1) baseline characteristics (age, sex, and history of smoking and drinking); (2) disease severity scores (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II [APACHE II], Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA], and Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS]); (3) primary ICU admission diagnosis; and (4) relevant comorbidities. The laboratory parameters included complete blood count, IFMMs (CRP and PCT), comprehensive metabolic panels (liver and kidney function, electrolytes, and lipid profiles), coagulation mediators, IL levels, and lactate levels. The primary study endpoint was all-cause mortality within 28 days after ICU admission, defined as death occurring during index hospitalization within 28 days of ICU admission.

GBMTM of IFMMs

CRP, PCT, and multiple ILs (IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10) were measured daily during the first five ICU days to characterize the dynamic inflammatory profiles. The mean value was used for patients who underwent multiple measurements in a single day. All IFMMs were natural log-transformed (Y=ln(mediator + 1)] to enable direct comparison across different measurement scales and to facilitate trajectory modeling.

GBMTM[11] was performed using the TRAJ package of Stata 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA) to identify distinct IFMM patterns and analyze CRP, PCT, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10 measurements during the first five ICU days, which characterized the temporal evolution of IFMM levels within distinct trajectory groups. Inflammatory markers were naturally log-transformed to normalize distributions and enable cross-marker comparisons. Patients were assigned to trajectory groups based on maximum posterior probability of membership, with model selection guided by: (1) statistical fit criteria (maximized log-likelihood, entropy ≥0.7, minimized AIC/BIC values); (2) clinical validity requirements (average posterior probability ≥70% per group, minimum group size ≥5% of sample, and clinically interpretable patterns); and (3) the ability of the model to capture heterogeneous biomarker dynamics while maintaining clinical relevance for critically ill populations.

Subgroup analysis and efficacy analysis

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of trajectory–mortality associations across key clinical subgroups (aged >70 years or not), sepsis status, smoking history, respiratory diseases, hypertension, diabetes, and SOFA score >8 or not) while simultaneously assessing treatment efficacy by examining differential responses to vasopressors and corticosteroids across the identified inflammatory trajectories.

Data management

To ensure data quality, a rigorous cleaning process was implemented for all manageable variables: (1) outliers, defined as values exceeding 1.5 × interquartile range (IQR) beyond the upper or lower quartiles, were treated as missing; (2) variables with ≥30% missing value were excluded; (3) variables with ≤10% missing value were imputed using mean substitution; and (4) variables with >10% but <30% missing value underwent Random Forest imputation to preserve data integrity while minimizing bias. This systematic approach maintained its robustness while addressing incomplete and extreme values.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as the mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or median (IQR) for nonnormally distributed variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate, based on data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. The 28-day hospital cumulative survival probability was examined using the Kaplan–Meier method. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were also constructed to explore the association between the IFMM trajectories and 28-day hospital mortality. Three models were constructed: model 1, with age, hypertension, diabetes, and sepsis included as confounders; model 2, containing the confounders of model 1 as well as initial platelet count, creatinine level, lactate level, and oxygen partial pressure; and model 3, which included the confounders of model 2 as well as APACHE II scores, vasopressors, corticosteroids, and ulinastatin use in the first 5 days.

An inverse probability weighting (IPW) model was used to achieve stable results. The IPW analysis incorporated all imbalanced variables identified in the trajectory assignment model, including demographic factors (age), laboratory parameters (hemoglobin, potassium, lymphocyte count, low-density lipoprotein, creatine kinase, creatinine, and D-dimer), clinical APACHE II, comorbidities (sepsis, hypertension, and diabetes), and treatment exposures (vasopressors, corticosteroids, and ulinastatin) during the first five ICU days. The final IPW model estimated the average treatment effect between different IFMM trajectories, with stabilized weights applied to minimize variance. All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.3.1 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Statistical significance was determined using a two-sided P <0.05.

Results

Traj. 1 had a persistent mild inflammatory response (18.6% [n=95]), Traj. 2 had a moderate inflammatory response followed by a subsequent decline (55.6% [n=284]), and Traj. 3 sustained high inflammatory response (25.8% [n=132]). The evaluation of model adequacy is described in Supplementary Table S3. The entropy value was 0.885 (expected >0.7 for adequacy). IFMM trajectories and 28-day mortality

Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed significantly different 28-day survival rates across inflammatory trajectories (log-rank P <0.0001, Peto–Prentice P <0.0001; Figure 2). However, the log-rank test (P=0.430) indicated no significant difference between Traj. 1 and Traj. 2, which is evident from the overlapping confidence intervals (Cis; Figure 2). Time-dependent Cox models were employed to verify whether day 9 constituted a mortality risk node (in accordance with the Kaplan–Meier curve crossover; Table 3). These models demonstrated that, in the dummy-variable model, Traj. 3 exhibited a significantly higher pre-day 9 mortality rate than Traj. 1 (hazard ratio [HR]=4.65, 95% CI: 1.60 to 13.50, P=0.005). No cross-day 9 risk change was observed (interaction P=0.572), and Traj. 2 did not demonstrate a significant difference in comparison with Traj. 1 (P >0.05). Discussion

This study provides novel insights into the dynamic evolution of inflammatory responses in critically ill patients and their association with clinical outcomes. GBMTM was used to identify three distinct inflammatory trajectories characterized by varying patterns of CRP, PCT, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10 levels over the first 5 days of ICU admission. A three-trajectory model was used to investigate the dynamic correlations among clinical indicators, capture the heterogeneity in patient data more accurately, and better align with the theoretical assumptions of distinct clinical states during disease progression. Although the two-trajectory model demonstrated satisfactory performance in statistical metrics, the three-trajectory approach was ultimately selected after a comprehensive evaluation based on enhanced clinical relevance and the need for greater research granularity. This refined grouping strategy improves the adaptability of the model to individual patient variations and data fluctuations while providing more nuanced insights into disease dynamics. The key findings demonstrate that (1) early inflammatory trajectories are strongly predictive of 28-day hospital mortality, with sustained hyperinflammation (Traj. 3) conferring the highest risk, and (2) the therapeutic efficacy of vasopressors varies significantly across inflammatory trajectory groups, suggesting a potential interaction between inflammatory dynamics and treatment response.

These three inflammatory trajectories demonstrate distinct temporal patterns with significant clinical implications. Traj. 1 was characterized by stable, mild levels of most IFMMs (CRP, PCT, IL-2, IL-6, and IL-10) and exhibited the most favorable outcomes (9.5% mortality). IL-4 levels showed a moderate increase during the observation period, potentially reflecting IL-4-induced mild inflammatory phase tissue repair processes[12] while the concurrent decline in other mediators suggests effective inflammatory regulation. This balanced response and the transient fluctuations in IL-10 and IL-6 levels, which indicate the possible presence of progressively lower IL-10, reduced the inhibition of IL-6 to repair tissue function. This response likely enables the necessary host defense while preventing excessive tissue damage[[13], [14], [15], [16]] resulting in the lowest mortality risk among all trajectories. Controlled IL-6 elevation without progression to cytokine storm levels may represent an optimal immune activation state that clears threats while maintaining organ function.

In contrast, Traj. 2 demonstrated a controlled yet dynamic immune response, characterized by an early peak in CRP levels followed by gradual resolution, alongside progressive declines in IL-6, PCT, and anti-IFMM (IL-10) levels. This self-limiting pattern, associated with intermediate mortality (12.3%), suggests balanced immune activation–sufficient for pathogen clearance (via CRP Fcγ-mediated phagocytosis[17]) while avoiding prolonged hyperinflammation. IL-10 exerts its anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the nuclear factor-κB signaling pathway and reducing the production of pro-inflammatory factors[15] and its downward trend may indicate gradual resolution of the inflammatory response. Notably, the mortality risk of this phenotype was significantly lower than that of Traj. 3 (28.0%), and had a non-significant difference compared to Traj. 1, highlighting the prognostic value of early inflammatory resolution.[18] These results are consistent with those of studies linking timely immune de-escalation to improved outcomes.[19]

Notably, Traj. 3 demonstrated the highest mortality risk, characterized by persistently elevated CRP, PCT, and IL-6 levels, alongside dysregulated IL-10 dynamics. This pattern suggests a maladaptive immune response marked by simultaneous hyperinflammation and impaired resolution, which is a hallmark of immunoparalysis in critical illnesses. CRP and PCT levels in this group initially increased and then declined, but remained at relatively high levels on day 5, similar to the trends of IL-6 and IL-10 levels. Sustained high CRP and PCT levels indicate the persistence of the inflammatory response, while changes in IL-6 and IL-10 levels reflect an imbalance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses. An imbalance between IL-6 and IL-10 can lead to uncontrolled inflammation, resulting in tissue damage and organ dysfunction.[16] These findings corroborate prior evidence that early, uncontrolled inflammation predicts poor outcomes in sepsis and acute respiratory distress syndrome[20,21] reinforcing the prognostic value of longitudinal inflammatory monitoring.

Notably, the survival curves of Traj. 1 and Traj. 2 intersected on day 9 in the Kaplan–Meier curves. Traj. 3 patients had a significantly higher mortality risk before day 9, whereas no significant difference was observed between Traj. 1 and Traj. 2. Therefore, day 9 served as a risk amplification node for inflammatory trajectory grades. This finding indicates that Traj. 3 can be used as a marker for early high-risk in critically ill patients, and clinicians may accordingly intensify interventions for such patients before day 9. No differentiated management is required for Traj. 1 and Traj. 2 patients.

Furthermore, the efficacy analysis demonstrated a significant interaction effect between the use of vasopressors and the inflammatory trajectory groups, with therapeutic benefits confined exclusively to Traj. 3. This selective efficacy suggests that vasopressors may exert their effects by counteracting cytokine-mediated vascular hyporeactivity in states of severe inflammation.[22,23] Conversely, the absence of a benefit in Traj. 1 and Traj. 2 implies that the vascular tone is maintained normally in patients with mild inflammatory states.

In the corticosteroid-treated group, a favorable response was noted in Traj. 2 and Traj. 3. This finding pertains to the ongoing discourse on the “optimal timing for corticosteroid administration.”[[24], [25], [26]] The findings of this study support the use of corticosteroids in patients exhibiting sustained high inflammatory states but not in those who rapidly develop high inflammatory states.

This study has several limitations. First, the observational design precluded causal inferences, although rigorous methods were used, including IPW, to strengthen the associations. Second, as this was a single-center study, the results may reflect local practice patterns; therefore, multicenter validation is required to confirm the generalizability. Third, although the cohort size (n=511) provided adequate power for the primary analyses, certain subgroup analyses (particularly in Traj. 3 patients receiving vasopressors, n=132) may have been underpowered. In addition, the lack of granular treatment data (e.g., vasopressor dosing[27] schedules and corticosteroid regimens) limited the assessment of dose–response relationships. Finally, the P values did not exhibit evident statistical significance, and further verification of the conclusions requires support from large-scale randomized controlled trials.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that early inflammatory trajectories derived from multibiomarker longitudinal profiling robustly predicted the 28-day mortality in critically ill patients. Sustained hyperinflammation identified a high-risk subgroup with differential responses to vasopressors, offering a framework for precise immunomodulation in critical care. Integrating dynamic inflammatory phenotyping into clinical decision-making may improve risk stratification and therapeutic targeting, ultimately enhancing the outcomes in this heterogeneous population. The results of this study also demonstrate the significance of inflammatory phenotyping in the development of treatment regimens for vasoactive drugs and corticosteroids in patients receiving intensive care. However, although clinically meaningful trajectory groups were identified, future studies should incorporate functional immune assays (e.g., human leukocyte antigen-DR expression) to better characterize these inflammatory phenotypes.
