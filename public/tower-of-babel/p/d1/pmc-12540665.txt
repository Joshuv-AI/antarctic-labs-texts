Obesity trends and early-life risk factors in preschool children in Northwest China from 2012 to 2024
Authors: Xue Yang, Juan Zhang, Baozhu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Childhood obesity is a growing public health concern globally and in China, with long-term health and economic consequences. Evidence suggests that early life, particularly the first 1000 days, is a critical window for obesity prevention. This study aimed to assess the prevalence, temporal trends, and associated risk factors of obesity among children under three years of age in Shaanxi province from 2012 to 2024. Data were obtained from the national children’s nutrition and health surveillance system (NCNHSS), a population-based registry covering urban and rural sites in Shaanxi. A total of 8201 children aged 0–3 years were included. Anthropometric and sociodemographic data were collected through standardized physical examinations and caregiver interviews. Temporal trends were analyzed using Joinpoint regression. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify risk factors for obesity at 36 months. The overall obesity prevalence at 36 months was 1.91%, with a fluctuating trend between 2012 and 2024. A significant decline was observed in rural areas, followed by a temporary increase in 2021, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic, and a subsequent reduction. In contrast, urban children exhibited a modest upward trend. Most children classified as obese at 36 months had already become overweight or obese by 18 months. Low socioeconomic status was strongly associated with increased obesity risk in both rural (AOR = 2.15; 95% CI 1.54–2.99) and urban settings (AOR = 1.83; 95% CI 1.29–2.60). In rural areas, children whose mothers worked in agriculture had significantly higher odds of obesity (AOR = 2.30; 95% CI 1.23–4.30), as did those born small for gestational age (AOR = 1.63; 95% CI 1.02–2.60). Among urban children, prolonged breastfeeding without appropriate complementary feeding was associated with increased obesity risk (AOR = 1.41; 95% CI 1.01–1.99). Although obesity prevalence among children in Shaanxi remains relatively low, early onset and socioeconomic disparities persist. Targeted interventions during the first 1000 days, particularly for low-income and high-risk families, are essential. Policies should focus on nutrition education, support for appropriate infant feeding, and early growth monitoring to prevent obesity in early childhood and reduce long-term health risks.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization, an estimated 35 million children under the age of five were affected by overweight or obesity worldwide in 20241—an increase of nearly 2 million since 20002. Strikingly, nearly half of these cases occurred in Asia1. In China, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children aged 0–6 years has risen sharply over the past two decades, increasing from 6.5% in 2002 to 10.4% in 20153,4. Forecasts suggest that by 2030, approximately 30% of preschool-aged children in China could be classified as overweight or obese5.

Childhood obesity not only increases the risk of cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome during adolescence and adulthood, but is also associated with poor mental health outcomes, increased healthcare expenditures, and premature mortality6–8. Importantly, the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis highlights the first 1,000 days of life as a critical window of biological plasticity9. Recent research suggests that specific periods during infancy marked by rapid and sustained weight gain may serve as critical inflection points for the development of obesity10. Preventive interventions implemented within this timeframe have the potential to shape lifelong health trajectories and reduce the risk of obesity.

A growing body of literature has identified various determinants of childhood obesity, including perinatal conditions, socioeconomic status, infant feeding practices, caregiver behaviors, and broader environmental factors11,12. Understanding these factors is essential for designing targeted and age-appropriate prevention strategies. These research efforts align with the objectives of China’s “Healthy China 2030” initiative, which emphasizes the early prevention of chronic diseases and prioritizes child health as a foundation for national development13. Nevertheless, despite increasing public health attention, there is still a lack of large-scale, regionally representative studies exploring early-life obesity risk factors, especially in underrepresented regions such as western China.

To address this gap, the present study examined temporal trends and key influencing factors associated with obesity among children under the age of three in both rural and urban areas of Shaanxi Province between 2012 and 2024. A comprehensive set of variables was assessed, including demographic characteristics, socioeconomic indicators, perinatal conditions, and caregiving environments. By identifying high-risk subgroups and critical developmental periods, our study aims to generate evidence to inform targeted early-life interventions and guide the development of regionally tailored public health policies to curb the rising burden of childhood obesity.

Methods

Study design and population

Data for this study were obtained from the National Children’s Nutrition and Health Surveillance System (NCNHSS), a population-based registry established by the Health Ministry of China in 2011. The NCNHSS covers 80 districts/counties across 31 provinces (33 rural and 47 urban) using stratified sampling. Detailed information on the NCNHSS methodology is available elsewhere14.

In Shaanxi Province, two surveillance sites were selected (one rural and one urban), encompassing a population of approximately 1 million, representing 2.4% of the national population. The system monitors the nutritional and health status of all children under five residing in the surveillance areas, including non-local households where mothers have lived in the area for over one year. Between January 2012 and December 2024, 11,334 children under 5 years of age met the eligibility criteria for inclusion in the follow-up study. After excluding those lost to follow-up or lacking complete questionnaire or physical examination data (n = 3,133), 8,201 children were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

A minimum sample size of 246 children per site was estimated to detect a 20% prevalence of overweight, assuming a 5% margin of error, a two-sided test with α = 0.05, and based on previous prevalence estimates in the study region15.

Data collection and quality control

Upon the birth of a child within the surveillance area, local village doctors or community officials were required to promptly record the case in the Birth Register and to regularly encourage or remind caregivers to attend scheduled health assessments at the local township hospital or community health service center. Trained maternal and child health (MCH) workers conducted structured caregiver interviews and performed standardized assessments of the child’s physical growth and developmental progress. Each visit included evaluations of anthropometric indicators, developmental milestones, recent illness history, and caregiver-reported child-rearing practices. Children were scheduled to receive ten free health check-ups, accompanied by caregiver-administered questionnaire surveys, at the following ages: 1, 3, 6, 8, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, and 48 months. Hemoglobin concentrations were measured via capillary blood samples obtained through fingerpricks at 6, 18, 30, 36, and 48 months.

Anthropometric measurements, including weight and height, were taken three times under standardized conditions by trained pediatricians. Weight was measured using an electronic scale (model HD 305, Tanita, Dongguan, Guangdong Province, China) to the nearest 100 g. Height was measured without shoes using a portable measuring board to the nearest millimeter. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²). Additional data on delivery mode, maternal health status during pregnancy, gestational age, sex, birth weight, parental education and occupation, household size, family income, caregiving arrangements, and breastfeeding duration were collected at the health visit through caregiver interviews using standardized questionnaires. All data were documented in a standardized Nutrition and Health Monitoring Record Book by MCH staff under project supervision. The data were subsequently entered into the NCNHSS platform and underwent multi-level validation at the county, municipal, and provincial levels.

Variable definitions

Nutritional status was defined using the China Child Growth Standards (WS/T 423–2022)16. Underweight was defined as BMI-for-age < − 2 standard deviations (SD); overweight, as ≥ + 1 SD; and obesity, as ≥ + 2 SD. Low birth weight (LBW) was defined as < 2,500 g, and preterm birth as gestational age < 37 weeks. Small for gestational age (SGA) was defined as birth weight below the 10th percentile for sex and gestational age, using national reference data17. Gestational age was estimated using the first day of the mother’s last menstrual period. Child anemia was defined as hemoglobin < 110 g/L. A “sickly mother” was defined as a mother diagnosed with gestational illnesses, including diabetes mellitus, hypertensive disorders, or hypothyroidism, that were not present prior to pregnancy18.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means and SD, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Obesity prevalence was calculated as the number of children classified as obese within a specific age group divided by the total number of valid observations for that group during the study period. Temporal trends in obesity prevalence were assessed using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.3.0; National Cancer Institute)19. This method estimates annual percentage change (APC) and average annual percentage change (AAPC), with model selection guided by the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Statistical significance of trend changes was assessed using the Monte Carlo permutation method, with p-values < 0.05 considered significant. An APC > 0 indicates an increasing trend, while an APC < 0 indicates a decreasing trend. When APC equaled AAPC, it indicated a consistent trend over the study period. Joinpoints represent statistically significant changes in trend direction or rate, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of temporal dynamics in obesity prevalence.

BMI status transitions over time were visualized using line graphs. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to identify factors associated with obesity at 36 months of age. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Covariates were selected a priori based on prior literature and included: parental sociodemographic characteristics (education, occupation, household size, and household income); maternal health factors (delivery mode and gestational illness); and child-level variables (birth year, birth weight, gestational age, sex, anemia, breastfeeding, and primary caregiver). All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Background characteristics of children and households

Between 2012 and 2024, a total of 8,201 children were included in the study, comprising 4,740 from rural areas and 3,461 from urban areas. Of these, 51.6% (n = 4,229) were male and 48.4% (n = 3,972) were female. Most parents had only primary or secondary education (73.7% of mothers and 72.3% of fathers). In rural areas, the majority of parents were employed in agriculture (87.3% of mothers and 82.1% of fathers). Parents were the primary caregivers for the majority of children in both rural (90.4%) and urban (92.7%) settings (Table 1).

Among urban children, the mean age at assessment was 37.7 ± 2.0 months, and the mean BMI was 15.8 ± 1.5. A higher proportion of urban families (25.5%) reported annual incomes above 60,000 RMB compared to rural families (10.2%, P < 0.01). Additionally, 80.9% of urban children were born in provincial or municipal hospitals, a much higher rate than among rural children (30.0%, P < 0.01). The prevalence of cesarean section was nearly 1.5 times higher in urban areas (39.5%) compared to rural areas (26.9%, P < 0.01). The proportion of overweight and obese children was significantly higher in urban areas (22.2%) than in rural areas (14.5%, P < 0.01).

Annual changes in the prevalence of obesity, 2012–2024

Joinpoint regression analysis identified significant shifts in obesity prevalence trends between 2012 and 2024. At both the provincial and rural levels, a three-stage pattern was observed, with AAPCs of -13.09% (p = 0.022) and − 22.52% (p = 0.003), respectively. In contrast, urban areas experienced a two-stage pattern, with an AAPC of -7.21% (p = 0.073), which was not statistically significant. The steepest declines in obesity prevalence occurred during 2012–2018 at the provincial level (APC = -24.63%, p = 0.058), 2021–2024 in rural areas (APC = -31.72%, p = 0.022), and 2012–2015 in urban areas (APC = -31.72%, p = 0.013). By 2024, the overall obesity prevalence at 36 months of age in Shaanxi Province had declined to 1.91% (Fig. 2).

In rural areas, the prevalence of obesity declined markedly from 10.43% in 2012 to 0.50% in 2018, rose slightly to 7.55% in 2021, and then decreased again to 0.73% by 2024. In contrast, urban areas exhibited a modest but non-significant increase in obesity prevalence, from 2.61% in 2015 to 3.19% in 2024 (APC = 2.78%, p = 0.351). Overall, urban and rural areas displayed different temporal trends.

BMI trajectories from infancy to early childhood

Figure 3 presents the results of the retrospective analysis of BMI status from infancy to age 36 months. Province-wide, most children classified as obese at 36 months had a normal weight during early infancy. However, by 18 months, 41.0% had become overweight and 32.2% were already obese, indicating progressive weight gain (Fig. 3a). In rural areas, among 36-month-old children with obesity, 36.7% were overweight and 35.8% were obese at 18 months, while 27.5% remained in the normal weight range (Fig. 3b). In urban areas, a majority of obese 36-month-old children had been consistently overweight during infancy (Fig. 3c).

Figure 4 shows the results of prospective analyses, assessing weight status transitions from infancy to age 36 months. Among children who were obese at 24 months, 73.5% remained overweight or obese at 36 months, while only 26.5% returned to normal weight (Fig. 4a). In rural areas, 80.0% of children obese at 24 months remained overweight or obese at 36 months (Fig. 4b). In urban areas, 45.8% of children who were obese at 12 months returned to normal weight by 36 months; however, among those obese at 24 months, 63.7% remained overweight (33.6%) or obese (30.1%) at 36 months (Fig. 4c). Factors associated with obesity at 36 months

Multivariable logistic regression identified several factors associated with obesity at 36 months. In rural areas, children from larger families had a higher risk of obesity compared to those from smaller families (AOR = 1.63; 95% CI: 1.18–2.26). Children whose mothers were engaged in agricultural work were more likely to be obese than those with non-agricultural maternal occupations (AOR = 2.30; 95% CI: 1.23–4.30). Being born SGA was also associated with increased obesity risk (AOR = 1.63; 95% CI: 1.02–2.60). Cesarean delivery (AOR = 1.41; 95% CI: 0.98–2.04) and grandparent-led caregiving (AOR = 1.51; 95% CI: 0.94–2.42) showed marginal, non-significant associations with increased obesity risk (Table 2).

Discussion

In our study, the prevalence of obesity among 36-month-old children in Shaanxi Province was 1.91%, a rate considerably lower than those reported in China’s eastern coastal provinces20 and in many high-income countries. For instance, studies from the United States and various European countries have documented obesity prevalence ranging from 7% to 13% among children under five years of age21,22. This comparatively low rate may be attributed to lower urbanization levels, adherence to traditional dietary patterns, and regional differences in socioeconomic and cultural factors20.

Our findings also revealed distinct temporal trends in obesity prevalence among children under three years of age from 2012 to 2024, with marked differences between urban and rural areas. In rural areas, obesity prevalence declined markedly from 2012 to 2018, rose temporarily in 2021, and declined again by 2024. This three-phase pattern, identified by Joinpoint regression, yielded an overall AAPC of -22.52%, suggesting a significant long-term decline despite short-term fluctuations, likely influenced by behavioral disruptions during the COVID-19 pandemic, such as reduced physical activity, increased screen time, and altered eating patterns23. In contrast, urban areas exhibited a two-phase trend: a sharp decline from 2012 to 2015, followed by a plateau and slight increase through 2024, with an overall AAPC of -7.21%. These divergent trends may reflect contextual factors such as delivery mode, feeding practices, and environmental constraints. Urban children were more likely to be born via cesarean section and in tertiary hospitals, both linked to elevated obesity risk24. Additionally, limited outdoor space, safety concerns, and increased exposure to digital media may constrain toddlers’ physical activity in urban settings, fostering sedentary behaviors early in life. These results mirror national trends and suggest an expanding urban–rural disparity in early childhood obesity25, emphasizing the need for targeted, context-specific interventions tailored to local risk profiles.

Our longitudinal data further revealed that the majority of obese children at 36 months had already exceeded healthy BMI thresholds by 18 months of age, aligning with previous research in Chinese populations showing that elevated BMI z-scores at 12 and 18 months are strong predictors of obesity at age three26. Similar evidence from the United States links rapid weight gain during the first two years of life to early-onset obesity27. This underscores the critical need for interventions during the first 1,000 days, particularly around 18 months, when growth trajectories begin to diverge28. Community-based strategies such as routine growth monitoring, caregiver-targeted nutritional counseling, and early adjustments in feeding practices may serve as cost-effective and scalable approaches for identifying and supporting at-risk children. Integrating these interventions into existing maternal and child health platforms could enhance feasibility and sustainability, especially in resource-limited settings.

Our analysis also identified vulnerable subgroups that warrant prioritized attention. These include children from low-income households, large families, and those whose mothers are engaged in agricultural labor, especially in rural areas. In such contexts, reduced caregiver supervision and reliance on calorie-dense convenience foods may increase obesity risk29,30. Mothers involved in seasonal agricultural work often spend long hours away from home, increasing dependence on grandparents for daily childcare and feeding. While grandparental care is common and often necessary, prior research suggests that grandparents may adopt more permissive feeding practices and have limited familiarity with modern nutritional guidelines29. Additionally, the increasing availability of processed snack foods has reshaped dietary patterns even in early childhood31. Despite caregivers’ control over children’s diets, these environmental shifts influence food accessibility and choices. Children from low-income households exhibited higher obesity risk, likely driven by a combination of food insecurity, preference for calorie-dense low-cost foods, and reduced access to health education, pediatric services, and structured nutrition programs32. Effective prevention strategies must therefore extend beyond individual behavior change to address structural and environmental determinants, including food systems, health service access, and socioeconomic inequalities.

Furthermore, our study identified associations between adverse birth outcomes, cesarean delivery, and childhood obesity, even after adjusting for potential confounders. Infants born SGA who experience rapid postnatal catch-up growth are at elevated risk for excessive adiposity, a finding that aligns with evidence from other cohort studies conducted in China29,33. Although cesarean section was not statistically significant in our study, its high prevalence (32.2%), substantially exceeding the WHO’s recommended threshold of 15%34, raises important concerns. Non-medically indicated cesarean deliveries may disrupt gut microbiota development and immune programming, which are increasingly recognized as contributors to early-life obesity risk35.

Breastfeeding practices also played a significant role in shaping obesity risk. In line with previous evidence suggesting that adherence to recommended breastfeeding guidelines could prevent up to one million cases of childhood obesity annually36, our study found that early initiation of breastfeeding was associated with a protective effect. However, prolonged breastfeeding beyond 24 months was linked to an increased risk of obesity among urban children, consistent with prior studies reporting a U-shaped relationship between breastfeeding duration and child BMI37. This pattern may reflect inadequate or delayed complementary feeding, or overreliance on breastfeeding as a primary nutrition source beyond the recommended age. These findings highlight the need to pair breastfeeding promotion with tailored guidance on the timing, quality, and balance of complementary foods to support optimal growth and prevent excess weight gain.

This study employed a population-based design involving a large cohort of 8,201 children, spanning the critical developmental window from infancy through early childhood, and provides important empirical evidence to inform region-specific public health policies and guide targeted early intervention strategies for young children in Northwest China. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the data were derived from a nationally standardized surveillance platform, the study was restricted to two sentinel sites within Shaanxi Province, which may affect generalizability to other western regions with different demographic or cultural contexts. Second, consistent with many long-term cohort studies, participant attrition was non-trivial, with nearly one-quarter of the original sample lost to follow-up. However, baseline comparisons between participants who remained and those who were excluded, specifically in terms of sex (male: 51.6% vs. 50.9%, p = 0.571), birth weight (3296.8 ± 415.1 g vs. 3296.8 ± 415.1 g, p = 0.781), and primary caregiver type (parents: 91.4% vs. 92.3%, p = 0.158), revealed no statistically significant differences, suggesting that selection bias was likely minimal. Third, while the longitudinal design allowed for the assessment of BMI trajectories and associated factors over time, the observational nature of the study precludes causal inference. Associations between early-life exposures and subsequent obesity risk should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, the study did not incorporate objective assessments of key behavioral factors, such as dietary intake, physical activity, and screen time, which are increasingly recognized as influential in early childhood weight outcomes. Future research should aim to address these limitations by utilizing multi-center cohort designs and integrating more detailed behavioral and nutritional assessments to validate and expand upon the present findings.

In conclusion, our study highlights the prevalence of obesity among 36-month-old children in Shaanxi Province, characterized by pronounced urban-rural disparities and socioeconomic gradients. The early onset of excessive weight gain, particularly from around 18 months of age, emerges as a critical predictor of obesity in preschool-aged children. Additionally, factors such as socioeconomic status, maternal occupation, SGA birth, and breastfeeding practices were found to significantly influence obesity risk, underscoring the multifaceted interplay of biological, behavioral, and environmental determinants in early life. Policy efforts should aim for coordinated, multisectoral action that links health, education, and community resources to create sustainable change in early childhood obesity risk. Furthermore, improving the nutritional landscape for low-income families, strengthening support for timely breastfeeding initiation and appropriate complementary feeding, and empowering both parents and grandparents with accurate, evidence-based health knowledge are crucial steps toward promoting healthier growth trajectories among young children in Northwest China.
