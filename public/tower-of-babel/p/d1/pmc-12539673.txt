A Randomized Controlled Trial Comparing Early Outcomes Following Duct-duct Biliary Anastomosis With or Without Transanastomotic Biliary Stenting in Adult Whole Liver Transplant Patients
Authors: Sarah J. Byrne, Nick A. Butler, Janske Reiling, Thomas R. O’Rourke, Shin Yeung, Kellee Slater, Peter J. Hodgkinson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background.Despite advances in liver transplantation, the rate of anastomotic biliary complications (ACs) remains unchanged. In the event of a biliary stricture or bile leak after duct-duct reconstruction, endoscopic retrograde biliary stenting is recognized as first-line treatment. Data on prophylactic biliary stenting performed at the time of the biliary anastomosis are conflicting. The aim of this study was to compare early AC in liver transplant recipients undergoing duct-duct reconstruction, who were randomly assigned to receive either a biliary stent (control) or no biliary stent (treatment).Methods.A single-blinded, randomized controlled, noninferiority trial with parallel treatment arms was conducted. Only patients receiving whole adult liver allografts from brain-dead donors were included. ACs included biliary stricture, bile leak, and endoscopic retrieval of retained stents from patients in the control arm. After the exclusion of patients by a priori defined criteria, 84 patients were randomly assigned to equal control and treatment arms.Results.A total of 161 adult liver transplants occurred between March 2019 and May 2022 at a single institution. The AC rate was 18% (n = 15), all of which were strictures. All ACs were managed with endoscopic retrograde biliary stenting. There was no difference in ACs between the stent and no-stent groups, but 41% of patients (n = 16) in the stented group required endoscopic removal of retained stents at 4 wk posttransplant.Conclusions.There was no difference in the rate of AC between patients who received a prophylactic biliary stent and those who did not. However, a significant number of stented patients required intervention for stent removal.

INTRODUCTION

Biliary complications remain a persistent problem despite advances in liver transplantation (LT). These complications can cause significant morbidity, retransplantation, and mortality; therefore, trying to understand factors that contribute to biliary complications is essential to improve outcomes. The biliary complication rate is higher in living donor LT (LDLT), donation after circulatory death (DCD), and split and pediatric LTs, but the majority of LTs performed use whole adult liver allografts donated after brain death (DBD).1,2 Therefore, the purpose of this study was to look specifically at biliary complications in patients who received whole adult liver DBD allografts. We believe that mastering the technique in this cohort could then be translated to more complex liver grafts, where there are many more confounding factors pertaining to biliary complications.

The incidence of biliary complications after LT has not changed for decades and ranges between 10% and 40%.1,3-6 This is largely attributed to more complex recipients and marginal donors, but there are modifiable risk factors particularly pertaining to surgical technique and anastomotic biliary complications (ACs). The incidence of AC after deceased donor LTs (DDLTs) ranges from 12% to 26%,2,4-7 with the majority occurring within 6 mo of transplant.1,8

Most institutions favor a duct-duct anastomosis in the presence of healthy native bile ducts.9-13 This technique is associated with a lower rate of biliary complications compared with hepaticojejunostomy.9,12,13 Restoration of primary biliary continuity preserves the physiologic bilioenteric cycle and the anatomic Sphincter of Oddi complex, which prevents enteric reflux, but most importantly, it facilitates easier endoscopic access to the bile duct.10-12

Early biliary complications include leaks and more commonly strictures.10,13 Early strictures can result from compromised biliary blood supply, immunological factors, and technical problems, all of which lead to ischemia and fibrosis of the anastomosis.12,14 Modifiable risk factors pertaining to surgical technique for AC include the use of nonabsorbable suture material, tension on the anastomosis, excessive use of electrocauterization, compromising the blood supply to the bile duct, and the use of biliary splintage.2,10,11,13,15

Research on biliary splintage is conflicting. Historically, the decision to place a transanastomotic biliary stent has been at the discretion of the surgeon, influenced predominantly by previous practice.4 Internal biliary stents are generally favored over externally draining T-tubes and are better tolerated by patients, but there are fewer studies on internal biliary stents.4

The perceived advantages of placing a transanastomotic stent include biliary decompression, which reduces the intrabiliary pressure and the theoretical risk of an anastomotic leak or stricture.16 The stents are generally radiopaque, hence visible on plain X-ray imaging, which is a simple investigation to assess position. They are also well tolerated by the patient, with the majority being unaware of their presence. Disadvantages include stent blockage, cholangitis, and the requirement for postoperative endoscopic removal in the event of a retained stent. Despite the rationale for placing a stent, the efficacy of whether they reduce AC remains controversial, with conflicting published data.

Elkomos and Abdelaal17 performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of biliary stents and complications in LTs. The results of their analysis did not identify a benefit to biliary stenting, but their cohort of research articles was relatively heterogenous, including LDLT, Roux-en-Y anastomoses, suprapapillary, and biodegradable stents.17 Including various types of liver allografts has the potential for more confounders when looking at biliary complications. We chose to specifically look at DBD allografts, which is the population with the lowest rate of biliary complications, comparatively.

Yoon et al4 published their retrospective data on 132 patients who had a stent compared with 350 patients with no stent in DDLT. They found no difference in the rate of anastomotic biliary strictures (ASs) or overall biliary complication rate between the stent and no-stent groups.4 This contrasts with the results published by Jung et al18 and Girard et al,19 supporting the use of stents. These retrospective cohort studies identified lower rates of biliary complications, including AS, in those with a stent.18,19

There are a few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on transanastomotic biliary stenting in DDLT recipients. Bawa et al20 reported results from an RCT of 37 patients. They randomly assigned 18 patients to receive a Cotton Leung stent with planned endoscopic removal and the remainder to have no stent.20 Fewer biliary complications were found in the no-stent group versus the stent group (5.2% versus 50%). The results were so significant that the trial was terminated prematurely.20 The perceived problem with this RCT is the relatively small sample size. No power calculations were mentioned in the article.20 The larger BILIDRAIN-T RCT looked at a customized transanastomotic T-tube and found no difference in the rate of biliary complications between the groups that had a stent or not.6 It is worth mentioning that in this study, they used a suprapapillary stent and so endoscopic removal was planned for all stent patients.6

The retrospective observational study performed at our institution and published by Ong et al21 showed no difference in AS rate between the stent and no-stent group, but the stent group was 3 times more likely to require intervention, mostly in the form of endoscopic stent removal. This RCT was subsequently established to validate these findings.

The objective of this RCT was to quantitatively compare outcomes after duct-duct anastomosis during whole adult DBD allografts with or without a transanastomotic transpapillary internal biliary stent. Although we did not identify a published RCT in the literature that used the same stent that we used, the authors acknowledge that the use of infant feeding catheters as stents is a long-standing practice. The perceived benefit of this stent is the transpapillary positioning so as to allow for spontaneous passage of the stent, obviating the need for endoscopic removal. Before this study, the use of stents at our institution was not standardized. The impact of this study resulted in a change in standard practice at our institution.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Population

From March 2019 to May 2022, all patients aged 18 y and older who were listed on the LT waiting list at the Princess Alexandra Hospital, Brisbane, Queensland, Australia, and planned to receive a whole liver DBD allograft with planned duct-duct reconstruction were eligible for the RCT. Patients were prospectively consented at the time of listing.

Study Design

A single-blinded, randomized controlled, noninferiority trial with parallel treatment arms was conducted. Patients were randomly assigned to receive a stent (control) or no stent (treatment arm). Reporting of the trial was done according to the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) statement (Figure 1). Patients were excluded from the trial if they were to receive any graft other than a whole liver from a brain-dead donor, as were patients undergoing hepaticojejunostomy.

CONSORT 2010 flow diagram. CONSORT, Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials.

The trial was conducted in compliance with the ethical principles of the Metro South Hospital and Health Service. The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee, Princess Alexandra Hospital, Brisbane, Queensland, Australia (HREC/20188/QMS/43468). The trial was registered on the Australia and New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (Trial No.: 377469).

A sample size of 80 patients was calculated to provide a statistically significant result with 95% confidence interval and 80% power using the power calculator (http://powerandsamplesize.com). Randomization was performed before the commencement of the study and was blinded to all participants except for the 3 investigators. Randomization was achieved by a computer-generated table created with an online tool (https://www.sealedenvelope.com). Allocation to study arms was performed with a 1:1 ratio. Each allocation (stent or no stent) was concealed in an opaque envelope within a combination lock safe. The allocation was revealed intraoperatively once the decision was made to perform a duct-duct biliary reconstruction.

The patients were blinded to the allocation until they were informed that an abdominal X-ray would be required to assess stent position at 4 wk post-LT.

Surgical Technique

The organ retrieval procedure was performed in accordance with the best practice guidelines of the Australian Government on Organ and Tissue Authority. The University of Wisconsin preservative solution was infused through aortic and portal vein cannulas after an initial cold saline flush. Organs were transported in static cold storage and cold ischemic times were documented. In 1 case, the liver was placed on the normothermic perfusion machine after transport from the donor hospital in static cold storage.

Orthotopic LTs were performed at the Princess Alexandra Hospital by 5 consultant surgeons. The duct-duct anastomosis was performed superior to the cystic duct junction using either a 5/0 or 6/0 polydioxanone suture in an interrupted or continuous manner according to the individual surgeon. The biliary stent was an infant feeding catheter composed of polyvinyl chloride with a radioopaque longitudinal marker cut to a length that allowed it to be positioned across the anastomosis and through the Ampulla of Vater. The length was approximately 150 mm with the proximal end positioned in the common hepatic duct and the distal end in the duodenum. The diameter of the catheter used was at the discretion of the surgeon and was either a 05 or 06 CH, corresponding to outer diameters of 1.67 mm and 2.00 mm, respectively.

Data Collection

Data were prospectively collected, and outcomes were recorded for a period of 6 mo postoperatively. Preintervention baseline characteristics of the donor liver allograft and recipient were documented. The primary outcomes assessed were AS, bile leak, and the need for endoscopic removal of stents for those retained at 4 wk in the control arm. Secondary outcomes included the need for invasive procedures postoperatively and mortality.

Statistical Analysis

All continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range) and all categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage). Univariable factors were tabulated, and statistical analysis was performed using the Mann-Whitney U test and chi-square test. Results were considered significant with a P value of <0.05. Multivariable logistical regression analysis was performed to determine risk factors for AS.

RESULTS

Population Characteristics

The CONSORT flow diagram is presented in Figure 1. Between March 5, 2019, and May 5, 2022, a total of 161 consecutive adult LTs were performed by the Queensland LT service. Seventy-seven transplants were excluded, of which 65 did not meet the inclusion criteria and 9 declined consent. One patient underwent a multivisceral heart transplant/lung transplant/LT at a different hospital and was therefore excluded. The remaining 2 were randomized but not allocated. In 1 case, which occurred early after the study commencement, the surgeon forgot to allocate the patient before completing the biliary anastomosis. In the second case, the recipient had previously undergone gastric bypass surgery, and an intraoperative decision was made to exclude the patient due to concerns over impaired endoscopic access in the event of a retained stent (no stent was placed). Forty-two were analyzed in each treatment arm.

The baseline demographics are presented in Table 1. Interestingly, there were more men in the no-stent versus stent group (86% versus 60%) although this difference was not statistically significant. The predominant indication for LT was synthetic failure followed by hepatocellular carcinoma. The predominant cause of liver disease was alcohol and/or viral hepatitis. There was no difference in transplant procedural characteristics between the stent and no-stent groups, including the time to hepatic artery (HA) perfusion, defined as the time from when the liver was taken out of ice to HA perfusion.

DISCUSSION

Decreasing the incidence of biliary complications by focusing on modifiable risk factors is a major goal in LT; however, the use of biliary splintage with an intraoperatively placed stent remains controversial.

In this RCT, we analyzed the outcomes of patients undergoing duct-duct biliary anastomosis with and without transanastomotic biliary stents that were not scheduled for planned removal. Although there are articles specifically investigating higher risk groups, such as LDLT, pediatric, DCD, and split LTs, we looked at patients who received whole adult livers from brain-dead donors because they comprise the most common graft type in our jurisdiction. This provides validity to our study population and adds to the current literature.

Studies that focus on identifying risk factors for AC have identified several anatomic and physiological factors that contribute to AC. Kaldas et al15 identified 7 independent risk factors on multivariate analysis from their retrospective cohort of 509 LTs. These include recipient HA thrombosis, second LT, recipient HA stenosis, donor hypertension, recipients with hepatocellular carcinoma, donor death due to anoxia, and the use of nonabsorbable suture material for the biliary reconstruction.15 Subgroup analysis of our cohort, using the recipient factors identified in the article by Kaldas et al,15 did not find an association between stent use and the development of biliary strictures, even when corrected for significant differences between treatment groups (Table 4).

Our overall biliary complication and AS rate were comparable with those of other studies. We did not have any bile leaks in either treatment arm. This may be due to the surgical technique using a small-sized suture/needle that was absorbable, but further research could explore this finding. This was a noninferiority study that did not identify a difference in AC rates between patients who received stents and those who did not. The significant shortcoming of stent placement is the high proportion of patients who require endoscopy, including ERCP, for stent removal despite positioning of the stent to allow for spontaneous passage. These findings support the observational data published by Yoon et al.4 Our RCT had a larger sample size compared with the similarly designed study performed by Bawa et al,20 who reported significantly more complications in the stent group. Bawa et al20 do not mention any power calculations used to determine the sample size needed to detect a significant difference. They also did not indicate whether participants were blinded to treatment groups. The main point of difference in the article by Bawa et al was the type of stent used compared with our stent. The stents used in our study were made of pliable polyvinyl chloride, and a considerable length was positioned in the duodenum to encourage spontaneous passage. In contrast, the stents in Bawa’s study were made of a rigid polyethylene with proximal and distal flaps to prevent migration, requiring scheduled removal. Despite the paucity of literature on the use of infant feeding catheters as internal biliary stents, it is an accepted practice. A retrospective study by Kakati et al23 analyzed the use of infant feeding catheters in LT recipients and did not identify a difference in biliary complications. Our robust blinded RCT further supports these findings, providing a controlled baseline for comparison and minimizing bias.

The use of an infant catheter as a biliary stent is a simple, readily accessible device that has the potential to spontaneously pass through the bilioenteric gastrointestinal tract, thereby negating the requirement of planned endoscopic extirpation. We believe that our article contributes to the body of literature on biliary stents because of the type and positioning of the stent used, as well as the higher-quality methodology presented.

Our RCT is also novel with respect to the meta-analysis by Elkomos and Abdelaal17 because they included non-RCTs as well as RCTs and a heterogenous cohort of both stent types (internal and external) and types of biliary reconstruction (duct-duct and HJ) and different liver allografts (pediatric, split, LDLT, DDLT). The article by Bawa et al is included in the meta-analysis by Elkomos and Abdelaal,17 but no articles using an internal biliary stent capable of spontaneous passage were included.

A higher proportion of stented patients required endoscopic removal, including ERCP. ERCP is associated with an overall complication rate of 6%–10%, which can have significant clinical implications in this immunocompromised cohort.24 We had 1 patient develop post-ERCP pancreatitis, which is the most common complication occurring in 3.5%–14% patients requiring ERCP.24,25 Infections, gastrointestinal bleeding, and perforation occur less commonly but are more often associated with a blood transfusion requirement and mortality.24 The avoidance of biliary stents eliminates the requirement for endoscopic removal of a retained stent and therefore avoids the associated risks of ERCP.

The RCT design is the gold standard for assessing the difference between interventional arms; however, there were some limitations with our study. First, although we performed power calculations to determine the target sample size for this study, the relatively small sample size resulted in a significant difference in demographic data (male sex) across the study arms. Second, the follow-up period of 6 mo was determined to capture the majority of AC a priori. In an attempt to capture late-occurring anastomotic complications, we proceeded to review the AC rate up to 12 mo posttransplant and did not identify any further biliary anastomotic complications requiring ERCP in either the treatment or control arms; therefore, the 6-mo timeframe was deemed appropriate. As with most single-center RCTs, external validation would be improved by a multicenter design. Given the explicit methodology and exclusion of complex, high-risk grafts, this study is reproducible, which would help determine any confounding factors within our population. We specifically used DBD allografts because they are the most abundant type of allograft available.

Kaldas et al15 did identify specific donor factors, such as donor hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and death due to anoxia, to be associated with a higher rate of AC, but the results were not statistically significant. We used these risk factors for multivariate analysis but did not find associations with AC in our cohort. Future research could explore whether donor factors influence ACs; however, it is unclear how this might impact further practice. Given that AC remains an Achilles’ heel of LT, future research exploring whether different stent designs could have a therapeutic impact might be worth considering. Any such study would need to consider not only improvements in AC rates but also the need for endoscopic intervention in the event of retained stents, the impact on recipient quality of life, and an evaluation of cost metrics when assessing patient outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

This RCT demonstrates that stent placement during duct-duct anastomosis has no beneficial impact with respect to AC but results in a significant proportion of stented patients requiring endoscopic removal, some of whom experience significant morbidity as a result. These findings have directly impacted clinical practice in that a previously heterogenous cohort of surgeons has now standardized duct-duct anastomosis without the use of biliary splintage.
