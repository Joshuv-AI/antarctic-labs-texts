Influence of anthropometric factors on estimating intraoperative challenges in bariatric surgery: a prospective correlational study
Authors: Justyna Rymarowicz, Izabela Karpińska, Izabela Powalacz, Magdalena Pyzik, Bartosz Iwanowski, Jakub Pośpiech, Piotr Panek, Piotr Major
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLaparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (SG) is a widely performed bariatric procedure that trainee surgeons are increasingly performing. While body mass index (BMI) is commonly used to predict surgical challenges, its limitations necessitate exploring alternative anthropometric factors. This study examines the role of additional anthropometric factors in predicting intraoperative difficulty, with the goal of improving surgical planning and outcomes.MethodsA prospective cohort study included 100 SG patients treated between January and July 2023 at a tertiary bariatric center. Preoperative assessments included anthropometric measurements, ultrasound evaluation of abdominal fat, and bioelectrical impedance analysis. Intraoperative difficulty was assessed using two measures: deviation of operative time from each surgeon’s mean operating time and a standardized 13-item surgeon questionnaire rating the technical difficulty of specific surgical steps. Potential predictors, including patient characteristics, anthropometric indices, and surgeon experience, were analyzed using univariate and multivariable regression models, with collinearity checks to ensure model validity.ResultsThe mean BMI was 43.6 ± 5.6 kg/m², the median visceral fat rating (VFR) was 15.0 [IQR 12.00–21.00], and the mean waist-hip ratio (WHR) was 0.92 ± 0.10. Surgical specialists performed 75% of procedures, with a median operating time of 50 min, compared to 90 min for residents. Surgeons’ questionnaire responses indicated overall low perceived technical difficulty, with median scores ranging from 1.00 to 2.00 across all surgical steps. In multivariable regression analysis, higher BMI, VFR, WHR, and waist circumference were significant independent predictors of longer operative times after adjusting for confounders. Regression model including BMI showed the strongest association with increased surgical difficulty, explaining up to 21% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.214).ConclusionHigher BMI, VFR, WHR, and waist circumference were independently associated with increased intraoperative difficulty during sleeve gastrectomy, with BMI emerging as the strongest predictor.

Introduction

Laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (SG) is the most commonly performed bariatric procedure worldwide [1] and is typically the first procedure that bariatric surgeons learn during their training [2]. SG has become popular due to its relatively standardized surgical steps and comparatively low postoperative complication rate [3–7]. However, as the proportion of SG procedures performed by less experienced, trainee surgeons has increased [2], understanding factors that could complicate the procedure is increasingly essential. Severe obesity, widely associated with increased intraoperative challenges in laparoscopy, can add complexity to bariatric surgeries [8–10]. Due to this, bariatric procedures, including SG, demand advanced laparoscopic skills and extensive training for safe and effective outcomes [11].

While Body Mass Index (BMI) is commonly used to classify obesity and assess surgical risk, it has limitations in predicting body fat distribution and obesity-related complications [12–14]. Other anthropometric measures, including bioelectrical impedance analysis, have been developed for a more precise evaluation of body composition and are frequently used in clinical practice [15–17]. Previous studies suggest that a higher BMI may correlate with increased difficulty in bariatric surgeries, based on surgeon-reported experience [18, 19].

However, specific patient-related factors contributing to surgical difficulty during SG remain underexplored. To address this gap, we conducted a single-center, prospective correlational study to objectively analyze which anthropometric and clinical factors are most associated with intraoperative challenges in SG. By identifying these factors, the study aims to provide insights to enhance surgical training, inform procedural planning, and improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This prospective cohort study included bariatric patients meeting predefined eligibility criteria who underwent surgery between January and July 2023 at the Tertiary Bariatric Center x. Upon admission, anthropometric measurements, ultrasound assessments, and bioelectrical impedance analysis (via Tanita) were performed. Post-surgery, the duration of the procedure was recorded, and the operating surgeon completed a 13-item questionnaire assessing the difficulty of the surgery. Relevant clinical data were later extracted from patients’ medical records for analysis.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria for bariatric surgery in Cracow University Hospital are: (1) > 18 years old with BMI over 40kg/m2 (Class III obesity, also termed severe obesity)”; (2) > 18 years old with BMI 35–40 kg/m2 (Class II obesity) when surgical intervention may help with obesity-associated diseases (for example diabetes type 2, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), cardiovascular disease, sleep apnea, hyperlipidemia or female infertility). Patients undergoing bariatric procedures, different than SG or revisional SG surgery, were excluded from the study.

Intaopreative details

All bariatric procedures were performed at a single center using a standardized, step-by-step surgical technique. Surgical trainees carried out the procedures under the direct supervision of an experienced staff surgeon. If a trainee encountered significant difficulty despite guidance, the supervising surgeon would intervene and complete the challenging portion of the operation. When a staff surgeon served as the primary operator, the procedure was assisted either by two trainees or by one staff surgeon and one trainee.

The patient is placed in the French (split-leg) position and tilted 30–45° reverse Trendelenburg. Five trocars are routinely inserted. Pneumoperitoneum is established using a 10 mm optical trocar placed at Palmer’s point [20]. A 10 mm camera port is inserted in the midline, 15–18 cm caudal to the xiphoid process. A 5 mm trocar is placed in the right subcostal area along the midclavicular line for use by the operating surgeon, and an additional 5 mm trocar is placed in the left subcostal area along the anterior axillary line for the assisting surgeon. The operating surgeon stands between the patient’s legs, the camera operator is positioned to the patient’s right, and the first assistant to the patient’s left. A Nathanson retractor is used for liver retraction when necessary. The gastrocolic ligament is dissected along the greater curvature of the stomach, starting approximately 6 cm from the pylorus up to the gastroesophageal junction. A 34Fr orogastric tube is used to calibrate the gastric sleeve, which is then created using a 3–6 powered linear stapler. The stapler line is oversewn with a continuous barbed suture, followed by a leak test with 100 ml of methylene blue dye. The specimen is extracted through the laparoscopic port incision. The extraction incision (enlarged port site) was closed in layers; the fascia was closed with a suture passer (“crochet hook technique”).

Anthropometric data

The following measurements were taken: height, weight, BMI, waist circumference [21], hip circumference, and waist-hip ratio (WHR), all measured according to World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines. Android and gynoid obesity types were determined based on WHR [22].

Total body fat percentage and visceral fat rating (VFR) were measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis (TANITA).

Subcutaneous abdominal fat thickness was measured using a Canon Aplio i700 ultrasound with a linear probe (i18LX5). A strict, standardized protocol was followed: the probe was positioned in the midline, 15 cm caudal to the xiphoid process, at the level of the operator trocar insertion. The distance from the skin to the parietal peritoneum was measured. To minimize measurement error, the average of three repeated measurements was taken. NAFLD was identified on an ultrasound scan by the loss of distinct echogenicity between the liver and the right kidney parenchyma.

Clinical data

Clinical data, including obesity related conditions, were collected from patient records and included diagnoses of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, atherosclerosis, and obstructive sleep apnea. Type 2 diabetes was defined according to ADA guidelines [23], while hypertension was identified by systolic blood pressure > 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure > 90 mmHg [24].

Additionally, records were reviewed for factors potentially contributing to increased surgical difficulty, such as previous intragastric balloon placement, abdominal wall hernias, and a history of prior abdominal surgeries.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was intraoperative surgical difficulty. It was measured in two ways: by the duration of surgery and by the surgeon’s opinion, as reported in the post-operative questionnaire.

Surgical difficulty was operationally defined as the difference between the actual operating time for each procedure and the surgeon’s mean operating time, resulting in a continuous numerical variable that could take both negative and positive values depending on how far each case deviated from the surgeon-specific mean. This approach utilized the whole dataset and allowed for direct adjustment for covariates.

Following each procedure, the surgeon was asked to complete a 13-item questionnaire evaluating the difficulty level of each surgical step using a five-point Likert scale. Responses were translated into numerical scores as follows: 1 = very easy, 2 = easy, 3 = moderate, 4 = difficult, 5 = very difficult. The assessed steps included difficulty in: establishing pneumoperitoneum, operative field visualization, adhesion removal, liver retraction, mobilization of the greater curvature, division of short gastric vessels, bougie-guided sleeve calibration, gastric resection with stapler, staple line reinforcement, hemostasis of staple line, intraoperative leak test, specimen extraction, and port site closure. Completed questionnaires were obtained for 61 out of 100 cases. The missing questionnaires (n = 39) occurred at random and were not associated with specific surgeons, patient characteristics, or case complexity. Instead, the missing data were primarily due to logistical challenges in study coordination, including timing conflicts. As a result, no systematic bias was identified in the collection of subjective difficulty assessments.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) for normally and non-normally distributed variables, respectively. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms.

Potential predictors of surgical difficulty included age, gender, previous intragastric balloon placement, previous abdominal surgeries, abdominal wall hernia, surgeon experience level (staff surgeon vs. resident) along with various anthropometric measures: body mass index (BMI), waist-hip ratio (WHR), waist circumference, (VFR), subcutaneous fat thickness. To assess possible multicollinearity among the anthropometric variables, a two-step approach was used. First, a pairwise correlation Matrix was generated, using a correlation coefficient cutoff of 0.7 to indicate high correlation. This analysis showed significant pairwise collinearity between BMI and waist circumference (correlation coefficient of 0.81), between visceral fat rating (VFR) and waist circumference (0.75), and between VFR and BMI (0.72). Based on these results, a Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) analysis was then performed to detect multivariate collinearity. The calculated VIF values were 2.76 for WHR, 5.46 for BMI, 2.24 for visceral fat, and 7.36 for waist circumference; the intercept had a VIF of 217.99. A VIF value greater than 5 was considered indicative of potentially problematic multicollinearity. These findings were taken into account in the final model selection to minimize redundancy and ensure the stability and interpretability of the regression model.

First, univariate regression models were constructed for each predictor variable to identify potential associations with the outcome. Predictors demonstrating a significance level of p < 0.1 in the univariate analyses were then included in multivariable regression models. To address concerns about multicollinearity identified during preliminary checks and final analysis, waist circumference, BMI, and VFR were not included in the same model simultaneously. Instead, three separate multivariable regression models were built. The performance and explanatory power of these alternative models were subsequently compared to assess the impact of using either waist circumference, BMI, or VFR as an indicator of body composition in predicting the outcome. To check for autocorrelation of residuals, the Durbin–Watson statistic was calculated for each final model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The normality of residuals was assessed using the Jarque-Bera test. A significant result indicated some deviation from normality, which was taken into account when interpreting the model’s estimates. All analyses were performed using Python 3.11.13 with the statsmodels package.

Results

General study group characteristics

The study group consisted of 100 bariatric patients undergoing SG; 76% were female, with a mean age of 41.1 ± 11.27 years. Fifty-two patients underwent previous abdominal surgery, nine patients had abdominal wall hernias, and six patients underwent previous intragastric balloon treatment.

Anthropometric measurements

The mean BMI was 43.62 ± 5.64 kg/m2, the median VFR was 15.00 [IQR 12.00–21.00], The mean percentage of fat tissue was 45.55 ± 5.75%, the mean WHR was 0.92 ± 0.10, the mean waist circumference was 126.50 ± 14.49 cm, the mean hip circumference was 136.50 ± 12.98 cm, 81 patients had android and 14 gynoid obesity type (5 missing values). The median subcutaneous fat thickness was 50.50 mm [IQR 36.05–70.64].

Procedure-related results

Surgical specialists performed 75 procedures, and surgical residents conducted 25. The mean operating time was 55.00 min [IQR 45.00–75.00]. The operating time ranges for the specialists were 28 min to 134 min, and for the surgical residents, the ranges were 47 min to 145 min. The median operating time for the specialist was 50.00 min [IQR 45.00–55.00], and the median operating time for the surgical resident was 90 min [IQR 78.00 −100]. The lowest average operating time for the operator was 50 min, and the highest was 110 min. The mean for the average operating time for the specialist was 61.36 min, and the mean for the average operating time for the surgical resident was 99.95 min. The mean BMI of patients operated on by trainees was 42.87 ± 4.38, and by specialists 43.84 ± 5.82.

Perceived difficulty of surgical steps

Responses to the questionnaire of the difficulty level of each surgical step, rated on a 5-point Likert scale, indicated generally low perceived difficulty across all procedural steps. Median scores for individual steps ranged from 1.0 to 2.0, with the Majority of first quartiles at 1.0 and third quartiles at or below 2.25, demonstrating a notable floor effect. The most frequently selected ratings were “very easy” or “easy” for all steps, with over 50% of responses at the lowest scale point for several steps. Predictors of surgical difficulty: regression models

Univariate linear regression analyses demonstrated that age, sex, BMI, visceral fat ratio (VFR), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), subcutaneous fat thickness, and waist circumference were each significantly associated with surgical difficulty (p < 0.1).

Three different multivariate linear regression models were constructed to examine the combined effects of these predictors while minimizing collinearity. In these models, waist circumference (p = 0.050), VFR (p = 0.035), BMI (p = 0.006), and WHR (p = 0.016–0.156) remained statistically significant predictors.

Model 1, which included age, sex, waist circumference, WHR, and subcutaneous fat thickness, accounted for approximately 18% of the variance in surgical difficulty (adjusted R² = 0.181). Model 2, which replaced waist circumference with VFR, explained about 19% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.193). Model 3, which included BMI instead of waist circumference or VFR, had the highest explanatory power, accounting for approximately 21% of the variance in surgical difficulty (adjusted R² = 0.214).

Discussion

This prospective single-center correlation study demonstrated that higher BMI, VFR, WHR, and waist circumference are independently associated with increased surgical difficulty, with BMI showing the strongest predictive value among the anthropometric measures analyzed. However, the relatively modest proportion of explained variance highlights the multifactorial nature of surgical complexity.

In our models, patient-related factors accounted for up to 21% of the variability in intraoperative difficulty, with the model including BMI and WHR performing best. Specifically, each 1-unit increase in BMI was associated with an increase of approximately 0.86 min in deviation from the surgeon’s mean operative time (p = 0.006), while a 0.1-unit increase in WHR corresponded to an increase of about 5.17 min (p = 0.016), after adjusting for age, sex, and other covariates.

Previous studies comparing visceral fat and BMI in minimally invasive cancer surgeries have shown that both measures perform similarly in predicting prolonged operative times [25, 26]. In contrast, for transperitoneal robotic nephrectomy, visceral fat area (VFA) rather than BMI was associated with longer operative times [27]. Notably, these studies assessed general populations, including lean patients, and measured VFA using abdominal CT scans.

In bariatric surgery, a BMI exceeding 60 kg/m² has been identified as a predictor of intraoperative challenges [18, 19], based mainly on surgeons’ subjective assessments. However, direct comparison with our findings is limited, as the highest BMI in our cohort was 59 kg/m².

Han et al. [28] demonstrated that excess VFA measured by CT was an independent predictor of early postoperative complications after sleeve gastrectomy (SG), whereas BMI was not significant in their models. Importantly, their definition of complications included all 30-day adverse events, and the two cases of anastomotic leakage, which could be directly attributed to intraoperative challenges, occurred in the low VFA group. Other complications, such as infections and cardiopulmonary events, were more common in the high VFA group. While our study did not examine postoperative outcomes, it used intraoperative difficulty as a surrogate measure, and the relationship between technical difficulty and clinical outcomes remains to be explored in future research. The mean operation time did not differ significantly between the group with the high and low VFA, unlike in our study, where VFR was one of the predictive factors for surgery duration. The mean operation time did not differ significantly between the group with the high and low VFA, unlike in our study, where VFR was one of the predictive factors for surgery duration. However, they treated VFA as a binary variable and did not account for the specific surgeon’s mean operative time while analyzing the correlation between operation time and VFA, which hinders direct comparison between our studies.

In our analysis, patient-related factors accounted for approximately 20% of the variability in intraoperative difficulty, suggesting that factors unrelated to patient characteristics contribute more substantially to surgical complexity. Pisarska-Adamczyk et al. [29], in their study predicting intraoperative complications during sleeve gastrectomy (SG), defined intraoperative difficulty as an operative time exceeding 200 min, blood loss greater than 200 mL, or technical issues requiring conversion to laparotomy. They found that lack of surgical experience was the only significant predictor of increased intraoperative complications. Notably, their definition of prolonged surgery used a higher time threshold (over 200 min) than in our cohort, where the longest operative time was 145 min. Additionally, their study included a larger sample size (316 patients vs. 100). In contrast to their findings, in our analysis, surgeon experience level (trainee versus staff) did not emerge as a significant predictor of intraoperative difficulty after adjusting for each surgeon’s mean operative time.

There was a substantial difference in the mean operating time between specialists and surgical trainees (53.37 min vs. 89.72 min). The operating time for laparoscopic (SG) is significantly longer when the surgeon is on the learning curve for the procedure [30, 31]. This explains the difference in mean procedure duration between residents and specialists [32].

Limitations

This study was conducted at a single high-volume bariatric center, with a sample size of 100 patients over a six-month period, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other surgical settings or patient populations. Variability in surgical techniques, perioperative protocols, and patient characteristics across institutions could influence outcomes. Additionally, the sample size, while adequate for exploratory analysis, may be underpowered to detect smaller effect sizes; larger, multi-center studies are needed to validate these findings and strengthen their applicability.

Another significant limitation is that the study focused solely on intraoperative difficulty as a surrogate outcome. It did not assess whether technically challenging cases were associated with differences in clinical outcomes such as complications or recovery metrics.

Regarding body composition assessment, this study used bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) to measure visceral fat rating (VFR), rather than visceral fat area (VFA) from CT scans, which is considered a more precise measure of visceral obesity. However, CT-based VFA is expensive, involves radiation, and is not routinely performed in preoperative bariatric assessments. BIA, while less precise, is accessible, non-invasive, and widely used in clinical practice. Nevertheless, reliance on VFR may reduce the accuracy of visceral obesity assessment compared to CT-derived measurements.

We also acknowledge that factors unrelated to patient characteristics, such as the experience of anesthesiologists, the use of deep neuromuscular blockade, and the presence of a dedicated operating team, were not accounted for in this study [19, 33, 34]. While our research was conducted in a high-volume bariatric center with standardized anesthetic protocols and customized procedure packs [35], the inability to control for these external variables might have introduced potential bias. Future studies could address these factors to refine further the understanding of variables influencing intraoperative difficulties in bariatric surgery.

Additionally, we observed deviation from normality in the model residuals, as indicated by the Jarque-Bera test. Although this does not bias the estimated coefficients, it may affect the accuracy of p-values and confidence intervals, particularly given the moderate sample size. Future studies with larger cohorts or alternative model specifications could help to confirm the robustness of these findings.

Potential multicollinearity may exist between waist circumference and WHR, which were used together in the multivariate analysis model. Although pairwise correlations and variance inflation factors did not yield problematic values, the loss of statistical significance for WHR when waist circumference was included suggests that overlapping information may affect the precision of estimated effects. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the unique contribution of each measure.

Furthermore, only 61 out of 100 procedures had a completed questionnaire. Although missing data occurred randomly and were not linked to surgeon identity or case complexity, the limited response rate may reduce the generalizability of the findings. The use of a non-validated questionnaire to assess intraoperative difficulty likely limited the tool’s sensitivity, as evidenced by a narrow range of scores and clustering toward the lower end of the scale. This suggests a potential floor effect and possible variability in how individual surgeons interpreted and applied the rating scale. Surgeons may have been hesitant to label steps as “very difficult,” or their calibration of what constitutes difficulty may have differed. As a result, the subjective findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. No validated instruments currently exist for assessing intraoperative difficulty in bariatric procedures, highlighting the need for future studies to adopt or develop standardized, validated tools to ensure reliable and meaningful subjective assessments.

Conclusions

According to our analysis, BMI, VFR, WHR, and waist circumference were significant predictors of intraoperative difficulty during sleeve gastrectomy, with BMI demonstrating the strongest association. However, patient-related factors accounted for only about 20% of the variability, underscoring the multifactorial nature of surgical complexity.
