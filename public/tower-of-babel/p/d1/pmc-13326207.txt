Thiocyanate adlayer on a gold surface: an efficient strategy for enhanced electroreduction of hydrogen peroxide
Authors: Md. Fahamidul Islam, Mohebul Ahsan, Motasim Bin Islam, Md. Rezwan Miah, Debashis Sen, Kentaro Aoki, Yuki Nagao, Mohammad Afsar Uddin, Mostafizur Rahaman, Afzal Khan, Mohammad A. Hasnat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explores the electrochemical reduction of H2O2 on an Au electrode containing potassium thiocyanate (KSCN) in an alkaline medium. The presence of thiocyanate (SCN−) ions in the reaction system modifies the Au electrode surface (SCN−-modified Au) via self-assembled compact layer formation, confirmed by voltammetry, amperometry, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Acting as a ‘gatekeeper’, this adsorbed layer (abbreviated as ‘adlayer’) simultaneously overturns the oxidative degradation of H2O2 with dynamic adsorption–desorption behavior and significantly boosts the reduction reaction. Kinetic diagnosis reveals that the reaction is irreversible and diffusion-controlled, following first-order kinetics with a transfer coefficient (α) of 0.39 ± 0.02. Key analytical merits include a broad linear dynamic range spanning 50 to 2000 µM, a sensitive detection limit of 9.95 µM and operational stability with 97% signal retention. The practical utility of the SCN−-modified Au electrode is validated through successful deployment in analyzing real industrial effluents, where it achieved high recovery rates, underscoring its potential for routine environmental and industrial analysis.

Introduction

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is an extensively used chemical due to its broad application scope, yet its accurate, real-time monitoring remains a significant analytical challenge. For instance, H2O2 is used as a bleaching agent in the paper/pulp and textile industries, as a precursor for the synthesis of organic and inorganic peroxides,1 as a source of reactive hydroxyl radicals for scavenging pollutants from industrial effluent,2–4 as an antimicrobial and oxidizing agent in clinical applications,5,6 as a fuel and oxidants in fuel cells,7–9 and as a propellant.10 However, its use is not limited to an oxidizing agent and fuel; this species is also used as a signaling molecule in biological systems, where it promotes guard cell opening,11 is involved in root meristem maintenance, elongation, and lateral root development,12 affects vascular health,13 weakens body's immune response against tumor cells,14 induces oxidative stress,15 among other biological effects. Therefore, routine analysis and monitoring of H2O2 concentrations with accuracy, selectivity, and sensitivity is of great significance to ensure efficacy and safety, and maintain regulatory standards.

A number of techniques, such as titrimetry,16 fluorimetry,17 spectrophotometry,18–20 fluorescence,21–23 chemiluminescence,24,25 and colorimetry,26,27 have been developed over the years for determining the concentration of H2O2. However, the application of these methods remains inconvenient due to the complex and painstaking procedures involved and a reliance on sophisticated equipment, making them unsuitable for rapid and accurate detection. In contrast, electrochemical methods have attained great appreciation because of their sensitivity, selectivity, operational convenience, and financial viability.28,29 Generally, the electrochemical determination of H2O2 by means of conventional electrodes such as Pt, Au, and Ag requires a large overpotential. At the same time, the reduction and oxidation of H2O2 on these electrodes is kinetically slow. The combined effect of these problems may hamper the precise detection and accurate determination of H2O2. To effectively overcome these obstacles, developing simple modified electrodes or reaction systems with reasonable stability, sensitivity, and selectivity is highly indispensable. In this regard, various materials have been investigated for the electrochemical determination of H2O2. Based on the modifying protocols, the electrodes are generally classified into two broad categories: enzymatic30–32 and non-enzymatic sensors.33,34 In terms of catalytic activity, fast response time and sensitivity, enzymatic electrodes are recognized as superior candidates; however, their practical application is handicapped by rapid denaturation, low stability in variable environments, complex immobilization procedures, high overall cost and lower apparent substrate affinity.35,36 In contrast, non-enzymatic electrodes have attracted considerable attention owing to their excellent stability in different environments, good catalytic response, and superior sensitivity; metal catalysts, metal oxides, metal–organic frameworks, carbon-based materials, and composite materials have been investigated as non-enzymatic electrochemical sensors.37 While these materials have shown promising performance as electrode materials for sensing H2O2, particularly at low concentrations, many of them, such as metals, metal oxides, and carbon materials in contact with metal electrodes, tend to catalyze the rapid decomposition of H2O2.38–43 Consequently, the accurate quantification of H2O2 at relatively high concentrations remains challenging with different metal electrodes and metal-particle-modified carbon electrodes. To mitigate such catalytic decomposition, the utilization of self-assembled monolayers and potential-controlled adsorbed layers of different compounds has emerged as a viable strategy. As per previous literatures,44–46 these methods effectively suppress the catalytic decomposition of H2O2 while enhancing its electrochemical oxidation or reduction.

For performing different electrochemical reactions, the functionalization of Au electrodes with self-assembled monolayers or potential-controlled adlayers is more common than for other conventional metal electrodes, such as Ag, Cu, Pt, and Pd. The choice of Au metal as a substrate for adlayers is due to its biocompatibility, resistance to forming stable oxides under atmospheric conditions, convenient surface cleaning, and weak interactions with impurities.47–49 Along with these properties, Au forms stable and strong covalent bonds with compounds containing terminal sulfur (S) or S-containing groups like thiols (–SH).49 Yang et al.50 functionalized the surface of an Au electrode with a self-assembled monolayer of l-cysteine to detect and determine Cu(ii) ions with an LOD of 5 ppb. Similarly, Galal et al.51 developed an electrode by forming a chemisorbed adlayer of cysteine on Au nanoparticles for the detection of dopamine. Shen et al.52 also determined dopamine without the inference of uric acid by developing a self-assembled l-cysteine layer on Au(111) nanoparticles deposited on an indium tin oxide (ITO) substrate. In addition to thiol-based compounds, the formation of a monolayer on an Au surface is also possible with inorganic sulfides, and such electrodes have been applied in the development of electrochemical sensors.53–56 Another S-containing group, the thiocyanate (SCN−) ion from both inorganic and organic compounds, has been reported to form a compact monolayer on Au and Cu electrode surfaces.57–61 For example, Ahsan et al.57 modified the surface of an Au electrode by the immobilization of thiocyanate for the detection of uric acid under basic conditions.

From the preceding discourse, it is evident that adsorbed layers (adlayers) of different sulfur groups have been used to construct electrochemical sensors targeting different analytes; however, SCN−-modified Au electrodes have not been explored in depth for such purposes. Sulfur adlayers typically function as electrochemical ‘gatekeepers’, suppressing parasitic reactions like the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and H2O2 decomposition, but requiring substantial pre-desorption (e.g., at −0.93 V for S2−–Au57) to expose and activate the surface for H2O2 reduction; the SCN− adlayer reported herein exhibits a more dynamic adsorption–desorption behavior under cathodic potentials, generating active sites with surface stabilization, thereby enabling an efficient and reproducible reduction reaction. To the best of our knowledge, this work represents the first exploration of an SCN−-modified Au electrode for H2O2 reduction, where the adlayer directly facilitates the reaction process through a dynamic adsorption–desorption mechanism without necessitating a complete pre-desorption step. Moreover, the proposed in situ modification overcomes the challenge of achieving high catalytic activity without requiring complex electrode engineering or surface reactivation steps. A comprehensive suite of characterization and electrochemical techniques was utilized to obtain insights into the kinetics of H2O2 reduction by the sensor and confirm its robustness for real-world analytical applications.

Experimental

Chemicals and reagents

Analytical-grade chemicals, including sulfuric acid (H2SO4), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), and potassium thiocyanate (KSCN), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA and used without additional purification for conducting this study. All aqueous solutions were prepared with ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm).

Electrochemical experiments

Electrochemical experiments were performed using a 600 E model Electrochemical Workstation (CH Instruments, USA). A standard three-electrode system was utilized, comprising a polycrystalline Au working electrode (geometric area 2.01 × 10−2 cm2; diameter: 0.16 cm), Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl) reference electrode, and a Pt wire counter electrode. Further analysis and validation were conducted using an Autolab potentiostat (PGSTAT 128 N, Metrohm Autolab, The Netherlands) and a WaveDriver 10 (Pine Research Instrumentation, USA). For all experiments, the temperature was maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. To avoid oxygen interference, the solution was de-aerated by bubbling nitrogen gas (N2) through it for 20 minutes prior to each experiment (denoted as N2-sat).

Prior to the electrochemical experiments, the Au electrode surface was cleaned by means of mechanical polishing using a 0.06 µm alumina (Al2O3) slurry, followed by sonication in ultrapure water for 15–20 minutes to remove adsorbed species. The electrode was then electrochemically treated in 0.05 M H2SO4 by cycling the potential from −0.3 to 1.5 V (vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl)) at a scan rate of 0.1 V s−1 until a stable, characteristic Au voltammogram was obtained. The voltammetric experiments for the H2O2 reduction reaction were conducted in an N2-saturated 0.1 M NaOH solution containing 10 mM KSCN (in situ condition) at a scan rate of 0.1 V s−1. The H2O2 concentration was typically maintained at 2.0 mM. To study the effect of scan rate, it was varied from 0.005 to 1.0 V s−1. In order to evaluate the detection performance, square wave voltammograms were recorded by employing an amplitude of 0.025 V, a frequency of 15 Hz and the same potential range from 0 to −1.3 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl). Alongside the detection performance, the stability of the developed system and electrode was analyzed with a well-known batch injection method (BIM) in an amperometric mode. The applied potential for this method was selected based on the cyclic voltametric results, typically the potential nearest to the peak potential. In this regard, a Au electrode was placed in a BIM reactor with its active surface oriented downward in a vertical configuration, and the cell was filled with a 0.1 M NaOH solution containing 10 mM KSCN such that the solution level was slightly above the electrode surface. The measurements were then performed at an applied potential of −1.03 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl) for 1200 s, by injecting 100 µL aliquots of 0.1 M H2O2 at 100 s intervals onto the electrode surface using a micropipette. By utilizing cyclic voltammetry, the stability tests were also performed in a N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution containing 10 mM KSCN at a scan rate of 0.1 V s−1, and the concentration of H2O2 was 10 mM. For electrode characterization, most cyclic voltametric experiments were performed in a 0.1 M NaOH solution with 10 mM KSCN at 0.1 V s−1. For experiments with variable KSCN concentrations, it was varied from 5 to 20 mM while the scan rate was maintained at 0.1 V s−1. The open circuit potential (OCP) of the electrode was examined using a setup similar to that employed in the BIM by injecting a 10 mM KSCN solution onto the Au electrode surface. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopic (EIS) analyses were carried out in a normal electrochemical cell containing oxygen-free electrolyte (0.1 M NaOH). In this case, the frequency range was from 0.1 MHz to 0.1 Hz with 50 data points at an excitation potential of −1.03 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl). The obtained Nyquist plots were fitted using Nova software (version 2.1). The other electrochemical data were processed, plotted and visualized with SigmaPlot software (version 10 & 14). Notably, each electrochemical experiment was performed at least three times, and the error bars are reported in the corresponding figures where necessary.

Characterization

The chemical composition and surface characteristics of the SCN−-modified Au electrode were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on a Kratos Axis-Ultra spectrometer (Kratos Analytical Ltd, UK) equipped with a delay-line detector (DLD) and an Al Kα radiation source (1486.6 eV). Samples were mounted on conductive carbon tape, and all binding energies were referenced to the C 1s peak at 284.5 eV. Spectral deconvolution and background subtraction were carried out using the Shirley method in XPSPEAK 4.1 software.

Results and discussion

H2O2 electroreduction

The cyclic voltammograms (CVs) portrayed in Fig. 1 illustrate the electrochemical behavior of an Au electrode surface having multiple crystal facets (polycrystalline) in a N2-sat. solution of 0.1 M NaOH with and without 2 mM H2O2, and an oxygen-saturated (denoted as O2-sat.) solution of 0.1 M NaOH. The reduction potential sweep revealed that the Au electrode exhibited a pronounced reduction wave at approximately −0.160 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl), along with a less distinct reductive wave at around −1.03 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl), as shown in Fig. 1(A).

Cyclic voltammograms of a polycrystalline Au electrode at a scan rate of 0.1 V s−1: (A) in a solution of N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH with 2.0 mM H2O2 (blue solid), compared with O2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH (pink solid) and N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution (black dotted); (B) in a N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution with 2.0 mM H2O2 in the presence (red solid) and absence (blue solid) of 10 mM KSCN, and in a N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution with 10 mM KSCN (black dotted).

The electroreduction reaction of H2O2 on the Au surface is considered to proceed sequentially. However, it is well-established that H2O2 decomposes into molecular O2 on various metal surfaces, such as Pd, Pt, and Au.42,43,46 Consequently, the presence of an Au electrode surface in a H2O2-containing NaOH solution likely promotes the decomposition of H2O2 into O2, which may subsequently undergo reduction during the cathodic potential sweep. To confirm this, the Au electrode was used to examine the ORR in an O2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution. The findings in Fig. 1(A) clearly indicate that the peak potential and shape observed in the ORR are similar to those observed in the first reduction wave of the H2O2 reduction reaction in a N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution. Therefore, it can be inferred that the initial wave at a lower negative potential (−0.160 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl)) corresponds to the electrochemical reduction of O2 generated from the H2O2 decomposition reaction on the Au electrode surface, while the subsequent wave at a higher negative potential (−1.03 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl)) is the direct electrochemical reduction of H2O2 on the Au surface. In order to overcome this issue, the concerned reduction reaction on Au was carried out in a N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution in the presence of 10 mM KSCN. The resultant cyclic voltammogram, presented in Fig. 1(B), depicts that the initial reduction peak at −0.160 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl) is eliminated, and subsequently the reduction wave at higher negative potential, −1.03 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl), is significantly enhanced in the presence of KSCN. Thus, it is obvious that the added KSCN enhances the H2O2 electroreduction and inhibits its decomposition by blocking direct contact between the Au surface and the analyte. Under strongly cathodic potentials, when KSCN or SCN− ion is present in the electrolyte, dynamic adsorption–desorption equilibrium maintains the active site formation on the electrode surface, resulting in stable and reproducible catalytic activity. Thus, SCN− plays a dual role by suppressing parasitic reactions at lower overpotentials while enabling controlled electrocatalytic reduction at higher cathodic potentials, resulting in enhanced and more selective H2O2 detection.

Effect of scan rate

The scan-rate-dependent linear sweep or CVs of an electrode process provide crucial insights into the reaction kinetics and mechanistic aspects. Consequently, CVs were recorded for the Au electrode in a N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution containing 2.0 mM H2O2 and 10 mM KSCN at various scan rates (ν), as shown in Fig. 2(A).

Kinetic analysis of H2O2 reduction in a N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution with 10 mM KSCN at the Au electrode: (A) Scan-rate-dependent cyclic voltammograms ranging from 0.025 to 1.0 V s−1; (B) linear logarithmic plot of peak current density (jp) with respect to corresponding scan rates (ν); (C) peak difference as a function of scan rate (ν); and (D) Tafel plots (E vs. ln |j|) at scan rates of 0.025, 0.60, and 1.0 V s−1.

As the scan rate increased from 0.025 to 1.0 V s−1, the cathodic peak potential (Ep) shifted from −0.98 to −1.11 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl), indicating an irreversible electrode process. Concurrently, the peak current density (jp) increased systematically with scan rate. In connection to this fact, the peak current density in logarithmic form was plotted against the logarithmic scan rate, as illustrated in Fig. 2(B). From the linear regression plot, the slope was found to be 0.46 ± 0.03 (R2 = 0.998), consistent with eqn (1), indicating that the reduction of H2O2 on the Au electrode surface in the presence of SCN− ions in the system is governed by the diffusion of H2O2 from the bulk to the electrode surface.62,631where,

At this point, the potential difference between the peak potential (Ep) and half-peak potential was assessed at each scan, as shown in Fig. 2(C), and found to be approximately 0.086 ± 0.003 V. As this peak separation remained nearly constant over the scan rate range, it indicates that the electrochemical reduction of H2O2 in this system follows Butler–Volmer kinetics64. Thus, it is now possible to gain insight into the kinetics of the electrode reaction. In this connection, the Tafel analysis was performed in the kinetic region of the scan-rate-dependent CVs according to eqn (2).622E = E0′ + b ln(j0) − b ln(j)where E0′ is the formal potential, b (=RT/αnF) represents the Tafel slope, and j0 represents exchange current density.

The Tafel slopes obtained at scan rates of 0.025, 0.60, and 1.0 V s−1 were nearly constant, averaging 155 ± 10 mV dec−1 (Fig. 2(D)). Recalling the Tafel slope from the E vs. ln (j) plot, the value of αn was calculated to be 0.39 ± 0.02. According to previous reports,65,66 when an elementary one-electron transfer is the rate-determining step (RDS), αn typically approaches 0.5 (for n = 1) under ideal conditions. The observed α value of 0.39 ± 0.02 remains within the plausible range for a one-electron transfer RDS, provided that the barrier is asymmetric or the step is modified by adsorption/desorption phenomena. However, two parameters, E0′ and j0, are unknown in eqn (2); therefore, it is impossible to determine one parameter without knowing the other. This limitation can be overcome by employing j0 = nFCk0, which illustrates the relationship between j0 and the standard heterogeneous constant (k0). Furthermore, k0 can be calculated using eqn (3), which portrays the dependence of the rate constant on the diffusion coefficient of the analyte (for H2O2, D = 2.34 × 10−6 cm2 s−1 (ref. 67)), and the voltametric parameters (mainly the peak difference between Ep and ) of cyclic voltammogram at a scan rate, beyond which the log(jp) vs. log(ν) plot in Fig. 2(B) deviates from linearity.623

In this study, the deviation of the logarithmic plot from linearity was observed at a scan rate of 1.5 V s−1. Therefore, the magnitude of k0 at a scan rate of 1.5 V s−1was estimated to be 6.45 × 10−3 cm s−1 using eqn (3). The j0 was then calculated to be 2.49 mA cm−2 by inserting the k0 value in the relation j0 = nFCk0, where n is the total number of electrons transferred, F represents the faraday constant, and C represents the bulk concentration of the electroactive species. By employing the calculated value of j0, the formal potential for the H2O2 reduction reaction in the presence of SCN− ions was derived as −0.75 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl) from the intercept of E vs. ln (j) plot.

Sensing performance and stability

To evaluate the analytical applicability of the developed system, the H2O2 sensing performance of the Au electrode in the presence of SCN− ions was investigated using square wave voltammetry. Representative voltammograms are shown in Fig. 3(A), demonstrating the response to H2O2 concentrations ranging from 0.05 to 4.50 mM in a N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution containing 10 mM KSCN at 25 ± 1 °C.

To evaluate its performance, a calibration curve was constructed by plotting jp against H2O2 concentration (Fig. 3(B)). The electrode exhibited a linear response over the concentration range of 50 to 2000 µM, with a sensitivity of 0.365 mA cm−2 mM−1. Next, the limit of detection (LOD) was calculated to be 9.95 µM using the formula LOD = (3 × SD)/K, where SD represents the standard deviation of the current density of the square wave voltammogram in the absence of H2O2, and K is the slope of the calibration plot. The stability of the SCN−-modified Au electrode was assessed through chronoamperometric measurements (Fig. 3(C)). Successive additions of 100 µL aliquots of 0.1 M H2O2 at 100-seconds intervals while applying −1.03 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl) over 1200 s produced almost consistent current transients, with only minor deviations observed in three instances, indicating excellent operational stability and reproducible performance. Moreover, the stability of the electrode was evaluated using cyclic voltammetry with the Au electrode in a N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution containing 10 mM KSCN and 10 mM H2O2, as illustrated in Fig. S1. After 500 consecutive cyclic voltametric cycles, the Au electrode in this reaction system retained almost 97% current density compared to the initial value for the H2O2 electroreduction, while other parameters, such as the Ep and onset potential (Eo), remained essentially unaltered. It can be concluded from these findings that the developed sensing system provides reliable, accurate determination of H2O2 concentrations over long-term use.

Characterization

Electrochemical characterization

To elucidate the inhibitory role of the SCN− ions from KSCN, electrochemical techniques were employed using a polycrystalline Au electrode. Primarily, cyclic voltammetry was employed to gain insights into the electrochemical response of the Au electrode when exposed to SCN− ions. Fig. 4(A) illustrates the CVs obtained from the Au electrode immersed in a N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution, both with and without the addition of 10 mM KSCN.

Influence of KSCN on the electrochemical response of the Au electrode in N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH. CVs (0.1 V s−1) recorded: (A and B) in the presence (solid line) and absence (dotted line) of 10 mM KSCN; (C) successive CVs recorded using an Au electrode with pre-adsorbed SCN− (obtained by soaking the electrode in 0.1 M NaOH solution containing 10 mM KSCN for 10 min), followed by measurements in KSCN-free 0.1 M NaOH solution.

The Au electrode immersed in a 0.1 M NaOH solution, with no KSCN, exhibited a cathodic wave at approximately −0.15 V (C1) vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl), followed by two consecutive anodic waves between −0.2 and −0.1 V (A1) vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl), as shown in Fig. 4(A). Based on prior scholarly works, the anodic wave A1 corresponds to OH− adsorption and partial oxidation, while the cathodic wave C1 represents OH− desorption.81 In contrast, with 10 mM SCN−, the voltammogram showed two cathodic waves at −0.46 V (C2) vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl) and −1.1 V (C3) vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl), and one anodic wave at −0.452 V (A2) vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl). It is apparent that the electrode surface might get altered in the presence of SCN− ions. As per previous reports,56,57,59,60,81 Au surface facets are susceptible to potential-induced adsorption and desorption reactions. Thus, anodic wave A2 can be attributed to SCN− adsorption on various Au facets, while cathodic waves C2 and C3 correspond to desorption of the adlayer from the Au(111) and Au(110) facets (peak assignments summarized in Table S1). At this point, the Au electrode was employed for different thiocyanate concentrations to determine the effect of concentration on adlayer formation, as illustrated in Fig. 4(B). It can be seen that the A2, C2, and C3 waves remained essentially unchanged in the presence of different thiocyanate concentrations, indicating fixed adsorption sites on the polycrystalline Au surface.

Moreover, the possibility of SCN− adsorption on the surface of the Au electrode was investigated without applying an external potential. To achieve this, we immersed the Au electrode in a 0.1 M NaOH solution supplemented with 10 mM KSCN under open circuit conditions, followed by a transfer to an electrochemical cell containing a N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution. Multiple scans from 0.1 to −1.3 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl) (Fig. 4(C)) revealed desorption peaks C2 and C3 in the first cycle, confirming potential-independent SCN− adsorption. With successive scanning, desorption peaks diminished while the OH− desorption peak (C1) emerged, indicating gradual SCN− desorption into the bulk solution. After thirteen cycles, the voltammogram resembled that of the Au electrode in NaOH solution, demonstrating that maintaining SCN− in the electrolyte is essential for preserving the adlayer. Note that continuous cycling in a KSCN-free NaOH solution led to the desorption of SCN− from the Au surface into the bulk solution due to the applied potential, ultimately leaving the electrode surface free of SCN− ions.

To further validate the SCN− adsorption, the variations in OCP of the electrode were examined following the injection of 10 mM KSCN solution onto the Au electrode surface. Fig. 5(A) represents the change in the OCP of the Au electrode from the point of KSCN solution injection onto the surface of the electrode held in N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution. From the figure, it can be seen that the OCP shifts toward negative potential from the point of KSCN solution injection onto the electrode surface.

Electrochemical response of the Au electrode to KSCN injection in N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH: (A) OCP transient after adding 10 mM KSCN; and (B) chronoamperometric (j–t) response following surface injection of 25 mM KSCN at an initial potential of 0 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl).

The negative OCP shift is consistent with the accumulation of negatively charged ions on the electrode surface through electron transfer from SCN− to the Au electrode, as reported in previous literature.44,81 The subsequent gradual positive shift and eventual stabilization suggest partial desorption or reorientation of thiocyanate species. The observed negative shift in the OCP confirms the adsorption of SCN− ions on the Au surface, though the nature of the adsorption process, particularly whether it involves charge transfer, remains a subject for discussion. To justify this phenomenon, using the electrode, amperometric experiments (Fig. 5(B)) were conducted under static conditions at a zero potential following the addition of 25 mM of KSCN into the N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution. A sharp anodic current spike is formed, indicating that the adsorption process involves oxidative charge transfer.

Furthermore, EIS analysis was performed at −1.03 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl) for the Au electrode in N2-sat. 0.1 M NaOH solution with and without 10 mM KSCN. The significant drop in Rct (approximately 87%) indicates a substantial improvement in interfacial charge-transfer kinetics at the Au surface in the presence of SCN− ions. On the other hand, the marginal change in Rs can be attributed to interfacial modifications rather than bulk electrolyte conductivity. Therefore, the marked enhancement of Au's electron transfer in the presence of KSCN points to the chemisorption of SCN− ions on the electrode surface, which alters the interfacial electronic environment, thereby decreasing the charge-transfer barrier.

Concurrently, the rise in CPE along with the slight drop in N indicates an increase in interfacial capacitance and surface heterogeneity. The reduction of the N value from unity signifies a distribution of time constants, often linked to surface roughness and variations in local charge distribution.82,83 Therefore, the EIS responses collectively demonstrate that the presence of SCN− ions in the reaction system significantly influences the performance of Au in an electrochemical process, as evident in Rct reduction and CPE enhancement, which leads to faster interfacial kinetics with an electrochemically active surface.

Conclusion

The electrochemical reduction of H2O2 on Au electrodes is hindered due to catalytic decomposition upon direct contact with the metal surface. This study demonstrates that the presence of SCN− ions in the electrolyte solution effectively suppresses this parasitic decomposition, while remarkably enhancing the H2O2 reduction performance. The improvement is attributed to the chemisorption of SCN− ions, forming an adlayer on the Au surface that modifies its interfacial properties. Extensive kinetic diagnosis confirmed that the enhanced reduction process is an irreversible and diffusion-controlled reaction with an α value of 0.39 ± 0.02. The Tafel slope was determined to be 155 ± 10 mV dec−1, yielding an exchange current density (j0) of 2.49 mA cm−2. Furthermore, the standard rate constant (k0) was calculated as 6.45 × 10−3 cm s−1, and the formal potential was established at −0.75 V vs. Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl). XPS analysis corroborated the voltammetric and OCP evidence of SCN− adlayer formation on Au, revealing that the continuous presence of SCN− is essential to prevent adlayer depletion during operation. The developed sensor demonstrated excellent analytical performance, with a LOD of 9.95 µM, a wide linear dynamic range from 50 to 2000 µM, and a sensitivity of 0.365 mA cm−2 mM−1. This work presents a simple yet effective strategy for application in environmental monitoring, biomedical diagnostics, and industrial process control due to its enhanced selectivity and stability, leveraging the unique interfacial properties of the SCN−-modified Au electrode surface.
