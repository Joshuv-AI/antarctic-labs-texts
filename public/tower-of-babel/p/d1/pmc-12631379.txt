Cooling Rate Effects on Morphological, Optical, and Piezoelectric Properties of Melt-Recrystallized PVDF-TrFE Thin Films
Authors: Milan Shrestha, Abhinay Sreeram, Syed Ikhwan Bin Syed Ismail Alsagoff, Michael Renaud, Maharaja Sankaralingam, Leonardus Depari, Zhi Kai Ng, Ranjana Shivakumar, Edwin Hang Tong Teo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) and its copolymers have recently garnered significant attention for their applications in sensors, actuators, and energy-harvesting devices due to their strong piezoelectric properties, flexibility, chemical resistance, ease of processing, and optical transparency. Consequently, PVDF and its copolymers are increasingly utilized as optical piezoelectric elements in consumer electronics, human–machine interfaces (HMI), and wearable sensors and actuators. Among these materials, the copolymer PVDF-TrFE (polyvinylidene fluoride-trifluoroethylene) is particularly notable for its superior piezoelectric properties, ferroelectric stability, and thermal stability. PVDF-TrFE thin films are predominantly produced through solution casting or doctor-blading methods, followed by annealing to enhance their piezoelectric properties. However, the impact of annealing that involves melt-recrystallization and cooling rates on the piezoelectric, optical, and mechanical properties remains inadequately understood. This study explores the doctor-blading process of PVDF-TrFE using a PVDF-TrFE, Dimethylformamide (DMF), and acetone-based solution, followed by annealing above its melting temperature and cooling at varying rates. PVDF-TrFE films annealed and cooled at slower rates (1–2 °C/min) demonstrated a higher β-phase content but exhibited haziness and poor optical transparency. In contrast, films rapidly cooled by immersion in water displayed a lower β-phase content but maintained high transparency. A comprehensive analysis of the surface properties of these films, coupled with a correlation of surface roughness with a light scattering model, indicated that the optical transparency of both films was predominantly influenced by large micrometer-scale surface roughness. These findings could provide valuable insights into developing surface treatment processes to optimize both optical and piezoelectric properties in PVDF-TrFE thin films.

Background

Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) and its copolymers have recently garnered significant attention for their applications in sensors, actuators, and energy-harvesting devices due to their strong piezoelectric properties, flexibility, chemical resistance, ease of processing, and optical transparency. − Consequently, PVDF and its copolymer-based thin films are increasingly utilized as optical piezoelectric elements in consumer electronics, human–machine interfaces (HMI), and wearable sensors and actuators. Among these materials, the copolymer PVDF-TrFE (polyvinylidene fluoride-trifluoroethylene) thin film is particularly notable for its superior piezoelectric properties, ferroelectric stability, and thermal stability. −

Currently, PVDF-TrFE thin films in the range of 10–50 μm thickness are predominantly produced through solution casting or doctor-blading methods, followed by annealing to enhance their piezoelectric properties. − The impact of annealing and/or melt-recrystallization − and cooling rates − on piezoelectric/ferroelectric has been investigated, but the impact on the morphological, mechanical, as well as optical properties of these piezoelectric polymers remains inadequately understood. For instance, some researchers reported that the formation of nanometer-sized voids formed by slow cooling of the PVDF from high annealing temperature decreased the transparency of the slowly cooled PVDF. Meanwhile, some mentioned higher crystallinity of the PVDF films reduced the optical transparency due to increased light scattering from larger crystalline domains. Few studies have explored thermal treatment methods, various cooling rates, and their effects on the surface morphology of PVDF-TrFE thin films. , Yet, the effect of thermal treatments and the inter-relationship between resulting morphologies on the optical properties of PVDF-TrFE films has not been sufficiently explored. Additionally, when it comes to PVDF-TrFE, there are numerous studies linking the annealing temperature of PVDF-TrFE to the formation of various phases and its piezoelectric properties. ,− , However, the mechanism linking the macro-optical properties obtained by annealing and quenching for PVDF-TrFE is not clearly understood. ,

For numerous applications of transparent piezoelectric polymer thin films in high-end consumer electronics and automotive applications, high optical transparency is a stringent requirement. Therefore, this study focused on enhancing the optical transparency of PVDF-TrFE films by evaluating the effect of various cooling rates during annealing and melt-recrystallization on their optical and piezoelectric properties. We explored the doctor-blading process of PVDF-TrFE using a PVDF-TrFE, Dimethylformamide (DMF), and acetone-based solution, followed by annealing above its melting temperature (180 °C) and cooling at varying rates. PVDF-TrFE films annealed and cooled at slower rates (1–2 °C/min) demonstrated higher β-phase content but exhibited haziness and poor optical transparency. (Note: higher β-phase content provides better piezoelectric properties.) In contrast, films rapidly cooled by immersion in DI (deionized) water displayed lower β-phase content but maintained high transparency. A comprehensive analysis of the surface properties of these films, coupled with a correlation of surface roughness with a light scattering model, was done to evaluate the extent of the surface roughness effect on the haziness of the films. These findings could provide valuable insights for developing surface treatment processes to optimize both optical and piezoelectric properties in PVDF-TrFE thin films.

Material and Method

The PVDF-TrFE thin films are coated by a doctor-blading method, heat-treated, poled, and finally tested for optical, material, morphological, and piezoelectric properties. The PVDF-TrFE powder (PiezoTech FC 20) was purchased from Arkema Global. As shown in Figure A, 15 wt % of the powder is mixed with a solvent made of Dimethylformamide (DMF) and Acetone in a 1:1 volume ratio. The mixture is magnetically stirred at 50 °C for 3–4 h to get a clear and viscous solution. The solution is coated by using the semiautomated applicator (ZAA 2300 Zehntner-Automatic film applicator). A wet film thickness of 600 μm was coated onto the glass substrates. After drying them at 60 °C for 30 min, PVDF-TrFE thin films of 20–30 μm thickness were obtained. The films were annealed at 180 °C for 60 min, and eventually, two modes of cooling were used. First, PVDF-TrFE films were annealed and then cooled at slower rates (1–2 °C/min) (termed as annealed films); and second, the films were rapidly cooled by immersion in DI water at room temperature (termed as Quenched films, refer Figure B).

Schematics showing PVDF-TrFE ink: (A) preparation; (B) film fabrication, annealing, and quenching process. (C) The corona poling setup was used to pole the PVDF-TrFE films. (D) A piezometer and ferroelectric analyzer setup was used to evaluate the piezoelectric property of the PVDF-TrFE films.

As shown in Figure C, the PVDF-TrFE films were polarized using the corona poling method using a corona poling bar from Green Techno. The poling was done at a 100 °C substrate temperature on a hot plate using the voltage of 20 kV with the distance between the corona bar’s needle and PVDF-TrFE films of 2–3 cm for 5 min. The temperature was gradually reduced to 50 °C while poling within approximately 5 min. Afterward, the piezoelectric properties like d 33 were measured using a Piezometer from PolyK Technologies, LLC, and the hysteresis loop was measured using a ferroelectric analyzer from PolyK Technologies, LLC as well. The optical transparency was measured using a UV–vis spectrometer (Shimadzu UV 2700), and surface roughness was evaluated using a laser confocal microscope (Keyence VK-X3000 Series 3D Surface Profiler). Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) spectrum for the films was obtained using PerkinElmer Frontier FT-IR Spectrometers, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were taken using a Bruker D2 Phaser XE-T.

Results and Discussions

Heat treatment of PVDF-TrFE thin films affects their surface morphology, optical transparency, piezoelectric properties, and mechanical properties. Studies have shown that PVDF-TrFE films, when cast and annealed between their Curie transition temperature and melting temperature, promote the formation of a low-temperature ferroelectric β-phase. ,− , However, such films are usually hazy in appearance. However, if the polymer is melt-recrystallized, the ferroelectric and optical properties can be altered through controlled cooling. − This study shows that controlled cooling through melt-recrystallization can drastically affect surface morphology and hence optical properties, specifically for PVDF-TrFE thin films.

Surface Morphology and Optical Properties

The PVDF-TrFE solution was coated, dried, and annealed at 180 °C, which is sufficiently above its melting temperature. Afterward, two extreme cooling rates of the annealed thin films were tested to evaluate their influence on the surface morphology and the optical transparency of the films. First, PVDF-TrFE thin films were annealed and then cooled at slower rates (1–2 °C/min). Second, the PVDF-TrFE films were rapidly cooled by immersion in DI water at room temperature. As shown in Figure A, the films that were rapidly quenched were highly transparent, with the image behind the films being clearly visible. However, the films that were gradually cooled turned hazy, as seen in Figure B. The surface of the corresponding films was traced by using 3D confocal microscopes and SEM imaging. As seen in Figure C–E, the quenched PVDF-TrFE thin film has a smooth surface with root-mean-square (rms) roughness of 7 nm measured using the confocal microscope (see Figure C). Meanwhile, the slow-cooled PVDF-TrFE thin films have micrometer-sized bumps with an RMS roughness of 75 nm (see Figure D–F).

Optical and surface characterization of PVDF films after either rapid quenching or slow cooling processes. (A,B) Photos showing the optical transparency or Haze of the films; (C,D) 3D confocal images showing the surface roughness of the films and the corresponding optical transmittance of the films that was measured using a UV–vis spectrometer and compared with a prediction from a theoretical light scattering model; (E,F) SEM images of the quenched and slow-cooled PVDF-TrFE thin films.

The smoother surface formation in quenched PVDF-TrFE films (see Figure C–E) is due to its higher amorphous and α-phase fractions. The XRD data in Figure B indicate that the melt-recrystallized PVDF-TrFE formed by quenching has a higher amorphous fraction and α-phase content compared to the slow-cooled films. The XRD absorbance plots were deconvoluted to obtain the total crystallinity (see Supplementary Figure S1) of each film and found about 56% crystallinity for quenched PVDF-TrFE films, while the annealed films had a higher crystallinity of 66%. This observation is consistent with previous studies on annealing with various cooling rates of PVDF films and PVDF-TrFE films. , Moreover, higher α-phase fractions arise from the less ordered molecular structure of the TGTG’ conformation in the α-phase, which leads to smaller and more evenly distributed crystallites. , Additionally, the α-phase in itself has lower crystallinity compared to the β-phase. Therefore, in the quenched films, the higher overall amorphous content reduces grain boundaries and allows for more uniform shrinkage or relaxation of the films, which consequently prevents the formation of micrometer-scale surface roughness. These characteristics make films with dominant α-phases and amorphous regions inherently smoother.

On the contrary, larger microroughness (micrometer-sized surface bumps) is generated on the surface of annealed (i.e., slow-cooled during melt-recrystallization) PVDF-TrFE thin films. This occurs due to various contributing factors, namely, higher β-phase content with larger crystal sizes and higher crystallinity that arise from the highly ordered all-trans (TTTT) conformation of the beta phase. As seen in Figure F, the slow-cooled thin films consist of micron-scale spherulites with an average diameter of 6–8 μm made of lamellae crystal structures of 50–200 nm width, consistent with reports in literature. These structures are nucleated from numerous sites and promote the formation of multiple large, well-defined, rigid crystalline domains with softer amorphous boundaries, which eventually give rise to humps and valleys.

The micrometer-scale surface roughness of the PVDF-TrFE films is likely the primary contributor to the light scattering and consequently haziness, particularly in thin films. Analysis of the XRD absorbance spectra in Figure B indicates that both types of PVDF-TrFE films possess crystallite sizes in the range of 8–12 nm, which are significantly smaller than the wavelength of visible light (∼400–700 nm). Additionally, the refractive index contrast between crystalline and amorphous phases of the PVDF-TrFE is relatively low (Δn ≈ 0.04) compared to the much larger mismatch at the film–air interface (Δn ≈ 0.5). Therefore, for PVDF-TrFE films with thicknesses in the tens of micrometers, the bulk light scattering via Rayleigh and Mie scattering effects due to internal phase inhomogeneities is minimal. However, as the thickness of the films increased to 100 s of microns, the bulk scattering effect becomes more significant.

In the case of slow-cooled PVDF-TrFE thin films, the development of spherulitic structures induces micrometer-scale surface roughening. This postcrystallization roughening significantly increases the optical haze of the films, as evident in Figure B. The observed haze is hypothesized to originate predominantly from this micron-scale surface roughness, rather than from internal phase scattering. Due to the substantial refractive index mismatch (Δn ≈ 0.5) between air and the polymer, surface features become highly effective in scattering incident light. According to the Rayleigh-Rice surface scattering theory, the intensity of scattered light scales with the square of the surface roughness (σ2) and the square of the refractive index contrast (Δn 2). Thus, micron-scale surface topography plays a critical role in degrading optical clarity. These surface microroughness (σ) could be correlated to the optical transparency using surface light scattering models. − Based on these models, the specular transmission (T spec) though one rough surface is given by 1 Tspec=T×exp{−[2πσλ(n1cos⁡θi−n2cos⁡θs)]2} Here, T is the total transmission given by the Fresnel equation for normal incidence as, T=1−|n1−n2n1+n2|2 , here n 1 = 1 is the refractive index of the air and n 2 = 1.4 is the refractive index of the PVDF-TrFE, λ is the wavelength of light, θ i and θs are the incident and the scattered angles, and σ is the rms roughness of the surface. It was found that while casting the films, the exposed surface’s roughness was dependent on the cooling rates. However, despite any cooling rates, the film’s surface in contact with the glass substrate was as smooth as the glass. Therefore, we revised the surface scattering model to mimic light transmission through one rough and the other smooth surface as 2 Tspec=T2×exp{−[2πσλ(n1cos⁡θi−n2cos⁡θs)]2}

Under normal incidence and specular transmission conditions, both the θ i and θs angles are 0°. As shown in Figure E,F, the quenched films exhibit a higher transmittance exceeding 95% at a 550 nm wavelength, whereas the slow-cooled films show a reduced transmittance slightly above 80%. The close agreement between the theoretical predictions and the experimentally measured specular transmittance supports the hypothesis that in thin films optical transmittance is primarily governed by surface microroughness. The substantial micrometer-scale surface roughness observed in slow-cooled films is the dominant factor contributing to the reduction in transparency.

These results provide valuable guidance for optimizing both the optical and piezoelectric properties of PVDF-TrFE films via surface treatment techniques. Methods such as surface machining, etching, application of refractive index-matching coatings, or lamination with optically clear adhesive (OCA) can effectively reduce surface roughness or mitigate its impact, thereby eliminating the hazy appearance observed in annealed slow-cooled PVDF-TrFE thin films. This leads to enhanced optical clarity alongside optimal ferroelectric performance. For example, as illustrated in Figure , the haziness caused by surface roughness was significantly reduced following lamination with an acrylic-based OCA on the rougher side of the slow-cooled film. The OCA lamination performed at room temperature does not alter the inherent roughness of the PVDF-TrFE films; instead, the compliant acrylic adhesive fills surface irregularities and the close refractive index match suppresses surface scattering of light, resulting in the disappearance of haze. The laminated films exhibit slightly lower transparency (reduced by 1–2% compared to quenched films) due to absorption from the additional OCA layer. This finding further confirms that, in thin films, surface scattering dominates over bulk scattering of light.

Piezoelectric Properties

The ferroelectric and piezoelectric properties of PVDT-TrFE depend on the crystallinity and phase contents of the films. Usually, it consists of ferroelectric β-phase, paraelectric α-phase, semipolar γ-phase, and amorphous phases. The PVDF-TrFE films prepared in this study mostly contained β-, α-, and amorphous contents. The β-phase is the polar phase with an all-trans (TTTT) chain conformation, which leads to highly polar crystals with permanent dipoles. It is characterized by FTIR peaks at 844 cm–1 and 1279 cm–1 (see Figure A) and XRD peaks at around 20.4°–20.6° (2θ) (see Figure B). , Similarly, the α-phase is the nonpolar phase with TGTG′ chain conformation, characterized by FTIR peaks at 746 cm–1 and 976 cm–1 (see Figure A) and XRD peaks at around 18.5°–20° (2θ) (see Figure B). A higher β-phase content in PVDF-TrFE (poly­(vinylidene fluoride)-trifluoroethylene) is preferred due to its higher polar alignments that enhance the piezoelectric properties, making it ideal for sensors and actuators applications.

This study showed that annealed and slowly cooled PVDF-TrFE have a higher β-phase content compared to quenched PVDF-TrFE. The β-phase fraction (F (β)) was calculated using the FTIR absorbance in Figure A using the Lambert–Beer Law in eq 3 F(β)=Aβ(KβKα)Aα+Aβ where A β and A α are the absorbances at 844 cm–1 and 764 cm–1; K β and K α represent absorption coefficients at the respective wavenumbers. The controlled and gradual crystallization process of the slow-cooled films had a F (β) of nearly 92%. As the β-phase is kinetically more stable compared to the α-phase, the slow cooling enables the polymer chains to align in a more orderly manner, promoting the formation of the β-phase. However, the α-phase is thermodynamically more stable compared to the β-phase. Hence, the rapid quenching tends to lock the polymer chains in a less ordered α-phase and amorphous phases. As such, the F (β) of quenched films dropped to 86%.

The variation in the β-phase content of the quenched and slow-cooled PVDF-TrFE films is the cause of the difference in their piezoelectric properties. This difference is confirmed by the hysteresis plot in Figure D,E, which shows annealed and slow-cooled PVDF-TrFE has a higher remanent polarization of about 11 μC/cm2, while quenched PVDF-TrFE merely has a remanent polarization of 5 μC/cm2 . Additionally, Figure C also shows that annealed and slow-cooled PVDF-TrFE has a higher d 33 of ∼20 pC/N while quenched PVDF-TrFE only has a d 33 of 14 pC/N. Therefore, we can conclude that annealed-slow-cooled PVDF-TrFE has better piezoelectric properties compared to quenched PVDF-TrFE owing to its comparatively higher β-phase fraction. The OCA lamination performed at room temperature does not alter the inherent crystallinity or β-phase content of the films. However, it can widely vary the sensitivity of the piezoelectric sensor depending on choice of the OCA and sensor design. In our case, the piezoelectric properties (d 33) decrease by approximately 11–12% as the OCA material is dielectric and does not contribute to the piezoelectric effect. Notably, the d 33 reduction due to strain dampening depends on the thickness, stiffness, and viscoelastic properties of the OCA layer. When a sufficiently thin and relatively stiffer OCA is used, the decrease in piezoelectric performance can become negligible. Additionally, the PVDF-TrFE/OCA laminate can be sandwiched between electrodes using a generally used electrode coating or layering approach to operate as a normal transducer.

Figure C shows that as the operating temperature of the films is increased, the slow-cooled film’s d 33 drifts to a higher value while the quenched film’s d 33 remains consistent. This trend can be attributed to two reinforcing mechanisms documented in prior studies. Hafner et al. reported that remanent polarization (Pr) remains largely stable while the Young’s modulus decreases substantially as the polymer warms. A lower modulus means the material is more mechanically compliant, so for the same applied stress, it produces more strain. Since d33 represents the amount of electric charge generated per unit of mechanical force in the thickness direction, this increased compliance naturally boosts its value. Complementing this, Toprak & Tigli observed that the coercive field diminishes with increasing temperature, facilitating easier dipole reorientation. The combined effect of mechanical softening and enhanced dipole mobility boosts both the material’s polarization response and its mechanical strain output, leading to the steady rise in the measured d 33 at these moderately elevated temperatures. The tensile test results in Figure show that the slow-cooled films were almost twice as stiff as the quenched films. This is due to the higher crystallinity and tightly packed molecular chains in the slow-cooled films that resist deformation more effectively. As such, the softening effect could be more pronounced in the slow-cooled films due to their much higher initial stiffness, leading to an apparent increase in d 33. Second, the higher fraction of ferroelectric phase in the slow-cooled films could add to more effective dipole alignment at the elevated temperature, leading to an apparent increase in d 33. Figure F shows that the d 33 values were measured at room temperature after exposing the films to a gradually increased temperature for 1 h. For both types of PVDF-TrFE films, d 33 gradually drops after long exposure to temperatures above 80 °C, indicating that the materials start to depole at such high temperatures.

Conclusion

This study investigated the doctor-blading method of PVDF-TrFE thin films using a PVDF-TrFE, Dimethylformamide (DMF), and acetone-based solution, followed by annealing above the melting temperature and cooling at different rates. Melt-recrystallized films that are cooled slowly (1–2 °C/min) exhibited higher β-phase content but showed haziness and poor optical transparency, whereas films rapidly cooled in iced water had lower β-phase content but retained high transparency. Analysis of surface properties and the correlation between surface roughness and a light scattering model revealed that optical transparency was primarily influenced by micrometer-scale surface roughness. The reduced transparency of slow-cooled films is directly attributed to their increased surface roughness in the microscale and indirectly attributed to the crystallinity and grain sizes. These results offer guidance for optimizing both the optical and piezoelectric properties of PVDF-TrFE films through surface treatment processes. Techniques such as surface machining, etching, applying refractive index-matching coatings, or laminating with OCA can effectively reduce surface roughness and eliminate the hazy appearance of annealed slow-cooled PVDF-TrFE films, giving optimal optical and ferroelectric properties.
