Dataset of Potential Tsunami Scenarios in the South China Sea
Authors: Guangsheng Zhao, Xiaojing Niu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe South China Sea is surrounded by many large cities with developed economy and dense population along the coast, where tsunamis are major potential disasters within the sea area. Therefore, a dataset and comprehensive assessment of potential tsunami scenarios are an important foundation for design of coastal and ocean engineering, disaster prevention and mitigation strategy. Based on historical seismic records and geodetic observation data, this study integrates ocean dynamic model, the fault coupling inversion and source uncertainty analysis, to construct a potential tsunami scenario dataset in the South China Sea. The scenario dataset covers all possibilities of tsunamis caused by earthquakes with Mw ≥ 7. Both tsunami height and period have been systematically provided, which has not been given by existing researches. The dataset allows users to obtain the joint probability distribution of tsunami wave height and period at any location of interest in the South China Sea, providing key parameters for hazard prevention and mitigation.Subject terms: Natural hazards, Physical oceanography

Background & Summary

The 2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami and the 2011 Tohoku Tsunami prompted extensive researches on mitigating tsunami hazard worldwide. Probabilistic tsunami hazard assessment (PTHA) has gradually become the preferred method for quantifying tsunami hazard to provide the relationship between hazard level and likelihood1,2. The South China Sea is affected by differences in tectonic environment and tsunami sources, resulting in significant spatial variations in tsunami hazard distribution3–6. There have been studies on tsunami hazard in specific regions7,8. Considering the high population density and developed economy along the coastal areas, as well as the numerous islands, reefs and offshore projects distributed in the sea area, there is still a lack of tsunami hazard assessment for the entire South China Sea under a unified assessment framework, especially the joint distribution pattern of tsunami related hazard factors is not clear.

With the accumulation of long-term observation data, including records of historical earthquakes and tsunamis, observations of global plate movements, it becomes possible to estimate tsunami hazard in the future. Because the South China Sea is fairly enclosed basin that does not allow effective tsunami propagation from outside9, the Manila subduction zone within the South China Sea is the most likely source area for large earthquakes and tsunamis. In recent years, more and more evidence of paleotsunami event has gradually been discovered10–13, indicating that a destructive tsunami originating from Manila subduction zone was likely encountered in the South China Sea about 1000 years ago. Existing researches have estimated the seismic potential of the Manila subduction zone14–17, but due to limited historical event catalogues, there is still a high degree of uncertainty regarding the magnitude of large earthquake events and its relationship with frequency. With the rapid development of Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS), geodesy has made great process in the past few decades. The continuous accumulation of global observation data has improved our understanding of plate movements and energy accumulation at plate boundaries. The seismic potential is closely related to the cumulative rate of seismic moments and can be estimated through the geodetic coupling model18–21. However, the occurrence of earthquake events still has strong randomness. In addition to magnitude, source parameters such as epicenter, depth, fault geometry and slip distribution also exhibit strong randomness. With the same magnitude, variations in other source parameters have significant impacts on tsunami hazard. Considering too many random source scenarios with extremely low probability will bring huge computational burden, therefore it is necessary to limit the randomness and uncertainty of tsunami sources, and establish a reasonable set of potential tsunami scenarios as input conditions for tsunami hazard assessment system.

At present, the output results of tsunami hazard assessment research are still mainly based on the single hazard factor, which is the wave height, and the joint distribution law of tsunami wave height and period has not been systematically revealed. The wave height and period together determine the dynamic characteristics of tsunami waves, which are important parameters for predicting the tsunami inundation, tsunami impact frequency and resonance effects. The period of tsunami waves is closely related to the tsunami source, and is influenced by the geometric size of faults and the water depth in the source area22,23. It is dominated by low frequencies and also has multi-scale fluctuations excited by the heterogeneity of slip distribution. After entering the shallow water area of the continental shelf, tsunami waves are affected by the bathymetry at the same time, and the main period range and the leading wave period may undergo significant changes. In hazard assessment based on large-scale tsunami scenarios, it is often impossible to achieve high-precision simulation of tsunami inundation. In this case, the tsunami wave height and period at offshore locations are effective boundary conditions for predicting nearshore tsunami hazard24.

The dataset of potential tsunami scenarios in the South China Sea starts with the establishment of a set of all possible tsunami scenarios based on the integration and reorganization of existing public data. With the relatively mature tsunami simulation technology currently available, and to facilitate the practical application of dataset, the study presents statistical analysis results of tsunami hazard in the South China Sea obtained through simulations based on potential tsunami scenarios. The dataset provides the tsunami hazard level at target points with a resolution of 0.1° for the entire South China Sea, including tsunami heights with different recurrence periods. The dataset also stores statistical results of tsunami wave height and period at each target point, which is convenient for querying the joint probability distribution of tsunami wave height and period at any location in the South China Sea, studying the influence of tsunami source and propagation path on wave height and period characteristics, and providing design parameters for research and hazard prevention and reduction in the area of interest.

Methods

Basic data source

The South China Sea is characterized by frequent earthquakes but a lack of tsunami records in recent centuries. The study first collects the historical earthquake data from the earthquake hazard database of the United States Geological Survey (https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/earthquakes). From 1900 to 2024, there are a total of 4405 recorded earthquakes in the Manila subduction zone (119.0~123.0°E, 13.0~22.0°N) with magnitude larger than 4.5, including 31 earthquakes of magnitude 7 or above, with the largest recorded historical magnitude being 7.7. Historical earthquake records are effective data for analyzing the distribution characteristics of source parameters, but due to the insufficiency of historical earthquake records, other data support is still needed.

The surface GPS horizontal velocity field data is used to invert plate movement and fault coupling through geodetic model. In the past few decades, a large amount of long-term observation data has been accumulated globally. The global GPS velocity field data collected by Kreemer et al.25, all within the ITRF08 reference framework, is selected for inversion. The coupling distribution can be used to estimate the static seismic moment accumulation rate, and then estimate the seismic potential and maximum possible magnitude. Based on all basic data, potential earthquake and tsunami scenarios can be reasonably constructed.

Potential tsunami scenario set

To conduct a tsunami hazard assessment, the largest earthquake magnitude in the tsunami source area should be determined first. The main source of tsunami in the South China Sea is the Manila subduction zone, and its three-dimensional geometric structure can be obtained from the Slab2.0 dataset26. The earthquake scenarios are assumed to only occur on the Manila subduction interface, which is divided into grids with a length and width of 0.1°, consisting of 90 × 20 = 1800 sub faults. The latitude, longitude, depth, strike angle and dip angle of each sub fault are obtained by interpolation from Slab2.0 data.

The study uses the TDEFNODE model to invert GPS surface horizontal velocity, obtaining fault coupling and slip deficit rate, and then estimating the cumulative seismic moment rate seismic potential. The main plates in the research area are Sunda Plate, the Philippine Sea Plate and the Philippine mobile belt formed by their convergence. The Manila subduction zone is divided into two sections as there are differences between the north and south part in terms of fault geometry, plate properties and seismic activity, and recent researches suggest that the Manila subduction zone is unlikely to rupture from north to south in one event16,27,28. Referring to Hsu et al.15, the initial values of Euler poles for all plates are provided for inversion. The global GPS velocity field data collected by Kreemer et al.25 is selected for inversion. The detailed information of the model is presented in the published paper17. In this study, the inversion results are used, which show that the moment magnitude with 1000-year recurrence period in the southern section of the Manila subduction zone (13.0~14.5°N) is 8.1 and the moment magnitude with 1000-year recurrence period in the northern section (14.5~22.0°N) is 9.1.

Assuming that earthquakes all occur on the subduction interface, using Slab2.0 data to establish the geometric structure of subduction interface, the depth, strike angle and dip angle of sub faults are related to the location of earthquake source. The independent source parameters are magnitude, epicenter and slip distribution. In order to ensure that the rupture area of an earthquake does not exceed the range of subduction interface, the possible epicenter positions are determined based on the fault size at different magnitude, as shown in Table 1. There are a total of 20153 possible combinations of magnitude and epicenter. The probability of magnitude and epicenter occurrence is estimated based on statistical analysis of historical earthquake catalogues. The magnitude is distributed using the truncated G-R distribution29. For each magnitude, the length and width of fault are calculated by the scaling law of Blaser et al.30, and possible epicenter positions are divided on the subduction interface at intervals of 0.1°. The probability of each epicenter is also obtained through historical earthquake distribution statistics.

Fault slip has strong randomness, but is also subject to certain constraints31,32. Early tsunami simulation typically assumed that fault slip was uniform, but recently scientists generally realize that the slip of large earthquakes has significant spatial variability33,34. On the basis of random slip model, this study further generates heterogeneous slip distribution constrained by the geodetic coupling model. Based on the Code for Earthquake Rupture and ground-motion Simulation (CERS) of Mai and Beroza35, modifications were made to the slip calculation part, using the coupling distribution obtained from seismic potential assessment. CERS has been validated and widely applied36. In this model, slip is simulated as a spatial random field, and the slip distribution follows the von Karman autocorrelation function. The final slip distribution consists of two parts: one is the random slip calculated through the spatial random field model, and the other is the deterministic slip uniformly distributed on the rupture, whose value is the average slip calculated based on the scaling law. After two parts are superimposed, the slip will be further scaled to meet the total seismic moment. In order to consider the influence of geodetic coupling, the random slip part is not changed, while the deterministic part is rescaled based on the coupling distribution. The originally uniformly distributed slip is proportionally distributed to have the same characteristic as the coupling distribution. Subsequently, the new deterministic part is superimposed on the random part to obtain the final heterogeneous slip distribution. The seismic moment of the synthesized slip matches the seismic moment estimated based on the magnitude. In the synthesized slip distribution, high coupling areas have a greater probability of generating large slip, and on average, the spatial distribution of slip is the same as coupling distribution. The uncertainty of rupture size is also considered while generating random slip, and for the same magnitude, the uncertainty of rupture length and width is achieved through random sampling with a certain range.

Using the modified heterogeneous slip model, a large number of potential earthquake tsunami scenarios can be generated after determining the source parameters such as magnitude. For each combination of magnitude and epicenter, 100 scenarios with randomly generated heterogeneous slip distribution are generated with the same probability. A total of 2015300 potential tsunami scenarios are generated and their probability of occurrence is calculated, forming a huge scenario set.

Efficient tsunami simulation and analysis method

The existing tsunami simulation technology is developed and can be implemented in various ways. However, the direct simulation of a large number of potential tsunami scenarios is very expensive. In order to reduce the computational burden, the linear reconstruction technique with unit water surface disturbance sources is adopted. Based on the linear assumption of waves in deep water, the process of tsunami waves is given by superimposing waves generated by water level disturbance of unit point sources, thus analyzing infinite tsunami scenarios using the propagation of finite unit sources. A two-dimensional Gaussian-shaped distributed water field is set as initial water level disturbance on each unit source, and the distribution of its center is shown in Fig. 1a. The Gaussian distribution is featured with symmetry and smoothness, which can effectively approximate complex initial water level field. The unit water surface disturbance sources are independent on the fault geometry and mechanism, and similar type of unit source has also been applied for fast evaluation of tsunami scenarios37–39. The standard deviation of the Gaussian-shaped distribution is taken as 0.05°40. The resulting horizontal scale of the unit water surface distribution source is greater than 0.1°, which basically satisfies the shallow water assumption in this region. To simulate the spatial variability of slip distribution and generate the initial water surface field, the Manila subduction zone is divided into many sub faults. This method is widely used for simulating non-uniform slip distribution41–43. For earthquake tsunami scenario with any combination of source parameters, the Okada model44 is used to calculate the initial water level field of each sub fault. The initial water level field generated by sub faults are approximated by the linear superposition of the initial water level disturbance of unit sources. The proportional coefficients of each unit source are calculated by solving a system of linear equations, and the propagation process of a tsunami event are generated by superimposing the propagation process of unit sources with the same proportional coefficients.

Research area, including unit source layout (orange dots) and observation point layout (blue dots).

First, it is necessary to established a unit source database. The simulation of unit source is implemented using the ocean hydrodynamic model FVCOM45, which can be parallelized on high-performance computers to simulate a large number of unit source scenarios. FVCOM is also used to the direct simulation of wave propagations by solving shallow water equations in spherical coordinates. The arrangement of unit sources needs to cover the tsunami source area, and the density of unit sources needs to be high to ensure the accuracy of the approximate tsunami propagation results. Considering the large range of initial water field caused by large tsunami events, the distribution range of unit sources should be larger than the source area. Therefore, unit sources with interval of 0.1° are uniformly arranged in the (116.5°~123.5°E, 12.0°~23.0°N), as shown in Fig. 1a. The orange dashed line represents the range of Manila subduction zone, and the orange points are unit sources. Excluding the onshore area, there are a total of 7838 unit sources.

In this study, unstructured grids are used for modelling the South China Sea and surrounding areas. The average spatial resolution of the grids is around 2.0 arc min, with a maximum resolution of 2.5 arc min and a minimum resolution of 1.0 arc min. Due to the fact that unit source technology is based on linear assumption, the study selected observation points with a water depth greater than 50 m to output the simulation results. The observation points have an interval of 0.1°, with a total of 23754 observation points covering the entire South China Sea and surrounding areas, as shown in Fig. 1b. The simulation results will provide basic data and boundary conditions for tsunami hazard assessment and risk management in any area of the South China Sea.

Data Records

The dataset of potential tsunami scenarios in the South China Sea obtained in the current study was uploaded to the figshare repository46, which includes many Microsoft ExcelTM worksheets and a Matlab script. The first Microsoft ExcelTM worksheet, titled (Target Point Information), consists of 23755 rows and 3 columns. Each row describes the serial number and longitude and latitude information of a target point. A total of 23754 target points cover the South China Sea and surrounding areas, serving as the foundational information for other documents.

The second Microsoft ExcelTM worksheet, titled (Tsunami Wave Height Assessment), stores estimated tsunami wave heights at all target points with different recurrence periods, consisting of 23755 rows and 11 columns. Each row corresponds to a target point, with the first column being the serial number of target points, and the second to eleventh columns being the tsunami wave heights corresponding to the recurrence periods of 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000 and 10000 years. Results are shown in Fig. 2, depicting the spatial distribution of tsunami hazard in the South China Sea. It is noted that tsunami heights for 10–100 years are below 1 m, which are not shown and visually similar to that of 100 years. And the hazard curve indicating the relationship between tsunami wave height and recurrence period is shown in Fig. 3a, where the data markers represent the recurrence periods calculated based on the certain tsunami heights using the PTHA method.

Spatial distribution of tsunami height with recurrence periods from 100 to 10000 years.

Hazard curve and joint probability density distribution of tsunami wave height and period for specific target point.

The third file uploaded to the figshare repository is a compressed file titled (Joint Probability Distribution of Wave Height and Period), which contains 23754 Microsoft ExcelTM worksheets corresponding to 23754 target points. The data format in each worksheet is consistent, consisting of 152 rows and 102 columns. The first rows is the tile row, corresponding to the tsunami wave period increasing from 0 to 400 min with an interval of 4 min. The first column is the title column, corresponding to the tsunami wave height increasing from 0 to 15 m with an interval of 0.1 m. Any cell in the table, except for the title row and title column, represents the probability that the tsunami wave heights at this target point are greater than or equal to the value in that row, less than the value in the next row, and the tsunami wave periods are greater than the value in that column but less than the value in the next column, in all potential tsunami scenarios. The total probability of all cells is 1. The data from all cells of one target point is plotted as shown in Fig. 3b, which represents the joint probability density distribution of tsunami wave height and period.

The Matlab script titled (pointLocatorApp) aims to help users navigate the dataset and find the required data from the Dataset of Potential Tsunami Scenarios in the South China Sea. This script will draw a graphical interface that allows users to input coordinates or select desired points on the map to easily visualize the corresponding PTHA results. To use this script, all files in the dataset need to be downloaded to the same directory and the compressed file needs to be decompressed. After running the script, users can input coordinates or select desired points on the map, and a visual PTHA results as shown in Fig. 3 will be generated.

Technical Validation

The validation of the seismic potential assessment results in this study is presented in the published paper17. FVCOM, which is used to simulate the propagation process of tsunami waves, has been widely applied to many problems, including coastal ocean, estuarine circulation and tsunami simulation, and has been fully validated through analysis, laboratory and field benchmark testing45,47–49. The potential scenario set is reasonable and representative, as the inversed plate movement and coupling of specific areas reflect the local geological characteristics well, and the estimated maximum possible magnitude is highly consistent with other researches. For example, the long-term subduction rate of the Manila trench is 90–100 mm/a at the northwest tip of Luzon15,17,50, and the maximum possible magnitude for 500–1000 years is estimated to be 8.9–9.1, which is in good agreement with the estimates of 8.8–9.2 in other studies15–17.

What needs to be verified is the applicability of efficient simulation methods based on unit sources in the South China Sea. Assuming four hypothetical earthquake tsunami scenarios, including large and small magnitude examples. The magnitudes are 9.1, 8.0, 7.5 and 7.0, respectively. The parameters of sub faults are obtained from Slab2.0, and the slip distribution is randomly generated. Selecting an observation point on the continental shelf along the southeast coast of China (114.69°E, 20.78°N) and one on the Xuande Atoll in the central part of the South China Sea (112.39°E, 16.91°N) which has a relatively complex bathymetry environment, the linear approximation results of efficient methods are compared with results of direct simulation, as shown in Fig. 4.

Comparison of direct simulation and linear approximation results at two observation points in scenarios of magnitude 9.1, 8.0, 7.5 and 7.0.

There is a high degree of consistency between direct simulation and linear approximation results for all large and small magnitude scenarios, especially in the leading tsunami wave components, including the leading and largest tsunami waves. Due to the observation points located in deep water, tsunami waves are less affected by nearshore terrain, and the leading wave of tsunami is usually the largest wave, making it our main focus. The tail wave of continuous oscillation that is not accurately simulated by linear methods is not the focus of this study. Comparative analysis shows that the efficient simulation method based on unit source linear approximation is accurate and effective, and is suitable for the area of concern in this study. Due to the high randomness of tsunami source, which manifests as the randomness of rupture size and slip distribution in this study, the set of potential tsunami scenarios cannot cover all possible random scenarios. However, the current size of the scenarios set is sufficient to obtain stable statistical characteristics of tsunami hazard and stable spatial distribution of tsunami hazard, to construct this dataset.

The dataset provides PTHA results for the South China Sea and is also compared with previous studies. Taking the observation point of Hong Kong as an example, the tsunami heights with 500 and 1000 year recurrence period in this study are 1.77 and 2.63 m, respectively. In contrast, the tsunami heights with 500 and 1000 year recurrence period are 0.68~1.80 and 1.00~3.11 m in study of Li et al.43. The evaluation results of this study are similar to those of Li et al. based on the geodetic catalogue, which is considered the upper bound of tsunami hazard level. The methods of constructing earthquake catalogues and tsunami modelling in this study are different from the research of Li et al. The general relationship of earthquake magnitude and recurrence period is constructed based on the historical earthquake catalogues, and the maximum possible magnitude and corresponding recurrence period are estimated through geodetic methods. In addition, the results of geodesy have also been used to simulate the heterogeneous slip distribution. At the same time, due to the simulation of a larger number of potential tsunami scenarios, the hazard curves obtained in this study exhibit better continuity.

In summary, current research still has limitations and requires future investigations. Firstly, it should be noted that the dataset only focuses on the major tsunami source from earthquakes in the Manila subduction zone, while intraplate earthquakes near coast areas and landslides on the continental slope are not considered due to insufficient historical records to determine stable source parameters and recurrence rates. Secondly, although only tsunami scenarios caused by earthquakes with magnitude above M7.0 is considered in this study, the recurrence period is estimated based on the G-R relationship, which is widely recognized in the field of seismology. A larger range of earthquake magnitudes (greater than M4.5) is applied for the fitting of the G-R relationship to obtain relatively stable statistical parameters. The earthquake data used is currently one of the most comprehensive internationally, with a catalogue from 125 years ago. However, there are not many large earthquakes in the existing catalogue that may cause tsunami. This situation may lead to statistical instability in earthquake recurrence periods, which in turn may affect tsunami recurrence periods and may cause systematic bias in tsunami hazard curves. This study is based on one of the most comprehensive data currently available, and may be improved in the future with a deeper understanding of the earthquake recurrence in this region. Thirdly, the fault geometry used in this study follows that constructed by Slab2.0, in which the strike and dip of the slab surface are derived from the gradient of the depth surface. The depth and geometry of faults have little uncertainties, but according to study of Li et al., PTHA results are not sensitive to fault geometry and the differences caused by the fault geometries are ignorable. Therefore, early research focused more on the impact of differences in magnitude and recurrence period, non-uniform slip, and other factors on the evaluation results, which forms the basis of this study. With the continuous advancement of fault modelling in the future, the impact of fault geometry uncertainties will be further limited. Finally, the research is based on linear reconstruction techniques using unit sources, which are considered effective in deep water and leading waves. Therefore, the current analysis results are aimed at the large wave in the leading waves in deep water. In shallow water areas near the coast, linear reconstruction models should be used with caution, but they can act as boundaries for local refined assessment and be combined with nonlinear model for nearshore analysis51,52.
