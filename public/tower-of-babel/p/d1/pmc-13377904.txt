The effect of romifidine on endoscopic grading of laryngeal function in horses
Authors: Catherine Murphy, Matteo Baglioni, Olivier M. Lepage, James Schumacher, Lucy Kelleher, Joseph Burke, Monika Gangl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo our knowledge, the effect of sedation with romifidine on the grading of laryngeal function in horses has not been studied. Our objective was to examine the effects of romifidine on the grading of laryngeal function and the cross‐sectional area of the rima glottis (CSARG).MethodsTen horses underwent three endoscopic examinations of the larynx: once while unsedated (group R1), once after being sedated with romifidine (0.04 mg/kg intravenously; group R2) and once again, while still sedated, after receiving a repeat dose of romifidine (0.08 mg/kg intravenously, total dose; group R3). The recorded examinations were randomised and examined by three blinded reviewers who graded laryngeal function. The CSARG was measured from still images obtained during peak inspiration.ResultsSedation had no significant effect on the endoscopic laryngeal function grade assigned. There was no statistical difference in CSARG between Groups R1 and R2 (p = 0.164). Significant reduction in CSARG was noted between Groups R1 and R3 (p < 0.001) and Groups R2 and R3 (p = 0.045).LimitationsThe low number of horses examined may have resulted in the analyses being insufficiently powered. As such, the findings should be interpreted cautiously.ConclusionA dose of 0.04 mg/kg romifidine administered intravenously had no significant effect on horses’ laryngeal function grade and CSARG.

INTRODUCTION

Resting endoscopic examination of the upper respiratory tract (URT) is a critical part of the pre‐purchase examination of Thoroughbred racehorses, when requested. Horses with a higher aerobic demand are more likely to undergo endoscopic examination of the URT as part of a pre‐purchase examination; for example, event horses are more likely to undergo such examination than show ponies. 1 Endoscopic examination of the URT is also part of the inspection of Warmblood stallions for approval for entry into the studbook when a respiratory noise is evident during the clinical phase of the inspection. 2

Recurrent laryngeal neuropathy (RLN) is a progressively debilitating disease that results in loss of function of the abductor muscles of the left arytenoid cartilage during high‐intensity exercise. Exercise intolerance of horses resulting from collapse of the left arytenoid cartilage is associated with decreased ventilation caused by a reduction in the cross‐sectional area of the airway. 3 , 4 Endoscopic examination of the larynx performed with the horse resting is used to assess horses for RLN by evaluating the degree of symmetry and synchrony of movement of the arytenoid cartilages, as well as the ability to achieve and/or maintain complete abduction of the left arytenoid cartilage. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

Grades of laryngeal function can be assigned according to observations made during endoscopic examination using one of the multiple grading scales. 9 , 10 Though clinically apparent RLN is a condition that affects the horse during exercise, 11 , 12 results of endoscopic examination of the larynx performed with the horse resting are used as a predictor of function for racing Thoroughbreds sold at public auction. Studies have shown that yearlings at auction that have a Havemeyer laryngeal Grade ≥II.2 have an increased risk of requiring a prosthetic laryngoplasty to maintain abduction of the left arytenoid cartilage. 13

Performing endoscopic examination of the URT of young, fractious horses can be hazardous to personnel and the horses involved. Though most horses tolerate endoscopic examination of the URT after a nose twitch has been applied, some do not, creating a danger to the examining clinician, assistants and the horse. 5 A survey of equine practitioners showed that in 58.9% of the incidences where an equine veterinary clinician sustained an injury from a horse, the horse was restrained with only a nose twitch. 14 Although application of a nose twitch may assist with restraint, the authors believe there are instances when the use of a nose twitch can make the horse even more fractious.

Chemically restraining a horse to facilitate endoscopic examination of the URT and to increase the safety of personnel and the horse may affect arytenoid cartilage movement, leading to an inaccurate assessment of laryngeal function. 5 , 15 , 16 The effect of xylazine hydrochloride or detomidine hydrochloride alone and of detomidine in combination with butorphanol tartrate on the laryngeal function of horses has been investigated. 5 , 16 Detomidine and butorphanol administered in combination produced no noticeable effect on laryngeal function, 5 whereas xylazine was shown to reduce the cross‐sectional area of the rima glottis (CSARG) of 45% of horses. 16 Detomidine administered alone was shown to significantly affect abduction of the left arytenoid cartilage. 17 To our knowledge, there has been no study investigating the effect of romifidine administered intravenously on endoscopic assessment of laryngeal function. We hypothesised that sedating horses with romifidine would not worsen the grade of laryngeal function, reduce the CSARG, or reduce the left‐to‐right rima glottidis ratio (L:R RGR).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Ten healthy horses belonging to the École Nationale Vétérinaire de Lyon (VetAgro Sup) were enrolled in the study. These 10 horses were a sample of convenience. No power analysis was performed. Each horse was weighed and clinically examined.

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of VetAgro Sup (Number 2454) on the provision that equine‐track, final‐year veterinary students perform the endoscopic examination of the URT as part of their training.

Study design and experimental protocol

The study was an observer‐blinded, crossover study, with each horse serving as its own control. The larynx of each horse was examined endoscopically three times sequentially, once with the horse unsedated (group R1), once with the horse sedated with 0.04 mg/kg intravenously (IV)‐dose of romifidine (group R2), and once after the horse was administered a second consecutive 0.04 mg/kg IV‐dose of romifidine (0.08 mg/kg total dose; group R3). The insertion tube of a videoendoscope (Olympus EVIS EXERA III CV‐190, Olympus) was passed into the nasopharynx through the ventral meatus of the left nasal passage by an equine‐track, final‐year veterinary student. The end of the insertion tube was positioned just rostral to the tip of the epiglottis. Endoscopic examinations of the larynx were video recorded according to the conditions for pre‐sale of Tattersalls Auction House. 18 All horses were induced to swallow by flushing isotonic saline solution into the nasopharynx through the working channel of the endoscope. The nostrils were occluded to induce maximal abduction of the arytenoid cartilages. A nose twitch was applied to each horse for all endoscopic examinations. Ten students participated in the endoscopic examinations.

After the endoscopic examination was performed with the horse unsedated (group R1), romifidine (0.04 mg/kg IV) was administered by one of the authors (Catherine Murphy) or by a veterinary intern supervised by one of the authors (Catherine Murphy), and 5 minutes later, the degree of sedation was scored using the following descriptive scoring system described by Almonte et al. (2022) 5 : 1 = no clinically apparent sedation; 2 = mild sedation; 3 = moderate sedation; and 4 = marked sedation. The nose twitch was applied, and the endoscopic examination was repeated, as described before, by the same student.

The horse was again sedated within 1 minute of the second endoscopic examination, with the same dose of romifidine (0.04 mg/kg IV), so that the two doses of sedative were administered 10–15 minutes apart, making the combined dose used to sedate the horse for the third examination 0.08 mg/kg (group R3). Sedation was scored 5 minutes after the second dose of romifidine was administered, 5 and the laryngeal function was endoscopically observed and video recorded, as described before, for a third time by the same student.

Endoscopic examination

Each endoscopic examination was recorded for 1–2 minutes. The 30 video recordings (three recordings per horse) were downloaded to a computer and randomised using a random number generator (random.org) to create three different studies, each containing the 30 video recordings randomised to produce a different order, so that intraobserver agreement could be assessed.

Subjective evaluation of laryngeal function

Three blinded observers, all diplomates of the European or American College of Veterinary Surgeons, analysed the three sets of randomised video recordings and assigned a grade of function to the larynx according to the Havemeyer endoscopic laryngeal grading scale. 10 All reviewers were unaware of the horse's identity and whether or not the horse had been sedated. The observers, each provided a copy of the Havemeyer grading system, were allowed to watch the videos as many times as necessary to establish a grade of function.

Objective evaluation of laryngeal function

Three still images from each video of each horse were captured when the arytenoid cartilages were believed to be maximally abducted, such as after swallowing, and analysed using a Java‐based processing program (ImageJ; National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) by one of the authors (Catherine Murphy). The rima glottidis was outlined using a cursor, as previously described, 5 , 16 , 19 , 20 and the CSARG was calculated in pixels.2 The height of the rima glottidis was measured by digitally drawing a line between the estimated dorsal and ventral midpoints of the rima glottidis. This line divided the rima glottidis into left and right sides, each of which was outlined digitally by using a cursor to calculate the area of the left and right sides of the rima glottidis. The height and the area of the right and left sides of the rima glottidis for each still image were measured three times. These measurements for each image were then normalised, as previously described, 5 and the mean normalised CSARG (nCSARG) and the mean normalised L:R RGR (nL:R RGR) were calculated.

Statistical analyses

The data were recorded using Microsoft Excel 365 (Microsoft), and the software JASP (Version 0.18.3, University of Amsterdam, The Netherlands, JASP Team [2024]) was used to analyse the data statistically. The Shapiro–Wilk test showed that the nCSARG and the nL:R RGR were normally distributed, and they are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Ordinal variables, including the grade of laryngeal function assessed using the Havemeyer grading system, 10 are reported as the median across observations.

Paired Student's t‐tests were performed to compare the nCSARG and the nL:R RGR of each yearling before sedation (R1) with those obtained after each level of sedation (R2 and R3).

Intra‐ and interobserver agreements in the grading of laryngeal function (graded using the seven‐grade Havemeyer endoscopic laryngeal grading scale) 10 were calculated using weighted Cohen's kappa. The grades of laryngeal function assigned to each examination were categorised into a dichotomous scale for statistical purposes. Grades I and II.1–2 were considered to represent normal laryngeal function, whereas Grades III.1–3 and IV were considered to represent abnormal laryngeal function. 2 , 10 Cohen's kappa statistic was then calculated to determine intra‐ and interobserver agreements in the dichotomous grading scale. Cohen suggested the kappa result be interpreted as follows: values ≤0 indicating no agreement; values 0.01–0.20 indicating no‐to‐slight agreement; values 0.21–0.40 indicating fair agreement; values 0.41–0.60 indicating moderate agreement; values 0.61–0.80 indicating substantial agreement; and values 0.81–1.00 indicating almost perfect agreement. 21

The intraobserver median grade of laryngeal function, calculated as the median of the three blinded assessments performed by each of the three observers, was used for exploratory association of the median grade with the mean nCSARG and with the level of sedation (Groups R1, R2 and R3) for each observer separately. Associations were assessed using Spearman's rank‐order correlation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Objective grading

The mean nCSARG decreased progressively as the level of sedation increased (R1: 0.544 ± 0.092; R2: 0.485 ± 0.089; R3: 0.403 ± 0.095). However, there was no statistically significant difference in the mean nCSARG between the horses in group R1 and those in group R2 (p = 0.164). In contrast, the mean nCSARG of horses in group R3 was significantly lower than that of horses in Groups R1 (p <0.001) and R2 (p = 0.045). The mean nL:R RGR did not differ significantly between the three sedation groups (R1: 1.006 ± 0.112; R2: 1.045 ± 0.121; R3: 0.997 ± 0.094) (p > 0.05).

Subjective grading

For the seven grades of laryngeal function, intraobserver weighted kappa (κ w) values indicated slight‐to‐moderate agreement among the three observers (Observer 1, κ w = 0.38–0.50; Observer 2, κ w = 0.31–0.54; Observer 3, κ w = 0.16–0.43). Interobserver agreement was more variable, ranging from slight to substantial (Observer 2 vs. Observer 1, κ w = 0.24–0.64; Observer 2 vs. Observer 3, κ w = 0.23–0.35; Observer 1 vs. Observer 3, κ w = 0.19–0.62). The nCSARG of nine of the 10 horses in group R3 was less than that of the horses in group R1. A concomitant worsening of the grade of laryngeal function was observed in seven of 10 horses in group R3, according to Observer 2 (with six of seven horses having a reduction in the nCSARG), and five of 10 horses, according to Observer 1 (with four horses having a reduction in the nCSARG), and five of 10 horses according to Observer 3 (with five horses having a reduction in the nCSARG). However, using Spearman's correlation, we found no statistically significant association between the nCSARG and the median grades of laryngeal function for any of the observers (between −0.14 and 0.01; p > 0.46) or between the sedation group (R1, R2 and R3) and the median grades of laryngeal function (between −0.12 and 0.03; p > 0.50).

DISCUSSION

The results of this study confirmed our hypothesis that sedation with romifidine does not worsen the grade of laryngeal function. A dose of 0.04 mg/kg (IV) romifidine did not significantly affect the nCSARG or the grade of laryngeal function. Likewise, two successive doses of romifidine (0.04 mg/kg per dose; 0.08 mg/kg total dose), administered 10–15 minutes apart, did not worsen the grade of laryngeal function, although it did significantly reduce the nCSARG. We found no significant correlation between the nCSARG and the grade of laryngeal function. Subjective grading may be less sensitive to sedation‐induced changes than are changes in the CSARG. Perhaps sedation affects the movement of the left and right arytenoid cartilage similarly, so even though CSARG is reduced, the grade of laryngeal function, which assesses symmetry, is not affected. Indeed, the nL:R RGR did not differ significantly between the three groups (R1, R2 and R3). Our results indicate that administering a 0.04 mg/kg IV dose of romifidine does not produce an endoscopically detectable effect on the grade of laryngeal function.

Previous studies have evaluated the effect of various doses of other sedatives on laryngeal function. 5 , 16 Almonte et al. (2022) showed that intravenous administration of a combination of detomidine, an alpha 2 agonist, and butorphanol had no effect on the grade of laryngeal function of 46 Thoroughbred yearlings. 5 Ducharme et al. (1991) tested the impact of intravenous administration of xylazine, another alpha 2 agonist, on the grade of laryngeal function of 20 horses and found that it reduced the median grade of laryngeal function in 45% of horses, whereas the CSARG and L: R RGR remained unchanged. 16

The Havemeyer grading scale was used to evaluate laryngeal function, because the laryngeal grades of function identified using this grading system closely correlate to the histological assessments of the cricoarytenoideus dorsalis muscle for each of the grades 22 and to laryngeal function observed when the horse is exercised. 11 , 23 McLellan and Plevin (2019) found that despite well‐established grading scales used by experienced veterinarians, differences in grading occur. 24 We found interobserver agreement to be slight to moderate according to weighted kappa values for the Havemeyer grading scale. With three observers, as in our study, perfect interobserver agreement becomes less likely than if there were two. 25 To achieve a more objective approach to grading equine laryngeal function, Hardwick et al. (2024) developed a diagnostic decision tree (DDT) to use with the Havemeyer grading scale. 26 This decision tree involves using a flowchart with a series of yes or no questions that lead the observer to the appropriate grade. Using the DDT in our study may have increased our inter‐ and intraobserver agreements.

The CSARG is an objective measurement obtained from a still image, whereas a Havemeyer grade is a subjective evaluation determined from a videoendoscopic examination, and this subjectivity may account for our lack of correlation between the CSARG and the Havemeyer grade of laryngeal function. The CSARG does not allow the evaluator to ascertain whether abduction is maintained. It only assesses abduction captured on a still image, whereas the subjective grade assigned to a video assesses the degree of abduction achieved and whether that degree of abduction is maintained. The subjective grade compares the movement of the left arytenoid cartilage to that of the right, so if the sedation affects both equally, the grade assigned may remain unchanged, but the CSARG becomes reduced.

Intraobserver agreement using the Havemeyer grading scale was better than that using the dichotomous scale, and this finding differs from that of previous reports. 5 Laryngeal endoscopic grading scales are semi‐quantitative, so by increasing the number of grades, evaluators are less likely to agree on a score. 9 There were 30 out of the 270 grades where the Havemeyer grade assigned was II.2 (Table 2) and 38 assigned a Grade III.1. This cluster of grades at the lower limit of normal and upper limit of abnormal may explain why there is worse intraobserver agreement using the dichotomous scale.

According to the Tattersalls’ guidelines, endoscopic examination of the URT may be performed with the insertion tube of the endoscope inserted through either nasal passage. We performed all endoscopic examinations with the insertion tube of the endoscope in the left nasal passage because Ducharme et al. (1991) found that evaluation of laryngeal function was most consistent when the insertion tube was inserted through the left nasal passage. 16

Equine veterinary clinicians, over a 30‐year working period, can expect to sustain seven or eight injuries from horses. 14 The three leading causes of injury are a kick from a hindlimb; a strike from a forelimb—the most likely injury to be incurred during endoscopic examination of the URT; and a crushing injury. 14 Endoscopic examination of the URT of yearlings for sale poses a substantial threat to the safety of the veterinary examiner, assistants and the horse being examined. Because physical restraint alone (e.g., application of a nose twitch) is often insufficient to avoid injury, chemical restraint, alone or in addition to physical restraint, may be required. Administration of a dose of romifidine (0.04 mg/kg IV) produced at least a mild level of sedation in all horses (minimum sedation score of 2).

Our study population consisted of the 10 horses owned by the university, and this small number of horses was a limitation of our study, but even in this group, a large spectrum of grades of laryngeal function was observed. One limitation of our study was that the endoscopic examinations were performed in often‐handled, well‐mannered horses and not in unhandled, fractious yearlings. Another limitation was that the endoscopic examinations were performed by veterinary students inexperienced in performing endoscopic examinations of the URT of horses. Each of the 10 horses had a different student performing its three endoscopic examinations under the supervision of one of the authors (Catherine Murphy), and therefore, the positioning of the insertion tube of the endoscope was not uniform throughout the examinations. To maintain some consistency among the endoscopic examinations, all examinations were performed with a nose twitch applied to the horse. Applying a nose twitch to a horse for endoscopic examination has been shown to have no discernible effect on the score of laryngeal function. 15

Based on the results of our study, we conclude that chemically restraining a horse by administering a 0.04 mg/kg dose of romifidine intravenously affects neither the grade of laryngeal function nor the nCSARG. The 0.04 mg/kg dose provided at least a mild level of sedation to all horses, and therefore, it may improve the ease and safety of performing endoscopic evaluation of laryngeal function.
