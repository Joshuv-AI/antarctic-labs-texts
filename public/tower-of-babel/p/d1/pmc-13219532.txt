Indoor environmental factors and pneumonia in preschool children in Haikou China
Authors: Shuomin Wang, Qisheng Wu, Yifan Hu, Bingxian Zhou, Shiheng Fan, Dee Yu, Zhen Yan, Jing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to investigate the prevalence of pneumonia in preschool children in Haikou City and assess the correlates of indoor environmental factors on this condition. A cross-sectional survey was conducted from December 2020 to January 2021. Using a stratified random sampling method, eight kindergartens in Haikou City were selected for a questionnaire-based investigation. Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the associated factors for Pneumonia in Preschool Children. The prevalence of pneumonia among the 3,049 preschool children was 20.44%. This study reveals that pneumonia in preschool children is influenced by both modifiable environmental exposures and non-modifiable host factors. Among the key modifiable associated factors​ within the household, paternal smoking(OR = 1.253, P = 0.030) and keeping furry pets(OR = 1.713, P = 0.017) significantly increased the risk, whereas the use of a fresh air filtration system(OR = 0.566, P = 0.018) showed a strong protective effect. These actionable findings​ suggest that targeted interventions, such as promoting air filtration and reducing indoor airborne pollutants from smoking and pets, could effectively lower disease prevalence. Additionally, non-modifiable factors​ like female sex (OR = 0.823, P = 0.036)and a family history of allergies (OR = 1.423, P < 0.001) help identify children who may benefit most from such environmental interventions. Pneumonia in preschool children is associated with various factors, including demographic characteristics, exposure to tobacco smoke, pet ownership, and the presence of air filtration systems in the home. This study found that avoiding these associated factors may reduce the prevalence of pneumonia.

Introduction

Pneumonia, a prevalent respiratory infection, primarily affects the distal bronchi and alveolar tissues and is caused by bacteria, viruses, and fungi. It disproportionately affects children under five and the elderly with chronic diseases1,2. Globally, pneumonia accounts for approximately 120 million new cases annually, leading to nearly one million deaths in children under five, making it one of the leading causes of childhood mortality worldwide3. Similarly, in China, pneumonia is a major cause of death in children under five4. A comprehensive survey conducted in seven Chinese cities reported an age-adjusted prevalence of childhood pneumonia of 32.7% in 2011 and 26.4% in 20195. However, these studies are region-specific and may not be representative of the entire country, particularly given the distinct climatic conditions of Haikou City.

China’s rapid urbanization has significantly altered indoor living environments. Research indicates that indoor environmental factors are closely associated with the incidence and progression of respiratory diseases in children6. According to data from the Under-Five Child Mortality Surveillance System (U5CMSS) in China, between 1996 and 2013, pneumonia mortality in children under five decreased by 85.5%, with the proportion of pneumonia-related deaths declining from 23.4% in 1996 to 12.8% in 20137. In a recent survey, pneumonia prevalence was 29.5% in Urumqi and 26.7% in Nanjing for children under eight5. Variations in sampling methods, data collection, and diagnostic standards across regions may contribute to the observed discrepancies in prevalence rates.

Located at the southernmost part of China, Hainan Island lies on the northern edge of the tropics and experiences a tropical monsoon climate. Characterized by year-round high humidity and temperatures, the island frequently encounters issues such as mold growth on walls and furniture, food spoilage, and increased dust mite populations in indoor environments. Results from a study on allergic diseases among preschool children in Hainan confirmed that house dust mites, dust mites, and tropical mites are the most important allergens in the region, with both their positivity rates and sensitivity levels being higher than in other domestic areas8.

To support national policy development aimed at reducing the burden of childhood pneumonia, we conducted the China Children and Household Health (CCHH) study in Haikou City, Hainan Province, South China. This study assesses the prevalence and associated factors for pneumonia in preschool children, focusing on the relationship between indoor environmental factors and pneumonia in Haikou City, thus providing a regional foundation for preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Subjects and methods

Subjects

A stratified random sampling method was employed in this study, which was conducted from December 2020 to January 2021. Haikou city comprises four main administrative districts (Meilan, Longhua, Qiongshan, and Xiuying). To ensure geographical representativeness, we first stratified all registered kindergartens by district. Subsequently, two kindergartens were randomly selected from each district using a computer-generated random number list, resulting in a total of eight participating kindergartens. A priori sample size calculation determined that 2,101 participants were required. In this study, 4,248 questionnaires were distributed, and 3,049 valid responses were received, yielding a valid response rate of 71.77%, which meets the required sample size. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Hainan Medical College, and informed consent was secured from the parents or guardians of all participating children. We also complied with all relevant ethical regulations regarding the use of the data in our study. All reports adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Methods

The questionnaire used in this study was primarily adapted from the International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood (ISAAC)9 and Dampness in Buildings and Health (DBH)10 surveys, with minor modifications to suit the specific context of Haikou City. It comprised five sections: (1) General demographic characteristics, including the child’s gender, age, ethnicity, birth method, permanent residence, family income, etc.; (2) Child’s feeding and family structure, covering factors such as whether the child is an only child, number of cohabitants, breastfeeding status, parental education, dietary habits, history of food allergies, family health information, etc.; (3) The child’s pneumonia status; (4) Living environment, including residence type, area, flooring and wall materials, heating method, ventilation measures, furniture additions, home decoration, and residential hygiene, etc.; (5) Lifestyle habits, such as pet and plant ownership, frequency of room cleaning, and family smoking habits, etc.

Survey administrators briefed kindergarten teachers on the purpose, significance, and content of the questionnaire to ensure cooperation from parents. Parents were instructed to complete the questionnaires, which were collected by the teachers three days later. Trained research team members subsequently reviewed the questionnaires and excluded any invalid responses.

Statistical analysis

A database was created using EpiData 3.1 (http://www.epidata.dk), with data entered twice to ensure accuracy. Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) 20.0(https://www.ibm.com/cn-zh/products/spss/). Variables showing an association with pneumonia in univariate analyses and/or identified a priori based on previous literature were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Prior to model fitting, the absence of multicollinearity was confirmed, with all variance inflation factors (VIF) below 5. Associations between indoor environmental factors and pneumonia were assessed using this model, with a two-sided P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Univariate analysis of preschool children’s pneumonia

The prevalence of pneumonia among preschool children in Haikou City was 20.44%. The prevalence was higher in boys than in girls, and higher in only children compared to those with siblings (all P < 0.05). The analysis identified the following factors as being significantly associated with pneumonia in children (all P < 0.05): family history, keeping furry pets in the past 12 months, keeping furry pets when the child was 0–1 years old, past experience of keeping furry pets, passive smoking exposure of the child in the past 12 months, passive smoking exposure when the child was 0–1 years old, passive smoking exposure during mother’s pregnancy, father’s history of smoking, mold on the floor, in the year before pregnancy, dampness in clothes, in the year before pregnancy, mold on the floor, during pregnancy, dampness in clothes, during pregnancy, mold on the floor, when the child was 0–1 years old, dampness in clothes, when the child was 0–1 years old, dampness in clothes, in the past 12 months, airing of bedding, use of fresh air filtration systems, presence of tobacco smell in the living room, and presence of flies and mosquitoes in the living area (Table 2).

Discussion

The prevalence of pneumonia in preschool children in Haikou was found to be 20.44%, which is lower than the study of pneumonia in preschool children in seven cities in China by Haonan Shi et al. (26.4%)5. Previous studies have reported higher rates in eight cities of China (32.3%)13 and 205 preschools in six cities in China (34.5%)14, both significantly higher than in Haikou City. Key factors significantly associated with pneumonia included a family history of allergies, paternal smoking history, a history of keeping furry pets, female sex, and the use of a fresh air filtration system. Furthermore, this research helps fill the gap in studies on the relationship between pneumonia and indoor environmental factors among preschool children in Haikou, southern China.

Our findings reveal two important associations: a lower prevalence of childhood pneumonia in Hainan, and a protective effect associated with the use of fresh air filtration systems. These results are consistent with the possibility that​ continuous indoor air refreshment may play a role in reducing pneumonia risk. In Hainan, the common practice of “year-round window ventilation,” facilitated by the local warm climate, may function similarly to mechanical ventilation by promoting air exchange and potentially diluting indoor pathogen concentrations. Thus, we hypothesize that​ maintaining adequate indoor air circulation and cleanliness could be a contributing factor to lower pneumonia risk, independent of allergen exposure. However, it is important to emphasize that the present study demonstrates associative relationships and does not establish causality or a definitive biological mechanism.​ Further experimental and longitudinal studies are needed to test this hypothesis and clarify the underlying pathways. Finally, although childhood pneumonia prevalence in Haikou is relatively low, our study highlights that its occurrence in this population is influenced by multiple factors.

Several factors associated with pneumonia in preschool children were identified in this study. First, the prevalence of pneumonia was lower in girls than in boys. This disparity may be due to boys being more physically active and thus more exposed to associated factors. Second, a family history of allergies emerged as a significant risk factor for pneumonia, aligning with previous research15,16. In addition to its direct correlates on child mortality, pneumonia is a susceptibility factor for other respiratory diseases17–19. Studies have shown that pneumonia is a significant contributor to asthma, allergic rhinitis, and wheezing5. Children with a family history of allergies tend to have more vulnerable respiratory systems, making them more susceptible to environmental triggers leading to pneumonia.

Third, children of fathers who smoked had a higher prevalence of pneumonia. Numerous epidemiological studies have established that exposure to tobacco smoke is a risk factor for childhood pneumonia20,21. Prenatal or early-life exposure to secondhand smoke can adversely affect lung development and function22. Given the high smoking rate among Chinese men, smoking prevention and cessation should be prioritized as key strategies to reduce childhood pneumonia in China.

Fourth, keeping furry pets indoors was identified as a risk factor. Pets’ saliva, dander, and hair can harbor pathogens that are easily dispersed into the air23, increasing the risk of respiratory diseases in children. Finally, Our study identified the use of a fresh air filtration system as a significant associated factor against pneumonia in preschool children. This finding has important practical implications. In child-centric settings such as kindergartens, the installation of such systems can effectively reduce the concentration of airborne pathogens and help prevent group infection outbreaks, and should therefore be promoted as a critical health infrastructure. For households, especially those with poor natural ventilation or during periods of extreme weather, using high-efficiency air purifiers represents a viable intervention to safeguard children’s respiratory health.

Moreover, the absence of strict surveillance systems for childhood pneumonia in China poses a significant public health challenge. Many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) continue to experience high prevalence and mortality rates of childhood pneumonia, and these numbers are still rising24. As the largest developing country, China’s efforts in this area could have a positive correlates not only on the health of its preschool population but also on providing a framework for addressing childhood pneumonia in LMICs.

It is worth noting that in the univariate analysis, this study identified several factors that were significantly related to the research outcome, such as being an only child, observing mold and a damp environment in the living space, and the presence of flies/mosquitoes in the room. However, these variables were not included in the final multivariate model. There are several reasons for this: Firstly, in the initial analysis, we found that “mold” and “dampness” had a high degree of collinearity with the comprehensive “house type”, which had a broader explanatory scope and a more stable statistical performance in the model. Therefore, we retained the latter to simplify the model and avoid the problem of multicollinearity. Secondly, the effect of “being an only child” significantly weakened after adjusting for “family income” and “parental education level”, indicating that the association may be partially mediated by socio-economic factors. Finally, although “the presence of flies/mosquitoes in the room” was significant at the univariate level, it did not show independent predictive value after adjusting for core environmental and socio-economic variables. We chose to present and focus on discussing those factors that remained significant after multivariate adjustment and have clear public health intervention significance, to ensure the robustness and action-oriented nature of the conclusion. Future research can further explore the complex interactions among these factors in a larger sample.

However, this study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional survey, it cannot establish causal relationships. To better understand the determinants of pneumonia in preschool children, future studies should adopt more robust designs, such as case-control or cohort studies. Second, potential confounding factors, such as nutritional status and vaccination history, may not have been fully accounted for. Third, non-response bias is a concern, as 28.2% of the distributed questionnaires were not returned. Additionally, the diagnosis of pneumonia relied on retrospective physician diagnoses and parental recall, lacking objective clinical or radiological criteria, which may have introduced misclassification bias.
