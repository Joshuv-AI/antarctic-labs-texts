IL4Rα blockade inhibits proliferation of malignant lymphocytes and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment of mycosis fungoides
Authors: Alyxzandria M Gaydosik, Alyson Wang, Jishnu Das, Brigit Lapolla, Oleg E Akilov, Larisa J Geskin, Patrizia Fuschiotti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited therapeutic options are available for patients with advanced-stage mycosis fungoides (MF), and the 5-year survival rate is 25%. Due to its critical role in MF pathogenesis, the IL4/IL13 pathway presents a promising target for treatment. Here, we analyzed blocking of IL4Rα, the common subunit of the IL4 and IL13 receptors, within the advanced-stage MF cutaneous tumor microenvironment (TME).MethodsCellular Indexing of Transcriptomes and Epitopes by sequencing was employed to assess the transcriptional profile of the cells expressing the IL4 and IL13 receptors within the advanced MF TME. We combined IL4Rα inhibition by REGN668 (dupilumab) in ex vivo skin explants from advanced MF patients with single-cell transcriptome and machine learning analysis to identify the molecular mechanisms of IL4Rα signaling within the MF skin lesions. Parallel analysis of the T cell immune repertoire allowed identification of the expanded malignant clone within each MF sample. Multicolor immunofluorescence microscopy was employed to validate molecular findings.ResultsREGN668 inhibits patient-specific processes in malignant lymphocytes; however, we also identified downregulation of common pathways among patient samples, including those associated with cell cycle progression, PI3K/AKT signaling, janus kinase (JAK)/Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (STAT) signaling, DNA damage/repair, and TP53 regulation. Furthermore, we showed that IL4Rα blockade in the TME of advanced MF downregulates multiple immunosuppressive pathways associated with myeloid-derived suppressor cells and LAMP3+ dendritic cell recruitment and function, cytokine production, and antigen presentation in B cells, as well as differentiation and degranulation of mast cells.ConclusionsThese results provide new insights into advanced MF tumorigenesis and show that the effects of IL4Rα blockade are specific to the STAT6 pathway, inhibiting proliferation of malignant lymphocytes, as well as repressing several immunosuppressive mechanisms within the MF TME. However, the extensive genetic, transcriptional, and functional heterogeneity across MF patients indicates that inhibition of multiple driver pathways and a personalized therapeutic approach will be important for developing new therapeutic strategies against this disease.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Our study provides a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms of IL4Rα blockade by dupilumab within the advanced-stage MF tumor microenvironment and demonstrates that dupilumab exerts pathway-specific effects. Background

Mycosis fungoides (MF) is a clonal disorder of skin-resident memory CD4+ T cells and is the most common subtype of cutaneous T-cell lymphomas (CTCL).1 2 Although MF typically runs an indolent course where most malignant T cells reside in the skin, approximately one-third of patients progress to more advanced disease (stages IIB–IVA) with a marked increase in mortality (26% survival at 5 years)3 4 due to a paucity of effective medications. Precision MF pharmacotherapy lags behind most other malignancies in part because targeted therapy development requires refined knowledge of the pathogenic mechanisms underlying MF, and this is lacking.

Application of single-cell methods to advanced MF skin tumors from patients revealed important insights into disease pathogenesis, including the transcriptional profile of malignant clones as well as features of the MF tumor microenvironment (TME).57 The TME of advanced MF promotes type-2 immune responses that contribute to suppress anti-tumor immunity and to activation of pro-tumorigenic pathways, including angiogenesis, tissue remodeling, and tumor growth.6 8 We have previously shown that malignant T cells from advanced MF skin lesions produce high levels of IL13,9 which signals through the Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 6 (STAT6), a critical upstream mediator of both IL-13 and IL-4 signaling.10 By combining STAT6 inhibition with genome-wide transcriptome analysis of advanced MF tumors, we found that STAT6 promotes proliferation and invasion of MF tumor lymphocytes while inducing a progressive immunosuppression.10 Significantly, inhibition of the IL13 signaling pathway by ruxolitinib yielded a good clinical response in relapsed/refractory CTCL in a phase 2 clinical trial.11 12 Due to its critical role in MF pathogenesis, targeting the IL4/IL13 pathway with dupilumab,13 a monoclonal antibody that blocks IL4Rα, the common subunit of the IL4 and IL13 receptors, has been investigated as a promising treatment strategy. However, dupilumab was linked to CTCL initiation and/or progression in several patients,1417 warranting further investigation to determine the underlying mechanism(s).

Using ex vivo skin explant assays of advanced MF skin tumors and exploiting single-cell methods, including analysis with the recently developed interpretable latent factor (LF) regression approach, Significant Latent Factor Interaction Discovery and Exploration (SLIDE),18 we evaluated the transcriptional changes resulting from blocking IL4Rα by REGN668 (dupilumab) in advanced MF tumors. Focusing on the cells that expressed the highest levels of IL4 and/or IL13 receptors, such as malignant and reactive T lymphocytes, myeloid cells, B lymphocytes, and mast cells (MCs), we revealed the molecular mechanisms activated by IL4/IL13 signaling in the TME of advanced MF tumors. Although we observed that treatment affected patient-specific pathways, we found that REGN668 inhibited proliferation of malignant lymphocytes without affecting their survival in all patient samples. Furthermore, REGN668 blocked immunosuppressive mechanisms by myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and dendritic cells (DCs), as well as the production of immunosuppressive cytokines and chemokines by memory B cells and the differentiation and function of MCs. Thus, IL4Rα blockade inhibits both proliferation of tumor cells and various immunosuppressive pathways that contribute to a tumor-permissive MF TME.

Methods

Patient samples

We analyzed skin punch biopsies from 22 patients obtained from clinically active lesions, including tumors, plaques, and erythrodermic skin in patients with advanced-stage (IB-IVA) MF or Sézary syndrome (SS) enrolled at the Comprehensive Skin Cancer Center, Columbia University Medical Center and the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (online supplemental table S1). Patients were staged according to the most recent consensus.19 20 Adult men and women (≥18 years) were included. Pregnant women were excluded. No exclusion criteria based on race, ethnicity, or gender were applied. Treatment at the time of sample collection is reported in online supplemental table S1. Healthy control skin samples (HC, n=10) were obtained from the National Disease Research Interchange. Controls for immunofluorescence microscopy included atopic dermatitis (AD; n=3) and psoriasis (PS; n=3) skin samples, all obtained from The Health Sciences Tissue Bank, University of Pittsburgh. Of these MF samples, 6 were used for CITEseq, 18 for ex vivo skin explant assays with/without REGN688 treatment followed by either scRNAseq (n=8) or immunohistochemical methods (n=10), and 5 for validation by immunofluorescence microscopy.

Ex vivo skin explant cultures

Organ culture of full-thickness skin explants was performed as we previously reported.10 Advanced MF skin punch biopsies (tumors, 2×3 mm) were cultured with either medium alone or REGN668 (5 µM). Inhibitory concentrations of proliferation by REGN688 were previously determined on CTCL cell lines and Sézary cells.21 Skin explants were cultured in an air–liquid interface epidermal side up for 2 days followed by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). In other experiments, MF skin explants were cultured for 5 days followed by formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedding (FFPE), and immunohistochemical microscopy. The experimental workflow is shown in online supplemental figure S1. All cultures were performed in complete RPMI-1640 medium (ThermoFisher) containing rhIL-2 (10 ng/mL, PeproTech) and IL-7 (5 ng/mL, PeproTech).9 Cells were activated by the Dynabeads CD3/CD28 T cell Expander (ThermoFisher, bead-to-cell ratio: 1:1).

scRNA-seq and immune repertoire analysis

Skin samples were digested enzymatically (Whole Dissociation Skin Kit, Miltenyi Biotec) for 2 hours at 37°C and further dispersed using the Miltenyi gentle MACS Octo Dissociator. Treatments were hashed together using Hashtag Antibodies (TotalSeq-C, BioLegend) at optimized concentrations per BioLegend protocol. Experimental procedures followed established techniques2225 using the Chromium controller, Single Cell 5’ Library V2 kit (10x Genomics). Sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system (Illumina). Genes detected (6000 genes/cell) had a plateau at about 200 000 reads/cell (10X Genomics). We obtained ~200 million reads/sample through the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center Genome Center, Sequencing Facility. CellRanger V.7.0.1 was used to create h5 files from the FATSq files from sequencing. Seurat V.5.3.0, a single-cell analysis R package,2224 was used to identify distinct cell populations and visualize cell clusters.26 R V.4.4.0 was used with R Studio V.2024.04.01. The unique molecular identifier (UMI) matrix was filtered such that only cells expressing at least 200 genes were analyzed. Samples were filtered for percentage of mitochondrial gene content under 25% and unwanted sources of variation were regressed out of the data by constructing linear models to predict gene expression based on the number of UMIs per cell as well as the percentage of mitochondrial gene content. Cells were clustered using Louvain clustering and visualized using a t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding projection plot. For cell-type identification, we used the “FindAllMarkers” function in Seurat, which uses the Wilcoxon rank sum test to show differential genes with a minimum percentage of cells of 25% per cluster, log-fold change >0.25, and Bonferroni-adjusted p value<0.05 for significance. For gene differential tests throughout the analysis, we used the “FindMarkers” function in Seurat, which uses the Wilcoxon rank sum test to show differential genes with a minimum percentage of cells of 10% per identity requested, log-fold change >0.1, and Bonferroni-adjusted p value <0.05 for significance. Immune repertoire matrices were added to the gene expression dataset and analyzed with Seurat to determine major clones and differential gene expression of clonal cells, as described.5

Dataset integration

Inter-dataset integration was performed using reciprocal principal component analysis (PCA) in Seurat for batch correction analysis based on sample. Cell-gene UMI counting matrices generated were analyzed using Seurat to identify distinct cell populations using Louvain clustering.23 24 Each sample was analyzed to find the top 2000 highly variable genes and run PCA. Integration anchors were then found for each sample to combine the samples without batch effects.

Cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing and analysis

Cellular Indexing of Transcriptomes and Epitopes by sequencing (CITE-seq) followed the protocol of BioLegend and 10X Genomics.25 CITE-seq involves staining cells with DNA-barcoded antibodies coupled with oligonucleotides (Antibody-Derived Tag), which are available from BioLegend as TotalSeq-C, and running the droplet-based scRNA-seq platform and analysis as described above. Specifically, we used IL13RA1-ADT and IL4R-ADT antibodies at optimized concentrations per BioLegend protocol, which identifies cells expressing IL13Rα1 and IL4Rα on their surface.

SLIDE analysis

To uncover key LFs that can differentiate between untreated and/or REGN668-treated cells, we applied SLIDE to our scRNA-seq datasets. SLIDE, a new interpretable machine learning technique,18 comes with rigorous guarantees regarding identifiability of the LFs and corresponding inference, and it incorporates both linear and non-linear relationships, including complex hierarchical structures. The discovery of these relationships is also coupled to rigorous false discovery rate (FDR) control with stringent cross-validation and permutation.27 SLIDE provides these novel features without compromising any of the rigorous statistical guarantees regarding identifiability of the LFs and corresponding inference of significant interacting LFs that essential regression28 provides. For all analyses, we maintained a type I error threshold (alpha) of 0.1. SLIDE integrates a modified knockoff technique for this FDR control, providing a formal statistical guarantee that the discovered LFs represent genuine biological signals rather than statistical artifacts.

Of the nine cell-type-specific clusters in our combined MF skin samples, we focus on those expressing IL4Rα such as T and B lymphocytes, myeloid, and MCs (figure 1). SLIDE analysis was performed as previously described.18 For each cell type, with the exception of B cells, we first ran single-cell aggregation of cell states to create metacells, which are a more stable representation of the cell states in each sample. Due to the smaller number of B cells obtained, we chose to analyze the dataset directly. With these metacells, or single-cell transcriptomes in the case of the B cell type, we subset to the top 3000 most highly variable genes before running SLIDE to uncover the differential gene programs between the treated and untreated groups.

Pathway analysis

The differential gene lists were filtered for p<0.05 for significance and then run in Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (Qiagen) for significant upregulated or downregulated pathways. Pathways were represented by enrichment scores (−log p values, Fisher’s exact test) and selected by absolute z-scores ≥2.29

Immunohistochemical microscopy

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on FFPE skin samples following already established techniques.5 Multicolor staining used the tyramide signal amplification kit (ThermoFisher) as previously described.5 8 The antibodies employed in these experiments were reported in online supplemental table S2. Confocal images were captured on an Olympus Fluoview 3000 inverted confocal microscope. Immunohistochemistry images were obtained with an Evos FL Auto microscope (Life Technologies).

In situ cell death detection assay

The In Situ Cell Death Detection assay (Roche) was used on FFPE skin samples to show DNA breaks in the cells. A positive control was used by adding DNAse to a control slide for 10 min before incubating with all slides in the TUNEL mixture for 60 min at 37°C. DAPI stained nuclei.

Statistics

For analysis of single-cell data, relevant statistical analyses are indicated in the respective Method section. A p<0.05 was used throughout to determine significance.

Data access

All single-cell datasets have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus: GSE293752.

Results

The IL4/IL13 receptors are highly expressed within the advanced MF TME

We employed CITEseq25 to assess simultaneously gene expression and the immunophenotype of IL4Rα- and IL13Rα1-positive cells in the MF TME. We analyzed 75 091 cells from the skin tumors of 6 MF patients and 6 skin biopsies from HCs (figure 1A, online supplemental table S1A). The different cell-types in the skin were identified by the expression of known markers8 (figure 1B). CITEseq has a lower dropout rate compared with scRNAseq and a better ability to detect under-represented transcripts. Accordingly, transcript detection was rare despite clear IL13Rα1 and IL4Rα protein expression, especially in T and B lymphocytes, myeloid cells, and MCs (figure 1C). Focusing on the T lymphocytes (figure 1D), parallel analysis of the T cell immune repertoire allowed the identification of the expanded clonotypes within each MF sample (figure 1E). As previously described, individual MF samples revealed the expansion of various numbers of clonotypes, characterized by distinct V, D, J gene usage (online supplemental table S3) and frequencies (figure 1E). However, all samples exhibited the expansion of a dominant (more abundant) clonotype (clonotype 1, ct1), which upregulated various pro-oncogenic pathways and therefore was considered malignant (figure 1F).5 Strikingly, IL13Rα1 and IL4Rα were mainly expressed by ct1 cells from all samples compared with non-clonal T cells, while IL4Rα was also expressed at low levels by non-clonal T cells (figure 1G). By multicolor immunofluorescent microscopy, we visualized expression of IL13Rα1 and IL4Rα by CD3+ cells in MF samples, whereas no expression was detected in HC or PS skin samples. AD skin CD3+ cells expressed IL4Rα but not IL13Rα1 (figure 1H). To further validate expression of IL13Rα1 by malignant T cells, we visualized co-expression of IL13Rα1 and CD3 with various markers that we recently identified by scRNAseq in ct1 malignant lymphocytes,5 including PCNA, PCLAF, TYMS, CENPF, as well as TOX, a marker of malignant T cells9 (figure 1I, online supplemental figure S2).

Thus, the IL4 and IL13 receptors are mostly expressed by malignant lymphocytes but also by reactive immune cells in the MF TME.

Blockade of IL4Rα alters gene expression of benign and malignant MF T cells

We performed ex vivo skin explant assays from advanced MF skin tumors (n=8, online supplemental table S1B), cultured with/without REGN668 to block the IL4Rα chain of the IL4 and IL13 receptors, followed by scRNAseq (figure 2A). REGN668 treatment did not induce major changes to the transcriptional profiles and frequencies of the various cell-type populations within the MF TME, including T lymphocytes (figure 2B, online supplemental figure S3). Focusing on non-clonal T lymphocytes, of the nine Louvain clusters identified in CD4+ T cells (figure 2C–E), clusters #0, 1, 2, 5 comprised cells from all patient samples and IL4Rα blockade down-regulated senescence, Th2 pathway, leukocyte extravasation, and interferon signaling (figure 2F). Other clusters were patient-specific and treatment inhibited pathways such as IL33 signaling (#3, MF28), TCR signaling (#4, MF38), glycolysis (#6, MF40) as well as NFE2L2 (#7, MF34) and TNFR2 signaling (#8, MF30, 22). Non-clonal CD8+ T-cell clusters #0, 2, 3, and 6 comprised cells from most samples (figure 2G–I) and REGN668 treatment repressed several processes associated with NF-kB, TCR, 4–1BB, and HIF1α signaling (figure 2J). In patient-specific clusters #1 and 4 (MF40) REGN668 inhibited the RAF/MAP kinase cascade, NIK, and IFNαβsignaling; whereas in cluster #5 (MF38) JAK/STAT signaling and Th2 pathway were downregulated (figure 2J).

We found that REGN668 treatment did not significantly change the frequency of each T-cell clone in MF samples, neither it affected their V, D, J gene usage (figure 3A, online supplemental Table S4). However, IL4Rα blockade induced patient-specific transcriptional changes (figure 3B) in cells from malignant ct1 and downregulated several patient-specific pro-oncogenic pathways (figure 3C). These included neddylation and EIF2 signaling (MF22); hypoxia and DNA damage bypass (MF28); IGF-1 and HIFα signaling in MF31; and several pathways associated with cell cycle control in MF30. Pathways associated with other human cancers were also downregulated by REGN668, including BAG2 signaling (MF34), KEAP1-NFE2L2 (MF38), polyamine metabolism (MF40), and various DNA repair mechanisms in MF35. However, we also found downregulation of common pathways including those associated with cell cycle progression, PI3K/AKT signaling, PTEN regulation, JAK/STAT signaling, DNA damage/repair, TP53 regulation, as well as several processes also identified in other human cancers (figure 3D). Interestingly, we found that several of these pathways were also downregulated after STAT6 inhibition in advanced MF tumors10 (online supplemental figure S4), and we identified 118 genes common within these pathways, including AURKB/A, CCNB1/2, CDK1, CENPF, PCNA, TYMS (figure 3E, online supplemental Table S5), validating targeting of the STAT6 pathway by REGN668.

To further investigate the T-cell gene programs affected by treatment, we utilized SLIDE,18 an interpretable machine learning framework with theoretical guarantees and rigorous FDR control. The SLIDE model, built on our scRNAseq datasets, provided discrimination between untreated and REGN668-treated explants in a rigorous k-fold cross-validation framework with permutation testing (figure 3F, online supplemental figure S5A). SLIDE identified 13 significant LFs (context-specific co-abundant modules, LFs) in T lymphocytes (online supplemental figure S5B), of which examples of the most relevant are shown (figure 3G). SLIDE not only revealed the expected downregulation of Th2 immune genes (Z121: PTGDR2, IL17RB) underlying REGN668 treatment but also encompassed mechanisms of tumor cell proliferation (Z223, Z36) and suppression (Z140, Z27, Z301), the latter mostly found in REGN668-treated samples. Key genes that contributed to these altered transcriptomic states included TYMS, PCLF, ADAM29 (proliferation) and RASSF3, NPAS3, TNS1, MIR29B2CHG (tumor suppressors). Of these LFs, we found that only the gene expression scores corresponding to Z223, containing TYMS and PCLAF, were significantly different between the two groups (figure 3H). Noteworthy, we have previously identified TYMS and PCLAF as markers of malignant lymphocytes in advanced MF (figure 1H).5

Collectively, REGN668 treatment of advanced MF skin samples inhibited multiple pro-oncogenic pathways in malignant T lymphocytes and downregulated immunosuppressive mechanisms in non-malignant T cells.

REGN668 blocks proliferation of T lymphocytes within advanced MF skin tumors

Advanced MF skin explants cultured for 5 days with/without REGN668 showed intact anatomical structure after culture (figure 4A). Expression of PCNA and Ki67 was dramatically decreased after REGN668 treatment within the mononuclear tumor infiltrate of the REGN668-treated compared with the untreated explants (figure 4B,C). A more modest effect was observed when explants were cultured with either anti-IL4 or anti-IL13 mAbs (online supplemental figure S6A). In contrast, we detected no PCNA+ or Ki67+ mononuclear cells in either untreated or treated HC skin explants (figure 4B,C). By mIF microscopy we showed that PCNA and Ki67 are mainly co-expressed by CD3+ T lymphocytes in untreated explants and that IL4Rα blockade by REGN668 inhibits their expression by T cells (figure 4D). Markers associated with cell cycle progression, such as CCN2 and CDK2 that we previously detected in malignant lymphocytes,10 were also downregulated by REGN668 treatment (online supplemental figure S6B). Strikingly, we show that TYMS and PCLAF were also downregulated after treatment (figure 4E) confirming the SLIDE results (LF Z223: figure 3F–H). We verified that the IL4Rα/JAK1/STAT6 pathway is the target of REGN668 by demonstrating that STAT6 activation (pSTAT6) is inhibited by REGN668 in CD3+IL4Rα+ cells (figure 4F). Specificity of REGN668 is shown in figure 4G,H where we demonstrated that REGN668 downregulated pJAK1 and pSTAT6 but not pJAK2 and pSTAT3 expression. Finally, we established that REGN668 did not induce tissue damage and apoptosis in the treated explants (figure 4I, online supplemental figure S7). Collectively, we showed that REGN668 dramatically inhibits proliferation of malignant T cells in the advanced MF TME without affecting their survival.

IL4Rα inhibition modulates gene expression of MF myeloid cells

Unlike T lymphocytes, the transcriptional profile of MF myeloid cells showed some overlap across patients (figure 5A). Analysis by SLIDE revealed significant LFs encompassing genes involved in type-2 polarization (IL13, FAM66B) and immunosuppressive mechanisms (ALDH3B2, RASGEF1C, DKK4, ADGRF1, KITLG, SPINK1) (online supplemental figure S5C). We performed Louvain clustering to gain further insights into the effect of IL4Rα blockade on specific myeloid-cell subpopulations. We identified six major clusters, whose composition was minimally affected by REGN668 treatment (figure 5B). Focusing on the clusters expressing the IL4/IL13R receptors (figure 5C), we identified distinct myeloid cell populations based on the expression of known markers from the TME of other human cancers30 (figure 5D) and that likely contribute to the immunosuppressive microenvironment of MF tumors. Specifically, we identified two subsets of MDSCs CD14+ITGAM+ITGAX+TGFB1+CD83+IDO1+CD274+/− (clusters #1 and 3) as well as two subsets of immunosuppressive LAMP3+ + highly expressing IDO1, CD274 and TGFB1 (clusters #2 and 5). Significantly, we found that REGN668 inhibited expression of several genes involved with the immunosuppressive activity of these cells such as IDO1, TGFBI, TGFB1, FCN1 (figure 5E) and downregulated pathways associated with immunosuppression including TGFβ, TNRF2, and IL4/IL13 signaling, hypoxia, and antigen presentation (figure 5F). By mIF microscopy we validated some of these findings by showing that MF explants cultured with REGN668 exhibit decreased TGFβ and FCN1 expression by CD14+ cells (figure 5G). Thus, IL4Rα blockade inhibited various immunosuppressive pathways in MDSCs and DCs of the MF TME.

REGN668 induces transcriptional changes in MF B cells

Using mIF microscopy we visualized CD20+IL13Rα1+ and CD20+IL4Rα+ B cells within the advanced MF TME but not in HC skin (figure 6A). No major changes were detected in the transcriptional profile of B lymphocytes after REGN668 treatment (figure 6B). We identified 3 Louvain clusters, containing mostly cells from MF38 (cluster #0), and from MF28 (clusters #1 and #2) (figure 6C). All three clusters identified subsets of CD20+ (encoded by MS4A1) memory B cells (figure 6D). Significantly, REGN668 treatment reduced expression of several genes that were associated with memory B-cell differentiation and function including SLC35D2, FOSL2, VHL (cluster #0); BFAR, CD24, METTL3 (cluster #1); USF1, BAP1, CTBS (cluster #2) as well as genes also expressed by B cells in the TME of other cancers, such as IL10RA, CCR6, IRF7, CCL22, IL15, BAP1 (figure 6E). Accordingly, several pathways associated with IL10, IL27, IL15, IFNαβ signaling as well as TNFR2 non-canonical signaling cascade, antigen presentation, and B-cell senescence were also downregulated by the treatment (figure 6F). Moreover, SLIDE revealed two significant LFs (Z16, Z41), of which Z16 was significantly downregulated after treatment (figure 6G, online supplemental figure S5D). Among others, Z16 included genes associated with antigen presentation (HLA-DPA1, HLA-DPB1, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DRA, B2M), B-cell migration (VIM, ACTG1, RAC1), and regulatory B-cell differentiation (LGALS1). Finally, we used mIF microscopy to validate some of the transcriptional data by showing inhibition of CCL22 expression by CD20+ memory B cells in MF explants cultured with REGN668 (figure 6H). Thus, REGN668 inhibits B cell immunosuppressive pathways in the advanced MF TME.

The transcriptional profile of MCs is affected by IL4Rα inhibition within MF skin lesions

Higher numbers of MCs were found in the advanced MF TME compared with HC skin31 (figure 7A). MCs from patients were found to express several genes associated with MC activation (HDC, CMA1, KIT, TPSAB1) (figure 7B) and SLIDE revealed two LFs (Z307 and Z137 (online supplemental figure S5E) comprising genes involved in intracellular trafficking, degranulation (ZNF79, ARL4D, ZFAND2A) and phagocytosis (SNAP29). Transcriptome analysis of MF and HC MCs (figure 7C) identified 3 Louvain clusters (figure 7D). A large proportion of cells from clusters #0 and #1 derived from MF31, while most cells from cluster #2 derived from MF28 and HC samples. Treatment with REGN668 induced a modest increase in the proportion of MCs in clusters #0 and 2. figure 7E shows that REGN668 downregulated expression of several genes associated with MC function including MAPK6, TESK2, NFIL3, CELF1 (cluster #0); CTSG, TNFSF10, CD81, CMA1 (cluster #1); and EDN1, CCL18, CA2, NSF (cluster #2). Immunofluorescence microscopy of treated explants validated transcriptional data by showing decreased production of CMA1, TPSAB1, and cKIT by MCs following REGN688 treatment (figure 7G). Discussion

Due to its critical role in MF pathogenesis, the IL4/IL13 pathway presents a promising target for treatment. Here we analyzed blocking of IL4Rα, the common subunit of the IL4 and IL13 receptors, within the advanced MF TME. We combined single-cell transcriptome analysis with IL4Rα inhibition by REGN668 in ex vivo skin explants from advanced MF patients to identify the molecular mechanisms of IL4Rα signaling, focusing on the cell populations expressing the highest levels of the IL4 and IL13 receptors, such as T lymphocytes, myeloid cells, B lymphocytes, and MCs. While we observed both heterogeneity across patient samples in response to treatment as well as downregulation of patient-specific processes, REGN668 demonstrated high efficacy in inhibiting proliferation of malignant cells ex vivo. Moreover, we found that REGN668 also downregulated multiple immunosuppressive mechanisms by inflammatory cells that likely contribute to tumor immune evasion and a permissive TME.

Malignant MF T lymphocytes express both IL4Rα and IL13Rα1 and signals through the IL4Rα/pJAK1/pSTAT6 pathway.9 Most transcriptional changes induced by REGN668 in tumor lymphocytes were patient-specific and targeted oncogenic processes linked to tumor cell growth, proliferation, and survival as well as migration and invasion. However, treatment also inhibited common pathways in malignant T cells across patients, some of which we had previously identified after STAT6 inhibition,10 including those associated with cell cycle progression, PI3K/AKT signaling, PTEN regulation, JAK/STAT signaling, DNA damage/repair, and TP53 regulation. Dysregulation of these pathways has been previously related to abnormal proliferation of MF malignant lymphocytes3234 and now we demonstrated that REGN668 efficiently inhibits proliferation of malignant cells in advanced MF ex vivo explants. Moreover, recurrent genomic alterations within these pathways such as PLCG1, CARD11 (cell cycle), TNFRSF1B (non-canonical NF-kB signaling), TP53, SBS7 (DNA damage repair), PTEN (PI3K/AKT signaling), and JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, STAT5B, STAT3 (JAK/STAT pathway) lead to uncontrolled cell proliferation, tumor cell survival, and angiogenesis and are often linked to MF progression and invasion.32 34

REGN668 inhibited multiple immunosuppressive mechanisms in tumor-infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes (TILs), including Th2 immune responses and IL33, NTRK1, NFE2L2 signaling as well as the anti-inflammatory TNF/TNFR2 axis through the activation of the non-canonical NF-κB signaling pathway.3537 Senescence was also impaired by REGN668 in several clusters of CD4+ TILs, which could affect cytotoxicity and production of immunosuppressive cytokines that potently amplify immunosuppression within the TME.38

The TNFR2 non-canonical NFκB pathway is the main mechanism downregulated by REGN668 in MDSCs and LAMP3+ DCs within the advanced MF TME. Activation of the TNFR2 cascade promotes MDSC expansion, activation, and enhances their immunosuppressive activity by enabling them to suppress T-cell responses and promoting Treg formation.37 LAMP3+ DCs are particularly responsive to TNFR2 signaling through the non-canonical NF-κB pathway, which suppresses T-cell activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, while promoting the development of T cells to an immunosuppressive Treg phenotype.39 REGN668 also inhibits TGFβ production and signaling by myeloid cells, which drives immunosuppressive lineage commitment and activates TGFβ-mediated mechanisms that contribute to suppressed immunity, increased susceptibility to infections, and decreased antitumor immunity in the MF TME.40 Additionally, REGN668 downregulates PDGF and the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling cascades, limiting the expansion, function, and recruitment of MDSCs at the tumor site as well as preventing DC differentiation into non-inflammatory cells.41 Activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway is a major feature of MF, which is induced by multiple factors, including genomic alterations, aberrant cytokines and chemokines expression, and hypoxia.42 Under hypoxic conditions, the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling induces HIF1α expression and function43 that in turn promotes the differentiation of highly immunosuppressive MDSCs and tolerogenic DCs, boosting immunosuppression within the MF TME.

The role of B cells has only been recently investigated in CTCL. Our groups and others have shown that CD20+ memory B cells are numerous in advanced MF skin lesions,6 8 likely promoting type-2 immune responses. Recent work indicates that B-cell enrichment is associated with MF progression and poor clinical outcomes.6 8 Here we show that REGN668 downregulates various differentiation and effector functions of MF memory CD20+ B cells, including anti-inflammatory cytokine signaling and development of IL10-producing regulatory B cells, which promote immune tolerance and prevent antitumor immunity.44 REGN668 also inhibits production of CCL22 by MF B cells. It was previously reported that chemokine CCL22 is overexpressed in MF skin lesions45 and together with CCL17 plays a crucial role in MF tumorigenesis by recruiting cells expressing the CCR4 cognate receptor at the tumor site, namely Tregs, Th2 cells, and malignant lymphocytes. In this context, the CCL22-CCR4 axis is the target of mogamulizumab, a recently FDA-approved monoclonal antibody for CTCL therapy.46 Mechanisms of B-cell senescence are also a target of REGN668, likely improving B-cell antigen-presenting capability as well as downregulating B-cell SASP release, a mixture of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and stromal metalloproteinases that could further stimulate immune suppression, angiogenesis, and ultimately tumor growth and invasion.47

Activated MCs expressing the IL4 and IL13 receptors are numerous in the advanced MF TME and release mediators such as histamine, proteases, cytokines, and chemokines that could regulate trafficking of inflammatory and malignant cells, angiogenesis, leading to tumor invasiveness as observed in other human cancers.48 Accordingly, previous reports indicate that MC counts correlate positively with MF progression and microvessel density.49 Moreover, MC-deficient mice showed decreased tumor growth in experimental cutaneous lymphoma.49 By releasing histamine and tryptase, as well as by recruiting eosinophils, MCs can also contribute to pruritus, a frequent clinical symptom in MF that greatly impacts the patient’s quality of life.31 REGN668 treatment downregulated several processes associated with MC development, survival, proliferation, and function. Among these, the SCF-cKIT axis, which is crucial for migration of MCs into tumor, activation, and mediator secretion. In this context, REGN668 also inhibits processes such as SNARE signaling and COPII-mediated vesicle transport that are associated with MC degranulation and secretion of inflammatory mediators by exocytosis.50 Among these mediators, REGN668 appears to downregulate expression of tryptase and chymase, as well as SASP factors by senescent MCs, which could contribute to inflammation, matrix destruction, tissue remodeling, as well as angiogenesis and vascularization within the MF TME.48

In conclusion, our preclinical results indicate that the effects of REGN668 (dupilumab) are specific to the STAT6 pathway, inhibiting proliferation of malignant lymphocytes, as well as repressing several immunosuppressive mechanisms within the MF TME that likely hinder anti-tumor immune responses. Some patients were receiving systemic or skin-directed therapies at the time of biopsy, which may have influenced gene expression profiles. However, treatment exposure was heterogeneous across patients, reducing the likelihood of systematic bias. Moreover, consistent transcriptional patterns observed across samples suggest that the signals identified reflect underlying disease biology rather than treatment-specific effects. This approach also reflects real-world clinical conditions in advanced CTCL. Several case reports have raised concerns over dupilumab treatment linked to onset and/or progression of CTCL, especially in AD patients.1417 However, a combination of emerging clinical, transcriptomic, and epidemiological data indicates that rather than being causative of CTCL, dupilumab may instead be unmasking pre-existing or misdiagnosed disease.1417 We note that one patient in our cohort (MF26) had prior dupilumab exposure before biopsy, but this single-patient exposure is unlikely to have influenced the overall transcriptional patterns observed or the study conclusions. The underlying mechanisms will nonetheless require further investigation to fully understand these apparently paradoxical observations.

Significantly, we also demonstrated that dupilumab is ineffective against other established pro-oncogenic mediators of CTCL,21 51 such as STAT3 or JAK2, and their activating cytokines, while other factors such as enhanced IL13 availability and IL13Rα2 signaling within the MF TME, and the emergence of dupilumab-resistant malignant clones resulting from treatment1417 could also circumvent any beneficial effects by dupilumab. Additionally, disease heterogeneity, combined with genetic variations in pathways affected by dupilumab, including the JAK/STAT pathway itself, suggest that patient responses may vary. While the ex vivo skin explant model offers the opportunity to test patient-specific responses to IL4Rα blockade, the short time exposure may not recapitulate prolonged treatment, and a longitudinal study in patients will be required.
