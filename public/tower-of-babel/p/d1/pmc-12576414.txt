Endothelial to mesenchymal transition: a central mechanism in diabetes-induced vascular pathology
Authors: Giang-Huong Vu, Byeong Hwa Jeon, Cuk-Seong Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus is a major global health concern associated with micro- and macrovascular complications. Among the diverse mechanisms that contribute to vascular dysfunction in diabetes, endothelial to mesenchymal transition (EndMT) has emerged as a key pathological process. EndMT involves the loss of endothelial cell characteristics and the acquisition of mesenchymal features, resulting in impaired endothelial function, increased fibrosis, and inflammation. In addition to findings from preclinical models, recent human studies support the clinical relevance of EndMT. This review summarizes the molecular mechanisms governing EndMT, including key signaling pathways such as TGF-β, Notch, and Wnt, and examines how environmental, metabolic, and inflammatory cues influence this process. Furthermore, we discuss the maladaptive role of EndMT in diabetic complications, including nephropathy, retinopathy, atherosclerosis, and impaired wound healing, highlighting recent advances in anti-EndMT therapies and the clinical implications. Understanding the mechanisms of EndMT in the diabetic milieu may reveal novel therapeutic targets for preventing or reversing diabetic vascular diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic, noncommunicable disease (NCD) characterized by long-term hyperglycemia due to insulin deficiency or resistance. It is not only the fourth main NCD and the leading cause of death globally [1] but also a heavy healthcare burden affecting over 300 million adults worldwide, with prevalence rates continuing to rise, particularly in developing countries [1]. The prolonged elevation of blood glucose levels is problematic for the body’s organs and tissues, as it could lead to excess oxidative stress, inflammation, and structural damage over time, which could eventually impair and disrupt their normal functions [2,3].

As the main transporter in the blood, the vascular system is highly sensitive to fluctuations in blood glucose levels and is particularly susceptible to pathological damage resulting from sustained hyperglycemia. Vascular complications are responsible for most morbidities, hospitalizations, and deaths in patients with DM [4]. Micro-vascular damage plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy, ultimately leading to severe complications, such as blindness, renal failure, foot ulcers, and limb amputations [5]. Additionally, diabetes predisposes individuals to macrovascular disorders, including cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular pathology, and peripheral arterial disease, with major clinical manifestations such as myocardial infarction (MI), ischemic stroke, and progressive atherosclerotic occlusions [6]. The direct pathological effect of chronic hyperglycemia on the endothelial cell layer of the vascular system is considered a key factor among the various mechanisms contributing to diabetes-induced vascular injury.

Endothelial cells play a crucial role in vascular homeostasis by forming the inner lining of the blood vessels and heart chambers. Their most important functions include regulating vascular tone, controlling the permeability of nutrients and immune cells, and maintaining anti-inflammatory and antithrombotic environments [7]. Endothelial cell dysfunction results in impaired vasodilation, heightened inflammatory responses, altered vascular permeability, and dysregulated coagulation, all of which contribute to the pathogenesis of macrovascular and micro-vascular complications [8]. Under the hyperglycemic conditions associated with diabetes, endothelial cells undergo a significant morphological transformation known as endothelial to mesenchymal transition (EndMT), a process that disrupts endothelial integrity and function [9]. This phenotypic shift profoundly affects endothelial cell and vascular function, further exacerbating the progression of diabetes (Table 1). Recent studies using human biopsy specimens and transcriptomic profiling have confirmed the clinical presence of EndMT, highlighting the significance in diabetic complications [10,11]. Understanding how EndMT contributes to diabetic vascular complications could help develop new treatments to prevent long-term blood vessel damage.

ENDOTHELIAL TO MESENCHYMAL TRANSITION

Endothelial cells are flattened cells with apicobasal polarity, with the apex facing the blood and basal attachment to the basal lamina. They are tightly bound to each other by specialized adherens junctions and tight junctions and interact with blood cells such as platelets and immune cells using membrane glycoproteins, some of which are used as biomarkers to distinguish them from other cells, such as vascular-endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin), platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule (PECAM1/CD31), Tie1, Tie2, and von Willebrand Factor (vWF). While endothelial cells were once thought to be highly differentiated, in recent years, with the help of technologies such as single-cell sequencing, lineage tracing, and intersectional genetics [12], their plasticity and heterogeneity have been reported across different organs and in the same vessel at different times [13]. This is apparent during EndMT, in which endothelial cells can be activated under certain conditions and transformed partially or completely into a mesenchymal morphology (Fig. 1).

Unlike endothelial cells, mesenchymal cells exhibit weak cell-cell adhesion and possess front-to-rear polarity, which are essential for cell migration and invasion. Additionally, mesenchymal cells play a central role in fibrotic regulation through their ability to secrete the extracellular matrix (ECM) components [14,15]. While the endothelial cells markers are downregulated, mesenchymal biomarkers like alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), extra domain A fibronectin, neural cadherin (N-cadherin), vimentin, fibroblast activating protein, and fibroblast specific protein 1 are overexpressed during the EndMT process [16]. The transition can be partial, where the cells still retain some endothelial characteristics and markers, or complete, where they fully detach and migrate away from the basal lamina and other endothelial cells [17]. An example of partial EndMT is vessel sprouting angiogenesis, where endothelial cells transform into tip cells (TCs) and stalk cells (SCs). While these cells detach from the basement membrane, migrate, and lose their apical-basal polarity, they continue to express endothelial molecular markers, and the junctions that bind endothelial cells remain intact [17]. In contrast, cell lineage tracing analysis showed that at least half of the pericardial mesenchymal cells after MI injury were of endothelial origin, suggesting a role for complete EndMT in MI tissue recovery [18]. The dynamic balance between these states ensures proper vascular function and adaptation to various physiological conditions, such as embryonic heart valve development, wound healing, tissue repair, and angiogenesis [19].

MECHANISMS

EndMT is a complex process orchestrated by the coordinated activation and crosstalk of multiple signaling pathways. Among these, the transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), Notch, and Wnt pathways are recognized as principal drivers facilitating its induction and progression (Fig. 2).

The TGF-β superfamily comprises TGF-βs, bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), activins, and related proteins. The canonical TGF-β pathway induces EndMT via the phosphorylation of suppressor of mothers against decapentaplegic 2 (Smad2) and Smad3 proteins, resulting in their activation. These proteins form a complex with Smad4, which translocates to the nucleus to encode the profibrotic ECM transcription factor Snail (SNAI1) [20]. Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) released during the inflammation process could synergize with TGF-β2 to promote activation and nuclear translocation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), which may induce EndMT via interaction with Smad2 [21]. However, TGF-β and NF-κB signaling also induce the expression of Smad6 and Smad7, which has an inhibitory effect and acts as an auto-inhibitory feedback mechanism [22]. TGF-β signals could also induce the expression of other mesenchymal transcription factors like Slug (SNAI2), TWIST1, some members of zinc finger E-box binding homeobox (ZEB) family via canonical or non-canonical pathway by activating extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling, Rho guanosine triphosphatase (GTPase) signaling, p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, JNK signaling, phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKR mouse thymoma proto-oncogene (Akt) signaling, as well as Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling [23-25]. The lack of TGF-β2 signaling leads to impairment in the development of mice heart valves due to suppressed mesenchymal cell differentiation [26].

Notch signaling is another pathway required for early cardiac valve development [27]. The Notch intracellular domain can directly upregulate SNAI2 [28] and increase SNAI1 stability and nuclear retention by inhibiting glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3β)-mediated proteolysis and translocation of SNAI1 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm [29]. However, Notch is also known to be the main factor determining the development of SCs and inhibiting them from transforming into the more migrating TCs during angiogenesis [30].

Canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling has also been reported to induce GSK3β phosphorylation and activate the transcription of SNAI2, TWIST1, and ZEB1 [31-33]. β-catenin nucleus translocation was also reported to induce the expression of EndMT marker α-SMA, as well as the downregulation of CD31 in cultured endothelial cells [18]. Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP), an effector of the Hippo pathway, is also involved in the process of EndMT by positively contributing to the expression of TGFβ-Smad3. YAP prevents Smad3 from binding to GSK3β, thus inhibiting its subsequent phosphorylation and degradation [34].

The vascular-endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling pathways inhibit EndMT inhibiting effect [35,36]. VEGF treatment was reported to inhibit EndMT and prevent the deposition of collagen through the downregulation of TGF-β and Smad2 [35]. VEGF signaling also strengthens endothelial cell identity by activating GATA2, which regulates VE-cadherin expression [37]. This role of VEGF is especially important in maintaining the endothelial features of TCs and SCs during the process of neoangiogenesis, where those cells exhibit partial EndMT morphology [38,39]. In contrast, FGF reduces the TGFβ receptor complex, namely ALK5 (TGFBR1), TGFBR2 and SARA by the overexpression of miRNA-20a, inhibiting TGF-β1-induced activation of Smad2 and Smad3 [36]. FGF can also activate transcription factors from the E26 transforming-specific (ETS) family, including ETS variant 1 (ETS1) and ETS variant 2 (ETV2), which further promote endothelial cell identity and activate vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR2) transcription [40].

The ETS family of transcription factors, such as ETV2, Fiend leukemia integration 1 (FLI1), and ETS1, is required for endothelial specification. ETV2 directly binds to the promoters of VE-cadherin, Tie2, VEGFR2, Scl, GATA2, Meis1, Dll4, Notch1, Nrp1/2, Flt4, RhoJ, MAPK, and FLI1 [41] and act as the endothelial identity promoting factor during early embryonic development in both mice and zebrafish; and FLI1 later help maintain endothelial differentiation by binding the promoters of VE-cadherin, Tie2, CD31, Erg and itself [42]. Similarly, ETS1 also functions during angiogenesis [43]. GATA2, another transcription factor, is also involved in the regulation of endothelial cell identity by activating the transcription of VE-cadherin, CD31, VEGFR2, Nrp1, vWF, and GATA2 itself [37].

The most prominent transcription factors associated with EndMT are SNAI1, SNAI2, TWIST1, ZEB1, and ZEB2. SNAI1, SNAI2, and TWIST were reported to be able to directly repress the transcription of VE-cadherin in tumors [44]. ZEB1 represses multiple key determinants of epithelial differentiation and cell-cell adhesion, including the cell polarity genes Crumbs3, HUGL2, and Pals1-associated tight junction proteins, by associating with their endogenous promoters in vivo [45], whereas ZEB2 has been reported to regulate the same target with few exceptions [46]. A computational model behavior analysis by Weinstein et al. [47] showed that the loss of any of the transcription factors SNAI2, TWIST1, ZEB1, or ZEB2 prevents mesenchymal cell differentiation, and TWIST1, ZEB1, and ZEB2 appear to work sequentially with SNAI1 and SNAI2 during EndMT.

GENERAL INITIATING FACTORS

Physical or chemical stimulus

Under normal conditions, EndMT is tightly regulated by physical and chemical stimuli. As the inner lining of blood vessels, the main mechanical and physical signal that directly affects endothelial cells is the force exerted by flowing blood or laminar shear stress (LSS). Uniform LSS maintained by normal blood pressure was reported to hinder EndMT through Krüppel-like factor 2/4 (KLF2/ KLF4) in a MEK5/ERK5-dependent manner, whereas disturbed flow could induce EndMT both in vitro and in vivo [48]. ERK5 sustained activation was observed in endothelial cells from 15 min to at least 48 h of exposure to uniform LSS, and ERK5 silencing caused spontaneous morphological changes suggestive of EndMT. As mechano-activated downstream effectors of MEK5/ERK5, KLF2/KLF4 can inhibit EndMT through the transcriptional activation of VE-cadherin [49]. Pathologic high shear stress has been reported to induce EndMT by reducing ERG expression [50]. Low levels of LSS have also been reported to mediate EndMT via MEST-induced TWIST1 overexpression, which is negatively regulated by H2S [51].

Oxygen availability is a key regulatory factor in the phenotypic modulation of endothelial cells. Elevated intracellular oxidative stress within these cells is a well-characterized inducer of endothelial dysfunction and EndMT via TGF-β1/2-dependent pathways [52]. Conversely, hypoxic conditions may also facilitate EndMT, as hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF1α) can directly bind to the promoter of SNAI1, thereby activating its transcription and promoting mesenchymal transformation in human coronary endothelial cells [53]. Intriguingly, HIF1α also upregulates ETV2, a critical transcriptional regulator of endothelial identity, suggesting a context-dependent and potentially dualistic role of HIF1α in EndMT regulation [41].

Inflammation

Inflammation plays a pivotal role in regulating EndMT, with TGF-β1 acting as a key pro-inflammatory mediator in this process [54]. Inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, have synergistic effects that promote EndMT along with TGF-β [21,55]. During EndMT, activated endothelial cells enhance leukocyte adhesion and infiltration, which perpetuate the inflammatory milieu and drive the progression of EndMT [56]. This establishes a self-reinforcing pathological cycle that may exacerbate vascular complications associated with diabetes.

Cell metabolism

Recently, it has been suggested that endothelial cell metabolic activity could be another regulatory factor for EndMT. Most ATP in endothelial cells is generated by glycolysis rather than mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. An increase in mitochondria-produced acetate increases the acetylation of TGF-β signaling complex proteins, including Smad2, 3, and 4, thereby augmenting and sustaining TGF-β signaling and EndMT [57]. Additionally, lactate, which is a product of glycolysis when oxygen is scarce, accumulates in patients with heart failure and is correlated with heart failure mortality [58,59]. Fan et al. [60] demonstrated that lactate promoted EndMT via SNAI1 lactylation after MI. Endothelial fatty acid oxidation is a critical in vitro and in vivo regulator of EndMT through alteration of intracellular acetyl-CoA levels, modulating the TGF-β-induced Smad2 downstream signal [61].

MALADAPTIVE ENDOTHELIAL TO MESENCHYMAL TRANSITION IN DIABETIC VASCULAR DISEASES

The association between diabetes and EndMT is well-documented. Hyperglycemic conditions have been shown to induce EndMT in human umbilical vein endothelial cells, accompanied by increased TGF-β1 expression [9]. Renal biopsies from type 2 diabetes patients also confirmed the increase in mesenchymal markers and decrease in endothelial markers [62]. In addition to the direct effects of high glucose levels, diabetes-related metabolic disturbances, such as elevated reactive oxygen species levels, chronic inflammation, hyperinsulinemia, and epigenetic modifications, may create a microenvironment conducive to both the initiation and persistence of endothelial dysfunction via EndMT [63-65]. Endothelial impairment contributes to the pathogenesis of a wide range of diabetes-related macro- and micro-vascular complications in a tissue-specific manner (Fig. 3).

Macrovascular complication

While healthy endothelial cells provide an anti-inflammatory and anticoagulant surface to maintain normal blood flow and vascular homeostasis, they also play key roles in initiating and maintaining the progression of inflammation. Increased expression of intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) in endothelial cells was reported to promote leukocyte binding and stimulation [66]. The process of endothelial inflammation is a hallmark feature of diabetic pathology, involving many pro-inflammatory mediators, such as NF-κB, TNF-α, and IL-6 [67]. EndMT is an important link between inflammation and endothelial dysfunction in diabetic complications [64].

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by the buildup of plaques in the walls of arteries, which can lead to narrowing, blockage of blood flow, and accumulation and release of the thrombosis cap, leading to lethal complications such as acute MI or stroke [68]. Endothelial dysfunction marks the beginning of this process, as it enables the endothelium to become more permeable to low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, inflammatory cells, and fibrous tissue, enabling them to bind and migrate to the injured sites [69]. Diabetes significantly increases the risk of atherosclerosis, and type 2 diabetes is associated with an increased risk of both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [70]. A recent study identified 14 distinct cell populations within human atherosclerotic plaques, including endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, mast cells, B cells, myeloid cells, and T cells. Notably, one endothelial cell subtype exhibited expression of smooth muscle markers- alpha-actin 2, notch receptor 3, and myosin heavy chain 11, indicating that this subset may be undergoing EndMT [71]. Single-cell sequencing analysis confirmed the existence of EndMT of endothelial cells during diabetes-associated atherogenesis in mouse hearts after a diabetogenic high-fat diet [72], suggesting a potential role for EndMT in diabetic atherosclerosis.

The transformation of endothelial cells to partial or complete mesenchymal morphology has been reported to play a key role in the onset and maintenance of the development and progression of atherosclerosis. Chen et al. [73] reported that EndMT serves as the link between factors initiating atherosclerosis, such as inflammation and disturbed shear, and tissue remodeling that leads to plaque formation by reduced endothelial FGFR1 expression and activated TGF-β signaling. EndMT also increases the expression of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, further promoting the binding and recruitment of circulating monocytes and leukocytes, leading to the expansion of the neointima. This enhances leukocyte recruitment, exacerbates vascular inflammation, and amplifies EndMT. This feedback loop reinforces EndMT progression and perpetuates a self-sustaining pathogenic cycle. Notably, unlike normal fibroblast cells, endothelial-derived fibroblast-like cells secrete higher levels of collagen-matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) into the ECM. MMPs function as atherosclerotic plaque-destabilizing factors that may enhance clinical disease progression towards a collagen-poor, rupture-prone plaque phenotype with a thin fibrous cap [74].

Atherosclerosis is not the only risk factor for intra-vascular thrombosis. Malignancy, pregnancy, systemic inflammation, surgery, trauma, varicose veins, and immobilization are the major contributors to the development of abnormal thrombosis [75]. Endothelial cells play an essential role in the blood coagulation cascade by initiating and propagating thrombin [76]. Tissue factor, also known as coagulation factor III, an important component of the coagulation cascade. It is expressed in extracellular tissues, such as fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells, and appears on the surface of endothelial cells only after activation [77]. EndMT further promotes the thrombogenic phenotype by increasing leukocyte recruitment [56]. Endothelial dysfunction and EndMT have been suggested in the prothrombotic status of diabetic patients with COVID-19 [78], pregnancy [79], and varicose vein disease [80]. TGF-β/Smad2/3 and Notch pathways were long reported as important players in the development of atherosclerosis, as well as venous thrombosis by promoting EndMT [81-83]. It is worth highlighting that hearts form patients with diabetes demonstrated pathological EndMT which causes cardiac dysfunction and fibrosis [84,85].

Micro-vascular diseases

Endothelial cells in smaller vessels, such as arterioles and capillaries, have important roles other than anti-inflammatory and anti-thrombosis functions. They form a semi-permeable barrier that regulates the exchange of oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and waste products between the bloodstream and surrounding tissues and regulates local blood flow and pressure by producing vasoactive substances such as nitric oxide and endothelin, as well as angiogenesis and vascular remodeling [86]. While EndMT is required for some of these functions, aberrant EndMT signals during diabetes heavily disrupt all of these functions, leading to endothelial dysfunction and serious micro-vascular complications, including diabetic retinopathy, diabetic nephropathy, diabetic neuropathy, and peripheral wound healing process [10,87-89].

The adherens and tight junctions not only enable endothelial cells to adhere to each other but also form a seal that restrict the passage of molecules between cells; the tightness of these junctions varies between different organs, with the blood-brain barrier being the tightest [90]. By downregulating these proteins, EndMT not only allows endothelial cells to break loose from each other and the basal lamina but also disrupts the function of various barrier systems by greatly altering their selective permeability, resulting in serious diabetes-related complications. One example is the pathological development of diabetic retinopathy, where the leakiness of the blood-retina barrier leads to hard exudates and edema, which can be observed on fundoscopy [91]. EndMT can also lead to the thickening of the basement membrane and increased deposition of matrix proteins due to its mesenchymal phenotype, further contributing to the breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier [92]. Progressive retinal fibrosis, also known as tractional retinal detachment (TRD), contributes to vision-threatening complications in diabetic retinopathy, known as TRD. Pathological neovascularization, together with aberrant fibrovascular proliferation, can lead to vitreous hemorrhage, retinal scarring, and ultimately irreversible vision loss [93,94]. TGF-β/ERK1/2, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, Wnt/β-Catenin and Notch pathways are reported to promote EndMT in diabetic retinopathy [95-98].

The kidney is another organ that is profoundly affected by diabetes-induced EndMT. Endothelial cells in the kidneys, particularly those in the glomeruli, play a crucial role in filtering blood and maintaining kidney function. The loss of tight junctions due to EndMT in glomerular endothelial cells is associated with the increase in permeability in human renal glomerular endothelial cells induced by high glusoce condition [99] albuminuria resulting from hyperpermeability [62]. The S1PR2/Wnt3a/RhoA/ROCK1/β-catenin signaling pathway could promote diabetic nephropathy by inducing EndMT and impairing endothelial barrier function [100]. The TGF-β/Smad3 and MEK/ERK have been reported to promote the disease development [101-103]. As the disease progresses, prolonged EndMT promotes renal fibrosis, which is another serious complication of diabetes [104,105]. EndMT markers FSP-1 and α‐SMA are found to be elevated in the kidneys from diabetic mice [106] and patients with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) [103]. Additionally, knocking down TWIST1 and SNAI1 in endothelial cells inhibited EndMT and improved renal fibrosis [11]. In DKD, renal fibrosis encompasses both interstitial fibrosis (RIF) and glomerulosclerosis [107]. EndMT in glomerular endothelial cells is associated with early fibrosis in DKD [108]. EndMT is also considered a crucial mechanism that drives RIF [10]. Abnormal lymphangiogenesis has been reported to accelerate RIF progression, and human lymphatic endothelial cells directly contribute to the progression of DKD-related RIF via EndMT [109].

Distal sensory polyneuropathy (DSPN) is a common micro-vascular complication in individuals with diabetes. Peripheral nerves rely on a specialized microcirculatory network known as the vasa nervorum for oxygen and nutrient delivery [110]. Previous studies have demonstrated a significant association between enlargement of the endoneurial capillary basement membrane within the vasa nervorum and impaired peroneal nerve conduction velocity, reduced myelinated fiber density, and heightened vibratory and thermal sensory thresholds in diabetic patients, which are reportedly absent in diabetic individuals without DSPN [111]. Furthermore, disruption of the blood-nerve barrier, which facilitates the paracellular leakage of macromolecules such as albumin, immunoglobulin, and mannitol, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of DSPN [112]. Although a direct link between EndMT and DSPN has not yet been established, EndMT has been shown to contribute to vascular barrier dysfunction, basal lamina thickening, and perivascular fibrosis in other diabetes-associated complications, including diabetic retinopathy and nephropathy, as previously described, suggesting the need for further investigation to clarify the link between them. However, the connection between EndMT and dysregulation of diabetes wound healing has been well reported. Yang et al. [89] found that excessive neutrophil extracellular trap formation induces EndMT by inhibiting the Hippo-Yap pathway, leading to Yap binding to Smad2 and its translocation to the nucleus [89]. This impairs angiogenesis, delays wound healing, and exacerbates the progression of diabetic foot ulcers, one of the most prevalent and severe complications associated with diabetes [113]. Additionally, YAP/smad3-mediated EndMT contributes to erectile dysfunction in rats with type 2 diabetes [114].

THERAPEUTIC TARGETING ENDOTHELIAL TO MESENCHYMAL TRANSITION IN DIABETIC VASCULAR COMPLICATIONS

The studies on EndMT mechanisms and its regulations allowed for better understanding and more refined approach in handling cardiovascular diseases and complications, especially in the context of DM. Spironolactone and losartan, two of the regularly used agents to control hypertension in DM patients, has been widely reported to confer beneficial effects in preventing cardiac remodeling without knowing the precise underlying mechanisms [115,116]. Furthermore, a phase 3 study evaluated renoprotection of losartan in early diabetic nephropathy in American Indians with type 2 diabetes. The findings suggest that Losartan may slow the progression of DKD when administered after the onset of microalbuminuria [117]. Both spironolactone and losartan were recently reported to be able to prevent EndMT processes [118,119], which could partially explain the anti-fibrosis as well as vascular protective effect previously observed. Empagliflozin, a clinically approved SGLT2 inhibitor, attenuates lymphatic EndMT and renal fibrosis in DKD via modulation of VEGF-C/VEGFR3 signaling [109]. Anti-VEGF has been the mainstay of treatment to diseases like choroidal neovascularization [120], but scar tissue develops in 45% of patients after 2 years of treatment [121] and 41% after 10 years [122]. The VEGF’s role in EndMT inhibition and its interplay with TGF-β2 during angiogenesis suggest a potential explain for this adverse effect and paved way for more nuanced treatment options [123].

New agents are also being developed to target diabetic vascular complications through EndMT. Among these, targeted inhibition of the TGF-β/Smad signaling cascade using Smad3-siRNA-loaded nanocarriers has shown potent anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects in diabetic aortic and valvular endothelial models [124]. Similarly, fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) and bone morphogenetic protein 7 (BMP7) have been reported to suppress EndMT by antagonizing TGF-β-driven mesenchymal transition and reinforcing endothelial phenotype [125,126]. In addition, Apelin signaling mitigates EndMT and fibrotic remodeling by enhancing BKCa channel activity and downregulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling in diabetic nephropathy [127]. However, translating these insights into clinical therapies remains in its early stages. Currently, a number of therapeutically promising agents undergoing clinical evaluation have been investigated for their potential ability to inhibit EndMT. A phase 2 study assessing the safety and efficacy of TGF-β1 monoclonal antibody in patients with diabetic nephropathy was terminated early for futility. Final results confirmed that TGF-β1 monoclonal anitbody showed no therapeutic benefit at all three doses [128]. Several TGF-β inhibiting agents are being trialed clinically for fibrosis and cancer therapy [129]. Pirfenidone is currently the most effective anti TGF-β agent, having demonstrated therapeutic benefits in multiple phase 2 clinical trials for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). As a result, it is now approved for use in treating adult patients with IPF [130]. Omipalisib, a potent and selective inhibitor of the PI3K/mTOR pathway, has been shown to reduce abnormal glucose signaling in fibrotic areas of IPF lungs as measured by F-2-fluoro-2-deoxy-d-glucose-positron emission tomography/computed tomography [131].

CONCLUSION

In summary, EndMT plays a pivotal role in the progression of diabetic vascular complications by driving endothelial dysfunction, promoting fibrosis, recruiting excess inflammatory cells, and compromising vascular barrier integrity. Evidence across multiple tissues, including large arteries, the retina, kidneys, and chronic wounds, supports EndMT as a common pathological mechanism underlying micro-vascular deterioration in diabetes. While further investigation is needed to fully elucidate the role of EndMT in diabetic neuropathy, targeting the process holds promise presents a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention. Modulating EndMT may help preserve vascular homeostasis and reduce the burden of long-term complications in individual with diabetes.
