Introduction

Andersen–Tawil syndrome (ATS) is a rare genetic disorder affecting potassium channels in the heart and skeletal muscle. The estimated prevalence is 1 of 1,000,000. It is autosomal dominantly inherited and predominantly caused by a pathogenic variant in KCNJ2 or, less commonly, KCNJ5.1,2 ATS is clinically characterized by episodic muscle weakness, symptomatic cardiac arrhythmias or asymptomatic electrocardiographic (ECG) changes, and dysmorphic features. The latter includes short stature, small hands and feet, dental anomalies, low-set ears, orbital hypertelorism, micrognathia, syndactyly of the second and third toes, and fifth digit clinodactyly. The ECG may portray classical findings including prolonged corrected QT/U interval, premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), or enlarged U waves.2

We present a case of an adolescent female with a severe ATS phenotype possibly owing to a potentiated effect of a second genetic variant. Furthermore, we describe the efficacy of a tailored antiarrhythmia treatment including flecainide in this patient.

Case report

A 15-year-old adolescent female was referred for cardiac evaluation owing to a medical history with 2 alarming syncopes within the 2 months before referral. Initially, the patient had been referred from her general practitioner to a neurologic evaluation because the episodes were interpreted as seizures. This evaluation included an electroencephalogram investigation, which was normal, but the simultaneously obtained ECG revealed a substantial number of PVCs. Hence, the patient was referred for cardiac assessment.

Clinically, the patient had short stature (height 153 cm), but no dysmorphic features. Blood analysis revealed normal plasma potassium levels. The echocardiographic examination showed a structurally normal heart. The ECG in the outpatient clinic demonstrated enlarged U waves and frequent multifocal PVCs (Figure 1), and a 48-hour Holter monitoring was performed. This illustrated multifocal PVCs with a burden of 34% and nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT). A cardiopulmonary exercise (CPX) test demonstrated a substantially reduced PVC burden with maximal exertion. The cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was normal.

Electrocardiograms of the proband. A: At initial evaluation with multiple premature ventricular contractions. B: Illustrating the Andersen–Tawil syndrome–associated U wave.

Whole-genome sequencing was performed, applying an in silico gene panel (see Supplemental Data), consisting of genes associated with hereditary cardiac disorders. This identified a pathogenic heterozygote nucleotide substitution in KCNJ2 (NM_000891.2): c.245G>A. This results in an amino acid change [p.(Arg82Gln)]. An in silico prediction tool (REVEL) rates the variant as pathogenic (score 0.94; range 0–1). The allele frequency Genome Aggregation Database (version 4.1.0) states an allelic frequency of 0.0002% (4 of 1,614,098 alleles). In ClinVar (RCV000791354) and in other case reports, this variant is described as pathogenic and associated with ATS.3,4 In addition, the patient also had a heterozygote pathogenic variant in KCNH2 (hERG) (NM_000238.3): c.1143dup p.(Ala382Argfs∗6). This variant, a duplication of a single nucleotide, introduces a frameshift in exon 6 of 15. This variant is not seen in the Genome Aggregation Database (version 4.1.0), nor is it stated in ClinVar or the literature. However, pathogenic loss-of-function variants in KCNH2 are generally associated with long QT syndrome type 2 (LQTS-2). The parents were analyzed for the presence of the variants reported in the proband. The mother had the KCNJ2 variant and had ECG changes with enlarged U waves similar to the proband. She had no dysmorphic features nor a history of syncope or other cardiac symptoms, and her Holter monitoring was normal. The father carried neither of the genetic variants. Hence, the KCNH2 variant in the index patient is de novo. A family pedigree is presented in Figure 2. Siblings were tested for both variants, and both carry the KCNJ2 variant but not the variant in KCNH2. None of them have reported symptoms. They both have ECGs with large U waves. The brother had a normal Holter monitoring. Several family members on the maternal side of the proband carry the KCNJ2 variant, none having the same severe phenotype as the index case. The grandmother included in the family pedigree had no certain ECG findings and a normal Holter monitoring.

Medical treatment was commenced for the patient when the PVC burden was demonstrated. First, the nonselective β-adrenergic receptor antagonist and an α-adrenergic receptor antagonist carvedilol was chosen. However, the patient’s medication was changed to the nonselective β-adrenergic receptor antagonist nadolol and the class Ic antiarrhythmic agent flecainide. The change in medicine was because of a Holter monitoring showing no significant reduction in the PVC burden on initial medication and the knowledge of the molecular genetic testing. A CPX test was repeated after initiation of flecainide and nadolol and demonstrated no PVCs or other arrhythmias. Holter monitoring was repeated 2 weeks and 6 months after the medication had been altered to nadolol and flecainide and illustrated a PVC burden of less than 2% and 1%, respectively, and no NSVT nor sustained ventricular tachycardia. The family was provided recommendations on risk factors and specific triggers with regard to ATS and LQTS-2.

An informed consent for publication has been obtained.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, only 1 previous case has been published describing coexisting pathogenic variants in 2 cardiac potassium-channel coding genes KCNJ2 and KCNH2.5 Polyak et al5 reported a similarly severe phenotype, including recurrent syncopes and resuscitated cardiac arrest requiring implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation. Together with the present case, this supports the concept that dual-molecular findings in ion channel variants can synergistically reduce repolarization reserve, producing a phenotype far more severe than either variant alone.6

A novel and clinically important contribution of our case is the availability of a multigeneration family pedigree, which was not presented by Polyak et al.5 The pedigree clearly demonstrates that the KCNJ2 variant alone produces a minimally symptomatic ATS phenotype in multiple maternal relatives. This is in line with the well-described reduced penetrance and variable expressivity of ATS.2 It highlights that the severe arrhythmic phenotype in the proband very likely cannot be explained by the inherited KCNJ2 variant alone. Instead, the additional de novo KCNH2 variant seems to “unmask” or potentiate the electrical instability, resulting in high-burden PVCs, NSVT, and syncope. This genotype–phenotype correlation, supported by family data, strengthens the argument for synergistic pathophysiology in dual-molecular potassium-channel variants and clearly distinguishes our report from previously published cases.

A possible mechanistic framework for interpreting our findings is reduced repolarization reserve. Ventricular repolarization depends on the coordinated contribution of multiple outward potassium currents, which provide partially overlapping functions in maintaining normal action potential duration. In individuals with congenital channelopathies, 1 component of this repolarization network is already impaired. Although compensatory mechanisms may initially preserve near-normal repolarization, the overall reserve is reduced. Consequently, having more than 1 protein genetically affected may compromise repolarization further and precipitate disproportionate QT prolongation and ventricular arrhythmias.

The phenotypic differences between the present case and the patient described by Polyak et al5 may reflect differences in the specific variants, inherent variability in clinical expressivity, or importantly earlier recognition and initiation of therapy in our patient. Early referral and cardiac evaluation enabled targeted medical treatment before progression to malignant ventricular arrhythmia in our case.

Our management approach also differed from that described by Polyak et al.5 Although they used metoprolol, we chose the nonselective beta-blocker nadolol, based on evidence and guideline recommendations supporting its superior efficacy in LQTS-2.1,7 The addition of flecainide was guided by the patient’s ATS-dominated phenotype and supported by existing data for KCNJ2-related ventricular arrhythmias.1,8 Although flecainide in LQTS-2 may raise concern for potential proarrhythmia, careful monitoring of our patient showed an excellent clinical response, with near-complete suppression of PVC on follow-up CPX and Holter monitoring.

This case illustrates the value of comprehensive genetic testing, which not only clarified the underlying etiology but also paved the way for a precision-based therapeutic strategy. The dramatic improvement on a combined nadolol–flecainide regimen underscores that tailored, genotype-guided management can significantly modify arrhythmic risk even in complex channelopathy. Careful monitoring of possible ECG changes (including QRS widening) and CPX is recommended when administering flecainide.

In summary, this case provides unique pedigree-supported evidence that an isolated KCNJ2 variant may produce a mild ATS phenotype, whereas a second de novo KCNH2 variant can markedly potentiate disease severity. This insight, alongside the successful genotype-guided therapy, distinguishes our report and contributes meaningful new evidence to the understanding and management of potassium-channel disorders caused by dual-molecular findings.
