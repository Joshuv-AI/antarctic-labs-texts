Targeting the neuropilin 1/interferon γ axis restores glucocorticoid-suppressed T cell function and rescues bone formation to prevent steroid-induced osteonecrosis
Authors: Hongduo Lu, Benlu Chen, Changbo Cheng, Yantong Luo, Hongfen Chen, Yuehui Zhou, Yunhao Hu, Shihua Gao, Hanjun Fang, Wei He, Zhenqiu Chen, Yinuo Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsImmune cells exert influence on skeletal homeostasis, and re-establishing metabolic equilibrium is central to the management of steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SIONFH). However, the osteoimmune mechanisms underpinning this pathology remain largely under-researched. This study aimed to delineate these mechanisms, thereby informing early therapeutic strategies to restore bone homeostasis.MethodsWe identified the key immune cell subsets associated with SIONFH, and investigated their upstream regulatory factors and downstream effector molecules by integrating multiomics analyses (samples derived from human peripheral blood, femoral head tissues, and rat peripheral blood), mining of the GSE123568 dataset, and performing in vivo and in vitro experiments.ResultsClinical transcriptomics revealed enrichment of immune-related pathways, with ssGSEA consistently highlighting activated CD8+ T cells and Th1 cells. Correlation analyses demonstrated a stronger association between T cells and osteoblasts than osteoclasts (r = 0.8728 vs r = –0.065). Staphylococcal enterotoxin C2 (sec2) activated T cells both in vitro and in vivo, thereby augmenting osteogenic markers, enhancing bone marrow stromal cell (BMSC) differentiation, and elevating T cell-derived interferon gamma (IFNγ). Mechanistically, IFNγ facilitated signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1)–runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) dissociation and Runx2 nuclear translocation, thereby potentiating osteogenesis. Multiomics and functional studies identified neuropilin 1 (NRP1) as a critical IFNγ/T cell-associated protein, downregulated by staphylococcal enterotoxin C2 (sec2). Pharmacological activation of NRP1 with certepetide validated its role in modulating IFNγ secretion and downstream signalling. Glucocorticoids suppress IFNγ production in T cells and weaken NRP1/IFNγ-driven osteogenic differentiation. Ultimately, sec2 reshaped antigen-presenting cell-mediated regulation of the T cell-NRP1/IFNγ axis, promoting bone formation and mitigating SIONFH.ConclusionGlucocorticoids suppress T cell-derived IFNγ via NRP1 upregulation, thereby impairing Runx2 nuclear translocation and BMSC osteogenesis. Sec2 counteracts this cascade through antigen-presenting cell modulation, restoring the NRP1/IFNγ axis to preserve femoral head integrity.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(7):795–810.Keywords: Steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head, NRP1/IFNγ, STAT1-Runx2 complex, Osteogenic, glucocorticoids, bone formation, femoral heads, bone marrow stromal cells, rat models, secretion, blood, osteogenesis, osteonecrosis of the femoral head, antigen-presenting cells

Strengths and limitations

Our study substantiates that glucocorticoids impair osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs through modulation of the NRP1/IFNγ axis in T cells, thereby revealing a potential mechanistic basis for SIONFH. This finding offers novel insights into the osteoimmune pathogenesis of SIONFH, and establishes a conceptual foundation for the development of systematic and efficacious preventive strategies targeting bone–immune crosstalk.

Introduction

Traditionally, the immune system is recognized to coordinate host defense mechanisms through specialized cellular responses.1 Emerging research reveals systemic crosstalk between the immune system and other physiological systems.2-5 Osteoimmunology focuses on studying bidirectional crosstalk mechanisms, integrating orthopedic and immunological principles to elucidate how immune cells and bone cells interact through the secretion of cytokines and other means to maintain bone health. This theoretical framework confirms that immune cells regulate the skeletal system via direct effects or secretion of cytokines (e.g. IFNγ), collectively maintaining skeletal integrity.6,7 Under pathological conditions, aberrant expression of immune cell-associated proteins (e.g. neuropilin 1 (NRP1)) dysregulates cytokine secretion and cellular functions, thereby impairing skeletal homeostasis.8-10 Therefore, osteoimmunology provides a novel perspective to assist in uncovering the pathogenesis of orthopaedic diseases, and developing innovative therapeutic strategies accordingly.

Glucocorticoids, as clinically essential immunosuppressants, are currently administered to 1% to 3% of the global population for therapeutic purposes.11-15 Glucocorticoids exert immunosuppressive effects by reducing immune cell counts and blunting their activation.16,17 However, excessive use of glucocorticoids causes adverse effects that impose a substantial health and economic burden.18 Steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SIONFH) is a notable complication.19 Despite the substantial advances achieved in the diagnosis and treatment of orthopaedic disorders, the therapeutic regimens for SIONFH still have considerable limitations.20-28 This necessitates more in-depth investigations into the pathological mechanisms underlying SIONFH. Current research primarily focuses on the direct modulation of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, whereas investigations into their upstream regulatory mechanisms remain relatively insufficient. Building on our previous work, which elucidated the role of osteoblast-osteoclast mediated bone metabolic homeostasis in the pathological progression of SIONFH, we observed a distinct correlation between the onset and development of SIONFH and immune cells, particularly T lymphocytes.29-31 This key finding prompted us to conduct further mechanistic investigations to delineate the potential role of T cells in the pathogenesis of SIONFH.

Staphylococcal enterotoxin C2 (sec2) is a superantigen elaborated by bacterial strains.32 Distinct from conventional antigens, sec2 exerts a direct effect on the exterior of the peptide-binding groove of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II) molecules expressed on the surface of antigen-presenting cells (APCs), thereby engaging specific domains of T-cell receptors.33 During this process, activated T cells secrete substantial quantities of cytokines, which in turn participate in osteoimmunology processes.34

This study explores the changes in immune cells after glucocorticoid administration that lead to local bone metabolic homeostasis disorders in the femoral head, aiming to provide new insights into the osteoimmunological mechanisms of SIONFH.

Methods

Collection of clinical specimens

This study enrolled healthy volunteers and patients with SIONFH at different stages as classified by the Association Research Circulation Osseous (ARCO).35 Peripheral blood samples from six healthy volunteers and six patients with ARCO stage II SIONFH were collected for transcriptome sequencing. Femoral head specimens were obtained from six patients with ARCO stage IV SIONFH who underwent total hip arthroplasty; both healthy and necrotic regions of the specimens were subjected to proteome analysis. All patients were recruited from the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (Ethics Approval No.: K-2023-181), and all study procedures were conducted in strict adherence to ethical guidelines. Eligibility screening of participants strictly adhered to the following inclusion criteria: 1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for SIONFH as specified in the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Osteonecrosis of the Femoral Head in Chinese Adults (2019 Edition);35 2) having a definite history of glucocorticoid administration, without a history of hip trauma or chronic alcohol abuse; 3) aged between 18 and 55 years; 4) exclusion of other bone metabolic disorders; and 5) all participants voluntarily signed a written informed consent form after fully understanding the study objectives, procedures, and potential risks. Only those who met all the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study. The sample collection protocol was as follows: peripheral blood samples were collected from all enrolled patients and healthy volunteers; for SIONFH patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty, femoral head specimens were harvested intraoperatively. All collected samples were subjected to standardized preprocessing and subsequently stored in a −80°C ultra-low temperature refrigerator. For femoral head specimens, bone tissues were harvested from both necrotic and healthy regions for proteomic profiling, aiming to delineate the differential protein expression between these two regions. In contrast, peripheral blood samples were submitted for transcriptomic sequencing to facilitate subsequent mechanistic analyses.

Animal experiments

Recombinant sec2 was generously provided by Dr Wang Haixing from Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology.36 A total of 40 SPF-grade male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were selected based on specific criteria: eight-week-old rats with a body weight of approximately 250 g. Rats with a body weight less than 200 g or those showing obvious trauma were excluded. These rats were subsequently randomized into four groups: the control group, the experimental group, the sec2 group (which received sec2 administration via intraperitoneal injection), and the sec2 + certepetide (CEND1) group (which, on the basis of sec2 administration, received intraperitoneal injection of the NRP1 agonist CEND1 (MedChemExpress, USA, HY-P3448) at a dose of 1.5 mg/kg). Each group consisted of ten rats. The animal modelling and drug intervention protocols are presented in Supplementary Figure S1. No animal deaths occurred during the processes of modelling and drug intervention. This animal experiment was conducted at the Animal Experimentation of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine after obtaining approval from the Experimental Animal Ethics Review Committee (Ethics Approval No. 20230526001). We have included an ARRIVE checklist to show that we have conformed to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Micro-CT scanning

For experimental procedures, five samples were randomly selected from the animal specimens of each group. The proximal femur was scanned with a SkyScan micro-CT centered on the femoral head. Scanner parameters were set as: pixel size 1 mm, resolution 9 μm/L, voltage 65 kV, current 385 μA, and scanning length 18 mm. After scanning, the original image data were reconstructed using NRecon software (Bruker, USA). The reconstructed model was adjusted in 3D with Data Viewer software (Bruker) for precise alignment and positioning. For quantitative analysis of trabecular bone mass in the proximal femur, CTAn software (Bruker) was used to calculate metrics such as bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular-cortical separation (Tb.Sp). Database Viewer software was used for visualization and analysis of the reconstructed 2D images.

Omics detection and bioinformatics analysis

Omics detection and analysis provide an effective and reliable approach to explore research directions. In this study, four distinct omics datasets were integrated, with clear specification of sample sources as follows. 1) Human blood transcriptomic data (RNA-seq): peripheral blood samples were collected from healthy controls and patients with SIONFH. This dataset was used to perform single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses, and principal component analysis (PCA). 2) Human femoral head-derived proteomic data (data-independent acquisition, DIA): proteins were extracted from femoral head tissues of SIONFH patients, with samples further divided into ‘healthy regions’ and ‘necrotic regions’ of the femoral head. This dataset was employed to identify NRP1 as a target associated with T cell-derived IFNγ signalling, and boxplots were generated to illustrate NRP1 expression differences between healthy and necrotic regions. 3) Rat peripheral blood transcriptomic data (RNA-seq): peripheral blood samples were collected from two groups of rats—a SIONFH model group and a sec2-intervened SIONFH model group (sec2 + model group). Comparative transcriptomic profiling between the two groups delineated sec2-responsive gene signatures, particularly sec2-induced alterations in NRP1 expression, with boxplots visualizing these changes. 4) Publicly available GSE123568 dataset: retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO),37 this dataset contains transcriptomic data from femoral head tissues of SIONFH patients and healthy controls. It was used for supplementary ssGSEA and GO/KEGG enrichment analyses to validate findings from our in-house data.

Subsequently, all raw sequencing data (from human blood, rat peripheral blood, and femoral head tissues) and the preprocessed GSE123568 expression matrix were imported into Rstudio (v2023.06.1+524; Posit, USA) for unified analysis and visualization.38 The "limma" package was used to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs),39,40 and the "clusterProfiler" package was applied for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis.41 We retrieved relevant gene sets from Genecards (LifeMap Sciences, USA) using the keywords "T cell", "osteogenic", and "osteoclast", respectively, for subsequent analytical procedures. Leveraging data from the STRING database (STRING Consortium, Switzerland), protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed for DEGs associated with osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis. To achieve dimensionality reduction and correlation analysis, we employed principal component analysis (PCA) to transform gene expression data pertinent to T cells, osteogenesis, and osteoclastogenesis into principal components (designated as T, OB, and OC, respectively), followed by the calculation of Pearson correlation coefficients to quantify the inter-relationships among these principal components. Finally, machine-learning methods, including LASSO regression, support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), and Random Forest, were employed to screen core proteins associated with T cells, SIONFH, and IFNγ from DEGs identified in the data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomic dataset.42-44 Intersection analysis between sec2-responsive gene signatures and core protein datasets yielded T cell-specific proteins that exhibit superantigen-induced expression modulation and are associated with SIONFH; these proteins were prioritized for subsequent functional characterization.

Haematoxylin and eosin staining

For experimental procedures, five samples were randomly selected from the animal specimens of each group. Femora of the left lower limbs from rats in each group were retrieved. They were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China, BL539A) for 48 hours and then decalcified with 14% (pH = 7.4) ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). Subsequently, the tissues underwent dehydration and paraffin-embedding, and were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm, followed by haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Beyotime, China; C0105S). Briefly, the sections were dewaxed in xylene (twice, 20 min each) and rehydrated through a descending ethanol series (100%, 95%, 90%, 80%, and 70% ethanol, 5 min each), followed by rinsing in distilled water. Then, the proximal femoral region was scanned using a tissue section scanner. The number of empty bone lacunae and the ratio of empty bone lacunae to total bone lacunae were calculated and compared among groups.

Isolation and culture of cells

SD rats were anaesthetized prior to euthanasia via decapitation. Tibiae and fibulae were dissected to reveal the bone marrow cavity, which was subsequently flushed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Servicebio, China; G4202) to retrieve bone marrow fluid. Harvested bone marrow fluid was cultured in α-minimum essential medium (MEM) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; C12571500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Procell, China, 164210), 1% penicillin, and streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122). The culture medium was refreshed every 48 hours. Upon reaching 80% to 90% confluence, cells were passaged. Third-passage cells were harvested for subsequent experiments.

To prepare splenic lymphocyte-conditioned medium, spleens were harvested from eight-week-old SD rats. Briefly, spleens were minced in culture medium using the flat end of a sterile syringe plunger to generate single-cell suspensions. Subsequently, splenic lymphocytes were isolated via Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated lymphocytes were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 8 × 10⁶ cells/mL in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The medium was supplemented with sec2 at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL, with PBS serving as the vehicle control. Cells were incubated for 12 hours at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere. After incubation, cell suspensions were collected and centrifuged; the resulting supernatants were harvested as conditioned medium. Total splenic T cells were enriched using a rat pan-T cell isolation kit (HY-001067, Stemcell Technologies) following the manufacturer’s protocols, and the enriched cells were used for subsequent bone marrow stromal cell (BMSC) intervention assays.

Immunofluorescence staining

For experimental procedures, five samples were randomly selected from the animal specimens of each group. In the case of cell experiments, samples from three independent trials were used. Following sample pretreatment, permeabilization was executed by incubating the samples in Triton X-100 (Beyotime, P0096) for 15 minutes. Subsequently, the samples were blocked with blocking solution (Beyotime, P0102) at room temperature for 15 minutes. Primary antibodies against NRP1 (Affinity, China, DF7877), IFNγ (Wanleibio, China; WL02440), CD3 (Wanleibio, WL01420), STAT1 (Santa, USA, sc-398524), and Runx2 (Affinity, AF5186) were added in sequence and incubated overnight at 4°C. After being washed, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Elabscience, USA; E-AB-1055, E-AB-1056, and E-AB-1010) were introduced and incubated for one hour in the dark. The specimens were then mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Beyotime, P0131) and visualized under a fluorescence microscope. Using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA), background subtraction was performed, followed by quantitative analysis of the fluorescence signals.

Cell viability

Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay (Elabscience, E-CK-A362). Cells were treated and incubated with 10 μl of the detection solution for two hours, followed by absorbance measurement at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The relative cell viability was calculated by comparing the absorbance values of the treated groups with the control group.

Alkaline phosphatase assay

BMSCs were immobilized with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, BL539A) for 20 minutes at room temperature, as detailed in the protocol by the manufacturer’s instructions. Following a PBS wash, the cells were incubated with ALP staining solution (Beyotime, C3206) for 30 minutes, and the results were imaged under a microscope (Olympus, Japan). The ALP staining intensity was quantified using the ImageJ software.

Alizarin red S assay

Following a 21-day osteogenic induction protocol, BMSCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for a duration exceeding 30 minutes. Post-fixation, the cells were rinsed with PBS and subsequently stained with alizarin red S solution (Beyotime, C0138) for 30 minutes. Excess stain was removed by washing with PBS. The intensity of the alizarin red S staining was quantified using ImageJ software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

After preparing cell or tissue samples according to the manufacturer’s instructions, the IFNγ enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Beyotime, PI510) was used for detection. The ELISA protocol involved sequential addition of standards/samples, biotinylated antibodies, and enzyme conjugates to the microplate wells. Following the washing and colour development steps, optical density values were determined using an ELISA reader, and sample concentrations were derived from standard curves. Intergroup differences were evaluated by appropriate statistical analysis.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were loaded and separated using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked for 15 minutes at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with NRP1 (Affinity, DF7877), osteocalcin (OCN) (Wanlei, WLH4378), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (Proteintech, 60004) antibodies at 4°C. On the following day, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Wanlei, WLA023), and protein bands were visualized using ImageQuant LAS 4000 (GE Healthcare, USA).

RNA isolation and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using SevenFast RNAExtraction kit (Seven, China, SM130-02). Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed using a cDNA synthesis kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, China), followed by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with SYBR Green Master Mix (Accurate Biotechnology, China, AG11718). Statistical analysis

The bioinformatic analysis was collected and analyzed by Rstudio (v2023.06.1+524; Posit). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (v. 24; IBM, USA). Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For comparisons between two groups, the independent-samples t-test was applied when variances were homogeneous, whereas the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test was used when variances were heterogeneous. A p-value < 0.05 indicated statistically significant differences.

Results

T cells are pathologically suppressed in SIONFH

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms of SIONFH, we conducted transcriptomic profiling on peripheral blood samples from SIONFH patients and healthy volunteers. Subsequently, these transcriptomic findings, in conjunction with data from the publicly available GSE123568 dataset, were subjected to further in-depth exploration. Differential expression analysis, a widely used method in genomic research, identified 1,013 and 390 DEGs in SIONFH patients and healthy volunteers, respectively (Supplementary Figure S3A). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of these DEGs showed marked enrichment in a variety of immune-related functions and signalling pathways (Figures 1a and 1b, Supplementary Figures S1A and B). Accordingly, we proceeded to further analyze the putative differential immune cell populations. The ssGSEA across both datasets revealed the presence of distinct cellular subpopulations, consistently identifying activated CD8 T cells and Th1 cells in SIONFH (Figures 1c and 1e). To functionally assess T cell involvement, we applied the superantigen sec2, a specific T cell activator, to a SIONFH rat model. Histopathological evaluation via H&E staining demonstrated a significant reduction in the empty lacunae rate within the femoral head specimens of the sec2-treated group compared to controls (Figure 1f).

T cells have a stronger association with osteogenic differentiation

Subsequently, by intersecting with relevant datasets downloaded from the Genecards database, 45 T cell-associated DEGs, 468 osteogenic-related DEGs, and 99 osteoclastic-related DEGs were identified (Supplementary Fig. S2B). A PPI network was constructed to delineate the interaction patterns between osteogenic-related genes and osteoclastic-related genes (Supplementary Figs. S2C and D). Additionally, PCA was employed to compress the valid information of osteogenic-related, osteoclastic-related, and T cell-associated DEGs, which were respectively integrated into three composite variables: OB, OC, and T (Supplementary Figs. S2E to H). Spearman correlation analysis indicated a robust positive association between the OB and T variables (r = 0.8728), suggesting a strong trend of similar ranking order in their variations. Conversely, the OC and T variables demonstrated a negligible correlation (r = −0.065), implying no consistent ranking order relationship (Supplementary Fig. S2I). This indicated that in SIONFH, T cell-associated DEGs exhibit a stronger correlation with osteogenic-related DEGs. In light of this, we conducted further investigations through in vivo and in vitro experiments. Micro-CT analysis revealed a significant increase in the bone volume fraction (BV/TV) in the sec2 group, which consisted of SIONFH model rats treated with intraperitoneal injection of sec2, compared with the untreated model group (Figure 2a). To further validate our hypothesis, splenic T cells were isolated from rats and subjected to in vitro culture. The resulting cell culture supernatants were then harvested for subsequent experimental assays. Specifically, the supernatant obtained from untreated T cell cultures was referred to as control medium (Sup group). ALP and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining demonstrated that the osteogenic differentiation capacity of cells was enhanced in the activated T cell group (sec2 group) compared with the Sup group. The osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs was significantly enhanced by the treatment, with the most pronounced effect observed at a 10% concentration, as evidenced by the results in Figures 2b and 2c and Supplementary Figure S4. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) validated that the mRNA expression levels of Runx2, OPG, OSX, and BMP2 in the sec2 group were significantly elevated (Figure 2d). WB analysis also showed that the protein expression of OCN in the sec2 group was significantly increased compared to the Sup group (Figure 2e). In summary, we demonstrated that T cells have a stronger correlation with osteoblasts than with osteoclasts, and the supernatant of T cells activated by sec2 can effectively promote osteogenic differentiation.

IFNγ mediates activated T cell-induced osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs

Considering that IFNγ, a pivotal cytokine secreted by Th1 cells, plays a crucial role in immune regulation and is produced by activated T cells, we aimed to further explore its influence on osteogenic differentiation. In vitro anti-rat IFNγ recombinant mAb (anti-IFNγ) was employed to further elucidate the role of IFNγ in activated T cell-mediated promotion of BMSC osteogenesis. qPCR analysis revealed significantly elevated IFNγ expression in the sec2-activated group, whereas administration of anti-IFNγ markedly reduced IFNγ concentration in the supernatant (Figure 3a). ALP and alizarin red S staining demonstrated that the pro-osteogenic effect of activated T cells on BMSCs was effectively attenuated by anti-IFNγ (Figures 3b and 3c). Within the cytoplasm, STAT1 can associate with Runx2, preventing it from entering the nucleus to exert its osteogenic effect. Accordingly, facilitating the dissociation of STAT1-Runx2 constitutes a critical pathway for promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Immunofluorescence assays indicated a significant reduction in STAT1/Runx2 co-localization in the sec2 group, with increased binding observed in the anti-IFNγ-treated group (the group treated with supernatant of activated T cells supplemented with anti-IFNγ) (Figure 3d). Consistent with these findings, qPCR showed upregulated mRNA levels of OCN, and BMP2 in the sec2 group, whereas anti-IFNγ treatment substantially suppressed their expression (Figure 3e). In summary, within this subsection, we found that activated T cells promote the dissociation of the STAT1-Runx2 complex via the IFNγ cytokine, thereby facilitating the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.

NRP1 is a glucocorticoid-responsive regulator of IFNγ

In order to clarify the fundamental processes by which local factors in the femoral head affect T cell-induced osteogenic differentiation, we performed proteomic DIA analysis on necrotic and healthy regions of femoral head samples from SIONFH patients. Proteomic analysis identified 908 differentially expressed proteins. Among these, 19 were associated with IFNγ and T cells (Figures 4a and 4b). Machine-learning techniques, including least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), and random forest, selected 4, 16, and one significant features, respectively. NRP1 emerged as the highest-priority candidate, being consistently identified across all three algorithms (Figure 4c, Supplementary Figures S5A to D). Notably, NRP1 expression was elevated in necrotic regions (Supplementary Figure S5e).

To elucidate the role of sec2 in a SIONFH rat model, we performed transcriptomic profiling on the peripheral blood samples of the rats. Transcriptomic profiling of peripheral blood from rat models following sec2 intervention revealed 111 differentially regulated targets (Figure S5F). Sec2 treatment led to a significant reduction in NRP1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels in T cells, as evidenced by transcript analysis (Figure 4, Supplementary Fig. S5g to S5h). In summary, within this section, we screened and experimentally validated NRP1 as the upstream target governing T cell-mediated osteogenic differentiation. This target is upregulated in SIONFH, yet its expression is downregulated in activated T cells.

NRP1 agonism reverses sec2-mediated osteoprotection

To delineate the functional role of NRP1 in T cell-derived IFNγ secretion and subsequent osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, we employed the NRP1 activator certepetide (CEND1) for in-depth investigation. CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that concentrations of 1 nM and 3 nM CEND1 did not exert cytotoxic effects on T cell viability (Figure 5a). WB revealed that 1 nM CEND1 partially reversed sec2-induced NRP1 downregulation in T cells (Figure 5b). Consistent with NRP1 restoration, ELISA results indicated significantly reduced IFNγ production in activated T cells following 1 nM CEND1 treatment (Figure 5c). Subsequent experiments demonstrated that, compared with the sec2 group, the supernatant of T cells treated with CEND1 plus sec2 (CEND1 group) impaired osteogenic differentiation capacity, as evidenced by decreased ALP activity, reduced mineralized nodules in alizarin red S staining, and downregulated mRNA expression of BMP2, a key osteogenic marker (Figure 5d, Figure S6 A to B). Immunofluorescence quantification further validated reduced osteogenic potential, with signal intensity in the CEND1 group significantly lower than in the sec2-treated group (Figure S6C). In summary, we demonstrated that the NRP1 agonist mitigated the sec2-mediated osteoprotective effect, which confirms the role of NRP1 as an upstream regulator in activated T cell-mediated osteogenic differentiation.

Dexamethasone impairs osteogenesis via NRP1/IFNγ axis

To investigate glucocorticoid-induced immune dysregulation in SIONFH pathogenesis, we assessed dexamethasone effects on T cell-antigen-presenting cell (APC) interactions. Splenocytes pretreated with dexamethasone were co-cultured with untreated T cells, untreated APCs, or untreated T cells + APCs (Figure 6a). ELISA revealed that untreated T cells alone partially restored IFNγ secretion, with higher efficacy than APCs alone (Figure 6b), indicating T cells as primary targets of dexamethasone. CCK-8 assays confirmed that dexamethasone (0.1 to 1 μM) did not impair T cell viability (Figure 6c).

Dexamethasone (DEX) impairs osteogenesis via the neuropilin 1 (NRP1)/interferon γ (IFNγ) axis. a) Experimental design for IFNγ quantification by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). b) IFNγ concentrations in supernatants across experimental groups detected by ELISA. c) Cell counting kit 8 (CCK-8) assay detecting the effects of dexamethasone at varying concentrations on T cell viability. d) Assessment of osteogenic differentiation potential in bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) across experimental groups by alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining. e) Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis of NRP1 expression changes post-dexamethasone intervention, with quantitative analysis. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001. APCs, antigen-presenting cells; GAPDH, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase; NC, negative control; ns, not significant; OIM, osteogenic-induced medium.

ALP staining demonstrated enhanced osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs treated with supernatant from activated T cells (vs dexamethasone-treated T cells) (Figure 6d). WB and qPCR showed that dexamethasone partially reversed sec2-induced NRP1 downregulation in T cells (Figure 6e, Figure S7). Consistent with NRP1 restoration, dexamethasone reduced osteogenic capacity in BMSCs compared to sec2-treated controls, demonstrated by diminished ALP activity (Figure 6g). qPCR confirmed downregulation of osteogenic markers (BMP2, ALP, OCN) (Figure 6h). Immunofluorescence quantification revealed significantly reduced Runx2 fluorescence intensity in the dexamethasone group compared to sec2-treated controls (Figure 6i). In summary, the results of this subsection indicate that dexamethasone primarily acts on T cells rather than APCs. It upregulates NRP1 in T cells, suppresses the secretion of IFNγ by T cells, and attenuates the osteogenic differentiation-promoting effect of T cells.

In vivo validation of the NRP1/IFNγ osteoimmune axis in osteogenesis

To further clarify the role of glucocorticoids in regulating NRP1/IFNγ-dependent local bone formation in vivo, rats were stratified into control, model, sec2, and sec2 + CEND1 groups. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated a substantial upregulation of CD3 in the sec2 group, which was markedly attenuated in the sec2 + CEND1 group compared to the control group (Figure 7a). Consistently, serum IFNγ concentrations were significantly elevated in rats subjected to sec2 treatment, while a reduction was observed in the sec2 + CEND1 group relative to the sec2 group (Figure 7b). Further quantitative immunofluorescence analysis of femoral heads revealed that the sec2 group exhibited decreased NRP1 expression accompanied by enhanced IFNγ signalling. Conversely, administration of the NRP1 agonist CEND1 on this basis led to increased NRP1 expression and a concomitant reduction in IFNγ expression. Spearman correlation analysis confirmed a significant negative correlation between NRP1 and IFNγ intensity (r = -0.679) (Figure 7c). In summary, we established that the NRP1 activator CEND1 can reverse sec2-induced T cell activation, and elevated NRP1 expression diminishes IFNγ expression (Supplementary Figure S6).

Discussion

Immune cells play pivotal roles in maintaining skeletal homeostasis, with their dysregulation underpinning the initiation and progression of orthopaedic disorders.45,46 SIONFH is a refractory bone disease with obscure mechanisms, and restoring local bone homeostasis in the femoral head is pivotal for its treatment.47,48 Nevertheless, the precise role of osteoimmunology in the pathogenesis of SIONFH remains incompletely elucidated. This study integrates multiomics analyses with in vivo and in vitro validations to demonstrate that glucocorticoids upregulate NRP1 in T cells, thereby suppressing IFNγ secretion and impairing its osteogenic capacity. Herein, the NRP1/IFNγ axis is established as a novel preventive target for SIONFH.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses demonstrated that immune dysregulation exerts a pivotal role in disease pathogenesis. Consistent with prior reports,49,50 ssGSEA analysis revealed activated CD8+ T cells and Th1 cells as key immune subsets implicated in disease progression. Given that activated CD8+ T cells primarily mediate endogenous cytotoxicity, while Th1 cells specialize in combating exogenous insults—such as glucocorticoids, a core aetiological factor in SIONFH—we prioritized Th1 cells for mechanistic investigation.51,52 Functional validation using sec2, a superantigen that potently activates T cells, significantly reduced the porosity of the SIONFH rat model (vs untreated controls), suggesting that targeted T cell activation attenuates SIONFH. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms linking T cell activation to SIONFH remain elusive.

Given the critical role of restoring bone metabolic homeostasis in SIONFH intervention, we explored the correlation between T cell activation and bone remodeling equilibrium. Micro-CT analysis demonstrated that T cell activation re-established local bone metabolic homeostasis in the femoral head of SIONFH models. To determine whether activated T cells primarily regulate bone formation or resorption, we analyzed peripheral blood transcriptomes of SIONFH patients. The results revealed that osteogenic genes exhibited significantly greater complexity than osteoclastic counterparts, with T cell signatures showing a stronger correlation with osteogenic profiles, indicating a preferential coupling of T cells with bone formation in SIONFH. However, the regulatory effect of Th1 cells on osteogenic differentiation via IFNγ remains controversial, which prompts us to conduct further investigation.53,54 Our results demonstrated that activated T cells promote osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Collectively, sec2-driven T cell activation mitigates the progression of SIONFH primarily by enhancing BMSC osteogenesis rather than suppressing osteoclastogenesis.

IFNγ is reported as a signature cytokine of Th1 cells, and the differentiation degree of Th1 cells can even be evaluated by detecting IFN-γ secretion.55 Accordingly, we sought to investigate the underlying mechanism by which T cells promote osteogenic differentiation using IFNγ. Our findings revealed that sec2-mediated T cell activation markedly increased IFNγ secretion, thereby enhancing osteogenic differentiation. Conversely, administration of neutralizing antibodies reduced IFNγ levels to < 50 pg/ml and attenuated the expression of osteogenic-related genes, confirming that IFNγ is the key cytokine mediating the osteogenic effect of activated T cells on BMSCs. Subsequent literature review indicated that IFN-γ upregulates the expression of transcription factor Aiolos, which directly binds to conserved sequences in the STAT1 gene promoter region to drive STAT1 transcription. STAT1 forms a complex with Runx2, preventing nuclear translocation of Runx2 and thus inhibiting osteogenic differentiation.56,57 Further investigations revealed that the sec2 group (IFNγ > 850 pg/ml) diminished STAT1-Runx2 co-localization, whereas the anti-IFNγ treatment group (IFNγ < 50 pg/ml) enhanced the stability of this complex. These findings confirm that T cell-derived IFNγ triggers the dissociation of STAT1-Runx2, liberating Runx2 for nuclear translocation and thereby activating the transcription of osteogenic genes.

To elucidate the underlying mechanism by which sec2 intervention enhances IFNγ secretion in T cells, we identified NRP1 (a key protein associated with IFNγ secretion and T cell function) in SIONFH through screening. Transcriptomic analysis and in vitro experiments demonstrated that NRP1 expression is downregulated upon T cell activation. The potential mechanism involves reduced NRP1 expression following T cell activation, which may diminish the binding of SEMA3G to NRP1, thereby relieving the inhibition of T cell activation.58 Building on these findings, we hypothesize that NRP1 downregulation following sec2 intervention may underpin the enhanced T cell activation and increased IFNγ secretion.

To further validate that reduced NRP1 expression is a critical driver of enhanced IFNγ secretion by T cells and accelerated osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, we performed extended experiments using the NRP1 activator CEND1. Results demonstrated that upregulated NRP1 expression attenuated excessive IFNγ secretion induced by sec2 intervention, while downregulating osteogenic markers (e.g. BMP2 and Runx2), thereby impairing the osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs. In terms of the underlying mechanism, upregulated NRP1 expression diminishes cytoplasmic retention and inactivation of FOXO3a, thereby suppressing IFNγ secretion.59,60 In this part of the study, we verified that the decreased expression of NRP1 after sec2 intervention is an important cause of the increase in IFNγ.

This study further investigated the impact of glucocorticoids, a key pathogenic factor, on NRP1 and its downstream pathways. Given that sec2-mediated T cell activation necessitates APC participation, APCs were retained in the experimental system.36 Subsequently, to determine whether glucocorticoids exert their IFNγ-suppressive effect via T cells or APCs, glucocorticoid-intervened T cell-APC co-cultures were supplemented with fresh T cells or APCs. Results revealed that following DEX intervention, supplementation with T cells restored IFNγ concentrations to a greater extent, indicating that DEX primarily inhibits IFNγ secretion by acting on T cells. Furthermore, to rule out the possibility that DEX directly suppresses BMSC osteogenic differentiation (thereby compromising the validity of experimental results), we observed that BMSCs in the ‘activated T cells + DEX’ group exhibited stronger osteogenic differentiation capacity than those in the ‘DEX-intervened activated T cells’ group. This confirms that DEX significantly attenuates the pro-osteogenic effect of activated T cells. Based on these findings, the study validates that glucocorticoids predominantly target T cells, leading to reduced IFNγ secretion. Further investigations demonstrated that glucocorticoid intervention markedly upregulates NRP1 expression, accompanied by impaired osteogenic differentiation capacity. This segment of the research elucidates the mechanism by which glucocorticoids inhibit BMSC osteogenic differentiation by regulating the NRP1/IFNγ pathway.

Compared with existing literature, our study builds on the established regulatory role of the T cell/IFNγ/STAT1/BMSC axis in osteogenic differentiation, and further employs multiomics approaches to identify NRP1 on T cells as a potential target.61,62 Subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments were performed to validate these targets, with the aim of elucidating the molecular mechanism by which glucocorticoids suppress IFNγ secretion in T cells. In contrast to most current studies in the field of SIONFH, which mainly focus on bone metabolic homeostasis itself, our work goes a step further to explore osteoimmunity-related upstream mechanisms that trigger bone metabolic dysregulation.63-65 This not only deepens our understanding of the pathological processes underlying SIONFH, but also lays a solid foundation for future in-depth investigations.

Given the limitations of in vitro experimental evidence, this study further pursued in vivo validation. Results revealed that sec2 intervention increased the number of locally activated T cells in the femoral head of SIONFH models, whereas NRP1 upregulation reduced the count of activated T cells, indicating that enhanced NRP1 expression attenuates sec2-mediated T cell activation. Further assessments demonstrated that IFNγ concentrations in both the peripheral blood and local femoral head tissues of model rats significantly rebounded following sec2 intervention, only to decrease again upon NRP1 upregulation, confirming that elevated NRP1 inhibits T cell secretion of IFNγ. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis further verified a significant negative correlation between NRP1 and IFNγ fluorescence intensity values. These findings establish that glucocorticoids exert regulatory effects through the NRP1/IFNγ axis, suppressing local bone formation in the femoral head and thereby driving the pathogenesis of SIONFH.

This study has certain limitations. First, although SIONFH predominantly affects middle-aged and young males, this study only included male rats as research subjects without involving female rat models. Future studies should supplement control analyses with female rats. Second, vascular injury is a key pathological feature of SIONFH, however this study focused on the regulation of local bone metabolism in the femoral head mediated by immune cells, and did not systematically investigate vascular lesions, which will be an important direction for future research.

In summary, our research delves into the largely uncharted territory of osteoimmunity associated mechanisms in SIONFH. To our knowledge, this study pioneers the delineation of the potential molecular mechanisms underlying T cell-mediated regulatory roles in SIONFH pathogenesis. Our findings reveal that glucocorticoids, by upregulating NRP1 expression on T cells, suppress IFNγ secretion and stabilize the STAT1-Runx2 complex, thereby orchestrating the pathogenic cascade of SIONFH. This signalling cascade impairs Runx2 nuclear translocation and the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, ultimately disrupting the homeostasis of the femoral head. For the first time, our work elucidates this NRP1/IFNγ-mediated osteoimmunity axis, laying a mechanistic foundation for targeted prophylaxis against SIONFH .
