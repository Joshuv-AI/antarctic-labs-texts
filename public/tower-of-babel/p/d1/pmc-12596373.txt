Mortality and Causes of Death in Patients With Mycobacterium avium Complex Isolates Through 28 Years
Authors: Victor N Dahl, Andreas A Pedersen, Isik S Johansen, Michael H Ibsen, Andreas Fløe, Anders Løkke, Ole Hilberg, Aase B Andersen, Christian M Wejse, Troels Lillebaek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) disease, predominantly caused by Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), is an emerging global health concern. The evidence regarding MAC-specific mortality and causes of death remains limited. This study investigated mortality and causes of death among patients with MAC isolates.MethodsA nationwide study in Denmark (1991–2018) included all patients with MAC isolates, excluding those identified using gastric lavages alone. Data from the International Reference Laboratory of Mycobacteriology, Statens Serum Institut, Copenhagen, were linked with national registries. Patients were classified as having pulmonary (PMAC), extrapulmonary (EMAC), or disseminated MAC (DMAC) disease. Cumulative mortality rates were calculated, and adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) for mortality were estimated using Cox models, adjusting for age, sex, disease manifestation, and comorbidities.ResultsAmong 2,027 patients, 1-year mortality rates were 19% (PMAC), 15% (EMAC), and 52% (DMAC), increasing to 68%, 22%, and 84% at 10 years, respectively. Mortality decreased over time, especially for EMAC and DMAC. The risk factors for higher mortality included older age (aHR 1.04), male sex (aHR 1.85), DMAC (aHR 2.66), and higher comorbidity (aHR 1.21). Over time, age-related mortality hazards increased (0.4%/year on average), while hazards related to EMAC (-9%/year), DMAC (-13%/year), and comorbidities (−1%/year) declined. Chronic respiratory disease and HIV were the leading causes of death.ConclusionsDespite improvements over nearly three decades, PMAC remains associated with substantial mortality. This study offers valuable insights into mortality trends and risk factors for MAC disease, providing findings that can assist clinicians in delivering more accurate prognoses for different patient groups.Keywords: epidemiology, mortality, mycobacterium avium complex, nontuberculous mycobacteria, prognosis

Nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) disease, mainly pulmonary, has emerged as a significant global health concern [1]. Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) disease is the most prevalent species group, accounting for 31–71% of NTM isolates worldwide [2]. The predominant species within the complex include M. avium, M. intracellulare, and M. chimaera [3]. However, limited evidence exists regarding species-specific mortality and different disease manifestations.

A 2018 meta-analysis estimated a 5-year all-cause mortality rate of 27% among individuals with MAC lung disease [4]. In Denmark, nearly 40% of patients with pulmonary MAC (PMAC) die within 5 years, according to a previous study [5]. Although the mortality directly attributable to MAC disease is likely lower [6], accurately estimating it remains challenging. Still, treatment of MAC and achieving culture conversion has been associated with reduced mortality [7–10].

Extrapulmonary NTM disease, excluding disseminated disease, represents a heterogeneous group of disease manifestations for which mortality data are limited [11]. Disseminated MAC (DMAC) disease, by contrast, has historically been a common cause of death among people living with human immunodeficiency virus (PLWH), affecting up to 40% of individuals with acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) [12–14]. Fortunately, the incidence and mortality associated with DMAC in these patients have significantly declined with the introduction of antiretroviral therapy [13, 15, 16]. Nevertheless, MAC remains a concern in untreated PLWH, particularly given that about one-third of newly diagnosed PLWH in high-income countries still present with advanced HIV disease [17–19]. Similarly, an outbreak of disseminated Mycobacterium chimaera infections linked to contaminated heater-cooler units used in cardiac surgery has been associated with a case fatality rate of 45.5% [20].

In this study, we used nationwide registers to investigate mortality and causes of death among patients with MAC isolates in Denmark from 1991 through 2018.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Patient Eligibility

This nationwide, register-based study included all patients in Denmark with MAC isolates identified between 1991 through 2018. Data were retrieved from the International Reference Laboratory of Mycobacteriology, a centralized laboratory in Copenhagen responsible for all mycobacterial diagnostics in Denmark. Each patient was included only once, with the index date defined as the date of the first positive NTM isolate during the study period. Consistent with a previous study [21], patients with MAC isolates obtained solely from gastric lavage samples were excluded.

Data Sources and Variables

Using Denmark's national person identifier system [22], which enables cross-linking of data across multiple registers, we linked information from the International Reference Laboratory of Mycobacteriology [3] with data from the Danish Civil Registration System, the National Patient Register, and the Danish Register of Causes of Death [23–25]. The National Patient Register includes detailed information on in- and outpatient episodes, including International Classification of Disease (ICD) codes (8th and 10th revisions) and procedural codes [23]. These were used to identify and characterize comorbidities within the study cohort. In Denmark, ICD-8 codes were used from 1991 to 1994, and ICD-10 from 1994 to 2018 [23, 26]. Comorbidity data were available for the entire study period, ending in 2018. The Danish Register of Causes of Death provided data on the place, date, and cause of death (ICD-8/10 codes) for all registered Danish citizens, which were used in the mortality and cause-of-death analyses [24]. Detailed descriptions of these registers are available elsewhere [22–24].

NTM culturing, species identification, and the classification of patients into pulmonary, extrapulmonary, or disseminated MAC disease categories based on body site of isolation were performed as previously described [3]. To assess comorbidities, ICD-8/10 codes from the 3 years preceding MAC isolation (excluding the index date) were retrieved. Additionally, a Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated for all patients using 12 diagnostic codes from ICD-8/10, following the method by Quan et al [27].

Statistics

Descriptive statistics were presented as numbers and percentages for categorical variables and as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. Analyses were stratified by age, sex, and disease manifestation (i.e., pulmonary, extrapulmonary, and disseminated). Group differences were assessed using the Chi-squared test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables, as appropriate. To ensure patient anonymity, variables with fewer than three observations were reported as “not available” in compliance with national legislation.

The cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality was plotted by age, sex, common comorbidities (present within 3 years before MAC isolation; n > 100), disease manifestation, and MAC species, with group comparisons performed using 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) and log-rank tests. Cumulative mortality was also calculated for different diagnosis periods (1991–1999, 2000–2007, 2008–2018) to evaluate potential improvements in mortality over time.

A Cox proportional hazards model was used to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) for mortality, adjusting for age, sex, disease manifestation, and comorbidity (as measured by the CCI score). In line with previous studies [11, 28], average hazard ratios (HRs) and HRs for the interaction between the variable and time were calculated to account for non-proportional hazards, reflecting yearly increases or decreases in risk [29]. Follow-up was from the first MAC isolation until censoring by emigration, death, or the end of the study (mortality data were available until 2020).

The most common causes of death were reported for each disease manifestation. A P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using Stata 16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) and SAS 9.4 TS Level 1M5 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA).

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

During the study period, we identified 2,027 patients with MAC isolates (Supplementary Table 1) with a median age of 58 years (IQR: 30–86) and 50.5% male. The median follow-up time until censoring was 3 years (IQR: 9) until death or censoring. The majority had PMAC (59.7%), followed by extrapulmonary MAC (EMAC) (33.7%) and DMAC (6.6%). Patient characteristics varied significantly across disease manifestations (Table 1). Detailed lists of comorbidities, including CCI, are available in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Cumulative All-cause Mortality and Risk Factors for Mortality

Mortality was significantly lower for EMAC and significantly higher for DMAC compared to PMAC throughout the study period (Figure 1). One-year mortality rates were 19% (95% CI 17–21) for PMAC, 15% (95% CI 12–18) for EMAC, and 52% (95% CI 44–61) for DMAC. At three years, these rates increased to 36% (95% CI 34–39), 19% (95% CI 17–23), and 76% (95% CI 69–83), respectively, and by 10 years, to 68% (95% CI 65–71), 22% (95% CI 19–25), and 84% (95% CI 77–90).

Among children and adolescents (ages 0–17), mortality rates were very low (0.8%, n = 4/509). In contrast, mortality surged to 70.9% (n = 141/199) among individuals aged 18–39 years. Of these, 24 of 54 (44.4%) with PMAC, 69 of 86 (80.2%) with EMAC, and 48 of 59 (81.4%) with DMAC died. Overall, PLWH accounted for 102 of 141 deaths (72.3%; 45.8% of PMAC, 75.4% of EMAC, and 81.3% of DMAC deaths). Mortality for PMAC increased significantly with age, although the trend varied over time (Figure 2). For EMAC, no significant differences in mortality were observed across age groups. Males exhibited significantly higher mortality rates than females for both PMAC and EMAC (Figure 3).

Causes of Death

By the end of the follow-up period, 61.3% (n = 742) of patients with PMAC, 23.3% (n = 159) of patients with EMAC, and 82.7% (n = 110) of patients with DMAC had died. The median age at death was 74 years (IQR: 16) for PMAC, 44 years (IQR: 20) for EMAC, and 42 years (IQR: 19) for DMAC.

Chronic lower respiratory disease was the most common cause of death among PMAC patients (35.0%), whereas HIV disease was the leading cause of death for both EMAC (39.0%) and DMAC (49.1%) patients (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

In this study of 2,027 unique individuals with MAC isolates, we observed substantial variation in mortality rates depending on disease manifestation. DMAC was associated with significantly higher mortality compared to PMAC and EMAC. However, mortality rates declined over time for all manifestations. The main risk factors for increased mortality included older age, male sex, DMAC, and higher CCI scores. Importantly, hazards associated with age, EMAC, DMAC, and comorbidities shifted over time, potentially reflecting changes in patient demographics and clinical management. Chronic lower respiratory disease and HIV were identified as leading causes of death among individuals with MAC isolation, highlighting the importance of these comorbidities on patient outcomes.

Not surprisingly, mortality varied considerably across the heterogeneous disease manifestations. The 3- and 5-year all-cause mortality rates for patients with PMAC in our study were higher (36% [95% CI 34–39] and 49% [95% CI 46–52], respectively) than previous estimates from Denmark (33.2% and 39.7%) reported between 1997 and 2008 [5]. A meta-analysis of 9,035 patients across 17 studies reported a pooled 5-year all-cause mortality rate of 27% (95% CI 21.3–37.8), considerably lower than our estimate [4]. Mortality rates in the meta-analysis ranged from 10% to 48%, demonstrating a higher degree of heterogeneity. These differences are likely attributable to variations in study methodology, study period, patient population, and disease definitions. For example, our study included older data and excluded gastric lavages, which may influence mortality risk. For EMAC and DMAC, we observed lower and higher mortality rates, respectively, compared to PMAC. A recent study using ICD codes for patient identification reported a 5-year mortality rate of 26.8% (95% CI 23.1–31.0) among patients in Denmark with presumed extrapulmonary NTM. This finding is comparable to, but slightly higher than, our estimate of 20% (95% CI 17–23) [11]. The difference in mortality may reflect that patients identified through diagnostic codes are more likely to have clinically relevant disease compared to all patients with NTM isolation. We are not aware of any large population-based studies exclusively reporting on mortality in patients with EMAC or DMAC outside of Denmark, particularly not studies limited to PLWH. Most studies on extrapulmonary NTM disease have focused on specific clinical presentations (e.g., peritonitis) or specific populations (e.g., patients undergoing allogeneic stem cell transplantation) and have not been restricted to MAC as the causative agent [20, 30, 31].

Our analyses showed that mortality rates declined over time for all disease manifestations, particularly for EMAC and DMAC disease. These findings suggest that the management and awareness of NTM have improved in recent decades. However, these declines are more likely attributable, to a significant extent, to improvements in the management of underlying chronic diseases, such as COPD and other comorbidities [32, 33]. Additionally, the reduction in NTM isolation from extrapulmonary organs or multiple organs over time in Denmark may reflect improvements in HIV treatment [3, 13, 34]. In the United States, NTM-related deaths among PLWH have decreased dramatically, from 33% in 1999 to 4% in 2014. Interestingly, during the same period, NTM-related mortality rates increased among individuals without HIV infection [35].

Older age, male sex, DMAC, and higher CCI scores were significantly associated with a higher mortality hazard in our study, consistent with findings from previous research [6, 11, 28, 36, 37]. Interestingly, age-related mortality hazards have increased over time, whereas hazards associated with DMAC, EMAC, and comorbidities have declined. The increased age-related hazards over time may reflect an ageing population with a higher prevalence of chronic lung diseases (e.g., COPD), resulting in more individuals being susceptible to MAC disease and contributing to the rising incidence of pulmonary NTM isolation [3, 34]. Conversely, the declining hazards associated with DMAC, EMAC, and comorbidities are likely attributable to advancements in healthcare and improved management of these conditions, as previously discussed.

We did not observe any species-specific differences in mortality; however, our analysis was limited by the small number of deaths among patients with M. chimaera (n = 18) and “other’ species (n = 8). Previous studies have shown mixed results regarding M. intracellulare, with some suggesting better outcomes and others worse outcomes for patients with NTM pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) [38–40]. Additionally, a small study reported that M. colombiense bacteremia was associated with higher 30-day mortality compared to M. avium subspecies hominissuis [31].

Chronic lower respiratory disease and HIV disease were the most common causes of death in patients with MAC isolates [41]. To our knowledge, no previous studies have specifically investigated the causes of death in this cohort. However, MAC has historically been the predominant species causing disseminated NTM in PLWH, accounting for 96% of cases, and it has been strongly associated with increased mortality [42]. In individuals with advanced HIV, a MAC prevalence of up to 42% within 3 years has been reported [43], and autopsy studies have indicated that NTM infections are a frequent cause of death in this population [44]. Furthermore, a previous study demonstrated that DMAC coinfection in PLWH is associated with a threefold higher risk of mortality, independent of CD4 count, and a higher risk compared to HIV/TB coinfection [45, 46]. As discussed in recent studies, we cannot determine the cause of death more specifically than what is reported in the available data, as causes of death in Denmark are recorded by the certifying physician and are rarely confirmed by autopsy, limiting diagnostic precision [11, 28]. Nonetheless, we believe that MAC likely contributes to an excess mortality in patients with chronic lung disease or advanced immunosuppression, as has been reported in the literature [28, 47]. For PMAC, the causes of death coded as “chronic lower respiratory diseases,” including asthma, COPD, and bronchiectasis, likely reflect the patients’ underlying chronic lung conditions—particularly COPD, which is common among individuals with NTM-PD and associated with substantial mortality on its own. It is possible that NTM-PD contributed to clinical deterioration in these patients, but the classification does not allow us to disentangle NTM-PD from the underlying disease. Whether the cause of death is COPD itself, NTM-PD, or a combination of the two may be less clinically relevant. Since NTM-PD is almost always observed in patients with pre-existing lung disease, we find it implausible that many patients had NTM-PD without an underlying pulmonary condition.

A strength of the study is the nationwide inclusion of patients over 28 years, incorporating mycobacteriological and administrative hospital data. However, the study was limited by the lack of clinical information on symptoms and radiological findings needed to determine the clinical relevance of pulmonary isolates. Additionally, data on confounders of mortality, such as smoking status and body mass index, were unavailable. Patients may also have been misclassified as having EMAC rather than DMAC disease due to the reliance solely on microbiology data. This is supported by the high prevalence of HIV and HIV-related mortality in the EMAC group, as well as the substantial decline in mortality over time within this group. Furthermore, excluding gastric lavages may have led to the omission of patients with NTM disease [48]. Despite these limitations, our study provides important insights to help clinicians in managing MAC diseases and providing prognoses.

In summary, this study improves our understanding of mortality and its risk factors among patients with various manifestations of MAC disease. While overall mortality has declined, particularly for EMAC and DMAC, PMAC continues to be associated with substantial mortality, showing limited improvement over nearly three decades.
