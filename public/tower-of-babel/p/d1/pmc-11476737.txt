Accuracy of self‐reported treated hypertension in the women's health initiative: Comparisons with medication inventories
Authors: Michael J LaMonte, Grace M Milton, Connor R Miller, Kathleen M Hovey, Ahmed Soliman, Amy E Millen, Jean Wactawski‐Wende
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFew studies have reported on the accuracy of self‐reported hypertension history among older postmenopausal women, which was this study's objective. Participants were postmenopausal women enrolled in the Osteoporosis and Periodontal Disease (OsteoPerio) study, an ancillary investigation of the Women's Health Initiative Observational Study (WHI‐OS) at the Buffalo, New York, clinical site. Participants self‐reported their history of physician diagnosed hypertension treated with medication at WHI‐OS enrollment (1993–1998; n = 1342, mean age 63 years), then 3 years later at OsteoPerio enrollment (1997–2001; n = 1342), and again at OsteoPerio Year 5 follow‐up (2002–2005; n = 1020). At each time point, medication inventories were recorded and served as the criterion with which self‐report was compared in the present study. Physician diagnosed‐treated hypertension was also self‐reported annually on mailed health update questionnaires in the WHI‐OS and were compared against medication inventory at the subsequent clinic exam. Of those participants who self‐reported a history of hypertension at WHI enrollment, OsteoPerio enrollment, and OsteoPerio Year 5 follow‐up, 41.2%, 90.3%, and 94.4%, respectively, had anti‐hypertensive pills in their medication inventory. Across the three time points, sensitivity and specificity ranged from 0.72 to 0.98 and from 0.85 to 0.95, and kappa coefficients ranged from 0.52 to 0.79 when comparing self‐report with medication inventory. For self‐reported newly physician‐diagnosed and treated hypertension on the annual health update questionnaire, 88.4% and 95.2% of those reporting hypertension had anti‐hypertensive pills in the subsequent medication inventory. In general, sensitivity and kappa were lower in women aged ≥70 versus < 70 years and in those with history of cardiovascular disease and diabetes compared to those without these comorbidities. In this cohort of postmenopausal women, self‐reported physician diagnosed and treated hypertension demonstrated moderate to high accuracy when compared against anti‐hypertensive medication use documented by pill inventory, particularly for those who were younger and managing fewer comorbidities.Keywords: assessment, epidemiology, hypertension, validation, women

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension, currently defined as a measured resting blood pressure of ≥130/80 mmHg, 1 is a major risk factor for adverse cardiovascular health outcomes, including heart disease, stroke, and kidney failure. 2 , 3 The likelihood of developing hypertension increases with age, remaining higher in men than women up to age 70, after which sex‐specific differences attenuate. 4 , 5 Change in vascular function following onset of menopause is a prominent mechanism underlying this shift. 6 , 7 Epidemiologic studies of older women are important for understanding and reducing the health burden of hypertension within this demographic which continues to grow in frequency within the U.S. population. In large epidemiologic cohort studies, assessment of hypertension status is often based on self‐reported diagnosis or medication use rather than actual blood pressure measurements. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Therefore, understanding the accuracy of self‐reported hypertension status is critical when interpreting epidemiological study results based on this assessment method. The objective of the present study was to determine accuracy of self‐reported hypertension status in a prospective cohort of older ambulatory postmenopausal women enrolled from the community setting.

METHODS

Participants in the present study were enrolled in the Buffalo Osteoporosis and Periodontal Disease (OsteoPerio) study, which is ancillary to the national Women's Helth Initiative Observational Study (WHI‐OS). WHI‐OS is a prospective cohort study that enrolled postmenopausal women, ages 50−79 years, at 40 clinical centers across the U.S. to investigate the incidence and determinants of chronic diseases among older postmenopausal women in the community setting. 12 Eligibility for WHI‐OS was U.S. residency, age 50−79 years, and documented post‐menopausal status based on absence of vaginal bleeding for 6 months (12 months for 50−59 years), had hysterectomy, or had ever used postmenopausal hormone therapy. Exclusions were anticipated to move from residence within 3 years of enrollment, existing disease likely to limit survival to three years or less, inability to provide written informed consent for enrollment. Between 1993 and 1998, there were 2249 women enrolled into the WHI‐OS at the Buffalo, New York, clinical center.

Women who initially enrolled in WHI‐OS at Buffalo were then invited to enroll in the OsteoPerio study, which coincided with Year 3 of WHI‐OS (1997‐2001). 13 Eligibility criteria for OsteoPerio were as follows: (1) have at least six teeth present; (2) no bilateral hip replacement; (3) no history of bone disease other than osteoporosis; (4) no history of cancer in the past 10 years; and (5) no other serious illness. Of the 2249 women in the Buffalo WHI‐OS cohort, 1362 (61%) women (ages 53−82 years) enrolled into the OsteoPerio study. Those who (1) remained free of cancer or immunosuppressive disorders and (2) were without a history of prolonged use of antibiotics or exposure to dental x‐rays in the preceding year were asked to return for a follow‐up study visit 5 years later (2002–2006; corresponding with Year 8 of WHI‐OS), of which 1026 (75%) women completed the reexamination. In the present analysis, 20 women from the OsteoPerio enrollment exam and 6 women from the OsteoPerio Year 5 exam were excluded due to incomplete data, resulting in n = 1342 and n = 1020, respectively, here. Additional details on the OsteoPerio cohort and study procedures are published elsewhere. 13 Written consent was provided for all aspects of WHI‐OS and the ancillary OsteoPerio study and was approved annually by the University at Buffalo Institutional Review Board.

Assessment of self‐reported hypertension

Hypertension status was assessed by questionnaire in the clinical setting at three different time points, WHI‐OS enrollment (1993–1998), OsteoPerio enrollment (1997–2001), and OsteoPerio Year 5 (2002–2006). The question used to determine history of physician‐diagnosed hypertension at WHI‐OS enrollment was “Did a doctor ever say that you had hypertension or high blood pressure?”. The response options were no or yes. At OsteoPerio enrollment, the question was “have you been diagnosed and/or treated by a physician for any of the following… (check all that apply)” with hypertension/High blood pressure as one of the listed conditions. For OsteoPerio Year 5, the question used was “since your study visit, have you been newly diagnosed and/or treated by a physician for any of the following… (Circle Yes or No),” with hypertension/high blood pressure as one of the listed conditions. Of those surveyed, 1211 participants had available response data for the hypertension question at WHI‐OS enrollment, 1336 participants had available response data for the hypertension question at OsteoPerio enrollment, and 1020 participants had available response data for the hypertension question at OsteoPerio Year 5.

Additionally, in the national WHI‐OS, newly physician diagnosed hypertension treated with medication was assessed annually on a mailed health update survey. The case finding question was “Since the date on the front of this form [date of last returned survey], has a doctor or other health care provider prescribed for the first time pills for high blood pressure or hypertension? (Circle Yes or No).” For the present study, we use annual health survey data from two intervals that fell between in‐person clinic examinations. The first interval was between WHI‐OS enrollment and OsteoPerio enrollment (approximately 3 years). The second interval was between OsteoPerio enrollment and OsteoPerio Year 5 (approximately 5 years). For these time periods, self‐report hypertension was considered only among participants’ who had not reported hypertension at the beginning of the respective interval but did report hypertension on an annual mail survey prior to the next in‐person visit and medication inventory.

Assessment of medication use

Hypertension medication use was assessed by medication inventory collected at WHI enrollment and at each of the OsteoPerio clinical examinations. Study participants were asked to bring to the clinic all medications (prescription and over the counter) including nutritional supplements currently used. Trained staff recorded the medication name, dose, and frequency of use directly off the container into an electronic database. The WHI medication records are coded using the Master Drug Data Base (Medi‐Span) according to therapeutic function (e.g., antibiotic, antipsychotics, anti‐hypertensives) and by therapeutic class (e.g., beta‐blocker, ACE inhibitor, diuretic). 14 The OsteoPerio medication records are coded using a medication coding system with comparable classification functionality. Medication inventory was taken at the same three time points as self‐reported history of physician diagnosed hypertension in the clinic setting. Self‐reported newly physician diagnosed hypertension identified on an annual mailed health questionnaire was compared against the medication inventory completed at the subsequent clinic examination.

Covariate information

Women completed questionnaire assessments pertaining to sociodemographics, medical history, and lifestyle at each of the three clinic examinations. Body weight (kg) and height (m) were measured using a calibrated clinical scale and wall stadiometer, and body mass index (BMI; kg/m2) was calculated. Resting blood pressure was measured using auscultatory methods after 5 min of quiet sitting in a straight backed chair, legs not crossed, with the first and fifth Korotkoff sounds recorded as systolic and diastolic blood pressure, respectively. 15 Two measurements were taken separated by 60 s and the average was recorded for analysis. Physical function was quantified using the RAND‐36 instrument producing a score of 0 to 100 with higher scores reflecting higher functional status. 16 Neighborhood socioeconomic status (nSES) was determined as a score ranging from 0 to 100 (higher score reflects greater affluence) based on census track information. 17

Statistical analyses

Participant characteristics were tabulated for each of the three examination time points, as well as the prevalence of self‐reported physician diagnosed and treated hypertension. To evaluate the accuracy of self‐reported treated hypertension, we quantified its agreement with the pharmacologic treatment of hypertension based upon medication inventories at each of the three examination timepoints (WHI‐OS enrollment, OsteoPerio enrollment, OsteoPerio Year 5). Self‐reports of treated hypertension that were accompanied by an anti‐hypertensive drug in the corresponding medication inventory were considered true positives, whereas self‐reports that lacked an anti‐hypertensive drug in the corresponding medication inventory were considered false positives. Self‐reported absence of treated hypertension accompanied by an anti‐hypertensive medication on inventory was considered a false negative, and self‐reported absence of treated hypertension accompanied by an absence of anti‐hypertensive medication on inventory was considered a true negative. Measures of agreement included sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and Kappa coefficients. We constructed 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for all measures of agreement. Stratified analyses were conducted at each time point to evaluate consistency of findings according to cohort subgroups defined by age (< 70 vs. ≥70 years) and by presence or absence of cardiovascular disease and diabetes for which recall of hypertension diagnosis might differ due to aging‐related memory decline or to more frequent chronic disease recall with multimorbidity. Certain antihypertensive medications might be used for conditions other than treatment of high blood pressure, such as beta‐blockers following myocardial infarction. This would spuriously weaken agreement between self‐report and medication inventory. Therefore, we completed a sensitivity analysis that excluded participants with history of CVD. All statistical procedures were completed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA) and p values are two‐sided at alpha .05.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Characteristics of the cohort at WHI‐OS enrollment, OsteoPerio enrollment, and OsteoPerio Year 5 are summarized in Table 1. The cohort was comprised mostly of well‐educated non‐Hispanic White women of middle‐to‐higher socioeconomic strata (< 3% non‐White; 78.8% attended college; nSES mean = 76.1). At OsteoPerio enrollment, mean age of the cohort was about 67 years, average BMI was 26.6 kg/m2, almost half (47.2%) were current menopausal hormone therapy users, 44.0% were past smokers, and 3.3% were current smokers. In terms of comorbidities, 5.0% of women reported a history of diabetes, 16.9% were taking pills for high cholesterol, and 8.8% had a history of prior clinical cardiovascular disease (myocardial infarction or stroke) at OsteoPerio enrollment.

Self‐reported hypertension at each examination

The prevalence of self‐reported diagnosed‐treated hypertension increased across the three in‐person study visits from 22.5% at WHI‐OS enrollment to 31.5% at OsteoPerio enrollment to 42.3% at OsteoPerio Year 5 (Table 2). When comparing self‐reported hypertension against medication inventory (criterion measure) in all women at WHI‐OS enrollment, sensitivity, specificity, and kappa were 0.98, 0.85, and 0.52, respectively. At OsteoPerio enrollment, sensitivity was lower (0.82) but specificity (0.95) and kappa (0.79) were higher than at WHI‐OS enrollment. At OsteoPerio Year 5, agreement was again lower relative to the previous examination (sensitivity 0.76; specificity 0.94; kappa 0.69).

Stratified analyses were conducted to evaluate agreement between self‐reported hypertension and medication inventories in cohort subgroups defined by age and comorbidity status. Prevalence of self‐reported hypertension was higher at each of the three examinations in women aged ≥70 years (30.3%, 40.2%, 45.0%) compared to < 70 years (20.8%, 26.8%, 39.5%), and in women with history of cardiovascular disease or diabetes (52.1%, 55.6%, 63.3%) compared to absence of these conditions (19.2%, 28.0%, 38.5%). Sensitivity was similarly high in women ≥70 and < 70 years at WHI‐OS enrollment (1.00 vs. 0.98) and remained similar by age group but somewhat lower at OsteoPerio enrollment (0.81 vs. 0.83) and OsteoPerio Year 5 (0.73 vs. 0.80). Specificity was similar for age groups but tended to increase across examination time points (WHI‐OS: 0.81 vs. 0.86; OsteoPerio enrollment: 0.96 vs. 0.95; OsteoPerio Year 5: 0.95 vs. 0.95). Kappa indicated similar moderate agreement for age groups at WHI‐OS enrollment (0.55 vs. 0.50), somewhat stronger agreement at OsteoPerio enrollment (0.77 vs. 0.79) and lower agreement in older compared to younger women at OsteoPerio Year 5 (0.63 vs. 0.76).

Sensitivity was similarly high in women with history of cardiovascular disease or diabetes compared to absence of these conditions at WHI‐OS enrollment (0.96 vs. 0.99), were lower and somewhat disparate at OsteoPerio enrollment (0.77 vs. 0.84) and Year 5 (0.73 vs. 0.80). Specificity differed at WHI‐OS (0.60 vs. 0.88) but was similarly high at OsteoPerio enrollment (0.94 vs. 0.95) and Year 5 (0.91 vs. 0.95). Kappa was low at WHI‐OS enrollment (0.38 vs. 0.53), higher at OsteoPerio enrollment (0.62 vs. 0.80), and disparate at OsteoPerio Year 5 (0.39 vs. 0.72).

Self‐reported hypertension on annual mailed questionnaire

Between WHI‐OS enrollment and OsteoPerio enrollment (approximately 3 years), among 1006 women without a history of hypertension at WHI‐OS enrollment, there were 138 (13.7%) cases of newly diagnosed‐treated hypertension self‐reported via mailed health update questionnaires (Table 3). Between OsteoPerio enrollment and OsteoPerio Year 5, among 737 women without a history of hypertension at the beginning of the interval, there were 173 (23.5%) cases of newly diagnosed‐treated hypertension self‐reported on the mailed health update questionnaires. When comparing self‐reported treated hypertension from the annual mailed health questionnaires against medication inventories at the subsequent clinic examination, for the interval between WHI‐OS enrollment and OsteoPerio enrollment sensitivity, specificity, and kappa were 0.58, 0.98, and 0.64, respectively. For the interval between OsteoPerio enrollment and Year 5, the corresponding values were 0.61, 0.98, and 0.64, respectively.

When stratified on age, sensitivity tended to be lower in women aged ≥70 compared to < 70 years for both time intervals (0.46 vs. 0.67; and 0.59 vs. 0.65), whereas specificity was high and comparable by age (0.98 vs. 0.98; and 0.97 vs. 0.99) (Table 3). The same patterns for sensitivity, specificity, and kappa were found when comparing women with history of cardiovascular disease or diabetes to those without these conditions (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis excluded participants with history of CVD (WHI‐OS enrollment: 106; OsteoPerio enrollment: 118; OsteoPerio Year 5: 96), whose medication inventory might include antihypertensive agents for reasons other than treatment of high blood pressure. Findings were materially the same as the primary results (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

Hypertension burden is substantial among older U.S. adults and epidemiological investigations will continue to provide insights on etiologic factors and potential intervention targets to enhance primary hypertension prevention with aging. Questionnaire assessments are a mainstay in epidemiologic research, so understanding whether hypertension status can be accurately self‐reported is critical when interpreting study findings. The results of the present epidemiologic cohort study in well‐educated postmenopausal women enrolled from the community setting indicate that physician diagnosed‐treated hypertension is self‐reported with moderate to high accuracy (kappa 0.52 to 0.79) when compared against medication inventories conducted in the clinical setting concurrent to reported hypertension status. We also found moderate agreement (kappa 0.64) between newly physician diagnosed and treated hypertension self‐reported on annual mailed health questionnaires and the medication inventory completed at a subsequent clinical examination. This is a novel finding, as few epidemiologic studies have evaluated multiple formats (in‐clinic and mailed questionnaires) for ascertaining self‐reported hypertension information. Stratification on age (< 70 or ≥70) and comorbidity (defined as history of cardiovascular disease or diabetes) suggested that agreement between self‐reported hypertension and medication inventory was somewhat higher in younger compared to older women, and in those without these comorbidities. Declining memory in older women and challenges managing multiple medications in those comorbidities could contribute to the pattern of findings in the stratified analysis.

Results of our study are generally consistent with previous findings in large epidemiological investigations that evaluated accuracy of hypertension self‐report. A substudy involving women aged 30−55 years and residing in the Greater Boston area who were enrolled in the Nurse's Health Study cohort demonstrated that hypertension could be accurately self‐reported by middle‐aged women. 11 Among 85 participants who reported having hypertension on a mailed questionnaire in 1980, 98.8% (n = 84) confirmed having hypertension on a separate questionnaire assessment 2 years later. Of these women, 51 had their medical records reviewed which yielded 100% agreement with self‐reported hypertension status, perhaps in part a reflection of these women being licensed nurses. Among 161 women who reported not having hypertension, only 6.8% (n = 11) were found to be false negatives. Self‐reported hypertension status was compared against measured blood pressure and/or use of anti‐hypertensive medications in the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Study (1988–1991). 18 Among all women aged 25 years and older, sensitivity was 0.77 and positive predictive value was 0.72. Among non‐Hispanic white women aged 60 years and older, sensitivity was 0.74 and positive predictive value was 0.90, which is comparable to our findings among predominantly white women of similar age at OsteoPerio enrollment and Year5 examinations (Table 3). In Korean adults aged 50−79 years, self‐reported physician diagnosed hypertension was compared against measured blood pressure and/or use of anti‐hypertensive medication yielding sensitivity, specificity, and kappa of 0.73, 0.98, and 0.72, respectively. 19 Again, these are consistent findings among adults of similar age as the women in our present study. In the Spanish subset of the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer (EPIC), a random sample of 248 adults aged 29−69 years had self‐reported prior hypertension diagnosis compared against medical record review. 20 Sensitivity, specificity, and kappa were 0.69, 0.90, and 0.61 among women; were 0.70, 0.86, and 0.58 among all adults aged 55 years and older; and were 0.85, 0.82, 0.68 among all participants with a history of prior cardiovascular disease (0.81, 0.77, 0.58 in those with diabetes). Unlike in our study, the agreement in EPIC‐Spain was high among those with history of morbid condition. Based on the published findings from epidemiologic studies, including our present study, there seems to be general consistency in observing moderate to high agreement between self‐reported history of diagnosed hypertension and criterion measures that have included medical record review, measured blood pressure and/or reported anti‐hypertensive medication, or medication inventory.

A substudy in the WHI‐OS at enrollment indicated self‐reported diagnosed and treated hypertension had strong test‐retest reliability with kappa 0.86 for agreement between two reports separated by three months. 12 Similarly, strong test‐retest reproducibility was demonstrated for other chronic morbidities common in postmenopausal women, including diabetes (kappa 0.86), hypercholesterolemia (kappa 0.82), myocardial infarction (kappa 0.93), and breast cancer (kappa 0.89), the consistency with which enhances our confidence in reported hypertension. A separate substudy in 223 WHI women compared medication inventory information with individual‐level participant pharmacy records for two blood pressure medications and found sensitivity, specificity, and kappa of 0.97, 0.99, 0.87, respectively, for beta‐blockers and 1.00, 1.00, 0.89, respectively, for calcium channel blockers. 21 While both medications can be prescribed for conditions other than hypertension, they were first‐line therapies for hypertension in the 1990s when WHI was enrolling. 22 These previous findings in WHI are comparable to a community‐based case‐control study on breast cancer wherein among controls, the sensitivity and specificity was 0.92 and 0.91 comparing self‐reported anti‐hypertensive drug use against pharmacy records over a 6‐month period and was 0.90 and 0.95 over a 2‐year period. 23 These findings on reproducibility of self‐reported hypertension and on strong agreement between reported blood pressure medication use and individual‐level pharmacy records strengthen confidence in the overall result of our present study showing reasonably strong agreement between self‐reported diagnosed and treated hypertension with medication inventories in older postmenopausal women.

There are limitations to the present study that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Questionnaire wording for history of prior hypertension diagnosis and treatment differed between WHI‐OS enrollment and the OsteoPerio examinations, which might have influenced lower agreement with medication inventory at WHI‐OS compared with OsteoPerio assessments. The results in the present study for comparison of self‐reported hypertension status with medication inventory at WHI‐OS enrollment might provide better perspective than when comparing hypertension identified by annual health questionnaires because frequency of the latter might preempt subsequent responses and make the agreement appear stronger. Use of medication inventory as the criterion measure, which required bringing medications into the clinical setting, might have aided participants in recalling hypertension history. On another hand, it is possible that we have underestimated the true concordance between self‐reported hypertension status and medication inventories. Participants may not have brought all their medications for inventory during the clinic visit. However, the previous WHI finding of strong agreement between medication inventoried blood pressure medications and individual‐level pharmacy records 21 makes incomplete inventories less likely. Hypertension treated by lifestyle modification alone was not considered here; however, at the time of WHI‐OS enrollment (1993–1998) and OsteoPerio enrollment (1997–2001), reliance on lifestyle alone for hypertension management was not a cornerstone in clinical guidelines. It was not until the 2003 clinical guideline update that therapeutic lifestyle change was more strongly advocated as part of initial treatment for patients. 24 As follow‐up time accrued in our study, a larger proportion of participants had anti‐hypertensive drugs in their medication inventories, and we cannot discern the primary indication for their prescription. This may explain in part why false negatives for self‐reported hypertension were more common at OsteoPerio Year 5 (NPV = 0.78) than earlier time points in the study. Hypertension awareness within the community might be substantial at present as compared to the time of WHI‐OS; thus, it is possible that findings might not completely generalize to a more contemporary population. Our study participants had higher levels of education, which might have enhanced accuracy of the questionnaire assessments.

CONCLUSIONS

In a cohort of older postmenopausal women enrolled from the community setting into the WHI‐OS and additionally into one of its ancillary studies (OsteoPerio), self‐reported physician‐diagnosed hypertension had moderate to strong agreement with medication inventories. This is true of hypertension status reported concurrent to medication inventory in the clinical setting and hypertension status reported on annual mailed health questionnaires prior to the clinic visit and medication inventory. Agreement tended to be higher in younger compared to older women (< 70 vs. ≥70 years) and in women without comorbidity (cardiovascular disease or diabetes). Our present findings together with those previously published in other cohorts suggest that self‐reported physician‐diagnosed and treated hypertension is reasonable for use in epidemiological investigations.
