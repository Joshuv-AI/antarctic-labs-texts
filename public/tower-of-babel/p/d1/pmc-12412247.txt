The association between the uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR) index and obesity in postmenopausal women: a cross-sectional analysis based on the NHANES
Authors: Jing Tian, Yiming Huang, Zhun Xiao, Shuaipeng Yuan, Suping Ma, Xiaonuo Zhao, Yang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity has emerged as a critical global public health challenge. Postmenopausal women experience significantly elevated risks of metabolic disorders and a marked increase in obesity prevalence due to declining estrogen levels. The uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR), an emerging biomarker for metabolic syndrome, is gaining clinical recognition. This study systematically investigates the association between the UHR index and obesity in postmenopausal women and evaluates its predictive value.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 7,811 postmenopausal women from the 2005–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Weighted multivariable logistic regression models, adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, behavioral patterns, and clinical covariates, were employed to examine the UHR index’s association with three obesity indices: body mass index (BMI), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and weight-adjusted waist index (WWI). Robustness was assessed through stratified subgroup analyses, interaction tests, restricted cubic spline (RCS) modeling, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and sensitivity analyses to evaluate nonlinear relationships and predictive performance.ResultsThe weighted obesity prevalence was 44.05%. After full adjustment, the highest UHR quartile (Q4) showed significantly elevated obesity risks versus the lowest quartile (Q1): BMI-defined obesity (adjusted OR = 8.08, 95% CI: 6.49–10.09), WHtR-defined obesity (adjusted OR = 29.95, 95% CI: 17.08–52.51), and WWI-defined obesity (adjusted OR = 4.58, 95% CI: 3.70–5.67). Subgroup analyses revealed significant effect modifications by diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease status (P for interaction < 0.05 for all three obesity indices). The RCS analysis demonstrated a nonlinear dose-response relationship. ROC analysis indicated superior predictive performance for WHtR-defined abdominal obesity (AUC = 0.795, 95% CI: 0.778–0.813), with sensitivity analyses corroborating the primary findings.ConclusionThe UHR index exhibits a strong, dose-dependent association with obesity risk in postmenopausal women, persisting after comprehensive covariate adjustment. As a metabolic indicator, the UHR index provides clinically meaningful supplementation to conventional obesity assessments, particularly in capturing metabolically driven obesity risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02715-2.Keywords: Uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR), Postmenopausal women, Obesity, NHANES database, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Obesity has become one of the most serious global public Health challenges in the 21st century. According to the latest statistics from the World Health Organization (WHO), over 650 million adults worldwide are affected by obesity, with the global obesity rate having nearly tripled since 1990 [1]. Postmenopausal status is generally defined as the cessation of menstruation for 12 months or more (natural menopause), or permanent cessation due to medical reasons such as hysterectomy [2, 3]. During this stage, the decline in ovarian function results in a significant decrease in estrogen levels, triggering a series of metabolic disorders, including central fat distribution, reduced insulin sensitivity, dyslipidemia, and decreased basal metabolic rate [4, 5]. Postmenopausal women represent a high-risk population for obesity. Epidemiological studies show that the prevalence of obesity is generally higher in women than in men, peaking in the 50–65 age group (the high-risk period for postmenopausal women) [6, 7]. This trend has been consistently observed in population statistics from the UK, China, and other regions [8–10]. In countries and regions with lower sociodemographic indices, postmenopausal women show a more pronounced increasing trend in obesity prevalence [11].

In clinical practice, selecting robust obesity assessment indicators is crucial. Body mass index (BMI), defined by WHO as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²) [1], remains the most commonly used screening tool for obesity. However, BMI has limitations - it cannot distinguish between fat and muscle mass, nor reflect fat distribution characteristics [12, 13]. This has prompted researchers to develop more precise obesity assessment indicators: waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) reflects abdominal fat accumulation through the ratio of waist circumference to Height, with a typical cutoff value of 0.5 [14]; while the newly developed weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) corrects for weight effects by dividing waist circumference (cm) by the square root of weight (kg), showing greater sensitivity in predicting metabolic risk [15].

The uric acid (UA) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ratio (UHR) has emerged as a novel biomarker for metabolic syndrome, demonstrating unique value in assessing obesity and related metabolic disorders. For postmenopausal women, obesity is not simply a “morphological issue.” As mentioned earlier, the sharp decline in estrogen levels in postmenopausal women triggers a series of metabolic disorders that directly affect the two core components of the UHR index (UA and HDL-C): on one hand, hyperuricemia is often accompanied by systemic inflammation, and estrogen deficiency in menopause may exacerbate this inflammatory state, indirectly affecting UA metabolism [16]; on the other hand, estrogen deficiency inhibits HDL-C synthesis and metabolic activity, leading to decreased HDL-C levels [17]. This dual change of “increased uric acid and decreased HDL-C” makes the UHR index particularly sensitive in capturing the metabolic imbalance state in this population. Metabolic changes may appear earlier than obesity detected through anthropometric indicators, making the UHR index a potential “early warning” indicator. However, the predictive value of the UHR index for obesity in postmenopausal women remains unclear. Therefore, this study utilizes data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to examine the relationship between the UHR index and three obesity indicators—BMI, WHtR, and WWI in postmenopausal women. Our findings may provide a new biomarker for early obesity screening and metabolic risk stratification in this high-risk group, guiding precision prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) dataset (2005–2018) was obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm). All participants provided informed consent, and the study was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board.

Among the initial 70,190 participants, we excluded males (n = 34,709), those missing uric acid or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol data (n = 12,892), women who did not complete the reproductive health questionnaire or were not postmenopausal (n = 14,311), participants lacking complete BMI and waist circumference data for obesity diagnosis (n = 395), and those with missing covariate data (n = 72). Finally, 7,811 female participants were included in this study (Fig. 1).

Definition of exposure and outcome variables

NHANES personnel measured participants’ height and weight using standardized techniques and equipment at mobile examination centers (MECs). Waist circumference was measured by NHANES Health technicians who located the right iliac crest through palpation of the hip region. Measurements were taken at the end of normal expiration with an accuracy of 0.1 cm. This study incorporated three obesity surrogate indicators: BMI, WHtR, and WWI. The anthropometric measures were calculated using the following formulas:

BMI = weight (kg)/height (m²).

WHtR = waist circumference (cm)/height (cm).

WWI = waist circumference (cm)/weight (kg0.5).

According to clinical guidelines established by the American College of Cardiology (ACC) and the American Heart Association (AHA) [18], individuals with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² were classified as obese. Most studies suggest that WHtR ≥ 0.5 indicates excessive abdominal fat [19], and this threshold is applicable to most adults. In this study, individuals with WHtR exceeding 0.5 were defined as obese. Concerning previous literature [20], low WWI was defined as WWI < 11.25 ㎝/, while high WWI was defined as WWI ≥ 11.25 ㎝/. We classified individuals with high WWI as obese. In this study, obesity was determined as the outcome variable.

The exposure variable in this study was the UHR index. Blood samples collected from fasting participants in the morning were used to determine HDL-C and UA levels. The UHR index was expressed as a percentage and calculated using the formula: UHR (%) = [UA (mg/dL)/HDL-C (mg/dL)] × 100.

Assessment of menopausal status

The menopausal status in this study was evaluated using the NHANES Reproductive Health Questionnaire. The specific determination process was as follows: participants were first asked whether they had experienced menstrual periods in the past 12 months. Those who reported no menstruation were further questioned about the reason for cessation. Women who responded with “menopause” or “hysterectomy” were classified as being in postmenopausal status.

Covariates

The covariates collected in this study were selected based on previous literature [21, 22], clinical practice experience, and statistical rationale. Data were collected through standardized questionnaires, techniques, and equipment, including: Demographic information (age, race, education level, marital status, and family income-to-poverty ratio [PIR]); physical examination measurements (weight, height, waist circumference, BMI, systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP]); and laboratory tests (UA, HDL-C, total cholesterol [TC], serum creatinine [Scr], urinary creatinine, blood urea nitrogen [BUN], urinary albumin, fasting plasma glucose [FPG], glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c], and oral glucose tolerance test [OGTT]). Questionnaire data included: smoking status (categorized as never smokers, former smokers, and current smokers); alcohol consumption (participants were classified as non-drinkers or drinkers based on reported alcohol quantity and frequency); and sleep disorders (determined by affirmative responses to the question “Have you ever had trouble sleeping?“). Cardiovascular disease (CVD) was defined as a self-reported diagnosis of any of the following: congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack, or stroke. Diabetes mellitus diagnosis included any of: self-reported physician diagnosis, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL, or 2-hour OGTT plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL. Hypertension was defined by any of the following: self-reported physician diagnosis, SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medications. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was diagnosed by either estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥ 30 mg/g. Complete operational definitions for all study variables are documented in the standardized National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) methodology guidelines, available at: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis in this study strictly followed the analytical guidelines of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). Given that NHANES employs a complex sampling design (incorporating sample weighting, stratification, and clustering) to ensure national representativeness, we accordingly applied weighted statistical methods, integrating sampling weights, stratification, and clustering variables to guarantee result reliability and population representativeness. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) and STATA (version 18.0, Stata Corporation), with statistical significance set at two-sided P < 0.05.

Baseline demographic characteristics were described using weighted statistics: categorical variables were expressed as weighted percentages, with between-group differences assessed by weighted chi-square tests; continuous variables were presented as weighted mean ± weighted standard deviation, with between-group differences evaluated by weighted t-tests. To assess the association between the UHR index and obesity in postmenopausal women, the UHR index was categorized into quartiles (Q1-Q4), with the lowest quartile (Q1) as the reference. Three progressively adjusted models were constructed using weighted multivariable logistic regression analysis, and trend tests were performed for the UHR index quartiles. Simultaneously, stratified subgroup analyses were conducted based on demographic characteristics, behavioral factors, and comorbidities. Interaction tests were employed to evaluate the statistical significance of between-group differences (P for interaction < 0.05 indicated statistically significant differences). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used for nonlinear analyses in the overall population and diabetes/non-diabetes subgroups to identify possible threshold effects. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC) were utilized to quantify the predictive performance of the UHR index for obesity. To accommodate NHANES’s complex sampling design, weighted ROC analysis was performed, incorporating sampling weights, stratification, and clustering variables into AUC estimation to adjust for sampling probabilities and design effects. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify result robustness: Variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated for all covariates in regression models to detect multicollinearity (VIF < 5 indicated no significant multicollinearity); Firth-corrected logistic regression was employed to address potential low event probabilities or data separation issues; The UHR index was standardized as a continuous variable [formula: standardized UHR = (original UHR value - UHR mean)/UHR standard deviation], and the UHR index was re-categorized into Q1 (reference), Q2, and Q3 + Q4 (combined) groups, with weighted logistic regression analyses repeated for each grouping.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 7,811 postmenopausal women, of whom 3,441 (44.05%) met the BMI criteria for obesity. Table 1 presents the core baseline characteristics stratified by obesity status (complete data available in Supplementary Table S1). Compared with the non-obese group, the obese group had significantly higher UHR index values and a younger mean age. Significant differences were observed in racial distribution: the obese group had higher proportions of non-Hispanic Black and Mexican American women, and a lower proportion of non-Hispanic White women. Regarding behavioral characteristics, the obese group had fewer current smokers but higher rates of alcohol consumption and sleep disorders. Laboratory tests showed the obese group had significantly lower total cholesterol but higher serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels. Significant differences were found in chronic disease prevalence (all P < 0.001): the obese group had significantly higher rates of diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease compared to the non-obese group.

Association between the UHR index and obesity in postmenopausal women

Multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess the association between the UHR index and obesity risk in postmenopausal women, with results shown in Table 3 for BMI-defined, WHtR-defined, and WWI-defined obesity, respectively. After stepwise adjustment across three models, the analysis demonstrated that for each 1% increase in the UHR index as a continuous variable, the risk of BMI-defined obesity increased by 20% (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.17–1.23), WHtR-defined obesity by 58% (OR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.48–1.69), and WWI-defined obesity by 16% (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.14–1.19), with all models showing statistically significant results (P < 0.001). Further analysis by the UHR index quartiles revealed that compared to the Q1 group, the Q4 group showed significantly increased obesity risks. In the fully adjusted Model 3, the Q4 group exhibited 7.09-fold (OR = 8.09, 95% CI: 6.49–10.09), 28.95-fold (OR = 29.95, 95% CI: 17.08–52.51), and 3.58-fold (OR = 4.58, 95% CI: 3.70–5.67) higher risks for BMI-defined, WHtR-defined, and WWI-defined obesity, respectively. The positive association between the UHR index and obesity risk remained consistent regardless of whether the UHR index was treated as a continuous or categorical variable.

Subgroup analysis

Figure 2 presents the association characteristics between the UHR index and obesity defined by three different indicators (BMI, WHtR, WWI) in postmenopausal women. Subgroup analysis results demonstrated that the UHR index showed significant positive correlations with all three obesity definitions (all P-values < 0.001), but with specific effect modifiers: diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease status significantly influenced the association between the UHR index and obesity defined by all three indicators (P for interaction < 0.05), while hypertension affected the association between the UHR index and BMI-defined/WWI-defined obesity. Other demographic and clinical characteristics did not consistently show statistically significant interaction effects across all three obesity indicators. These findings suggest that when evaluating the relationship between the UHR index and obesity in postmenopausal women, special attention should be paid to the potential modifying effects of metabolic abnormalities.

Nonlinear associations and threshold effects

This study utilized RCS regression models to systematically evaluate the nonlinear dose-response relationships between the UHR index and obesity defined by three indicators (BMI, WHtR, and WWI), with analyses performed both unadjusted and fully adjusted for covariates (Fig. 3A-C). The UHR index demonstrated significant nonlinear positive correlations with all three obesity definitions (all P for nonlinear < 0.05). Threshold effect analysis using likelihood ratio tests (Tables 4, 5 and 6) revealed critical inflection points at UHR values of 9.04% for BMI-defined obesity, 6.32% for WHtR-defined obesity, and 8.35% for WWI-defined obesity. In the diabetes subgroup (Fig. 3D-F), the positive association between the UHR index and obesity remained consistent, with diabetic patients exhibiting significantly higher obesity risk than non-diabetic individuals. These findings indicate that the UHR index exhibits a clear dose-response relationship with obesity risk, while potentially demonstrating enhanced predictive value for metabolic risk in diabetic populations.

ROC curve

We systematically compared the predictive efficacy of the UHR index, UA, and HDL-C for different obesity types in postmenopausal women through three sets of weighted ROC curve analyses. The results in Fig. 4 show that the UHR index exhibited superior predictive value in all obesity definition scenarios (all AUC > 0.65), and its AUC value was significantly higher than that of a single biomarker. Among them, the UHR index had the best predictive efficacy for abdominal obesity defined by WHtR (AUC = 0.795, 95% CI: 0.778, 0.813), followed by the predictive efficacy for obesity defined by BMI (AUC = 0.695, 95% CI: 0.683, 0.707), and the predictive efficacy for obesity defined by WWI was relatively lower (AUC = 0.663, 95% CI: 0.651, 0.676), but it still had significant predictive value.

Sensitivity analysis

To verify the robustness of our primary findings, we conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses. First, variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis confirmed the absence of significant multicollinearity among variables (all VIF values < 5, mean VIF = 1.91; Supplementary Table S3), effectively excluding potential overestimation of effect sizes due to collinearity. Second, addressing potential bias from extreme sample sparsity in the UHR index Q4 group (only 21 non-abdominally obese cases) that might affect maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) in conventional logistic regression, we implemented Firth’s penalized likelihood regression. The corrected analysis maintained significant associations between the UHR index and abdominal obesity (OR = 3.28, 95% CI: 3.28–3.29), indicating our original effect estimates were not artifacts of small sample bias. Furthermore, we adjusted the grouping strategy by standardizing the UHR index and merging Q3 + Q4 groups (increasing non-abdominally obese cases to 82). Recalculated odds ratios still showed significantly elevated abdominal obesity risk in the merged Q3 + Q4 group versus Q1 (OR = 12.98, 95% CI: 9.06–18.60), with consistent effect size trends (Table 7). Collectively, these sensitivity analyses demonstrate stable, high OR values across different statistical approaches and grouping strategies, confirming the strong robustness of our study findings.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study enrolled 7,811 postmenopausal women, among whom those with obesity and elevated UHR index levels exhibited a higher prevalence of chronic metabolic diseases. After comprehensive covariate adjustment, the UHR index showed significant positive associations with obesity defined by BMI, WHtR, and WWI criteria. Notably, participants in the highest UHR quartile (Q4) demonstrated a 28.95-fold increased risk of WHtR-defined abdominal obesity (OR = 29.95, 95% CI: 17.08–52.51) compared to the lowest quartile (Q1). This pronounced association may be attributed to two key characteristics of the UHR index: its inherent amplification effect and threshold phenomenon. The computational nature of the UHR index creates a unique “magnification effect” for metabolic dysregulation. Elevated UA possesses intrinsic lipogenic properties [23], while reduced HDL-C diminishes inhibitory effects on cholesterol deposition [24]. When combined as the UHR index, it simultaneously captures both “metabolic injury” (elevated UA) and “loss of protection” (reduced HDL-C). This dual-pathway mechanism synergistically amplifies its impact on abdominal obesity, particularly magnifying its association with WHtR—an inherently sensitive indicator of abdominal adiposity. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear dose-response relationship between the UHR index and abdominal obesity (P for nonlinearity < 0.001). The rate of WHtR increase became markedly steeper when the UHR index exceeded 6.32% compared to levels below this threshold, suggesting the existence of a potential “metabolic threshold” at approximately 6.32%. Beyond this critical value, cascading metabolic responses—including accelerated fat accumulation and inflammatory activation—may be triggered, leading to the observed extreme effect sizes. Considering the initial sample sparsity in the UHR Q4 group, we conducted rigorous sensitivity analyses. The persistently high odds ratios across different statistical approaches and grouping strategies confirmed the robustness of these findings.

Consistent with our findings, existing studies have well-established the critical roles of UA and HDL-C in lipid metabolism and obesity pathogenesis. At the molecular level, UA directly regulates fatty acid synthase (FASN), a core component of the de novo lipogenesis (DNL) pathway. Experimental evidence demonstrates that UA upregulates FASN expression to promote fatty acid synthesis and intracellular lipid accumulation in both HepG2 cells and zebrafish models [25]. Further investigations revealed that knockdown of the lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) gene in UA-treated human HepG2 cells significantly reduced lipid deposition, confirming its direct involvement in UA-induced lipogenic effects [26]. Beyond direct actions, UA indirectly promotes lipid synthesis by inducing insulin resistance in peripheral tissues (including skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and liver). Animal studies show UA disrupts insulin signaling pathways, reducing glucose uptake while stimulating lipid synthesis [27]. The “chicken-and-egg” relationship between UA and insulin resistance creates a vicious cycle that exacerbates lipid accumulation [28]. HDL-C modulates lipid metabolism through multiple mechanisms: it uptakes surface lipids (including free cholesterol and phospholipids) released from triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TGRLs) during lipolysis, maintaining continuous fat breakdown mediated by lipoprotein lipase (LPL) while preventing accumulation of LPL-inhibiting byproducts; it also clears cholesterol-rich remnant lipoproteins via the “reverse remnant-cholesterol transport” (RRT) pathway, thereby reducing arterial cholesterol deposition and sustaining lipolysis [29]. Additionally, HDL’s anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties participate in metabolic regulation. For instance, HDL dysfunction under pathological conditions like diabetes exacerbates oxidative stress and impairs lipolytic metabolism [30, 31]. Serum UA levels show a significant inverse correlation with HDL-C. Analysis of xanthine oxidase (XO) expression in atherosclerotic plaques revealed XO negatively correlates with serum HDL-C levels (P = 0.010, r=−0.30) but positively associates with circulating UA, suggesting UA may reduce HDL-C by promoting oxidative stress [32]. A large clinical study involving 16,094 participants confirmed [33] that hyperuricemia and abdominal obesity synergistically lower HDL-C. The UHR index we selected appropriately reflects the dynamic balance between uric acid-induced lipid synthesis and HDL-C lipolysis function.

Postmenopausal women experience significant alterations in UA and HDL-C homeostasis, primarily mediated through estrogen-dependent mechanisms. Estrogen regulates the expression of uric acid transporters via estrogen receptors (ER). The cholesterol metabolite 27-hydroxycholesterol (27HC) activates estrogen response elements (EREs) on the SLC22A12 promoter through ER, thereby upregulating URAT1 transporter expression in renal tubules and promoting uric acid reabsorption [34]. Clinical studies demonstrate significantly reduced estradiol levels in hyperuricemic patients. Animal experiments confirm that estradiol benzoate (EB) injections upregulate ABCG2 expression and decrease serum uric acid levels [35]. Additional studies indicate estrogen suppresses intestinal and systemic uric acid production by downregulating xanthine oxidase (XO) [36]. Estrogen levels show positive correlations with both HDL-C concentration and functionality. Experimental research reveals [37] that estrogen upregulates lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT) gene expression, enhancing HDL-C synthesis and reverse cholesterol transport, thereby exerting Hepatoprotective effects. A retrospective observational study of 4,483 Indian immigrants found significantly higher HDL-C levels in women (particularly reproductive-aged women) compared to men (47.1 mg/dL vs. 40.6 mg/dL). Menopausal status (independent of age) was associated with decreased HDL-C levels [17]. Hormone therapy (HT) has been shown to significantly increase HDL-C concentrations and the quantity of medium-to-large HDL particles [38, 39].

Clinical studies have demonstrated [40] that both CT and MRI scans reveal a significant increase in abdominal adipose tissue among postmenopausal women, a phenomenon that persists even after adjusting for age. During the menopausal transition, the proportion of visceral fat rises from 5% to 8% in premenopausal women to 15%−20% in postmenopausal women [41], suggesting that estrogen deficiency may lead to fat redistribution, primarily manifested as abdominal fat accumulation. Our findings indicate that the UHR index exhibits high sensitivity and predictive value for abdominal obesity in postmenopausal women. As a novel composite metabolic biomarker, the UHR index combines robustness with cost-effectiveness, enabling the simultaneous evaluation of two critical metabolic pathways: purine metabolism and lipid metabolism. This approach overcomes the limitations of traditional studies that measure only uric acid or HDL-C in isolation. By elucidating the dynamic interplay between uric acid and HDL-C in postmenopausal women with obesity, the UHR index provides a more precise tool for the comprehensive assessment of metabolic disorders in this population. It is important to note that the UHR index is not intended to replace established anthropometric measures (e.g., BMI, WHtR), but rather to complement them by reflecting underlying metabolic dysfunction—a particularly valuable feature given the close association between postmenopausal obesity and estrogen-related metabolic disturbances. The clinical utility of the UHR index lies in its ability to identify individuals at risk of obesity due to metabolic dysregulation, even when their anthropometric measurements are borderline, through routine blood testing. Evidence suggests that integrated interventions—including estrogen regulation, adherence to healthy dietary patterns (e.g., Mediterranean diet [42, 43]), regular exercise (combining resistance training with aerobic activity) [44], smoking cessation [45], and moderate alcohol consumption [46]—can effectively modulate uric acid and HDL-C levels. These comprehensive approaches may help reduce the UHR index in postmenopausal women, thereby improving metabolic health and lowering the risk of obesity-related complications.

This study provides novel insights into obesity in postmenopausal women through several key findings: we have established for the first time that the UHR index serves as an effective biomarker for assessing obesity risk in this population; we have elucidated the population-specific characteristics of the relationship between the UHR index and different obesity phenotypes (BMI, WHtR, and WWI); and we have generated robust, large-scale evidence directly applicable to clinical practice. These findings not only advance our understanding of the pathological mechanisms underlying metabolic dysfunction in postmenopausal women but also provide practical tools for targeted screening and preventive interventions.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in the current investigation. First, although we adjusted for numerous potential confounding factors, residual confounding may persist due to the complex interplay of elements influencing both the UHR index and obesity. Second, while the cross-sectional design provides value for exploratory analysis, it precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between the UHR index and obesity. Third, certain variables (e.g., medical history) in NHANES relied on self-reported data, potentially introducing recall bias. Finally, although we utilized a nationally representative U.S. sample, the generalizability may be limited due to population-specific variations in genetic background, lifestyle factors, and healthcare systems, particularly when extrapolating findings to non-U.S. populations (e.g., Asian or European cohorts). The lack of external validation in independent cohorts (domestic or international) further limits the broad applicability of the UHR index as a metabolic indicator. To address these limitations, future research should: (1) employ longitudinal study designs to examine temporal relationships between dynamic UHR index changes and obesity incidence, thereby elucidating potential causal pathways; (2) conduct external validation across diverse populations, including non-U.S. cohorts, to enhance generalizability; and (3) implement multicenter studies with expanded sample sizes, particularly in sparse subgroups (e.g., high UHR strata), while establishing clinically meaningful UHR cutoff values to facilitate translation into clinical practice.

Conclusion

The UHR index demonstrates a significant positive association with obesity risk in postmenopausal women. This composite biomarker shows particular promise as a screening tool for identifying metabolically driven obesity in this population, providing valuable clinical utility beyond conventional anthropometric measures.
