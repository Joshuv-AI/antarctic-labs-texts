Biological aging phenotypes mediate gut microbiota effects on age-related macular degeneration subtype progression: genetic causality by mendelian randomization and mediation analysis
Authors: Yifan Zhou, Jiabiao Liu, Zhenyu Wang, Chen Huang, Qingyun Wang, Xiaotong Yu, Cuixia Dai, Di Zhao, Yuchen Cai, Tianyu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The mechanisms driving age-related macular degeneration (AMD) progression into two major but distinct vision-threatening subtypes, geographic atrophy (GA) and choroidal neovascularization (CNV), are unclear. This study identifies causal gut microbiota (GM) taxa involved in AMD and their connections to biological aging phenotypes, including epigenetic clock acceleration, telomere length, mitochondrial DNA copy number, 731 immune cell traits, and 91 inflammatory proteins through genetic prediction. Analyzing 207 GM taxa and 205 functional pathways alongside AMD progression GWAS data, we found that class_Gammaproteobacteria significantly influences both CNV and GA, and a bidirectional gut-retina axis involving Erysipelotrichaceae was also identified. Genus_Flavonifractor, species_Ruminococcus_obeum, and species_Streptococcus_thermophilus may attenuate AMD progression. Mediation analysis revealed pathways linking Ruminococcus obeum to GA progression via SSC-A expression on CD4 + T cells, and a CNV-associated pathway mediated by CD33dim HLA-DR + CD11b- cell counts. This study provides novel genetic evidence linking GM to dynamic AMD progression, offering genetic insights for future experimental research and clinical strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13568-025-01972-9.

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) represents the predominant cause of central vision loss in senior populations (Fleckenstein et al. 2021). Notably, the development of choroidal neovasculature (CNV) and geographic atrophy (GA) are the primary subtypes responsible for over 90% of severe visual impairment in AMD patients by incidence, which typically necessitate lucubration and clinical intervention (Kataoka et al. 2025). Nevertheless, existing mechanism research has mainly focused on dichotomous affected/unaffected AMD phenotypes, neglecting disease progression phenotypes. Therefore, the underlying reasons for the divergence into these two distinct advanced subtypes and their specific pathogeneses during the prolonged course of AMD progression remain poorly understood (Wong et al. 2022).

The ‘gut-eye axis’ hypothesis has garnered considerable interest in recent years (Floyd and Grant 2020), with increasing recognition of the role of gut microbiota (GM) dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of multifactorial neurodegenerative fundus disease like AMD (Li and Lu 2023; Mao et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023). Since AMD is a chronic progressive disease, existing research has underscored the pivotal role of GM in the dynamic disease progression (Saigal et al. 2025). Nevertheless, ‘temporal blindness’ is a common limitation in the existing GM-related literature, since most studies enroll comparable patients at a single disease stage, which inevitably leads to a cross-sectional design with a flawed capacity for causality establishment. As such, a majority of GM-related observational reports fall short in investigating the GM’s impact on dynamic disease progressions. To the best of our knowledge, no existing clinical observation has been attached to the GM role in the lengthy AMD progression, let alone the identification of causal GM taxa and potential mechanisms in diverse pathogeneses of GA and CNV.

GM may influence multiple pathophysiological processes in AMD progression, including immune dysregulation and metabolic disturbances (Grant et al. 2022; Xiao et al. 2023). Concurrently, intestinal dysbiosis has recently been classified as one of the hallmarks of biological aging, alongside other newly classified aging-related phenotypes, including epigenetic aging clock acceleration, telomere shortening, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation (López-Otín et al. 2023). However, the impacts of AMD progression-associated GM taxa, the genetic interplay and crosstalks of GM dysbiosis with other biological aging phenotypes in AMD development are yet to be discovered as well.

To address these gaps, the present study employed Mendelian randomization (MR), a novel analytical method capable of establishing genetic causality (Skrivankova et al. 2021), with an integrated and comprehensive design. First, we quoted a rare genome-wide association study (GWAS) dataset focusing on diverse disease progressions in AMD genetic studies, a longitudinal genome-wide bivariate time-to-event analysis on survival outcome (CNV or GA), with a mean follow-up time of 12 years in AMD patients (Saigal et al. 2025). This GWAS data provides unique SNP information and novel genetic insights into AMD disease progression, which enables the genetic investigation of GM’s causal role in the dynamic AMD progression. Next, we accepted the largest GM GWAS data of a shotgun metagenomic sequencing of human faecal samples, to accurately identify the GM taxa at the species-level. Precise identification of specific species eases future corresponding mechanism research. Additionally, 205 GM functional pathways were also encompassed as exposures in the present study, attributing to the shotgun metagenomic sequencing approach, thereby providing more comprehensive GM targets and functional indications for future studies in the ‘GM-AMD’ area. We also investigated the genetically predicted causal impacts of biological aging phenotypes, including epigenetic aging clock acceleration, leukocyte telomere length (LTL), mtDNA copy number, circulating inflammatory proteins, and immune cell traits, in the AMD trajectory. Finally, to further unravel the interplay and underlying mechanism within the genetically causal GM-AMD progression links, we estimated the mediation effect of potential biological aging phenotypes through a two-step MR (TSMR) and multivariable MR (MVMR) approach, thereby providing a novel genetic insight into the role GM’s impacts in the AMD disease progression from the perspective of biological aging.

Material and methods

Overall survey design

Initially, we obtained summary data from GWAS to investigate the causal relationships between GM, biological ageing phenotypes—including epigenetic ageing clock acceleration, leukocyte telomere length variation, 731 immune cell traits, and 91 inflammatory proteins—and AMD progressions to GA/CNV using a TSMR approach. The validity of the MR analysis is contingent upon is three fundamental assumptions (Lawlor et al. 2008). Subsequently, sensitivity analyses, including a reverse MR analysis, were conducted to ascertain the robustness and directionality of the TSMR findings. To delve deeper into the mechanism pathways from GM to AMD, a two-step mediation analysis, enriched with a MVMR, was employed to analyze the mediation effects of causal biological ageing phenotypes. Data sources

Gut microbiota

The GM data were derived from the Dutch Microbiome Project (DMP), including 7738 individuals of European descent (Lopera-Maya et al. 2022). According to a shotgun metagenomic sequencing of faecal samples, 207 taxonomic units (5 phyla, 10 classes, 13 orders, 26 families, 48 genera, and 105 species) were identified. Along with gut microbiome composition, 205 functional pathways were also encompassed by analyzing metagenomic sequencing data. In total, 412 GM features (207 microbial taxa and 205 functional pathways) were enrolled as exposures in the current MR analysis.

AMD progression to distinct ending events

To focus on the genetic variations representing dynamic AMD progressions and diverse AMD trajectories, we obtained genetic information from a genome-wide bivariate time-to-event analysis of 2721 Caucasians (Yan et al. 2018), a rare GWAS dataset of a longitudinal Genome-wide bivariate time-to-event analysis on survival outcome (CNV or GA), with a mean follow-up time of 12 years in AMD patients. According to its survival outcomes, we managed to identify specific genetic variations of ‘Disease progression to geographic atrophy form in AMD’ (GCST005360) and ‘Disease progression to choroidal neovascularization form in AMD’ (GCST005358). To our knowledge, it is the first GWAS study of disease progression (bivariate survival outcome) in AMD genetic studies, providing novel insights into the genetic predisposition in AMD development (Yan et al. 2018).

Epigenetic clocks

Methylation clock data (HannumAge, IEAA, PhenoAge, and GrimAge) were derived from a GWAS meta-analysis of biological ageing, consisting of 34,710 individuals of European ancestry from 28 cohorts. A detailed description may be found in the previous publication (McCartney et al. 2021).

LTL measurements and mitochondrial DNA copy number

Summary-level data on LTL were obtained from the OpenGWAS database (ID: ieu-b-4879), comprising approximately 472,174 individuals of European ancestry, and genetic variations of mtDNA copy number were also acquired from the OpenGWAS (ID: ebi-a-GCST90026372).

91 Inflammatory proteins and 731 immune cell traits

Aging-related chronic inflammation was represented by circulating inflammatory proteins and immune cell traits. We integrated GWAS data on 91 circulating inflammatory proteins from a meta-analysis of 11 cohorts involving 14,824 European ancestry participants (Zhao et al. 2023). Summary statistical data of 731 immune-related whole-genome features from 3757 individuals of European ancestry was obtained from GWAS (Orrù et al. 2020). The immune features encompass median fluorescence intensity reflecting surface antigen levels (MFI, n = 389), absolute and relative cell counts (AC, n = 118; RC, n = 192), and morphological parameters (MP, n = 32), which encompass mature stages of CDCs, monocytes, myeloid cells, TBNK (T cells, B cells, and NK cells), and Treg panels.

Detailed information on enrolled GWAS datasets is displayed in Table 1. The determination of the European ancestry and the Caucasian ethnic was genetically validated, primarily through the use of principal component analysis (PCA). Both the exposure (gut microbiota) and outcome (AMD progression) cohorts demonstrated consistent genetic backgrounds, predominantly of Caucasian ethnicity. This genetic homogeneity effectively minimizes concerns regarding population stratification and sample overlap in our Mendelian randomization analysis. Instrumental variable selection

We utilized the following stringent criteria to obtain qualified IVs. GM GWAS datasets rely on sequencing of fecal samples, which is relatively costly, leading to relatively fewer participants. Under these circumstances, the application of a relaxed p < 1 × 10−5 thresholds due to the polygenic nature of GM traits and limited SNP availability at stricter thresholds (p < 5 × 10−8) has been widely accepted in the majority of microbiome MR studies (Li and Lu 2023; Mao et al. 2023). Similarly, for IVs related to GM data in the present study, we employed a relaxed GWAS significance threshold of p < 1 × 10−5 for exposure-associated SNPs, ensuring adequate SNP quantity. Subsequently, we employed a chained unbalanced aggregation method to filter out SNPs in linkage disequilibrium (LD) with one another, using an LD threshold of R2 < 0.001, a clumping distance > 10,000 kb, and reference data from the 1000 Genomes Project European samples, thus minimizing the selection of SNPs in LD and preserving the independence of each SNP as a distinct source of genetic variation. We further selected SNPs with effect allele frequencies above 0.01 and excluded those with F-statistics below 10 to ensure the strength of the relationship between SNPs and exposure. F-statistics were derived using the formula F = Beta2 / SE2. This altered threshold was also applied for 731 immune cell traits. We applied the standard GWAS thresholds for SNPs associated with AMD progressions and other biological ageing phenotypes (p < 5 × 10−8).

Statistical analyses

Bidirectional two-sample MR

Initially, we employed a conventional two-sample univariate MR (UVMR) approach to assess the causal relationships between GM and AMD progressions to GA/CNV. For exposures with a single IV, the Wald ratio was utilized to infer causality. When exposures involved multiple IVs, we employed inverse variance-weighted (IVW), MR-Egger, Weighted median, and Weighted mode methods to ascertain causality, with IVW serving as the primary analysis. A reverse MR analysis was conducted to verify the direction of causality. The reverse MR methods paralleled the forward MR approach, merely designating AMD as the exposure and GM as the outcome. For MR results to be considered significant, reliance on IVW analysis was paramount. Statistical significance was determined by a corrected P value (PIVW) for multiple testing, employing false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) < 0.05. The causal relationships with a PIVW < 0.05 and PFDR < 0.05 were considered significant associations. Moreover, the causal relationships with a PIVW < 0.05 but PFDR ≥ 0.05 were considered as potential causal relationships. More importantly, we also required that the correlation coefficients across all four MR approaches shall be consistently directed to ensure the robustness of our estimations.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to ascertain the robustness of the inferred causal relationships. We assessed directional pleiotropy by examining the intercept of the MR-Egger regression; a non-zero intercept with p < 0.05 was interpreted as a statistically significant indication of genetic pleiotropy (Bowden et al. 2015). Heterogeneity among the IVs was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test, where smaller p values suggest greater heterogeneity and potential for directional pleiotropy. In cases where heterogeneity was detected, a random-effects IVW analysis was conducted to yield conservative and robust estimates. In the absence of heterogeneity, a fixed-effect model was applied.

Ultimately, only those causal relationships involving GM taxa or biological aging phenotypes that 1) exhibited no heterogeneity or pleiotropy; 2) demonstrated IVW results with a significance threshold of p < 0.05; and 3) showed consistent directionality of correlation coefficients across all four MR approaches were advanced to mediation analysis.

Mediation analysis of ‘GM–biological ageing–AMD progression’ pathway

Mediation analysis aims to disentangle further potential mechanisms underlying causal GM-AMD relationships via biological ageing phenotypes. We applied a two-step MR (Burgess et al. 2015), enriched with the MVMR (Sanderson 2021). The causal relationships between GM and biological ageing phenotypes were initially assessed using two-sample MR methods to estimate the effect size βEM. Subsequently, the causal relationships between biological ageing phenotypes and AMD were evaluated to estimate the effect size βMO. The directions of βEM, βMO, and βEO were tested following the logical framework outlined below. Lastly, MVMR was utilized to evaluate the causal effects of biological ageing phenotypes on outcomes (βMO*), adjusting for exposure effects (βEO*) (Carter et al. 2021). Mediators with a p-value < 0.05 in the MVMR-IVW were considered as the final causal biological ageing phenotypes. The indirect effect of GM on AMD through each biological ageing phenotype was calculated as the product of βEM and βMO*, while the direct effect of GM on AMD was βEO*. The total effect was determined as the sum of the direct and indirect effects (βEM × βMO* + βEO*). Moreover, the proportion of mediation was calculated as the ratio of the indirect effect to the total effect ([βEM × βMO*] / [βEM × βMO* + βEO*]).

The correlation coefficients of the indirect effect, direct effect, and total effect must adhere to the following logic: if the total effect βEO is positive, then both βEM and βMO should be either positive or negative; if the total effect βEO is negative, then βEM and βMO should be of opposite signs. Biological ageing phenotypes that do not conform to this directional logic were excluded from further mediation analysis.

Software

All analyses were conducted in the R Studio environment, utilizing R version 4.3.1. We employed the R packages “TwoSampleMR version 0.6.1”, “MendelianRandomisation version 0.10.0” and “MVMR version 0.3.1”.

Results

Genetically predicted causal GM taxa and AMD progressions

In total, 5 GM taxa (including 1 class, 2 families, and 2 species) and 9 GM metabolic pathways were associated with disease progression to GA (Figs. 2A and 3A; See Supplementary Tables S2 and 4 for full details of all TSMR and sensitivity analyses results). Meanwhile, 2 GM taxa (including 1 class and 1 genus) and 9 GM metabolic pathways were associated with disease progression to CNV (Figs. 2B and 3B; See Supplementary Tables S2 and 4 for full details of all TSMR and sensitivity analyses results). In parallel, when AMD progressions serve as exposures, the reverse MR analysis revealed potential causal impacts of disease progression to GA on 3 GM taxa/7 GM metabolic pathways, and disease progression to CNV on 1 GM taxa/3 GM metabolic pathways (See Supplementary Tables S3 and 5 for full details of all reverse TSMR and sensitivity analyses results). Sensitivity analysis

Along with outlined significant MR results, critical intermediate results, including the number of SNPs used as IVs, a detailed list of IVs, and F-statistic distributions, can also be found in Supplementary Tables 9 and 10. Briefly, all SNPs exhibited satisfactory validity, with all F values greater than 10, which assures that the identified genetic causal associations are robust against weak instrument bias. All TSMR results passed comprehensive robustness tests for heterogeneity and pleiotropy, with all p-values from heterogeneity analysis (Cochran’s Q test), MR-Egger intercept, and MR-PRESSO global test exceeding 0.05. Leave-one-out analysis further confirmed that no single SNP significantly influenced the genetic causal estimates (Supplementary Figs. S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7 and S8). In summary, sensitivity analyses demonstrated the reliability and robustness of these significant results. In addition, a PIVW < 0.05 and PFDR < 0.05 were considered significant associations, while a PIVW < 0.05 but PFDR ≥ 0.05 were presented as potential causal relationships.

Discussion

The current study pioneers an investigation into the comprehensive impacts of GM in the dynamic AMD progressions to diverse advanced events (GA/CNV) through the perspective of biological aging phenotypes, including epigenetic ageing clock acceleration, leukocyte telomere length variation, immune cell traits, and inflammatory proteins. Notably, existing large-scale genome-wide studies have mainly focused on dichotomous affected/unaffected AMD phenotypes but ignored disease progression phenotypes, which might be more crucial for understanding AMD development. Here, we discovered a rare GWAS dataset of a longitudinal observation on diverse AMD trajectories to distinct vision-threatening advanced events (GA/CNV), which provides specific AMD progression-associated genetic variants information, to better capture the dynamic function of GM within the lengthy course of AMD. When observational studies are limited, MR findings are particularly compelling, especially attributing to its capability of leveraging multi-GWAS datasets across multiple cohorts. Since no existing observational report has referred to the causal relationship of GM in the dynamic AMD trajectory, our MR results might also be important to future longitudinal observation in this area, providing testable hypotheses and theoretical evidence for microbiome-targeted AMD interventions.

Class_Gammaproteobacteria and lipid IVA biosynthesis, shared pathogenetic taxa/metabolic pathway of AMD progressions

One of our previous publications is the only existing literature concerning phylum_proteobacteria in AMD condition, in which we reported that phylum_proteobacteria abundance may increase in wet AMD patients (Zhang et al. 2023). Here, our MR analysis further provides a potential causal relationship between class_gammaproteobacteria and both AMD development to GA and CNV. Concurrently, we identified lipid IVA biosynthesis as the sole gut microbial metabolic pathway potentially linked to both GA and CNV development. Critically, Gammaproteobacteria (e.g., Escherichia and Enterobacter, known LPS producers enriched in diabetic retinopathy) leverage their LPS-rich outer membranes to drive systemic inflammation via endotoxemia (Pedersen et al. 2018; Serban et al. 2023). Lipid IVA serves as the tetra-acylated precursor of lipid A, the endotoxic core of LPS (Scior et al. 2013). Given the established role of LPS in multiple AMD subtypes and reports of upregulated lipid IVA biosynthesis in HLA-B27-associated uveitis (Larsen et al. 2023; Tsioti et al. 2024; Morandi et al. 2024), our MR findings may implicate a Gammaproteobacteria-lipid IVA-LPS axis as a unifying mechanism amplifying systemic inflammation and progressive pathogenesis in AMD development.

Self-reinforcing gut-retina axis, erysipelotrichaceae and GA

The identification of potential causal GM taxa exhibiting bidirectionality with AMD represents a pivotal advance, revealing a novel pathogenic paradigm wherein the microbiome acts as both a mediator and amplifier of disease progression. This reciprocal interplay transcends the classical unidirectional gut-retina axis, establishing a self-reinforcing pathogenic cycle that demands high-priority investigation into mechanisms and targeted interventions. Strikingly, our bidirectional MR analysis first establishes a potential causal feedback loop between elevated gut microbial abundance of genus_Erysipelotrichaceae_noname and disease progression to GA. Such a bidirectional loop may underpin GA’s relentless progression, where Erysipelotrichaceae-derived inflammatory mediators accelerate retinal damage, while advancing GA pathology remodels the gut microenvironment to favor this taxon’s expansion, thereby creating a pathogenic positive-feedback loop. This necessitates interventions targeting both directions: suppressing Erysipelotrichaceae (e.g., via phage therapy or bile acid sequestrants) while modulating neuroimmune signaling to disrupt disease-driven dysbiosis. Validating this model in GA animal models could unlock novel microbiome-targeted strategies to attenuate GA development.

Streptococcus_thermophilus and Ruminococcus_obeum, potential therapeutic targets for GA

The recent focus of clinical intervention of GA, complement inhibitors, is encountering ongoing controversies: while the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted approval in 2023 based on phase III trial data showing a modest reduction in GA lesion progression, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) rejected marketing authorization twice in 2024, citing insufficient risk–benefit justification. To date, we are still short of developing reliable and cost-efficient therapeutic management for the huge number of patients suffering GA progression. The present MR analysis predicted two specific species, Streptococcus_thermophilus and Ruminococcus_obeum, as potential causal protective taxa for disease progression to GA. Specifically, the Streptococcus_thermophilus is a well-established commercial probiotic in the dairy industry. On the other hand, mediation analysis further discovered that the protective effects of Ruminococcus_obeum may be mediated by SSC-A on CD4 + T cells, indicating its potential of immune modulation in the GA development, since AMD patients may encounter an imbalanced composition of CD4+ T cells, which leads to potent proinflammatory effects on immune cells and consequent tissue damage to retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (Chen et al. 2017). As such, our findings may propose novel insights and potential interventional targets for the management of GA, which is noteworthy in future research, including clinical intervention and experimental validation.

Divergent functions of family_Clostridiales and its subordinate genus_Flavonifractor in diverse AMD progressions

We noticed family_Clostridiales_noname was identified as a contributing taxon for disease progression to GA. Interestingly, one of its subordinate genera, Flavonifractor, was identified as a protective taxon for disease progression to CNV. The Clostridiales results align with a recent experimental report of dietary glycemia-induced AMD, where authors indicated order_Clostridiales being associated with AMD features (AMDf) in high-glycemia (HG) diet-fed mouse model, including RPE hypopigmentation and atrophy (Rowan et al. 2017). Our MR results further located and predicted a potentially similar contributing function of its subordinate family_Clostridiales, particularly in AMD disease progression to GA.

However, it is also noteworthy that a specific taxon under family_Clostridiales, genus_Flavonifractor, exhibited protective effects on disease progression to CNV. Members from the genus_Flavonifractor derive their names from the ability to metabolize dietary flavonoid compounds, generating substances with antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties, and they are potential producers of short-chain fatty acids, such as butyrate (Faramia et al. 2024). Although understudied in the AMD area, previous studies have pointed out that genus_Flavonifractor may suppress TNF-α expression in the inflammatory milieu (Mikami et al. 2020), mitigate antigen-induced immunological responses in a Th2-dominated environment (Ogita et al. 2020), thereby contributing to in vivo immune homeostasis. As such, the protective role of genus_Flavonifractor in AMD progression to CNV, and its divergent function with other genera under family_Clostridiales in diverse AMD progressions, deserve further attention.

This integrative MR analysis provides novel genetic evidence predicting potential causal links between specific GM taxa and dynamic AMD progression, complementing current research gaps in the ‘GM-AMD’ area. However, MR findings should be cautiously interpreted as theoretical evidence-predicting causal inference clues rather than definitive empirical conclusions. At present, empirical evidence on the dynamic relationship between GM and AMD is scarce, with no large-scale, and especially longitudinal observations available to validate these predicted associations. Future research may prioritize well-powered prospective cohort studies or controlled clinical interventions targeting candidate GM taxa identified here, and ideally integrate with multi-omics profiling and functional experiments, which will be essential to confirm our MR causality, elucidate underlying mechanisms, and assess translational feasibility in AMD prevention and treatment.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths that deserve attention. Primarily, whereas most MR analyses rely on cross-sectional GWAS data—a limitation pervasive in GM-related studies that fail to capture the dynamic disease progression—we utilized a unique longitudinal GWAS dataset tracking AMD progression to vision-threatening endpoints (GA/CNV). This approach enabled, for the first time, the establishment of genetic causality between GM taxa and dynamic AMD development. Consequently, our MR findings provide a critical foundation for future longitudinal investigations in this field. Second, quite a few MR studies on causal roles of gut microbiota relied on GWAS data of 16S rRNA gene-sequencing reports (Li & Lu 2023; Liu et al. 2023; Mao et al. 2023), limiting taxonomic resolution to the genus level and may not capture species-specific associations. We accepted the largest GWAS data of a shotgun metagenomic sequencing of human faecal samples, to accurately identify the GM taxa at the species-level. Precise identification of specific species eases future corresponding mechanism research. Additionally, 205 GM functional pathways were also encompassed as exposures in the present study. Third, we set a variety of potential mediators according to the novel theory of biological aging including GM dysbiosis, epigenetic aging clock acceleration, telomere shortening, mitochondrial dysfunction, and systemic inflammation. With rigorous mediation analysis, we managed to disentangle potential mechanisms underlying causal GM-AMD relationships via specific biological aging phenotypes, attributing to the capability of MR to leverage multi-GWAS datasets across multiple cohorts. Last but not least, we implemented stringent quality control measures to ensure robust genetic prediction power, including a requirement for consistent directionality of correlation coefficients across all MR methods. This rigorous approach may reduce false-positive findings and enhance the robustness of MR prediction.

Meanwhile, several limitations shall also be acknowledged. First, the limited statistical power of current microbiome and immune cell traits GWASs necessitated the use of a relaxed genome-wide significance threshold (p < 1 × 10−5) to ensure at least 3–5 independent SNPs for robust estimation. While this approach is supported and accepted by precedents in this field (Li and Lu 2023; Mao et al. 2023), and we followed a restricted selection of SNPs with F-statistics > 10, applied stringent LD clumping, and conducted multiple sensitivity analyses, the potential for weak instrument bias and residual horizontal pleiotropy may not be completely ignored. Additionally, the constraints in data sources and sample size may limit the statistical power and generalizability of our findings, especially for results from the GWAS data of 731 immune cell traits (Supplementary Table 11). Second, the reliance on a Eurocentric data source restricts the generalizability of our findings, given that GM composition and its disease associations can vary considerably across different ethnic groups. To enhance the reliability and applicability of our results, it is essential to conduct further analyses involving diverse populations. Third, while we have made efforts to address the influence of pleiotropy, the possibility of unrecognized pleiotropic effects remains, which could compromise the robustness and accuracy of our findings. Last but not least, MR findings should be cautiously interpreted as theoretical evidence-predicting causal inference clues rather than definitive empirical conclusions. Future studies integrating large-scale cohorts, high-resolution metagenomics, and longitudinal observational follow-up will be essential to validate genetically predicted causality and enhance the translational potential of our MR findings. Additional experimental studies are also necessary to clarify the intricate molecular mechanisms linking GM to the progression of AMD, thereby supporting the conclusions drawn from our genetic analysis.

Conclusions

This study provides the genetically associated evidence of potential causal GM taxa and GM metabolic pathways within the dynamic AMD progressions to distinct vision-threatening advanced events, geographic atrophy and choroidal neovasculature. We identified a pair of shared pathogenetic GM taxa and GM functional pathway for both AMD trajectories, a self-reinforcing gut-retina axis of Erysipelotrichaceae and GA, and several understudied GM taxa with promising potential as novel microbial targets in AMD intervention, offering unprecedented insights into the GM’s role in the dynamic AMD development. Mediation analysis also unravelled potential biological aging phenotypes linking genetically causal GM-AMD progression relationships, especially the pivotal mediating role of immune cell traits, shedding light on future mechanistic research.
