The Excelsatoxin A‐Receptor TMEM233 Modulates Nav1.8
Authors: George Oprita, Marvin J. A. Meyer, Daniel Rudolf, Inês C. A. Pombeiro Stein, Jennifer R. Deuis, Hue N. T. Tran, Thomas Durek, Klasfauseweh Tabea, Jami Sina, Stephen G. Waxman, Sulayman D. Dib‐Hajj, Alexandru Babes, Irina Vetter, Andreas Leffler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe transmembrane protein (TMEM) 233 mediates Excelsatoxin A (ExTxA)‐induced pain by removing fast inactivation of the sodium channel Nav1.7. In contrast, TMEM233 itself seems to stabilize the inactivated state of Nav1.7. ExTxA‐induced activation of dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons is only partly inhibited by tetrodotoxin (TTX), possibly indicating that the TTX‐resistant sodium channel Nav1.8 is also modulated by ExTxA. To address this possibility, we performed patch clamp and calcium imaging experiments on mouse DRG neurons and on neuroblastoma ND7/23 and CHO cells expressing Nav1.8 and TMEM233. ExTxA‐induced calcium influx in DRG neurons was almost completely inhibited by TTX applied in combination with the selective Nav1.8‐inhibitor suzetrigine (VX‐548). ExTxA removed fast inactivation of TTX‐resistant sodium currents in DRG neurons, as well as of recombinant human or rat Nav1.8 channels co‐expressed with TMEM233 in ND7/23 or CHO cells. The co‐expression of Nav1.8 and TMEM233 was associated with an ExTxA‐independent hyperpolarizing shift of the voltage‐dependencies of fast and slow inactivation, an impeded recovery from fast inactivation and an increased use‐dependent inhibition by the local anesthetic lidocaine. These effects were constant across physiological temperatures (~10°C, 21°C and 37°C), and TMEM233 also modulated temperature‐dependent properties of Nav1.8. Our data suggest that ExTxA‐induced pain is likely to involve a TMEM233‐mediated regulation of Nav1.8. Furthermore, TMEM233 seems to be a relevant interacting protein of Nav1.8 that modulates the channel's distinct functional and pharmacological properties. These data warrant further investigations into how TMEM233 regulates nociceptor excitability.

Introduction

Voltage‐gated sodium channels (Nav) are essential for neuronal excitability, and peripheral sensory nerves express several Nav α‐subunits [1]. The TTX‐resistant α‐subunit Nav1.8 is expressed in medium to small‐sized nociceptive dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons [2], where it produces a slowly inactivating current [3] that drives repetitive action potential activity [4] and seems to be responsible for an altered electrogenesis in both inflamed and injured sensory neurons [5]. Accordingly, Nav1.8‐knockout mice exhibit reduced pain‐related behavior in models for acute pain [6, 7], diabetic neuropathic pain [8], visceral pain [9], osteoarthritic pain [10], vincristine‐induced mechanical allodynia [11], and stress related hyperalgesia [12]. Nociceptor hyperexcitability can result from a post‐translational modification of Nav1.8. This modification is typically a phosphorylation mediated by various inflammatory mediators ([13, 14, 15, 16]), but it can also be due to glycosylation [17], ubiquitination ([18]), or a direct modulation by reactive substances including methylglyoxal or oxidants [8, 19]. Nav1.8 can also be modulated by interacting membrane proteins such as auxiliary β‐subunits [20], aquaporin‐1 ([21]), the multi PDZ‐domain protein Pdzd2 [22], and Magi‐1 [23]. Several natural toxins are known to modulate sodium channels, and recent reports have identified toxins that can directly activate or inhibit Nav1.8 ([24, 25, 26, 27, 28]).

A brief contact with the nettles of the stinging tree (Dendrocnide excelsa) can cause long lasting pain followed by weeks of mechanical and cold allodynia [29, 30]. This effect is mainly induced by Excelsatoxin A (ExTxA), a peptide that activates sensory neurons by removing the fast inactivation of Nav1.7 [29]. This modulation of Nav1.7 is mediated by TMEM233, a transmembrane protein expressed in a large subpopulation of both mouse and human DRG neurons [31, 32]. The physiological role of TMEM233 in DRG neurons is poorly understood, but the expression of TMEM233 is associated with an ExTxA‐independent stabilization of the inactivated state of Nav1.7 [33, 34]. Thus, in addition to being a receptor for pain‐producing toxins like ExTxA, TMEM233 may primarily be a relevant Nav‐associating membrane protein.

ExTxA‐induced calcium influx in DRG neurons was reported to be only partially inhibited by TTX [29], which may indicate that ExTxA also modulates Nav1.8. Indeed, transcriptomic data suggests that more than half of both mouse and human DRG neurons co‐express Nav1.8 and TMEM233 [31], and we previously reported on a weak but significant modulation of Nav1.8‐like TTX‐resistant currents by ExTxA in mouse DRG neurons [34]. In this in vitro study, we aimed to characterize in more detail the effects of TMEM233 on recombinant and native Nav1.8 channels, in the absence and in the presence of ExTxA. Taking into account that ExTxA‐induced pain often includes prominent thermal hyperalgesia or cold allodynia [34], and that Nav1.8 control the excitability of sensory neurons depending on the ambient temperature [7], we investigated TMEM233‐dependent effects at room temperature (~21°C), near body temperature (~37°C) and at a low temperature (~10°C). Our data describes TMEM233 as an ExTxA‐receptor that is necessary for the toxin induced strong removal of the fast inactivation of Nav1.8, but also as an interacting protein that can modulate relevant biophysical and pharmacological properties of Nav1.8.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Transient Transfection of cDNA

ND7/23 neuroblastoma cells were used for most Nav channel electrophysiology recordings. As was previously describes, ND7/23 cells enable a proper functional expression of Nav1.8 due to an endogenous expression of the β3‐subunit [35]. Cells were maintained in normal growth conditions, incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2, in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium, supplemented with up to 4,5 g/L D‐Glucose, 1% L‐glutamine, 10% FBS, 1% PenStrep, 25 mM HEPES and 3 mM taurine. Similarly, human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) T cells were maintained in standard cell culture conditions (5% CO2 at 37°C) in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Bio&Sell, Feucht, Germany) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Bio&Sell, Feucht, Germany), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Bio&Sell, Feucht, Germany), glutamine (Bio&Sell, Feucht, Germany). Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells stably expressing hNav1.8/β3 in a tetracycline‐inducible system (ChanTest, Cleveland, OH, United States) were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 2 mM L‐glutamine (GlutaMax; Gibco) in T‐75 flasks at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cells were passaged every 3–4 days using TrypLE Express (Gibco). Expression of hNav1.8 was induced 48 h prior to the electrophysiology assays by adding tetracycline (1 μg/mL) to the growth medium. Furthermore, the CHO‐hNav1.8/β3 cells were transfected with human TMEM233 (GenBank accession number NM_001136534.2) cloned into a pcDNA3.1 plasmid (GenScript, Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, China) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Control cells were transfected with an empty pcDNA3.1 vector. The ND7/23 and HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with the jetPEI (Polyplus transfection, Illkirch, France) transfection reagent, according to manufacturer guidelines. The plasmids used were human Nav1.8 (hNav1.8), rat Nav1.8 (rNav1.8), human TMEM233, enhanced Green Fluorescent Protein (eGFP), human TRPA1 (hTRPA1), rat TRPV1 (rTRPV1) and rat TRPV2 (rTRPV2). Cells were plated on 35 mm petri dishes in normal growth medium approximately 24 h after transfection and all recordings were conducted up to 48 h after transfection.

Chemicals and Solutions

The extracellular solution used for electrophysiology recordings consisted of (in mM) 140 NaCl, 3 KCl, 1 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 15 Glucose, pH adjusted with TEA‐OH to 7.4 at room temperature. The extracellular solution for automatic patch clamp experiments contained (in mM) 145 NaCl, 4 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 10 HEPES and 10 Glucose. The pipette solution for manual patch clamp experiments contained (in mM) 140 CsF, 10 NaCl, 10 HEPES, 1 EGTA, pH adjusted with CsOH to 7.4 at room temperature. For the automatic patch clamp, the intracellular solution contained (in mM): 140 CsF, 0.2 EGTA/CsOH, 10 HEPES, 10 NaCl with the pH adjusted to 7.3 with CsOH and osmolarity to 320 mOsm with sucrose. The calcium imaging solution contained (in mM): NaCl 145, KCl 5, CaCl2 1.25, MgCl2 1, glucose 10, HEPES 10 or MES 10 with the pH adjusted to 7.4. In order to establish neuronal viability in calcium microfluorimetry, the appropriate solution was supplemented to 60 mM KCl and was used at the end of recordings for every experiment as a positive control. ExTxA was synthesized as previously described [29, 34] and the working solution was always prepared fresh on the day of each experiment. The desired dilution was achieved in the employed extracellular solution with 0.1% BSA added. Lidocaine (Sigma Aldrich/Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), TTX (Biotrend Chemicals AG, Zurich, Switzerland), and suzetrigine (MedChemExpress EU, Sollentuna, Sweden) were diluted according to guidelines provided by the manufacturer.

Mouse DRG Culture

For DRG cultures, adult male C57Bl/6 mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and were immediately killed by decapitation. During the primary excision, the DRG tissue was kept in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM). Enzymatic digestion of the DRGs was achieved with a mixture of collagenase 1 mg/mL and protease 0.5 mg/mL for 60 min (both from Sigma‐Aldrich). The DRGs were afterwards dissociated using a fire‐polished, silicone‐coated Pasteur pipette. Isolated cells were plated on 12 mm coverslips pretreated for 30 min with poly‐L‐lysine (0.01 mg/mL, Sigma‐Aldrich) and cultured in a mixture of DMEM and F12 media supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% L‐Glutamine and 1% Pen/Strep (all from Biochrom, Berlin, Germany). Recordings on neurons were conducted 24 h after plating. All procedures of this study were approved by the animal protection authorities (local district government, Hannover, Germany). Animal studies are reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines [36].

Calcium Imaging

Sensory neurons in primary culture were stained with Fura‐2 AM (3 μM) facilitated with 0.01% pluronic F‐127 (both from Biotium Inc., Fremont CA, USA) dissolved in the calcium imaging extracellular solution for up to 50 min in normal incubator conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). All solutions were applied on the adhered cells with a gravity‐driven superfusion system. Recordings were conducted on an inverse microscope (Axio Observer D1, Zeiss, Jena, Germany), Fura‐2 excitation was achieved with a microscope light source and an LEP filter wheel (Ludl Electronic Producs Ltd., Hathorne, USA) to switch between 340 and 380 nm. Images were exposed for 20 ms for each wavelength, and acquired at a rate of 1 Hz with a CCD camera (CoolSNAP EZ, Photometrics). Data were recorded using VisiView 2.1.1 software (Visitron Systems GmbH, Puchheim, Germany). Background fluorescence was subtracted before the calculation of ratios. F340/F380 of ExTxA calcium influx was quantified as the mean value of the fluorescence ratio over the ExTxA stimulation period from which a baseline value was subtracted. Results are shown as means (± SEM). Only cells with a clear KCl response were taken into consideration. ExTxA induced calcium influx was considered valid if the mean fluorescence ratio over the 3 min of treatment was 5% larger than baseline.

Manual Patch Clamp

Plated ND7/23, HEK293T or mDRG cells were observed on an inverted Axiovert 135 microscope (Zeiss; Oberkochen, Germany), and isolated eGFP positive cells were selected when cell lines were used. Voltage clamp recordings were conducted using a EPC9 Patch‐Amplifier (Serial No 920366‐C, HEKA Electronic; Lamprecht, Germany). Working pipettes were pulled from borosilicate glass tubes (TW150F‐3; World Precision Instrument, Berlin, Germany), to a resistance of 1–2.5 MΩ. After achieving the whole‐cell configuration, cells were left idle for 5 min before starting the voltage protocols. For hNav1.8 recordings, the extracellular solution was supplemented with 200 nM TTX in order to block the endogenously expressed sodium channels in the ND7/23 cells. Working temperatures were established with a LKB Bromma 2219 Multitemp II Thermostatic Circulator. The extracellular solution temperature was determined using a Lab Thermometer LT‐101 (TFA Dostmann; Wertheim, Germany) in separate petri dishes under the same conditions, as described further. For the cold measurements, the thermocycler was set to 1°C and the environment was left to cool for 30 min in order to reach the working temperature of ~10°C. Prior to this, cells were taken out of the incubator and the growth medium was replaced with cold extracellular solution. A 5 min long waiting period was employed in order to reach a stable working temperature. For the heat measurements, the thermocycler was set to 65°C, and the dish was left to warm up for 30 min. Before positioning in the thermocycler, cells were taken out of the incubator and the growth medium was replaced with warm extracellular solution stored in the incubator. To determine the voltage dependence of slow inactivation, cells were held at −140 mV. Following a series of 10 000 ms step pulses ranging from −120 to +20 mV in 10 mV increments, a subsequent 10 ms test pulse to −10 mV, preceded by a 100 ms step to −140 mV to remove fast inactivation, was used to determine the remaining current. The proportion of slow‐inactivated channels was determined by normalizing peak current from each of the pulse sweeps to the peak current of the first sweep (I/I0). Current–voltage (IV) curves were determined by employing a series of step pulses (100 ms) ranging from −100 to +65 mV (5 mV increments) from a holding potential of −120 mV. Peak currents for each sweep were normalized to the largest inward current of the recording (I/Imax). Voltage dependence of steady‐state fast inactivation was determined by a subsequent 20 ms pulse to −10 mV immediately following a 100 ms depolarization step (from −120 mV to −10 mV in 5 mV increments) to assess the available non‐inactivated channels. Peak currents generated by the 20 ms pulse were determined and all quantified sweeps were normalized to the first (I/I0). A Boltzmann equation, namely y=A2+A1−A21+expx−x0dx, OriginPro was used to fit the normalized data in order to determine the V50. To determine the recovery time from fast inactivation, a 100 ms pulse to −10 mV from a holding potential of −100 mV was used to induce fast inactivation. The following recovery period was variable, ranging from 1.25 ms to 986 ms in 50% increments at −140 mV. A second 10 ms pulse to −10 mV was used to determine the time dependency of recovery from fast inactivation. The proportion of available channels was determined by normalizing peak currents from the last to the first pulse for each sweep. An exponential decay fit (OriginPro y = A1*exp(−x/t1) + y0) was used to calculate the time constants (τ1) of recovery from fast inactivation. Ramp currents for voltage dependent sodium channels were elicited by depolarizing ramps from −100 mV to +20 mV with decreasing durations, from 500 ms to 50 ms with a 0.75‐fold decrease each step. Ramp currents for TRP channels were elicited by 500 ms voltage ramps from −100 to +100 mV. Data was analyzed using the PatchMaster v2x73.2 Software (HEKA) FitMaster version 2x91 (HEKA), as well as Origin Pro 2017 (Microcal Software). Statistical significance was established using either unpaired t‐tests (OriginPro), when comparing two distinct data sets, paired t‐tests (OriginPro), when comparing different measurement on the same cell, or One‐Way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey test (OriginPro) for more than two data sets. Data is represented as box plots, the horizontal line is the mean, and the box edges are the S.E.M., while the whiskers are 1.5 S.E.M. The column graphs are represented as mean ± S.E.M. Each individual point represents one measurement.

Automated Patch Clamp

Automated whole‐cell patch‐clamp experiments in CHO cells were performed on a QPatch II automated electrophysiology platform (Sophion Bioscience, Ballerup, Denmark). Cells were detached using TrypLE Express at 37°C and resuspended in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM, Sigma‐Aldrich) supplemented with 25 mM HEPES (Sigma‐Aldrich), 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), and 40 μg/mL trypsin inhibitor from soybean (Glycine max; Sigma‐Aldrich). 16‐channel multi‐hole patch plates (QPlates, Sophion Bioscience) with resistance of 2 ± 0.04 MΩ and patch hole diameter of 1 μm were used. Cell positioning and sealing parameters were set as follows: positioning pressure −60 mbar, minimum seal resistance 0.1 GΩ, holding potential −100 mV, holding pressure −20 mbar. Whole‐cell currents were filtered at 8 kHz and acquired at 25 kHz. Series resistance compensation was set to 70%. Recordings with > 10 MΩ series resistance were excluded. TTX (1 μM) was added to the extracellular solution to inhibit endogenous TTX‐sensitive current. Current–voltage curves and voltage‐dependence of steady‐state fast inactivation were determined. Cells were held at −90 mV. The current–voltage (IV) relationship was assessed by applying a series of 500 ms pulses ranging from −110 mV to +85 mV in 5 mV increments. Then, a 10 ms pulse to +10 mV was applied to test for steady‐state fast inactivation. For IV curves, the peak current from each voltage step was normalized to the highest current elicited (I/Imax). For the steady‐state fast inactivation, the peak current during each voltage step was normalized to the maximal current elicited (I/Imax). Voltage‐dependence of steady‐state fast inactivation curves were fitted to a Boltzmann equation to obtain the V50 of fast inactivation. For the recovery from fast inactivation the holding potential was set to −90 mV. Cells were pulsed to +10 mV for 50 ms and then returned to holding potential for recovery that lasted 1 ms and increased in 50% increments each sweep. Following the recovery period, another 50 ms pulse to +10 mV was applied. The proportion of available cells was calculated by normalizing the second to the first peak current (I/I0). The plotted normalized current was fitted to a two‐phase association curve from which τ1 was retrieved. The persistent current was analyzed by normalizing the current at 40 ms of the depolarization to the peak current (I 40 ms /I peak ). Data was analyzed using Sophion Analyzer software (version 7.0) and Origin Pro 2017 (Microcal Software).

Results

TMEM233 Mediates an ExTxA‐Induced Modulation of Nav1.8

Gilding et al. demonstrated that an ExTxA challenge leads to calcium‐influx in mouse DRG neurons, and that TTX reduced this effect from 68% to 26% responding cells [29]. We first examined if Nav1.8 accounts for this remaining TTX‐resistant activation of DRG neurons by performing experiments with 200 nM TTX and 10 μM of the selective Nav1.8‐inhibitor suzetrigine. The relatively high 10 μM concentration of suzetrigine was chosen because Osteen et al. found that Nav1.8‐like Na+ currents in mouse DRG neurons required μM‐concentrations of suzetrigine to be fully inhibited [37]. When 200 nM ExTxA was applied without any inhibitors, we observed a large calcium‐influx (0.18 ± 0.02) in 25% (51/204) of all DRG neurons (Figure 1A,B). The application of TTX reduced both magnitudes of ExTxA‐evoked responses (0.11 ± 0.02) and the percentage of sensitive cells (12%, 26/217) (Figure 1A,B). Suzetrigine reduced the magnitude of ExTxA‐evoked responses (0.1 ± 0.01), but not the percentage (27%, 40/146) of responding DRG neurons (Figure 1A,B). When TTX and suzetrigine were co‐applied with ExTxA, we observed a significant reduction in both magnitudes (0.05 ± 0.01) and percentage of responding cells (8%, 12/150) (Figure 1A,B).

As these data suggest that Nav1.8 indeed contributes to the ExTxA‐induced activation of DRG neurons, we explored if this is due to a direct modification of Nav1.8. In order to isolate the TTX‐resistant current generated by Nav1.8 in DRG neurons, 200 nM TTX was included in all extracellular solutions to inhibit TTX‐sensitive α‐subunits. To minimize activation of the TTX‐resistant Nav1.9, cells were held at −80 mV, a holding potential at which Nav1.9 is largely inactivated [38]. In 5 of 6 investigated cells, ExTxA (200 nM) induced a rapid and strong removal of the inactivation of TTX‐resistant Na+ currents (Figure 1C,D, n = 5, paired t‐test, p < 0.001). Similar experiments were performed on neuroblastoma ND7/23 cells transiently expressing TMEM233 together with human or rat Nav1.8. Again, 200 nM TTX was applied in order to inhibit endogenous TTX‐sensitive Na+ channels. As demonstrated in Figure 1E,G, the ExTxA‐induced removal of inactivation was evident in cells expressing both hNav1.8 + TMEM233 (Figure 1E,F, n = 5, paired t‐test, p < 0.001) and rNav1.8 + TMEM233 (Figure 1G,H, n = 5, paired t‐est, p < 0.001). In ND7/23 cells expressing human or rat Nav1.8 but not TMEM233, the application of ExTxA only evoked a small reduction of the peak current amplitudes, with no apparent impact on inactivation (Figure S1A–F). Taken together, these data strongly indicate that ExTxA interacts with TMEM233 to modulate the activity of Nav1.8.

A small fraction (8%) of DRG neurons still displayed an ExTxA‐induced increase in intracellular calcium in the presence of both TTX and suzetrigine. To follow up, we briefly examined if the calcium‐permeable TRPV1, TRPA1, or TRPV2 channels—that are expressed in DRG neurons—exhibit a TMEM233‐mediated activation induced by ExTxA. However, HEK293T cells expressing TMEM233 together with one of these three TRP channels did not display any ExTxA‐induced increase of membrane currents evoked by the voltage ramps from −100 to +100 mV (Figure S1G–I).

TMEM233 Modulates Human Nav1.8 Expressed in ND7/23 Cells

We next studied ExTxA‐independent effects of TMEM233 on hNav1.8 in ND7/23 cells at room temperature (21°C–22°C). As shown in Figure 2A,B, the expression of TMEM233 did not result in a change of the voltage‐dependent activation (n = 38 and 25 respectively). The peak current densities were also not significantly altered by TMEM233 (Figure 2C, hNav1.8: −141 ± 12 pA/pF; hNav1.8 + TMEM233: −164 ± 16 pA/pF, p = 0.27, unpaired t‐test). However, the amplitudes of the non‐inactivating persistent currents at the end of the 100 ms long pulse were significantly reduced in the presence of TMEM233 (Figure 2D, hNav1.8: −10 ± 1 pA/pF; hNav1.8 + TMEM233: −7 ± 1 pA/pF, p < 0.05, unpaired t‐test). It was previously demonstrated that large persistent currents enable hNav1.8 to produce large ramp currents evoked by slow depolarizations [39]. To examine if TMEM233 also reduces the magnitudes of ramp currents, voltage ramps with different velocities (2.4–24 mV/ms) were applied. As demonstrated in Figure 2E,F, the mean current amplitudes of the resulting ramp currents were similar in cells expressing hNav1.8 as compared to hNav1.8 + TMEM233 (Figure 2E,F: hNav1.8: n = 9, hNav1.8 + TMEM233: n = 9, 500 ms: q = 0.9, 375 ms: q = 0.1, 281 ms: q = 0.37, 210 ms: q = 0.56, 158 ms: q = 0.6, 118 ms: q = 0.59, 89 ms: q = 0.93, 67 ms: p = 1.2, 50 ms: p = 0.98 one way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc tests). The expression of TMEM233 induced a significant shift of steady‐state fast inactivation (Figure 2G,H; hNav1.8, n = 23, V50–54 ± 2 mV; hNav1.8 + TMEM233, n = 14, V50–65 ± 2 mV, unpaired t‐test, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the recovery from fast inactivation was significantly impeded by TMEM233 (Figure 2I,J; hNav1.8: n = 9, τ = 20 ± 5 ms; hNav1.8 + TMEM233: n = 11, τ = 32 ± 2 ms, unpaired t‐test, p < 0.05). The expression of TMEM233 also resulted in a strong shift of the voltage‐dependency of slow inactivation to more negative membrane potentials (Figure 2K, n = 12 and 7 respectively). Rodent Nav1.8 was shown to exhibit a use‐dependent current reduction due to slow inactivation [40]. Although not reaching statistical significance, the co‐expression with TMEM233 indeed increased a modest use‐dependent current reduction in control solution at both 1 Hz (p = 0.07, unpaired t‐test) and 10 Hz (p = 0.06, unpaired t‐test) (Figure 2L, hNav1.8: n = 9 and 12; hNav1.8 + TMEM233: n = 11 and 11).

TMEM233 Modulates Human Nav1.8 Expressed in CHO Cells

In order to exclude the possibility that the observed effects of TMEM233 on hNav1.8 depend on specific properties of ND7/23 cells as an expression system, we also examined CHO cells expressing hNav1.8, the β3‐subunit and TMEM233. These experiments were performed with the QPatch II automated electrophysiology platform with slightly different experimental protocols as described in the methods section. As shown in Figure 3A,B, an ExTxA‐induced removal of inactivation of hNav1.8 was also evident in CHO cells (n = 12, p < 0.001, paired t‐test). While the expression of TMEM233 did not affect voltage dependent activation of hNav1.8 (Figure 3C,D, hNav1.8: n = 7; hNav1.8 + TMEM233: n = 14), it induced a prominent shift of steady‐state fast inactivation (Figure 3E,F, hNav1.8: n = 7, V50–31 ± 2 mV; hNav1.8 + TMEM233: n = 14, V50–43 ± 2 mV, n = 14, unpaired t‐test, p < 0.001). Furthermore, TMEM233 impeded recovery from fast inactivation (Figure 3G,H, Nav1.8: n = 11, τ = 1 ± 0.2 ms; hNav1.8 + TMEM233: n = 8, τ = 2 ± 0.4 ms, unpaired t‐test, p < 0.05).

TMEM233 Enhances Use‐Dependent Block Induced by Lidocaine

Given that expression of TMEM233 seems to stabilize the inactivated state of hNav1.8, we also asked if it modifies its pharmacological inhibition. As an established local anesthetic, lidocaine inhibits Nav1.8 in a state‐dependent manner, with a high affinity to inactivated channels [35]. In contrast, inhibition by the ultra‐potent hNav1.8‐selective blocker suzetrigine has been reported to exhibit a reverse state‐dependency under some conditions [41]. We examined tonic inhibition (0.1 Hz) on resting hNav1.8 channels in ND7/23 cells held at −120 mV. TMEM233 did not significantly alter tonic block by 300 μM lidocaine (Figure 4A,B, hNav1.8: n = 5, 40% ± 10%, hNav1.8 + TMEM233: n = 5, 40% ± 5%, unpaired t‐test, p = 0.61) or by 0.3 nM suzetrigine (Figure 4C,D, hNav1.8: n = 5, 50% ± 10%, hNav1.8 + TMEM233: n = 5, 40% ± 10%, unpaired t‐test, p = 0.81). While suzetrigine does not induce use‐dependent block on Nav1.8 [42], such an effect is pronounced for lidocaine [35]. In agreement with our observation that TMEM233 can enhance use‐dependent inhibition in control solution, it significantly enhanced use‐dependent block by 300 μM lidocaine at both 1 Hz (Figure 4E,G, n = 6, hNav1.8: 10% ± 2%, hNav1.8 + TMEM233: 40% ± 6%, unpaired t‐test, p < 0.01) and 10 Hz (Figure 4F,G, n = 5, hNav1.8: 30% ± 4%, hNav1.8 + TMEM233: 80% ± 7%, unpaired t‐test, p < 0.001).

TMEM233 Modifies hNav1.8 at Body Temperature

Very little is known about the physiological function of TMEM233 in sensory neurons. While the search for endogenous TMEM233‐agonists was out of the scope of this study, we asked whether TMEM233 modulates hNav1.8 in a temperature‐dependent manner. We first examined cells constantly kept at ~37°C, i.e., at body temperature. As is demonstrated in Figure 5A,B, the voltage‐dependency for activation was identical for hNav1.8 expressed alone or together with TMEM233 (hNav1.8: n = 14; hNav1.8 + TMEM233: n = 10). While cells expressing hNav1.8 + TMEM233 generated significantly larger peak currents as compared to cells with just hNav1.8 (Figure 5C, hNav.8: n = 14, −189 ± 21 pA/pF; hNav.8 + TMEM233: n = 14, −387 ± 69 pA/pF, unpaired t‐test, p < 0.01), the amplitudes of the persistent currents were similar in both groups (Figure 5D, hNav.8: −31 ± 4 pA/pF; hNav.8 + TMEM233: −35 ± 6 pA/pF, unpaired t‐test, p = 0.57). The effect of TMEM233 on fast inactivation was conserved at 37°C, i.e., the expression of TMEM233 resulted in significantly lower V50‐values (Figure 5E,F, hNav.8: n = 13, −54 ± 2 mV; hNav.8 + TMEM233: n = 11, −67 ± 3 mV, unpaired t‐test, p < 0.01). The recovery from fast inactivation at 37°C revealed a clear temperature‐dependent effect in cells expressing only hNav1.8, i.e., ~40% of the channels exhibited an instant recovery within the first 2 pulses (Figure 5G, hNav1.8: n = 13, hNav1.8 + TMEM233: n = 14). In cells expressing hNav1.8 + TMEM233, “instant recovery” was completely absent, and the time constant for recovery was similar to that observed for the slow fraction in cells expressing only hNav1.8 (Figure 5G,H, hNav1.8: n = 11, τ = 4.5 ± 1 ms, hNav1.8 + TMEM233: n = 11, τ = 7 ± 1, unpaired t‐test, p = 0.19). In contrast to recordings performed at room temperature, TMEM233 did not seem to increase slow inactivation of hNav1.8 at 37°C (Figure 5I, hNav.8: n = 10, V50 = −51 ± 1 mV; hNav.8 + TMEM233: n = 12, V50 = −62 ± 2 mV). Thus, TMEM233 seems to modulate hNav1.8 at body temperature as well, but the effects were slightly different from those observed at room temperature.

TMEM233 Modifies hNav1.8 at Cold Temperatures

Rodent Nav1.8 was previously demonstrated to be “cold resistant” and required for nociceptor excitability at cold temperatures [7]. Therefore, we performed experiments on cells held at approximately 10°C. Again, the voltage‐dependency of activation was not modified by TMEM233 (Figure 6A,B, n = 17 and 25 respectively). In the cold, the expression of TMEM233 was associated with a reduction of both peak current amplitudes (Figure 6C, hNav.8: −36 ± 5 pA/pF, n = 17; hNav.8 + TMEM233: −20 ± 2 pA/pF, n = 25, unpaired t‐test, p < 0.01) and persistent currents (Figure 6D, hNav.8: −9 ± 1 pA/pF, n = 16, hNav.8 + TMEM233: −4 ± 1 pA/pF, n = 24, unpaired t‐test, p < 0.01). The co‐expression of TMEM233 also resulted in a significant modulation of both fast inactivation (Figure 6E,F, hNav1.8: V50 = 47 ± 1 mV, n = 8; hNav.8 + TMEM233: V50 = 57 ± 2 mV, n = 9, unpaired t‐test p < 0.01) and recovery from fast inactivation (Figure 6G,H, hNav1.8: τ = 55 ± 3 ms, n = 8; hNav.8 + TMEM233: τ = 105 ± 10 ms, n = 10, unpaired t‐test p < 0.001). Similar to what was already demonstrated for rodent Nav1.8 [7], hNav1.8 displayed a cold‐resistant slow inactivation (Figure 6I, n = 7). However, the co‐expression with TMEM233 strongly increased slow inactivation at 10°C (Figure 6I, hNav1.8 n = 7; hNav1.8 + TMEM233: V50 = −85 ± 2 mV, n = 12).

Temperature‐Dependent Function of hNav1.8

We next looked at the data obtained at 10°C, 21°C and 37°C in terms of a primary effect on hNav1.8. Instead of comparing hNav1.8 versus hNav1.8 + TMEM233, we asked if certain functional properties of hNav1.8—without or with TMEM233—exhibit prominent temperature‐dependencies. When superimposing the current–voltage plots from the three different temperatures, it became evident that the voltage‐dependencies for activation were identical at 37°C and 21°C, but that cooling to 10°C resulted in a shift towards more depolarized potentials (Figure 7A). The expression of TMEM233 did not change this pattern (Figure S2A). The peak current amplitudes displayed a reduction at lower temperatures, but this was only significant from 21°C to 10°C (Figure 7B, One‐Way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey test, p < 0.001). This effect was even more evident in cells with hNav1.8 + TMEM233 (Figure S2B, One‐Way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey test, p < 0.001). Independent of TMEM233, the peak currents evoked at 10°C were remarkably small. In contrast, the persistent currents were not smaller at 10°C as compared to 21°C, e.g., possibly indicating a less pronounced “cold‐induced” block of persistent currents as compared to the peak currents (Figure 7C, One‐Way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey test, p < 0.001). However, the size of the persistent currents was significantly increased at body temperature (37°C), and the expression of TMEM233 did not alter this temperature‐dependency (Figure 7C, and Figure S2C). As previously demonstrated for Nav1.7 [43], these data suggest that the relative magnitude of the persistent current of hNav1.8 increases at cold temperatures. When the amplitudes of persistent currents were normalized to the peak current amplitude, we could indeed determine that the relative contribution of the persistent current was significantly increased at 10°C (Figure 7D and Figure S2D; One‐Way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey test, p < 0.001). The steady‐state fast inactivation was not significantly changed by the three different working temperatures, although a slight but non‐significant shift towards more depolarized membrane potentials could be observed at 10°C in both groups (Figure 7E, Figure S2E). Also independent of TMEM233 was a prominent slowing of the recovery from fast inactivation towards lower temperatures (Figure 7F, and Figure S2F, One‐Way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey test, p < 0.001). In cells expressing only hNav1.8, slow inactivation was marginal at 10°C and increased towards warmer temperatures (Figure 7G). In cells which co‐expressed hNav1.8 and TMEM233, the temperature‐dependency of slow inactivation was reversed, i.e., cooling aggravated slow inactivation by shifting the voltage‐dependency towards more hyperpolarized membrane potentials (Figure 7H). Thus, as expected, multiple properties of hNav1.8 are clearly regulated by temperature. TMEM233 seems to slightly modify the temperature‐dependencies of several properties, with the most prominent effect on slow inactivation.

ExTxA/TMEM233 Can Override Inactivation of hNav1.8 Caused by Slow Depolarizations or Cooling

Some of our data support previous studies showing that Nav1.8 can produce large ramp currents induced by slow depolarizations [39], and that it exhibits a relative resistance to cold [7]. However, our data also reveal limitations of these properties by showing that long voltage ramps as well as cooling to 10°C result in a strong inactivation‐induced reduction of hNav1.8‐mediated currents. We finally examined if ExTxA/TMEM233 can override the inactivation of hNav1.8 in these conditions. Indeed, small ramp currents evoked by 500 ms long voltage ramps at 21°C were strongly increased upon application of 100 nM ExTxA in cells expressing hNav1.8 + TMEM233 (Figure 8A, n = 9). Furthermore, small currents recorded at 10°C were strongly potentiated by 100 nM ExTxA (Figure 8B,C, n = 12, paired t‐test, p < 0.01). These observations suggest that TMEM233‐ligands such as ExTxA can tune nociceptor excitability by extending the physiological ranges at which hNav1.8 is functional.

Currents were recorded in normal extracellular solution (Control—black) or after ExTxA 100 nM application (ExTxA—blue). Discussion

The data presented in this study shows that TMEM233 is a bidirectional modulator of both human and rodent Nav1.8 orthologues. Similar effects of TMEM233 were previously reported for Nav1.7, and in addition to functional data, that study presented conclusive evidence for a physical interaction between Nav1.7 and TMEM233 [34]. Although our study is limited to functional data showing that TMEM233 modulates Nav1.8 to a similar extent as was shown for Nav1.7, it seems reasonable to assume that TMEM233 also directly associates with Nav1.8. Recent transcriptomic analyses of human and mouse DRG neurons suggest that Nav1.8 and TMEM233 are co‐expressed in a large population of DRG neurons [31]. The co‐expression seems to be particularly high in non‐peptidergic prurireceptors and polymodal nociceptors [31], and this expression pattern perfectly correlates with the intense pain and itch that follows contact with the nettles of the stinging tree. As there is little co‐expression of TMEM233 and the cold‐receptor TRPM8, the cold allodynia that can also be prominent following exposure to the stinging tree is unlikely to be mediated by TRPM8‐expressing neurons [31]. The pronounced allosteric effects of ExTxA/TMEM233 on Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 are quite remarkable, and our data suggest that even Nav1.8 channels that are almost completely inactivated by slow depolarizing ramps or extreme cold can be re‐activated by TMEM233 in the presence of ExTxA. Our data also shows that a TMEM233‐mediated modulation of Nav1.8 is relevant for the direct ExTxA‐induced activation of mouse DRG neurons, and our findings on recombinant hNav1.8 imply that this is also likely to apply for human sensory neurons. The inhibition of Nav1.8 with suzetrigine was not sufficient to reduce the percentage of ExTxA‐sensitive neurons, suggesting that Nav1.7 and possibly other Nav channels in these neurons are sufficient to drive action potential electrogenesis following modulation by ExTxA. However, the inhibition of TTX‐sensitive channels revealed a clear TTX‐resistant activity of ExTxA that was partially inhibited by suzetrigine. The mechanism(s) of the remaining ExTxA‐induced increase in intracellular calcium remain unclear, but it is possible that an incomplete inhibition of Nav1.8 by suzetrigine allows the channel to produce this effect [44]. In a clinical perspective, the long‐lasting pain caused by the stinging tree is likely to be more effectively alleviated by local application of non‐selective sodium channel inhibitors than by α‐subunit specific substances.

The mechanistic principles of this ExTxA/TMEM233‐mediated modulation of sodium channels are not fully understood, and there is a need for further studies on the binding of ExTxA to TMEM233 as well as to the physical interaction of TMEM233 with Nav α‐subunits [34]. While examination of these mechanisms was beyond the scope of this work, the opposite effects of “unbound” TMEM233 on recombinant hNav1.8 channels observed in this study suggest that there is a constant allosteric modulation that is regulated by the conformational state of TMEM233. In all protocols that examined properties of inactivated hNav1.8 channels (fast and slow inactivation, recovery from inactivation, use‐dependent inhibition), the expression of TMEM233 was associated with a significant stabilization of inactivated channels. This effect was constant across the three different temperatures and was evident in both ND7/23 and CHO cells. An obvious limitation of the study is that this effect of TMEM233 still needs to be examined in native DRG neurons. Nevertheless, TMEM233 seems to be a Nav‐accessory protein that can not only enhance but also reduce nociceptor excitability by putting a break on both Nav1.7 and Nav1.8. This may be the inherent property of resting or unbound TMEM233, but it may as well be a property that can be induced or further increased by yet unknown modulators of TMEM233. Considering the rather specific expression of TMEM233 in sensory neurons and the modulation of both Nav1.7 and Nav1.8, it is possible that the development of such substances may be a feasible strategy for the development of novel analgesics.

The varying expression levels of TMEM233 among subpopulations of DRG neurons may be relevant for their distinct functional properties. In agreement with the high expression levels of TMEM233 in non‐peptidergic DRG neurons, it has been demonstrated that Nav1.8‐mediated sodium currents in non‐peptidergic DRG neurons that bind the isolectin B4 exhibit a stronger slow inactivation and use‐dependent current inhibition as compared to “isolectin B4‐negative” neurons that are regarded to be peptidergic [45]. Moreover, in current clamp experiments, non‐peptidergic neurons display higher action potential thresholds and fewer action potentials induced by slow depolarizations [45, 46]. As our data demonstrate that the expression of TMEM233 results in an increased slow inactivation and use‐dependent inhibition, it is tempting to speculate about a role of TMEM233 as an important determinant for these different functional properties of non‐peptidergic and peptidergic neurons. To address this possibility, studies on DRG neurons derived from wildtype and TMEM233‐knockout would be expedient. While TMEM233‐knockout mice display reduced pain‐related behavior induced by ExTxA [34], their phenotype in other pain models has not been investigated. Surprisingly, the complete ablation of TMEM233‐expressing neurons did not seem to result in an obvious phenotype [47], which suggests, among other possibilities, the presence of compensatory changes or the functional redundancy of TMEM233 activity.

Overall, our experiments at 37°C and 10°C demonstrated that TMEM233 modulates hNav1.8 across these temperatures, except for slow inactivation that exhibited an almost reversed temperature‐dependency when TMEM233 was co‐expressed. While TMEM233 did not affect slow inactivation at 37°C, it enhanced inactivation at both 21°C and 10°C. In contrast, cells expressing only hNav1.8 exhibited less slow inactivation at lower temperatures. This property of hNav1.8 partially aligns with the previously reported cold resistance of the slow inactivation of rodent Nav1.8 [7]. Interestingly, our data suggest that this cold resistance of Nav1.8 is likely to be reduced in TMEM233‐expressing DRG neurons. Zimmermann and colleagues did not examine the effect of temperature on the recovery from fast inactivation [7]. Similar to what has been demonstrated on Nav1.7 [43, 48], we observed a strong cold‐induced slowing of repriming that was further increased by TMEM233. At 10°C, hNav1.8 (~80%) and hNav1.8 + TMEM233 (~60%) did not even fully recover within 100 ms. This reveals a relevant limitation of our protocol for slow inactivation that includes a 100 ms long step to −140 mV to allow recovery from fast inactivation. Thus, the prominent effect of TMEM233 at 10°C may at least be partially due to the fact that a larger fraction of channels resided in a fast inactivated state. Considering that Zimmermann et al. employed a similar protocol to examine slow inactivation, the cold resistance of rodent Nav1.8 as compared to Nav1.7 may have been due to distinct effects on temperature on recovery from inactivation. However, one should also keep in mind that human and rat Nav1.8 exhibit distinct functional properties [39, 49], and this might also include their regulation by temperature. hNav1.8 produces a larger persistent current compared to rodent Nav1.8 [39]. Our data show that the persistent current of recombinant hNav1.8 exhibits a clear cold resistance, as it was not reduced by cooling from 21°C to 10°C. The relative contribution of the persistent current to the total current was even strongly increased by cooling, and this effect was independent of TMEM233. Even if smaller in magnitude, this cold‐resistant property of the persistent current was also demonstrated for human Nav1.7 [43, 48].

Conclusions

Nav1.8 is regarded as promising target for novel analgesics [50, 51]. The ultra‐potent Nav1.8‐inhibitor suzetrigine (VX‐548) was recently approved for pain treatment in adults, but clinical trials on suzetrigine demonstrate only a partial analgesic effect [52, 53, 54]. This mismatch between pre‐clinical research on Nav1.8 and clinical trials underscores the need for further research into functional and pharmacological properties of hNav1.8 [55]. This study identifies TMEM233 as a bimodal modulator of hNav1.8. In addition to mediating an impressive ExTxA‐induced effect on hNav1.8, the co‐expression of TMEM233 alone can inhibit the activity of hNav1.8. These properties were predominantly observed in heterologous expression systems, and further studies in intact preparations are needed. Nevertheless, our results provide evidence for the physiological relevance of TMEM233 as an accessory protein to Nav1.8.
