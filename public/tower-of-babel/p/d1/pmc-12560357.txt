Association of remdesivir treatment with long-term mortality after COVID-19 hospitalization
Authors: Trisha Agarwal, Laurel E. Beaty, Nichole E. Carlson, Tellen D. Bennett, Lindsey E. Fish, David A. Mayer, Kyle C. Molina, Jennifer L. Peers, Brandon J. Webb, Mengli Xiao, Adit A. Ginde, Neil R. Aggarwal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffectiveness of remdesivir (RDV) treatment on short-term mortality and other outcomes has been well-studied, yet the impact of RDV on long-term outcomes is less well-known. The objective of this study was to determine if inpatient RDV use in survivors of COVID-19 hospitalization is associated with reduced mortality after discharge.MethodsThis is a retrospective observational cohort study of patients hospitalized with COVID-19 between November 2020 and October 2022 in three health systems in Colorado and Utah. Real-world data were identified from electronic health records and state-level vaccination and mortality records. Our primary cohort were patients hospitalized with COVID-19, either treated or not treated with RDV, who survived to hospital discharge. Unadjusted and adjusted Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the hazard ratio of all-cause mortality following hospital discharge for those administered vs. not administered inpatient RDV. Sensitivity analyses included propensity-matching the primary cohort with in-hospital mortality as a competing risk. Secondary outcomes, including hospital and ED readmissions respectively, within 28 days after index hospitalization discharge, were also evaluated using Cox proportional hazard models.ResultsThe primary cohort consisted of 9760 patients who survived index hospitalization and had between 6 and 29 months of post-hospital follow up. Of the primary cohort, 4771 (48.8%) were treated with inpatient RDV, inpatient RDV was associated with a decreased mortality hazard (aHR 0.73; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.61–0.87) among survivors with up to two and a half years of follow-up. Results from a sensitivity analysis using in-hospital mortality as a competing risk were similar to the primary model (aHR 0.76; CI 0.63–0.92). RDV treatment was also associated with decreased re-hospitalization (aHR 0.77; CI 0.67–0.89) and ED readmission rates (aHR 0.79; CI 0.67–0.92). Most subgroups appear to benefit from RDV, with possible exceptions for patients infected during the first Omicron wave, having received at least 1 vaccine dose, and those not requiring supplemental oxygen during index hospitalization.ConclusionsIn this real-world analysis of three large health systems in Colorado and Utah, RDV use was associated with decreased long-term mortality among survivors of initial COVID-19 hospitalization. Inpatient RDV treatment may provide a mortality benefit after COVID-19 hospitalization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11860-z.

Background

COVID-19 continues to carry global morbidity and mortality [1–4]. Remdesivir, a direct-acting nucleotide analogue prodrug inhibitor of coronavirus RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, is U.S. FDA-approved and recommended by the current NIH guidelines for early treatment of COVID-19 to prevent progression [5–7]. The efficacy of remdesivir on short-term recovery and 28-day mortality has been established among non-hospitalized and hospitalized patients in studies including ACTT-1, Solidarity, PINETREE, as well as several additional retrospective studies [8–20]. Specifically in the randomized controlled trials such as PINETREE, non-hospitalized patients that received a 3-day course of remdesivir reduced the risk of hospitalization or death by 87% compared to placebo [9]. Solidarity demonstrated that in hospitalized patients remdesivir had no benefit among those already on ventilation but did reduce risk of death or progression to ventilation in others [10].

There has been limited evaluation of the association of remdesivir use with long-term outcomes, including mortality, among patients who survive hospitalization due to COVID-19. The SOLIDARITY Finland study followed hospitalized patients up to one year after randomization to treatment with remdesivir or standard-of-care and compared unadjusted mortality risk by remdesivir use along with collecting self-reported symptoms to ascertain long COVID-19 presence and severity [21]. However, the mortality rate in the follow-up period in this study was quite low. Another cohort study conducted in Spain compared 90-day mortality among ICU-patients treated or not treated with remdesivir and did not observe a significant association with adjusted mortality risk, though was limited by low rates of remdesivir use and higher missingness in the follow-up phase [20]. We are not aware of other studies that have examined the longer-term impact of inpatient remdesivir use on survivors of COVID-19 hospitalization using real-world data that can provide efficient and more complete vital status information.

We have utilized a real-world data platform leveraging electronic health record (EHR) and state-level data to provide timely information on impact of antiviral therapies in high-risk outpatients acutely infected with COVID-19 to prevent hospitalization and improve survival [22, 23]. In this study, we pursued a novel application of our real-world platform to evaluate the association of remdesivir received during hospitalization for COVID-19 on post-discharge mortality, hypothesizing that in-hospital remdesivir use would be associated with lower risk of mortality among patients surviving to hospital discharge.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted an observational cohort study as a collaboration at the University of Colorado, University of Colorado Health (UCHealth), Denver Health (DH), Intermountain Health (IH), and the Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment (CDPHE) [22, 23]. The study was approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent. We obtained data from EHRs at UCHealth (Epic), DH (Epic) and IH (Cerner) and merged it with statewide data on vaccination status from the Colorado Comprehensive Immunization Information System and mortality from Colorado Vital Records using deterministic methods. Similarly, vaccination and mortality records from the state of Utah were included in IH data.

We included any adult patient (age 18 or older) with an index COVID-19 positive date (defined as either the documented SARS-CoV-2 positive date or the imputed SARS-CoV-2 positive test date based on the start date of COVID-19 treatment for cases when documentation of the test date was missing) on or between 11/20/2020 and 10/04/2022 that occurred outside of an inpatient hospitalization or on the same day as admission (N = 336223). We selected all patients that had at least one hospitalization within the first 28-days following the index COVID-19 date with complete admission and discharge data (N = 10386). To focus on effectiveness of remdesivir on long-term mortality, our primary cohort of interest included hospitalized patients, treated or not treated with remdesivir, and successfully discharged. We excluded patients that received outpatient COVID-19 antiviral treatments after their index hospitalization (N = 130) and patients who died during the index hospitalization (N = 490). After removing patients missing covariate data (N = 6), the final analytic sample size was 9760. We pre-specified the last date of follow-up as 4/4/2023, such that all patients had at least 6 months of follow up data, and some had as many as 29 months (Supp. Figure 1).

Exposures and covariates

We included pre-index hospitalization exposures: age, legal sex, race/ethnicity, insurance status, comorbid conditions, treatment status, vaccination status, and health system. We parameterized comorbid conditions as immunocompromised status, cardiovascular disease and/or pulmonary disease, other comorbid conditions including diabetes mellitus, obesity, renal disease, hypertension, and liver disease. We determined presence and status of comorbid conditions based on previously described methods using the Charlson and Elixhauser Comorbidity Indices [22–24]. Pre-index hospitalization treatment was categorized as any patient with an administration of a SARS-CoV-2 antiviral agent, or an order for nirmatrelvir–ritonavir (NMV/r). However, if a patient only had an administration of tixagevimab/cilgavimab (authorized only for pre-exposure prophylaxis) that was more than 10 days before their SARS-CoV-2 positive date, they were categorized as not treated. If a patient received an administration of any agent plus an order for NMV/r, we used the first treatment as the primary treatment. For all adjusted analyses, we included pre-index hospitalization treatment to be binary. We adjusted for the potential time and variant trends by including pandemic phase with 5 predominant variants (Pre-alpha, Alpha, Delta, Omicron BA.2/BA.2.12.1, Omicron BA.4/5), based on previously used definitions that relied on state-wide variant data [22–25].

Index hospitalization variables of interest included in-hospital severity described by hospital length of stay (LOS) in days, the maximum level of oxygen support, and any use of inpatient remdesivir, the primary exposure variable.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was all-cause mortality following discharge from index hospitalization. All patients had at least 6 and up to 29 months of follow-up data available. We also evaluated secondary outcomes including re-hospitalization or repeat emergency department (ED) visits defined as occurring within 28-days after discharge from the index hospitalization.

Statistical methods

We included descriptive statistics for all pre-hospital and in-hospital variables of interest in our primary cohort and used these to compare characteristics of patients who survived with those who died during the index hospitalization. For the pre-specified primary analysis, we fit both unadjusted and adjusted Cox proportional hazard models to estimate the hazard ratio of mortality for those administered vs. not administered inpatient remdesivir use and associations with each of the variables of interest in our primary cohort of patients that survived index hospitalization. For the adjusted model, we employed causal inference principles in model variable selection by creating a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to conceptualize mediation and confounding between variables to determine the final adjustments in the model (Supp. Figure 2). We also included a forest plot for cohort sub-groups that were associated with mortality risk stratified by inpatient remdesivir use.

For re-hospitalization and post-discharge ED visit secondary outcomes, we applied a competing risk Cox proportional hazards survival analysis to account for mortality risk. We adjusted for all variables of interest. We parameterized the secondary outcomes in a competing risks survival framework such that we defined the outcome as either occurring within 28 days, dying prior to experiencing the secondary outcome, and censored.

We conducted several pre-specified sensitivity analyses to test the validity of the primary analysis under varied experimental conditions. First, in order to limit analysis to patients admitted due to COVID-19 infection severity, we ran the primary analyses on a subset of patients that had an index hospitalization defined as COVID-19-specific based on ICD-10 codes or the use of supplemental oxygen [22, 23]. Second, to account for hospital-to-hospital transfers that may have been incorrectly coded as an index hospitalization, discharge, and subsequent re-hospitalization, we removed patients readmitted within six hours of index hospitalization discharge. Third, to evaluate outcomes in patients for whom the probability of receiving IP RDV treatment was more balanced, we performed the primary analysis on a lower severity group excluding hospitalized patients who required humidified high-flow nasal cannula (HHFNC), non-invasive ventilation (NIV) and invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) levels of supplemental oxygen support.

Finally, to further mitigate indication bias and immortal time bias due to inpatient mortality prior to IP RDV, we conducted a fourth sensitivity analysis using a propensity-matched, competing risks model with IP RDV as a time-dependent variable. To do this, we first fit a propensity model predicting the odds of receiving IP RDV. In a cohort consisting of all patients hospitalized within 28 days of a positive SARS-CoV-2 test, we used the model described above to propensity match on the odds of receiving inpatient RDV [26–30]. We then fit a competing risks survival model for the primary outcome of post-discharge mortality that incorporated the competing risk of in-hospital mortality with time-to-treatment with IP RDV use as a time-dependent variable. All adjustment variables included in the primary analysis were also included, other than in-hospital severity as assessed by level of supplemental oxygen support. For this fourth model, we ran two sub-sensitivity analyses to assess the role of propensity matching and removal of in-hospital severity, respectively. The first included only propensity-matched patients who survived the index hospitalization, for which we used classic cox proportional hazard regression to model the time to mortality or censoring from hospital discharge. The second included all patients who survived the index hospitalization (same cohort as the primary analysis), and all variables other than in-hospital severity.

All statistical analyses were done using R Statistical Software Version 3.6.0 (R Foundation, Vienna, etc.).

Results

Demographics, pre-hospital, and in-hospital variables

In the primary cohort that survived index hospitalization (9760), 4771 (48.8%) received inpatient remdesivir (IP RDV) (Table 1). Compared to patients not treated with RDV, a higher proportion of IP RDV-treated patients were ≥ 65 years (37.8% vs. 23.0%) or male (53.9% vs. 32.6%). Immunocompromised patients (36.3% vs. 23.5%) and patients with two or more comorbid conditions (62.3% vs. 39.5%) were also more likely to receive IP RDV. In general, patients treated with IP RDV had higher acuity, as evidenced by a longer index hospitalization (median 4 days (IQR 2–7) vs. 2 days (IQR 1–4)) and increased needs for respiratory support: standard low-flow supplemental oxygen (67.9% vs. 34.3%), humidified high-flow nasal cannula (HHFNC) (22.7% vs. 5.3%), and invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) (6.2% vs. 2.5%) as compared to patients not treated with RDV.

Compared to hospital survivors, patients that died during their index hospitalization were older (66.5% vs. 30.2% were ≥ 65 years), were more frequently immunocompromised (42.8% vs. 29.8%), more likely to have underlying cardiovascular disease (24.3% vs. 13.5%) but not pulmonary disease, more likely to have two or more comorbid conditions (76.9% vs. 50.6%), more likely to be hospitalized with a Delta variant (50.4% vs. 35.4%), and more likely to be treated with IP RDV (75.3% vs. 48.9%) (Supp. Table 1).

Primary outcome: mortality after index hospitalization survival

We described the incidence and timing of mortality during the post-hospitalization period among the primary cohort of survivors followed for up to 29 months after hospital discharge (Supp. Figure 3). IP RDV use was associated with a decreased adjusted risk of post-discharge mortality (adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) 0.73; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.61–0.87), despite a numerically higher mortality rate (7.4%, 355/4771) as compared to patients who did not receive RDV (6.1%, 303/4989) (Table 2). The results of the primary analysis were robust to all four sensitivity analyses. In patients whose hospitalization was coded as due to COVID-19 (91.3%, 8909/9760), IP RDV was associated with similarly reduced post-discharge mortality risk as observed in the primary cohort (sensitivity analysis 1.0: aHR 0.73; CI 0.61–0.88). Similar results were observed among patients re-hospitalized more than six hours after index hospitalization discharge (sensitivity analysis 2.0: aHR 0.69; CI 0.57–0.83), and among hospitalized but less sick patients who required no supplemental oxygen or only low-flow oxygen support (sensitivity analysis 3.0: aHR 0.71; CI 0.57–0.87) (Table 2). Importantly, results of the fourth sensitivity analysis treating in-hospital mortality and IP RDV as competing risks in a propensity-matched cohort also identified a similar association with IP RDV and decreased long-term mortality as the primary model (sensitivity analysis 4.1: aHR 0.76; CI 0.63–0.92) (Table 2). Interestingly, we observed that the differential cumulative hazard for mortality between the IP RDV treated and no RDV treated groups appears to increase over time during the follow-up period after index hospitalization discharge (Supp. Figure 4).

Secondary outcomes: re-hospitalization and post-discharge ED visits 28 days after index hospitalization

Among 9760 patients that survived index hospitalization, 1057 (10.8%) were re-hospitalized and 1053 (10.8%) had an ED visit within 28 days after index hospitalization discharge. Remdesivir treatment during the index hospitalization was associated with a decreased risk of re-hospitalization (aHR 0.77; CI 0.67–0.89) and ED visit after discharge (aHR 0.79; CI 0.67–0.92) in adjusted models (Table 2). Other factors associated with an increase in re-hospitalization and ED revisit risk were also described (Supp. Tables 2–3).

Sub-group analysis of relevant variables by RDV use

In addition, we evaluated relevant sub-groups to better understand the impact of RDV on long-term mortality (Fig. 1). Many subgroups appeared to benefit from inpatient RDV use. The association of RDV with improved long-term mortality among survivors does not seem to be impacted by the index hospitalization length of stay. Further, in contrast to patients who did not require any respiratory support, patients requiring supplemental oxygen or higher levels of respiratory support during their index hospitalization and treated with IP RDV appeared to have reduced risk of mortality in the post-hospitalization period. Patients never vaccinated also appear to benefit from IP RDV with reduced post-discharge mortality risk. Association of other variables of interest with primary and secondary outcomes

Other variables of interest associated with long-term mortality were also assessed (Supp. Table 4). Notable among these data is that pre-existing cardiovascular disease (aHR 2.61; 2, 3.41) but not pulmonary disease (aHR 1.00; 0.73, 1.38) was associated with increased long-term mortality and 28-day re-hospitalization risk (Supp. Table 2). Discussion

In a cohort comprised of survivors of acute COVID-19 hospitalization from three health care systems, inpatient remdesivir use was associated with reduced mortality risk post-discharge up to two and a half years. This finding was consistent when we treated index hospitalization mortality as a competing risk and is supported by patient-centered secondary outcomes of re-hospitalization and post-discharge ED visits within the first 28 days. The results of the primary outcome were also consistent among the subset of patients who only required low-flow supplemental oxygen during their index hospitalization, suggesting that inpatient remdesivir may protect against detrimental outcomes that follow index hospitalization.

Our results add to the growing body of literature describing the recovery period following acute COVID-19, with a focus on mortality and other relevant patient-centered outcomes that benefitted from the completeness of EHR and statewide vaccination and mortality databases. Although we cannot ascertain why remdesivir may be associated with decreased mortality risk in the post-hospitalization period, possible explanations that warrant further exploration include the drug’s antiviral effect to enhance viral clearance that may negate direct impact of viral reservoirs [31], or mitigate subclinical inflammation that has been linked to increased cardiovascular disease risk following community-acquired pneumonia due to influenza [32] and other viral- or bacterial pathogens. This potential explanation is supported by the absence of a perceived remdesivir benefit among the subgroup of patients who had received at least 1 vaccine dose, and the persistent negative association of pre-existing cardiovascular disease but not pulmonary disease on post-discharge mortality or 28-day hospital readmission risk.

This study has important limitations. Foremost, the data pipeline did not include corticosteroid use or other in-hospital COVID-specific treatments that have been shown to impact in-hospital mortality and other short-term outcomes. Since unmeasured, we cannot determine how exposure to these therapies may have modified the effect of remdesivir on post-hospitalization outcomes, limiting the interpretation of our findings. Second, by excluding patients who died during the index hospitalization from the primary analyzed cohort, we acknowledge the potential for survivor bias. However, it is worth noting that compared to patients requiring higher levels of respiratory support, patients requiring minimal (low-flow oxygen) respiratory support are less likely to receive additional COVID-19 specific therapies, and are less likely to die during their acute hospitalization, yet still benefitted from IP RDV with reduced long-term, all-cause mortality with a similar hazard ratio to the primary analyzed cohort. Second, the available data set did not include the duration of remdesivir treatment and time from symptom onset to treatment initiation. Given the proposed mechanism of remdesivir on early COVID-19 infection and viral replication, the lack of this information may confound the impact of remdesivir on hospital course and illness severity. Third, this model lacks the level of care received at discharge and during the follow-up period, and does not account for the impact of residual differences in illness severity at discharge that may bias our survival outcome. Lastly, based on our model criteria we excluded patients who died from COVID-19 prior to acute hospitalization and are unable to quantify the impact of this subgroup.

Conclusion

This real-world analysis of 3 large health systems in Colorado and Utah, remdesivir use was associated with a reduction in post-discharge mortality among survivors of COVID-19 hospitalization followed for up to 29 months. These findings suggest that remdesivir treatment may provide a mortality benefit after COVID-19 hospitalization.
