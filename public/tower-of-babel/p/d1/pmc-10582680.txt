Genomics and transcriptomic alterations of the glutamate receptors in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Amani Alqahtani, Mengxi Wang, Mimi Lou, Houda Alachkar
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlutamine and glutamate have been widely explored as potential therapeutic targets in acute myeloid leukemia (AML). In addition to its bioenergetic role in leukemia cell proliferation, L‐glutamate is a neurotransmitter that acts on glutamate receptors. However, the role of glutamate receptors in AML is largely understudied. Here, we comprehensively analyze the genomic and transcriptomic alterations of glutamate receptor genes in AML using publicly available data. We investigated the frequency of mutations in the glutamate receptor genes and whether an association exist between the presence of these mutations and clinical and molecular characteristics or patient's clinical outcome. We also assessed the dysregulation of glutamate receptor gene expression in AML with and without mutations and whether gene dysregulation is associated with clinical outcomes. We found that 29 (14.5%) of 200 patients with AML had a mutation in at least one glutamate receptor gene. The DNMT3A mutations were significantly more frequent in patients with mutations in at least one glutamate receptor gene compared with patients without mutations (13 of 29 [44.8%] vs. 41 of 171 [23.9%], p value: 0.02). Notably, patients with mutations in at least one glutamate receptor gene survived shorter than patients without mutations; however, the results did not reach statistical significance (overall survival: 15.5 vs. 19.0 months; p value: 0.10). Mutations in the glutamate receptor genes were not associated with changes in gene expression and the transcriptomic levels of glutamate receptor genes were not associated with clinical outcome.

INTRODUCTION

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a hematological malignancy of the myeloid lineage characterized by abnormal proliferation and block of differentiation of myeloid cells. 1 AML is a heterogeneous disease with various cytogenetic abnormalities, mutations, and aberrant gene expressions. 1 The molecular and cytogenetic abnormalities have proven clinical utility in determining a patient's prognosis, guiding clinical stratification, and selecting the optimal therapeutic strategy. 1 , 2 In addition, the identification of specific molecular and cytogenetic abnormalities has led to the development of novel targeted therapies that have significantly improved the overall survival (OS) of patients with AML, such as all‐trans retinoic acid for patients with t(15;17); FLT3 inhibitors, IDH inhibitors, and BCL2 inhibitor. 1 , 2 Despite the improved OS that comes with incorporating these targeted therapies, still a significant number of patients die of their disease, and only 5% of patients older than 70 years old survive for 5 years. 3 Therefore, exploring further molecular alterations and cytogenetic abnormalities will expand our understanding of the disease biology and contribute to developing new biomarkers and therapeutic strategies for AML.

Glutamine is a non‐essential amino acid that is important for normal and cancer cell growth. Glutamine is required for both energy production and as a source of carbon and nitrogen for the biosynthesis of protein and nucleotides in rapidly dividing cells. 4 Glutamine is converted by mitochondrial glutaminase to an ammonium ion and glutamate, metabolized by glutamate dehydrogenase to α‐ketoglutarate. 4 , 5 The α‐ketoglutarate is incorporated into the TCA cycle to generate ATP through NADH and FADH2. 4 , 5 Besides the production of α‐ketoglutarate, the aminotransferases will transfer nitrogen from glutamate to produce aspartate and alanine. 4 , 5 Carbon and nitrogen from glutamate can be used to produce proline and other non‐essential amino acids apart from transamination reactions. 4 , 5 In addition, glutamine metabolism is essential for cellular reactive oxygen species homeostasis. 4 , 5 Because glutamate is a precursor of glutathione that acts as a free radical scavenger and a detoxifying agent in cells. 4 , 5 Furthermore, glutamine stimulates mTOR activity, promoting protein translation, and fatty acid synthesis while reducing autophagy. 4 Glutamine addiction and the TCA cycle dysregulation were found in many cancers. 6 In addition, glutamine metabolism is found to be upregulated by various oncogenic alterations, such as myelocytomatosis oncogene (MYC), Ki‐ras2 Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutations, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 ERBB2 (HER2) upregulation further indicating that glutamine metabolism serves as an attractive target for anticancer therapy. 4

In addition to its role as a bioenergetic metabolite, L‐glutamate is also a neurotransmitter that exerts its action through two different types of receptors: ionotropic (iGluR) and metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR) Figure S1. 5 Glutamate and its receptors are known for their expression in the mammalian central nervous system (CNS), where they function as critical mediators of excitatory signals. 5 , 7 Several studies reported the abundance of glutamate and the expression of its receptors in peripheral organs and neoplastic cells. 5 , 7

Approximately 20% of all cancers, including AML, harbor mutated G‐protein coupled receptors, with the mGluRs being frequently mutated. 8 However, to our knowledge, the frequency of glutamate receptor mutations and the patterns of their transcriptomic alterations have yet to be studied in AML. In this study, we aim to investigate genomics and transcriptomic alterations of glutamate receptor genes in AML and evaluate how they associate with a patient's clinical and molecular characteristics.

METHODS

Patients' data

Publicly available data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset including molecular and clinical data of patients with AML were retrieved from cBioportal. 9 , 10 The clinical and molecular data of 200 patients with AML from two different studies under the TCGA dataset, including NEJM 2013, 11 and PanCancer Atlas, 12 were used for this analysis. Both studies have the same patients but differ slightly in the data availability. The AML TCGA NEJM 2013 study analyzed the genomes of 200 clinically annotated adult cases of de novo AML, using either whole‐genome sequencing (50 cases) or whole‐exome sequencing (150 cases), along with RNA and microRNA sequencing and DNA‐methylation analysis. The PanCancer Atlas conducted comprehensive integrative molecular analyses of the complete set of tumors in the TCGA, consisting of ~10,000 specimens and representing 33 types of cancer. The clinical and expression data, including age, sex, French American‐British (FAB) classification of AML, white blood cell (WBC) count, bone marrow (BM) blast percentage, peripheral blood (PB) blast percentage, risk status, cytogenetic status, and transplantation, were retrieved from the NEJM study due to their completeness and high quality, whereas genomic data, including mutations in the glutamate receptor genes or genes that are frequently mutated in AML, were obtained from the PanCancer Atlas; because this dataset contains the most updated genomic data and is unified across all tumor types analyzed in the TCGA project. The molecular data we focused on included somatic mutations frequently reported in AML, such as FLT3, TP53, DNMT3A, IDH1/2, TET2, NRAS, WT1, NPM1, CEBPA, and RUNX1 data, in addition to mutations in the glutamate receptor genes. The age of patients included in this study ranged from 18 to 88 years. All 200 patients included in this study were enrolled in a single‐institution tissue‐banking protocol approved by the human studies committee at Washington University between November 2001 and March 2010. Additionally, glutamate receptors mutation data from Oregon Health and Science University (OHSU), Nature 2018 dataset from cBioPortal was used to validate the frequency of glutamate receptors gene mutations among patients with AML. 13 Ethical approval for using human material was unnecessary because the patient data used in this analysis were from published studies.

Gene expression analysis

The mRNA expression z‐score relatives to all samples, either diploid or not, of glutamate receptors genes were retrieved from the NEJM study from cBioPortal. 11 Z‐score of expression data was used to stratify patients into high expression when Z‐score is greater than or equal to 1 and low expression when Z‐score is less than 1. Survival analyses were performed using this cutoff for 26 glutamate receptor genes. Heat‐map was generated to visualize glutamate receptor genes' expression in patients with AML using FlaskI. 14 In addition, we generated a heat map for the expression of glutamate receptor genes in patients with mutations versus those without mutation to assess whether the mutations in the glutamate receptor genes may influence their expression.

Statistical analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare OS and disease‐free survival (DFS) between patients with mutations in at least one of the glutamate receptor genes and those with wild type. OS is defined as the time between diagnosis and death due to any cause, whereas DFS is defined as the time between diagnosis and exclusion from the study due to a lack of complete remission, relapse, or death. Patients were stratified into eight groups for the analysis: patients with a mutation in at least one of all glutamate receptors genes versus others, patients with a mutation in at least one of AMPA receptor genes versus those without mutations, patients with a mutation in at least one of NMDA receptor genes versus those without mutations, patients with a mutation in at least one of kainate receptor genes versus those with wild type, patients with a mutation in at least one of class II receptor genes versus those without mutations, patients with a mutation in at least one of class III receptor genes versus those without mutations, patients with a mutation in at least one of Ionotropic receptor genes versus others, and patients with a mutation in at least one of metabotropic receptor genes versus those without mutations. To assess the association between glutamate receptors gene mutations and patients' clinical, molecular characteristics, or gene expression, we used Mann–Whitney U nonparametric for non‐normal distributed data or two independent samples t‐tests for normally distributed data for continuous variables and Chi‐square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, using GraphPad Prism software package (version 9.1.0 (216); GraphPad Software Inc.) and SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc.). We used a statistical cutoff of p value less than or equal to 0.05 for significance determination, and the p value was adjusted by the Bonferroni method for multiple testing where required.

RESULTS

Patterns of genomic and transcriptomic alterations of glutamate receptor genes in AML

Genes encoding for the glutamate receptors showed a broad spectrum of genomic and transcriptomic alterations in AML. The alterations include missense, splice, truncating, amplification, deep deletion, and transcriptomic dysregulation, either as gene upregulation or downregulation. It is considered an amplification when the mean copy number log2 value exceeds 1. A total of 100 of 200 (50%) patients have at least one of these genetic alterations in at least one of the glutamate receptor genes. In the NEJM 2013 study, all 26 genes encoding glutamate receptors were genetically or transcriptionally altered in at least one patient with AML (Figure 1). Although GRIN2B and GRM4 are the least frequently altered glutamate receptor genes in patients with AML (0.6%), GRM5 is the most altered glutamate receptor gene, where 9% of patients had either mRNA upregulation or amplification (Figure 1). In the PanCancer Atlas study, 23 out of 26 genes encoding glutamate receptors were genetically or transcriptionally altered in at least one patient with AML; GRIA1, GRIA2, and GRID2 were unaffected by alterations.

Glutamate receptors are frequently mutated in AML

The frequency of glutamate receptor gene mutations in patients with AML in the TCGA dataset is shown in Table 1. Among the 200 patients in TCGA AML data, 29 (14.5%) patients had a mutation in one or more glutamate receptor genes. Among the seven classes of glutamate receptors, the kainate receptor‐encoded genes were the most mutated in patients with AML, as mutations were identified in 15 (7.5%) patients. Kainate receptor has five genes that were mutated as follows: GRIK1 (1.5%), GRIK2 (1.5%), GRIK3 (1.5%), GRIK4 (2.5%), and GRIK5 (1%). NMDA and AMPA glutamate receptor genes were mutated in five (2.5%) and four (2%) patients with AML, respectively. The NMDA receptor has seven genes. Four of these genes were mutated in AML, including GRIN2B in two (1%), GRIN2C in one (0.5%), GRIN2D in one (0.5%), and GRIN3A in one (0.5%) patient. Four genes encode the AMPA receptor; only one of these genes (GRIA3) was mutated in four (2%) patients. Genes encoding the class III of metabotropic glutamate receptors were mutated in six (3%) patients with AML. Three (1.5%) patients had either a missense or frameshift mutation in the GRM8 gene, one (0.5%) patient had a missense mutation of GRM4, and two (1.0%) patients had synonymous and missense mutations in the GRM6 gene.

We also evaluated the frequency of glutamate receptor gene mutations in a separate dataset (OHSU, 2018) in Table S1. Among 532 patients with AML included in the OHSU study, 21 (3.9%) had a mutation in one or more glutamate receptor genes. Five (1%) patients with AML had a mutation in NMDA ionotropic glutamate receptor genes that included one (0.2%) patient with a mutation in GRIN2A and four (0.8%) patients with a mutated GRIN2B gene. Similarly, five (1%) patients with AML had a mutation in kainate receptor encoded genes, four (0.8%) patients with missense, frameshift, or stop gained mutation in GRIK2, and one (0.2%) patient had a mutation in GRIK4 gene. Class II metabotropic glutamate receptor genes were mutated in four (0.8%) patients with AML, where one (0.2%) patient had a missense mutation in the GRM2 gene, and three (0.6%) patients had a missense mutation in the GRM3 gene.

Mutations in the glutamate receptor genes are absent in patients with M3 AML

The associations between glutamate receptors' gene mutations and patient clinical characteristics were evaluated in patients with AML from the TCGA dataset and summarized in Table 2. There were no significant differences in patient's clinical features, including age, sex, median WBC count, BM blasts, and PB blasts, risk status, including molecular and cytogenetic risk, cytogenetic status, and transplantation, between patients with mutations in at least one glutamate receptor gene and those without mutations. Mutations in the glutamate receptor genes were negatively associated with the M3 class of FAB classification of AML; none of the patients with M3 carried mutations in the glutamate receptor genes (p value: 0.049).

We performed similar subanalyses considering each subtype of glutamate receptor genes to assess whether mutations in a specific group were associated with any of the patient's clinical characteristics. Patients were dichotomized into seven groups: patients with a mutation in AMPA (n = 4), NMDA (n = 5), kainate (n = 15), class II (n = 4), class III (n = 6), inotropic (n = 22), and metabotropic (n = 10) glutamate receptors. There was no significant difference in clinical characteristics among patients with AMPA, NMPA, kainate, class II, class III, ionotropic, and metabotropic glutamate receptors mutations and those with wild type.

Mutations in glutamate receptor genes are associated with the presence of DNMT3A mutations

We compared the total number of all mutations in patients with and without mutations in the glutamate receptor genes. The number of mutations was higher in patients with mutated glutamate receptor genes than in patients without mutations (median number of mutations: 10 vs. 9, p value: 0.052). To evaluate the association between mutations in the glutamate receptor genes and the patient's molecular characteristics, we compared the frequency of glutamate receptor gene's mutations according to the most common mutations in AML. The frequency of DNMT3A mutations was significantly higher in patients with mutations in at least one glutamate receptor gene compared with those carrying all wild‐type genes (13 of 29 [44.8%] vs. 41 of 171 [23.9%]; p value: 0.02; Table 3). Furthermore, we found a strong relationship between DNMT3A mutations and glutamate receptor mutations (Figure 2). To assess whether mutations in specific groups of the glutamate receptor gene contribute to this association, a subanalysis was conducted to evaluate the association between AML common mutations and the presence of glutamate receptor gene mutations in each subgroup of the glutamate receptor genes separately. Notably, the frequency of DNMT3A mutation was found to be significantly higher in patients with a mutation in AMPA (GRIA3) glutamate receptor gene compared with those without mutations (GRIA3: 4 of 4 [100.0%] vs. 50 of 196 [25.5%]; p value < 0.01). Similarly, mutations in class II metabotropic receptors were significantly associated with mutations in DNMT3A (GRM2 and GRM3: 4 of 4 [100.0%] vs. 50 of 196 [25.5%]; p value < 0.01). However, after adjusting the p values of this subanalysis, none of these associations reached statistical significance.

Mutations in the glutamate receptor genes are not associated with the clinical outcome of patients with AML

To assess the association between mutations in the glutamate receptor genes and the patient's clinical outcome, the OS and DFS were compared among patients with glutamate receptor gene mutations and those with wild type. We found that patients with mutations in at least one glutamate receptor gene survived shorter than patients without mutations; however, the results did not reach statistical significance (OS: 15.5 vs. 19.0 months; p value: 0.10, DFS: 12.1 vs. 19.3 months; p value: 0.31; Figure 3). In addition, OS and DFS were not different after excluding patients with the M3 class of FAB classification of AML (patients with M3 AML, or t[15;17] have acute promyelocytic leukemia, which responds well to all‐trans retinoic acid and have good overall survival; OS: 15.5 vs. 15.4 months; p value: 0.27, DFS: 12.1 vs. 14.9 months; p value: 0.62; Figure 3). The loss of the trend of association with shorter OS after excluding patients with M3 is consistent with the observed lack of mutations in the glutamate receptor genes among patients with M3 AML.

We also analyzed each subtype of the glutamate receptor genes separately in terms of how the presence of these mutations correlates with clinical outcomes. We found that patients with mutations in GRIA3, a gene of AMPA glutamate receptor, had significantly shorter median OS than patients with wild type (7.0 vs. 18.0 months; p value: 0.05; Figure S2). Similarly, patients with mutant AMPA had a trend for shorter median OS after excluding patients with the M3 class of FAB classification (7 vs. 15.1 months; p value: 0.07; Figure S2). However, after adjusting the p values of this subanalysis, they did not reach statistical significance. There was no significant difference in median survival between patients with NMPA, kainate, class II, class III, ionotropic, and metabotropic glutamate receptors mutations and those with wild type.

Upregulation of glutamate receptor genes is not associated with the outcome of patients with AML

To assess whether transcriptomic alterations of glutamate receptor genes have an impact on the survival of patients with AML, patients were dichotomized into high expression (Z ≥ 1) and low expression (Z < 1) of the specific gene. The data of these survival curves, including median survival for high expressed versus low expressed patients and adjusted p value, were summarized in Table S2. The analysis was conducted for all patients with or without excluding patients with M3 AML. We conducted the survival analysis using the gene expression data as a continuous and categorical variable, and neither analysis showed statistically significant results. The transcriptomic alterations of glutamate receptor genes did not affect the OS or DFS of patients with AML.

Mutations in the glutamate receptors genes are not associated with transcriptomic levels

Next, we analyzed whether the transcriptomic levels of the glutamate receptor genes are influenced by the presence of glutamate receptor mutations. All 26 glutamate receptor genes were dysregulated in patients with AML, and the expression data were available for 173 of 200 patients. Twenty‐four of 173 patients with AML with expression data harbored mutations in at least one of the glutamate receptor genes. Figure S3 shows a heat map to visualize expression patterns of 26 glutamate receptor genes in 173 patients with AML. Several genes, including GRIN1, GRIN3B, GRID1, GRIA4, GRIK1, GRIK3, GRIK4, GRM1, and GRM6 were overexpressed in a subset of patients with AML. Patients with mutations in at least one glutamate receptor gene versus those with wild type showed similar gene expression patterns (Figure 4).

Moreover, to investigate whether the mutations were associated with changes in the expression of glutamate receptor genes, the expression of each gene was compared between patients with mutated and non‐mutated AML (Figure S4). Because of the small sample size of mutated patients in each gene, this analysis was only performed on genes with at least two mutated samples. The data showed that the mutations do not influence the expressions of their corresponding genes (Figure S4).

DISCUSSION

The ionotropic glutamate receptors (iGluRs) are ligand‐gated ion channels, which are divided into four groups based on structural similarities: NMDA receptors (NMDARs), AMPA receptors (AMPARs), kainate (KA) receptors (KARs), and delta receptors. 5 , 7 , 15 NMDARs consist of seven heterogeneous subunits: NR1, NR2A/B/C/D, and NR3A/B. 5 Whereas AMPARs are assembled from four subunits: GluR1/2/3/4, showing 70% of homology. 5 Kainate receptors have two subunits: GluR5/6/7 and KA1/2, which form homo or heterodimers. 7 Similarly, metabotropic glutamate receptors are classified into three subgroups, group I consists of mGluR1 and mGluR5, coupled to the phospholipase C; group II consists of mGluR2 and mGluR3, whereas group III includes mGluR4, mGluR6, mGluR7, and mGluR8, which are negatively coupled to adenylate cyclase. Each subunit of these subgroups is the product of a different gene. 5 , 7 Transcriptomic alterations in several glutamate receptor genes have been identified in diverse malignancies, highlighting their potential relevance as therapeutic targets in specific cancer types. 5

The current study assessed the frequency of glutamate receptor gene mutations and their potential association with clinical and molecular characteristics in a cohort of patients with AML. We found that mutation in at least one or more glutamate receptor genes was present in about 15% of patients in the TCGA data and only 4% in the OHSU dataset. The TCGA dataset accounted for each mutation regardless of the number of mutated patients, whereas in the OHSU dataset, mutations were only accounted for when observed in at least 10 patients. 12 , 13 Specifically, mutations in kainate receptor encoded genes, a subclass of ionotropic glutamate receptors, were identified in 15 (7.5%) of the TCGA cohort. The high frequency of mutations in the glutamate receptor genes in AML may be due to the location of these genes within the most affected chromosomes in AML, such as 21, 16, 9, 11, 6, 3, and 1. 1 Our result shows that the combined frequencies of glutamate receptors mutations among patients with AML is greater than that of RUNX1 (10.0%), IDH2 (11.0%), CEBPA (7.0%), TET2 (9.0%), and TP53 (8.0%) well‐known recurrent somatic mutations in AML and that may indicate possible role of these mutations in AML pathogenesis. Glutamate receptor mutations have not been studied yet in AML; however, in AML whole‐genome sequencing study, heterozygous, a nonsynonymous somatic mutation was found in glutamate receptor, ionotropic, N‐Methyl D‐Aspartate‐Like 1B gene POLR2MP1 (GRINL1B). 16 This previous study was conducted to sequence the genomic DNA of tumor and normal skin cells obtained from a patient with a typical presentation of FAB M1 AML with normal cytogenetics. 16

Patients with AML harbored different types of glutamate receptor gene mutations, which include missense, synonymous, frameshift, non‐sense, intron variant, and splice site. In other types of cancer, glutamate receptor alterations, such as transcriptomic dysregulation have been identified and investigated as therapeutic targets. 5 Although the glutamate receptor genes were analyzed as individual subunits, glutamate receptors are composed of various subunits, leading to differences in signaling and pharmacological properties that may be context‐dependent. Whether the various identified alterations of glutamate receptor genes have a shared functional role in AML remains to be studied.

Recent studies have shown that glutamate receptors are expressed in peripheral tissues outside the CNS and play some roles in tumorigenesis. 5 For instance, the activation of NMDA receptors has been found to affect the mTOR signaling pathway that controls cell growth. 17 , 18 The upregulation of iGluRs subunits has been identified in various malignancies, such as thyroid, lung, breast carcinomas, multiple myeloma, colon adenocarcinoma, T cell leukemia, 19 larynx, 20 gastric cancer cells, 21 osteosarcomas, 22 prostate, 23 as well as in hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines. 24 Accordingly, iGluRs antagonists have been investigated for treating various types of cancers. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Similarly, upregulation of mGluR1, mGluR2, mGluR6, and mGluR3 has been reported in malignant medulloblastomas, ependymomas, and glioblastomas. 30 Clinically, the upregulation of mGluR3 was associated with inferior outcomes in patients with glioblastoma. 31 Additionally, overexpression of mGluRs subunits has been reported in non‐neuronal cancers, including melanoma, breast, prostate, renal and colorectal cancers, and osteosarcoma as well, and this overexpression promotes the growth of these cancers. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34

Targeting glutamine metabolism through different pathways has been studied in AML. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 The knockdown of SLC1A5 high‐affinity transporter for glutamine induces apoptosis and inhibits tumor formation in AML mouse model by inhibiting mTORC1 activity. 35 In addition, glutaminase inhibitor (CB‐839) has been reported to significantly damage antioxidant glutathione production in various types of AML, leading to mitochondrial reactive oxygen species and apoptotic cell death. 36 , 37 Additionally, glutaminase inhibitors (CB‐839 and BPTES) suppressed the growth of AML cells expressing mutant IDH and induced myeloid differentiation. 39 , 40

Interestingly, DNMT3A mutation was significantly associated with mutations in at least one of the glutamate receptor genes. DNMT3A mutations occurred in about 18% to 23% of patients with AML and are associated with adverse prognosis. 41 , 42 IDH1/2 mutation is a recurrent somatic mutation that occurs in about 20% of patients with AML. 43 DNMT3A and IDH1/2 are epigenetic mutations that cause dysregulated DNA methylation and cause gene expression alteration, cell proliferation, and abnormal differentiation. 42 , 43 Predominantly, DNMT3A mutations are mutually occurring with IDH mutations, and in our cohort, there are 57.9% of DNMT3A mutant AML patients with an IDH1 mutation, and 28.6% of them with IDH2 mutation. 43 In addition, DNMT3A mutations can alter the epigenetic footprint of IDH‐mutant AML and may trigger leukemic transformation. 43 IDH1 mutation has an impact on glutamate metabolism by inducing glutamine addiction and utilizing it as the primary source of α‐Ketoglutarate. 4 , 43

Interestingly, we found that mutation in GRIA3, a subunit of AMPA ionotropic glutamate receptor, was associated with inferior OS and with DNMT3A mutation. The role of GRIA3 in AML or hematological malignancies has not been studied yet; however, it was found to be expressed in normal human T cells and human T leukemia cells. 44 Additionally, the knockdown of GRIA3 reduced proliferation and migration and promoted apoptosis of pancreatic cancer cells. 45

One limitation of our study, especially those related to the association of mutations with transcriptomic alterations, is the relatively small sample size of patients with mutations in each glutamate receptor subgroup compared to patients without mutation. However, because this study analyzed the largest publicly available dataset of AML, this provokes the need to include glutamate receptor genes in the mutational analysis of patients with AML.

In summary, we have identified that glutamate receptors are frequently mutated in AML, and these mutations were significantly associated with DNMT3A somatic mutation. Whereas these mutations have not affected the expression pattern of their corresponding genes in patients with AML and were not found to associate with clinical outcomes, their presence at high frequency warrants further investigation. To our knowledge, this is the first report to describe the genomic and transcriptomic alterations of glutamate receptor genes in AML. Thus, because of the small sample size and lack of glutamate receptor mutation data in publicly available datasets, studies are needed to investigate the prevalence and significance of these mutations in a larger cohort of patients with AML.
