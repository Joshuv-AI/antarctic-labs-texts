BACKGROUND

The Consortium for Clarity in Alzheimer's disease related dementias (ADRD) Research Through Imaging (CLARiTI) will provide support for standardized imaging (i.e., amyloid positron emission tomography [PET]; tau PET; and brain magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) and plasma biomarkers into the Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC) network, with the overall goal to improve in vivo detection of multi‐etiologies in a clinically heterogenous national cohort. 1 CLARiTI builds upon the existing ADRC network infrastructure, including deeply characterized clinical core cohorts that are enrolled across 36 centers.

Importantly, the necessary local expertise and capabilities exist to meet the goals of CLARiTI regarding critical gaps in detecting mixed etiologies. The outcome of CLARiTI will be, in part, a comprehensive, publicly available dataset that reflects the biological complexity and clinical heterogeneity inherent in the ADRC population. 2 , 3 , 4 The imaging and plasma data collected in CLARiTI will be fully integrated with the existing accessible datasets housed at the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC, https://naccdata.org/). 5 , 6 , 7 Imaging data will be processed through the Standardized Centralized Alzheimer's & Related Dementias Neuroimaging (SCAN) initiative (https://scan.naccdata.org/). Plasma data will be processed through the National Centralized Repository for Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias (NCRAD; https://ncrad.iu.edu/). A particularly unique aspect of the ADRC program is the high rate of consent to brain donation, enabling measurements collected in vivo to eventually be compared with neuropathological assessment. 8

CLARiTI is fundamentally a biomarker study superimposed on the standardized clinical and cognitive phenotyping currently underway across all ADRCs. There are currently over 19,000 active individuals enrolled across ADRC Clinical Cores that could be considered for enrollment into CLARiTI. By recruiting and phenotyping 2000 of these potential individuals, CLARiTI seeks to leverage the full spectrum of ADRD etiologies. Building on our first publication describing the goals and study protocol of CLARiTI 1 , this follow‐up work focuses on the anticipated composition of enrollees into CLARiTI by summarizing site level data. Although PET and MRI data collection are common across sites, less is understood about the characteristics of participants that each site prioritizes for imaging studies. Further, although the CLARiTI study aims to leverage the established heterogeneity across sites, it also imposes some restrictions on enrollment in an attempt to maximize scientific impact. These restrictions include having an overall ratio of clinically impaired to clinically unimpaired of 2:1, requiring individuals with clinical impairment to have Alzheimer's disease (AD) as a possible contributing factor even if not the primary clinical etiology, and requiring clinically unimpaired individuals to have at least one risk factor associated with dementia. Lastly, participating ADRCs may choose to focus on de novo recruitment or longitudinal follow‐up of participants already enrolled at the site. Here, we provide an overview of the ADRC sites, including their thematic areas and their initial enrollment plans into CLARiTI.

METHODS

Survey development

Inclusion criteria for the CLARiTI study are intentionally broad, focusing on the enrollment of both clinically unimpaired individuals with at least one risk factor for dementia (eTable 1) and clinically impaired individuals where AD is a suspected etiology, though it need not be primary. The rationale behind these broad criteria was to enable imaging and plasma characterization across a range of Alzheimer's Disease and ADRD in which AD might be a contributing factor. Importantly, this broad inclusion criteria leverages the heterogeneity across sites with regard to their expertise and clinical focus. We developed a survey to capture this heterogeneity and site‐specific strategies to enrollment.

The final survey included questions on the site's theme and/or expertise (open text), the suspected primary etiologies enrolled in CLARiTI for participants with clinical impairment (multiple choice), the factors targeted for enrollment of cognitively unimpaired participants (multiple choice), whether the strategy for completing other ADRC imaging efforts would differ from the enrollment strategy for CLARiTI (open text), and what opportunities CLARiTI may provide to the site (open text; Appendix I).

Survey distribution and respondents

The survey was sent to all 36 CLARiTI site principal investigators (PIs) in November 2024.

Analysis survey results

Answers to multiple‐choice questions were summarized quantitatively. Answers to open text questions were summarized qualitatively. The resulting synthesis was checked for accuracy and completeness by all authors. Additional correspondence with two sites was used to confirm survey responses.

Current ADRC population

To place survey results in the context of the broader ADRC network, we concurrently detail the composition of the current ADRC population (NACC Uniform Dataset [UDS]; https://naccdata.org/nacc‐collaborations/uds4‐updates) and the composition of the proportion of that population that would potentially be eligible for CLARiTI. Data were downloaded in November 2024, and all information pertinent to this study was included in investigator_nacc67.csv. We used demographic, clinical, and genetic information to identify all cognitively unimpaired NACC participants that would potentially be eligible for CLARiTI. To identify all cognitively impaired NACC participants that would potentially be eligible for CLARiTI, we selected all cognitively impaired NACC participants for whom AD, probable AD, or possible AD was a presumptive etiologic diagnosis (variables NACCALZD, PROBAD, POSSAD).

RESULTS

Survey response rates

A 100% response rate was achieved 1.5 months after the initial request to complete the survey was sent out. All surveys were completed by the CLARiTI site PI or co‐lead investigator.

Composition of the current ADRC cohort

We first sought to characterize the current cohort of participants with data already available at NACC across all ADRC sites. As of November 2024, there were data available across 52,537 unique individuals, of whom 19,225 are “active” (i.e., actively undergoing follow‐up by the Clinical Core at their ADRC). Sample characteristics for both groups are presented in Table 1. A total of 31% of individuals are apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carriers (26.0% heterozygotes, 5.1% homozygotes), 73% are non‐Hispanic White individuals, 51% have at least one first‐degree family member with cognitive impairment, and 61% have cognitive impairment. The distribution of primary etiologies for individuals with cognitive impairment is shown in Figure 1 (1A: all individuals; 1B: active individuals). AD, Lewy body disease, frontotemporal lobar degeneration, and vascular brain injury are the most common suspected etiologies for individuals with clinical impairment (>80% of cases).

Anticipation of CLARiTI enrollment

Two‐thirds of CLARiTI enrollees will have cognitive impairment. Sites endorsed anticipated enrollment of individuals with suspected AD (at 35 sites), vascular brain injury (at 24 sites), Lewy body disease (at 22 sites), limbic‐predominant age‐related TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43) encephalopathy (at 18 sites), frontotemporal lobar degeneration (at 14 sites), cerebral amyloid angiopathy (at 11 sites), chronic traumatic encephalopathy (at sites), multiple system atrophy (at two sites), Down syndrome (at two sites), corticobasal degeneration (at one site), and progressive supranuclear palsy (at three sites; Figure 1C). Whereas three sites would focus only on AD, all other sites enrolling clinically impaired participants selected two or more categories. One site indicated they would not be enrolling clinically impaired individuals.

Factors targeted for enrollment of clinically unimpaired participants include underrepresented race/ethnicity (at 35 sites), an age of 70 or greater (at 25 sites), a first‐degree family history of dementia (any neurodegenerative disease; at 24 sites), APOE ε4 carriership (at 23 sites), vascular health factors (at 22 sites), cognitive complaints (at 22 sites), cognitive decline (at 20 sites), rural location or specific rural–urban commuting area code (RUCA; 17 sites), 12 or fewer years of education (15 sites), and neurobehavioral factors (at 14 sites Figure 2). The majority of sites (34) endorsed two or more high‐risk factors for the enrollment of clinically unimpaired individuals.

Intersection with other imaging efforts

There are multiple imaging efforts currently underway across ADRCs. Previously, imaging was performed using investigator‐initiated research studies and/or funds directly from the ADRC P30 budget. CLARiTI was launched in 2023 as a multi‐site study to provide needed resources for image acquisitions, given that the majority of clinical core participants were not undergoing amyloid and tau PET imaging. In addition to CLARiTI, P30 National Institute on Aging (NIA) ADRC budgets were increased to support additional scanning of 24 Clinical Core participants per year for each center. Given the availability of center funds for additional imaging of clinical core participants, we sought to understand how strategies for enrollment into CLARiTI would align with this parallel imaging effort. Whereas 19 sites indicated strategies would be similar across CLARiTI and their P30 center funds, 17 sites indicated they would take complementary strategies (eTable 2).

Opportunities provided by CLARiTI

All respondents reported that they anticipate CLARiTI will provide their site with new opportunities. Motivation for this primarily pertained to the provision of resources to support neuroimaging and allowing for more comprehensive biomarker characterization in their clinical core participants. Respondents also indicated that they expect participation in CLARiTI to aid in participant recruitment and retention and provide momentum and resources for biomarker disclosure (Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

AD and related dementias are biologically heterogeneous conditions, frequently underpinned by multiple interacting pathologies. The CLARiTI study was conceived with this complexity in mind, recognizing that AD rarely occurs in isolation. Instead, it often coexists with other contributors to cognitive decline, including Lewy body disease, vascular brain injury, and TDP‐43 proteinopathy. 2 , 3 , 4 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 CLARiTI addresses this gap by embedding multimodal biomarker collection—including amyloid and tau PET, structural MRI, and plasma assays—within a deeply phenotyped and autopsy‐rich cohort. By aligning this effort with the ADRC infrastructure, CLARiTI is uniquely positioned to generate data that both reflect and elucidate the multi‐etiologic nature of dementia in older adults.

The goal of the current work was to survey the 36 ADRC sites that will be participating in CLARiTI to summarize the breadth of scientific focus and anticipated enrollment strategies across the ADRC network. Survey results confirmed substantial heterogeneity in scientific focus, participant demographics, and anticipated enrollment strategies into CLARiTI. Sites reported targeting a broad range of primary etiologies in clinically impaired participants, with AD nearly universal but frequent inclusion of vascular injury (24 sites), Lewy body disease (22 sites), and limbic‐predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy (18 sites). Among clinically unimpaired participants, nearly every site (35/36) reported plans to recruit underrepresented racial and ethnic groups, and over half targeted individuals with a family history of dementia (24 sites) or APOE ε4 carriership (23 sites). These results affirm that CLARiTI will yield a heterogenous cohort spanning both typical and atypical disease trajectories, providing a robust foundation for understanding early and mixed pathology in ADRD.

The anticipated cohort that will result from CLARiTI builds from recent program‐wide initiatives to support standardized imaging acquisitions and sharing of this data with the scientific community as part of the SCAN initiative. Given the alignment in scanning protocols with SCAN, CLARiTI data will be easily mergeable with imaging data collected on additional ADRC participants. Taken together, these imaging efforts have positioned the ADRC program to represent the fastest‐growing multimodal imaging and plasma‐characterized cohort in the world. This imaging dataset will also be able to be combined with ongoing standardized clinical data (UDS, in place since 2005) already collected across sites and made publicly available via the NACC. Given the high brain donor enrollment and autopsy success rates (>60%; 8 ), there will be future opportunities to compare biomarker profiles directly to postmortem analyses.

Finally, while the scientific themes and priorities of individual CLARiTI sites vary, nearly all centers indicated that CLARiTI offered unique opportunities for local innovation, including enhanced recruitment and biomarker disclosure. Yet, the extent to which underrepresented groups and etiologies will be fully realized in the resulting cohort remains an empirical question. Our findings suggest that many sites have deliberately aligned their CLARiTI recruitment strategies to emphasize breadth of pathology. As the study progresses, the current synthesis provides a benchmark to assess whether those aims are met in practice and can help guide adaptive recruitment strategies, ensuring the final dataset reflects the heterogeneity and complexity that CLARiTI was designed to capture.
