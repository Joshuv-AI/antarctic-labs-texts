Guselkumab as a therapeutic option for recalcitrant pyoderma gangrenosum and Crohn disease: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Mahmut Esat Tanrıbilir, Osmanege Atliya, Tarkan Karakan, Esra Adışen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare, chronic neutrophilic dermatosis characterized by rapidly progressive, painful ulcers. It is frequently associated with systemic inflammatory conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease. The pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, and treatment of refractory cases poses a significant challenge. We present a 26-year-old woman diagnosed with PG, initially managed with systemic corticosteroids and ciclosporin. During follow-up, she developed gastrointestinal symptoms and was subsequently diagnosed with Crohn disease (CD). Despite treatment with azathioprine, infliximab and, later, ustekinumab, the patient experienced repeated flares of both PG and CD. Upon recurrence of PG lesions and CD exacerbation under ustekinumab therapy, systemic corticosteroids were reintroduced, and subcutaneous guselkumab, an interleukin (IL)-23 p19 inhibitor, was initiated. A rapid and sustained clinical response was observed following the second dose of guselkumab, with complete ulcer healing and tapering off corticosteroids. The patient has remained in complete remission from both PG and CD for over 2 years under guselkumab monotherapy. This case adds to the growing body of evidence supporting the use of IL-23 inhibitors, particularly guselkumab, in recalcitrant PG associated with systemic inflammatory comorbidities. Guselkumab may offer a promising therapeutic alternative in the management of treatment-resistant PG, especially in patients with coexisting CD.

Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare neutrophilic dermatosis that is characterized by papules, nodules or pustules that rapidly ulcerate with undermined, irregular borders, peripheral erythema and cribriform scars.1 The pathogenesis of PG is incompletely understood and treatment of PG remains a significant challenge. Current therapeutic approaches primarily target inflammation through immunosuppressive and immunomodulatory treatments. Several reports highlight the efficacy of biologics and small molecules, demonstrating successful outcomes in PG.2 In this report, we present a case of recalcitrant PG in a patient with concomitant Crohn disease (CD), both successfully treated with guselkumab.

Case report

A 26-year-old woman presented with a painful, rapidly progressing, haemorrhagic and necrotic ulcer on the posterior aspect of the right lower leg 4 years ago. Based on clinical and histopathological findings, she was diagnosed with PG. After the diagnosis, systemic methylprednisolone (1 mg kg–1 daily) and ciclosporin (3 mg kg–1 daily) treatments were started, which resulted in improvement of the ulcers. Methylprednisolone treatment was tapered gradually and treatment continued with ciclosporin. During her follow-up, the patient developed abdominal pain and melena. Total colonoscopy was performed, which revealed aphthous ulcers in the terminal ileum and sigmoid colon. The patient was diagnosed with CD (Figure 1).

Colonoscopic images demonstrating active Crohn disease (2019). Representative colonoscopy images obtained during disease flare. The mucosa exhibits diffuse erythema, oedema, friability, and multiple aphthous and deep ulcerations with irregular borders.

One month later, new ulcers developed on her right leg despite ciclosporin treatment. Systemic methylprednisolone (1 mg kg–1 daily) was reinitiated; however, the patient did not respond. Ciclosporin was gradually tapered and discontinued, and azathioprine (100 mg daily) was started. However, disease activity remained uncontrolled after 6 weeks of azathioprine, at which point infliximab (5 mg kg–1 intravenously at weeks 0, 2 and 6, followed by every 8 weeks) was initiated and azathioprine was discontinued. In addition, mesalazine was initiated for CD. Under infliximab therapy, the patient’s skin lesions were resolved completely by week 8. She remained in complete remission for 1 year under infliximab therapy.

One year later, the patient presented with abdominal pain and melena, consistent with a CD flare. During the active period, her faecal calprotectin level was measured as 707 µg g–1. Following multidisciplinary evaluation with the gastroenterology department, infliximab was discontinued and ustekinumab treatment was started. Ustekinumab was administered with the CD dosing regimen, consisting of an intravenous induction dose of 260 mg, followed by 90 mg subcutaneously every 8 weeks, and the patient responded well to therapy. She was under complete remission for both PG and CD under ustekinumab therapy. However, approximately 1 year later, rapidly ulcerating, papulopustular lesions developed on the patient’s back and right lumbar area while receiving ustekinumab therapy (Figure 2).

A 3 × 2 cm ulcerated lesion with undermined borders on the right lumbar area prior to guselkumab treatment.

Due to the rapid progression of the lesions, ustekinumab treatment was stopped and systemic methylprednisolone 1 mg kg–1 daily was started again. Her follow-up colonoscopy in 2022 was suggestive of inactive or low activity CD (Figure 3). After consultation with the gastroenterology department, subcutaneous guselkumab treatment was initiated in June 2022. The initial dose of guselkumab was 200 mg, followed by 100 mg 4 weeks later and then 100 mg for every 6 weeks. After the second dose of guselkumab, significant improvement in the patient’s lesions was observed. Systemic corticosteroid was gradually tapered and stopped. Complete clinical response was observed and only an atrophic scar remained following the third dose of guselkumab (Figure 4). After maintaining complete remission for 1 year, the guselkumab dosing interval was extended to every 8 weeks. The patient has remained in complete remission for more than 2 years under guselkumab treatment (Table 1). After more than 2 years of remission, guselkumab was stopped in 2025, and the patient continues to be followed in sustained remission of both PG and CD.

Discussion

PG is a rare, debilitating neutrophilic dermatosis with an incompletely understood pathogenesis. Although its aetiopathogenesis is unclear, it is regarded as an autoinflammatory disorder, accompanied by neutrophilic infiltration, dysregulated innate immunity and cytokines.3 In addition to innate immunity and proinflammatory cytokines, adaptive immune mechanisms also contribute to PG pathogenesis. The interleukin (IL)-23/T-helper 17 (Th17) axis plays a central role, and IL-17A is essential for neutrophil recruitment and migration.3,4 In one study, IL-23 was shown to be upregulated in tissue samples from recalcitrant PG lesions, at the ­transcriptional and protein levels.5

CD, ulcerative colitis and inflammatory arthritis are common immune-mediated disorders associated with PG.6 In a US population-based study, Xu et al. reported that the prevalence of inflammatory bowel disease among patients with PG was 34% compared with 0.9% among control participants.7 It is also well known that IL-23 plays an important role in the pathophysiology of CD.8,9

Management of PG includes wound care, pain management and immunomodulation. Recalcitrant cases of PG can be challenging and may necessitate use of biologics. Although there is no standard consensus on biologic therapies for PG, an increasing amount of evidence supports their use as a therapeutic option, particularly in severe cases.3,10 Tumour necrosis factor-α inhibitors, IL-1β inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, ustekinumab and IL-23 p19 inhibitors, including guselkumab, risankizumab and tildrakizumab, are among the biologics used in treatment.11

Guselkumab is a monoclonal antibody that targets the p19 subunit of IL-23, with expanding off-label use in several immune-mediated inflammatory skin disorders beyond plaque psoriasis.12 In several case reports, guselkumab has recently shown promise in refractory PG. In the literature, all patients successfully treated with guselkumab for PG have several systemic comorbidities, including monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, type 2 diabetes, lower extremity deep venous thrombosis and ulcerative colitis.13–15 Our case contributes to this growing body of literature in several aspects. Our patient had CD as a comorbidity, which, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported in conjunction with guselkumab-treated PG. Most notably, dual remission of both PG and CD was achieved and sustained over 2 years on guselkumab monotherapy, marking a uniquely favourable ­outcome in a recalcitrant setting. In addition, 1 year after discontinuation of guselkumab, the patient has remained in remission without systemic treatment, suggesting the possibility of drug-free remission in selected cases.

The successful response in our patient highlights the potential mechanistic role of IL-23 in PG pathogenesis. By selectively targeting p19 subunit of IL-23, guselkumab may reduce the pathogenic cascade driven by this cytokine, which promotes the expansion of Th17 cells. These cells secrete proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-17 and IL-22, contribute to neutrophil recruitment and tissue damage.4 This cascade is particularly important in patients with comorbid CD and PG, where overlapping cytokine pathways are involved.

In conclusion, our case emphasizes the successful use of guselkumab in a patient with refractory PG and concomitant CD with long-term disease control in both diseases. Guselkumab may represent a promising therapeutic option for difficult-to-treat PG, particularly in patients with immune-mediated comorbidities. However, further studies are needed to elucidate its role within the treatment strategies of PG.
