Factors influencing fenestration and dehiscence in the anterior teeth after clear aligner treatment: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Zhixin Song, Qian Liu, Houzhuo Luo, Xinyue Fan, Mingxin Zhang, Baoning Liu, Bin Yan, Xiaodong Zhang, Liling Ren, Yanning Ma, Zuolin Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis retrospective clinical study, using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), examined the presence of fenestration and dehiscence in anterior teeth following non-extraction clear aligner therapy, along with the associated risk factors.MethodsThe study included 224 adult patients who underwent non-extraction clear aligner treatment. CBCT scans taken before (T1) and after treatment (T2) were analyzed to detect the presence of fenestration and dehiscence. Patient-related and treatment design-related variables were collected, with the latter from the planned tooth movements specified in the aligner software. The exact McNemar test was used to compare the occurrence of alveolar bone defects between T1 and T2. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between posttreatment alveolar bone defects and various factors.ResultsFrom T1 to T2, the occurrence of fenestration and dehiscence increased on most maxillary and mandibular anterior root surfaces. At T2, the presence of alveolar bone defects at different tooth positions was influenced by multiple factors, including crowding, curve of Spee depth, alveolar bone thickness, gingival thickness, sagittal skeletal pattern, magnitude and type of intrusion, amount of retraction, molar distalization, and number of attachments. Specifically, labial fenestration of the maxillary lateral incisors and canines, as well as the mandibular central incisors and canines, and lingual dehiscence of the mandibular central incisors were significantly associated with the amount of intrusion (P < 0.05). Labial dehiscence of the mandibular central and lateral incisors was significantly associated with the number of attachments (P < 0.05). No significant associations were found between alveolar bone defects and factors such as age, gender, vertical skeletal pattern, changes in arch width, use of miniscrews, the amount of interproximal enamel reduction, attachment type, or treatment duration.ConclusionsIn patients undergoing non-extraction clear aligner therapy, factors such as crowding, curve of Spee depth, alveolar bone thickness, gingival thickness, sagittal skeletal pattern, amount and type of intrusion, amount of retraction, molar distalization, and number of attachments were significantly associated with the presence of fenestration and dehiscence at T2. Orthodontists should assess these factors, conduct thorough pretreatment evaluations of individual oral conditions, and develop tailored treatment plans accordingly.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40510-025-00585-z.Keywords: Clear aligner therapy, Fenestration, Dehiscence, Alveolar bone defects, CBCT

Introduction

In recent years, clear aligner therapy has undergone substantial advancements. Owing to their esthetic appeal, comfort, and removability, clear aligners have gained widespread popularity among adult patients. Approximately 25% of orthodontists now consider them their preferred treatment modality [1]. Clear aligners have demonstrated effectiveness in managing cases involving mild to moderate crowding, deep overbite or overjet, molar distalization, and arch expansion, and are commonly used in non-extraction cases [2]. Some studies suggest that clear aligners apply light, intermittent forces, which may favor periodontal tissue remodeling [3]. However, other studies have reported that after non-extraction clear aligner therapy, some patients develop alveolar bone defects, whereas others are less susceptible [4, 5]. This highlights the need to investigate the risk factors associated with these defects and to identify high-risk individuals before initiating treatment.

Alveolar bone defects are recognized iatrogenic complications of orthodontic treatment, primarily manifesting as fenestration and dehiscence [6]. Fenestration is typically characterized as a localized, round or oval defect that does not involve the alveolar crest, whereas dehiscence presents as a V-shaped defect that extends to the alveolar crest [7]. These defects can lead to gingival recession and root exposure after treatment, negatively impacting esthetics and potentially resulting in complications such as dentin hypersensitivity, root caries, and tooth mobility [8]. Such outcomes may compromise both the results and long-term stability of orthodontic therapy. Importantly, not all alveolar bone defects are caused by orthodontic treatment. Previous studies have reported that some patients present with preexisting fenestration or dehiscence prior to initiating orthodontic therapy [9, 10]. The reported prevalence of fenestration in untreated dentitions ranges from 0.23% to 16.9%, while dehiscence has been observed in 0.99% to 13.4% of cases [6, 11–19]. Most evidence suggests that fenestration occurs more frequently in the maxilla, whereas dehiscence is more common in the mandible. Labial defects tend to be more prevalent than lingual ones, and the anterior region is more frequently affected than the posterior region [20–22]. Notably, the presence of preexisting fenestration and dehiscence increases both the complexity and risk of orthodontic treatment, while inappropriate orthodontic therapy may induce or aggravate these defects [23].

Previous studies on alveolar bone defects following orthodontic treatment have primarily focused on conventional fixed appliances [24–29]. For instance, Luo et al. [27] reported an increased occurrence of fenestration and dehiscence on the labial and lingual surfaces of anterior teeth after treatment with fixed appliances, except on the lingual surfaces of maxillary anterior teeth and the labial surfaces of the mandibular canines. They also found that factors such as age, tooth extraction, sagittal skeletal pattern, and use of miniscrews significantly influenced the occurrence of posttreatment alveolar bone defects in the anterior region. In contrast to fixed appliances, clear aligners represent a newer orthodontic modality that places greater emphasis on the orthodontist’s pretreatment, three-dimensional planning and precise tooth positioning. To date, only a limited number of studies have investigated the relationship between clear aligner therapy and alveolar bone defects [4, 5, 30–32]. Jiang et al. [4] reported a trend toward increased alveolar bone defects following non-extraction clear aligner therapy; however, their analysis was limited to the mandibular anterior teeth and did not include the maxillary anterior region. Similarly, Allahham et al. [5] found that non-extraction clear aligner therapy was associated with an increased risk of fenestration and dehiscence, identifying age and labial tooth positioning as risk factors for posttreatment fenestration. However, their study did not explore site-specific risk factors. Moreover, previous studies were constrained by small sample sizes (ranging from 14 to 69 cases), limited methodological scope, and narrow focus on a single type of tooth movement or isolated patient characteristics, lacking a comprehensive evaluation of multiple potential influencing factors [4, 5, 30–32].

In recent years, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) has become widely adopted in clinical dentistry due to its lower radiation dose and superior resolution compared to conventional imaging modalities [33]. CBCT offers more accurate three-dimensional assessment of alveolar bone morphology and has demonstrated higher diagnostic sensitivity and specificity in detecting fenestration and dehiscence compared to traditional intraoral radiographs [34].

Therefore, the present study aimed to compare the distribution of fenestration and dehiscence in the anterior region before and after non-extraction clear aligner treatment using CBCT, based on a large, multicenter clinical data. In addition, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify multiple risk factors associated with alveolar bone defects in different anterior tooth sites, with the goal of minimizing their occurrence during clear aligner therapy and enhancing both the precision and safety of orthodontic treatment.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study included patients who completed clear aligner treatment with the Angelalign system (EA Medical Instruments, Shanghai, China) between December 2020 and December 2024 at the Department of Orthodontics of the Stomatological Hospital of the Fourth Military Medical University, Shanxi Medical University Hospital of Stomatology, the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, General Hospital of Northern Theater Command, and Hospital of Stomatology Lanzhou University (Fig. 1). Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Stomatological Hospital of the Fourth Military Medical University (Approval No. IRB-YJ-2022035), and all procedures were carried out in accordance with the approved guidelines.

Flowchart of patient selection

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) complete permanent dentition, excluding third molars; (3) completed non-extraction clear aligner treatment; (4) periodontal health, with no evidence of horizontal or vertical bone loss; (5) high-quality CBCT images at both pretreatment (T1) and posttreatment (T2), free of artifacts; and (6) well-defined digital dental models (DDMs) at T1, with clear visualization of soft tissue at least 2 mm apical to the free gingival margin. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of orthodontic treatment or orthognathic surgery; (2) anterior teeth with pulp or periapical disease or prosthetic restorations; (3) congenitally missing, impacted, or supernumerary teeth; (4) presence of alveolar clefts or other bony defects; (5) severe craniofacial deformities or facial asymmetry; and (6) presence of systemic diseases (e.g., diabetes, osteoporosis, or hypertension), smoking habits, or pregnancy.

Sites with no alveolar bone defects at T1 but showing defects at T2 were classified as newly developed alveolar bone defects. To evaluate the factors associated with newly developed fenestration and dehiscence, 16 variables were analyzed, comprising both patient-related and treatment design-related factors. Patient-related factors included: (1) age; (2) gender; (3) sagittal skeletal pattern: Class I (0° ≤ ANB ≤ 4°), Class II (ANB >4°), and Class III (ANB < 0°) [35]; (4) vertical skeletal pattern: hypodivergent (Mp-SN ≤ 29° or Mp-FH ≤ 22°), normodivergent (29° < Mp-SN < 40° and 22° < Mp-FH < 32°), and hyperdivergent (Mp-SN ≥ 40° or Mp-FH ≥ 32°) [9]; (5) crowding; (6) depth of the curve of Spee; (7) alveolar bone thickness; and (8) gingival thickness. Treatment design-related factors included: (1) changes in intercanine width; (2) amount of first molar distalization; (3) use of miniscrews; (4) amount of tooth intrusion; (5) type of intrusion, classified as absolute or relative, with relative intrusion including labial-inclined and lingual-inclined intrusion, and absolute intrusion referring to vertical intrusion [36]; (6) amount of tooth retraction; (7) amount of interproximal enamel reduction (IPR); (8) number of attachments per tooth; (9) type of attachments; and (10) treatment duration. Treatment design-related factors were extracted from the first-stage treatment plans recorded in iOrtho (version 9.3; Angelalign Technology Inc., Shanghai, China). Tooth movement data were derived from the planned movements from the digital setup rather than from the movements achieved in clinical practice.

CBCT scans were acquired using two types of scanners. One was the NewTom VG scanner (Verona, Italy), operated with a tube current of 3.8 mA, tube voltage of 110 kV, isotropic voxel size of 0.25 mm, and a field of view of 140 × 140 mm. The other was the Kavo Dental GmbH scanner (Biberach, Germany), operated with a tube current of 5.0 mA, tube voltage of 120 kV, isotropic voxel size of 0.25 mm, and a field of view of 160 × 190 mm. During CBCT acquisition, patients were instructed to avoid swallowing, maintain centric occlusion after exhalation, and align the Frankfort horizontal plane parallel to the floor. DDMs were obtained using the TRIOS 3 intraoral scanner (version 1.3.4.5; 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark), which has a measurement accuracy of 6.9 ± 0.9 μm.

Fenestration and dehiscence of maxillary and mandibular anterior teeth at T1 and T2 were assessed using 3D Slicer (version 5.6.2; https://www.slicer.org). The measurement process was carried out as follows. First, digital imaging and communications in medicine files from the CBCT scans were imported into 3D Slicer software. The measurement plane for each tooth was then defined using three reference lines corresponding to the axial (red), sagittal (yellow), and coronal (green) planes. In the coronal and sagittal views, the axial plane was adjusted to pass through the cementoenamel junction of the target tooth. In the axial view, the yellow line was rotated to pass through the widest portion of the root canal. Subsequently, in the coronal view, the yellow line was adjusted to intersect the midpoint between the root apex and the incisal edge. Finally, in the sagittal view, the green line was aligned to pass through the root apex and the cusp tip. Fenestration and dehiscence were diagnosed based on the criteria described by Choi et al. [37] and Cha et al. [38]. Alveolar bone defects were identified when discontinuity or absence of the labial or lingual cortical plate was observed in at least three consecutive sagittal slices. A defect was classified as fenestration if the defect did not involve the alveolar crest and the distance from the coronal to the apical margin of the defect (f/f’) was greater than 0 mm. Specifically, labial fenestration was diagnosed when f1 >0 mm, f2 >0 mm, and f3 >0 mm; lingual fenestration was diagnosed when f’1 >0 mm, f’2 >0 mm, and f’3 >0 mm. A defect was classified as dehiscence when the distance from the cementoenamel junction to the alveolar crest (d/d’) exceeded 2 mm. Labial dehiscence was diagnosed when d1 >2 mm, d2 >2 mm, and d3 >2 mm; lingual dehiscence was diagnosed when d’1 >2 mm, d’2 >2 mm, and d’3 >2 mm. The workflow for image analysis is illustrated in Fig. 2. Additionally, buccal and lingual alveolar bone thicknesses were measured on the largest sagittal section of each tooth using 3D Slicer software, at a point 6 mm apical to the cementoenamel junction.

Workflow for the analysis of alveolar bone defects: A, Definition of the CBCT measurement plane for an individual tooth using axial, coronal, and sagittal views; B, Diagnosis of fenestration and dehiscence in at least three consecutive sagittal slices

Following the method described by Mehta et al. [39], lateral cephalometric radiographs were generated from the T1 CBCT images using Dolphin (version 11.95; Dolphin Imaging and Management Solutions, CA, USA) and analyzed to obtain cephalometric measurements, including ANB, Mp-SN, and Mp-FH values. Crowding and curve of Spee depth were measured using Ortho Analyzer (version 1.0; 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) based on the T1 DDMs. The occlusal plane was established using three reference points: the midpoint of the incisal edge of the right and left central incisors and the distolabial cusp tips of the second molars [40]. Vertical distances from the cusp tips of the canines, labial cusps of the first and second premolars, mesiolabial cusps of the first molars, and mesiolabial cusps of the second molars to this plane were recorded. The depth of the curve of Spee was calculated as the average of the maximum vertical distances on both sides (Fig. 3A). To assess gingival thickness, T1 CBCT scans were registered with the corresponding DDMs using Mimics (version 21.0; Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) [41]. Automatic registration was first performed by aligning 8 to 10 hard tissue landmarks, followed by manual fine-tuning. Gingival thickness (GT/GT’) was measured on the sagittal slice showing the maximum root surface area of each tooth, at a point 2 mm apical to the free gingival margin (Fig. 3B) [42].

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was performed using the logistic regression module in PASS (version 21.0; NCSS, Kaysville, UT, USA). Based on findings from Allahham et al. [5] and our pilot study, a two-sided alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 80% were used. When all covariates were fixed at their mean values, the frequency of labial fenestration in the maxillary canine was estimated at 15%. Holding all other covariates constant, a 1 mm increase in the amount of intrusion was associated with an expected frequency of 30%. The coefficient of determination (R²) among the covariates was set at 0.3. Considering an anticipated dropout rate of approximately 10%, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 124. Ultimately, 224 cases were included in this study, which met the required sample size and did not compromise statistical power.

To minimize bias, all patient identifiers and orthodontic treatment records were anonymized during the measurement process. Twenty patients were randomly selected from the sample, and all measurable variables were reassessed. Intraobserver reliability was evaluated by having the same orthodontist repeat the measurements after a 4-week interval, while interobserver reliability was assessed by two orthodontists independently performing the measurements. The consistency of categorical variables was analyzed using the Kappa coefficient, and that of continuous variables was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed data were reported as means and standard deviations (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t-tests for normally distributed variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables. The chi-square test was employed for comparisons of categorical variables. The exact McNemar test was used to compare the occurrence of fenestration and dehiscence at various anterior tooth positions between T1 and T2. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify potential factors associated with newly developed fenestration and dehiscence at T2. Variables that reached statistical significance in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression model to determine independent risk factors. Moreover, because there were no significant differences between the left and right sides for the same tooth position, and to meet the assumption of independence of observations in logistic regression, only the right-side teeth were included in the analysis. Logistic regression was performed at the patient level. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.3; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria), with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

All measurement variables demonstrated excellent intraobserver and interobserver reliability. The Kappa values for categorical variables were all 1. For continuous variables, the ICC values for intraobserver reliability ranged from 0.961 to 0.986, and those for interobserver reliability ranged from 0.924 to 0.968, as detailed in Supplementary Table (1). The patient selection flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 224 patients were included in the final analysis, with evaluations conducted on both the maxillary and mandibular anterior teeth. Patient demographics and treatment design characteristics are summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Table (2). The median patient age was 24 years (IQR, 22.0–28.0), with 38.4% male and 61.6% female participants. The median treatment duration was 22.8 months (IQR, 19.6–30.6). Regarding sagittal skeletal classification, 19.2% of patients exhibited Class I patterns, 54.0% had Class II, and 26.8% had Class III. In terms of vertical skeletal patterns, 25.4% were hypodivergent, 42.4% normodivergent, and 32.1% hyperdivergent. The mean maxillary crowding was 5.3 ± 1.7 mm, while the mean mandibular crowding was 4.4 ± 1.8 mm. The average increase in intercanine width was 1.8 ± 1.6 mm in the maxilla and 1.1 ± 1.4 mm in the mandible. The median distal movement of the maxillary first molars was 1.1 mm (IQR, 0.7–1.8), and that of the mandibular first molars was 1.1 mm (IQR, 0.4–1.4). Miniscrews were employed in 34.4% of maxillary cases and in 26.8% of mandibular cases.

Table 2 compares the occurrence of fenestration and dehiscence among different types of anterior teeth between T1 and T2. Overall, the occurrence of fenestration and dehiscence increased on the root surfaces of most maxillary and mandibular anterior teeth from T1 to T2. Specifically, compared to T1, a significant increase in the occurrence of fenestration was observed on the labial surfaces of the maxillary lateral incisors, maxillary canines, mandibular central incisors, and mandibular canines at T2 (P < 0.001). Similarly, the occurrence of dehiscence significantly increased on the labial surfaces of the maxillary lateral incisors, mandibular central incisors, mandibular lateral incisors, and mandibular canines, as well as on the lingual surfaces of the mandibular central incisors (P < 0.001). In contrast, the occurrence of lingual fenestration in both maxillary and mandibular anterior teeth remained consistently low at both T1 and T2. No lingual fenestration was detected in the maxillary central or lateral incisors at either time point. Only one case (0.4%) of lingual fenestration was identified in the maxillary canines, and this remained unchanged between T1 and T2.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses of newly developed fenestration at T2

To identify risk factors associated with newly developed fenestration at T2 in different types of teeth following clear aligner treatment, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted. For newly developed labial fenestration of the maxillary lateral incisors, univariate analysis identified significant associations with maxillary crowding, the amount of intrusion, and the amount of retraction of the maxillary lateral incisors. Multivariate analysis further revealed that maxillary crowding (odds ratio [OR], 1.668; 95% confidence interval CI 1.353–2.056) and the amount of intrusion of the maxillary lateral incisors (OR, 1.552; 95% CI 1.212–1.912) were independent risk factors, whereas the amount of retraction of the maxillary lateral incisors (OR, 0.768; 95% CI 0.598–0.986) was identified as an independent protective factor (Table 3). For maxillary canines, univariate analysis revealed significant associations with the depth of the maxillary curve of Spee, labial alveolar bone thickness, and the amount of intrusion of the maxillary canines. Multivariate analysis further demonstrated that both the depth of the maxillary curve of Spee (OR, 2.572; 95% CI 1.626–4.080) and the amount of intrusion (OR, 1.546; 95% CI 1.141–2.093) were independent risk factors, whereas the labial alveolar bone thickness (OR, 0.041; 95% Cl 0.005–0.346) was an independent protective factor (Table 4).

Discussion

In non-extraction clear aligner therapy, dental crowding is typically resolved through two primary approaches: (1) increasing arch length via arch expansion, anterior tooth proclination, or molar distalization, and (2) reducing overall tooth volume through IPR [43]. However, both animal and clinical studies have demonstrated that tooth movement beyond the anatomical boundaries of the alveolar bone can elevate the risk of alveolar bone defects, such as fenestration and dehiscence [23, 44]. Therefore, identifying risk factors associated with the development of these defects and incorporating individual anatomical characteristics into treatment planning are essential for developing personalized strategies that minimize periodontal risks during clear aligner therapy.

Although previous studies have examined the presence of fenestration and dehiscence following non-extraction clear aligner treatment [4, 5, 31, 32], most were limited by small sample sizes, a narrow set of variables, and relatively simple analytical methods. Patient-specific and treatment design-related factors have not been thoroughly evaluated. In this study, we used a large, multicenter sample and applied both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses to comprehensively investigate the occurrence and associated factors of fenestration and dehiscence in anterior teeth. Our findings revealed that the presence of alveolar bone defects on the root surfaces of most anterior teeth increased after non-extraction clear aligner treatment. Notably, significant increases were observed in nine specific sites, including labial fenestration of the maxillary lateral incisors and labial dehiscence of the mandibular central incisors. With the exception of lingual dehiscence in the mandibular central incisors, most posttreatment defects were located on the labial surfaces. This distribution pattern likely reflects the labial movement of anterior teeth that characterizes non-extraction clear aligner therapy [23, 45]. Moreover, the labiolingual thickness of the basal bone in the mandibular symphysis is generally narrower than that of the maxilla, resulting in thinner alveolar bone coverage over the roots of the mandibular central incisors [46, 47]. This anatomical limitation increases the likelihood of root proximity to both the labial and lingual cortical plates during tooth movement, thereby raising the risk of developing labial and lingual dehiscence following treatment.

In the subsequent univariate and multivariate analyses, we identified crowding as an independent risk factors for newly developed labial fenestration and dehiscence of the maxillary lateral incisors, as well as for newly developed labial dehiscence of the mandibular lateral incisors. Previous studies have similarly reported that patients with greater crowding are more susceptible to labial vertical alveolar bone loss in the maxillary central and mandibular incisors following non-extraction treatment with fixed appliances [48, 49]. In addition, Luo et al. [27] found that moderate crowding was more strongly associated with maxillary labial dehiscence than mild crowding. This may be attributed to the higher likelihood of “through-the-bone” tooth movement during crowding relief in adult patients [50]. Given that alveolar bone remodeling is neither immediate nor always sufficient to compensate for bone loss induced by tooth movement [51], treatment planning for patients with severe crowding requires careful consideration. When a non-extraction approach is selected, clinicians should thoroughly assess labial alveolar bone thickness and the safe range of tooth movement at high-risk sites before initiating treatment.

Furthermore, this study found that the depth of the curve of Spee was an independent risk factor for newly developed labial fenestration of the maxillary canines, and for both labial fenestration and dehiscence of the mandibular canines. During orthodontic treatment, leveling of the curve of Spee is typically achieved through anterior intrusion or posterior extrusion. Zhu et al. [52], using a three-dimensional finite element model, analyzed the stress distribution in anterior teeth during posterior extrusion and found that the equivalent stress in the periodontal ligament was primarily concentrated in the labial cervical region of the canines. Their analysis also showed a labial tipping tendency of the anterior teeth, with the center of rotation located between the apical and middle thirds of the root. These biomechanical findings help explain our clinical results and highlight the importance of paying special attention to the canine region in non-extraction cases treated with clear aligners, particularly in patients with a deep curve of Spee. Such considerations may help mitigate localized stress concentrations and reduce the risk of posttreatment alveolar bone complications, such as fenestration and dehiscence.

Moreover, this study found that both alveolar bone thickness and gingival thickness were significantly associated with the development of fenestration or dehiscence at most anterior tooth sites after treatment. Given that individuals typically do not present with a uniform gingival phenotype and that gingival thickness varies across different tooth positions as well as between the labial and lingual aspects [53], gingival thickness, rather than gingival phenotype, was chosen as the analytical parameter. In addition, gingival thickness was measured using a method that integrates CBCT images with DDMs, which offers a non-invasive, reliable, and reproducible alternative to conventional methods such as visual inspection or the periodontal probe transparency test [54]. Previous studies have demonstrated a significant relationship between gingival thickness and alveolar bone morphology. Notably, patients with a thin gingival phenotype are more likely to have reduced alveolar bone thickness [55], and are more susceptible to fenestration and dehiscence in the natural dentition [56]. Building on this evidence, the present study suggests that individuals with thin alveolar bone and gingiva prior to treatment may be more likely to exceed the anatomical limits of the alveolar bone during clear aligner therapy. However, to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have specifically explored the impact of alveolar bone thickness or gingival thickness on the development of new alveolar bone defects following clear aligner treatment. The present findings provide preliminary evidence in this area.

In addition to patient-related factors, this study also examined the impact of treatment design variables on the development of anterior alveolar bone defects. We found that the amount of intrusion was an independent risk factor for newly developed labial fenestration in the maxillary lateral incisors, maxillary canines, mandibular central incisors, and mandibular canines, as well as for newly developed lingual dehiscence in the mandibular central incisors. During intrusion, the tooth root often tends to rotate and move labially [57]. As the magnitude of intrusion increases, stress becomes more concentrated in the mid-root region of the alveolar bone [58], thereby increasing the risk of new labial fenestration in most anterior teeth. Furthermore, the occurrence of new lingual dehiscence in the mandibular central incisors was significantly associated with the amount of intrusion. This may be attributed to two key factors: first, the maximum tensile stress in the periodontal ligament during intrusion occurs on the lingual side of the tooth [59]; second, as previously noted, the lingual alveolar bone in the mandibular central incisor region is inherently thin [46, 47], making it more vulnerable to dehiscence during bone remodeling.

This study also examined the effects of different intrusion modalities and labiolingual tooth movements on the development of new anterior alveolar bone defects. In fixed orthodontic therapy, as force is typically applied to the labial surface of the tooth, anterior teeth are prone to relative intrusion [60]. In contrast, clear aligners apply forces through full coverage of the dentition, and the direction of force is theoretically more aligned with the long axis of the tooth, which is thought to favor absolute intrusion of the anterior teeth [61, 62]. Nevertheless, our findings indicate that relative intrusion is more commonly used in non-extraction clear aligner treatment plans. Specifically, relative intrusion of the mandibular central incisors was associated with a 1.918-fold higher risk of newly developed labial fenestration compared to absolute intrusion. We speculate that during relative intrusion with clear aligners, both stress and strain in the mid-root region of the alveolar bone may be more intense and prone to concentration than with absolute intrusion. In addition, the amount of labiolingual tooth movement was closely associated with the occurrence of alveolar bone defect at certain tooth positions. For the maxillary lateral incisors, each 1 mm increase in retraction reduced the risk of newly developed labial fenestration and dehiscence to 0.768-fold and 0.522-fold, respectively. In contrast, for the mandibular central incisors, each 1 mm increase in retraction was associated with a 2.601-fold increase in the risk of newly developed lingual dehiscence. These findings highlight the importance of carefully limiting the extent of labiolingual movement in high-risk teeth during clear aligner treatment planning and emphasize the need to cautiously evaluate the potential adverse effects on the alveolar bone when creating space through relative intrusion.

In addition, the amount of first molar distalization was identified as a contributing factor to newly developed labial dehiscence in the maxillary lateral incisors, mandibular central incisors, and mandibular lateral incisors. From a biomechanical perspective, while distalizing forces are applied to the first molars during clear aligner treatment, reciprocal forces are simultaneously transmitted to the anterior teeth, particularly the incisors, causing them to move labially and thereby increasing the risk of labial dehiscence [63, 64]. A finite element analysis further supported this mechanism, demonstrating that during first molar distalization, the lateral incisors exhibit the most pronounced labial tipping, with the highest equivalent stress concentrated at the labial alveolar crest [65]. This corresponds to our observed association between increased molar distalization and newly developed labial dehiscence in the lateral incisors. Interestingly, despite the widespread use of miniscrews for anchorage reinforcement, our study did not find a significant reduction in the occurrence of anterior alveolar bone defects associated with their use. This may be attributed to the fact that anchorage loss cannot be completely eliminated, even with miniscrew support [65–67]. From a clinical perspective, careful assessment of the extent of molar distalization is essential. For tooth sites identified as high-risk, implementing a degree of lingual overcorrection of the crowns may be beneficial. Additionally, rigid ligation between the canines and miniscrews could be considered to help minimize adverse reciprocal forces exerted on the anterior teeth.

This study also quantitatively assessed the impact of the number and type of attachments on newly developed alveolar bone defects in the anterior region. We found that, compared to cases without attachments, the risk of newly developed labial dehiscence in the mandibular central and lateral incisors increased by 2.044-fold and 1.883-fold, respectively, when a single attachment was applied. Furthermore, no statistically significant difference was observed between conventional and optimized attachments in terms of their influence on newly developed alveolar bone defects. Attachments enhance aligner retention and assist in facilitating complex tooth movements. However, they also increase the magnitude and directionality of forces applied to the teeth and surrounding periodontal tissues. Compared to patients without attachments, those with attachments may experience greater orthodontic forces during wear and removal [68–70], potentially leading to localized stress concentration, especially in the mandibular incisor region. Zhang et al. [70] also highlighted potential adverse effects of attachments on periodontal health, reporting that the risk of open gingival embrasures was 9.501 times higher in patients with attachments on both mandibular central incisors compared to those without attachments. However, that study primarily focused on soft tissue changes and considered a limited number of influencing factors, without evaluating alterations in alveolar bone. To date, evidence on the relationship between attachment design and alveolar bone defects remains scarce and warrants further investigation.

In summary, non-extraction clear aligner therapy in adult patients should be approached with clinical caution. Orthodontists should be aware of patient-related risk factors for fenestration and dehiscence in different anterior tooth positions, including crowding, depth of the curve of Spee, alveolar bone thickness, gingival thickness, and sagittal skeletal pattern. For patients identified as high-risk or with preexisting alveolar bone defects, proactive prevention and close monitoring of fenestration and dehiscence are essential. During treatment planning, particular attention should be given to the amount and direction of intrusion, the amount of retraction, the extent of molar distalization, and the number and placement of attachments. Additionally, regular periodontal evaluations throughout treatment are strongly recommended, and augmented corticotomy may be considered when appropriate [71].

The CBCT images used in this study were obtained from two different machines, both with a voxel size of 0.25 mm, but with different fields of view (FOVs). Previous studies have shown that a larger FOV, while covering a broader anatomical area, is associated with higher radiation exposure and increased scattered radiation, which in turn leads to greater image noise and reduced image quality [72, 73]. In addition, compared with smaller voxel sizes, a 0.25 mm voxel can reduce radiation dose but also lowers image resolution, thereby compromising the diagnostic accuracy of alveolar bone defects [74, 75]. This effect is particularly pronounced in regions where the alveolar bone is thin (< 0.5 mm), as these areas are more susceptible to partial volume averaging effects [76, 77], potentially leading to an overestimation of alveolar bone defects [78]. However, in accordance with the “as low as reasonably achievable” (ALARA) principle, it is clinically impractical to acquire small-voxel CBCT images solely for the evaluation of alveolar bone defects [79, 80]. Moreover, as this was a retrospective study, it was not possible to reacquire CBCT scans with smaller voxel sizes or to standardize scanning parameters such as the FOV. Therefore, limitations in voxel size and variations in FOV represent inherent constraints of this study and should be considered when interpreting the results. It is worth noting that although these limitations may lead to an overestimation of alveolar bone defects in certain areas, from the perspective of risk prediction prior to orthodontic treatment, such overestimation may be viewed as a conservative bias rather than a misleading finding [37], and therefore does not compromise the validity of our results.

This study has several additional limitations. First, it only assessed the occurrence of fenestration and dehiscence before and after treatment, without evaluating the severity of alveolar bone defects. Second, some patients had pre-existing alveolar bone defects before treatment, but this baseline condition was not included as a variable in the regression model, which may have led to an underestimation of its impact on the development of new alveolar bone defects after treatment. Third, the tooth movement-related variables used in this study were based on planned movements rather than the actual movements achieved, which may have reduced the explanatory power of these indicators for alveolar bone defects. Finally, as a retrospective study, causal relationships could not be definitively established. Future studies are recommended to incorporate baseline alveolar bone defect status and actual tooth movement data, and to apply more advanced analytical methods, such as machine learning on larger sample sizes, to enable more accurate prediction of alveolar bone defects. Additionally, prospective studies are warranted to further elucidate the contributing factors to alveolar bone defects.

Conclusions

In adult patients undergoing non-extraction clear aligner therapy, the occurrence of fenestration and dehiscence significantly increased on the root surfaces of most maxillary and mandibular anterior teeth. Except for lingual dehiscence of the mandibular central incisors, the majority of posttreatment alveolar bone defects were located on the labial side.

Except for lingual dehiscence of the mandibular central incisors, the majority of posttreatment alveolar bone defects were located on the labial side.

The occurrence of posttreatment fenestration and dehiscence was associated with various patient-related and treatment design-related factors. Patient-related factors associated with the development of posttreatment fenestration and dehiscence included crowding, depth of the curve of Spee, alveolar bone thickness, gingival thickness, and sagittal skeletal pattern. Treatment design-related factors included the amount and type of intrusion, the amount of retraction, the extent of molar distalization, and the number of attachments.
