Effect of prior anti-EGFR therapy and baseline ctDNA profiling on the efficacy of pertuzumab plus trastuzumab in HER2-amplified metastatic colorectal cancer: an integrated analysis of TRIUMPH/MyPathway
Authors: Y Matsubara, M Wakabayashi, W Okamoto, T Kato, T Esaki, K Kato, Y Komatsu, T Masuishi, T Nishina, H Bando, T Misumi, M Fukui, S Matsuda, Y Ikeda, J Malato, K Schulze, R Price, A Sato, T Yoshino, Y Nakamura

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHER2-amplified metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) is a clinically relevant subgroup for HER2-targeted therapy. The TRIUMPH and MyPathway studies demonstrated the activity of pertuzumab plus trastuzumab (PER + TRA) in that population. Our integrated analysis assessed the effect of prior anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) therapy on PER + TRA efficacy and explored the role of baseline circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) profiling.Materials and methodsThe analysis enrolled patients with HER2-amplified mCRC from TRIUMPH and MyPathway who had baseline ctDNA available. Anti-EGFR resistance alterations were defined as RAS, BRAF, or PIK3CA mutations; EGFR ectodomain mutation; or MET amplification. Clinical outcomes with PER + TRA were analyzed in patients who had tissue-based RAS wild-type tumors, stratified by prior anti-EGFR therapy and the presence of resistance alterations.ResultsOf 66 enrolled patients, 47 had received prior anti-EGFR therapy. Prior anti-EGFR therapy was associated with inferior overall survival (OS) [median: 10.9 versus 19.7 months; hazard ratio (HR): 2.01] and numerically shorter progression-free survival. ctDNA profiling identified anti-EGFR resistance alterations in 18 patients (27.3%), with an associated absence of objective response (0% versus 31.3%) and a significantly shorter OS (median: 7.6 versus 16.5 months; HR: 2.15). Outcomes were markedly poorer (including shorter OS and lower response rates) in patients with either prior anti-EGFR therapy or resistance alterations than in patients with neither factor.ConclusionsPrior anti-EGFR therapy and resistance alterations detected by baseline ctDNA profiling are associated with reduced efficacy of PER + TRA in HER2-amplified mCRC. Integrating treatment history with baseline ctDNA profiling might enable improved patient selection for dual HER2-targeted therapy.Key words: colorectal cancer, HER2, ERBB2, trastuzumab, pertuzumab, EGFR

Introduction

Patients with HER2-amplified metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) constitute a small but clinically important subgroup for whom HER2-targeted treatment might provide therapeutic benefit, analogous to that in HER2-positive breast or gastroesophageal cancer.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6

HER2 amplification occurs in a limited proportion of mCRC, and yet it has emerged as a relevant oncogenic driver and a potential target for precision treatment strategies. The dual HER2-targeted combination of pertuzumab plus trastuzumab (PER + TRA) has demonstrated clinical activity in that setting. The phase 2 TRIUMPH trial demonstrated the feasibility and efficacy of PER + TRA in patients with HER2-amplified, RAS wild-type mCRC identified using tissue or circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) screening.1 Similarly, the phase 2 MyPathway trial reported meaningful antitumor activity for PER + TRA in patients with HER2-amplified mCRC confirmed by immunohistochemistry, in situ hybridization, or next-generation sequencing.2 Together, those studies established PER + TRA as a promising therapeutic option for this molecularly defined subgroup.

Despite similarities in trial design, TRIUMPH and MyPathway had a key difference with respect to patient selection. MyPathway enrolled patients regardless of anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) therapy history, whereas TRIUMPH required prior exposure to anti-EGFR therapy. Notably, objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rates appeared to be improved for MyPathway patients who received no prior anti-EGFR therapy compared with those who did receive such therapy.2 Those findings raised the possibility that resistance mechanisms induced by anti-EGFR therapy might influence the efficacy of subsequent HER2-targeted treatment. However, the individual trials were not sufficiently powered to formally assess the effect of prior anti-EGFR therapy on outcomes with PER + TRA.

Given that comprehensive ctDNA profiling can identify resistance-associated genomic alterations that might modulate treatment response and provide mechanistic insights into observed clinical heterogeneity, we conducted an integrated analysis of the TRIUMPH and MyPathway trials, incorporating baseline ctDNA evaluation to investigate the effect of prior anti-EGFR therapy and associated resistance alterations on the efficacy of PER + TRA in patients with HER2-amplified mCRC.

Materials and methods

Data source and patients

Our analysis enrolled patients with HER2-amplified mCRC who received PER + TRA in the phase 2 TRIUMPH trial or MyPathway trial. Patient-level data with consent for secondary use were made available through the secure Vivli data-sharing platform (https://vivli.org/), with TRIUMPH data supplied by National Cancer Center, Japan and MyPathway data supplied by Genentech. All analyses were conducted within the Vivli environment.

Eligible patients were those enrolled in either trial who had tissue-based RAS wild-type disease, available baseline ctDNA samples obtained before PER + TRA initiation, and consent for secondary data use. A key difference between the trials was prior anti-EGFR antibody exposure: TRIUMPH enrolled patients who were refractory or intolerant to anti-EGFR therapy, whereas MyPathway included patients regardless of prior anti-EGFR therapy.

Key eligibility criteria for TRIUMPH included age 20 years or older; histologically confirmed metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma; RAS wild-type status, defined by tissue testing or ctDNA profiling; prior treatment with fluoropyrimidines, oxaliplatin, irinotecan, and anti-EGFR antibodies; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status 0-1; measurable disease per RECIST version 1.1; no prior HER2-targeted therapy; and adequate organ function. For MyPathway, key eligibility criteria included age 18 years or older; progression after standard therapies or lack of suitable alternative treatment options; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status 0-2; and measurable or evaluable disease. HER2 positivity was defined in both studies primarily as 3+ or 2+ by immunohistochemistry, with in situ hybridization positivity (HER2/CEP17 ratio ≥2.0) on tumor tissue. TRIUMPH additionally permitted HER2 amplification identified by central circulating free DNA next-generation sequencing, whereas MyPathway accepted local testing results, including tissue- or blood-based techniques, provided that blood-based findings were supported by tissue confirmation.

Procedure

Patients received intravenous pertuzumab (840 mg loading dose, followed by 420 mg) and intravenous trastuzumab (8 mg/kg loading dose, followed by 6 mg/kg) every 3 weeks until disease progression, death, unacceptable toxicity, or treatment discontinuation for any other reason. Tumor assessment by computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging was carried out every 6 weeks during the first 24 weeks and at protocol-specified intervals thereafter (every 9 weeks in the TRIUMPH trial and every 12 weeks in the MyPathway trial). Tumor response was assessed according to RECIST version 1.1 by investigators at each study site.

ctDNA assessment

In both trials, ctDNA analysis was carried out using the Guardant360 assay (Guardant Health, Redwood City, CA), a clinically validated next-generation sequencing platform assessing 74 cancer-related genes, including single-nucleotide variants, insertions and deletions, gene fusions, copy number alterations, and microsatellite instability.7 Baseline ctDNA samples obtained before PER + TRA initiation were used to identify ERBB2 amplification and other genomic alterations.

For our analysis, anti-EGFR resistance alterations were defined as RAS, BRAF, and PIK3CA mutations; EGFR ectodomain mutation; and MET amplification, including both subclonal and clonal alterations. ctDNA fraction was measured using the maximum variant allelic fraction (VAF). To estimate the circulating free DNA clonality for somatic single-nucleotide variants, indels, and fusions, relative clonality was initially defined as alteration VAF divided by maximum somatic VAF in the sample. Mutations with a relative clonality of <0.3 were classified as subclonal mutations. The adjusted plasma copy number was defined as [observed plasma copy number − 2 × (1 − T%)]/T%, where T% = 2 × maximum VAF/100.8

Statistical analysis

Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) are examined using the Kaplan–Meier method. OS and PFS were defined from the initiation of PER + TRA to the date of death due to any cause and to the date of disease progression or death, whichever occurred first, respectively. Patients were censored at their last visit date. Survival differences were assessed using log-rank tests, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests, whereas categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. All statistical analyses were carried out using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC), except for the oncoprint and column chart, which were generated using R (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics approval

Both TRIUMPH and MyPathway were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee at each trial center. All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment. Our integrated analysis was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Cancer Center, Japan (2017-193). The requirement for additional informed consent was waived because of the retrospective observational nature of the analysis, with an opt-out mechanism provided via the institution’s website.

Results

Patient population

Of 30 patients enrolled in TRIUMPH and 57 enrolled in MyPathway, 29 and 46 respectively had baseline ctDNA samples collected before PER + TRA treatment. After confirmation of consent for secondary data use, 29 patients from TRIUMPH and 41 from MyPathway were eligible for analysis. After patients with tissue-based RAS mutation (n = 4) were excluded, 66 patients were available for the final analysis (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics in the two groups were generally well balanced, with the exception of higher rates of lung metastasis and ctDNA-detected ERBB2 amplification in patients with prior anti-EGFR therapy (Table 1). Most patients had left-sided primary tumors (82%) and liver (77%) or lung (73%) metastases. PER + TRA was administered as second-line therapy in 8% of patients and as third-line or later therapy in 91%, with no significant difference in the number of prior treatment lines by anti-EGFR therapy history (P = 0.246).

Overall efficacy of PER + TRA

In the 66 analyzed patients, the ORR was 22.7% (95% CI 13.3% to 34.7%) and the disease control rate was 72.7% (95% CI 60.4% to 83.0%). Median PFS was 4.0 months, with 6- and 12-month PFS rates of 26.9% and 14.3%, respectively. Median OS was 12.9 months, with 6- and 12-month OS rates of 76.8% and 52.3%, respectively (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107734).

The prognostic classification previously defined in TRIUMPH was then validated using ctDNA-based favorability factors, defined as a tumor-fraction-adjusted plasma copy number of 16.7 or greater and no concurrent RTK/RAS/PI3K alterations (Figure 2). A significantly longer PFS (median: 5.6 versus 2.9 months; HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.28–0.89, P = 0.017) and a numerically longer OS (median: 22.5 versus 10.5 months; HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.29–1.07, P = 0.073) were observed for patients with favorable ctDNA features compared with patients lacking such features (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107734). The ORR (55.6% versus 9.1%, P < 0.001) and disease control rate (94.4% versus 63.6%, P = 0.014) were also significantly higher in the favorable-features subgroup.

Efficacy by prior anti-EGFR therapy

Compared with patients who had received prior anti-EGFR therapy, those without such therapy experienced a numerically longer PFS and a significantly longer OS (Figure 3). Median PFS was 3.1 months in patients with prior therapy compared with 5.4 months in those without prior therapy (HR 1.55, 95% CI 0.89–2.68, P = 0.12), with 6-month PFS rates of 35.5% and 23.4%, and 12-month PFS rates of 23.7% and 10.6%, respectively. Median OS was 10.9 months in patients with prior anti-EGFR therapy compared with 19.7 months in those without prior therapy (HR 2.01, 95% CI 1.03–3.91, P = 0.036), with 6-month OS rates of 75.9% and 78.9%, and 12-month OS rates of 43.7% and 73.3%, respectively. Consistent with those survival outcomes, a higher ORR [26.3% (95% CI 9.2% to 51.2%) versus 21.3% (95% CI 10.7% to 35.7%), P = 0.658] and a higher disease control rate [89.5% (95% CI 66.9% to 98.7%) versus 66.0% (95% CI 50.7% to 79.1%), P = 0.052] were observed for patients without prior anti-EGFR therapy than for those with such therapy (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107734).

Effect of ctDNA-detected anti-EGFR resistance alterations

ERBB2 amplification detected by ctDNA before treatment was observed in 46 of the 66 analyzed patients, and more often in those with prior anti-EGFR therapy (78.7%) than in those without such therapy (47.4%). The median ERBB2 copy number was also higher in the prior anti-EGFR group (5.02; range: 0.0–102.1), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Figure 2). Anti-EGFR resistance alterations were detected in 13 of 47 patients (27.7%) with prior anti-EGFR therapy and in 5 of 19 patients (26.3%) without such therapy, a nonsignificant difference between the groups (P = 1.0). Individual alterations were distributed as follows: RAS mutations [7/47 (14.9%) versus 1/19 (5.3%); clonal: 1/47 (2.1%) versus 0/19 (0%); subclonal: 6/47 (12.8%) versus 1/19 (5.3%)], BRAF mutations [3/47 (6.4%) versus 2/19 (10.5%); clonal: 2/47 (4.3%) versus 1/19 (5.3%); subclonal: 1/47 (2.1%) versus 1/19 (5.3%)], PIK3CA mutations [6/47 (12.8%) versus 2/19 (10.5%); clonal: 3/47 (6.4%) versus 2/19 (10.5%); subclonal 3/47 (6.4%) versus 0/19 (0%)], and MET amplification [2/47 (4.3%) versus 0/19 (0%)]. Overall, resistance alterations were mostly subclonal (10/47, 21.3%) rather than clonal (5/47, 10.6%) in patients with prior anti-EGFR therapy, whereas a more balanced distribution (clonal: 3/19, 15.8%; subclonal: 3/19, 15.8%) was evident in patients without such therapy. No EGFR ectodomain mutations were identified.

Compared with patients lacking anti-EGFR resistance alterations, those with such alterations experienced a shorter PFS (median: 3.0 versus 4.1 months; HR 1.49, 95% CI 0.85–2.58, P = 0.161) and a significantly shorter OS (median: 7.6 versus 16.5 months; HR 2.15, 95% CI 1.15–4.02, P = 0.014; Figure 4). Notably, no objective responses were observed in patients with resistance alterations, whereas 31.3% of patients without such alterations achieved a response (P = 0.007).

Exploratory analysis combining prior anti-EGFR therapy and resistance alterations

Patients with either prior anti-EGFR therapy or ctDNA-detected resistance alterations were compared with patients having neither factor. Median PFS was, respectively, 3.1 months and 5.5 months in those groups (HR 1.50, 95% CI 0.81–2.77, P = 0.192). A marked difference in OS was also observed (median: 10.9 months versus 49.7 months; HR 3.06, 95% CI 1.34–7.02, P = 0.006; Figure 4). The ORR was numerically lower in patients with prior anti-EGFR therapy or resistance alterations (or both) than in those without either factor (19.2% versus 35.7%, P = 0.633), a difference that was, however, statistically nonsignificant (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107734).

Discussion

In this integrated analysis of two phase 2 trials, we evaluated the efficacy of PER + TRA in HER2-amplified mCRC and examined the effect of prior anti-EGFR therapy and baseline ctDNA alterations on treatment outcomes. Our results confirmed that PER + TRA is an active HER2-targeted regimen in this molecularly defined population but also demonstrated that prior anti-EGFR therapy and ctDNA-detected resistance alterations in this population are associated with reduced clinical benefit. Thus, treatment history alone identified a subgroup of patients less likely to benefit from PER + TRA, highlighting its potential value as a pragmatic clinical stratification factor.

The ORR observed in this analysis was lower than that reported in TRIUMPH and MyPathway individually.1,2 That difference is likely attributable, at least in part, to patient exclusions related to consent for secondary data use and the availability of baseline ctDNA samples, which reduced the effective sample size. Although the exclusions were not driven by treatment outcomes, they may have affected the efficacy estimates in this integrated cohort.

Consistent with prior subgroup analyses from MyPathway, PFS and OS with PER + TRA were shorter in patients with prior anti-EGFR therapy than in patients without such therapy, despite similar numbers of prior treatment lines.2 That observation suggests a potential for cross-resistance between anti-EGFR therapy and subsequent HER2 blockade rather than an effect driven solely by treatment intensity.

Cross-resistance between anti-EGFR and HER2-targeted therapies is unlikely to be explained exclusively by genomic resistance mechanisms. Nongenomic adaptive processes—such as tumor microenvironment remodeling, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, cancer-associated fibroblasts, and stromal interactions—have been implicated in acquired resistance to anti-EGFR therapy in colorectal cancer.9 Similar mechanisms have also been reported in resistance to HER2-targeted therapies in HER2-positive breast cancer.10 Those shared adaptive responses raise the possibility that nongenomic resistance induced by prior anti-EGFR therapy might attenuate the efficacy of subsequent PER + TRA therapy.

Several alterations in the MAPK pathway have been reported as acquired resistance mechanisms to anti-EGFR therapy.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Consistent with previous reports, EGFR-pathway resistance alterations detected by ctDNA were also associated with inferior outcomes of PER + TRA.1 Although the overall frequency of resistance alterations was similar in patients with and without prior anti-EGFR therapy, their clonal architecture differed. Resistance alterations were predominantly subclonal in patients previously treated with anti-EGFR agents, whereas the distribution of clonal and subclonal alterations was more balanced in patients without such exposure. In addition, MET amplification was detected exclusively in patients with prior anti-EGFR therapy. Those findings support the hypothesis that resistance alterations in previously treated patients largely represent acquired resistance driven by anti-EGFR selective pressure, reflecting dynamic tumor evolution rather than preexisting innate resistance. Nevertheless, resistance alterations were also identified in patients without prior anti-EGFR therapy, indicating that reduced efficacy of PER + TRA is not solely attributable to treatment-induced cross-resistance, but might also reflect primary resistance mechanisms. Previous ctDNA studies have demonstrated that tissue-based RAS wild-type mCRC can harbor resistance-associated alterations detected before subsequent therapy.17 In breast cancer, constitutive activation of MAPK signaling, through mutations in KRAS or BRAF, or loss of negative regulators, can confer resistance to HER2-targeted therapy independent of HER2 blockade.18, 19, 20 Persistent downstream signaling through those pathways might similarly undermine the efficacy of dual HER2 inhibition in mCRC. Recent clinical data further support the importance of treatment sequencing in HER2-amplified mCRC.

The randomized phase 2 S1613 trial demonstrated that higher levels of ERBB2 amplification were associated with greater benefit from PER + TRA than from irinotecan plus cetuximab as late-line treatment for patients with RAS/BRAF wild-type mCRC, after central confirmation of HER2 positivity.21 Given that HER2 is a member of the EGFR family, ERBB2 amplification might sustain downstream signaling despite EGFR inhibition, potentially explaining the poor efficacy of anti-EGFR therapy observed in HER2-amplified tumors. Indeed, several retrospective studies have reported inferior outcomes with anti-EGFR therapy in those circumstances. Taken together with our findings, the foregoing data suggest that early consideration of PER + TRA—before anti-EGFR therapy is used—might be advantageous for selected patients with HER2-amplified mCRC. Baseline ctDNA profiling might further refine patient selection by identifying resistance alterations associated with limited benefit. In this regard, Randon et al demonstrated in the PRESSING-HER2 study that a panel of genomic resistance alterations—including HER2 mutations/rearrangements and RTK/MAPK coalterations—assessed by comprehensive genomic profiling predicted primary resistance to dual HER2 blockade in HER2-positive, RAS wild-type mCRC, with further refinement when combined with HER2 copy-number assessment by next-generation sequencing, supporting the concept of negative hyperselection beyond RAS status in this setting.22

Our study has some limitations. First, the sample size of this integrated analysis was modest, although all data were prospectively collected within well-conducted clinical trials. Second, detailed response kinetics were not uniformly available across TRIUMPH and MyPathway, limiting any deeper analysis of response dynamics. Third, given the differing eligibility criteria used in the two trials, patients with prior anti-EGFR therapy were enrolled exclusively in MyPathway, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings related to treatment sequencing. These limitations underscore the exploratory nature of our analysis and highlight the need for prospective validation.

Conclusions

Prior anti-EGFR therapy is associated with reduced efficacy of PER + TRA in patients with HER2-amplified mCRC. Integration of baseline ctDNA profiling with treatment history might enable more precise patient selection and help to identify patients most likely to benefit from dual HER2-targeted therapy.
