Enhancing Immune Modulation, Growth Performance and Gut Health in Broiler Chickens Through Combined Pediococcus acidilactici Supplementation and Influenza Vaccination
Authors: Masoud Hosseinzadeh, Maryam Tajabadi‐Ebrahimi, Amir Tukmechi, Ghader Najafi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe poultry industry faces significant challenges from infectious diseases and environmental stressors, which negatively impact animal health and productivity. This study investigates the synergistic effects of Pediococcus acidilactici (PediGuard) supplementation combined with influenza vaccination on immune modulation, growth performance and gut health in broiler chickens. In a controlled trial, 300 Ross 308 broiler chicks were divided into four treatment groups: control (standard diet), probiotic‐only (diet supplemented with P. acidilactici), vaccine‐only (standard diet with influenza vaccination) and combined probiotic plus vaccine. The results demonstrate that P. acidilactici significantly enhances peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) viability, confirming its safety and biocompatibility. Supplementation led to marked upregulation of both pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐1, IL‐6, TNF‐α) and anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10, indicating potent immunomodulatory effects. Combining the probiotic with influenza vaccination resulted in a substantial increase in antibody titres, surpassing those achieved by vaccination alone, thereby highlighting the synergistic enhancement of adaptive immunity. In addition, P. acidilactici significantly improved growth metrics, including weight gain, specific growth rates and feed conversion ratios (FCR), alongside significant improvements in gut morphology characterised by increased villus height and crypt depth. These findings underscore the potential of P. acidilactici as an effective, non‐antibiotic probiotic strategy to improve immune function, vaccine efficacy, growth and gut health in poultry. This study supports the use of P. acidilactici as a natural approach to improving productivity and health outcomes in commercial poultry systems.

Introduction

The global poultry industry is essential for meeting the increasing demand for high‐quality animal protein, contributing significantly to food security. However, intensive poultry production faces numerous challenges, including infectious diseases, oxidative stress and economic losses associated with foodborne pathogens. These issues underscore the need for sustainable, innovative solutions to enhance poultry health and productivity while reducing reliance on traditional antibiotics, which can lead to resistance and residues in the food chain (Bajagai et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2018; Cutting 2011; Lee et al. 2016).

Recent advances highlight the role of probiotics, particularly lactic acid bacteria (LAB), in supporting poultry health. Probiotics such as Pediococcus acidilactici are gaining recognition for their ability to improve immune function, enhance gut health and promote nutrient absorption. Studies indicate that P. acidilactici supplementation can enhance the intestinal microflora balance, stimulate beneficial metabolites like lactic acid and bacteriocins and improve resilience against gastrointestinal and respiratory pathogens (Fu et al. 2019; Ghobadi et al. 2020; Cao et al. 2019; Ramlucken et al. 2020).

The probiotic P. acidilactici, particularly the strain CNCM I‐4622, has demonstrated potential in improving nutrient digestibility, calcium retention and bone mineralisation in poultry. This strain, shown to boost laying performance, nutrient absorption and egg quality, offers a promising alternative to antibiotics in supporting animal health without adverse effects on the food chain. In addition, CNCM I‐4622's impact on the gut‐bone axis the relationship between gut health and bone strength suggests a dual benefit in promoting intestinal and skeletal health, especially under conditions that challenge nutrient uptake (De Cesare et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2021; Marinho et al. 2007; Fortuoso et al. 2019).

Given the limitations of current influenza vaccination protocols in poultry, such as incomplete protection against various strains, the potential of probiotics as natural immunomodulators is notable. Probiotics, including P. acidilactici, have shown antiviral properties that may support immune responses to vaccinations, enhancing protection against infections like the H9N2 avian influenza. This adjunctive effect could improve immune resilience, reduce viral load and enhance cytokine modulation, thus offering a broader and more sustained defence against respiratory and intestinal pathogens (Chaucheyras‐Durand and Durand 2010; Taras et al. 2006; Agah et al. 2019; Ashrafian et al. 2020).

This study investigates, for the first time, the probiotic‐vaccine synergy of P. acidilactici CNCM I‐4622 supplementation and H9N2 influenza vaccination in broiler chickens through an integrative approach involving growth performance, intestinal morphology, immune cytokine profiling and molecular docking of probiotic‐derived peptides against host pattern recognition receptors. Unlike the previous research that focused either solely on vaccine‐free probiotic activity or evaluated immune modulation by lactobacilli without observation of molecular mechanistic events, in this study for the first time in vivo physiological and histological events are combined with in silico receptor–ligand interaction analysis to unravel how probiotic‐vaccine co‐administration would enhance host immunity. This integrative approach establishes a new paradigm for immunonutrition in poultry production and addresses important gaps in current knowledge of host microbe vaccine interactions (Al‐Khalaifah et al. 2019; Ramlucken et al. 2020; Ahmad et al. 2023; Jiang et al. 2021).

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Ethical Approval and Randomisation

The research was conducted as a completely randomised block trial from 1 September 2019 to 1 June 2020 at the Department of Animal Science, Urmia University, Iran. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IR.IAU.CTB.REC.1402.195). Day 1, 300 Ross 308 male chicks were weighed individually and allocated to four treatment blocks by matching body‐weight within ± 5% of the overall mean, a procedure previously used to minimise baseline heterogeneity in broiler trials.

Birds, Housing and Environmental Management

Chicks were reared in deep‐litter floor pens (35 × 65 × 95 cm) on 5–7 cm of kiln‐dried pine shavings renewed weekly to reduce microbial buildup. Brooder temperature started at 42°C and was reduced by 2°C–3°C weekly; relative humidity was 55%–65%. A 12 h light: 12 h dark photoperiod and negative‐pressure ventilation were employed at all times. Feed and water were given ad libitum via manual troughs and nipple drinkers (Sadeghi et al. 2013; Rostagno et al. 2017).

Diet Formulation and Feeding Phasesxperimental Diet and Feeding Schedule

Commercial mash diets were offered in three phases starter (Days 2–9), grower (Days 10–22) and finisher (Days 23–35) and were formulated to identical metabolisable energy (≈ 12.6–13.0 MJ kg−1) and crude‐protein levels (19%–23%), based on the nutrient matrices reported by Jazi et al. (2019). Ingredient composition and proximate values were confirmed by near‐infra‐red spectroscopy before trial commencement (Jazi et al. 2019; National Research Council 1994; Brenes and Roura 2010).

There were three replicate pens of 25 birds per treatment (n = 75): Control—basal diet only.Probiotic—basal diet + P. acidilactici at 1 × 10⁹ CFU g−1 feed.Vaccine—basal diet + inactivated H9N2 influenza vaccine.Probiotic + Vaccine—basal diet + P. acidilactici + vaccine.

Vaccine dose and probiotic inclusion rate regimens utilised herein were those that had been shown to enhance growth performance and immune status in broiler challenge models. High‐density polypropylene mesh dividers prevented inter‐pen cross‐contamination (Park et al. 2016; Al‐Khalaifah et al. 2019; Awad et al. 2009).

Probiotic Preparation, Top‐Dressing and Viability Rationale

P. acidilactici was cultured anaerobically in MRS broth (37°C, 24 h), centrifuged (3000 × g, 10 min, 4°C), washed twice with sterile 0.85% saline and resuspended to a concentration of 109 CFU g−1. Suspension was blended uniformly into cooled mash with a rotary paddle mixer for 90 s to ensure uniform distribution. This method ensured ≥ 9.0 log10 CFU g−1 viability upon drying, as is consistent with > 90% retention reported for maximally mixed lactic‐acid bacteria (Zhou et al. 2010; Ramlucken et al. 2020; De Cesare et al. 2020).

Influenza H9N2 Vaccine Selection and Administration

To provide immunity beyond that of the standard hatchery vaccine, an oil‐emulsion inactivated H9N2 vaccine based on strain A/Chicken/Iran/728/2012 closely similar to circulating field isolates was used. Chickens in the Vaccine and Probiotic + Vaccine groups received 0.5 mL intramuscularly on Day 3, coordinated with the expected decline of maternal antibodies and to capitalise on evidence that strain‐matched vaccines elicit enhanced systemic and mucosal immunity (Agah et al. 2019; Lee et al. 2016; Ashrafian et al. 2020).

Gut Health and Morphology

From each replicate (n = 6/group), intestinal health tests were performed on Day 35. In situ pH levels in ileal, jejunum and duodenal digesta were determined by a calibrated digital microelectrode probe following euthanasia to assess regional acidity patterns and fermentation kinetics.

Jejunal tissue biopsy specimens (3 cm mid‐jejunal segments) were excised, washed with saline and fixed in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin for 48 h. Dehydration was followed by paraffin embedding, sectioning to the thickness of 5 µm and haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Histomorphological measurements VH, CD and villus‐to‐crypt ratio (VCR) were obtained using ImageJ under light microscopy in accordance with the standardised protocol of Xu et al. (2003).

Aseptic caecal contents were collected to count microbes. The sample was 10‐fold serial dilution (10−1 to 10−7) in sterile peptone water and plated on MRS agar for Lactobacillus and Pediococcus genera, MacConkey agar for Escherichia coli and VRBA for total coliform count. The colony‐forming units (CFU) was calculated as log10 CFU per gram fresh digesta (Xu et al. 2003; Ghobadi et al. 2020; Cutting 2011).

Docking Studies

Docking simulations were conducted to explore potential molecular interactions between probiotic‐derived peptides and immune receptors. Three‐dimensional structures of selected peptides and receptors (e.g., TLR2, MHC‐II) were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Structure preparation was performed in MOE 2019 (Chemical Computing Group, Montreal, Canada), including protonation state assignment (Protonate3D), hydrogen atom addition, and removal of water molecules beyond 4.5 Å.

Docking followed a two‐tiered approach: MOE 2019 was used for initial docking, generating S‐scores (affinity estimates) and E_refine values after energy minimisation to evaluate shape complementarity and molecular interactions (Jones et al. 2022). ClusPro 2.0 (https://cluspro.bu.edu) was used for refinement and clustering analysis. Balanced and electrostatic scoring modes helped identify stable binding conformations, with clusters ranked by population and energy score (Kozakov et al. 2017; Vajda et al. 2017).

These simulations provided mechanistic insights into how probiotic components may enhance immunogenicity or modulate immune signalling pathways (Jones et al. 2022; Kozakov et al. 2017; Desta et al. 2020).

Statistical Analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± SEM and analysed using one‐way ANOVA (SPSS v26), followed by Tukey's HSD post hoc test. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. Experimental unit was the pen, and all analyses adhered to randomised complete block design (RCBD) assumptions, accounting for inter‐pen variability and replicate structure.

Results

In Silico Docking and Molecular Interaction Analysis

To elucidate the molecular basis of in‐vivo immunomodulation by P. acidilactici derived pediocin PA‐1, we performed an integrative in silico pipeline comprising (i) curation of structures, (ii) rigid body docking, (iii) energetic filtration and (iv) preliminary dynamic‐stability assessment. The ligand receptor pair was pediocin PA‐1 (PDB ID 7VLY) and the extracellular leucine‐rich‐repeat domain of chicken TLR4 (Gallus gallus); the two structures were protonated at pH 7.4 using Protonate3D and energy‐minimised in MOE 2019 to remove steric clashes.

Interaction Fingerprint Validation Under Quasi‐Dynamic Constraints

To compensate for the lack of full molecular dynamics (MD) simulation and to establish the stability of interactions, Protein–Ligand Interaction Fingerprint (PLIF) analysis was performed using MOE. Binding poses before and after implicit‐solvent energy minimisation (ε = 80) were compared using the Tanimoto similarity measure with a stringent overlap threshold of 85%. The results revealed that over 70% of significant hydrogen bond and hydrophobic contacts (e.g., residues 10, 12, 13, 16, 23) were preserved across the majority of top‐ranked docking poses. The findings are corroborative of the hypothesis that the Pediocin PA‐1–TLR4 complex is maintaining a structurally stable binding interface even without the support of full dynamic simulation, thereby contributing to the biochemical viability of its immunomodulatory interaction. The interaction fingerprint analysis showed over 70% similarity in hydrogen‐bond and hydrophobic contact patterns across the top‐ranked poses, confirming the structural robustness of the Pediocin PA‐1TLR4 complex. Mechanistic and Immunological Significance of the Docking Study

Spatial the molecular‐docking arm of this project was undertaken to answer a basic but important question that cannot be addressed by animal studies alone: How does a non‐absorbed, bacteriocin‐sized peptide ingested with feed trigger the cytokine cascades that we observe in vivo?

Physicochemical profiling of pediocin PA‐1 (MW ≈ 1 045 Da, TPSA ≈ 474 Å2, 16 H‐bond acceptors, 15 donors, Consensus logP = −2.3) positions the molecule in the ‘very soluble’ but poorly permeable category it violates three Lipinski and two Veber rules and is predicted to exhibit poor gastrointestinal absorption and zero blood–brain penetration. From a pharmacokinetic perspective, pediocin is therefore destined to remain in the aqueous lumen–mucus interface of the intestine rather than entering systemic circulation. That restriction immediately focuses mechanistic hypotheses on local pattern‐recognition receptors (PRRs) embedded in the gut epithelium rather than distal endocrine pathways or immune organs.

Docking simulations were therefore established to explore whether pediocin can interact with the leucine‐rich‐repeat (LRR) domain of chicken TLR4 a prominent PRR that surveil the intestinal milieu. The resultant spatial orientation places the peptide's aromatic/charged motifs over the GLU83–PHE38/MET40 hot‐spots that nucleate ligand‐dependent dimerisation, with a consensus S‐score of –106.4 kcal mol−1 and RMSD(refine) = 0.94 Å across > 2000 poses. These scores fulfil modern consensus cut‐offs for ‘high‐confidence’ protein–peptide identification and, importantly, map onto residues conserved in avian TLR4 activation.

As pediocin polarity precludes deep membrane transit, its MAMP‐mimicry within the extracellular LRR groove offers an economical connection to the in‐vivo results: interaction fingerprints predict MyD88‐dependent NF‐κB activation at an early point, exactly the pathway reflected by dose‐dependent up‐regulation of IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α and counter‐balancing IL‐10 we quantified in Sections 4.1–4.3. That is, the docking study translates a descriptive cytokine profile into a causally anchored mechanism local TLR4 ligation by a gut‐limited bacteriocin that explains why probiotic feeding augmentes both innate and vaccine‐induced adaptive immunity.

Limitations and Future Directions

Limitations: (i) The present docking is restricted to a rigid receptor; thus, side‐chain plasticity and long‐range electrostatics are likely to be under‐represented. (ii) The extracellular domain of TLR4 was only modelled; simulations of the full‐length protein containing the TIR domain may reveal allosteric effects.

Planned mitigation: We are doing a 100‐ns atomistic MD in explicit TIP3P water using CHARMM36m parameters, preceded by MM‐PBSA free‐energy profiling. Moreover, transcript‐level validation (RT‐qPCR of TLR4–MyD88 pathway genes) is being conducted to cross‐validate the in silico findings.

Evaluation of Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cell Viability Upon Exposure to P. Acidilactici and Its Derivatives

The biocompatibility of P. acidilactici and its derivatives was investigated through their effects on the viability of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Treatment groups included whole probiotic cells, various cytoplasmic extracts (Cyto 10, Cyto 20, Cyto 30, Cyto 40), and cell wall fractions (CW 50, CW 100, CW 200, CW 400). The experimental analysis was conducted over four time points (24, 48, 72 and 96 h), and cell viability was assessed using standard protocols. Across all treatment groups, PBMC viability remained consistently above 99.7%, with no significant reduction observed when compared to the control (p > 0.05). One‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc analysis, revealed no significant cytotoxic effect for any of the tested groups, suggesting that the probiotic derivatives are non‐toxic under the conditions tested. Effect sizes (partial η 2 < 0.01) confirmed the minimal impact on cell viability, demonstrating the excellent compatibility of these compounds with PBMCs.

Figure 2 illustrates the detailed results, with error bars representing standard deviation across replicates (n = 9 per condition), reflecting minimal variability and high reproducibility. In addition, 95% confidence intervals were reported to ensure robust conclusions regarding the lack of cytotoxicity. These findings indicate that the evaluated probiotic and its components do not induce adverse effects on PBMCs, providing preliminary evidence of their safety for further preclinical studies. This data supports the potential use of P. acidilactici and its derivatives in therapeutic applications, pending in vivo evaluations that include chronic exposure and assessment of immune modulation capabilities.

Cytokine Production by P. Acidilactici and Its Derivatives: Immunomodulatory Insights

The immunomodulatory potential of P. acidilactici and its derived fractions was evaluated by measuring key cytokines IL‐1, IL‐6, IL‐10 and TNF‐α across multiple treatment groups, including whole probiotic cells, cytoplasmic extracts (Cyto 10, 20, 30, 40) and cell wall fractions (CW 50, 100, 200, 400). The results, as depicted in Figure 3, demonstrate distinct cytokine induction patterns, suggesting diverse immunomodulatory effects specific to each fraction.

Effect of PediGuard probiotic and other compounds on cytokine levels.

Interleukin‐1 (IL‐1) and Interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) Responses

Cytoplasmic extracts, especially Cyto 20 and Cyto 40, significantly increased IL‐1 and IL‐6 levels compared to controls (p < 0.05), indicating robust pro‐inflammatory activation. These extracts appear to act as potent stimulators of innate immune pathways, enhancing inflammatory signalling and potentially preparing the immune system for pathogen defence. The absence of data on temporal dynamics, however, limits our understanding of the duration of these responses, which is critical for clinical application. Notably, larger error margins observed for IL‐1 levels in Cyto 40‐treated samples suggest some variability; future studies with increased replicates may help confirm these preliminary findings.

Dual Role in Immune Modulation: Interleukin‐10 (IL‐10)

In addition to upregulating pro‐inflammatory markers, Cyto 40 significantly elevated IL‐10 (p < 0.05), a cytokine central to immune regulation. This dual effect suggests that Cyto 40 may promote a balanced immune response by modulating both activation and suppression pathways a feature potentially advantageous for managing inflammation in therapeutic applications. IL‐10 production was also increased by whole probiotic cells and CW 100, albeit to a lesser degree, indicating that these treatments may support a tempered immune response suitable for long‐term health maintenance.

Tumour Necrosis Factor‐Alpha (TNF‐α) as a Pro‐Inflammatory Indicator

TNF‐α levels were significantly elevated in groups treated with Cyto 20 and Cyto 30 (p < 0.05), reinforcing their pro‐inflammatory profile. In contrast, whole probiotic cells and high‐concentration cell wall fractions (CW 200 and CW 400) elicited significantly lower TNF‐α induction, suggesting these intact or more complex fractions might mitigate excessive inflammation, possibly through regulatory components such as cell wall polysaccharides. This selective pro‐inflammatory response implies that cytoplasmic extracts may be more suitable for applications requiring vigorous immune activation, while whole cells or cell wall fractions might be better suited for contexts needing controlled inflammation without excessive immune stimulation.

Figure 3 summarises these findings, with significant changes marked by asterisks (p < 0.05) and ‘ns’ denoting non‐significance, providing a clear comparative analysis of each component's immunomodulatory capacity.

Enhancement of Antibody Response to Influenza Vaccination

The synergistic effects of PediGuard in combination with an influenza vaccine were evaluated by measuring the antibody titres at multiple time points post‐vaccination. As shown in Table 3, the combination of PediGuard with the vaccine led to significantly higher antibody titres at Days 7, 14 and 21 compared to the vaccine‐only group (p < 0.05). The highest titres were observed in the group receiving both the probiotic and the vaccine, indicating a synergistic enhancement of the adaptive immune response.

Intestinal pH and Microbial Populations

Discussion

This study underscores the profound immunomodulatory, growth‐promoting and gut health‐enhancing effects of P. acidilactici (PediGuard) supplementation in broiler chickens. The findings contribute significantly to the growing body of literature supporting LAB as effective probiotics for enhancing animal health. Specifically, P. acidilactici was shown to bolster immune responses, improve gut morphology and optimise nutrient absorption, which in turn enhances growth performance in poultry (Peralta‐Sánchez et al. 2019; Liao and Nyachoti 2017; Gadde et al. 2017).

Immunomodulatory Effects and Synergy With Vaccination

The upregulation of both pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐1, IL‐6, TNF‐α) and the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 indicates the dual immunomodulatory capabilities of P. acidilactici. This suggests a finely tuned immune response, balancing activation with regulation a finding consistent with the work of Gharib Naseri et al. (2012), who showed that P. acidilactici supports immune homeostasis and may aid in infection management. Importantly, this probiotic's immunomodulatory properties make it particularly effective in scenarios requiring controlled immune activation, such as during viral infections or in conjunction with vaccines. The observed increase in antibody titres when P. acidilactici was combined with influenza vaccination further suggests a synergistic effect that may improve vaccine efficacy. This is likely due to enhanced antigen presentation and T‐cell activation, as reported in studies on similar LAB strains. These findings highlight the probiotic's potential as a complementary tool in poultry vaccination programs (Kogut and Arsenault 2016; Chaucheyras‐Durand and Durand 2010; Zhang et al. 2012)

Growth Performance and Gut Morphology

Improvements in growth metrics including weight gain, specific growth rate and FCR were observed following P. acidilactici supplementation. These results can be attributed to the enhanced gut morphology observed in histological analyses. Specifically, increases in VH and CD in the ileum were noted, indicating an expansion of the absorptive surface area. Probiotics inhibit the growth of harmful microorganisms in the gut, leading to more nutrients being available to the host. In addition, they affect gut tissue by increasing villus length, reducing epithelial thickness and decreasing cell turnover in the intestinal epithelium.

Among the experimental treatments, the highest VH and CD were observed in chicks receiving probiotics, suggesting that stimulation of intestinal epithelial cell proliferation is a key mechanism of action for probiotics containing lactobacilli. These probiotics stimulate the proliferation of intestinal epithelial cells by increasing levels of short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyrate, which play a critical role in maintaining the intestinal barrier. The positive effects of probiotics on increasing intestinal VH and activating villus function in chicks receiving diets supplemented with probiotics have been well‐documented. Greater VH is an indicator of active intestinal villus function, and with increased VH, the digestion and absorption of nutrients also increase due to the expanded absorptive surface area, enhanced expression of brush border enzymes and upregulation of nutrient transport systems (Bajagai et al. 2016; Mountzouris et al. 2007; Applegate et al. 2010).

Our findings are consistent with studies by Mountzouris et al. (2007), who reported that P. acidilactici significantly increased VH in the duodenum and ileum. Similarly, Awad et al. (2009) demonstrated that probiotic use significantly increased the ratio of VH to CD in the duodenum and ileum. It appears that using P. acidilactici yields better results in increasing daily weight gain, improving FCRs, enhancing nutrient digestibility, increasing intestinal VH and boosting the population of lactobacilli in the ileum of broiler chickens (Groups B and C).

Mechanisms Underlying Morphological Improvements

P. acidilactici re‐models the intestinal microbiota by means of a cascade of several layers that eventually improves the gut absorptive area. First, its lactic‐acid mediated acidification lowers luminal pH to create an inhibitory environment for enteric pathogens and simultaneously improves mineral solubilisation and brush‐border enzymatic activity. An acidic lumen also triggers the production of SCFA production namely butyrate whose energy sustains enterocyte expansion and tight junctions and hence barrier integrity.

Concurrently with its growth‐enhancing action, P. acidilactici stimulates gastric and pancreatic enzyme release and enhances mucin secretion, accelerating macromolecule hydrolysis and chyme transit. The resulting microenvironment of nutrients coupled with competitive inhibition of pathogens reduces inflammation cost and re‐directs metabolic energy to growth.

At the tissue level, these biochemical changes induce crypt stem‐cell activation and push–pull villus motions to develop taller villi and deeper crypts with increased functional surface area for the small intestine. The result is a reinforcing loop: improved epithelial structure enhances greater nutrient uptake, which enhances further mucosal maturation and immune function. Collectively, this mechanistic synergy explains the significant gains in VH, CD and feed conversion that were observed in this present study and demonstrates the probiotic's capacity for sustaining long‐term gut health in poultry (Markowiak and Śliżewska 2018; Manafi et al. 2016; Gharib Naseri et al. 2012).

Implications for the Poultry Industry

The implications of this study for the poultry industry are far‐reaching. The use of P. acidilactici as a probiotic could serve as a viable alternative to antibiotics in poultry production. By enhancing immune function, growth performance and gut health, this probiotic offers a sustainable solution to maintaining productivity without contributing to the global issue of antibiotic resistance. Moreover, the probiotic's ability to enhance vaccine efficacy suggests it could be an invaluable adjunct to poultry vaccination programs, potentially improving both health outcomes and productivity. As consumers increasingly demand antibiotic‐free poultry, incorporating probiotics like P. acidilactici into poultry farming practices may help meet this demand while ensuring sustainable production practices (Ricke 2018; Pan and Yu 2014; Oakley et al. 2014).

Limitations and Future Directions

Despite the positive findings of this trial, there are several limitations which render them noteworthy. To begin with, the trial did not have a live viral challenge model that would have represented a more absolute test of the immunoprotective potential of the probiotic versus infectious stress. Secondly, the trial was conducted only in the grow‐out phase and did not consider long‐term impacts or performance in breeder flocks. Follow‐up research will be necessary to extend the probiotic administration duration and examine its effect throughout extended production periods. In order to better clarify the longevity and physiologic range of its advantage.

Moreover, while there are enhancements in mineral retention and bone mineralisation, these molecular mechanisms are poorly characterised. Advanced omics technology transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics are recommended in future studies for delineating the targeted signalling networks and affected gene networks by P. acidilactici. These results can inform key regulators of mineral absorption, bone health and immunity resilience and provide a mechanistic foundation for probiotic action.

Finally, to establish translational efficacy, these findings must be confirmed in commercial production environments. Full‐scale field trials on real poultry farms would enable practical efficacy, economic and consistency of effects determination. Such studies are critical to solidifying P. acidilactici as an economically feasible, scalable and non‐antibiotic solution for enhancing poultry health and productivity in modern farming systems.

Conclusion

Lastly, the current work provides compelling evidence that P. acidilactici supplementation improves immune response, growth performance and intestinal morphology of broiler chickens. Mutual modulation by the probiotic of pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory cytokines enhances an even balance of immune profile, while a mix with influenza vaccine significantly enhances antibody responses. These physiological benefits were matched by anatomical enhancements in the structure of the gut, thus also facilitating improved nutrient uptake and feed conversion. Combined, these findings position P. acidilactici as a science‐based, antibiotic‐free means to improve poultry well‐being and productivity. Further research under commercial field conditions will be required to ascertain long‐term balance, economic value and scalability of such gains.
