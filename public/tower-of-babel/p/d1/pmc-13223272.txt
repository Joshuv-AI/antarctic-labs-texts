Motor control training versus soft tissue therapy in the treatment of upper crossed syndrome: a randomized controlled pilot trial
Authors: Aleksandra Adamik, Edyta Mikołajczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Upper crossed syndrome (UCS) is a prevalent postural pattern associated with myofascial pain and limited cervical mobility in sedentary adults. Evidence supports both motor control training (MCT) and soft-tissue therapy (STT), but their comparative effects remain uncertain. This exploratory randomized controlled pilot trial examined and compared the effects of MCT and STT on pain, cervical range of motion (ROM), and head posture in women with UCS. Thirty women aged 37–53 years with clinically identified UCS were randomly allocated to MCT (G1) or STT (G2) (n = 15 each). Interventions lasted 8 weeks. G1 performed supervised low-load MCT three times weekly (45 min/session) targeting deep cervical flexors, scapular stabilizers, and postural control. G2 received once-weekly 30-min STT (myofascial release, post-isometric relaxation, muscle energy techniques, and trigger-point techniques) targeting relevant cervical-thoracic and scapular muscles. The primary outcome was pain intensity (visual analogue scale, VAS). Secondary outcomes were Laitinen pain scale score, cervical ROM (CROM device) in habitual and corrected head positions, and head posture angles in standing and sitting. Outcomes were assessed pre- and postintervention. Analyses used appropriate parametric/non-parametric tests (α = 0.05) with effect sizes (Cohen’s d or r) and 95% confidence intervals. Both groups showed clinically meaningful pain reduction. The VAS score decreased from 3.26 ± 1.39 to 1.67 ± 1.23 in G1 (p < 0.001; d = 1.19, 95% CI 0.47–1.85) and from 3.33 ± 1.68 to 1.47 ± 0.91 in G2 (p < 0.001; d = 1.36, 95% CI 0.61–2.01). Laitinen scores also improved (G1: from 4.80 ± 1.66 to 2.93 ± 1.44, p < 0.001; d = 1.17, 95% CI 0.45–1.83; G2: from 4.47 ± 2.03 to3.00 ± 1.46, p < 0.001; d = 0.80, 95% CI 0.15–1.45). The between-group comparisons suggested larger improvements in several ROM and posture measures in G1, particularly for lateral flexion and rotation in habitual and corrected positions (e.g., corrected right lateral flexion change: 9.07° ± 5.08 vs 3.20° ± 4.28; p < 0.001). Postintervention between-group differences were observed for selected outcomes (e.g., standing retraction angle, habitual extension, and corrected extension and rotation; p ≤ 0.01). Eight weeks of both MCT and STT were associated with reduced pain in women with UCS. Pain improvements were comparable between groups, whereas MCT was associated with larger improvements in selected ROM and head-posture measures. These findings provide preliminary comparative data to inform adequately powered trials with balanced intervention dose and longer follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46716-2.

Introduction

Upper crossed syndrome (UCS) is one of the most common postural disorders and is characterized by an imbalance within the myofascial system of the shoulder girdle and cervical spine. It involves overactivation (increased tension) of the pectoralis major and minor, upper trapezius (descending), levator scapulae, sternocleidomastoid, and suboccipital muscles. Conversely, inhibition (reduced tension) occurs in the rhomboid muscles, serratus anterior, and lower trapezius (ascending part)1,2. The main contributing factor to the development of upper crossed syndrome is the prolonged maintenance of incorrect posture3. The muscle imbalance contributes to postural abnormalities and altered biomechanics of the cervicothoracic and shoulder-girdle regions. The disruption of normal biomechanics between successive structures may contribute to overload-related degenerative changes over time.4.

Chronic pain in the cervical and shoulder-girdle region is often associated with occupational factors, particularly prolonged sitting and computer-based work5. Disturbances within the musculoskeletal and myofascial systems can lead to numerous movement limitations, accompanied by a range of nonspecific symptoms that are often mistaken for other disorders, which poses a clinical challenge6.

The primary characteristic of myofascial pain is the presence of a tender area or a taut band within the skeletal muscles, often containing pain-sensitive spots known as trigger points. This condition is frequently accompanied by a restricted range of motion in the affected segment and muscle weakness7. Various conservative approaches are used, including exercise-based rehabilitation and soft-tissue manual techniques8. The most commonly applied methods include ischemic compression of trigger points, myofascial release, and postisometric relaxation. These techniques aim to improve tissue elasticity and interlayer glide within soft tissues and reduce pain symptoms9.

Motor control training is a key component of therapy for patients with postural disorders and myofascial pain in the cervical spine and shoulder girdle region10. Its primary goal is to restore proper activation, coordination, and synchronization of the muscles stabilizing the cervicothoracic spine and the scapulae11. Motor control exercises involve the training of conscious positioning of the head, neck, and shoulder girdle, accompanied by the activation of the deep cervical flexors, the serratus anterior, and the lower fibers of the trapezius muscle12. Proper activation of these structures leads to improved local stability, a reduction in compensatory movements, and decreased tension in superficial muscles13.

Despite the growing number of studies examining the effectiveness of manual soft tissue therapy and motor control training, there is still a lack of clear comparative evidence indicating which of these interventions provides more comprehensive therapeutic outcomes in terms of pain reduction and improvement in movement function.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore and compare the effects of motor control training and soft tissue therapy on pain intensity and the range of motion of the cervical spine in women with upper crossed syndrome in an exploratory randomized controlled pilot trial.

Subjects and methods

The study included 30 women aged between 35 and 55 years who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The participants were randomly assigned to two equal groups. Group 1 (G1) underwent an 8-week low-load motor control training program, whereas Group 2 (G2) received soft tissue therapy.

The inclusion criteria for the study were the presence of upper crossed syndrome (UCS), confirmed through visual postural assessment14, age between 35 and 55 years, female sex, the presence of chronic (over 3 months) cervical spine pain based on the participant’s subjective assessment, and good general health, including no injuries or surgical interventions in the cervical spine region within the last 10 years. Participants with medical conditions that could affect the outcomes or limit safe participation in exercise were excluded, including neurological diseases (peripheral neuropathies, multiple sclerosis), rheumatic diseases (rheumatoid arthritis), metabolic disorders (diabetes), and cardiovascular or respiratory conditions. Absence of regular structured physical activity was required, defined as participation in organized sports activities or individual moderate-to-vigorous exercise at least twice a week during the previous three months. Written informed consent for participation in the study was obtained.

The exclusion criteria were current acute conditions or a history of surgical interventions within the cervical spine, inability to attend the intervention sessions regularly, defined as expected attendance < 80% of the planned sessions, participation in other forms of organized or individual physical activity, comorbid conditions that could directly affect upper-body outcomes or prevent participation in the planned therapeutic program, and individually assessed contraindications to soft tissue therapy or physical exercise.

The participants were informed about the planned course of measurements and the type of therapy and subsequently signed informed consent for participation in the project. All examinations of the women included in the study were conducted twice: before and after eight weeks of therapy and between October and December 2023.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee at the Regional Medical Chamber in Kraków (No. 169/KBL/OIL/2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Visual analogue scale (VAS)

Pain intensity was assessed using a 10-cm visual analogue scale (VAS), ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain)15.

Laitinen pain scale

The Laitinen Pain Scale uses a scoring system ranging from 0 to 4, where 0 indicates no pain and 4 represents the maximum severity of the problem. The scale allows for the assessment of four indicators: pain intensity, pain frequency, frequency of analgesic use, and restriction of physical activity16 The participants were instructed on how to assess the presence and severity of pain symptoms in the cervical and thoracic spine regions.

CROM

In accordance with the study methodology17, measurements of head position and range of motion of the cervical spine were performed via a Cervical Range of Motion (CROM) device (Baseline FS625). The habitual position was defined as the participant’s naturally adopted posture of the cervical spine and shoulder girdle. The corrected position was achieved by placing a lumbar roll at the thoracolumbar junction to standardize an upright trunk posture.

Rehabilitation program

Motor control training

The women underwent an 8-week therapy program in the form of motor control training. The training protocol consisted of three distinct sections, including motor control and stabilization exercises, muscle strengthening, and stretching exercises. These are low-load motor control (LMC) exercises aimed at activating stabilizing muscles with minimal tension while avoiding excessive engagement of superficial muscles. The exercises were designed to restore proper movement patterns, postural control, and neuromuscular coordination, in accordance with the principles of motor control training. All tasks were performed in a neutral spinal position, with internal loading set at approximately 30% of maximal voluntary contraction, with a focus on precision, smoothness of movement, and conscious control of muscle activation18,19. Participants performed the prescribed exercises three times per week for 45 min under the supervision of a qualified physiotherapist. Incorrect exercise techniques were corrected in real time, which minimized the risk of potential pain or discomfort. The detailed training procedure is presented in Supplement 1.

Soft tissue therapy

The therapy cycle consisted of eight sessions, conducted once per week, each performed by the same therapist. During each 30-min session, the following areas were treated: the erector spinae muscles in the cervical and thoracic spine regions; the interscapular muscles; the latissimus dorsi; the trapezius; the pectoralis major; the suboccipital muscle group; the sternocleidomastoid; the scalene muscles; and the diaphragm.

In accordance with generally accepted therapeutic principles, the treatment incorporated myofascial release techniques, post-isometric relaxation, the muscle energy technique (MET), and manual therapy of myofascial trigger points. The therapy was performed first on the dominant side, followed by the nondominant side of the body. The detailed therapeutic procedure is described in Supplement 1 ref.20–22.

Participants were instructed to refrain from receiving any additional physiotherapy, manual therapy, or structured exercise programs during the 8-week study period to avoid potential confounding effects.

Statistical analysis methods

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistica 13.3 (StatSoft, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median (Me), and minimum–maximum (Min–Max), as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied due to the exploratory nature of this pilot study; therefore, findings for secondary outcomes should be interpreted cautiously.

The primary endpoint was the change in pain intensity assessed using the visual analogue scale (VAS). Secondary endpoints included the Laitinen Pain Scale, cervical range of motion (ROM) in habitual and corrected positions, and head alignment angles in standing and sitting positions.

Normality of distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and inspection of residuals. To appropriately account for the repeated-measures structure (two time points: baseline and postintervention) and within-participant correlation, the primary between-group comparison over time was conducted using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) with fixed effects for group (motor control training vs soft tissue therapy), time (baseline vs postintervention), and the group × time interaction, and a random intercept for participant. The group × time interaction term was used to determine whether changes over time differed between groups. This LMM interaction term constituted the prespecified primary between-group analysis for the pain outcomes.

For within-group comparisons (baseline vs postintervention), paired-samples t tests were used for normally distributed outcomes, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for non-normally distributed outcomes. For between-group comparisons of change scores (Δ = post- baseline), independent-samples t tests were used for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Effect sizes were calculated as Cohen’s d for t tests and r for nonparametric tests, each with 95% confidence intervals where applicable.

This study was designed as an exploratory randomized controlled trial and a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed; therefore, all secondary outcome analyses should be interpreted as exploratory. Random assignment was performed using a computer-generated random number sequence (RAND() function in Microsoft Excel). Allocation concealment was not implemented due to the pilot nature of the study. Outcome assessment and data entry were conducted by an assessor blinded to group allocation.

Trial registration

This randomized controlled trial was not prospectively registered because it was conducted as an exploratory academic pilot study. The absence of prospective registration is acknowledged as a limitation. No protocol changes were made after study commencement. Adverse events were monitored throughout the intervention period, and participants were instructed to report any discomfort or unexpected symptoms immediately. The study protocol and statistical analysis plan are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Pain intensity

A statistically significant reduction in pain intensity was observed in both groups (p < 0.001).

In the motor control training group (G1), the mean VAS score decreased from 3.26 ± 1.39 to 1.67 ± 1.23 (p < 0.001), whereas in the soft tissue therapy group (G2), it decreased from 3.33 ± 1.68 to 1.47 ± 0.91 (p < 0.001). Effect sizes were large within both groups, with Cohen’s d = 1.19 [95% CI: 0.47–1.85] in G1 and d = 1.36 [95% CI: 0.61–2.01] in G2, indicating a clinically meaningful reduction in pain levels.

Similar trends were observed for the Laitinen scale, where the mean score decreased from 4.80 ± 1.66 to 2.93 ± 1.44 in G1 (p < 0.001) and from 4.47 ± 2.03 to 3.00 ± 1.46 in G2 (p < 0.001). These changes also demonstrated large effect sizes (G1: d = 1.17 [95% CI: 0.45–1.83]; G2: d = 0.80 [95% CI: 0.15–1.45]).

In the linear mixed-effects model, the group × time interaction was not significant for VAS or the Laitinen Pain Scale (both p > 0.05), indicating that pain reduction over time did not differ between groups. The intergroup comparison of changes between baseline and postintervention measurements, performed via the independent samples t test (Table 6), revealed statistically significant differences between the motor control training group (Group 1) and the soft tissue therapy group (Group 2) in selected parameters of head alignment and mobility.

A statistically significant difference (p = 0.01) was observed in the habitual seated head position, where participants in Group 1 demonstrated greater improvement (0.17° ± 1.35) than did those in Group 2, where the mean difference was -2.30° ± 2.04. Similarly, a significant intergroup difference (p < 0.001) was found for right lateral flexion of the head in the corrected position; the mean improvement in Group 1 was 9.07° ± 5.08, whereas in Group 2, it was 3.20° ± 4.28.

For all other analysed parameters, including protraction in the standing position, retraction in the sitting position, and extension and rotation ranges in both the habitual and corrected positions, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups (p > 0.05).

Analysis of differences between baseline and postintervention measurements via the Mann‒Whitney U test

The analysis of differences between baseline and postintervention measurements performed with the Mann‒Whitney U test (Table 7) revealed statistically significant intergroup differences in selected parameters of head alignment and cervical mobility.

A significant difference was observed in the habitual standing head position (p = 0.03), with participants in Group 1 showing greater improvement than those in Group 2 did. For other parameters in the standing position, head retraction (p = 0.74) and sitting protraction (p = 0.84), no significant intergroup differences were found.

Regarding the cervical range of motion in the habitual position, significant intergroup differences were noted for lateral flexion, both on the left side (p < 0.001) and right side (p < 0.001), where Group 1 achieved greater range increases (mean gains of 10.67° ± 4.67 and 10.20° ± 4.52) than Group 2 did (4.80° ± 4.31 and 4.20° ± 4.48, respectively). No significant between-group differences were found for head flexion in the habitual position (p = 0.09) or for left rotation (p = 0.71).

In the corrected position, significantly greater improvements in Group 1 were recorded for left lateral flexion (p < 0.001), left rotation (p < 0.001), and right rotation (p < 0.001) than in Group 2. Differences in flexion in the corrected position (p = 0.07) did not reach statistical significance.

No statistically significant between-group differences were observed at baseline for the primary pain outcomes (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This exploratory pilot trial compared motor control training and soft tissue therapy in women with upper crossed syndrome. Both interventions reduced pain, and the magnitude of pain improvement was similar between groups. In contrast, between-group comparisons of change scores suggested larger improvements in selected cervical ROM and head-alignment measures in the motor control training group, particularly for lateral flexion and rotation outcomes. Because the study was small and secondary analyses were exploratory, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

A meta-analysis by Meng et al.23. reported an association between sitting time and the risk of neck pain, highlighting the need for preventive and therapeutic strategies in sedentary populations. Accordingly, the present study was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of motor control training and soft tissue therapy in reducing pain and improving the range of motion of the cervical spine in women diagnosed with upper crossed syndrome with pain as the primary outcome. The results of this study demonstrated a positive effect of both motor control training and soft tissue therapy on the level of pain in the participants. Both interventions reduced pain, and the magnitude of improvement was comparable between groups (no significant group × time interaction for VAS or Laitinen)24. The use of the CROM device in the present study ensured high repeatability of individual measurements, with reported reliability values ranging from 0.89–0.98 and a minimal detectable change (MDC) of approximately 3.6–6.5°25 In the present study, several improvements exceeded these MDC thresholds, suggesting that the observed changes were unlikely to be attributable to measurement error. Improved cervical mobility and postural control may facilitate daily activities requiring sustained head and neck positioning, such as computer-based work. The improvement in range of motion and postural control following motor control training may result not only from biomechanical changes but also from central adaptations. Previous studies suggest that training the deep cervical flexors may be accompanied by changes in neuromuscular control at central levels as confirmed by Falla et al.26. In the case of soft tissue therapy, the effects may arise from afferent modulation through improved fascial glide, a reduction in muscle tension, and the activation of type III mechanoreceptors (proprioception). Therefore, the combination of both approaches may exert a synergistic neuromechanical effect. Between-group comparisons suggested larger improvements in selected head-alignment and ROM outcomes in the motor control training group; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory pilot design and multiple secondary comparisons. The comparable pain reduction in both groups may reflect shared nonspecific effects (e.g., therapist contact, symptom monitoring, and reduced nociceptive sensitivity) as well as different, but convergent-mechanisms of analgesia. In contrast, the larger improvements observed in selected ROM and posture measures after motor control training may be explained by task-specific neuromuscular retraining, improved activation and endurance of deep cervical flexors and scapular stabilizers, and improved ability to voluntarily achieve and maintain a corrected alignment. Soft tissue therapy may primarily influence pain and short-term mobility through changes in tissue tone and afferent input, which may not translate to sustained postural control to the same extent. The differences observed between the habitual and corrected postures confirm the importance of conscious postural reeducation. As demonstrated by Titcomb et al.27 and Lee et al.28, interventions aimed at correcting forward head posture (FHP) result in significant changes in the craniovertebral angle (CVA), as well as improvements in range of motion and muscle bioelectrical activity. This suggests that achieving and maintaining a corrected posture may represent an important step in postural modification and could be considered in the later phase of rehabilitation. Our findings align with Bernal-Utrera et al.29, who reported effectiveness of both approaches and, in some studies, stronger short-term analgesic effects of manual therapy; in our trial, pain reductions were comparable between groups. The efficacy of motor control exercises targeting deep cervical flexors (DCFs) is well documented. In a meta-analysis, Tsiringakis et al.30 reported that motor control training of deep cervical flexor muscles is an effective approach for reducing pain and improving quality of life in individuals with neck pain. Conversely, soft tissue therapy (STT) provides strong evidence for short-term pain relief and improved range of motion (ROM) in individuals with neck discomfort. Randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews have shown that manual techniques targeting soft tissues significantly contribute to pain reduction and increased cervical mobility31. Kang et al.32 reported similar results, observing that participants performing exercise programs achieved greater improvements in cervical ROM and upper trapezius muscle relaxation. On the other hand, Demir et al.33 reported that manual therapy produced faster and more pronounced effects, suggesting that manual treatment may be beneficial as an initial phase to release tissue tension and enhance the effectiveness of subsequent exercise interventions. Soft tissue interventions often produce rapid analgesic effects through peripheral mechanisms, such as viscoelastic tissue modulation and improved fascial glide, as well as central mechanisms, including nociceptive modulation31. The present study demonstrated a strong short-term effect of both methods on pain reduction and range of motion improvement. Several ROM gains exceeded published MDC thresholds, suggesting potential functional relevance. These findings align with those of previous studies, where Titcomb et al.27 confirmed the significant impact of rehabilitation programs on the craniovertebral angle (CVA), indicating that correcting head posture leads to favourable habitual alignment. Among women undergoing motor control training, this postural correction likely reflects better coordination of deep cervical flexors and enhanced segmental stabilization. The pattern of results may suggest that neuromuscular retraining contributes to improvements in posture-related outcomes; however, this interpretation should be considered preliminary due to the pilot design and unequal intervention dose which is consistent with the findings of Kang et al.27. Recent reviews on myofascial release techniques further confirm that soft tissue therapy effectively improves ROM and pain levels, which is in line with the results of the present study. Additionally, evidence indicates that immediate improvements in movement range can occur after interventions targeting scapular alignment, suggesting the greater therapeutic potential of a combined approach that integrates soft tissue therapy and motor control training rather than applying either method alone34 Therefore, continuing research incorporating both therapeutic modalities within a single group seems reasonable. The corrected position observed in the current study reflects the patient’s functional potential to achieve specific ROMs; when segmental alignment is optimized, notable improvements are observed even after a short re-education process. However, habitual posture correction requires motor learning and task-specific exposure, which is more time-consuming but entirely achievable35.

The improvements in cervical mobility, particularly in the corrected position, may have practical clinical relevance, as they suggest an increased capacity to achieve more optimal cervical alignment and movement. Overall, both motor control training and soft tissue therapy were associated with reduced pain and improved cervical mobility in women with upper crossed syndrome. The pattern of results further suggests that interventions targeting motor control and segmental stabilization may be particularly useful for improving posture-related mobility outcomes in sedentary individuals. Structured, individualized exercise programs combined with postural education may help address movement deficits and support functional performance. This interpretation is consistent with Blomgren et al.36, who reported that motor control exercises can improve postural function and balance in individuals with chronic neck pain. However, because outcomes were assessed only immediately after the intervention, the durability of these improvements remains unknown, and maintaining benefits likely requires continued practice and reinforcement of corrected movement patterns in daily activities.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small and no a priori power calculation was performed, which limits statistical power and the precision of effect estimates. Second, the study was designed as an exploratory pilot trial, and secondary analyses should therefore be interpreted with caution. Third, the intervention dose was not matched between groups, which may have influenced between-group comparisons through nonspecific effects such as training volume or therapist contact. Fourth, the trial was not prospectively registered. Finally, outcomes were assessed only immediately after the intervention, and no long-term follow-up was conducted.

Conclusion

Both soft tissue therapy and motor control training improved pain and cervical spine function in women with upper crossed syndrome. While pain reduction did not differ between groups, motor control training produced larger improvements in selected head-alignment and cervical mobility outcomes, suggesting possible advantages for postural control, although these findings should be considered preliminary due to the pilot design and unequal intervention dose. Future trials with larger samples and follow-up are needed to determine the durability of these effects. Because the study was not prospectively registered, the analyses should be interpreted as exploratory.
