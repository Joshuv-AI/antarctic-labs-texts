Relative muscle mass is preserved during weight loss in rheumatoid arthritis: a post-hoc RCT analysis
Authors: David A Kellner, Joshua F Baker, Sitaram Vangala, David A Elashoff, Jin Long, Veena K Ranganath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesObesity and sarcopenia commonly coexist in RA, compounding metabolic risk and disability. Intentional weight loss may improve outcomes but raises concern for accelerated muscle loss. We conducted a post-hoc analysis of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) of a hypocaloric, high-protein diet in adults with RA and obesity to determine whether diet-induced weight loss was associated with disproportionate reductions in muscle mass.MethodsAdults with RA and obesity were randomized to a 12-week hypocaloric, high-protein diet or to control dietary advice (NCT02881307). DXA was performed at baseline and 12 weeks. Fat mass index (FMI), appendicular lean mass index (ALMI) and adiposity-adjusted ALMI (ALMIFMI) were derived and standardized to Z-scores using national reference data. Between-group differences in change were compared using t-tests, with FDR-adjusted P values reported for all analyses.ResultsParticipants in the dietary intervention achieved significant reductions in total fat mass (mean change in FMI Z-score −0.35 vs 0.04; P = 0.003). Changes in ALMI Z-score (−0.37 vs −0.43; P = 0.92) and ALMIFMI Z-score (−0.15 vs −0.55; P = 0.92) did not differ significantly between groups. In the intervention group there was minimal decline in ALMFMI, suggesting parallel reductions in lean and fat tissue.ConclusionIn adults with RA and obesity, diet-induced weight loss reduced adiposity without evidence of disproportionate muscle loss. These findings support the safety of structured, protein-adequate weight-loss interventions in this population.

Introduction

Obesity is a major comorbidity in RA, affecting over 30% of patients in North America and contributing to increased disease activity, poorer treatment response and worse long-term outcomes [1]. Sarcopenia, the loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, is also highly prevalent in RA, affecting >25% of patients and associated with higher risks of falls, fractures and disability [2]. The coexistence of excess adiposity and reduced muscle mass, termed sarcopenic obesity, is more common in RA than in the general population, reflecting the dual burden of altered body composition in this disease [3]. Given these intersecting risks, intentional weight loss, though beneficial for obesity-related outcomes, raises concern that it may inadvertently accelerate RA-associated sarcopenia by worsening muscle loss.

Whether intentional weight loss in RA results in adverse changes to muscle mass remains uncertain, despite strong rationale for weight reduction to improve cardiometabolic health and symptoms. To better understand this, we conducted a post-hoc analysis of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) of a hypocaloric, high-protein diet in adults with RA and obesity, examining whether diet-induced weight loss was accompanied by disproportionate skeletal muscle loss in this at-risk population [4].

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a post-hoc analysis of a 12-week RCT (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02881307) that evaluated the effects of a hypocaloric, high-protein diet in adults with RA and obesity [4]. Participants met the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for RA and had a BMI ≥30 kg/m2. The parent trial randomized participants to a caloric-restricted, protein-adequate dietary intervention or to a control group receiving general dietary guidance. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the institutional review board at the University of California, Los Angeles (16-000881-CR-00004).

Assessments

DXA was performed at baseline and 12 weeks to obtain whole-body and regional measures of lean and fat mass. From these data, appendicular lean mass index (ALMI) and fat mass index (FMI) were calculated as appendicular lean mass (kg) or fat mass (kg) divided by height squared (m2), respectively. Regional FMI values for the arms, legs, trunk, android and gynoid compartments were calculated as fat mass of each region (kg) divided by height squared (m2). An adiposity-adjusted ALMI (ALMIFMI) was also computed to account for the expected relationship between lean and fat mass. All three indices were standardized to Z-scores using published reference equations from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Conventional indices of sarcopenia, such as DXA-derived ALMI, are limited in individuals with obesity because they do not account for the close positive correlation between lean and fat mass. To address this limitation, the adiposity-adjusted ALMI (ALMIFMI) was developed using NHANES reference data [5]. This approach adjusts for the expected relationship between lean and fat mass across multiple demographic features within a reference population. By quantifying muscle relative to fat mass rather than relying on absolute thresholds, ALMIFMI provides a measure of whether muscle mass is appropriate for a given degree of adiposity. In RA cohorts, ALMIFMI has demonstrated stronger associations with physical function and disability than unadjusted indices, making it particularly well suited for evaluating body-composition changes during intentional weight loss [6].

Definitions of sarcopenia, obesity and sarcopenic obesity were based on criteria from Baker et al. [7] Sarcopenia was defined as an ALMIFMI Z-score ≤−1. Obesity was defined using FMI thresholds of ≥13 for women and ≥9 for men. Sarcopenic obesity was defined as the presence of both sarcopenia and obesity according to these criteria.

Statistical analysis

Changes in ALMI, FMI and ALMIFMI Z-scores were calculated as the difference between baseline and 12-week values. Between-group differences in change were assessed using independent-samples t-tests, with two-sided P values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. As a sensitivity analysis, a linear mixed model was used to compare between-group differences in change for each outcome, incorporating all participants with DXA data at either timepoint under a missing at random assumption. Regional changes in FMI (arms, legs, trunk, android and gynoid compartments) were also evaluated to explore whether fat loss differed by body region. To account for multiple comparisons, all P values were adjusted for multiplicity using the Benjamini-Hochberg step-up procedure for false discovery rate control and FDR-adjusted P values are reported throughout. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Parent trial outcomes have been reported previously; key findings relevant to this analysis are summarized here for context. In the 12-week RCT of 40 patients (20 per group), those in the dietary intervention lost an average of 9.5 kg compared with 0.5 kg in controls (P < 0.001), and 72% vs 11% achieved ≥5% weight loss. BMI and total body-fat percentage decreased significantly in the intervention group, accompanied by improvements in patient-reported outcomes and adipokine profiles. DXA measurements at baseline were available for 19 intervention and 15 control patients; at 12 weeks, measurements were available for 16 intervention and 13 control patients. Complete data at both timepoints were available for 16 intervention and 12 control participants.

In the post-hoc analysis of patients with complete DXA data at baseline and 12 weeks, change in FMI Z-score was −0.35 in the intervention group versus 0.04 in the control group (P = 0.003; Table 1). Changes in ALMI and ALMIFMI Z-scores did not differ between groups (−0.37 vs −0.43; P = 0.92 and −0.15 vs −0.55; P = 0.92, respectively; Table 1). Baseline characteristics and body composition measures at baseline and 12 weeks are shown in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Sensitivity analyses using a linear mixed model incorporating all participants with data at either timepoint yielded similar results (Supplementary Table S3). Baseline rates of sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity were similar between groups, and changes in the proportion of patients meeting criteria for sarcopenia or sarcopenic obesity did not differ over 12 weeks (Table 2). Subgroup analyses of patients with severe RA at baseline (defined as DAS28 > 5.1 or CDAI > 22) likewise showed no between-group differences in changes in ALMI or ALMIFMI Z-scores (Supplementary Table S4). Regional analyses of FMI revealed no between-group differences across body compartments after correction for multiple comparisons (Supplementary Table S5); although a signal for differential change in arm FMI was observed, it did not remain significant following adjustment.

Discussion

In this post-hoc analysis of an RCT of diet-induced weight loss in adults with RA and obesity, intentional weight reduction decreased adiposity without evidence of disproportionate loss of muscle mass. Between-group differences in change for both ALMI and ALMIFMI were not statistically significant. Correspondingly, changes in the proportion of patients meeting criteria for sarcopenia or sarcopenic obesity did not differ significantly between groups. In the intervention group, the within-group decline in ALMIFMI was minimal, suggesting that reductions in lean tissue largely paralleled decreases in adiposity.

These observations are clinically important. Concern for sarcopenia can deter patients and clinicians from pursuing weight reduction despite its cardiometabolic and symptomatic benefits. Our results provide reassurance that, within a structured hypocaloric programme with adequate protein, weight loss in patients with RA and obesity can reduce adiposity without compromising relative muscle mass. They also underscore the value of incorporating adiposity-adjusted indices in trials of weight-loss therapies, which better capture clinically relevant muscle deficits than absolute assessments of muscle mass that do not account for confounding by fat mass.

Subgroup analysis of patients with high RA disease activity showed no significant between-group differences in changes in ALMI or ALMIFMI. However, subgroup sizes were very small, limiting the ability to detect meaningful differences. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, and no firm conclusions can be drawn about the impact of high disease activity on muscle responses to weight loss.

Exploratory analysis of body compartments showed no significant between-group differences after correction for multiple comparisons, although a signal for differential change in arm FMI was observed. This pattern is difficult to interpret, and additional study would be needed to determine whether regional differences in fat loss truly exist.

Limitations of this analysis include its post-hoc design, modest sample size and the short duration of a single 12-week RCT not powered for body-composition outcomes. DXA assesses lean mass but not muscle quality or function, and no functional measures were collected. Generalizability is limited to patients with established RA and obesity. Longer studies with functional and metabolic outcomes are needed to clarify the durability and clinical implications of these changes.

In conclusion, among adults with RA undergoing intentional, diet-induced weight loss, adiposity decreased while relative muscle mass, assessed by an adiposity-adjusted lean mass index, was preserved. These findings support integrating weight-loss interventions into RA management for patients with obesity, providing reassurance that adiposity can be reduced without compromising muscle.
