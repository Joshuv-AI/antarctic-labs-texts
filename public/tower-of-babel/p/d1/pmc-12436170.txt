Stigma Towards Hospitalised Older Adults: A Concept Analysis
Authors: Sadaf Murad‐Kassam, Wenting Yan, Rachel G Khadaroo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe aim of this concept analysis paper is to explore the concept of stigma towards hospitalised older adults and propose a clear definition to understand this phenomenon.Design and Review MethodRodgers' evolutionary concept analysis method was used to evaluate the concept of stigma towards hospitalised older adults by identifying attributes, antecedents, and consequences.Data SourcesA systematic search was conducted using CINAHL, PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases. Seventeen research articles from 1963 to 2025 were identified as directly related to the concept of stigma towards hospitalised older adults.ResultsDiscrimination based on age, discriminatory practices, and negative stereotypes were the common attributes highlighted in research studies. Introduction

Aging populations are rapidly increasing. According to the United Nations, by 2050, the world will have more older adults than children aged 14 years and younger (World Health Organization [WHO] 2021). The rapidity and complexity of increasing numbers of aging populations require the healthcare system to be equipped with knowledge, skill and expertise to address the needs of older adults in various healthcare settings. Hospital settings are considered more acute and stressful for older adults due to their nature of acuity. Along with this, if healthcare providers have a negative mindset towards ageism and aged patients, this may contribute to fear of being judged, hesitation, and reluctance in older adults to seek healthcare or other services in the hospital environment. Stigma associated with aging infringes older adults' rights to receive dignified person‐centered care in hospital settings.

However, age, health status, and stigma intersect in complex ways when it comes to hospitalised older adults. Negative perceptions of aging and the stigma surrounding it often manifest in hospital care practices, influencing healthcare providers' attitudes and approaches. This can have a detrimental impact on the physical and mental well‐being of older adults, who may perceive the hospital as an unsafe environment where their dignity and autonomy are not respected. Understanding these dynamics is crucial to improving the quality of care and ensuring that older adults receive person‐centred, stigma‐free, dignified care in hospital settings.

Goffman (1963) defined stigma as the endorsement of negative attitudes towards individuals based on attributes and characteristics that certain cultures or societies have devalued. Stigma towards hospitalised older adults refers to negative attitudes or beliefs held by HCPs and nurses towards older adults who are receiving care in a hospital setting (Yakubu et al. 2022). The concept of stigma may be rooted in ageism based on discrimination and prejudice. However, this definition is too vague in describing stigma towards hospitalised older adults. The concept of stigma in relation to hospitalised older adults is complex and multifaceted, which requires conceptual clarity. A clear definition of the concept of stigma towards hospitalised older adults will allow clinicians, researchers, and policymakers to promote and protect the rights of older adult patients. It will also lead to achieving the United Nations' sustainable development goal of 2020–2030: to provide equalised care and address the needs of older adults in our society (WHO 2020).

Stigmatisation based on gender, race, disability, age, and ethnicity infringes on the rights of older adults in the hospital setting (Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). Older adults have reported neglect, inequality, lack of power and dignity, and discrimination in hospital settings because of perceptions based on their age and health needs (Ben‐Harush et al. 2017; Calnan et al. 2013; Parke and Hunter 2014; WHO 2021). Stigma towards hospitalised older adults can be apparent in several ways, including assumptions that they are frail, dependent, burdensome, or that their healthcare needs are not as important as those of younger patients. This type of negative perception and attitude towards hospitalised older adults can lead to discrimination in healthcare settings, such as inadequate pain management or delayed treatment (Higgins et al. 2007; Yakubu et al. 2022). As a response, older adults avoid hospitalisation or show care withdrawal when admitted to the hospital (Rahman 2017). One‐third of older adults develop new disabilities after hospitalisation, and half never fully recover due to the lack of comprehensive age‐appropriate care and rehabilitation programmes (Parke and Hunter 2014). Due to the dearth of knowledge on the concept of stigma towards hospitalised older adults and lack of conceptual clarity, this issue often gets unrecognised.

Background

Stigma in hospital settings is defined as a negative care approach towards certain groups of people, provision of substandard care, discrimination against certain patients based on age and disease, and physical or verbal abuse in a subtle form (Phibbs and Hooker 2018). Older adults hide their symptoms from HCPs because of the silent stigma towards them in the hospital and other healthcare settings (Phibbs and Hooker 2018). The loss of cognitive and physical abilities that results from aging is another area in which many hospitalised older adults fear stigmatisation and judgement from HCPs and tend to withdraw from participation in activities to protect their self‐identity (Horton et al. 2007; Wyman et al. 2018). Older adults report neglect, inequality, and discrimination in hospital settings based on their age and health needs (Ben‐Harush et al. 2017; Parke and Hunter 2014). Older adults who suffer from hospital marginalisation and discrimination may experience early functional decline and depression (Rogers et al. 2015). Age‐related stigma is embedded in healthcare practices unconsciously and often unrecognised (Nguyen et al. 2018; Richeson and Shelton 2006). Nursing staff and doctors have implicit age‐related behaviours for older patients, such as ordering less extensive diagnostic tests or offering less invasive treatments (Sousa Sao Jose et al. 2019) compared to younger or middle‐aged patients. Stigma related to aging can undermine older adults' trust in healthcare providers, leading to reluctance to engage with hospital care practices that are crucial for their health recovery (Nguyen et al. 2018).

To understand the negative influence of stigma on the health of older adults in hospital care settings, it is essential to analyse the concept of stigma of aging in such settings. This concept analysis is the initial step in unveiling the practices in hospital settings that perpetuate the stigmatisation of older adult patients to improve the care of hospitalised older adults in hospital settings. Thus, to provide stigma‐free, unbiased care to hospitalised older adults, we need conceptual clarity to understand the phenomena of stigma associated with hospitalised older adults. The aim of this concept analysis paper is to explore the concept of stigma towards hospitalised older adults and propose a clear definition to understand this phenomenon by using Rodgers' (2000) evolutionary model of conceptual analysis. This concept paper will address the knowledge gap among nurses, healthcare professionals (HCPs) and policymakers related to the stigma associated with providing care to older adults in the hospital setting. Further, it will enhance their knowledge to provide older adults with equalised person‐centred care (Ben‐Harush et al. 2017; Calnan et al. 2013).

Methods

Search Strategy

A literature search was conducted using CINAHL, PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases and examined reference lists of shortlisted articles. The search keywords were stigma, stereotypes, older adults, discrimination, and hospital settings. The following MESH terms were used and combined by Boolean operators: Older adult*, prejudice*, stereotype*, ageism*, discrimination*, and hospital*. The search terms were adapted to meet the requirements of different databases. In addition, we decided to restrict our search to using only the Title or Title and Abstract search in all databases to capture the most relevant articles. The search was limited to English‐language articles published between 1963 and 2025. 1963 holds critical significance as, for the first time, a sociologist, Ervin Goffman, recoined the concept of stigma in his health research, resulting in the concept receiving widespread attention across the health sciences. Covering the broader range of time also allowed us to examine how the concept has evolved over time (Rodgers' 2000). English language articles were selected as they are one of the highly used languages in scientific research thatare easy to comprehend and conduct a comprehensive systematic search of the literature. Each reference was documented to keep an audit trail of decisions made during the extensive literature search.

Concept Analysis

The concept of stigma towards hospitalised older adults is evolutionary and iterative in nature, having multiple dimensions which need to be explored to understand the impact of stigma on the care of older adults in hospital. Therefore, using Rodgers' Evolutionary Method of Concept Analysis can help to better understand the various dimensions of stigma towards hospitalised older adults, and can provide a framework for developing interventions and policies to address this important issue.

Rodgers' evolutionary model of concept analysis includes six phases: (1) identifying the concept of interest; (2) identifying and selecting an appropriate realm for data collection; (3) collecting data to identify the attributes and contextual features of the concept; (4) analysing data on the characteristics of the concept; (5) developing an exemplar of the concept (if appropriate); and (6) identifying implications for further concept development.

Concepts in evolutionary approach are not static; rather, they are abstractions that change with time and varied situations. The underlying assumptions of the evolutionary approach include (a) concepts are subject to continual change, (b) an inductive approach to concepts, and (c) the development of the concept is cyclical and contextually dependent (Rodgers' 2000). By examining the attributes, antecedents, and consequences of stigma related to hospitalised older adults, this will assist in developing a conceptual definition and suggest ways to promote more inclusive and equitable care in hospitals for older adults.

Data was analysed using Rodger's evolutionary method of concept analysis. During the analysis phase, all selected articles for concept analysis were first read in their entirety. A second reading was done with a focus on the concepts, antecedents, attributes, surrogate terms, and consequences, as specified in Rodger's evolutionary concept analysis model (Rodgers and Knafl 2000). The patterns repeated in the literature related to concepts, antecedents, attributes, and consequences were used as main themes. Data was considered saturated when no themes emerged in each phase of concept analysis, as defined by Rodger's method of concept analysis (Rodgers and Knafl 2000). Common related concepts, attributes, antecedents, and consequences found in the literature related to the concept of stigma towards hospitalised older adults were analysed in the results section.

Identify the Concept of Interest

Identifying the concept of interest is a crucial and fundamental step in determining the appropriate literature and terminology to guide analysis. Stigma distorts older adults' perceptions of aging and negatively influences their health (Dahlke et al. 2021; Horton et al. 2007; Richeson and Shelton 2006). However, stigma associated with hospital environments can impede the care of older patients directly and indirectly. For example, older patients in acute care settings are labelled as complex and recovering from illness with higher care needs than younger adult patients. Also, older adult patients may experience age discrimination by healthcare providers (Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). However, most HCPs are unaware of their biases towards older patients. Exploring stigma associated with hospitalised older adults and its consequences on their recovery will assist health care providers and organisations to be self‐conscious towards the above phenomenon and reduce stigma‐oriented practices towards older adults.

Attributes

Attributes are the characteristics of a concept that constitute an actual definition rather than simply a dictionary one; they are characteristics often associated with a concept (Rodgers and Knafl 2000). The common attributes of stigma found in the literature were: (a) Discrimination based on age, (b) Discriminatory practices, (c) HCP's attitudes towards care of older adults (Negative stereotypes and Impact factors). Discrimination Based on Age

Discrimination against older adults refers to negative actions and behaviours towards them based on their age and needs (WHO 2021). Discrimination not only affects the thinking process of older adults about their self‐ability, but also their cognition and abstract thinking. Discrimination based on age is prominent in the literature, and within hospital culture and HCP's attitudes towards older adults (Herrera‐Perez et al. 2022; Rogers et al. 2015). HCPs may hold implicit bias against older adult patients based on their age and capabilities because they may treat patients as time consuming, demanding, and consider them as complaining individuals, which increases their workload and disturbs their routine practice (Ben‐Harush et al. 2017; Herrera‐Pérez et al. 2022; Higgins et al. 2007). Older patients are mostly not involved in decision making nor informed about their own treatment plan due to informal discrimination towards them and their decision‐making capabilities (Ben‐Harush et al. 2017; Herrera‐Pérez et al. 2022; Higgins et al. 2007; Parke and Hunter 2014). Surgical delay from lack of resources and treatment options has been reported as consequences due to discrimination based on age. Older adults are offered fewer treatment options, and their concerns are ignored related to their health because of age discrimination (Richeson and Shelton 2006; Rogers et al. 2015).

Discriminatory Practices

Discriminatory practices in health care settings and hospitals are commonly reported, which can worsen the health of older adults, leading to new or worsening disabilities (Rogers et al. 2015). Discriminatory practices involve a lack of attention towards older adult patients in surgical or medical units, a lack of encouragement to participate in rehabilitation practices, inappropriate treatment, biased attitudes, inattention towards pain management, and a lack of consideration in providing medical or surgical recourses to older adult patients (Herrera‐Perez et al. 2022). As such, there is ignorance by HCPs in assisting with activities of daily living and providing hygiene care to older patients in comparison to younger patients. Such practices are associated with fewer treatment options, preventive health services, a decrease in prescribing medications, and delays in testing and recovery options (Rogers et al. 2015; Herrera‐Perez et al. 2022).

Discriminatory care practices may lead to poor health outcomes and increase the risk of morbidity in hospitalised older adults (Higgins et al. 2007; Parke and Chappell 2010; Parke and Hunter 2014). Furthermore, HCPs exclude older adult patients from being involved actively in their own care. Nurses assume older patients are dependent and physically immobile, and treat them with disrespect and consider them heavy patient assignments (Dahlke et al. 2021). HCPs bypass them by approaching family members to decide treatment modalities and do not allow them to express their views or concerns (Ben‐Harush et al. 2017). Hospitalised older adults are more vulnerable as they are in an acute care environment with health issues; they require quality, dignified treatment and resources for quick recovery. However, hospital discriminatory practices decrease the quality care outcomes in older adults drastically, leading to disability and morbidity (Ben‐Harush et al. 2017).

Negative Stereotypes

Studies show HCPs have a negative perception or attitude towards older adults admitted to the hospital. Older adults are stereotyped in hospitals by HCPs with negative perceptions based on their age, health conditions, and physical and cognitive health. For example, HCPs assume older adults are slow, have limited mobility, and require more hygiene and incontinence care, and cognitive and functional assessments (Herrera‐Perez et al. 2022; Gholamzadeh et al. 2022; Rogers et al. 2015). Older adults are perceived as being helpless, impatient, and often ignored within the busy hospital culture. Therefore, their care needs do not become addressed by nurses and physicians based on society‐oriented stigma (Phibbs and Hooker 2018). Negative stereotypes related to ageism are reflected in the way HCPs communicate, as they are impatient and rude in their communication style and care approach towards older adult patients in hospitals. Studies show nurses place incontinence pads on older adults in hospitals without even knowing their functional status (Higgins et al. 2007). Such negative stereotypes and attitudes may negatively affect healthcare decision‐making, leading to neglect and misdiagnosis of older adult patients.

Studies have explored HCPs' attitudes towards older adults by examining their demographic and professional characteristics (Modarres Sadraei et al. 2022; Turen et al. 2024; Yakubu et al. 2022), providing insights into which types of HCPs are more likely to hold negative attitudes towards elderly care. For example, HCPs such as nurses working in intensive care units, those with longer years of professional experience, or those who were married with children were found to exhibit more positive attitudes towards older adults, characterized by higher levels of compassion, patience, and professional responsibility (Modarres Sadraei et al. 2022; Turen et al. 2024). Yakubu et al. (2022) also found that female nurses had more positive attitudes than their male counterparts. These findings suggest that demographic and professional characteristics influence attitudes, which, in turn, can either mitigate or reinforce stigma towards hospitalised older adults. Negative attitudes, often rooted in stereotypes or biases, may result in poor behaviour and low‐quality care, thereby exacerbating the stigma faced by older adults during hospitalisation.

Antecedents

Rodgers (2000) defined antecedents as events, conditions, or precursors evident prior to the occurrence of a concept. They are events or situations that trigger the occurrence of the concept of interest. Stigmatisation towards hospitalised older adults is a complex issue, and the attributes leading to this occur at the personal and organisational levels. The main antecedent highlighted in the literature is social stigma, self‐stigma, and lack of training and resources in the hospital environment. These are the three main reasons that lead to unintentional and intentional stigmatised behaviour towards hospitalised older adults. (Please see Table S1 for detail systematic search result).

Social Stigma

Social stigma, sometimes referred to as public stigma or enacted stigma, is “the negative attitudes held by members of the public about people with devalued characteristics” (Corrigan and Rao 2012, 464). Cultural and societal stigmatised beliefs towards the needs of older adults, based on their age and comorbidities, leads to discrimination at the hospital level which impacts their care approach and HCPs' perception towards older adults in an acute care environment (Phibbs and Hooker 2018). Social stigma creates a distinction between ‘them versus us’ that provokes biased attitudes, beliefs and actions towards older adults at the hospital and other healthcare organisations (Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). As such, society‐imposed stigma related to older adult health needs leads to misperception and injustice care towards them.

HCP's demographic characteristics further interact with these stigmatised beliefs. Like male nurses or nurses who are young and single were found to be less likely to demonstrate empathy and responsibility in their approach towards elderly care (Modarres Sadraei et al. 2022; Turen et al. 2024; Yakubu et al. 2022), reflecting how social individual factors can influence the manifestation of stigma among elderly patients.

Studies also show HCPs in hospitals consider older adult patients as stubborn, attention seeking, uncooperative, pretensive, and dependent (Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). Therefore, HCPs consider them least priority and their health concerns become neglected, which can worsen health outcomes (Calnan et al. 2013; Ben‐Harush et al. 2017; Parke and Hunter 2014). Societal stigma reflects unconsciously in the hospital culture and attitude of HCPs, resulting in drastic impacts on older adults' physical and mental wellbeing.

Self‐Stigma

Social stigma unconsciously leads to negative self‐perception of aging in older adults. Moreover, hospitalisation is considered a daunting event for them due to the change of settings and negative attitude of HCPs towards the health needs of older adults (Calnan et al. 2013). Self‐stigma is more worrisome than social stigma because it can impact the self‐esteem and personal perception of the aging process of older adults, which may impact their motivation towards recovery after hospitalisation (Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). Older adults who self‐stigmatise due to social stigma tend to avoid hospitalisation and seeking help from HCPs or nurses to protect their self‐esteem and avoid the consequences of hospitalisation (Covinsky et al. 2003).

Lack of Training and Exposure to Older Adults

Another antecedent that leads to stigmatisation towards older adults is the lack of training to address the unique needs of older adult patients in the hospital environment. In the hospital, older adult patients suffer from inadequate care due to the lack of age‐specific care and understanding of the care needs for older adults. Studies show nurses display negligence in care and ignore older adult needs due to a lack of training. Often, patients complain there is a lack of privacy and a paternalistic attitude in the hospital environment; such as unclosing the curtains when providing personal care, not allowing them to be a part of the decision‐making process related to their health decisions, or bypassing them by approaching family members (Herrera‐Pérez et al. 2022; Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). Older adults suffer through marginalisation and loss of autonomy when they are admitted to hospital due to acute health concerns (Calnan et al. 2013; Herrera‐Perez et al. 2022). Studies show HCPs feel they lack the skills and knowledge to provide care for older adult patients in the chaotic hospital environment. Their attitude towards them is often considered rude or disrespectful due to the lack of expertise in geriatric care (Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). Furthermore, due to a shortage of time, staff, tight hospital space, high stress to perform routine care, and a lack of age‐specific policies and procedures in the hospital, older patients often get marginalised and struggle with stigmatised care and communication.

However, Modarres Sadraei et al. (2022) found that nurses living with elderly people or those with more professional experience tend to hold more positive attitudes towards elderly care and have a stronger sense of responsibility. This suggests that exposure to older adults and accumulated experience may mitigate some of the negative impacts of inadequate training, highlighting the importance of fostering practical geriatric experience and promoting empathy in healthcare professionals.

Consequences

Consequences are events or situations that occur because of stigma's presence (Corrigan and Rao 2012). Inequalities in the hospital environment and lack of motivation are the consequences due to stigmatised demeanour towards older patients in the hospital setting.

Hospital Environment Inequalities

The lack of training and resources to address the specific needs of older adults exacerbates inequalities in hospital care. Studies show nursing staff label older patients as complex and delay addressing their care needs because of the negative perceptions and stigma attached to them (Higgins et al. 2007; Sousa Sao Jose et al. 2019; Rogers et al. 2015). Without adequate training or exposure to geriatric care, nurses may still label older patients as heavy, demanding, dependent, and needing greater care, which they cannot provide (Higgins et al. 2007; Parke and Chappell 2010).

Older adults experience inequalities in treatment options within the hospital, such as the need for surgery, extensive treatment options, prescriptions of narcotics or excessive pain medications, and diagnostic and preventive measures (Parke and Hunter 2014). The lack of expertise in age‐specific care further leads some HCPs to perceive medical interventions for older adults as wasteful, further reducing the quality of care provided (Calnan et al. 2013).

Additionally, the demographic and social individual factors of HCPs such as gender, marital status and personal experience with older adults can also influence the quality of care they provide (Modarres Sadraei et al. 2022; Turen et al. 2024; Yakubu et al. 2022). These individual differences may result in inequalities in the hospital environment, as older adults may receive inconsistent care depending on the personal attributes of the attending HCPs.

Consequently, stigmatisation within hospital settings results in inadequate treatment modalities, leading to disabilities and increased risk of institutionalisation or mortality in older adult patients (Herrera‐Pérez et al. 2022; Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). Hospital‐acquired disability is very common in older adults; most report a decrease in functional strength and capacity to perform activities of daily living in comparison to pre‐hospitalisation (Covinsky et al. 2003).

Lack of Motivation

The negative stereotypes and stigma linked to older patients may directly influence their motivation to engage in health promotion activities in the hospital. They may perceive themselves as fragile, low status, and demonstrate disinterest in participating in the rehabilitation process to improve their physical and mental health (Dahlke et al. 2021).

Lack of motivation and encouragement from HCPs towards older patients due to stigmatised perceptions about their age, complex disease process and capability to participate in daily living activities also has an impact on their willingness to participate actively in health promotion behaviours (Nguyen et al. 2018). The above may lead to withdrawal of older adults seeking medical and nursing care. Stigmatisation in the hospital environment has serious impact on the motivation power of older adults; it affects their physical and mental health recovery and may influence negatively their discharge and alternate level of care process (Covinsky et al. 2003; Dahlke et al. 2021; Higgins et al. 2007).

Surrogate Terms and Related Concepts

The surrogate terms in the literature are marginalised older adults, ageist attitude, stereotypes, and biases in care. Throughout the literature, these terms were used interchangeably to explain the phenomena of stigmatisation of hospitalised older adults (Covinsky et al. 2003; Dahlke et al. 2021; Higgins et al. 2007; Herrera‐Pérez et al. 2022; Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). Related concepts depict the background, significance, meaning, and foundation of the concept of interest; however, they do not necessarily share the same attributes as the concept investigated. During the literature review, two related concepts of stigma were found: power differential and elder talk. Power differential in the healthcare setting is indirectly evident in older adult care. A power relationship between patients and HCPs is an abstract concept not directly related to stigma but may be a reason hospitalised older adults feel uncomfortable (Herrera‐Pérez et al. 2022; Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). Elder speak or talk is a patronising communication style HCPs use unconsciously when they care for or explain something to older adults (Shaw and Gordon 2021). Older adults consider elder speak demoralising and discriminatory. These terms may overlap with the concept of stigma but are not directly related to it.

Proposed Definition of Stigma Towards Hospitalised Older Adults

Based on attributes found in the literature, stigma associated with hospitalised older adults is defined as a destructive based attitude, discriminatory practices, and aged‐based biases exhibited in healthcare settings that result in limited treatment modalities, neglect, and marginalisation towards older adults admitted into hospital. This stigma is demonstrated in negative perceptions and behaviours of healthcare providers, influenced by societal stereotypes and hospital culture. It results in unfair treatment, poorer care, and a greater likelihood of patients developing disabilities or their health deteriorating while they are in the hospital. Furthermore, a lack of connection with senior patients can exacerbate this biased treatment, and many healthcare professionals lack the training necessary to address the unique requirements of older persons. Older people's physical and mental health deteriorate as a result.

Discussion

The lack of conceptual clarity about the concept of stigma in reference to hospitalised older adults may lead to unconscious biases in hospital culture and practice towards older adult patients. Knowledge derived from this concept‐analysis paper will address the gap at the practice level to provide stigma‐free nursing and hospital care to older patients. Conceptual clarity will also assist nurses and HCPs in transforming their perception and care practice towards older adults and improve their knowledge and skills to ensure competent care.

Impatient attitudes of nurses and HCPs towards older patients, stereotypical feelings related to the aging process, and a pessimistic viewpoint are factors that stigmatise their care in the hospital. Identifying the core belief of HCPs related to older patients will allow them to self‐analyse their negative perceptions and provide biased‐free care (Calnan et al. 2013). Self‐awareness of negative feelings will enable nursing staff and HCPs to avoid these behaviours consciously and ultimately improve health outcomes for older adults (Dahlke et al. 2021).

Furthermore, internalising the impact of societal and self‐stigma and lack of specialised training and resources to provide competent care to hospitalised older patients is the important antecedent that needs to be addressed to eradicate the phenomena of stigma towards hospitalised older adults. Older adult patients are admitted to the hospital with the potential for successful discharge and full recovery; however, lack of understanding of age‐related changes and underlying chronic health concerns slows the discharge and recovery process (Parke and Hunter 2014).

Inequalities in the hospital environment and discriminatory attitudes of nurses and HCPs to address the diverse age‐sensitive needs of older adults lead to a functional and physical decline in their health (Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). Older adults become more bed bound after hospitalisation, increasing institutionalisation risk (Covinsky et al. 2003; Parke and Hunter 2014; Rogers et al. 2015). Stigmatised attitudes towards older adults affect their intrinsic motivation factor, and they may feel less engaged and motivated to recover fully after hospitalisation. Societal stigma leads to a self‐stigmatised feeling in hospitalised older adults, impacting their recovery process, which may lead to prolonged hospitalisation and new or worsening disabilities.

Overall, this concept analysis is a starting point for further research on the provision of stigma‐free care to hospitalised older adults and the integration of research‐based knowledge into practice to eradicate discrimination based on age.

Future Implications

Based on the findings from this review, it is reflected that healthcare providers need more advanced knowledge and skills to provide dignified person‐centered care to older patients. Moreover, hospital policies need to be inclusive to create anti‐ageist hospital and healthcare practices. Older patients should have their voices in making decisions related to their health and quality of care in hospital settings. Active‐listening strategy and anti‐aging attitude need to be well integrated in the hospital environment and in the behaviour of healthcare professionals, so older adult patients feel safe to share their concerns. The perception of healthcare providers and mundane hospital practices towards aging and older adults needs to be changed, to have anti‐ageist healthcare practices and environment.

Hospital environments and culture play enormous roles in addressing stigma and discrimination based on age. Hospital infrastructure and routines need to be more flexible and age‐friendly to relieve the anxiety or confusion of older patients. Nursing staff, leaders, and policymakers must be thoughtful in creating policies to ensure person‐oriented care and acknowledge age‐specific dignified care for older adults (Gholamzadeh et al. 2022). Implementing positive change in the hospital environment will decrease the risk of stigmatising older adults and ultimately encourage nursing staff and other HCPs to actively involve older patients as partners of care.

Another major challenge which leads to stigmatised practice towards hospitalised patients is the lack of specialised geriatric knowledge and exposure to geriatric care HCPs, that unintentionally results in stigma‐orientated feelings and actions towards hospitalised older adults (Dahlke et al. 2021). It is crucial to integrate gerontological competency and knowledge into routine hospital practice to address the complex needs of older patients. Gerontological knowledge and education also need to be embedded at the curriculum level for healthcare aides, licensed practical nurses, registered nurses, and physicians because they spend more time with older adults as compared to other HCPs (Ben‐Harush et al. 2017; Dahlke et al. 2021). Furthermore, training is required at the interdisciplinary level for HCPs because incompetence in caring for older persons will create challenges in providing bias‐free care in hospital settings.

Conclusion

Conceptual clarity and a clear definition of stigma towards hospitalised older adults could enlighten change in hospital routine practices and policies, and HCP behaviours within the educational and organisational levels. Providing stigma‐free care in the hospital to older adults will enhance their health and boost their trust in healthcare providers. Ensuring competency in providing specialised geriatric care in hospital settings and shifting the negative perceptions of HCPs can positively change the concept of stigma and will eradicate this practice at the healthcare level.
