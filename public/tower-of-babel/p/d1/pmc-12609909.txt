Developing Subcutaneous Formulations for Irisin as a Potential Therapeutic
Authors: Gang Hu, Xi Luan, Michael J Hageman, Hao Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIrisin, a hormone secreted by skeletal muscle during physical exercise, shows promise for health benefits. However, limited research on its characterization and formulation development has impeded its pharmaceutical development. This study aimed to develop fit-for-purpose subcutaneous formulations for irisin.MethodsIrisin was expressed in E.coli and purified. The prepared irisin was characterized using orthogonal biophysical techniques, and its biological activity was assessed. Subcutaneous formulations were then developed by screening pH, excipients, and formats (lyophilized vs. aqueous), and the product stability profiles were investigated under various conditions.ResultsIrisin was successfully expressed in both soluble and refolded forms, and after purification, both forms displayed similar biophysical properties and induced adipocyte browning. Lyophilized formulations, incorporating phosphate buffer (pH 7), sucrose or trehalose, and polysorbate 80, preserved irisin’s properties and bioactivity after lyophilization and could be rapidly reconstituted for subcutaneous administration. The formulations also remained stable at 40°C/75% RH for at least one month.ConclusionsThese findings lay a foundation for advancing irisin-based therapies for obesity and related diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11095-025-03945-w.Keywords: irisin, lyophilization, obesity, stability, subcutaneous formulation

Introduction

Physical exercise is widely recognized for reducing the risk of metabolic syndromes, neurodegenerative diseases, and cardiovascular disorders [1–3]. Despite these benefits, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain only partially understood. The discovery of irisin, a myokine secreted by skeletal muscle during physical activity, has provided new insights into how exercise exerts its protective effects at the cellular and systemic levels [4]. Irisin is produced by the proteolytic cleavage of fibronectin type III domain-containing protein 5 (FNDC5), a membrane protein primarily expressed in skeletal muscle [4]. Once released into circulation, irisin binds to integrin receptors, such as αVβ5 or αIβ1, on various cells and tissues (i.e., fat and bone) [5], and activates one or more downstream signaling pathways, including the focal adhesion kinase (FAK), MAPK, AMPK, and PI3K/AKT pathways [6, 7]. These signaling cascades drive a range of beneficial effects. For example, Irisin is found to be associated with some bone diseases, such as osteoarthritis and osteoporosis [8]. Irisin may provide protective effects against some neurodegenerative diseases, such as Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease, and cardiovascular diseases in preclinical models [9–12]. In this study, we are interested in irisin’s therapeutic potential for obesity, as irisin can promote the browning of white adipose tissue (WAT) by upregulating uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1), thereby enhancing energy expenditure and improving insulin sensitivity [13–15]. Noteworthy, irisin’s 112 residues are highly conserved [4, 16], and the UCP1 functions are comparable between mice and humans [17], increasing the translatability of irisin’s treatments from preclinical studies to human subjects.

However, limited research on irisin’s biophysical characterization and formulation development hinders its progress toward clinical uses. In general, protein-based therapeutics such as irisin are administered intravenously or subcutaneously rather than orally, since for the oral routes, there are several barriers (i.e., enzymatic, sulfhydryl, mucus, epithelial barriers, etc.) that are difficult to overcome and can eventually lead to low oral bioavailability [18]. The subcutaneous (SC) administration is often preferred by patients and health providers and offers multiple advantages compared to the intravenous (IV) administration. Particularly regarding irisin’s effect on browning SC WAT, the SC administration can provide extra therapeutic benefits such as localized targeting. To date, studies have been conducted within laboratories to develop a wide variety of SC formulations/drug delivery systems for proteins, including aqueous solutions, lyophilized powders, spray dried powders, clusters/complexes, suspensions, nanoparticles, microparticles, hydrogels, etc [19]. However, to the best of our knowledge, commercial protein formulations are mostly limited to aqueous solutions and lyophilized powders at a roughly 2-to-1 ratio [20, 21]. In general, aqueous solutions are easier to manufacture, more cost-effective, and ready to use, but lyophilized formulations can be a better choice to achieve the desired shelf life (typically ≥ 2 years) if proteins undergo stability issues under aqueous environments. Despite the complexity of manufacturing units and the need for reconstitution before administration, lyophilized formulations provide several advantages, such as formulation stability improvement, long shelf life, economic storage conditions (i.e., ideally at room temperature), and ease of handling during transportation [22]. For SC formulations, the “high-concentration, low-volume” or the “low-concentration, high-volume” strategies would be necessary for a therapeutic requiring a high effective dose [23]. However, due to the high binding affinity of irisin to its receptor (i.e., for integrin αVβ5: Kd ~ 30 nM [24]) and SC localized targeting, we speculate that high doses may not be needed for irisin formulations, thereby making formulation development less challenging with a higher success rate.

In this study, we produced irisin in-house and then developed it into prototype SC lyophilized formulations. The developed irisin substances and formulations were characterized by various biophysical and bioactivity techniques, and the formulation stability was assessed.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Sucrose, trehalose, sodium chloride, dexamethasone, 3-iso-butyl-methyl-xanthine, insulin, imidazole, and tris base were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Polysorbate 80 was purchased from Fluka (Milwaukee, WI, USA). Luria–Bertani (LB) medium was purchased from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). DMEM media, calf bovine serum, and fetal bovine serum were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, MD, USA). Chloramphenicol, kanamycin, and AEBSF protease inhibitor were purchased from Goldbio (St. Louis, MO, USA). SYBRO Orange was purchased from Thermo Fisher (Waltham, MA, USA). Irisin expressed in HEK293 was purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA).

Generation of Plasmids for Irisin Expression in Soluble Form

To enhance irisin solubility during expression, the SUMO tag was engineered into the sequence, followed by a flexible linker (sequence: GG) and a coding sequence for the TEV protease cleavage site (sequence: ENLYFQG) linked with the codon-optimized sequence of irisin. To enhance the efficiency of the affinity purification, a 6 × histidine tag was added to the N-terminal. The complete sequence was 6 × His-SUMO-TEV-Irisin. The sequence was subcloned into the pET28a plasmid using NcoI and BamHI restriction sites (Genscript, Piscataway, NJ, USA). The resulting plasmid, pET28a 6 × His-SUMO-TEV-Irisin, was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) CodonPlus cells from Agilent (Santa Clara, CA, USA) using the 42°C heat shock for 20 s.

Generation of Plasmids for Irisin Expression from Inclusion Bodies

To express irisin from inclusion bodies, a 6 × histidine tag was added to the upstream of the irisin coding sequence to improve the affinity purification, and a TEV protease cleavage site (sequence: ENLYFQG) was inserted between the histidine tag and irisin. The codon-optimized construct (6 × His-TEV-Irisin) was subcloned into the pET9a plasmid using NcoI and BamHI restriction sites (Genscript, Piscataway, NJ, USA). The resulting plasmid, pET9a 6 × His-TEV-Irisin, was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) CodonPlus cells from Agilent (Santa Clara, CA, USA) using the 42°C heat shock for 20 s.

Expression and Purification of Soluble Irisin in E.coli

A pre-culture was prepared by inoculating the glycerol stock into 50 mL of LB medium containing chloramphenicol (34 µg/ml) and kanamycin (50 µg/ml) and incubating overnight at 30°C with shaking at 220 rpm. The next day, 10 mL of the pre-culture was transferred into 1 L of LB with the same antibiotics and then incubated at 37°C. When the OD600nm reached approximately 0.8, IPTG was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM to induce irisin expression. After 4 h, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6,000 g for 10 min and resuspended in a buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0 with 500 mM NaCl) containing 10 mM imidazole. The obtained suspension was stored at -80°C before purification.

For purification, the suspension was thawed and then supplemented with 0.1 mM AEBSF protease inhibitor, followed by cell lysis using sonication at 50% amplitude for 10 min. The cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 15,000 g for 30 min, and the supernatant was loaded onto a 5-ml HisTrap FF column (Cytiva, Wilmington, DE, USA), which was washed with a washing buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0 with 500 mM NaCl) containing 20 mM imidazole until the baseline was stabilized. To harvest His-SUMO-TEV-Irisin, the sample was eluted with an elution buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0 with 500 mM NaCl) containing 250 mM imidazole, and then dialyzed using a dialysis membrane (3.5-kDa MWCO) overnight at 4°C using a buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0 with 100 mM NaCl) containing TEV protease at a 1:20 protease-to-irisin ratio to remove the tag. The cleaved irisin was purified by passing the solution through another 5-ml HisTrap FF column, and the flow-through fraction containing the tag-free irisin was collected. Finally, the sample was dialyzed using a dialysis membrane (3.5-kDa MWCO) into a buffer (20 mM phosphate at pH 7.0 with 100 mM NaCl) containing 0.04 mg/mL polysorbate 80 and stored at -80°C for future use.

Expression and Purification of Refolded Irisin in E.coli

The procedure of protein expression was identical to that for soluble irisin. After the expression, the cell suspension was sonicated at 50% amplitude for 10 min. Subsequently, Triton X-100 was added to the lysate to a final concentration of 1% and the mixture was stirred for another 15 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 12 min, and the pellet containing inclusion bodies was collected. The pellet was washed using a buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0 with 50 mM NaCl) containing 1% Triton X-100, followed by another wash with the same buffer containing 3 M urea. After centrifugation at 10,000 g for 12 min, the pellet was solubilized in a buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0) containing 8 M urea, followed by the addition of β-mercaptoethanol to a final concentration of 10 mM to reduce disulfide bonds. The solution was incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and the soluble protein was recovered by centrifugation at 15,000 g for 20 min. The refolding step was performed by 50-fold dilution into a refolding buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0 with 100 mM NaCl) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C. The next day, the solution was filtered through a 0.22-μm filter and loaded onto a 5-mL HisTrap FF column. The column was washed with a washing buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0 with 500 mM NaCl) containing 20 mM imidazole, and the His-TEV-Irisin fraction was eluted with an elution buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0 with 500 mM NaCl) containing 250 mM imidazole. The eluted protein was dialyzed using a dialysis membrane (3.5-kDa MWCO) overnight into a buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0 with 100 mM NaCl) at 4°C in the presence of TEV protease at a 1:20 protease-to-irisin ratio. After digestion, the solution was passed through a second 5-mL HisTrap FF column, and the flow-through fraction containing the tag-free irisin was collected. The final irisin product was dialyzed using a dialysis membrane (3.5-kDa MWCO) into a buffer (20 mM phosphate at pH 7.0 with 100 mM NaCl), followed by the addition of polysorbate 80 at 0.04 mg/mL and stored at -80°C for future use.

Circular Dichroism (CD)

Far-UV CD Spectra were acquired using a Chirascan-plus CD Spectrophotometer (Applied Photophysics, Leatherhead, UK) equipped with a Peltier-controlled six-cell holder. An irisin sample at 0.2 mg/mL was loaded into a 1-mm quartz cuvette. Spectra were recorded from 200 to 260 nm in 1-nm increments with a 1-s integration time per data point. The secondary structure content was analyzed using the BeStSel (beta structure selection) online tool [25].

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

DSC was conducted using a MicroCal VP-Capillary calorimeter (Malvern Panalytical, Westborough, MA, USA) equipped with an autosampler. An irisin sample (450 µl at 1 mg/mL) was loaded into a 96-well plate, which was sealed and placed inside a sample container at 4°C. Each measurement was programmed to begin with a 15-min stabilization at 10°C and then run from 10°C to 90°C at a rate of 1°C/min. The DSC data were buffer-subtracted, baseline-corrected, and normalized. Apparent transition temperatures were determined using a non-two-state equilibrium model in Origin 7.0 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA).

Intrinsic & Extrinsic Fluorescence Spectroscopy

Intrinsic fluorescence was measured using an LS45 luminescence spectrometer (PerkinElmer Corporation, Waltham, MA, USA). The irisin samples were diluted in 10 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) to a final concentration of 0.004 mg/mL. The excitation wavelength was set at 280 nm, and the emission spectrum was collected from 300 to 450 nm with a 0.5-nm step interval.

For differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) measurements using extrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy, an irisin sample at 0.2 mg/mL was mixed with 10 μM SYPRO Orange, and the fluorescence was recorded from 20°C to 90°C with a ramp rate of 1°C/min using a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA).

Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC)

Irisin samples were evaluated by SEC using a Shimadzu HPLC system (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a Waters XBridge Protein BEH SEC Column (200 Å pore size, 3.5 mm particle size, 7.8 mm ID × 150 mm length). The mobile phase consisted of 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.7), 50 mM sodium sulfate, and 7.5% (v/v) isopropanol. Flow rate was 0.86 mL/min, column temperature was 25°C, UV absorbance was recorded at 214 nm and 280 nm, and injection volume was 10 µL.

Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

Ten microliters of irisin samples at 0.5 mg/mL were mixed with 2 × Laemmli buffer containing 50 mM dithiothreitol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and heated at 90°C for 10 min. Samples were cooled to room temperature and separated by SDS-PAGE on 12% Mini-PROTEAN TGX precast protein gels (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using Tris–glycine running buffer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Electrophoresis was performed at 150 V for 50 min. Gels were stained with Bio-Safe Coomassie Stain (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Differentiation of 3T3-L1 Fibroblast Cells into Adipocytes

3T3-L1 mouse embryonic fibroblast cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in DMEM media supplied with 10% calf bovine serum (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and maintained at below 70% confluence. The differentiation procedure was performed following the previous literature by Zhao et al. [26]. As cells reached the confluence, the culture medium was replaced with the differentiation medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1 μM dexamethasone, 0.5 mM 3-iso-butyl-methyl-xanthine (IBMX), and 1 μg/mL insulin. Cells were cultured in this medium for 2 days. Then the differentiation medium was replaced with an adipocyte maintenance medium of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 10 μg/mL insulin. Full differentiation into adipocytes typically occurred within 7 to 15 days, during which lipid droplets accumulated in the cytoplasm and became visible under a microscope.

Oil Red O Staining

To assess the differentiation of 3T3-L1 cells, Oil red O staining was performed following the protocol developed by Zebisch et al. [27]. Briefly, a 0.5% Oil red O stock solution was prepared in 100% isopropanol and stirred overnight. The prepared solution was filtered through a 0.22-μm filter and stored at 4°C as the stock solution. A working staining solution was freshly prepared by diluting the stock solution with DI water at a 6:4 (v/v) ratio. Differentiated 3T3-L1 cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% formaldehyde in PBS at room temperature for 1 h. After fixation, cells were washed with PBS, followed by an isopropanol/water mixture (60%/40%). Cells were then incubated with Oil red O staining solution for 2 h, followed by extensive washing before imaging capture.

RT-qPCR for mRNA Quantification

When 3T3-L1 cells were fully differentiated into adipocytes, irisin samples at 25 nM were added to the adipocyte maintenance medium for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and 500 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed using MMLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and random primers (Promega, Madison, WI). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the following primers:

GAPDH_Forward (5’-CATGGCCTTCCGTGTTCCTAC-3’), GAPDH_Reverse (5’-TTGCTGTTGAAGTCGC AGGAG-3’). UCP1_Forward (5’-AGGCTTCCAGTACCATTAGGT-3’), UCP1_Reverse (5’-CTGAGTGAGGCA AAGC TGATTT-3’). Primers were synthesized by IDT DNA (Coralville, IA, USA). RT-qPCR was performed using the Taq™ SYTO-82 Supermix (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Data was analyzed using 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR Software. Gene expression was normalized to mouse GAPDH using the 2-ΔΔϹt method. All samples were analyzed in triplicate, and results are presented as relative expression ratios.

Sample Preparation in Different pH Buffers

To evaluate the influence of pH ranging from pH 4 to 8, the irisin samples were dialyzed using a dialysis membrane (2-kDa MWCO) against a citrate–phosphate buffer of 20 mM with the ionic strength adjusted to 0.15 M by addition of NaCl. The citrate–phosphate buffer was prepared by mixing specific volumes of 0.4-M citric acid and 0.4-M sodium phosphate dibasic for targeted pH value. After the dialysis overnight at 4°C, the irisin samples were analyzed by CD, fluorescence, DSC, and SEC.

Lyophilization of Irisin

Irisin was formulated at 1 mg/mL in a solution containing 10 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0), 50 mg/mL sucrose or trehalose, and 0.04 mg/mL polysorbate 80. A 2-mL Fiolax® clear glass vial (Schott Pharma, Lebanon, PA, USA) was filled with 500 μl of the solution and partially closed with a rubber stopper (West Pharma Service, Exton, PA, USA). Lyophilization was performed using a SP VirTis AdvantagePro freeze dryer (ATS Scientific Products, Warminster, PA, USA). The freezing and primary drying temperatures were selected based on published literature for protein formulations [28–31]. The cycle began with freezing samples at a rate of -l°C/min to -45°C and held for 2 h. Subsequently, primary drying was performed at -30°C and 100 mTorr for 50 h, followed by secondary drying to 25°C and 100 mTorr for 18 h with a ramp rate of 0.2°C/min. Upon completion of the lyophilization cycle, vials were backfilled with nitrogen and capped under vacuum (580 Torr) before being removed from the freeze dryer. To reconstitute the lyophilized formulations, 0.5 mL of DI water was used per vial.

Residual Water Content Determination

Residual moisture content in lyophilized irisin samples was measured using a TGA550 thermogravimetric analyzer (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA). Approximately 5 mg of the lyophilized sample was weighed into a platinum sample pan. The temperature increased at 20°C/min to 105°C and held at 105°C until the completion of the TGA run (the criteria for completion: weight change ≤ 0.1%/min).

Aqueous Formulation

Irisin was formulated at 1 mg/mL in a solution containing 10 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0), 50 mg/mL sucrose, and 0.04 mg/mL polysorbate 80. The solution was filled into a 2-mL Fiolax® clear glass vial covered with a rubber stopper.

Accelerated Stability Study

Both lyophilized and aqueous irisin formulations were stored at 4°C, 25°C/60% RH, and 40°C/75% RH. At 0, 2, and 4 weeks, sample vials were retrieved for biophysical analysis using CD, fluorescence, DSC, and SEC.

Results

Irisin Production

Two approaches (approach 1: soluble expression; approach 2: refolding from inclusion bodies) were employed to express irisin. The soluble expression approach was accomplished by adding the SUMO tag before the coding frame of the target protein (irisin) with a linker and a TEV protease cleavage site in between. The product after expression, followed by purification and TEV protease cleavage, was analyzed by the reducing SDS-PAGE (Fig. 1A). As shown, Lane 1 confirmed that irisin was successfully produced with acceptable purity (target irisin MW: ~ 12 kDa). On the other hand, using the refolding from inclusion bodies approach, irisin could also be produced, as shown in Lane 2 in Fig. 1A. In addition, the refolding from inclusion bodies approach achieved a higher yield of irisin (25 mg/L) compared to the soluble expression approach (3 mg/L).

Irisin Biophysical Characterization and Bioactivity Assay

Orthogonal techniques were employed to characterize both the soluble and refolded forms of irisin. The secondary structure was investigated by CD. As shown in Fig. 1B, both forms exhibited similar CD spectra from 260 to 200 nm with a negative band at around 214 nm, suggesting they possessed similar secondary structures. Figure 1C shows a more detailed secondary structure analysis revealing that, for both forms, the antiparallel β-sheet was the most dominant structural component (close to 50%), while the α-helix was almost absent. In addition, both forms had comparable secondary structures to the crystalline irisin reported previously (PDB ID: 4LSD [32]).

The tertiary structure was evaluated by intrinsic fluorescence since irisin contains two tryptophan residues. As shown in Fig. 1D, both forms exhibited the same spectral shape with emission maxima at 356 nm, indicating they had similar tertiary structures.

The thermal stability was assessed by both DSC and DSF. The DSC thermograms, shown in Fig. 1E, demonstrate that both forms possessed the identical Tm at 48.3°C and onset temperature at 39.0°C. These results were confirmed by the DSF assay, where the Tm determined by DSF was 49.0°C and 48.8°C for the soluble and refolded forms, respectively, as shown in Figure S1 in the supplementary data.

The oligomeric state was evaluated by SEC. As shown in Fig. 1F, both forms after the concentration adjustment exhibit a main peak with almost identical peak shape and retention time at ~ 5.65 min. According to the calibration curve prepared using the gel filtration standard (as shown in Figure S2 in the supplementary data), this peak corresponded to an apparent molecular weight of around 26 kDa, indicating that both forms predominantly stayed in the dimeric state. It was worth noting that this observation was aligned with the crystalline irisin structure (PDB ID: 4LSD) reported previously, as the non-glycosylated irisin expressed from E.coli was prone to form an eight-stranded antiparallel β-sheet dimer [32]. Furthermore, the molecular weight shown in Fig. 1A supported that this non-covalent dimer could be disrupted in SDS-PAGE. Besides the main peak, the soluble form displayed a small peak (~ 10% of the total peaks’ AUC) at ~ 6.4 min, showing the apparent molecular weight at ~ 7.4 kDa according to the calibration curve, which may represent some contaminant proteins or irisin degradation products. The absence of this peak in the refolded form may be due to removal and inactivation of most protease over the purification process.

The expression of UCP1, a brown adipogenic marker, at the mRNA level was applied to assess the bioactivity (browning of mature adipocytes) of irisin [4]. Adipocytes (shown in Fig. 2A) were successfully derived from 3T3-L1 cells, confirmed by the lipid droplets with Oil red O staining shown in Fig. 2B, and then treated with our produced irisin (at 25 nM). As shown in Fig. 2C, both soluble and refolded forms significantly increased the mRNA expression of UCP1 by 4 to 6 times compared to the control group, demonstrating similar biological activities to the irisin obtained from a commercial source (listed as irisin_HEK in Fig. 2C).

Overall, the results presented above confirmed that both forms of irisin had acceptable and comparable biophysical properties as well as biological activities. It is also worth mentioning that the higher production yield was obtained from the refolding approach, likely due to substantially higher expression levels of irisin in inclusion bodies compared to the soluble expression approach. In E. coli, proteins expressed as inclusion bodies can accumulate at very high levels, whereas soluble expression is often limited by folding stress, proteolytic degradation, or aggregation during expression [33, 34]. Since the refolding from inclusion bodies approach had a higher production yield than the soluble expression approach, the refolding from inclusion bodies approach was chosen to supply irisin for subsequent formulation studies.

SC Formulation Development: pH Screening

It is critical to screen optimal pH(s) for irisin formulations. The irisin solutions prepared at different pHs were stored at 4°C overnight and then characterized. As shown in Fig. 3A, pH could profoundly impact the secondary structure. The CD spectra showed a similar pattern across pH 6 to 8. When pH was lowered to 4 or 5, the CD spectra showed increased negative ellipticity from 208 to 222 nm, reflecting a major conformational change at acidic pHs. As shown in Fig. 3B, the structures of α helix and parallel β strand were present in irisin at pH 4 and 5, whereas they were not found in irisin at pH from 6 to 8.

Physiochemical characterization of refolded irisin from pH 4 to 8. (A) CD spectra. (B) The secondary structure content derived from CD spectra. (C) Intrinsic fluorescence. (D) DSC thermograms. (E) SEC elution profile.

In terms of the tertiary structure of irisin at different pHs, the intrinsic fluorescence spectra (Fig. 3C) revealed that the fluorescence emission maxima were identical at 356 nm in the pH range from 6 to 8. As pH lowered to 4 or 5, the fluorescence intensity decreased, and the emission maxima shifted to 336 nm. This 20-nm blue shift suggested significant tertiary structural changes of irisin in acidic environments.

Regarding conformational stability, no Tm was observed on the DSC thermograms for pH 4 and 5 (Fig. 3D), suggesting that, before the DSC analysis, irisin had already lost its conformation and became partially unfolded. Furthermore, irisin’s Tm was remarkably lower (40°C) at pH 6 compared to pH 7 (47°C) and pH 8 (48°C), suggesting the lower thermal stability at pH 6.

Regarding the SEC analysis shown in Fig. 3E, at pH 7 and 8, the same peak (peak retention time at ~ 5.65 min) was observed, representing the dimeric form of irisin. At pH 6, this peak remained as the main peak, but a minor peak (~ 15% of the total AUC) representing high molecular weight (HMW) species appeared at 3 min. At pH 4 and 5, more than 95% of samples eluted at ~ 3 min, suggesting irisin was colloidally unstable to form HMW species, along with a small fraction eluting at ~ 6.5 min representing some fragments and degraded forms. Irisin demonstrated satisfactory conformational and colloidal stability at pH 7 and 8, but the basic environment (i.e., pH 8) may increase protein chemical stability issues (i.e., deamination) [35, 36]. Overall, pH 7 was selected for irisin formulations.

SC Lyophilized Formulation Development

For the SC administration, aqueous solution prefilled in a syringe or autoinjector is ready-to-use and preferred by patients and healthcare professionals. However, due to the relatively low onset unfolding temperature of irisin (~ 39.0°C), there is some concern about the long-term stability of the aqueous formulation. Accordingly, two lyophilized formulations were developed along with solution formulation development. As shown in Fig. 4A, the formulation containing sucrose formed “uniform and elegant” cakes, while the formulation containing trehalose showed some level of cake cracking, which generally is not considered a defect of the lyophile since it is not CQA-related [37]. Cake cracking in trehalose-contained formulations likely arises from internal stress during drying along with formulations’ solid-state properties such as glass transition temperature. The sucrose and trehalose formulations had moisture contents at 1.8 ± 0.2% and 4.2 ± 0.4%, respectively. Both lyophilized formulations could be completely reconstituted with 0.5 mL of water within 30 s. The 4.2% moisture content of trehalose-contained formulation is relatively high compared with an ideal moisture content (1–3%) of lyophilized protein products but is still within the generally acceptable limit of 5% of lyophilized protein products [38, 39]. The properties and functions of irisin in both lyophilized formulations were also evaluated as follows. Before and after lyophilization, (i) the CD spectra indicated that irisin had negligible change of secondary structure (Fig. 4B and C). It is also noted that there are minor CD differences between sucrose-contained and trehalose-contained formulations, which might be due to weak sugar–protein interactions during the freezing and drying processes. Nonetheless, the secondary structure analysis from the CD spectrum deconvolution shows that both sucrose and trehalose have negligible impact on irisin secondary structures; (ii) the intrinsic fluorescence spectra suggested that irisin held the identical tertiary structure (Fig. 4D); (iii) the DSC thermograms reported irisin had the same Tm at 52.2°C, supporting there was no substantial conformational change during lyophilization (Fig. 4E); (iv) the SEC profiles showed irisin had the same retention time and peak shape, indicating irisin remained in the dimeric form and no HMW species was formed during lyophilization (Fig. 4F); and (v) the bioactivity assay (Fig. 5G) confirmed that after lyophilization, irisin had slightly lower bioactivity (but no statistical difference) based on the UCP1 mRNA expression values, confirming that the lyophilized irisin maintained the capability of browning WAT.

Stability Studies

Stability studies were conducted over 4 weeks for both the lyophilized and aqueous solution formulations. Figures 5 and 6 show the 4-week stability data for the lyophilized formulation containing sucrose or trehalose, respectively. In addition, the 2-week stability data were presented in Figures S3 and S4 in the supplementary data. For both formulations after 4 weeks under 40°C/75% RH, the CD spectra (Figs. 5A, B, 6A, and B) showed no substantial change, indicating the stability of irisin’s secondary structure. Noteworthy, for trehalose-contained formulations in Fig. 6A, the CD spectra after 4 weeks of storage more closely resembled fresh samples compared to spectra obtained immediately after lyophilization (T0). This effect may be attributed to minor reversible conformational perturbations introduced during lyophilization and reconstitution, which equilibrated over time in the lyophilized matrix. The protective sugar matrix may also contribute to stabilization during storage, enabling reconstituted protein to refold into a conformation more like the initial state [40, 41]. The fluorescence spectra (Figs. 5C and 6C) had no peak shift (the fluorescence emission maxima kept the same at 356 nm), suggesting no notable tertiary structure changes. The identical Tm determined by DSC (Figs. 5D and 6D) indicated irisin in both formulations kept the thermal stability. Also, the SEC analysis (Figs. 5E and 6E) showed that the dimer form kept stable (~ 97% of AUC compared to T0) and there were no peaks of HMW species and fragments. Overall, irisin in the lyophilized formulations possessed conformational and colloidal stability throughout the accelerated stability tests for at least 4 weeks.

Biophysical characterization of lyophilized irisin formulation containing 5% trehalose after 4 weeks of stability tests. (A) CD spectra. (B) The secondary structure content derived from CD spectra. (C) Intrinsic fluorescence. (D) DSC thermograms. (E) SEC elution profile.

For comparison, the stability of aqueous formulations was investigated. The 2-week and 4-week data are shown in Fig. 7 and Figure S5 in the supplementary data, respectively. After 2 weeks under 40°C/75% RH, the CD spectra (Fig. 7A and B) had new parallel β-sheet and helix structures; the fluorescence spectra (Fig. 7C) exhibited a blue shift with the fluorescence emission maximum from 356 to 336 nm, suggesting the dramatic change of irisin tertiary structure; the DSC thermograms (Fig. 7D) could not detect Tm, implying most of irisin were denatured and lost its conformations; and the SEC spectra (Fig. 7E) displayed a new early peak representing the HMW species and the disappearance of the main peak. Overall, in order to develop a product with long shelf life (i.e., ≥ 2 years), the lyophilized formulations were superior to the aqueous formulations with respect to better stability based on the accelerated stability data and were thereby selected as the prototype SC formulations.

Discussion

Despite the therapeutic potential of irisin for various diseases, there have been limited studies conducted to develop pharmaceutical formulations of irisin. In this study, we primarily focused on developing prototype SC formulations for the treatment of obesity. The SC route of administration has become a preferable route in terms of cost and convenience. Particularly considering the effect of irisin on browning SC WAT, the SC administration can provide localized delivery. Moreover, after the systemic absorption from the SC injection site, irisin may still exert positive effects on inducing brown adipocyte thermogenesis via restoring the function of interscapular brown adipose tissue (iBAT) [42]. Recent studies report that iBAT is present in humans and some mammals (i.e., mice), but its functions are impaired by high-fat-diet-induced obesity [42].

It is worth noting that E.coli has been utilized in this study to express irisin predominantly in a dimeric form, which is contributed by a continuous eight-stranded β-sheet dimerization interface stabilized by a combination of hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and hydrophobic interactions [32]. In contrast, irisin expressed by mammalian cells is glycosylated and could stay in monomer or dimer, perhaps impacted by glycosylation patterns and expression conditions [24, 32]. It is reported that dimeric irisin had a lower affinity to its receptor (integrin αVβ5) compared to monomeric irisin [24]. By performing some single-point mutations (i.e., mutating Arg-75 to Glu-75), the dimerization interface could be effectively disrupted to form non-glycosylated monomers with higher affinity to the receptor [24]. In future studies, it would be valuable to investigate the molecular modifications of native irisin to develop analogs with higher potency, as well as better in vivo stability and ADME properties.

In terms of formulation development, pH plays a critical role in irisin conformational and colloidal stability in solutions. At the pH range (i.e., 4 or 5) near to its pI value (theoretical irisin pI: 4.99), irisin became more neutral and its conformation tended to destabilize, including (i) the transition of some antiparallel β-sheet to α-helix or parallel β-sheet; (ii) the blue shift of fluorescence spectra indicating that the tryptophan residues were buried in more hydrophobic environments; and (iii) no detection of Tm by DSC indicating that irisin had already been partially unfolded/misfolded before subjected to heat. In addition, a substantial increase in HMW species was detected by SEC, implying that misfolded irisin molecules at these pHs could irreversibly form larger aggregates. It is not uncommon that pH can profoundly impact protein folding and aggregation. For instance, apomyoglobin, a small α-helix dominant globular protein, becomes a molten globule with the loss of its tertiary structure at pH 4 and is misfolded at pH 2 [43]. The prion protein (PrP) implicated in Parkinson’s disease tends to misfold and aggregate under acidic conditions [44]. In contrast, while pH was 2 units above the pI value, i.e., pH 7, irisin demonstrated acceptable conformational and colloidal stability profiles. For pH 7, phosphate was the selected buffer because it offered superior stability in our assays and was widely used in parenteral protein formulations with established regulatory precedence [45]. Similarly, many commercially available protein formulations also had a 1- to 3-unit difference between pH and pI to improve protein formulation stability and viscosity [46, 47]. For SC administration, if sufficiently stable, a solution in a prefilled syringe, autoinjector, or pump is preferred for convenience and ease of use compared to a lyophilized formulation that requires reconstitution. However, the relatively low thermal stability of irisin, indicated by its relatively low onset unfolding and Tm, posed a challenge for developing long-term stable aqueous formulations. Instead, the present study demonstrated that our developed lyophilized formulation retained irisin’s bioactivity, was suitable for SC injection, and exhibited adequate stability profiles throughout the accelerated stability studies. Consequently, the lyophilized form was selected as the prototype formulation. Sucrose and trehalose, two common lyoprotectants, were chosen for the lyophilized formulations, since they can effectively stabilize proteins during drying by replacing hydrogen bonding interactions with water and forming amorphous glassy matrices [29]. The 50 mg/mL (~ 5% w/v) was finalized for the lyoprotectant level in our formulations because it provided acceptable stability during lyophilization and exhibited practical viscosity and rapid reconstitution. Future studies may include further formulation optimization (dosing strength, further pH screening with a 0.1-unit interval around pH 7, excipient screening such as lyoprotectant type/concentration, and long-term stability studies, etc.) using the “Design-of-Experiment” approach, pilot-scale manufacturing process development for drug substances and formulations (irisin expression, filtration, and lyophilization at a pilot scale, etc.), as well as pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and long-term use safety studies on preclinical animals (normal and obese models). Considering the therapeutic potential of irisin and analogs for various diseases, it is beneficial to develop different formulations tailored to indications and administration routes. Furthermore, for indications such as obesity, developing co-formulations containing irisin with other drug(s) can be an attractive strategy for synergistic therapeutic benefits, improved patient compliance, and less risk of dosing errors.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed irisin formulations mainly tailored to irisin’s benefits on browning WAT and combating obesity. First, we expressed irisin from E.coli using two approaches (soluble expression and refolding from inclusion bodies) and developed purification methods. The harvested irisin in both forms had almost identical biophysical properties and could upregulate the UCP1 mRNA expression (an indicator of WAT browning). Second, we developed two SC lyophilized formulations, containing irisin, sucrose or trehalose, polysorbate 80, and sodium phosphate. The optimal stability of irisin was found at pH 7, which was selected for the solutions before lyophilization and after reconstitution. The developed lyophilized formulations could be reconstituted rapidly, and the reconstituted solutions retained irisin’s biophysical and bioactivity properties. After a 4-week accelerated stability study, irisin in the lyophilized formulations displayed acceptable conformational and colloidal stability. Overall, this study provides insight into developing prototype SC formulations for irisin, which is an important step in transforming irisin from a promising lead molecule into a real pharmaceutical product.
