Digital twin–based multi-scale framework for seismic integrity assessment of deep underground caverns
Authors: Abubakar Sharafat, Waqas Arshad Tanoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep underground caverns have attracted considerable interest in recent years due to their critical role in hydropower and energy infrastructure systems. However, reliable post-earthquake assessment remains challenging due to the complexity of multi-scale seismic response, uncertainty in rock mass properties, and difficulties in interpreting heterogeneous monitoring data. Despite the importance of seismic integrity assessment, there remains a paucity of systematic frameworks that integrate modeling and monitoring in a unified manner. This study therefore proposes a Digital Twin–driven multi-scale framework for seismic integrity assessment of underground caverns, integrating analytical, two-dimensional, and three-dimensional numerical modeling with real-time monitoring data. The aim of this study is to develop a comprehensive framework that enables consistent integration and interpretation of model predictions and monitoring observations. Within this framework, model outputs are defined as Project Intent Information, while monitoring data are defined as Project Status Information, allowing structured comparison through a unified data and knowledge ontology. This study introduces three performance metrics to assess the seismic response, namely the Seismic Integrity Index, Model Consistency Index, and Sensor Consistency Ratio, which collectively provide a quantitative basis for evaluating structural performance and model reliability. In addition, a rule-based multi-sensor consistency logic is developed to distinguish between local anomalies and global structural response, thereby improving the reliability of post-earthquake interpretation. Furthermore, a feedback-based model updating mechanism is implemented to refine numerical models using monitoring-informed interpretation within an integrated Digital Twin-based assessment framework. The proposed approach demonstrates the applicability of an integrated Digital Twin-based framework for model–monitoring-based seismic assessment under the investigated conditions. The results demonstrate that seismic-induced deformation remains limited under high confinement conditions, while the Digital Twin framework enables accurate interpretation of monitoring data and identification of local anomalies. Introduction

Underground space has become an essential component of modern infrastructure development, supporting applications such as hydropower generation, transportation systems, energy storage, and urban expansion1,2. With increasing surface constraints and environmental considerations, the utilization of deep underground space is steadily expanding worldwide3–5 . In particular, large underground caverns play a critical role in ensuring the safe and efficient operation of energy and water resource systems. Consequently, understanding the mechanical behavior and safety performance of these structures under various loading conditions, including seismic events, has become a key concern in underground engineering6–8. In recent years, there has been a growing body of research investigating the integration of Digital Twin concepts and data-driven approaches within geotechnical and underground engineering applications9,10 . The increasing availability of monitoring technologies, combined with advances in computational modeling, has enabled new opportunities for linking physical systems with their virtual representations11. Several studies have demonstrated that Digital Twin frameworks can effectively combine real-time monitoring data with numerical simulations to improve prediction accuracy and enhance the understanding of structural response in complex underground environments. In particular, applications in underground space and tunnelling engineering have shown that new innovative construction technologies and continuous data assimilation can significantly improve the reliability of deformation prediction and support system evaluation12–14.

Parallel to these developments, significant progress has been made in the application of advanced numerical methods for seismic analysis of underground structures15,16. Three-dimensional dynamic finite element simulations have been widely used to capture wave propagation, stress redistribution, and interaction between excavation geometry and surrounding rock mass17–19. These approaches provide detailed insight into seismic response mechanisms; however, they remain highly dependent on model assumptions and input parameters, particularly rock mass properties and boundary conditions20–22. As a result, uncertainties in model assumptions, rock mass properties, and boundary conditions continue to pose challenges for practical engineering assessment, especially when field validation is limited23,24. Recent studies have also emphasized the importance of integrating monitoring systems into underground engineering analysis25–27. Instrumentation such as extensometers, load cells, and convergence measurement systems provides valuable information on deformation behavior and support performance during both construction and operation. These datasets offer an opportunity to validate numerical models and improve understanding of system response under varying loading conditions. Nevertheless, the interpretation of monitoring data is often complicated by measurement noise, sensor-specific behavior, and localized effects that may not reflect the global structural condition. Consequently, discrepancies between different monitoring instruments can lead to ambiguity in assessing the actual performance of underground caverns.

In addition, emerging research has explored the incorporation of machine learning and data analytics techniques within Digital Twin frameworks. These approaches aim to enhance predictive capabilities, automate anomaly detection, and support decision-making processes. While such developments have shown promise, they often rely on large datasets and are not always directly applicable to geotechnical systems, where data availability may be limited and physical interpretation remains essential28,29. Therefore, purely data-driven approaches are typically insufficient without integration with physics-based models30,31. Despite these advances, current research efforts remain largely fragmented, with limited integration between multi-scale modeling, monitoring data, and systematic interpretation frameworks. Most existing Digital Twin applications in geotechnical engineering focus on specific aspects, such as real-time monitoring or numerical simulation, rather than providing a comprehensive methodology that unifies these components. In particular, the application of Digital Twin concepts to deep underground caverns under seismic loading conditions remains relatively underexplored. Furthermore, there is a lack of structured approaches for consistently comparing predicted and observed responses and translating these comparisons into quantitative performance evaluation and engineering decision-making.

To address this gap, the present study develops a Digital Twin-based framework for evaluating the seismic performance of deep underground caverns. The proposed approach establishes a unified structure in which numerical simulations and monitoring observations are treated within a consistent representation. Model-derived responses are defined as Project Intent Information (PII), while field measurements are treated as Project Status Information (PSI), enabling direct comparison between predicted and observed behavior. The framework integrates multiple modeling scales, including analytical solutions, two-dimensional finite element simulations, and three-dimensional dynamic analyses, together with field monitoring data obtained from extensometers, load cells, and convergence measurements. This integration enables a comprehensive representation of both expected and observed system response. To support quantitative interpretation, a set of performance measures is formulated to characterize structural response, model agreement, and data consistency. These measures are further used to interpret system behavior and distinguish between global response and localized effects. In addition, a feedback mechanism is incorporated to update the numerical models based on observed data, ensuring that the Digital Twin evolves in accordance with the actual system behavior. The applicability of the proposed framework is demonstrated through a case study of a deep underground hydropower cavern subjected to seismic loading conditions. The present study focuses on demonstrating the feasibility and consistency of the proposed framework under the investigated case conditions rather than establishing universal applicability across all seismic scenarios. The present study does not aim to establish a fully autonomous operational Digital Twin platform, but rather proposes an integrated framework that combines numerical modeling, monitoring interpretation, ontology-driven data organization, and feedback-based updating for seismic assessment of underground caverns. The combined use of modeling and monitoring data provides improved insight into system behavior and supports more reliable assessment of structural performance. Unlike conventional monitoring-oriented digital twin applications, the proposed framework emphasizes a closed-loop seismic assessment process that integrates numerical simulation, monitoring-based interpretation, and adaptive model verification. The framework establishes virtual–physical mapping relationships between field monitoring responses and simulation-derived integrity indicators, enabling multi-source interpretation of cavern stability during seismic events. In addition, PSI- and PII-based performance evaluation provides a unified mechanism for interpreting seismic integrity from both structural response and monitoring perspectives. Literature review

Seismic response of underground caverns

The seismic behavior of underground caverns has been extensively investigated due to its importance in the design and safety evaluation of subsurface infrastructure systems. In contrast to surface structures, underground openings are generally subjected to reduced seismic amplification due to confinement provided by the surrounding rock mass. This confinement effect limits deformation, particularly in deep underground environments32–34. Analytical approaches such as free-field deformation methods have been widely used to estimate seismic-induced strain based on wave propagation characteristics and rock mass properties35,36. Previous studies indicate that the deformation response of underground caverns is governed by stiffness contrast, excavation geometry, and seismic wave characteristics37,38. Investigations on seismic wave coherence and fault mechanics further highlight the role of wave orientation and subsurface conditions in controlling seismic response39–41. In addition, studies on layered seabed response and wave interaction mechanisms demonstrate the complexity of seismic wave propagation in heterogeneous media38,42. These findings suggest that seismic response in underground systems is highly dependent on both geological heterogeneity and wave characteristics, introducing uncertainty in predictive approaches.

Numerical modeling approaches for seismic analysis

Numerical modeling has become a fundamental tool for analyzing the seismic response of underground structures. Finite element methods are widely applied to simulate stress redistribution, deformation behavior, and excavation–rock interaction43,44. Two-dimensional pseudo-static approaches provide simplified representations for design-level assessments, while three-dimensional dynamic simulations allow detailed analysis of wave propagation and structural interaction45,46. Recent developments have incorporated advanced numerical and hybrid techniques. Discrete element methods and fracture-based simulations have been used to capture failure processes in rock masses23,47,48. Studies on creep damage and microstructural evolution have further improved understanding of rock behavior under complex loading49,50. Additionally, data-driven and hybrid models have been introduced to enhance prediction accuracy51–53. Despite these advancements, numerical models remain highly sensitive to input parameters and often lack sufficient calibration with field observations, limiting their reliability in real-world applications.

Monitoring systems in underground engineering

Monitoring systems play a critical role in capturing the actual behavior of underground caverns during construction and operation. Instrumentation such as extensometers, load cells, and convergence measurement systems provides valuable insight into deformation evolution and support performance54,55. Advances in sensing technologies, including self-sensing materials and distributed monitoring systems, have significantly improved data acquisition capabilities56. Recent developments in sensor-based monitoring and early warning systems have enabled real-time assessment of underground conditions. In addition, technologies such as UAV photogrammetry and 3D laser scanning have enhanced the ability to characterize rock mass discontinuities and deformation patterns. However, the increasing volume and complexity of monitoring data introduce significant challenges in interpretation, particularly when measurements from different instruments exhibit inconsistencies. Localized effects, installation conditions, and sensor behavior may lead to discrepancies that do not necessarily represent global structural response.

Digital twin applications in geotechnical engineering

Digital Twin technology has emerged as a promising approach for integrating physical systems with their virtual counterparts through continuous data exchange and model updating57,58. In geotechnical engineering, Digital Twins have been applied to deformation prediction, monitoring integration, and infrastructure management59. Recent digital twin research in underground engineering has rapidly progressed from conceptual discussion to tunnel-specific implementation60,61. Recent studies have addressed DT-based tunnel construction safety early warning, status twins for excavation quality monitoring, smart metro service platforms for long-term tunnel structural performance, digital twin architectures for urban underground space, geological-condition prediction through “Data–Model–Decision” loops, and data-driven smart tunnel maintenance62,63. These studies collectively show that digital twins are increasingly being used to integrate multi-source sensing, virtual–physical mapping, simulation, and decision support across the construction and operation stages of underground infrastructure64,65. Applications in tunnelling and underground systems have demonstrated the potential of combining real-time monitoring with numerical modeling to improve system understanding and performance prediction66–68. Recent studies have explored the integration of machine learning and artificial intelligence within Digital Twin frameworks to enhance predictive capability and automate anomaly detection69,70. Generative models and deep learning approaches have also been applied in seismic exploration and subsurface modeling71. Parallel work in civil-infrastructure structural health monitoring has emphasised that effective digital twins require more than visualisation or digital shadows; they depend on bidirectional interaction, monitoring-informed model consistency, and explicit updating mechanisms72,73. Recent SHM-oriented reviews and application studies have highlighted the importance of sensing architecture, digital shadow-to-twin transition, multi-dimension model updating, Bayesian real-time updating of geotechnical responses, digital-physical fusion, and automated model-update strategies74–76. However, most existing studies still focus on construction monitoring, routine operation and maintenance, or generic structural health monitoring. Against this background, the present study is positioned as a Digital Twin framework specifically for seismic integrity assessment of underground caverns, combining numerical simulation, field monitoring, model updating, and performance-state evaluation within a closed-loop workflow. Furthermore, data assimilation techniques and adaptive monitoring systems have been proposed to improve real-time assessment and early warning capabilities. Despite these advances, previous studies have investigated digital twins for infrastructure monitoring, numerical simulation, and underground asset management, most existing frameworks primarily focus on visualization, operational monitoring, or isolated structural analysis. Limited research has addressed the integration of multi-scale seismic simulation, monitoring-informed adaptive updating, and performance-oriented integrity interpretation within a unified Digital Twin framework for deep underground caverns. Furthermore, the interaction mechanism between field monitoring observations, virtual numerical representations, and seismic integrity decision logic remains insufficiently developed in existing underground Digital Twin applications.

Research gap and motivation

Based on the above review, significant progress has been made in understanding seismic response, developing numerical modeling techniques, and implementing monitoring systems for underground caverns. However, these approaches are generally applied independently, and their integration remains limited. In particular, there is a lack of unified frameworks that combine multi-scale numerical modeling with multi-sensor monitoring data for seismic integrity assessment. Moreover, existing studies do not provide a structured methodology for systematically comparing predicted and observed responses or for quantifying discrepancies between them. The absence of such frameworks makes it difficult to evaluate model reliability and interpret monitoring data consistently. This limitation is particularly critical for deep underground caverns, where seismic response is subtle and highly influenced by both geological conditions and measurement uncertainty. To address these limitations, this study proposes a Digital Twin–based framework that integrates modeling and monitoring within a unified architecture. By introducing structured data and knowledge ontologies and defining quantitative performance metrics, the proposed approach enables consistent comparison between model predictions and field observations, thereby supporting reliable seismic integrity assessment.

Digital twin framework for underground seismic integrity

Concept and architecture of the digital twin

The proposed Digital Twin (DT) framework integrates physical systems, monitoring data, multi-scale numerical modeling, and decision-making into a unified architecture for seismic integrity assessment of underground caverns. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the framework consists of a physical entity layer, a Digital Twin core, and a virtual entity layer, which are interconnected through monitoring-informed updating data exchange and model updating. The physical entity layer represents the real underground system, including the rock mass, cavern geometry, support system, and monitoring infrastructure. These components define the physical domain of the problem and provide the basis for both data acquisition and numerical representation. The monitoring system, comprising extensometers, load cells, and convergence measurements, captures the structural response of the cavern during construction and seismic loading.

Multi-scale Digital Twin architecture integrating physical, virtual, and decision layers for underground seismic integrity assessment.

Within the Digital Twin core, the data layer organizes and manages monitoring observations and numerical simulation outputs through a structured data ontology. Monitoring data are treated as observations of system behavior and correspond to PSI. These data are associated with spatial locations within the cavern and temporal evolution, and are stored in external databases or structured repositories. The ontology maintains explicit links between the monitoring entities and their storage sources, enabling efficient access and integration of large-scale time-dependent datasets. In parallel, the multi-scale modeling layer generates PII through analytical free-field deformation models, two-dimensional pseudo-static finite element models, and three-dimensional dynamic time-history simulations. These models provide predictions of displacement, stress, and strain fields under seismic loading and are linked to the same physical entities as the monitoring data. Similar to monitoring data, model outputs are stored externally, while the ontology defines their relationships to model configurations, input parameters, and physical components.

A key feature of the data ontology is the explicit mapping between PII and PSI, which enables direct comparison between predicted and observed system responses. This comparison is implemented within the Digital Twin core through a consistency check and validation process, forming the basis for quantitative evaluation. Building upon this data structure, a knowledge ontology is introduced to transform integrated data into engineering interpretation, as illustrated in Fig. 2. At the core of the knowledge ontology is the PerformanceMetric entity, which represents quantitative indicators derived from the PII–PSI comparison. These include the Seismic Integrity Index (SI), Model Consistency Index (MCI), and Sensor Consistency Ratio (SCR), which respectively quantify structural demand, model reliability, and sensor consistency.

Knowledge ontology linking performance metrics (SI, MCI, SCR) with structural, sensor, and model states to support decision-making and Digital Twin updating.

The performance metrics are mapped to three categories of system states. The StructuralState represents the condition of the underground cavern based on deformation and strain response and is classified into elastic, minor deformation, significant deformation, and limit states. The SensorState evaluates the consistency of monitoring data and distinguishes between consistent response, inconsistent response, and localized anomalies. The ModelState reflects the agreement between numerical predictions and observed behavior, categorizing models as valid, partially consistent, or inconsistent. These state representations are integrated within the ontology to derive the DecisionEntity, which defines the required engineering action. Depending on the evaluated conditions, the system is classified into safe operation, monitoring required, further investigation, or emergency condition. This structured reasoning process enables the transformation of heterogeneous monitoring and modeling data into actionable engineering decisions.

A defining characteristic of the proposed framework is the incorporation of a feedback mechanism linking the decision layer to the modeling layer. The DecisionEntity is connected to the ModelingEntity through an updating relationship, allowing refinement of model parameters based on observed system behavior. In this manner, monitoring data collected during construction and seismic events are used to calibrate rock mass properties, boundary conditions, and model assumptions, ensuring that the Digital Twin remains representative of the physical system. The overall Digital Twin workflow can therefore be described as a iterative synchronization process in which monitoring data and model predictions are integrated, compared, and evaluated to generate performance metrics, which are then interpreted through the knowledge ontology to support decision-making and model updating. This closed-loop process ensures synchronization between the physical and virtual systems and enables adaptive and reliable seismic assessment.

By integrating architecture, data ontology, and knowledge ontology within a single framework, the proposed approach provides a systematic and transparent methodology for seismic integrity assessment of underground caverns. Unlike conventional approaches that treat modeling and monitoring independently, the Digital Twin establishes a direct linkage between predicted and observed behavior, enabling quantitative validation, identification of local anomalies, and feedback-based model refinement for improvement of model reliability. This integration transforms the Digital Twin into an intelligent, knowledge-driven system capable of supporting engineering decision-making under seismic conditions.

Multi-scale modeling strategy within the digital twin

The seismic response of deep underground caverns is governed by mechanisms that span multiple spatial and physical scales. No single modeling approach can adequately capture all relevant aspects of this behavior. Therefore, a multi-scale modeling strategy is adopted within the DT framework to balance computational efficiency, physical realism, and predictive reliability. At the first level, analytical free-field deformation models provide rapid estimation of seismic strain based on wave propagation characteristics. These models are computationally efficient and suitable for preliminary screening and early-stage assessment immediately following a seismic event. However, they neglect excavation-induced stress redistribution and structural interaction effects. At the second level, two-dimensional pseudo-static finite element models incorporate excavation stages, in-situ stress conditions, and rock mass nonlinearity. These models can be calibrated using monitoring data obtained during construction and operation, thereby providing a more realistic representation of cavern-scale deformation behavior under seismic loading. At the third level, three-dimensional dynamic finite element models simulate time-dependent seismic response, including wave propagation, rock–structure interaction, and stress redistribution around complex cavern geometries. These models provide the highest fidelity but require significant computational resources and detailed input parameters.

Within the DT framework, these three modeling scales are not used independently but are integrated through a centralized state estimation process. The analytical model provides rapid initial predictions, the 2D model offers calibrated intermediate response, and the 3D model delivers high-resolution dynamic behavior. Consistency across these scales enhances confidence in the predicted response and enables robust seismic integrity assessment.

Adaptive model updating mechanism

The Digital Twin framework incorporates an adaptive model updating procedure to maintain consistency between numerical predictions and observed monitoring response. The updating process is formulated as an inverse analysis problem in which selected rock mass parameters are iteratively adjusted to minimize discrepancies between predicted and measured deformation behavior.

Initial model parameters were derived from geological investigations, laboratory testing, empirical rock mass characterization, and previously reported design analyses. During the updating process, coupling between stiffness reduction and deformation redistribution was evaluated to ensure physically consistent model behavior.

The updated parameters were subsequently incorporated into the Digital Twin state representation, enabling monitoring-informed refinement of the analytical and numerical models. This iterative calibration process improves consistency between Project Intent Information (PII) and Project Status Information (PSI), thereby enhancing the reliability of seismic integrity assessment and future response prediction.

Within the proposed Digital Twin framework, PSI and PII serve complementary roles for interpreting seismic integrity from both numerical simulation and monitoring perspectives. PSI primarily represents the simulated structural response state obtained from analytical, two-dimensional, and three-dimensional numerical analyses under seismic loading conditions. The PSI evaluation incorporates stress redistribution, deformation response, strain concentration, and predicted integrity behavior derived from the virtual numerical environment.

In contrast, PII represents the monitoring-informed physical integrity condition of the underground cavern based on field observations obtained from convergence measurements, extensometers, and load cells. The PII evaluation reflects the measured structural response characteristics and monitoring consistency during seismic events. The interaction between PSI and PII is established through a virtual–physical mapping mechanism in which monitoring observations are associated with corresponding numerical response parameters. Simulated displacement, stress, and strain responses are compared with measured monitoring behavior to evaluate consistency between the virtual model and the physical system. Discrepancies between PSI and PII are subsequently used to support model verification, adaptive updating, and seismic integrity interpretation within the closed-loop Digital Twin framework.

Methodology for multi-scale seismic modeling

The proposed Digital Twin framework integrates analytical, two-dimensional (2D), and three-dimensional (3D) modeling approaches to evaluate the seismic response of deep underground caverns. The methodology is formulated to capture seismic behavior across multiple spatial and physical scales while maintaining a balance between computational efficiency and modeling fidelity. At the first level, analytical free-field deformation models are used to estimate seismic strain induced by wave propagation. At the second level, 2D pseudo-static finite element models simulate excavation-induced stress redistribution and cavern-scale deformation, incorporating in-situ stress conditions and rock mass behavior. At the third level, 3D dynamic finite element models simulate time-dependent seismic response, including wave propagation and rock–structure interaction. These modeling scales are integrated within the DT framework through a hierarchical workflow. The analytical model provides rapid initial screening, the 2D model refines predictions under calibrated conditions, and the 3D model provides high-resolution dynamic response. Cross-scale consistency is evaluated, and discrepancies between model predictions and monitoring data are used for interpretation and adaptive updating.

Two-dimensional pseudo-static finite element modeling

A two-dimensional plane strain finite element model is developed to represent the transverse section of an underground cavern (Fig. 5). The model domain is defined to extend sufficiently beyond the excavation boundary to minimize boundary effects. Boundary conditions typically include a fixed base, laterally constrained vertical boundaries, and a free surface at the top. Initial stress conditions are defined based on overburden depth D and in-situ stress ratios.

Two-dimensional pseudo-static finite element: (a) initial in-situ stress field applied to the rock mass; (b) representation of cavern geometry and staged excavation within the numerical model; (c) horizontal displacement distribution after excavation-induced stress redistribution; and (d) horizontal displacement distribution under pseudo-static seismic loading.

The excavation process is simulated in stages to capture progressive stress redistribution and deformation. Support systems, such as shotcrete lining and reinforcement elements, are incorporated at appropriate stages to represent construction sequences. Material properties of the rock mass are defined using elastic or elasto-plastic models, depending on the level of analysis. The model is calibrated using field monitoring data, such as convergence measurements, by adjusting stiffness and strength parameters to match observed deformation behavior.

Seismic loading is applied using a pseudo-static approach, where equivalent acceleration coefficients are imposed on the model to represent inertial effects. This approach provides a computationally efficient approximation of seismic loading. Sensitivity analyses are performed by varying rock mass stiffness and strength parameters to evaluate the influence of degraded conditions on cavern response. The results provide estimates of deformation increments under different seismic and material scenarios.

Three-dimensional dynamic finite element modeling

A three-dimensional finite element model is developed to represent the full geometry of the underground cavern and surrounding rock mass. The three-dimensional finite element model incorporates detailed mesh discretization, boundary conditions, contact interfaces, and layered geological representation, as illustrated in Fig. 6. The model domain is extended sufficiently in all directions to minimize boundary effects and allow realistic wave propagation. A refined mesh is used near the cavern to capture stress concentration and interaction effects, while a coarser mesh is applied in the far field to optimize computational efficiency.

Three-dimensional dynamic finite element model used for seismic analysis of the underground cavern: (a) finite element mesh and excavation representation, (b) boundary conditions and seismic input configuration, (c) rock–structure contact interface modeling, and (d) layered rock mass domain.

The rock mass is modeled as a continuum material, and structural components such as lining systems are explicitly represented. Interface elements may be used to simulate contact behavior between rock and structural components. To simulate seismic wave propagation, non-reflective (absorbing) boundary conditions are applied at model boundaries. Seismic loading is introduced as time-history acceleration, typically applied at the base or boundaries of the model. Input motions are processed to ensure compatibility with site-specific conditions, including deconvolution to represent free-field response at depth. Dynamic analysis provides time-dependent response, including displacement, stress, and interaction behavior.

Sensitivity and uncertainty analysis

Sensitivity analyses are conducted to evaluate the influence of key parameters on seismic response. These include variation in wave velocity, rock mass stiffness, and seismic input intensity. Reduced wave velocity represents degraded rock mass conditions and leads to increased strain demand, while reduced stiffness affects deformation distribution around the cavern. These analyses provide bounds on expected response and support uncertainty quantification.

Integration with monitoring data within the digital twin

The Digital Twin framework integrates numerical modeling with field monitoring data to provide a consistent representation of structural behavior. Monitoring systems typically include convergence measurements, extensometers, and load cells installed within the cavern. Numerical models are calibrated using monitoring data, and consistency among different measurement systems is evaluated. Discrepancies between model predictions and measurements are interpreted to distinguish between global structural response and localized anomalies. This integration enables adaptive updating of model parameters and supports reliable seismic integrity assessment. The combined use of multi-scale modeling and monitoring data forms the foundation of the Digital Twin methodology.

Practical implementation and case study

The proposed Digital Twin framework is demonstrated using a deep underground hydropower powerhouse cavern constructed under significant overburden in a mountainous geological setting. The selected case represents a large-span underground excavation subjected to high in-situ stress conditions and seismic loading, providing a suitable testbed for evaluating the applicability of the proposed multi-scale methodology. The cavern is located at an elevation of approximately EL 600 m, while the surrounding ground surface reaches elevations of approximately EL 1050–1060 m, resulting in an overburden of approximately 450 m. This depth induces a high confinement environment, where the in-situ stress field is primarily governed by gravitational loading. The corresponding vertical stress is on the order of 12 MPa, which significantly influences both static stability and dynamic response characteristics.

The underground complex comprises a main powerhouse cavern and associated auxiliary openings, including transformer caverns, busbar tunnels, and access galleries (Fig. 7). The main cavern is characterized by a large span and height with a typical geometry consisting of near-vertical sidewalls and a curved crown. The presence of adjacent openings introduces geometric complexity and interaction effects that influence stress redistribution and deformation patterns, particularly under seismic excitation. The surrounding rock mass consists predominantly of hard, moderately jointed rock with relatively high stiffness (Table 2). For modeling purposes, the rock mass is represented as an equivalent continuum with elastic properties derived from site investigations and design analyses. A representative Young’s modulus on the order of several tens of gigapascals is adopted, consistent with values used in the numerical models. To account for potential variability and degradation, variations in wave velocity and stiffness are considered in subsequent sensitivity analyses.

The approach assumes that the deformation of the underground cavern is governed by shear wave propagation through the rock mass, neglecting excavation-induced stress redistribution and structural interaction effects. Despite its simplicity, this method is widely used for preliminary seismic screening of underground structures.

Two-dimensional pseudo-static FEM response

The two-dimensional pseudo-static finite element model was developed to simulate excavation-induced stress redistribution and to evaluate the additional effects of seismic loading on cavern deformation (Fig. 9). The model incorporates staged excavation, in-situ stress conditions, and equivalent continuum representation of the rock mass based on the parameters summarized in Table 2. The initial stress field corresponds to gravitational loading due to overburden, with a vertical stress of approximately 12.26 MPa, consistent with a burial depth of approximately 450 m. The model domain was selected sufficiently large to minimize boundary effects, and appropriate boundary conditions were applied to represent deep underground confinement. Seismic loading was introduced using a pseudo-static approach, where horizontal acceleration corresponding to the Maximum Design Earthquake (PGA ≈ 0.62 g) was applied as an equivalent body force. This approach provides a conservative estimate of seismic effects while maintaining computational efficiency.

The model was calibrated using field monitoring data obtained during excavation, ensuring that the simulated deformation behavior is consistent with observed convergence measurements. To evaluate the influence of rock mass conditions on seismic response, two representative cases were considered: (i) Calibrated rock mass condition (based on monitoring data); (ii) Extremely weak rock mass condition (conservative scenario).

As shown in Table 4, the additional deformation induced by seismic loading is negligible compared to the static convergence associated with excavation. For the calibrated rock mass condition, the increase in convergence is less than 0.2%, indicating that seismic effects contribute only a minor increment to the overall deformation. Even under extremely weak rock mass conditions, the relative increase in convergence remains limited, generally not exceeding approximately 1.36%. Although the absolute deformation is larger in this conservative scenario, the contribution of seismic loading remains small compared to excavation-induced deformation. The results indicate that deformation induced by seismic loading is spatially confined and does not lead to large-scale instability. The stress redistribution remains localized, reflecting the high confinement conditions associated with deep underground caverns. Overall, the analysis demonstrates that excavation-induced deformation governs the mechanical response of the cavern, while seismic loading introduces only a minor incremental effect. These findings are consistent across both calibrated and conservative modeling scenarios, providing confidence in the robustness of the two-dimensional pseudo-static modeling approach.

Three-dimensional dynamic FEM response

The three-dimensional dynamic finite element model was developed to simulate the time-dependent seismic response of the underground powerhouse cavern, incorporating wave propagation effects, rock–structure interaction, and complex cavern geometry. This model represents the highest level of fidelity within the proposed multi-scale Digital Twin framework. The numerical model was constructed using a three-dimensional representation of the powerhouse cavern and surrounding rock mass. The model domain was extended sufficiently in all directions to minimize boundary effects and ensure realistic simulation of seismic wave propagation. The rock mass was modeled using equivalent continuum properties derived from site investigations, with representative elastic modulus values on the order of 30–33 GPa, consistent with the intact rock properties summarized in Table 2.

Dynamic boundary conditions were implemented using non-reflective (absorbing) boundaries to prevent artificial reflection of seismic waves at the model edges. Seismic loading was introduced through deconvolved acceleration time histories, representing both Operating Basis Earthquake (OBE) and Maximum Design Earthquake (MDE) scenarios. The peak ground acceleration for the MDE case was approximately 0.62 g, consistent with the input used in the analytical and two-dimensional models. The interaction between the rock mass and the support system, including shotcrete lining and reinforcement elements, was incorporated through appropriate contact and interface formulations, allowing for realistic simulation of load transfer and deformation compatibility under dynamic loading. The deformation is primarily concentrated around the cavern boundary, particularly near the crown and sidewall regions, while the surrounding rock mass exhibits relatively uniform and low-magnitude displacement. The maximum displacement is localized and remains on the order of a few millimeters, indicating that the response is limited despite the applied seismic loading. The deformation pattern reflects the strong confinement provided by the surrounding rock mass, which effectively restricts the propagation of seismic-induced displacement into the cavern. The absence of significant deformation amplification or stress concentration suggests that the structural system remains stable under dynamic loading conditions. The time-dependent response indicates that peak displacements and stresses occur during short-duration transient phases of seismic excitation and rapidly decay due to material damping and geometric confinement. The use of deconvolved input motions ensures that the applied loading is representative of in-situ conditions, avoiding overestimation associated with surface-level ground motion records.

Within the Digital Twin framework, the three-dimensional model provides high-fidelity simulation results that complement the analytical and two-dimensional predictions. While computationally intensive, this model serves as a reference for validating simplified approaches and capturing complex interactions that cannot be represented in lower-scale models. Overall, the results confirm that the seismic response of deep underground caverns is governed primarily by the stiffness and confinement of the surrounding rock mass. The predicted displacements and stress changes remain within acceptable limits, indicating that seismic loading does not significantly compromise the structural integrity of the cavern.

Digital twin adaptive updating

This updating process is done through the back-calculation procedure applied to the case study cavern. In the initial modeling phase, rock mass properties were assigned based on design-phase investigations, with an overall elastic modulus of approximately 32.8 GPa derived from laboratory testing and site characterization. Following excavation monitoring, convergence data recorded at the J-K level (EL 611) and P-Q level (EL 602) were used to iteratively calibrate the model parameters. The back-calculated calibrated rock mass yielded a reduced overall modulus of 20 GPa with peak cohesion of 6.4 MPa and peak friction angle of 29.5°, representing the updated Digital Twin state after integration of field observations. A further sensitivity update was performed using an extremely weak rock mass scenario (E = 15 GPa, cohesion = 2 MPa) to bound the uncertainty range. The ratio of predicted to measured convergence at the J-K level approximately 120 mm predicted versus greater than 137 mm observed at the final excavation stage indicates a stiffness correction factor of approximately 1.14, which is consistent with the updating formula above and reflects slight underestimation of rock mass deformability in the initial model. This updating process allows the DT to evolve with time and improve predictive accuracy under repeated or future seismic events.

A significant earthquake event was recorded during the operational phase of the underground powerhouse, providing a unique opportunity to evaluate the seismic response of the cavern under real loading conditions. The earthquake had a moment magnitude of approximately 7.5, with an epicentral distance of approximately 350 km from the project site and a focal depth of approximately 200–220 km (Fig. 11) 79. Despite the large magnitude of the earthquake, the significant distance and depth resulted in attenuated ground motion at the site. However, the event was sufficiently strong to produce measurable responses in the monitoring system installed within the powerhouse cavern.

Monitoring system and data acquisition

A multi-instrument monitoring system was installed in the underground powerhouse cavern to capture deformation and support response during construction and operation. The monitoring system forms the primary data source for the Digital Twin framework and supports the monitoring-informed calibration and verification process of the proposed seismic integrity assessment methodology. The instrumentation included 52 three-point vibrating wire extensometers for measuring internal rock mass deformation, 12 vibrating wire load cells (2 in the powerhouse cavern, 8 in busbar tunnels, and 2 in bonneted gate niches) for monitoring support force development, and convergence measurement stations for tracking relative displacement across the cavern. In addition, eight convergence arrays were strategically installed along the 137 m length of the powerhouse cavern, covering critical structural zones including the crown, sidewalls, and invert to comprehensively capture deformation behavior during excavation and operation.

All instruments were connected to a centralized and semi-automated data acquisition system. Measurements from extensometers and load cells were collected using digital vibrating wire readout units (e.g., BGK-405), enabling automatic logging of timestamps and instrument IDs. Instrument cables were routed to dedicated panel boards located within the powerhouse control room and transformer hall access gallery, facilitating efficient and secure data collection. The acquired monitoring data were subsequently transferred to central computing systems for processing, interpretation, and comparison with analytical and numerical simulation results within the Digital Twin environment.

Convergence measurements were initially obtained using reflective targets and total station surveys. Due to visibility limitations caused by structural and electro-mechanical installations during later construction stages, the monitoring system was upgraded to a high-precision 3D prism-based monitoring system consisting of 24 prism targets installed across six monitoring sections and two fixed reference points at the powerhouse end wall. This upgrade enabled improved three-dimensional displacement tracking and enhanced monitoring-informed updating within the Digital Twin framework. Monitoring observations were collected periodically throughout construction and operation, while convergence measurements were typically obtained at regular weekly intervals depending on excavation progress and observed rock mass behavior.It is noted that load cells installed without pre-tension initially recorded negligible values until sufficient deformation mobilized the support system, which is consistent with standard underground monitoring behavior under field conditions. Within the proposed Digital Twin framework, convergence data were primarily used for calibration and consistency verification of the numerical seismic models, while extensometer and load cell measurements provided independent validation of internal rock mass deformation behavior and support system response.

Observed earthquake response

The response of the underground powerhouse cavern during the recorded earthquake event was evaluated using data from convergence measurements, extensometers, and load cells. The multi-instrument monitoring system provided a basis for assessing both boundary-level observations and internal rock mass behavior. Post-earthquake review of the monitoring records showed that extensometer measurements remained essentially unchanged before and after the event, indicating that internal deformation within the rock mass was negligible. Likewise, load cell data did not show any significant increase in axial force, suggesting that the support system did not experience additional loading attributable to seismic excitation.

Although some convergence records initially appeared to indicate sudden displacement changes at selected stations, detailed engineering review showed that these values were not representative of global cavern deformation. The anomalous readings were attributed to local effects such as shotcrete spalling, anchor instability, or measurement uncertainty, rather than actual rock mass movement. Taken together, the monitoring evidence indicates that the earthquake did not induce significant global deformation in the underground powerhouse cavern. The stability of extensometer and load cell records confirms that both the rock mass and support system remained essentially unaffected by the event.

Discussion

The results obtained from analytical modeling, numerical simulations, and field monitoring provide a consistent and engineering-relevant understanding of the seismic performance of the underground powerhouse cavern. From an engineering perspective, the analyses demonstrate that seismic-induced deformation is negligible compared to excavation-induced deformation. The two-dimensional pseudo-static model indicates that the additional deformation due to seismic loading remains below approximately 0.2% for calibrated rock mass conditions and below 1.5% for conservative weak rock scenarios, while the three-dimensional dynamic analysis shows displacement levels on the order of millimeters. These findings confirm that the structural response is dominated by excavation effects rather than seismic loading.

The field monitoring data recorded during the earthquake event further support this conclusion. Extensometer measurements indicate negligible internal rock mass deformation, and load cell readings show no significant increase in support forces. This confirms that the global structural system remained stable during seismic excitation. Apparent anomalies observed in convergence measurements were not representative of actual deformation and were attributed to localized effects at the excavation boundary. The comparison between analytical, two-dimensional, and three-dimensional models shows that, despite differences in modeling assumptions and complexity, all approaches lead to consistent predictions of limited seismic response. Specifically, the analytical free-field method using the Wang77 formulation gives a maximum axial strain of 1.21 × 10−4 under MDE loading, representing an SI of 0.04 well within the elastic performance range. The two-dimensional pseudo-static finite element analysis, calibrated against convergence monitoring at J-K and P-Q levels, gives an additional earthquake-induced convergence of 0.21 mm, corresponding to 0.175% of the total static convergence of 120 mm. The three-dimensional dynamic finite element model, which incorporates the full cavern geometry, rock–structure interaction, and time-history loading, yields peak displacements on the order of a few millimeters, localized at the cavern boundary and rapidly decaying with distance. The fact that three methodologically distinct approaches spanning from closed-form analytical solutions to fully coupled three-dimensional dynamic simulations produce results that are mutually consistent and individually below threshold values constitutes strong evidence for the robustness of the seismic performance assessment. This cross-scale agreement is the physical basis for the MCI value approaching unity reported in Table 6. The analytical free-field model provides a rapid estimation of strain, the two-dimensional model captures excavation-induced behavior and allows calibration using monitoring data, and the three-dimensional dynamic model represents detailed wave propagation and interaction effects. This multi-scale consistency enhances confidence in the modeling results and supports their application in engineering practice.

Unlike conventional monitoring-oriented Digital Twin implementations, the proposed framework emphasizes monitoring-informed seismic integrity interpretation through iterative interaction between field observations, numerical simulation, and performance-based evaluation indices. A key engineering implication of this study is that deep underground caverns exhibit inherently stable behavior under seismic loading due to high confinement and rock mass stiffness. This suggests that conventional design approaches based solely on conservative analytical methods may overestimate seismic demand if not supported by calibrated numerical models and field observations. The integration of monitoring data within the Digital Twin framework provides a practical approach for improving reliability in both design and operation. During construction, convergence data are used to calibrate model parameters, ensuring that the numerical model reflects actual rock mass behavior. During operation, multi-sensor monitoring enables validation of model predictions and supports reliable interpretation of structural response following seismic events.

The use of quantitative evaluation metrics further strengthens this approach. The Seismic Integrity Index confirms that strain levels remain within acceptable limits, the Model Consistency Index demonstrates agreement between modeling and monitoring results, and the Sensor Consistency Ratio provides a clear basis for distinguishing between local anomalies and global structural response. Together, these metrics provide a structured and objective framework for decision-making. From a practical standpoint, the Digital Twin framework enables rapid post-earthquake assessment without relying on single-sensor interpretation, reducing the risk of false alarms and unnecessary operational interruptions. This is particularly important for critical infrastructure such as hydropower facilities, where operational continuity is essential. The current validation is based on a single deep underground powerhouse cavern subjected to a far-field, deep-focus earthquake event, for which the recorded structural response remained relatively small. Therefore, while the proposed framework demonstrates consistency between modeling and monitoring under low-to-moderate seismic response conditions, further validation under stronger seismic events, near-field ground motions, more complex geological environments, and higher damage states is required to fully evaluate the robustness and transferability of the framework.

Compared with conventional seismic assessment approaches that primarily rely on isolated numerical simulations or standalone monitoring interpretation, the proposed Digital Twin framework enables integrated interaction between monitoring observations and multi-scale numerical response analysis (Table 7). Conventional approaches often evaluate structural response either from predictive simulation or post-event monitoring independently, whereas the proposed framework establishes a monitoring-informed closed-loop interpretation process through adaptive comparison between virtual and physical system responses. This integration improves the interpretability of monitoring observations, supports model verification, and enhances the reliability of seismic integrity assessment for underground cavern systems. This study demonstrates that the combination of multi-scale modeling, monitoring-based calibration, and Digital Twin-based interpretation provides a robust and transferable methodology for seismic assessment of deep underground caverns. Although the proposed framework incorporates feedback-based updating and model-monitoring integration, the current implementation remains primarily an offline or semi-online assessment workflow. Future developments may focus on real-time data streaming, automated data assimilation, continuous state updating, and operational deployment of fully autonomous Digital Twin systems. The approach is applicable to a wide range of underground infrastructure systems and can support both design optimization and operational decision-making in seismic regions. Future research may focus on integrating hidden-state reconstruction and missing-information inference techniques to support Digital Twin initialization and calibration under limited monitoring conditions80,81. In addition, physics-informed and object-specific data-driven models may improve seismic response prediction and physical consistency across different underground environments82–84. The incorporation of transfer learning, meta-learning, and domain adaptation strategies may further enhance the adaptability and transferability of Digital Twin frameworks across different geological settings, seismic conditions, and monitoring datasets85.

Conclusion

This study presented a Digital Twin–based framework for the seismic integrity assessment of deep underground caverns, integrating multi-scale numerical modeling with field monitoring data within a unified architecture. The framework combines analytical, two-dimensional, and three-dimensional modeling approaches with real-time observations, enabling a consistent representation of both predicted and observed system behavior.

A key contribution of this work lies in the development of a structured data and knowledge ontology that enables systematic integration and interpretation of heterogeneous data sources. By defining model outputs as Project Intent Information (PII) and monitoring data as Project Status Information (PSI), the framework establishes a clear basis for comparing numerical predictions with field observations. This comparison is further quantified through the introduction of three performance metrics, namely the Seismic Integrity Index (SI), Model Consistency Index (MCI), and Sensor Consistency Ratio (SCR), which collectively provide a comprehensive assessment of structural performance, model reliability, and sensor consistency. The application of the proposed framework to a deep underground hydropower cavern demonstrated that seismic-induced deformation remains limited under high confinement conditions, confirming the relatively stable behavior of deep underground structures under seismic loading. The results also showed that the integration of multi-sensor monitoring data with numerical models enables reliable interpretation of structural response, particularly in distinguishing between localized anomalies and global deformation behavior. Furthermore, the incorporation of a feedback-based updating mechanism allows the Digital Twin to iteratively refine numerical models based on observed data. This capability enhances the reliability of predictions and supports adaptive assessment of structural performance throughout the lifecycle of the underground system.

The findings of this study highlight the importance of integrating modeling and monitoring within a unified Digital Twin framework for underground seismic assessment. The proposed approach provides a systematic methodology for linking numerical simulation, field observations, and engineering decision-making, thereby improving the robustness and transparency of post-earthquake evaluation. While the framework demonstrates significant potential, several limitations remain. The accuracy of the Digital Twin depends on the quality and spatial coverage of monitoring data, as well as the assumptions inherent in numerical modeling. Future work may focus on incorporating probabilistic uncertainty analysis, expanding the framework to different geological conditions, and integrating advanced data-driven techniques to further enhance predictive capability. Further validation using additional case studies, stronger seismic events, and different geological settings is recommended to evaluate the broader applicability and robustness of the proposed framework.

Overall, this study contributes to advancing Digital Twin applications in underground engineering by providing a comprehensive and scalable framework for seismic integrity assessment. The proposed methodology offers a practical pathway for improving the reliability of post-earthquake evaluation and supports the development of intelligent, data-driven infrastructure systems.
