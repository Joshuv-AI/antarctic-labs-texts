Prevalence and Risk Factors of Tungiasis Among Selected Regions of Ethiopia: A Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Abayeneh Girma, Indiris Abdu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsTungiasis, caused by the female sand flea Tunga penetrans, is a neglected tropical disease (NTD) with substantial health and socioeconomic burdens in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of tungiasis and identify associated risk factors in Ethiopia's Oromia and Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples' Region (SNNPR).MethodsWe systematically searched Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, Science Direct, and African Journal Online (AJOL) for studies published between October 2015 and September 2024. Study quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist. A random‐effects meta‐analysis model was employed to account for heterogeneity, quantified using I² statistics. Publication bias was evaluated via funnel plots, and Egger's regression test.ResultsAcross eight studies (n = 8502 individuals), the pooled prevalence of tungiasis was 39.0% (95% CI: 28.5–49.6). Subgroup analyses revealed regional disparities, with SNNPR exhibiting a higher prevalence (43.1%; 95% CI: 27.1–58.1) than Oromia (35.0%; 95% CI: 18.8–51.3). Temporal trends indicated a significant increase in prevalence post‐2017 compared to earlier years. Low education levels (adjusted odds ratio [AOR]: 2.7, 95% CI: 0.6–4.9), lack of footwear use (AOR: 8.8, 95% CI: 0.1–17.4) and mud/earthen flooring (AOR: 4.9, 95% CI: 2.6–7.2) were significantly associated with tungiasis among selected regions of Ethiopia.ConclusionThis meta‐analysis demonstrates a significant burden of tungiasis in Ethiopia's Oromia and SNNPR regions. Therefore, we recommend implementing integrated interventions that combine: (1) provision of community‐based health education programs to improve prevention awareness, (2) distribution of protective footwear to high‐risk populations, (3) housing improvements focusing on floor hardening, (4) expanded access to safe treatment modalities, and (5) targeted animal reservoir control measures. Such coordinated efforts, embedded within existing NTD control platforms, could substantially reduce tungiasis transmission while addressing the socioeconomic determinants that perpetuate high infection rates in these endemic regions.

Introduction

Tungiasis, a neglected tropical disease (NTD) caused by the parasitic female sand flea Tunga penetrans (commonly known as “jigger”), represents a significant public health challenge in resource‐limited communities, affecting both human and animal populations [1].

Once the flea penetrates the skin, it matures, and feet are by far the most common sites, whereas elbows, hands, buttocks etc. are ectopic sites [2]. A distinctive characteristic of T. penetrans is that the female flea burrows permanently beneath the host's skin, where it remains up to death. Over a period of 4–6 weeks, the vector rises in size dramatically, expanding nearly 2000 times its initial volume and getting a diameter of 10–12 mm. Before dying, it lays and releases eggs, continuing the cycle of infestation [3].

Children infected with T. penetrans often face difficulties in attending school as a result of symptoms persistently examined and the stigma associated with the condition that not only affects children but adults as well [4, 5]. The disease disproportionately affects vulnerable populations, particularly children < 15 years, the elderly, and persons with disabilities [6].

Tungiasis is generally self‐limiting and in endemic settings, the complications are common. Several patients experience severe itching, pain, and mobility restrictions. In some cases, individuals suffer from toenail loss, digit deformities, and secondary bacterial infections [7]. The protruding posterior end of the flea creates an open sore that serves as an entry point for disease causing microbes, leading to abscesses, suppuration, and, in severe cases, tetanus in unvaccinated individuals [6]. Intense itching, swelling, deep fissures, ulcerations, and abscess formation due to bacterial superinfection describe the critical phase of tungiasis. In its chronic form, the condition may cause prolonged pain, deformities, foot damage, and permanent disability [8].

Epidemiological studies have identified several risk factors associated with tungiasis, including poor hygiene and sanitation, inadequate water supply, absence of footwear, inadequate housing conditions (such as mud or earthen flooring), overcrowded living conditions, parental illiteracy, low family income (poverty), residence in pastoral settings, and the presence of rodents and/or livestock in the home [1, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Periodic variations and movement into contaminated regions also contribute to the spread of the disease [1]. In Ethiopia, scabies and tungiasis further exacerbated by environmental and socio‐political factors such as dust storms, drought, and civil unrest [14, 15, 16, 17]. The country's geographical locality, weather, and biological practices create favorable conditions for parasitic infestations [18].

Understanding the epidemiology and determinants of tungiasis is critical for developing targeted prevention and control strategies. Latest meta‐analysis conducted on tungiasis epidemiology in sub‐Saharan Africa (SSA) reported a pooled prevalence of 33.4% [1]., even though, Ethiopia lacks comprehensive national‐level estimates on tungiasis. Compared to other regions of Ethiopia, Oromia, and Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples' Region (SNNPR) provide a strong basis for understanding tungiasis in the country due to their high case rates, availability of epidemiological studies, and socio‐environmental diversity, making them appropriate choices for this meta‐analysis. By synthesizing existing evidence, this study provides the first pooled prevalence estimates and risk factor analysis for tungiasis in Ethiopia's most affected regions. The findings will inform policymakers in prioritizing cost‐effective interventions—including animal reservoir control, improved housing conditions, and footwear distribution programs—within national NTD control strategies.

Methods

Study Area

Ethiopia, Africa's second most populous nation with an estimated 130 million inhabitants (2025), exhibits remarkable geographical and cultural diversity [19]. The country's diverse geographical features, ranging from highland plateaus to lowland deserts, supporting numerous ethnic groups with distinct linguistic and cultural traditions. This study focuses on two particularly significant regions: Oromia, Ethiopia's largest regional state with approximately 40 million residents, demonstrates exceptional ecological variability ranging from the alpine ecosystems of the Bale and Arsi highlands to the semi‐arid Borana plains. Over 80% of Oromia's population resides in rural areas, where mixed subsistence farming and pastoralism predominate. Similarly, the SNNPR presents extraordinary biodiversity, encompassing Afromontane forests, fertile highland watersheds, and Rift Valley lacustrine systems. SNNPR's approximately 20 million inhabitants comprise 56 officially recognized ethnic groups, representing one of Ethiopia's most culturally heterogeneous areas. All identified records were imported into EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics), where automated duplicate detection was supplemented by manual verification. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion in view of the selection criteria. Data Extraction

The data extraction format of excel 2016 included the first author name, article's publication year, the region studied, study setting, sample size, cases, prevalence and response rate.

Data Analysis

We conducted all meta‐analyses using a DerSimonian–Laird random‐effects model to account for expected heterogeneity across studies. Between‐study variability was assessed through Cochran's Q test (p < 0.10 threshold) and quantified via I² statistics. To investigate heterogeneity sources, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by region (Oromia vs. SNNPR), setting (community vs. school‐based), sampling method (random vs. systematic), and publication period (2015–2017 vs. 2018–2024). Publication bias was evaluated through three complementary approaches: funnel plot symmetry analysis, Egger's regression test (p < 0.05 indicating significance), and Galbraith radial plots to identify outliers. Sensitivity analysis was also performed to examine a single study's effect on overall effect size. All analyses were implemented in Stata/SE 14.0 using two‐tailed tests with specialized meta‐analysis commands (metan, metabias).

Results

Selection of Studies

The systematic search across five international databases initially identified 80 potentially relevant articles. Following duplicate removal (n = 20), title/abstract screening excluded 25 additional records primarily due to irrelevant study populations (n = 12), nonobservational designs (n = 8), or geographical mismatches (n = 5). Full‐text evaluation of the remaining 35 articles led to the exclusion of 19 studies, with common reasons being incomplete prevalence data (n = 7), non‐English publications (n = 3), out‐of‐scope timeframes (n = 5), or inaccessible full texts (n = 4). A further eight articles were excluded during final vetting, comprising systematic reviews (n = 1), qualitative studies (n = 2), articles on unrelated dermatoses (n = 4), and correspondence pieces (n = 1). Ultimately, eight studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the meta‐analysis, representing a rigorous 87.5% exclusion rate from initial results. The complete selection process with exclusion rationales is detailed in the PRISMA flowchart (Figure 2).

Characteristics of Included Studies

All eight included studies employed cross‐sectional designs [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28], with five community‐based investigations and three conducted in school settings. The sampling methodology varied across studies, with five utilizing simple random sampling and the remaining three employing systematic random approaches. Collectively, these studies encompassed 8,502 participants, with individual study sample sizes ranging from 272 to 2676 subjects. A unique methodological consideration involved the study by Ramaswamy [23], which presented distinct prevalence data for both Oromia and SNNPR regions despite using a unified sampling framework. Consequently, this study was analytically treated as two separate datasets in our meta‐analysis to preserve regional‐specific accuracy (Table 2).

Pooled Prevalence of Tungiasis

The pooled proportion of tungiasis among two regions is 39.0% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 28.5–49.6) and a high level of heterogeneity is recorded, (I 2 = 97.6%, p < 0.001) (Figure 3).

Pooled proportion of tungiasis in selected regions of Ethiopia. CI, confidence interval.

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted by considering sample size, region, study design, sampling method and publication year of the included studies (Table 3). The highest disease burden was observed in studies with smaller sample sizes (< 384 participants), showing a pooled prevalence of 45.9% (95% CI: 31.8–60.0) compared to 35.0% (95% CI: 20.0–49.8) in larger studies. Regional disparities were evident, with SNNPR demonstrating significantly higher prevalence (43.1%, 95% CI: 27.1–58.1) than Oromia (35.0%, 95% CI: 18.8–51.3). Interestingly, school‐based studies reported marginally higher prevalence (39.2%, 95% CI: 21.4–57.0) than community‐based investigations (38.9%, 95% CI: 23.4–54.5), though this difference was minimal. Methodologically, studies employing systematic random sampling documented substantially higher prevalence estimates (48.7%, 95% CI: 36.8–60.6) versus those using simple random sampling (33.3%, 95% CI: 18.3–48.2). Temporal analysis indicated a notable increase in reported prevalence post‐2017 (48.4%, 95% CI: 35.7–61.2) compared to earlier studies (29.5%, 95% CI: 17.1–41.9).

Factors Associated With Tungiasis Infestation Among Selected Regions of Ethiopia

The current review check parental educational status, footwear and house floor type that may associate with the prevalence of tungiasis in selected regions.

Parental Education Level

Pooled data from two studies revealed a strong inverse relationship between education and disease risk. Individuals with lower educational attainment had 2.7‐fold higher odds of tungiasis infection (95% CI: 0.6–4.9) compared to their more educated counterparts. This factor shows significant association with tungiasis prevalence (Figure 6).

Lower educational status significantly associated with tungiasis in study regions. CI, confidence interval.

Footwear

Meta‐analysis of two studies demonstrated that barefoot individuals faced dramatically elevated risk, with an 8.8‐fold increase in infection odds (95% CI: 0.1–17.4) relative to regular footwear users. This factor also significantly associated with tungiasis (Figure 7).

Never use footwear showed significant association with tungiasis in study regions. CI, confidence interval.

House Floor Type

The association between tungiasis and earth and mud house floor type was computed from two studies. People living in mud and earth house floor homes had a 4.9 times greater chance (95% CI: 2.6–7.2) of developing tungiasis than other types of indoor cemented floor. This factor also showed significant association with tungiasis (Figure 8).

Discussion

Human tungiasis represents a significant global health burden, disproportionately affecting impoverished communities in low‐resource settings, particularly in Ethiopia. As a poverty‐associated dermatological condition, tungiasis serves as both a clinical and socioeconomic marker of disadvantage [29]. The findings of this study provide critical insights for responsible bodies and policymakers, highlighting the urgent need to prioritize and implement effective intervention mechanisms. This study aims to measure the pooled proportion of tungiasis and its associated risk factors in selected regions of Ethiopia. In this review, eight studies along 8502 participants were incorporated.

This meta‐analysis found a pooled tungiasis prevalence of 39.0%. The current result was comparable with community based studies in Nigeria (35.8%) [30], and Kenya (39.7%) [31] and school based study in Gabon (37.99%) [32] as well as meta‐analysis conducted in SSA (33.4) [1]. However, our finding is higher than community based studies conducted in Uganda (15%) [33] and eastern Tanzania (13%) [34]. On the other hand, our current outcome was lower than previously published school based study in Kenya (62.1%) [35], and community based studies in Uganda (62.8%) [36], Cameroon(53%) [37], and western Tanzania (42.5%) [38]. The differences in the tungiasis prevalence among various studies can be explained by a sophisticated interrelation of the sociodemographic factors including age, gender, occupation, and level of education play an important role in determining the risk of exposure, and children, barefoot workers, and poor populations are overrepresented. The season of the year during which data was collected is also a very important factor since fleas are known to be more active during dry seasons and barefoot walking during this time increases transmission. Heukelbach et al. [39] found that the prevalence of tungiasis in resource‐poor communities in Brazil varied significantly by season: 33.6% in March (rainy season), 23.8% in June (end of the rainy season), 54.4% in September (peak of the dry season), and 16.8% in January (onset of the rainy season). Moreover, the study population itself be it rural, urban, or a certain high‐risk group, can radically change the estimates of the prevalence because of the differences in the living conditions, contact with animals, and the use of footwear. Other factors leading to variations in reported rates would include methodological differences such as diagnostic methods (visual inspection vs. microscopy), sample size and study design (cross sectional vs. longitudinal). Many of these risk factors are economically empowered since poverty constrains the ability to access protective footwear, suitable housing, and health care, which continue to sustain a high rate of infections in endemic regions.

Addressing the high‐pooled prevalence of tungiasis in specific regions of Ethiopia necessitates targeted healthcare policies and strategic interventions. These efforts should prioritize improving access to sanitation and hygiene, boosting community income, strengthening screening and early detection, and integrating prevention‐focused approaches. Additionally, providing comprehensive training for healthcare professionals, increasing community awareness regarding tungiasis, and enhancing laboratory services and facilities are essential. These initiatives aim to improve treatment outcomes, reduce tungiasis risk, and ensure holistic care for affected individuals [28, 40].

Our analysis revealed that individuals with lower educational attainment faced a 2.7‐fold increased risk (95% CI: 0.6–4.9) of tungiasis infection compared to their more educated counterparts. This finding aligns with evidence from northeast Brazil, where communities lacking health education showed a 4.1‐fold higher disease acquisition risk [12]. Similarly, good tungiasis‐related knowledge decreases tungiasis acquiring among studies conducted in resource poor communities in Brazil, Kenya, and Uganda [41, 42, 43]. Illiteracy may contribute to a lack of awareness about the advantages of the various risk factors and its connection with tungiasis. Furthermore, the low economy of uneducated parents', which limits their ability to provide essential hygiene protection items such as shoes, soap, and other necessities.

Our meta‐analysis identified barefoot exposure as the strongest modifiable risk factor for tungiasis, with non‐footwear users facing an 8.8‐fold increased risk (95% CI: 0.1–17.4) compared to regular footwear wearers. This outcome aligns with a systematic review and meta‐analysis [44] and multiple epidemiological studies demonstrating that direct skin contact with flea‐infested soil is the primary risk factor for contracting tungiasis. Regular use of appropriate (affordable, well‐fitting, and durable) footwear can prevent or slow the progression of many NTDs, including tungiasis [2, 11, 45, 46]. Additionally, ensuring regular cleaning and drying of footwear is equally important.

This meta‐analysis identified a 4.9‐fold increased odds (95% CI: 2.6–7.2) of tungiasis among residents of homes with mud/earthen floors compared to those with cemented floors. This finding aligns with study conducted from Uganda [17]. This is because mud and earthen floors create a suitable environment for flea growth and reproduction.

Strengths and Limitations

This review stand out as the first to present the combined prevalence of tungiasis in specific regions of Ethiopia. Additionally, it identifies the aggregated risk factors linked to tungiasis infestation. However, certain limitations exist in this review. For example, prevalence of different sub‐population (communities/school‐attending children) were pooled together. Seasonal variation of prevalence was not taken into account in the analysis. Again, in this meta‐analysis, other regions of Ethiopia were not included, therefore the current results does not represent the country's tungiasis burden. Moreover, the small sample sizes in some of the incorporated articles could influence the meta‐analysis results.

Conclusion

The pooled prevalence of tungiasis among selected regions of Ethiopia is 39.0%. In the current study, low education levels, the absence of footwear usage, and mud or earthen floors in the house were significantly associated with tungiasis. Therefore, policymakers and health planners should prioritize the implementation of effective prevention and control measures. Further comprehensive research throughout Ethiopia is crucial for gaining a deeper understanding of the patterns, contributing factors, and overall dynamics of tungiasis in the country.
