Efficacy and safety of liposomal bupivacaine versus bupivacaine for retrolaminar block for postoperative analgesia in patients undergoing posterior lumbar interbody fusion: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Shi-Jiao Zhang, Yang Liu, Yong-qian Wei, Xiang-yu Ma, Ying-bin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study compared the analgesic effects and adverse effects of liposomal bupivacaine and bupivacaine in patients undergoing posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) with retrolaminar block (RLB). The study evaluated the efficacy and safety of liposomal bupivacaine for postoperative analgesia in patients undergoing PLIF.MethodsA total of 70 patients undergoing elective PLIF were enrolled and randomly divided into two groups: the liposomal bupivacaine group (LB group, n = 35) and the bupivacaine group (B group, n = 35), for RLB. The primary outcomes were postoperative 30-minute, 6-hour, 12-hour, 18-hour, 24-hour, 36-hour, 48-hour, and 72-hour static visual analog scale (VAS) scores and perioperative opioid consumption. Secondary outcomes included bupivacaine blood concentrations at 5 time points within 72 h after injection of the test drug, incidence of postoperative adverse events, Quality of Recovery-15 scores (QoR-15) of patients at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h postoperatively, length of postoperative hospitalization, and changes in IL-6 and IL-10 after 2 h, 24 h, and 72 h of administration of the intervention.ResultsCompared with B group, resting VAS in the LB group were significantly decreased at 18 h, 24 h, 36 h, 48 h, and 72 h postoperatively (P = 0.003, 0.022, 0.009, 0.001, and 0.000, respectively). The QoR-15 scores of the LB group at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h after surgery were significantly higher (P = 0.001, 0.009, and 0.001, respectively) compared with the B group. No significant differences were observed in opioid consumption, postoperative adverse events, length of postoperative hospitalization, and postoperative inflammatory factors at 72 h between the groups. The blood concentrations of bupivacaine in both groups did not exceed the toxicity threshold at 72 h postoperatively.ConclusionThe use of liposomal bupivacaine for ultrasound-guided RLB in PLIF patients did not reduce perioperative opioid consumption compared to bupivacaine. Howerve, liposomal bupivacaine did not increase complication rates and prolonged the duration of nerve block analgesia, which is safe and reliable in the postoperative analgesia in patients undergoing PLIF.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov. Background

Lumbar degenerative disease has a high global prevalence, affecting approximately 103 million people, primarily impacting the elderly and often leading to severe functional impairment [1]. Posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) is the standard surgical procedure, but it involves dissection of paraspinal muscles, nerve traction, and induces inflammatory responses, frequently resulting in severe postoperative pain [2]. Studies indicate that over half of spinal surgery patients still experience moderate pain six months postoperatively [3]. Inadequate analgesia may cause hemodynamic instability and cardiovascular complications, whereas effective pain management helps shorten hospital stays and improve outcomes [4, 5]. Current postoperative pain management for PLIF commonly relies on opioids, NSAIDs, wound infiltration, epidural analgesia, and nerve blocks [6–9].

With the advancement of ultrasound guidance technology, ultrasound-guided nerve blocks have gained widespread application in postoperative pain management due to their unique advantages. The retrolaminar block (RLB) is recognized as a safe, effective, and easily administered analgesic technique [10]. Clinical studies indicate that, compared to the erector spinae plane block (ESPB), RLB provides more effective analgesia during thoracic surgery, with fewer complications, simpler operation, and shorter hospital stays [11]. However, the key factor currently limiting the analgesic efficacy of RLB is the limited duration of action of conventional local anesthetics.

Liposomal bupivacaine is a novel sustained-release local anesthetic that employs liposomal encapsulation technology for gradual bupivacaine release, enabling continuous analgesia for up to 72 h [12]. Currently, this agent is primarily used for wound infiltration and interscalene brachial plexus blocks. Studies have confirmed its safe application in erector spinae plane blocks [13]. Liposomal bupivacaine has demonstrated favorable postoperative analgesia and opioid-sparing effects in arthroplasty and abdominal surgery settings [14]. However, no studies have reported the use of liposomal bupivacaine for postoperative pain management following PLIF. Therefore, this prospective randomized clinical trial comprehensively evaluates the efficacy and safety of liposomal bupivacaine versus bupivacaine hydrochloride in ultrasound-guided RLB for PLIF postoperative analgesia, providing evidence for clinical practice.

Methods

This is a single-institution, prospective, randomized clinical trial that has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Clinical Medical College of Lanzhou University (2024 A-1215, September 23, 2024) and registered with the China Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400091488, October 29, 2024). This study was reported according to the CONSORT guidelines. The experiment was conducted at the Second Hospital & Clinical Medical School of Lanzhou University in Gansu Province, China. Patients were informed about the study content before surgery, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. Liposomal bupivacaine or bupivacaine was used for ultrasound-guided RLB in patients scheduled for elective PLIF. Inclusion criteria included: patients aged 18 to 65, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification I–III, and elective posterior lumbar interbody fusion surgery. Exclusion criteria included: (1) having received bupivacaine liposome or bupivacaine hydrochloride within the past 30 days, (2) having a history of immune deficiency disorders or having used immunosuppressive agents within 30 days before surgery, (3) having a history of neurological disorders or being unable to communicate effectively, (4) having abnormal liver or kidney function, (5) having clinically significant abnormal heart rate or rhythm during the screening period. Patients who experienced anesthetic complications, drug allergies, local anesthetic poisoning, or severe surgery-related adverse events during the trial will have the trial discontinued. Appropriate treatment measures will be promptly implemented for patients whose condition worsens. The reasons for discontinuation will be recorded for patients whose trials are discontinued.

Sample size calculation

This study was a prospective randomized controlled trial with the primary outcome measure being the static visual analog scale (VAS) within 48 h postoperatively. To determine the minimum sample size, we treated the VAS as a continuous variable and assumed a significant difference of 1 in the static VAS at 48 h postoperatively. Based on preliminary pilot studies using liposomal bupivacaine and bupivacaine, the 48-hour resting VAS were (2.05 ± 1.08) and (3.00 ± 1.39), respectively. Assuming α = 0.05, a test power of 90%, and equal group allocation ratios, PASS software was used to calculate a minimum sample size of 29 cases per group. After considering the dropout rate at our hospital, the sample size was finalized at 35 cases per group.

Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomly assigned to study groups sequentially based on the order of enrollment using a computer-generated random number sequence. The randomization list was generated by an independent statistician not involved in the trial and kept in securely sealed envelopes. A designated anesthesiologist, who was not involved in any subsequent procedures or assessments, opened the envelope before surgery, reviewed the group assignment, and prepared the study drug accordingly.

Due to the visual differences between milky-white liposomal bupivacaine and clear conventional bupivacaine, a separate blinded operator performed the nerve block. This operator was not involved in the intraoperative anesthetic management or any postoperative patient evaluations. Throughout the study period, the patients, primary anesthesiologists, surgeons, follow-up physicians, and outcome assessors remained blinded to group assignments.

Study protocol

A separate anesthesiologist prepared the study drugs. LB group preparation method: 10 ml of liposomal bupivacaine (133 mg) was diluted with 20 ml of saline to 30 ml, with a concentration of 0.443%. B group preparation method: 10 ml of bupivacaine (75 mg) was diluted with 20 ml of saline to 30 ml, with a concentration of 0.25%. Upon entering the operating room, monitor electrocardiography (ECG), blood pressure (BP), pulse oximetry (SpO₂), heart rate (HR), end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂), temperature, and bispectral index (BIS). All patients underwent an ultrasound-guided RLB procedure performed by the same anesthesiologist before induction, using a single-injection technique: The patient was placed in the prone position. First, the corresponding spinous processes and transverse processes were located and marked based on the required spinal nerve segment for block. The area was disinfected and draped. The ultrasound probe (Sonosite P07576) with a low-frequency (2–5 MHz) convex array was covered with a sterile cover. The probe was placed at the marked midline of the spine, with its long axis parallel to the spine. The spinous processes were visible on ultrasound. The probe is then moved laterally by approximately 1 cm to visualize the lamina, which appears as a high-echoing, wedge-shaped structure. The superficial layer consists of the spinal transverse muscle group (semispinalis, multifidus, and rotator muscles). Local anesthetic (15 ml) is injected bilaterally into the plane between this muscle group and the lamina. After the nerve block, test the sensory block plane (cold sensation) every 5 min using an alcohol swab. Classify the block into three levels based on changes in skin temperature sensation: Level 2 indicates normal skin sensation, Level 1 indicates a significant reduction in cold sensation, and Level 0 indicates complete loss of cold sensation. The dermatomes with grade 0 skin sensation were marked and recorded as blocked. If no sensory loss was observed within 20 min, the block was considered a failure and the patient was excluded from the study.

After confirming the effectiveness of the block, the patient was administered 0.01 mg/kg of pentobarbital sodium, 0.05 mg/kg of midazolam, 0.5 µg/kg of sufentanil, propofol 1ཞ2 mg/kg, and cisatracurium 0.2 mg/kg to induce general anesthesia, followed by endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation via an anesthetic machine. Adjust ventilator parameters as needed to maintain PETCO₂ 35–45 mmHg, SpO₂ > 95%, and control airway peak pressure ≤ 30 cmH₂O. During surgery, continuous infusion of remifentanil at 0.1ཞ0.3 µg/kg/min, propofol at 4ཞ6 mg/kg/h, and dexmedetomidine at 0.2ཞ0.4 µg/kg/h. Intermittent boluses of cisatracurium were administered to maintain muscle relaxation, with the BIS maintained between 40 and 60. A warming blanket and intravenous fluid warming devices were used during surgery to maintain core body temperature above 36 °C. All patients received 0.1 µg/kg of sufentanil approximately 40 min before the end of surgery to prevent hyperalgesia after remifentanil withdrawal, and 8 mg of ondansetron approximately 30 min before the end of surgery. After surgery, the patient’s BIS rose above 90, spontaneous breathing resumed, the endotracheal tube was removed, and the patient was transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit(PACU) for recovery observation. Both groups received patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) postoperatively. The analgesia pump settings were as follows: sufentanil 100 µg diluted with normal saline to 100 ml (1 µg/ml), with a basal infusion rate of 2 ml/h, a single dose of 2 ml per press, and a lockout interval of 15 min.

Outcome indicators

The primary outcomes included the static VAS of patients at 30 min, 6 h, 12 h, 18 h, 24 h, 36 h, 48 h, and 72 h postoperatively, as well as the perioperative consumption of opioid medications.

The secondary outcomes included: (1) the blood concentrations of bupivacaine at 2 h, 6 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h after the initiation of the experimental drug injection, (2) incidence of postoperative adverse events, (3) Quality of Recovery-15 scores (QoR-15) at 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively and postoperative hospital stay, (4) changes in IL-6 and IL-10 levels 2, 24, and 72 h after intervention.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were analyzed and processed using SPSS 25.0 statistical software. All data underwent normality tests. Data were organized and plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software. Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and intergroup comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous data were expressed as M (Q1, Q3), and intergroup comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Count data were expressed as frequencies and percentages, which were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of subjects

This study evaluated inclusion and exclusion criteria for 89 patients from January to July 2025, with 19 refusing to participate in the trial. A total of 70 patients undergoing elective PLIF were enroll, with 35 in each group. In the liposomal bupivacaine group, 1 patient was excluded due to abnormal muscle strength postoperatively and transferred to the rehabilitation department, and another 1 patient was excluded due to cerebrospinal fluid leakage postoperatively. Ultimately, 33 patients in the liposomal bupivacaine group and 35 patients in the bupivacaine group were analyzed (Fig. 1). There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, gender, body mass index, ASA classification, ethnicity, personal history, number of surgical segments, surgical duration, or intraoperative blood loss (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

There was no statistically significant difference in postoperative adverse events between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the analgesic efficacy and safety of bupivacaine liposomes and bupivacaine RLB in PLIF through a randomized double-blind controlled trial. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of intraoperative and postoperative opioid consumption, postoperative adverse events, postoperative hospital stay, and inflammatory factors at 72 h postoperatively (P > 0.05). By comparing the static VAS of both groups within 72 h postoperatively, it was found that the LB group had significantly lower static VAS than the B group at 18, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h postoperatively (P < 0.05), demonstrating good analgesic effects. The QoR-15 of patients in the LB group at 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively were significantly higher than those in the B group (P < 0.05), indicating better postoperative recovery quality. The plasma concentrations of bupivacaine at 2, 6, 24, 48 h, and 72 h after receiving the intervention, no plasma concentrations exceeding the neurotoxicity and cardiotoxicity thresholds for bupivacaine (2000 and 4000 ng/ml, respectively) were detected. Neither the LB group nor the B group experienced neuroblocked complications, and there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of postoperative adverse events (P > 0.05). This indicates that 133 mg of liposomal bupivacaine is safe and reliable for RLB in PLIF.

The selection of total dose for nerve block agents was based on the unique pharmacokinetic properties of the two formulations. In a pharmacokinetic and safety analysis of liposomal bupivacaine, early peak concentrations following administration of both liposomal bupivacaine and bupivacaine occurred approximately 1 h post-dose. However, the peak level of bupivacaine was approximately 1.5 times higher than that of liposomal bupivacaine. The total dose of bupivacaine released at each time interval was generally lower for liposomal bupivacaine than for bupivacaine hydrochloride [17]. Previous studies have demonstrated a biphasic release profile for liposomal bupivacaine, with the first peak occurring within hours and the second peak appearing between 12 and 36 h post-administration [17, 18]. Although the total dose of liposomal bupivacaine (133 mg) in this study was higher than that of bupivacaine (75 mg), this did not affect comparisons between the two groups. The plasma pharmacokinetics of bupivacaine within 72 h after liposomal bupivacaine administration were consistent with previous reports.

In postoperative pain management for patients undergoing surgery under general anesthesia, RLB demonstrates superior analgesic efficacy compared to erector spinae plane block(ESPB) and percutaneous sacral canal nerve block (PSCNB) [19]. In a cadaveric thoracolumbar segment retrolaminar block anatomical and radiological study, contrast agent diffusion was observed in the surrounding intervertebral foramen, paravertebral, and dorsal laminar fascia planes after injection, providing direct evidence of the mechanism of action of RLB and indicating its promising potential for spinal analgesia [20]. In this study, liposomal bupivacaine RLB demonstrated superior analgesic effects. Although static VAS decreased in both groups at 72 h postoperatively, the LB group maintained a significant advantage, attributed to the sustained-release properties of liposomal bupivacaine, which maintains prolonged drug efficacy. In two clinical studies, liposomal bupivacaine was found to provide longer-lasting analgesic effects compared to traditional local anesthetics or placebo [13, 21], which is consistent with the results of this study.

There were no significant differences in intraoperative, postoperative, and total opioid consumption between the two groups. However, total opioid consumption was slightly higher in the B group than in the LB group. In a clinical trial, patients underwent elective wrist arthrodesis and proximal carpal bone resection surgery, receiving either liposomal bupivacaine or bupivacaine for intraoperative injection for early pain control. The results showed no significant difference in opioid use within 4 days postoperatively, but the first-day opioid use was slightly lower in the group receiving liposomal bupivacaine [22]. This is consistent with the results of this study.

The QoR-15 scale is a validated tool for assessing the quality of postoperative recovery, covering five dimensions: physical comfort, physical independence, psychological support, emotional state, and pain. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that the QoR-15 has excellent validity, reliability, responsiveness, and clinical acceptability, making it an excellent indicator for measuring postoperative recovery quality [23]. In this study, the QoR-15 scores in the LB group were significantly higher than those in the B group at 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively, indicating that liposomal bupivacaine RLB can improve the quality of postoperative recovery in patients undergoing PLIF, which may be attributed to more adequate pain control. The most common side effects of liposomal bupivacaine peripheral nerve block include nausea, fever, constipation, vomiting, and itching [24]. During postoperative follow-up, there were no significant differences in the aforementioned adverse reactions between the two groups, and these cannot be solely attributed to the side effects of liposomal bupivacaine.

Immune responses are a major factor influencing patients’ outcomes. In a meta-analysis of systemic, inflammatory markers in neck pain, surgical trauma and pain lead to the massive release of systemic inflammatory factors, and multiple inflammatory markers are associated with clinical variables such as pain intensity, disability, and hypersensitivity [25]. IL-6, as an inflammatory outcome biomarker, is not influenced by traditional prognostic indicators or evidence of infection, while IL-10 plays a key role in suppressing innate immune activation [26, 27]. This study evaluated the intensity of inflammatory responses in the two groups by measuring postoperative pro-inflammatory factor IL-6 and pro-inflammatory factor IL-10 concentrations, finding no significant differences. However, since preoperative inflammatory factor concentrations were not measured, the results do not reflect the trend of inflammatory markers in patients.

Safety is the primary consideration when evaluating new analgesic technologies. The systemic absorption of bupivacaine may lead to irreversible cardiac and neurotoxicity [28]. The lumbar region is richly supplied with arterial and venous networks, including the vertebral artery and intervertebral veins [29]. Measuring the plasma concentration of bupivacaine after administration of lipid-encapsulated bupivacaine and bupivacaine can serve as part of the safety assessment. In this study, neither group of patients experienced neuroblock-related complications or bupivacaine toxicity reactions. At 72 h postoperatively, bupivacaine concentrations did not exceed the plasma concentration thresholds associated with neurotoxicity and cardiotoxicity (2000 and 4000 ng/ml, respectively) [30, 31]. There were no significant differences in postoperative adverse events between the LB group and the B group, confirming that liposomal bupivacaine 133 mg administered via RLB for PLIF demonstrates good safety characteristics. Notably, the liposomal bupivacaine dose used in this study (133 mg) was lower than the FDA-approved maximum dose (266 mg) but achieved significant clinical efficacy.

Liposomal bupivacaine showed specific advantages in prolonged analgesia and QoR-15 in this study. It is important to note, however, that this advantage did not translate into a significant reduction in postoperative opioid consumption or a significant effect on measured systemic inflammatory markers. This suggests that the mechanism of action and clinical value of liposomal bupivacaine may focus primarily on optimizing the local analgesia and recovery experience, rather than having a broad effect through systemic pathways. A large number of experimental data are needed to verify the relevant results.

This study is the first to apply liposomal bupivacaine to RLB in PLIF surgery, expanding the clinical application scope of this drug. It systematically evaluated multidimensional outcome measures, including analgesic efficacy, opioid conservation, rehabilitation indicators, and safety, exploring the application value of RLB in spinal surgery. However, this study was a single-center, and the relatively small sample size (35 cases per group) may reduce statistical power and affect the sensitivity of outcome measure detection. Second, long-term pain and functional recovery were not assessed. Third, the study did not perform subgroup analyses based on the number of fused segments or patients’ baseline pain sensitivity, which may have masked efficacy differences in specific populations. Fourth, this study assessed only static VAS scores, which may not fully reflect the functional impact of pain. Dynamic VAS scores serve as a crucial indicator for evaluating patients’ recovery quality. Future prospective studies should incorporate both static and dynamic pain assessments to gain a more accurate understanding of pain outcomes. Additionally, a cost-effectiveness analysis was lacking, and the higher cost of liposomal bupivacaine may limit its application. The economic value of liposomal bupivacaine is not solely determined by its direct drug cost, rather, it primarily depends on whether the healthcare system can effectively translate its notable analgesic advantages into expedited postoperative functional recovery and reduced hospital length of stay through the optimization of perioperative clinical pathways. Nevertheless, it should be noted that this medication also carries a potential risk of delayed motor function recovery. Therefore, in clinical practice, individualized and precise dosing strategies should be implemented, along with early rehabilitation interventions, to mitigate associated risks and ensure optimal treatment outcomes.

Conclusion

This study confirms that liposomal bupivacaine epidural block significantly improves postoperative analgesia following PLIF surgery, and the technique is safe with no serious adverse reactions observed. Based on this study, future research should include multicenter, large-sample studies to compare the efficacy of different doses of liposomal bupivacaine, conduct long-term follow-up evaluations of chronic pain prevention effects, and comprehensively assess the clinical value of liposomal bupivacaine.
