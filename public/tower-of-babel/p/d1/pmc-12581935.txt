Understanding the Determinants of Use of the Australian Guide to Diagnosis of Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder: Informing the Review, Development and Dissemination of New National Clinical Practice Guidelines
Authors: Natasha Reid, Jayden Logan, Yasmine Wong, Zachary Munn, Chelsea Vanderpeet, Nicole Hayes, Storm Anderson, Storm Anderson, Diana Barnett, Andi Crawford, Robyn Doney, Elizabeth Elliott, Rowena Friend, Sarah Goldsbury, Matthew Gullo, Sophie Harrington, Katrina Harris, Nicole Hewlett, Delyse Hutchinson, Fiona Kay, Natalie Kippin, Philippa Middleton, Raewyn Mutch, Max Naglazas, Seema Padencheri, Carmela Pestell, Doug Shelton, Kelly Skorka, James Stewart, Haydn Till, Prue Walker, Kerryn Bagley, Alison Crichton, Elizabeth Elliott, Amy Finlay‐Jones, Rowena Friend, Sophie Harrington, Nicole Hewlett, Karen Moritz, Raewyn Mutch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe Australian Guide to the Diagnosis of fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD), developed in 2016, is currently under review. This study aimed to understand how the Guide is used in practice and identify factors influencing its implementation.MethodsA cross‐sectional online survey was conducted with Australian health practitioners involved in the assessment and diagnosis of FASD. The Clinician Guideline Determinants Questionnaire was used. Quantitative data were summarised descriptively and content analysis was applied to open‐ended questions.ResultsOf 333 survey sessions, 232 participants consented and 136 completed the survey. Most respondents were paediatricians (n = 54, 37%) or psychologists (n = 49, 33%). Although most clinicians felt confident using the Guide, only 44.1% agreed it clearly described the underlying evidence, and just 21% felt it adequately reflected patient preferences. Key enablers included clear, easy‐to‐follow guidance (n = 45, 36.3%); ease of access (n = 18, 14.5%) and familiarity with the Guide (n = 14, 11.3%). Common barriers were lack of access to appropriate clinicians, time and cost constraints (n = 25, 24.8%); concerns about the evidence base (n = 16, 15.8%); and lack of familiarity or skills (n = 8, 7.9%).Discussion and ConclusionsFindings highlight strong clinician engagement with the 2016 Guide but also identify areas for improvement, particularly around evidence transparency and alignment with person‐centred care. By engaging with stakeholders and improving guidelines based on clinician feedback, we can improve the quality of care and support for individuals with FASD and their families.

Introduction

Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD), also known as neurodevelopmental disorder associated with prenatal alcohol exposure [1], is a lifelong condition arising from prenatal alcohol exposure. Individuals with FASD can experience a range of co‐occurring conditions and, without appropriate support, may face adverse secondary conditions, such as school disruption, mental health and substance use disorders, employment and legal issues [2]. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis estimated 48% (95% confidence interval: 38%–57%) of Australian women reported any amount of alcohol use at any time during pregnancy [3]. Currently, there are no accurate prevalence estimates for the proportion of people with FASD in the general Australian population.

Research indicates that early diagnosis (i.e., before the age of 6 years) is associated with improved quality of life, fewer behavioural problems and co‐occurring conditions and reduced challenges at school (e.g., [4]). To standardise approaches to FASD diagnosis in Australia, the Australian Guide to Diagnosis of FASD was developed in 2016 [5] (herein referred to as the 2016 Guide). Although initial stakeholder consultation was undertaken [6], the 2016 Guide was primarily adapted from the Canadian FASD Guidelines [7], which also includes elements from the University of Washington's 4‐Digit Diagnostic Code [8]. For instance, the 2016 Guide adopted the same diagnostic terminology, neurodevelopmental criteria, and excluded physical size as a diagnostic feature—aligning with the Canadian Guidelines. However, unlike the Canadian Guidelines, the 2016 Guide did not include a minimum threshold for prenatal alcohol exposure. The Canadian Guidelines recommend considering diagnosis when there is an ‘estimated dose at a level known to be associated with neurodevelopmental effects’, specifying a threshold of 7 or more drinks per week, or 2 or more episodes ≥ 4 drinks an occasion. Key elements from the 4‐Digit Code that were retained in the 2016 Guide include the recommended tools and assessment approaches for facial dysmorphology. In 2020, the Australian Department of Health and Aged Care tasked a national consortium with reviewing and updating the 2016 Guide to ensure alignment with current evidence and international standards.

The review process adhered to the procedures and requirements for meeting the National Health and Medical Research Council Standards for Clinical Practice Guidelines [9]. This involved rigorous reviews of the best available evidence [10, 11, 12, 13] and consultation with a wide range of key stakeholders [14]. To further inform the development and implementation of new national clinical practice guidelines, it was important to understand how the 2016 Guide was used in practice. Despite significant resources being invested in the development of clinical practice guidelines, research shows that they are often underutilised [15, 16]. Guideline uptake is more likely when implementation efforts are informed by specific determinants, such as facilitators (also known as enablers) or barriers to guideline use [15, 17].

There has been limited research examining clinicians' experiences of using the 2016 Guide. One study [18] focused on the outcomes and needs of health and education professionals (n = 52) following FASD‐specific training and included two open‐ended questions inquiring about what practitioners found helpful about the 2016 Guide and any barriers that were experienced. Practitioners reported that helpful elements included the clear presentation and content, associated training and the consensus on diagnosis it provided for the country. Reported barriers included lack of time and resources to undertake assessments according to the recommended approach, concerns about the evidence underpinning some of the neurodevelopmental domains, lack of consideration of individuals with more ‘moderate impairments’ and the 2016 Guide's appropriateness for First Nations Australians and people from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds [18]. Another study [19] surveyed Australian psychologists (n = 106) to understand their knowledge and practices in the assessment and diagnosis of FASD and included a question assessing familiarity with the Guide. While most clinicians (60.4%) reported familiarity with the 2016 Guide, the majority (58%) were not confident in their ability to assess for prenatal alcohol exposure, and only 31% felt confident in conducting assessments required for a diagnosis of FASD. These findings indicate the need for further support to help Australian psychologists to feel confident and capable to undertake the assessment and diagnosis of FASD. The aim of the current study was to undertake a comprehensive survey of Australian clinicians to understand how the 2016 Guide is applied in clinical practice, including the factors influencing its use and key barriers and enablers to its implementation.

Methods

Study Design and Data Collection Methods

This was a cross‐sectional online survey in Australia. Data collection was undertaken between March 2022 to August 2023 using a validated questionnaire—The Clinician Guideline Determinants Questionnaire (CGDQ) [15]. The CGDQ includes four sections: (i) clinician demographic and background information; (ii) 26 close‐ended items reflecting clinician and guideline‐specific determinants; (iii) four open‐ended questions on perceived enablers and barriers; and (iv) three items on preferred learning style (i.e., preferred sources of guideline information). Supporting Information 1 provides a copy of the CGDQ as applied in the current study.

Sample Characteristics

Australian health practitioners who may be involved in the assessment and diagnostic process for FASD were eligible to participate. This included medical professionals (i.e., paediatricians, psychiatrists, general practitioners, nurse practitioners) and allied health professionals (i.e., psychologists, speech pathologists, occupational therapists, physiotherapists and social workers).

Survey Administration

Data were collected and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) [20] hosted at The University of Queensland. Practitioners were recruited via the Australian FASD Guidelines Project Advisory Groups, relevant Australian health professional associations (e.g., Australasian Society for Developmental Paediatrics, Royal Australian College of General Practitioners, Australian Psychological Society), relevant clinician Facebook groups and FASD‐related organisations (e.g., The FASD Hub, National Organisation for FASD Australia). A snowballing recruitment strategy was used, whereby clinicians were encouraged to share the survey link with colleagues in their networks.

Statistical Analysis and Ethical Approval

Quantitative data were summarised descriptively. Data were categorical nominal/dichotomous or ordinal (7‐point Likert scale, including a ‘not sure’ response option). Ordinal data assessing the determinants of Guide use were grouped into three overarching response groups ‘agree’, ‘neutral’ and ‘unsure/disagree’. Responses were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Chi‐squared tests were used to explore associations between professional characteristics and a selection of key determinants of Guide use. Content analysis [21] was used to analyse responses from the open‐ended questions exploring enablers and barriers to Guide use. This is a common analytic approach for open‐ended survey questionnaires, whereby key words are identified and quantified to summarise participant responses [21]. Responses were imported into Microsoft Excel, where J.L. conducted the initial coding. Key words and phrases were highlighted and grouped according to content categories. Frequencies of each content area were then tallied to quantify their prevalence across responses. The coding framework was reviewed and refined in collaboration with N.R. All other authors then reviewed the results and provided further refinement on content area labels and data presentation. Ethical clearance was granted by the Children's Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC/20CHQ69561).

Results

Respondent Characteristics

A total of 333 survey sessions were initiated. Of these, 232 (70%) provided consent, with 136 (41%) completing the survey. A further 11 participants partially completed the survey. Sample demographics are presented in Table 1. Most participants identified as female (n = 111, 76%) and were in their mid‐career stage (n = 81, 55%). Most respondents were paediatricians (n = 54, 37%) or psychologists (n = 49, 33%), working across public and private sectors. Survey respondents considered FASD as a possible diagnosis in their clinical assessments most commonly on a weekly (n = 53, 36%) or monthly (n = 52, 35%) basis. Most participants reported regularly using the 2016 Guide (n = 81, 55%). A small number of participants (n = 9, 6%) were not aware of the 2016 Guide prior to this survey.

Determinants of Guide Use

Table 1 provides an overview of responses. A majority of participants agreed with the content of the 2016 Guide (71.3%) and believed it would improve care delivery (76.2%) and patient outcomes (71.3%). Most participants felt the Guide provided advantages to their practice, while only 32.2% perceived disadvantages, suggesting overall positive sentiment towards its utility. Nearly 90% reported having the general knowledge needed to use the Guide, and 83.2% felt confident in their skills. However, only 69.9% had received formal training. A majority of participants saw the Guide as part of their professional responsibility. However, fewer people reported its use among colleagues (59.4% within their organisation and 56.6% outside), highlighting potential variability in adoption. Although 60.8% of respondents felt they had autonomy to implement the guide, only 49% felt supported by their organisation, suggesting structural barriers to implementation. Most participants found the Guide easy to navigate (65%) and the tools helpful (56.6%), though only 53.1% found the wording clear, 52.4% felt the procedures were easy to incorporate into practice, and only 44.1% agreed that the Guide clearly describes the underlying evidence. Just over half of respondents (51%–54%) believe the Guide aligned with patient values and that patients would accept its recommendations, but around one‐third disagreed, indicating mixed perceptions of patient receptivity. The lowest area of agreement was found for patient preferences, with just 21% believing that the Guide adequately addressed patient preferences.

Enablers and Barriers

Table 2 provides an overview of the most commonly reported free text responses regarding the enablers and barriers to use of the 2016 Guide. Supporting Information 2 provides details of all responses. The most commonly reported enabler to guideline use was having guidance that is easy to follow and user friendly (n = 45, 36.3%). This was followed by ease of access (n = 18, 14.5%) and having the required knowledge, skills or familiarity with the Guide (n = 14, 11.3%). Clinicians reported that the most common barrier was lack of access to appropriate clinicians, lack of clinician time and costs associated with lengthy assessment processes (n = 25, 24.8%). This was followed by some clinicians reporting that the current Guide was not evidence‐based and required updating (n = 16, 15.8%) and lack of familiarity, capability or skills to be able to apply the Guide (n = 8, 7.9%).

Learning Styles

Most participants (n = 95, 34.7%) would prefer to learn about guidelines through conferences, followed by peer reviewed publications (n = 63, 22.9%), viewing guidelines online (n = 57, 20.8%) and receiving an email about the guidelines (n = 44, 16.1%). Practitioners reported consulting with colleagues as the most common source of information (n = 117, 16.9%), followed by scientific literature (n = 105, 15.2%), the 2016 Australian Guide (n = 104, 15.1%) and conferences (n = 77, 11.2%). The preferred guideline formats were to have software (n = 78, 33.2%) or a software application (n = 35, 14.9%) to support implementation of the guidelines, accessing online (n = 73, 31%) and in print (n = 46, 19.6%) (Table 3).

Discussion

The aim of the current study was to survey Australian clinicians to understand the determinants of use and key barriers and enablers to implementation of the existing Australian Guide to Diagnosis of FASD [5]. This was achieved through the use of a validated questionnaire—the CGDQ. To the authors' knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively document the determinants of use of the 2016 Guide and the first time the CGDQ has been applied in an Australian context. Overall, most clinicians in the current study reported having the knowledge, skills and training required and were confident in using the 2016 Guide. However, several areas for improvement were noted, such as the need for greater consideration of patient preferences, improved description of the underlying evidence supporting the recommendations, and increased organisational support to facilitate the use of the Guide in practice. The findings underscore the value of clinician input in informing the review and future implementation of national guidelines for the assessment and diagnosis of FASD, as well as clinical practice guidelines more generally.

Few studies have previously examined clinician experiences with the 2016 Guide. Reid et al. [18] identified enablers and barriers through two open‐ended questions surveying a smaller sample of health and education professionals who had completed FASD‐specific training. Similar barriers, including time and resource constraints, concerns about the evidence base, and cultural responsiveness were identified. These concerns also aligned with stakeholder priorities for the Guide's review [14], such as revising diagnostic criteria, including the neurodevelopmental domains, definitions of neurodevelopmental impairment, prenatal alcohol exposure criteria and sentinel facial features criteria.

Kerimofski et al. [19] surveyed Australian psychologists, finding that although most were familiar with the 2016 Guide, many lacked confidence in assessing for prenatal alcohol exposure and conducting assessments required for a diagnosis of FASD. These findings contrast with the current study, where the majority of surveyed clinicians reported regularly using the 2016 Guide to complete assessments and felt confident in doing so. These differences may reflect variation in the professional backgrounds across the study samples, with the majority of participants in Kerimofski et al. [19] reported working in school/education settings. Notably, clinical neuropsychologists in their sample demonstrated greater knowledge and confidence compared to school and other psychologists, suggesting differences in training and professional roles.

Consistent with previous research in other clinical contexts, clinicians in the current study preferred learning about guidelines through conferences, peer‐reviewed publications, and online access, with peer consultation being the most common source of information [22, 23]. These preferences align with Ockene et al.'s [24] emphasis on interactive, context‐specific education to support guideline uptake. However, Ockene and colleagues also highlight that although traditional educational approaches such as lectures and online or print materials raise awareness and knowledge, these may not be sufficient in changing clinical behaviour without opportunities for skill‐building and peer engagement. Our findings also support this, with clinicians expressing interest in digital tools such as software applications to support guideline implementation. Together, these findings highlight the need for a multi‐faceted approach that combines clinician education, digital tools and structural support to enhance guideline uptake.

There have been a number of previous studies in other clinical areas that have applied the Clinician Guidelines Determinants Questionnaire [22, 23, 25]. Across studies, clinicians consistently endorsed determinants such as general knowledge, ease of implementation and responsibility. However, organisational support and perceived alignment with patient preferences were weaker areas. Notably, our study had the lowest agreement regarding patient preferences, highlighting a critical area for improvement in future guideline revisions and providing an opportunity to align future guidelines with person‐centred care principles. Patient and family preferences are an important component of high‐quality care [26] and incorporating these into guidelines, such as through the inclusion of shared decision‐making approaches and tools [27] could improve uptake.

Strengths and Limitations

This study represents the largest and most comprehensive survey to date of Australian clinicians' experiences and determinants of using the 2016 Guide. A key strength was the use of a validated questionnaire, providing detailed information to inform development and future dissemination and implementation of the forthcoming Australian clinical practice guidelines for assessment and diagnosis of FASD. However, the length of the questionnaire may have contributed to survey dropout, as evidenced by partial completions and instances where clinicians consented but did not proceed. Another explanatory factor for the survey dropout rate may be that clinicians did not feel they had sufficient awareness or knowledge of the 2016 Guide to complete the survey. Anecdotal feedback from clinicians recruited through general clinician online social media (Facebook) groups indicated concerns regarding this issue. Future research could explore the development of a brief version of the survey and provide additional recruitment information to ensure all levels of awareness and knowledge of the guidelines are captured. Additionally, while the majority of participants who took part in the current study were psychologists and paediatricians, reflecting current workforce participation in FASD assessment and diagnosis in Australia, it would be beneficial to include input from a broader range of practitioners, from those in training programmes through to experienced clinicians. This would help facilitate understanding on how to increase use of the guidelines among a more diverse range of clinicians across Australia and its utility as a clinical training resource.

Implications and Future Research Directions

The findings from this study highlight the importance of clinician feedback in the ongoing development and implementation of new national clinical practice guidelines for the assessment and diagnosis of FASD. Although most clinicians in the current study reported regularly using the 2016 Guide and feeling confident in its application, their feedback has provided valuable information for revising and enhancing new clinical practice guidelines. National clinical practice guidelines are critical for ensuring consistent diagnostic approaches for FASD and increasing diagnostic capacity across healthcare settings. The National Health and Medical Research Council process of developing the guidelines [9] ensures that rigorous evidence‐based guidelines are developed. However, successful implementation requires an understanding of the specific determinants that influence clinician uptake and use in practice.

To support the adoption of the new clinical practice guidelines, it will be important to employ a range of implementation strategies, including providing training workshops and resources to support local training initiatives. Additionally, developing user‐friendly summaries and decision support tools will facilitate easy reference and application in clinical settings. Establishing peer supervision and support networks will also encourage ongoing learning and adherence to the guidelines [17, 28].

Stakeholder engagement and feedback are critical throughout the guideline development process to ensure that recommendations are both evidence‐based and feasible in real‐world clinical settings. Engaging clinicians and consumer representatives throughout the revision process can help identify potential barriers early, refine recommendations to be more user‐friendly, and ensure that the guidelines are person‐centred. The current finding that only a small proportion of clinicians agreed the 2016 Guide reflected patient preferences further underscores the need for meaningful consumer input. Further consultation and co‐designing implementation strategies with a broad range of stakeholders will be essential to optimising the impact, accessibility and relevance of the new national clinical practice guidelines [29].

To further enhance the understanding and implementation of new national clinical practice guidelines, future research could focus on several key areas. First, exploring perspectives from various sectors such as health, education, child protection and justice systems. Individuals with FASD can interact with multiple systems and understanding how different barriers and facilitators to guideline use across these sectors could improve interdisciplinary collaboration in the assessment, diagnosis and ongoing care of individuals with FASD. Second, examining differences in guideline use across rural and remote areas of Australia compared to metropolitan regions is important. Identifying unique challenges and opportunities in these different contexts could inform tailored implementation strategies and support equitable access to FASD diagnosis and care across all regions. At last, as the CGDQ is designed for use at multiple timepoints in the guideline development process, a follow‐up study could be undertaken to repeat the CGDQ following dissemination and implementation of the new national clinical practice guidelines. This would assess changes in the determinants of guideline use and identify areas for further improvement, supporting continuous improvement of assessment and diagnostic practices in Australia.

Conclusion

In this study, we explored the determinants of using the Australian Guide to Diagnosis of FASD [5]. The findings provide valuable insights from clinicians' experiences and perspectives, which can inform the development, dissemination and implementation of new national clinical practice guidelines for assessment and diagnosis of FASD. Our research highlights several key factors that impact the adoption and implementation of the Guide in clinical practice. Organisational support, including availability of resources, peer support and collaboration influences utilisation of the Guide. Additionally, identifying barriers such as time constraints, costs and complexity of assessments, as well as feedback from some clinicians regarding the evidence base of the 2016 Guide, offers important directions for enhancing future use of the revised guidelines. Furthermore, our study underscores the importance of stakeholder engagement in the review and development of new national clinical practice guidelines. By addressing the identified determinants of use and incorporating feedback from practitioners, it is hoped that the new guidelines will be more effectively implemented. This collaborative approach ensures that the guidelines are not only evidence‐based but also tailored to the needs and realities of current clinical practice. In summary, understanding and addressing the factors influencing the use of the 2016 Guide is essential for improving clinical practice and ensuring better outcomes for individuals with FASD. Through continuous engagement with stakeholders and a commitment to refining the guidelines based on clinician feedback, the quality of care and support provided for individuals with FASD and their families can be enhanced.
