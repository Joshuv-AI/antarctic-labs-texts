Urethral prolapse following female genital mutilation in a 9-year-old Somali girl: A rare case report
Authors: Abdishakur Mohamed Abdi, Abdisalam Ismail Hassan, Abdirahman Ahmed Mohamud, Ismail Mohamoud Abdullahi, Mohamed Rage Ahmed, Ahmed Muhammad Bashir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Urethral prolapse (UP) is a rare benign condition, typically seen in prepubertal girls and postmenopausal women, with estrogen deficiency and raised intra-abdominal pressure as common factors. We report the first documented case of UP in a child following female genital mutilation (FGM). A 9-year-old Somali girl presented with painful genital swelling six months after undergoing type II FGM. Examination confirmed UP, and surgical excision was performed successfully. Recovery was uneventful, with no recurrence at six months. This case highlights FGM as a potential risk factor for UP and underscores the importance of early recognition and timely surgical intervention.

Introduction

Urethral prolapse (UP) is a rare, clinical, and benign condition that might have negative consequences if not treated properly. It manifests as a round, doughnut-shape secondary to total distal urethral mucosa protrusion through the external meatus of the urethra.1,2

Urethral prolapse has been documented in both prepubertal girls and postmenopausal women. Prepubertal girls are frequently asymptomatic, whereas postmenopausal women are often symptomatic, commonly presenting with vaginal bleeding and irreducible, painful, round, donut-shaped protrusions. In more severe cases, they may develop severe pain, visible thrombosis, necrosis of the tissue, and distress.3,4

Female genital mutilation (FGM) is any procedure that damages the female genital organs or involves partial or complete removal of a woman's or girl's external genitalia for non-medical reasons. It is a violation of women's human rights, and an estimated 200 million girls worldwide have undergone FGM.5,6

We report a rare and complex case of urethral prolapse in a 9-year-old girl with a history of female genital mutilation (FGM), aiming to explore a possible association between the two conditions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of urethral prolapse in a child with prior FGM reported from Somalia. This case underscores the importance of early recognition, culturally sensitive evaluation, and comprehensive management in similar settings.

Methods

The study was reported in accordance with the Surgical Case Report (SCARE) criteria, maintaining adherence to standardized guidelines for reporting surgical case studies.7

Case presentation

A 9-year-old Somali girl was brought by her mother to the pediatric outpatient clinic with a one-week history of progressive genital swelling and pain. The mother reported noticing a protruding mass in the genital area that had gradually increased in size. The child was uncomfortable but had no fever, urinary retention, or other systemic symptoms. She was referred to the pediatric surgery department for further evaluation.

The patient's demographic details included female sex, Somali ethnicity, and school-age status. She had no known allergies, and her family and medical history were unremarkable, except for a history of traditional female genital mutilation (type II), which involves partial or total removal of the clitoral glans and the labia minora was performed six months earlier. There was no history of previous hospitalizations, medications, or other surgical procedures.

On examination by the pediatric surgeon, a tender, irreducible, circumferential mucosal bulge was observed protruding from the urethral meatus (Fig. 1). Diagnostic assessment was primarily clinical. Routine preoperative laboratory investigations (complete blood count, renal function tests, and urinalysis) were within normal limits. Financial constraints limited the use of imaging studies; however, the diagnosis was considered clear based on clinical findings. Differential diagnoses included vaginal prolapse, ureterocele, and rhabdomyosarcoma, but these were excluded on clinical grounds. The findings were consistent with a diagnosis of urethral prolapse.

After counseling, the family was reassured about the treatable nature of the condition, and surgical management was planned. Preoperatively, the patient was optimized with nil per os for 10 hours and intravenous antibiotics. The surgery was performed in the operating theater of Mogadishu Somali-Türkiye Recep Tayyip Erdoğan Training and Research Hospital by a pediatric surgeon with over eight years’ experience.

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the lithotomy position. The genital area was prepared with povidone-iodine and draped under sterile conditions. A urinary catheter was inserted to aid in urethral identification and protection (Fig. 2). Four stay sutures were placed at the quadrants of the prolapsed mucosa to facilitate excision. Discussion

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), female genital mutilation (FGM) is practiced in at least 30 countries, primarily across Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Asia. It is estimated that over 200 million girls and women alive today have undergone some form of FGM.8 The WHO classifies FGM into four major types; our patient had undergone Type II FGM, which involves the partial or total removal of the clitoral glans and labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora. 8Somalia has one of the highest prevalence rates of FGM in the world, with approximately 98 % of women and girls affected, making it both a major public health issue and a violation of human rights.8

Female genital mutilation (FGM) can lead to serious health problems, both immediately and over time. In the short term, girls may experience intense pain, heavy bleeding, swelling, or infections like tetanus and sepsis.8, 9, 10, 11 As they grow older, many continue to face issues such as painful urination, recurrent urinary tract infections, and menstrual difficulties caused by scarring.8

Sexual health is often affected as well, with pain during intercourse and reduced satisfaction being common.8,11 Childbirth becomes riskier, with a higher chance of prolonged labor, excessive bleeding, cesarean delivery, or even newborn loss.8,11 The psychological effects can be long-lasting—many women live with anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, or post-traumatic stress. Some may also struggle with infertility or chronic reproductive problems.8,11

In our case, we believe the urethral prolapse was likely linked to the patient's history of FGM. The procedure may have damaged or weakened the normal anatomy of the perineum, altering local tissue support and predisposing her to prolapse. This highlights the importance of recognizing FGM not just as a cultural practice but as a significant medical risk factor that can affect girls and women throughout their lives.

Urethral prolapse mostly affects prepubertal Black girls or postmenopausal white women, with a higher incidence in girls of African origin.2,12,13

The etiology of urethral prolapse remains unclear; however, numerous explanations are proposed to explain why it occurs.14

Estrogen deficiency remains the most common explanation, reflecting the higher incidence of urethral prolapse during the prepubertal and postmenopausal periods.2

Another theory suggests a congenital defect in urethral architecture, resulting in a weak attachment between the inner and outer smooth muscle layers of the urethra, a neuromuscular abnormality, and a deficiency in elastic tissue.15

In addition to these intrinsic factors, several acquired risk factors may contribute to urethral prolapse, including conditions that cause elevated intra-abdominal pressure, such as a chronic cough, asthma, obesity, and constipation, as well as malnutrition, diarrhea, and urinary tract infection.14 In our patient, none of these commonly reported risk factors were identified. There was no history of chronic illness, respiratory issues, constipation, or malnutrition. Instead, we believe the underlying contributing factor may have been her previous history of type II female genital mutilation. The scarring and disruption of normal perineal anatomy resulting from FGM may have altered local tissue support and urethral dynamics, predisposing her to prolapse. This highlights the need to consider culturally specific risk factors in affected populations.

The typical presenting symptoms are vaginal bleeding and vaginal masses, while most prepubertal girls are asymptomatic, and the urethral prolapse is unknowingly identified as a round, soft, and plain red-colored mass, donut-shaped, from the vagina, measuring 0.5 to several centimeters in diameter, protruding from the distal urethra.12,15

The most common way of diagnosing urethral prolapse is physical examination and the detection of a soft circular mass of tissue surrounding the urethral meatus, which is typically in the middle and apart from the vaginal entrance.16 The diagnosis may appear clear-cut; however, misdiagnosis is still common.15 In our case, a physical examination was used to make the diagnosis.

The management of urethral prolapse remains somewhat controversial and is generally divided into conservative and surgical approaches.15 The choice of treatment depends largely on the severity of symptoms and the extent of the prolapse.14 Conservative management, which includes sitz baths, topical estrogen, and anti-inflammatory preparations, is often effective in patients with mild or asymptomatic presentations.17,18 However, several studies have highlighted the limitations of conservative therapy, particularly in cases with complications or poor response to initial treatment.3,19,20 While spontaneous resolution may occur in some instances,17 surgical intervention is typically indicated for complicated or persistent cases. Surgical excision of the prolapsed mucosa has been shown to be safe, with a low rate of recurrence and favorable outcomes when performed appropriately.16

There are two surgical options for treating urethral prolapse: a ligation over a Foley catheter or knife excision of the prolapsed urethral mucosa, either with or without a urethral catheter. 21,22 Recently, a modified approach was described that involved cutting the prolapse into elliptical segments in each of the four quadrants.20 In our case, we inserted a urinary catheter to aid in the identification and protection of the urethra. Four stay sutures were placed at the quadrants of the prolapsed mucosa to facilitate excision. The prolapsed tissue was excised circumferentially, and the urethral mucosa was sutured using interrupted Vicryl 5-0 sutures. The postoperative course was uneventful. The urinary catheter was removed on postoperative day five, and the patient was discharged in stable condition.

Strengths

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case worldwide of urethral prolapse occurring after female genital mutilation (FGM), making it highly novel and relevant to global surgical and women's health literature.

The report provides a clear and detailed surgical description, including operative technique, suture choice, and postoperative management, which enhances reproducibility for clinicians.

The patient was followed for six months with no recurrence, strengthening the reliability of the reported outcome.

The discussion situates the case in both a local (Somalia) and global context, addressing the intersection between cultural practices, pediatric urology, and women's health.

Limitations

Being a single case, generalizability is limited, and a causal relationship between FGM and urethral prolapse cannot be definitively established.

Preoperative imaging was not available, reducing the visual illustration of findings.

Longer-term follow-up beyond six months would provide stronger evidence for recurrence risk and long-term functional outcomes.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare and likely underrecognized complication of FGM, urethral prolapse, in a young girl from Somalia. In the absence of traditional risk factors, prior FGM appears to have contributed to anatomical weakening, making urethral prolapse more likely. The successful surgical outcome emphasizes that, while the condition is treatable, early recognition is essential.

Given the high prevalence of FGM in Somalia and similar regions, clinicians should be aware of this potential association. Further documentation and research are needed to clarify the relationship between FGM and urogenital complications and to inform culturally responsive healthcare strategies.

Patient perspective

The patient's mother reported significant anxiety upon seeing the genital mass, fearing a serious illness. After surgery, she expressed great relief and satisfaction with her daughter's recovery, noting improvement in both comfort and emotional well-being.
