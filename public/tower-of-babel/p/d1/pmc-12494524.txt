tRF-34-86J8WPMN1E8Y2Q promotes the occurrence and development of gastric cancer by combining with LRAT
Authors: Chunli Cao, Shengli Xu, Zhe Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA large number of studies have demonstrated that tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs) are implicated in the progression and nociception associated with various malignancies. However, the biological role of tRF-34-86J8WPMN1E8Y2Q (tRF-34) in cancer, particularly in gastric cancer (GC), remains elusive and warrants investigation. This study aimed to investigate the clinical and mechanistic significance of tRF-34 in GC pathogenesis and potential as novel biomarker for early detection.MethodsThe quantitative expression levels of tRF-34 in GC cell lines, tissues, and plasma were determined using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Subsequently, the functional impact of tRF-34 downregulation on GC progression was investigated using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays to assess cell proliferation and transwell assays to evaluate migration and invasion. Downstream target genes of tRF-34 were identified through database analysis and validated using RNA Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation (RIP) experiments.ResultstRF-34 exhibits elevated expression levels in GC tissues cells and preoperative (1 day) plasma compared with normal counterparts and postoperative (10 day) plasma. Also, it was associated with neural and vascular invasion in patients with GC. Moreover, tRF-34 downregulation impedes the proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities of GC cells. Mechanistically, RIP and bioinformatic analyses demonstrated that tRF-34 directly bound to lecithin retinol acyltransferase (LRAT), and this interaction promoted the progression of GC.ConclusiontRF-34 has high expression specificity in GC and the potential to be a biomarker for early detection. Introduction

Cancer poses a significant health burden globally. Cancer-related pain in advanced stages is one of the most debilitating and distressing clinical manifestations (Ruano et al. 2022). Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies of the gastrointestinal tract. The 2022 epidemiological data revealed over 968,000 new cases of GC worldwide, with mortality approaching 660,000 cases. This establishes GC as the fifth most common neoplasm in terms of both incidence and mortality globally (Bray et al. 2024). Although the diagnostic rates have improved with the widespread adoption of endoscopic techniques including gastroscopy, the detection of early-stage GC remains suboptimal. This underscores the critical need for identifying reliable biomarkers for early detection (Smyth et al. 2020).

Emerging evidence has demonstrated differential expression profiles of various noncoding RNAs in malignant tumors, where they play regulatory roles in tumor progression. These include long noncoding RNAs, microRNAs (miRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs) (Fu et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2019; Zou et al. 2022). Among these, transfer RNA-derived fragments (tRFs) represent a novel class of small noncoding RNAs (20–40 nucleotides in length) generated through the enzymatic cleavage of precursor or mature tRNAs. tRFs, initially considered as merely degradation by-products, are now recognized for their functional roles in protein synthesis and their regulatory potential (Xie et al. 2020).

tRFs are classified into five major subtypes (i-tRF, tRF-1, tRF-2, tRF-3, and tRF-5) based on their cleavage patterns by specific nucleases including angiopoietin, Dicer, and other RNA-processing enzymes (Zhang et al. 2022). Mechanistically, tRFs share functional similarities with miRNAs, capable of modulating gene expression through protein-mRNA interactions, cell cycle regulation, and chromatin/epigenetic modifications (Xie et al. 2020).

Recent studies elucidated specific oncogenic roles of tRFs. In colorectal cancer (CRC), tRF-3022b promotes tumor growth and M2 macrophage polarization by binding to LGALS1 and MIF (Lu et al. 2022). In contrast, Dicer-processed tRF-3013b exhibits tumor-suppressive effects by targeting TPRG1L, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic and therapeutic target in gallbladder cancer (GBC) (Lu et al. 2022), . Despite these advances, the functional characterization of tRFs in GC, particularly their diagnostic value, remains largely unexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically investigate the clinical and mechanistic significance of tRFs in GC pathogenesis and their potential as novel biomarkers (Zou et al. 2022).

This study demonstrated significant overexpression of tRF-34-86J8WPMN1E8Y2Q (tRF-34) in GC tissues. tRF-34 exhibited potent oncogenic properties by enhancing malignant phenotypes, including cellular proliferation, migration and invasion, in vitro. These findings suggested tRF-34 may be a promising molecular target for both diagnostic and therapeutic applications in GC management. Molecular interaction studies further revealed the interaction of tRF-34 with LRAT, functionally contributing to GC pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Human specimens

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University (No. 2023176 A-02). Both GC tissues and plasma specimens were collected from patients diagnosed with GC at the First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University, all of whom had not undergone any prior radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Inclusion criteria required a confirmed histopathological diagnosis of gastric cancer. Tissue samples were collected by experienced clinicians and immediately placed in RNA storage reagent (CWBIO, Jiangsu, China). Plasma samples were centrifuged promptly after collection. All specimens were stored at -80 °C until further analysis. Pathological staging was determined for each patient according to the 8th edition of the AJCC TNM Classification of Malignant Tumors. All participants were informed of the study’s purpose and procedures and provided written informed consent.

Cell lines and culture conditions

The human normal gastric mucosal epithelial cells (GES-1) and GC cells (AGS and HGC-27) used in this study were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA). All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol® reagent or TRIzol® LS reagent (Invitrogen, Karlsruhe, Germany). For each reverse transcription reaction, 2 µg of total RNA was converted to cDNA using the the GoTaq 2-Step RT-qPCR System (A5001, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed by the GoTaq qPCR Master Mix (A6002, Promega). U6 small nuclear RNA (snRNA) was used as an internal control. The relative expression levels of target genes in cells were calculated using the 2^(–ΔΔCq) method, while expression levels in plasma and tissue samples were determined using the –ΔCq method Primer sequences used in this study are shown in Table S1.

Cell transfection with mimics or inhibitor

The mimics and inhibitor of tRF-34 (Table S2) were synthesized from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Cell transfections were carried out using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfection efficiency was examined by qRT-PCR.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic sub-localization

Total RNA from nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions was isolated using the PARIS™ Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated RNA from each compartment was subsequently analyzed by qRT-PCR. U6 snRNA served as the nuclear reference control, while GAPDH mRNA was used as the cytoplasmic control.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

The proliferative capacity of gastric cancer cells was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Transfected cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well. Following the prescribed incubation periods, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Kumamoto, Japan) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance of each well was measured daily at consistent time points (0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h) using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) at a wavelength of 450 nm. Optical density (OD) values were recorded for further analysis.

Cell migration assay

Cell migration was assessed using Transwell chambers equipped with 8 μm pore size polycarbonate membranes (Chemicon, Temecula, CA, USA). Following digestion and resuspension, transfected cells were seeded in the upper chamber. The chamber was then placed into a 24-well plate containing medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) as a chemoattractant and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the chambers were carefully removed, and the cells on the upper surface were gently wiped away. The migrated cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and imaged under an inverted light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The number of cells was counted.

Cell invasion assay

Cell invasion was evaluated using Transwell chambers (Chemicon) precoated with Matrigel (Costar, Corning, NY, USA). Following digestion and resuspension, transfected cells were seeded into the upper chamber at a defined density in 200 µL of serum-free medium. The chamber was then placed in a 24-well plate containing medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) as a chemoattractant and incubated for 48 h. After incubation, non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed. The invaded cells on the lower surface were fixed, stained, and imaged under a microscope. The number of cells invading the stromal gel was calculated.

RIP assay

To investigate the interaction between tRF-34 and LRAT, RIP was performed using an AGO2 antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) with an anti-IgG antibody (Millipore) serving as a negative control. Both antibodies were conjugated to magnetic beads and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The co-precipitated RNA complexes were subsequently isolated, and the enrichment of LRAT was quantified via qRT-PCR.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

The corresponding plasmid constructs, LRAT-WT (wild-type) and LRAT-Mut (mutant), containing the putative tRF-34 binding sequence, were synthesized by GenePharma. After co-transfection into cells, luciferase activity was measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit to evaluate the binding interaction.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., CA, USA). Differences between or among groups were assessed using the Student’s t-test, chi-square test, or one-way ANOVA, as appropriate based on data type and distribution. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. P < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

tRF-34 was highly expressed in GC and showed potential as a diagnostic marker

Previous studies have demonstrated the involvement of tRF-34 in GC pathogenesis. A comprehensive literature review led to the identification of sequencing data published by Cao et al., where tRF-34 was significantly upregulated in GC tissues compared with normal gastric mucosa (Xu et al. 2021). Given the lack of reliable diagnostic biomarkers in clinical practice, tRF-34 was selected in this study to investigate its potential as a novel diagnostic biomarker with improved efficacy. The MINTbase database search revealed that tRF-34 was derived from the 5’ end of tiRNA-Glu-TTC-001 and consisted of 34 bases, with its shear site located in the anticodon loop (Fig. 1a). TA-cloning sequencing proved that the product was tRF-34 (Fig. 1b). Subsequently, the expression of tRF-34 in GC and adjacent paracancerous tissues was examined using qRT-PCR. The results indicated the upregulation of tRF-34 expression in 41 pairs of cancer tissues (Fig. 1c). The plasma analysis demonstrated markedly elevated tRF-34 expression levels in preoperative patients with GC (day 1) compared with postoperative patients (day 10) and healthy controls. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between postoperative patients and healthy controls (Fig. 1d), suggesting potential normalization of tRF-34 expression following tumor resection. Subsequently, qRT-PCR was performed to further detect tRF-34 expression in GC cells. The results demonstrated elevation of tRF-34 expression in GC cells compared with GES-1 cells (Fig. 1e). The results of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis showed that the area under the curve, sensitivity, and specificity of tRF-34 were 0.6905, 0.2222, and 0.9643, respectively. tRF-34 was found to be more specific for diagnosing patients with GC (Fig. 1f). The relationship between tRF-34 expression levels in the tissue of patients with GC and the clinicopathological characteristics of the corresponding patients was analyzed. tRF-34 expression levels were significantly associated with nerve invasion (P = 0.002) and vascular invasion (P = 0.005), but exhibited no association with other clinical and pathological factors such as age, sex, stage, and pathological type (Table 1).

Downregulation of tRF-34 expression inhibited GC cell proliferation

Considering the high expression of tRF-34 in GC tissues, tRF-34 is presumed to play a role in GC progression. Therefore, tRF-34 mimics and inhibitors were designed to explore its biological functions. The results of qRT-PCR after transfection of mimics or inhibitors showed significant upregulation or downregulation of tRF-34 expression in GC cells (Fig. 2a and b). It demonstrated that the design and synthesis of mimics and inhibitor were successful. CCK-8 assay was used to measure cell proliferation ability. The results showed that the downregulation of tRF-34 led to the opposite result (Fig. 2c and d). However, the downregulation of tRF-34 expression had no significant effect on the proliferative capability of GES-1 cells (Fig. 2e).

Effects of tRF-34 on the proliferation, migration, and invasion in GC and GES-1 cells. a The qRT-PCR results showed the upregulation efficiency of GC and GES-1 cells by transfected with mimics-tRF-34. b The qRT-PCR results showed the downregulation efficiency of GC and AGS cells by transfected with inhibitor-tRF-34. c Effect of inhibitor-tRF-34 on the proliferation of AGS cells tested by CCK-8 assay. d Effect of inhibitor-tRF-34 on the proliferation of HGC-27 cells tested by CCK-8 assay. e Effect of inhibitor-tRF-34 on the proliferation of GES-1 cells tested by CCK-8 assay

Downregulation of tRF-34 inhibited the migration and invasion of GC cells

Transwell migration assays showed that the downregulation of tRF-34 expression decreased the migration ability of AGS and HGC-27 cells (Fig. 3a). Transwell invasion assays were also performed. The results showed that the downregulation of tRF-34 expression decreased the invasion ability of AGS and HGC-27 cells (Fig. 3b). However, the results of both experiments suggested that altering tRF-34 expression did not influence the migration and invasion of GES-1 cells, which was in accordance with the results of CCK-8 assays (Fig. 3a and b).

Explore the mechanism of tRF-34

To further explore the mechanism of tRF-34, functional data prediction and assays were conducted. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that the most enriched terms of target genes of tRF-34 were “transforming growth factor beta receptor signaling pathway” in biological process (BP) category, “clathrin-coated endocytic vesicle membrane” in cellular component (CC) category and “growth factor binding” in the molecular function (MF) category (Fig. 4a). Some pathways were obtained in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, and the ten most significant were displayed (Fig. 4b). The apelin signaling pathway has been implicated in tumorigenesis across various cancer types, including pancreatic cancer (PC) (Chaves-Almagro et al. 2022). Comprehensive bioinformatic analysis using four independent databases was employed to predict the potential target genes of tRF-34, with Venn intersection analysis identifying five consensus candidate genes (Fig. 4c), among which LRAT expression was significantly downregulated in GC (Fig. 4d). Further analysis using the Sangerbox database demonstrated consistently downregulated expression of LRAT in GC tissues (Fig. 5a). Also, higher LRAT expression was correlated with prolonged overall survival (OS) in patients with GC (Fig. 5b). These findings were experimentally validated using qRT-PCR. This confirmed significantly lower LRAT expression in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 5c). It is widely accepted that tRFs exert their gene regulatory functions by binding to AGO2 and target mRNA to form the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). Therefore, we hypothesized that tRF-34 might exert its biological functions through interaction with LRAT, which was subsequently confirmed by RIP assays demonstrating indirect binding between tRF-34 and LRAT (Fig. 5d). After downregulating the expression of tRF-34, LRAT mRNA exhibited an opposite change in expression (Fig. 5e). To further verify whether LRAT directly binds to LRAT, a dual-luciferase reporter assay demonstrated a direct binding interaction between them (Fig. 5f).

Discussion

Cancer-related pain is a common symptom experienced by patients (Fizazi et al. 2023; Zhang 2022). The mechanism underlying the pain is complex, mainly including the following aspects: The tumor can cause pain by directly pressing on or invading surrounding tissues, including nerves, bones, internal organs, and other tissues (Mulvey et al. 2024). Tumor cells also produce some metabolites that stimulate surrounding tissues and nerve endings, causing pain (Swarm et al. 2019). Tumors and the resulting pain are significant challenges for patients with cancer. Understanding cancer-related pain and its occurrence, early diagnosis, and treatment is the most effective way to eliminate cancer pain. The incidence and mortality rate of GC are high in China, and the number of patients with GC-related pain is countless (Liang et al. 2020). The severe cancer-related pain reduces the quality of life and impacts the lifespan. Identifying effective diagnostic markers for the early detection of GC is crucial for reducing cancer-related pain through timely diagnosis and treatment. Patients with cancer often experience pain during their treatment. A systematic review showed that 33% of patients reported pain during radical cancer treatment, 59% reported pain during ongoing cancer treatment, and 64% reported pain in late or advanced stages of cancer (Zhang 2022). Despite the rapid development of new cancer therapies in recent years, the overall management of cancer-related pain still needs improvement.

Several studies have demonstrated the roles of tRFs in various tumors by modulating mechanisms such as translation regulation, stress response, RNA stability, and ribosome biogenesis (Zhang 2022). tRF-3013b is significantly downregulated in GBC. The overexpression of tRF-3013b, through its interaction with AGO3, leads to the silencing of both mRNA and protein levels of TPRG1L. This silencing markedly suppresses the proliferation of GBC cells (Zou et al. 2022). 5’-tRF-GlyGCC directly binds to the fat mass and obesity-associated protein, thus reducing eIF4G1 methylation, inhibiting autophagy, and promoting proliferation and metastasis of breast cancer (BC). These findings suggest 5’-tRF-GlyGCC could be a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for BC (Chen et al. 2023). Moreover, several studies indicate differential expression of tRFs in GC, with tRF-29-R9J8909NF5JP being associated with multiple pathological factors (Li et al. 2023). Despite the lack of biomarkers with high diagnostic and predictive value, our team has been committed to investigating the relationship between tRFs and GC to identify biomarkers with enhanced diagnostic and prognostic potential. In this study, we found high expression of tRF-34 in GC tissues, cells, and preoperative plasma, suggesting its role as a pro-oncogenic factor (Fig. 1c and e). Subsequent ROC curve analysis demonstrated superior diagnostic specificity of tRF-34 in GC (Fig. 1f). The in vitro cell experiments revealed that tRF-34 downregulation inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of GC cells (Figs. 2c and e and 3a, and 3b). These findings highlighted the diagnostic value of tRF-34 and its pro-oncogenic role in GC. Additionally, we demonstrated that tRF-34 predominantly existed in the cytoplasm and exerted its function (Fig. 3c).

tRFs are involved in tumorigenesis via diverse mechanisms. For instance, tRF-1:30-Gly-CCC-3 downregulates the protein expression of pyruvate carboxylase by binding to it, thereby influencing metabolic reprogramming and the progression of papillary thyroid cancer (Fu et al. 2024). Similarly, tRF-23-Q99P9P9NDD promotes tumorigenesis by targeting acyl-coenzyme A dehydrogenase short/branched chain in GC, thus, influencing lipid metabolism and ferroptosis in GC (Zhang et al. 2024). Moreover, 5’-tRF-19-Q1Q89PJZ expression is downregulated in PC, whereas its overexpression suppresses the development of PC by sponging and inhibiting the expression of hexokinase 1 (Cao et al. 2023). The database prediction and experimental verification in this study revealed that tRF-34 promoted the occurrence and development of GC by binding to LRAT (Figs. 4 and 5).

However, this study had some limitations. Further in vivo animal experiments are needed to verify the effects and specific mechanisms of action of tRF-34. Moreover, tRF-34 expression should be verified using multicenter clinical specimens.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study showed upregulation of tRF-34 expression in GC tissues and cells, and its potential to serve as a biomarker for GC. It also confirmed that the downregulation of tRF-34 expression inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of GC cells by binding to LRAT mRNA 3’-UTR. Future studies should focus on elucidating the precise mechanism by which tRF-34 influences GC development.
