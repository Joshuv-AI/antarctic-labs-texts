Differences across intersecting racial, ethnic, and gender identity in the spatiotemporal patterning of suicide clusters in the US from 2005-2020☆
Authors: Amanda Sursely, John R Pamplin, Megan E Gilster, Carol Coohey, Katherine M Keyes, Jonathan M Platt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify spatiotemporal trends in suicide clusters across multiple intersecting sociodemographic groups of US adults from 2005 to 2020.MethodsUS Vital Statistics data were used to calculate county-level suicide rates in adults from 2005 to 2020. Rates were calculated for 16 intersecting sociodemographic groups, defined by mutually exclusive combinations of race (American Indian/Alaskan Native (AIAN), Asian/Pacific Islander (API), Black, White), ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic), and sex (male, female). For each group, spatiotemporal suicide clusters were identified using the SaTScan space-time statistic, based on counties where suicide rates were higher (hot clusters) or lower (cold clusters) than expected. We also calculated the average proportion of deaths contained in clusters, to quantify the importance of clusters relative to total suicide rates.ResultsOverall, 275 clusters were identified, comprising 11.4 % of total suicides. Clusters ranged from 1 among non-Hispanic Black women to 135 among non-Hispanic White men. The number and magnitude of hot clusters (RR > 1) increased over time among Hispanic White men and women, and the highest magnitude clusters were observed in non-Hispanic Native American women. No clusters were identified for Hispanic AIAN women or Hispanic API men. Hot clusters were most prevalent in Western counties across all groups. Cold clusters were most prevalent in urban counties.ConclusionsSuicide rates and spatial clusters have increased since 2005, with distinct patterning between sociodemographic groups. The identification of specific communities that may be at disproportional risk for suicide highlight opportunities to tailor individual- and population-level prevention efforts in emerging high-risk groups.Keywords: Suicide, Suicide clusters, Cluster analysis, Mental health

Introduction

In 2022, suicide was the 11th leading cause of death and accounted for 1.5 % of total mortality in the United States (Centers for Disease C et al., 2020; Mortality in the United States, 2022). While global suicide rates have decreased in recent decades, US rates rose steadily from 2000 to 2018, briefly declined (Hedegaard et al., 2018; Stone, 2018), and then rose again beginning in 2021 with 49,449 (provisional) deaths in 2022 (Newsroom, 2022). Underlying these population trends are important group differences by race/ethnicity, gender, and geography.

Among American Indian/Alaskan Native (AI/AN) and non-Hispanic White racial/ethnic groups, suicide rates are historically higher than other groups (Rockett et al., 2006). However, racial and ethnic differences have narrowed due to greater rate increases among those with non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic White, and non-Hispanic Asian racial identities (Stone, 2021). Gender differences have also narrowed. Historically, suicide deaths are more likely among men than women, but rate increases have been greater among women in recent years (Hedegaard et al., 2018). Geographically, suicide rates have continued to increase in one-third of US states, particularly in those with a historically high suicide burden, including Alaska, Wyoming, South Dakota, and Montana (National Center for Health S, 2021). Suicide rates are substantially higher in rural areas than urban areas overall, a divide that continues to widen with time (Stone, 2021). These shifting trends underscore the importance of examining suicide patterns across both diverse social and geographic contexts. While sociodemographic and regional disparities are well-documented, analyses are typically based on single axes of identity, which may fail to capture important within-group differences in risk. For instance, suicide rates in rural areas are generally higher, but the specific communities or subgroups driving these disparities remain poorly understood.

Additionally, focusing solely on changes in suicide rates may obscure geographic areas or communities that are at particularly high risk. Individual suicide deaths are known to correlate in both space and time, referred to as “suicide clusters” (Gould et al., 1989). “Hot” clusters indicate areas or time periods where the relative risk of suicide is significantly higher than in surrounding areas or times, while “cold” clusters indicate significantly lower risk. These clusters represent between 5 and 10 % of all suicide deaths (Hawton et al., 2020). Historically, hot clusters have been conceptualized as events that spread between individuals via social contagion (Haw et al., 2013). Given this framing, prior studies have limited the geographic scope to a subset of states (Sy et al., 2019/12; Johnson et al., 2017), or the temporal scope to one year or less (Galvão et al., 2018/03). However, taking a broader geographical and temporal view, spatiotemporal cluster analysis can highlight larger areas in which suicide risk is disproportionately high or low, suggesting that contextual or structural factors may be contributing to localized risk. This could point to social contagion, environmental stressors, healthcare desserts, or other area-level risk factors. From a practical perspective, public health agencies often use cluster detection to guide surveillance, target prevention efforts, or respond to perceived increases in community suicides. Identified hot clusters can be used to guide decisions about where to allocate resources, such as mental health services, crisis response programs, or community outreach. In contrast, cold clusters may point to protective factors worthy of further study.

Given that suicide is hypothesized to result from the intersection of individual vulnerability and social or environmental conditions (Broerman et al., 2017), it is plausible that patterns of clustering may also differ in important ways based on social identity, in accordance with the temporal and spatial experiences of group members. It is well documented that structural mechanisms reinforce differences in access, distribution of wealth, and availability of resources in the United States, and that these mechanisms are the root cause of health disparities, exacerbated in marginalized populations (Egede et al., 2024/02). Supporting this, studies to date have identified important intra-group differences in suicide clustering among youth (Standley & Foster-Fishman, 2021) and minoritized racial/ethnic groups (Platt et al., 2022; Torres et al., 2018). However, these studies have comprised smaller samples and been limited to certain group identities.

Using an approach in line with the CDC Guidance for Community Assessment and Investigation of Suspected Suicide Clusters (Trinh et al., 2024) the objective of this paper is to utilize a non-traditional cluster analysis to identify the different spatial and temporal trends in suicide clusters and understand differences across intersecting sociodemographic groups in the US population. Understanding where, when, and in whom suicide risk is increasing not only broadens our understanding of where risk is concentrated but identifies context-specific factors where interventions may be effectively targeted.

Methods

Study population

County-level suicide rates (suicide deaths per 100,000 people) were identified using death certificates from the US National Vital Statistics System and National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) for the periods 2005 to 2020. Suicide deaths were classified based on International Classification of Disease, tenth revision (ICD-10), using underlying cause of death codes X60-X84, Y87.0, and U03. NCHS county population estimates for each year were used as the denominators to calculate mortality rates.

Sixteen groups were defined according to mutually exclusive combinations of three sociodemographic variables for each decedent: race (American Indian/Alaskan Native (AIAN), Asian/Pacific Islander (API), Black, White), ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic), and sex (male, female). Underlying causes of death are determined by a coroner, medical examiner, or physician, while sociodemographic variables are reported by next of kin. County of residence was indicated by unique state-county FIPS codes. The latitude and longitude coordinates corresponding to the centroids of each county were merged from the US Census Bureau Gazetteer Files using FIPS codes (United States Census Bureau). Cluster rurality was defined using the 2013 Rural-Urban Continuum Code at the cluster's centroid, with codes 1–3 designated as urban, and codes 4–9 as rural (USDA Economic Research Service, 2025).

Analysis

The analysis included three steps. First, we calculated the proportion of all suicide deaths in each sociodemographic group (group deaths/total suicide deaths) to understand the distribution of suicide deaths between groups. Second, we identified spatiotemporal suicide clusters in the US for each group, as well as among the whole population. The spatiotemporal analysis was implemented using SaTScan™ v9.11 (Kulldorff, 2018), which identifies clusters by scanning county centroids through a cylindrical window, where the circular base with a radius that represents the area of the cluster, and the height that represents the time period of the cluster. Discrete Poisson probability modelling was used to compare the observed number of events to the expected number given the observed population distributions both inside and outside the window. Under the null hypothesis, the expected number of cases in each area is proportional to the population size of the sociodemographic group in the window. This effectively controls for potential overidentification of clusters where group populations are highest, since the cluster is compared with itself (i.e., what is expected given the observed population). Statistical significance was evaluated through Monte Carlo hypothesis testing, where the maximum likelihood from the observed suicide risks was compared to the 999 simulated random datasets. The null hypothesis of no clustering was rejected at a p-value of ≤0.05, indicating that the observed maximum likelihood was within the largest or smallest 2.5 % of simulated replications. Areas with statistically significantly higher (relative risk >1) and lower than expected rates were considered hot and cold clusters (relative risk <1), respectively. Clusters comprised of fewer than 20 cases were excluded to avoid unreliable estimates as is indicated for CDC mortality data (CDC WONDER).

For this analysis, the parameters of the scanning window were set to a maximum of 3 US contiguous counties and up to 3 years, constraining the largest spatial and temporal clusters the software would identify. The models were run separately for each of the 16 sociodemographic groups, and once for the unstratified population (hereby referred to as the group-specific models and the full model respectively). In previous research to identify suicide clusters, the maximum size of clusters varied widely, spanning temporal periods from days to years and spatial areas from school classrooms to counties (Niedzwiedz et al., 2014). Window parameters were selected to balance the tradeoff between capturing localized clusters and obtaining the minimum sample sizes needed to achieve reliable model estimates. As a sensitivity analyses, we restricted the model temporal parameter to one and two years to examine the robustness of our results to changes in model specifications. Third, we sought to understand the importance of suicide clusters relative to all suicide deaths. To do this, we divided the number of suicide deaths that were included in a cluster by the total number of suicide deaths that occurred during each year. These proportions were then averaged across the study period for each sociodemographic group.

To present the maps, we created an interactive dashboard using the shiny package in R26, which presents the geographic distribution of the spatial clusters by sociodemographic group, allowing users to manipulate the figure for a more granular or dispersed view.

Results

Table 1 includes the distribution of suicide deaths and suicide clusters in each sociodemographic group. Over the study period, 653,730 suicide deaths were recorded. The greatest proportions of suicide deaths occurred among non-Hispanic White men (64.4 %) and non-Hispanic White women (18 %). In the total population, 312 suicide clusters were identified, comprising 95,211 cases and approximately 14.5 % of the total suicides during the study period. In the group-specific models, a total of 275 clusters were identified comprising 74,572 cases (see Supplemental Table 3: List of all clusters, organized by start year). Clusters ranged across groups, from 1 cluster in non-Hispanic Black women to 135 clusters in non-Hispanic White men. No statistically significant clusters were identified for Hispanic Black men, Hispanic Black women, and non-Hispanic API women. Additionally, 13 clusters contained too few cases to be statistically reliable (i.e., <20 cases) and were excluded (see Supplemental Table 1). After applying this exclusion, no clusters were identified for Hispanic AIAN women or Hispanic API men. The final set of results included 262 suicide clusters, across 11 groups. The proportion of suicide deaths within clusters also varied from 0.43 % among non-Hispanic Black women to 11.9 % among Hispanic White men.

For the group-specific models, significant hot clusters were present in 25 states, cold clusters in 20 states, and 12 states had no significant clustering. While the majority of clusters (75 %) were located in urban counties, rural counties showed a higher relative risk (rural mean = 3.15, urban mean = 1.18). Additionally, 98 % of rural clusters were classified as hot clusters. Overall, clusters were widespread, though they were most prevalent in Western, Midwestern, and Northeastern regions. Of the hot clusters, 84 % were located in the western region. Colorado contained the highest number of hot clusters (31), comprising approximately 16 % of all identified clusters. No cold clusters were observed for non-Hispanic Black women, Hispanic API women, or AIAN men or women of either ethnicity. Among non-Hispanic White men and women, hot clusters were common in Western and Southern regions, with cold clusters observed more frequently in the Northeast. For Hispanic White men and women, most hot clusters were observed in the southwest, particularly New Mexico and Colorado. Among both non-Hispanic AIAN men and women, hot clusters were most prevalent in South Dakota and Alaska. The strongest clustering for API men and women was seen in Hawaii, regardless of ethnicity.

When considering the full model (i.e., unstratified), most clusters were robust and appeared in the same locations as in the group-specific models. However, several additional clusters emerged, for a total of 312 clusters. New clusters were concentrated particularly in the central-eastern part of the country. States such as Kansas, Missouri, Tennessee, and Kentucky had no clustering in any of the group-specific models but several significant clusters in the full model.

The results of the sensitivity analysis, restricting the temporal window to one and two years, are presented in Supplemental Table 2. As expected, clusters were more likely as the temporal window increased, though the overall spatiotemporal patterning was similar.

Discussion

This study aimed to describe differences in the spatial and temporal patterning of suicide deaths across intersecting sociodemographic groups in the United States from 2005 to 2020. Summarizing across 11 groups, hot clusters increased over time. While hot clusters were geographically widespread, they were most prevalent in Western states, consistent with previous research (Sy et al., 2019/12; Rossen et al., 2018/07). However, the high degree of variation between groups underscores the need to consider heterogeneity underlying general trends. Both hot and cold clusters were most commonly identified among non-Hispanic White men, but the prevalence of clusters over time was generally stable. The stark patterning of hot clusters in Western states, and cold clusters in Northeastern states may reflect broader social and geographic risk factors that differ between regional areas in the US. Health care access (Rost et al., 2002), social support (Cantrell et al., 2012), means restrictions (Brent & Bridge, 2003), and economic growth and opportunity likely contribute to area-level risk for suicide deaths. The geographic clustering of suicide deaths also reflects complex intersections between place, economic opportunity, and racialized experiences that cannot be understood through geography alone. For example, hot clusters may reflect the experiences of working-class White men in declining industries (Affleck et al., 2018), Indigenous communities experiencing environmental degradation of traditional lands and socio-cultural isolation in urban areas (Redvers et al., 2023; Walker et al., 2024), or Hispanic agricultural workers facing both economic precarity and anti-immigrant hostility (Dadras & Hazratzai, 2025).

Hispanic White men and women experienced clusters at an increasing frequency and with higher relative risk, emerging primarily in the southwestern states such as New Mexico and Arizona. Acculturative stress, including discrimination, has been associated with suicidal ideation and attempt among Hispanic immigrants (Polanco-Roman et al., 2023; Silva & Van Orden, 2018). The increasing anti-Hispanic political rhetoric, particularly concerning the US-Mexico border, has created an environment where individuals of Hispanic descent may experience heightened stress and discrimination. These persistent negative experiences not only increase feelings of marginalization but also contribute to a sense of cultural disconnection, negatively impacting mental health and potentially increasing the risk of suicide. Compounding this, the national shortage of mental healthcare providers is acute along the US-Mexico border (Villalobos et al., 2012). This indicates a need to increase access to culturally and linguistically appropriate mental healthcare for Hispanic individuals along the US-Mexico border.

In addition to emerging risk differences and disparities, study results reflect both historical and continuing inequities. Non-Hispanic AIAN men make up only 0.4 % of the total US population but contribute to almost 1 % of the total number of suicide deaths, including 13 clusters. Clustering among this group occurred around prominent tribal communities and reservations. A persistent hot cluster was observed across all non-Hispanic AIAN groups near the Standing Rock Indian Reservation in South Dakota, which is the fifth largest reservation in the US. This geographical clustering is consistent with existing evidence that the highest suicide rates among AIAN people occur in the Northern Plains (Abuse & Administration, 2017). Our study also identified three hot clusters in southeastern Idaho for Non-Hispanic AIAN men. This finding is consistent with another study, which found that suicide clusters in this region of the state included a higher population of AIAN individuals than non-clusters (Kassem et al., 2019). Tribal communities have endured historical trauma, discrimination, economic marginalization, and fragmented access to healthcare resources. These structural factors have been shown to impact suicide risk and clustering in AIAN communities (Abuse & Administration, 2017; Bechtold, 1988; Heart & DeBruyn, 1998). These historical inequities have also led to the fracturing of cultural identities and traditions that could otherwise provide strength in times of crisis and reduce the risk of suicide (Abuse & Administration, 2017; Freedenthal & Stiffman, 2004). A recently published protocol using a mixed methods and community-based participatory approach to examine suicide clustering within Native American Reservation communities provides a promising model for future research needed to understand this phenomenon more comprehensively. This protocol underscores the importance of culturally grounded, collaborative research that centers the voices of AIAN communities, offering valuable insights into the unique drivers of suicide clustering within these populations (Brockie et al., 2023).

Despite experiencing thousands of suicide deaths between 2005 and 2020, no statistically significant clusters emerged among Hispanic Black men and women and non-Hispanic API women. The absence of clusters among these groups may reflect complex intersectional protective factors and dispersed risk patterns that require deeper analysis of within-group diversity and structural positioning. Hispanic Black communities may benefit from both Black community resilience traditions (Ellis et al., 2022) and Hispanic family support systems (Valdivieso-Mora et al., 2016), yet face unique challenges in accessing culturally appropriate and anti-racist mental health services. Similarly, the diversity within Asian American and Pacific Islander groups of women encompasses vastly different immigration histories, economic circumstances, and cultural contexts, from recent refugees experiencing trauma to multi-generational families facing model minority myth pressures (Ruiz et al.), suggesting that aggregated analysis may obscure important intersectional patterns between specific ethnic groups, immigration status, and economic positioning. Additionally, the existence of clusters specific to the full model highlight that race/ethnicity and gender are not the only factors driving suicide clusters, and additional group identities (e.g., SES, sexual orientation) may be important determinants of suicide risk. Building on our approach, more research is needed to investigate patterns and determinants of suicide clustering along these axes.

Overall, effective suicide prevention requires interventions that simultaneously address multiple, intersecting systems of oppression and recognize that individual and community protective factors operate differently across intersectional identities. Economic interventions must account for how racialized employment discrimination affects different groups distinctly, with job training programs addressing both skills gaps and employer bias, while firearm safety initiatives must consider how gun access and cultural attitudes toward firearms vary across rural White communities, communities of color in urban and rural areas, and Indigenous communities with different relationships to hunting and protection. Mental health services must move beyond cultural competency to intersectional responsiveness, training providers to understand how discrimination experiences compound across identities and developing community-based interventions that leverage existing social networks while addressing the specific ways that racism, sexism, homophobia, and classism may impact treatment pathways (Kyere et al., 2022/09).

These results should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, we opted to utilize US Vital Statistics data for the analysis. In comparison to a dataset such as the National Violent Death Reporting System (NVDRS), this dataset is limited in the sociodemographic variables available to operationalize social identities, but allows for consideration of a larger timespan that includes the entire US. Though the approach of using only three individual-level sociodemographic variables may be overly simplistic (Hicken et al., 1982), we believe that this approach represents an important foundation of understanding where suicide risk is growing most, over the broadest timespan. Future research will build on this understanding to ask how and why specific clusters are emerging, by identifying further individual, county and community-level indicators of structural risk to more fully understand how systems of oppression overlap to influence suicide risk, and how they may be targeted to attenuate spatial and social disparities. Second, the SaTScan algorithm is data-driven and will find the outliers in any given distribution. The estimated relative risks may be upwardly biased, since the scan statistic ‘cherry picks’ high-risk areas to include in the cluster. While this has been shown to be true when statistical power is low, with medium or high power any bias would be downward, attenuating true risks (Prates et al., 2014). Given the large national sample, our study was adequately powered (see below) to avoid upward bias and type II errors. Additionally, varying the temporal parameters in a sensitivity analysis increased our confidence that the general patterns we report reflect meaningful patterns and are not solely an artifact of any one set of parameters in the statistical model. Findings should be replicated using more theory driven methods which allow for greater control of a priori cluster identification settings. Third, death certificates do not contain information pertaining to the decedent's sexual orientation or gender identity, so we were unable to include these identities in our analysis, instead relying on sex. While previous work has documented the burden of suicide attempts and deaths among sexual- and gender-diverse groups (Ferlatte et al., 2018), more research is needed to highlight these disparities and identify targets to eliminate them. Finally, the presence of small population sizes among some groups and counties may have limited the statistical power to identify clusters. Indeed, we removed 13 clusters that were identified by the SatScan algorithm but contained too few cases to be statistically reliable. These sample size related limitations are a necessary tradeoff, as understanding trends among detailed group identities was a key aim of the study. Related to this, our stratified results do not include clusters where the gender, race, and ethnicity of the decedents is heterogeneous and may to some extent reflect the distribution of race, ethnicity, and gender in the general population. To briefly investigate this, we ran a model for the total population, combining all sociodemographic groups. A total of 312 clusters emerged, though spatiotemporal patterns were largely consistent.

Despite these limitations, the study is strengthened by the use of vital statistics data, which provides a complete census of all US deaths and allows for the detailed stratification by social identities for the US population as a first step to understanding patterns underlying group differences. The consideration of multiple social identities and the application of Space Time Permutation Scan Statistics offers a novel approach to understanding the heterogeneity underlying suicide risk and group disparities, thereby contributing to the development of more targeted and effective public health interventions. Future research endeavors should incorporate a multi-dimensional approach that integrates qualitative data and community-based participatory research to comprehensively capture the intricate interplay of social, psychological, and environmental determinants of suicide risk within diverse communities.

In summary, this study highlights meaningful variation in the geographic and social distribution of suicide rates in the US over time. Understanding group differences is further necessary to inform culturally appropriate suicide prevention efforts, particularly among marginalized communities. A population health approach to suicide prevention must account for both historical and emerging inequities in order to identify high risk groups and dismantle structural inequities to reduce preventable suicide deaths.
