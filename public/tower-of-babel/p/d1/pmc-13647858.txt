Background

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is an opportunistic Gram-negative bacterium widely distributed in the environment, commonly found in soil, water, and humans. Due to its virulence factors, innate and acquired resistance, it has high ability to adapt to the environment [1]. P. aeruginosa is responsible for nosocomial infections in critically ill or immunocompromised patients, especially those with severe burn/surgical wounds, with high morbidity and mortality rates [2–5] Hospital-acquired P. aeruginosa causes dermatitis, bacteremia, respiratory and other vital organs infections [6–8].

A key feature of P. aeruginosa is its resistance to antibiotics, which is a major challenge in the treatment. The prevalence of carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa varies around the world. For example, the prevalence of carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa in the United States is about 10–30% [9, 10]. In developing countries, such as Iran, its prevalence has been reported to be higher than 50% [11, 12].

Various mechanisms lead to the emergence of carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa, the most important of which is drug efflux through efflux pumps [13]. The efflux pump MexAB-OprM can efflux antibiotics such as quinolones, macrolides, tetracycline, lincomycin, chloramphenicol, novobiocin, and most beta-lactams [14, 15]. It leads to resistance in P. aeruginosa to most beta-lactams except imipenem [16]. Therefore, the treatment of P. aeruginosa infections is a global issue. So, we need new therapeutic strategies to address the challenge of antibiotic resistance of P. aeruginosa. These novel strategies include targeting key regulators of pathogenicity, bacteriophage therapy, immunotherapy, antimicrobial peptides, and RNA-based antibiotics [17, 18]. In recent years, extensive studies have been conducted on small regulatory RNAs (sRNAs) and their functions on the expression of genes involved in the pathogenesis of P. aeruginosa [19–22]. However, there are few studies about the association of sRNAs and antibiotic resistance in bacteria.

One of the major functions of sRNAs is to suppress the target gene expression under particular conditions or in response to external stimuli [23]. In P. aeruginosa, sRNAs regulate many physiological processes, such as quorum sensing, iron homeostasis [24, 25] and biofilm formation [26]. Most sRNAs are induced in post-translational events and bind to the target mRNA by the bacterial protein, hfq, affecting the translation and expression of the target gene [23, 26, 27]. Hfq can also act as a repressor and prevent mRNA translation by polyadenylation [28–30]. Hence, this study aimed to investigate the expression levels of three novel sRNAs AS1974, AS2779, IGR2780, and Hfq in clinical isolates of carbapenem-resistant and susceptible P. aeruginosa selected from the microbial archives of the Microbiology Department of Ahvaz Medical School. These three novel sRNAs were chosen among other well-known sRNAs due to their roles in pathogenicity and occurrence of antibiotic sensitivity and resistance in clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa. Also, no study has been done on these three sRNAs in Iran.The importance of this study is due to the increasing antibiotic resistance worldwide, especially in developing countries. New therapeutic strategies based on neutralizing bacterial virulence factors and reducing bacterial infections without directly killing them are being investigated, and we hope that our findings can provide new insights into the application of regulatory sRNAs to address the challenge of drug resistance in P. aeruginosa.

Methods

Study setting

This cross-sectional study was performed during January and December 2024. In this study, 80 clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa were selected from the microbial archives of the research laboratory of the Microbiology Department of Jundishapur Medical School, Ahvaz, Iran.

Phenotypic identification

The bacteria were inoculated onto cetrimide agar (Merck, Germany) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The suspected isolates were identified using standard microbiology tests, including Gram staining, urea test, citrate test, triple sugar iron (TSI), and oxidase spot test [12].

Molecular identification

Bacterial DNA of the collected isolates was extracted using the Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Roche, Germany). Final confirmation of bacteria as Pseudomonas aeruginosa was performed using gyrB amplification by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) [31].

Evaluation of antibiotic resistance pattern using the disk diffusion method

After phenotypic and molecular confirmation of clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa, the antibiotic resistance pattern of the selected isolates was evaluated using the disk diffusion method according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute (CLSI) guidelines, 2021 edition [32]. Antibiotic disks were as follows: piperacillin (100 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), imipenem (10 µg), meropenem (10 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), amikacin (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), and chloramphenicol (30 µg) (Mast, UK). Pseudomonas aeruginosa strain PAO1 was used as control for antibiogram.

Evaluation of the presence of MexA-MexB-OprM efflux pump, AS1974, AS2779, IGR2780 and Hfq genes in selected isolates of P. aeruginosa by PCR

All isolates were evaluated for the presence of the MexA-MexB-OprM efflux pump, regulatory sRNAs AS1974, AS2779, IGR2780, and Hfq genes using PCR. The primers used are listed in Table 1. PCR products were loaded in 2% agarose gel and stained with safe stain (Sinaclon Co., Tehran, Iran). Then the ultraviolet gel documentation device (Protein Simple, San Jose, CA, USA) was used to observe the bands.

Total RNA preparation

In order to extract RNA from the selected isolates (twenty susceptible, twenty resistant isolates, and PAO1 as control strain), they were grown and incubated overnight in 37 °C incubator. The cell pellet was lysed in 0.3 mg/mL lysozyme (Sigma-Aldrich). RNA extraction was performed using kit (Roche, Germany) according to its instructions. To ensure that the extracted RNA did not contain any DNA contamination, it was treated with DNase enzyme (New England Biolabs), and finally, cDNA synthesis was performed using cDNA synthesis kit (Roche, Germany). The quality of the extracted RNA was determined by the Nanodrop device (Thermo Scientific Waltham, USA).

Real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

RT-PCR was performed on an ABI real-time PCR machine (Applied Biosystems, USA). All real-time PCR assays were run in a total reaction of 12.5 µL consisting of 1X SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) and 200 nM primers. Cycling conditions were 15 s at 95 °C, 15 s at 58 °C, then 15 s at 72 °C, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (15 s at 95 °C and 1 min at 60 °C). Melting curves were determined by an auto-dissociation program. The house keeping gene, gyrB was used for normalization. The comparative CT method (2 ∆∆−CT) was used to calculate the expression level of the target genes. Data were presented as mean ± SD, and expression level comparisons were analyzed using the unpaired t test (two tailed: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001). (23).

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to perform unpaired t test, and the difference was considered to be statistically significant when the P-value < 0.05.

Results

Characterization of clinical isolates

In total, 80 clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa were selected from the microbial archives of the Microbiology Department of Ahvaz Medical School. All isolates were confirmed as P. aeruginosa by phenotypic tests and molecular amplification of gyrB.

Antibiotic resistance patterns

In this study, meropenem and imipenem were used to identify carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa. The results showed that out of 80 clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa, 25% (N = 20) were carbapenem-susceptible and 75% (N = 60) were carbapenem-resistant. Demographic information and antibiotic resistance patterns of the isolates were presented in Table 2. In total, among the carbapenem-resistant isolate of P. aeruginosa, 96.6% were XDR and 3.3% were MDR.

Gene expression level comparison

In total 40 isolates of P. aeruginosa were selected for gene expression analysis: 20 carbapenem-susceptible and 20 carbapenem-resistant isolates of P. aeruginosa were considered to equalize the number of isolates were studied and also laboratory and equipment limitations were considered. First, standard curve was drawn for the primers of gyrB, the results of which showed that the efficiency of the primers was 99.9%, so gyrB was used as an internal control, then standard curve was drawn for each primer pair. The efficiency of all primers was 99%. Also, melting curve was drawn for all amplicons, which showed that all melting curves at specific temperatures were consistent. The gene expression values of MexA-MexB-OprM efflux pump, AS1974, AS2779, IGR2780, and Hfq for carbapenem-susceptible and -resistant isolates of P. aeruginosa were evaluated according the comparative CT method (2 ∆∆−CT) was used to calculate the expression level of the target genes. Data were presented as mean ± SD, and expression level comparisons were analyzed using the unpaired t test (P < 0.05 and P < 0.001) [23].The results were measured based on log2 fold change and P-value ≤ 0.05. According to the results obtained, it was determined that the expression levels of the MexAB-OprM efflux pump were upregulated in carbapenem-resistant compared with carbapenem-susceptible isolates of P. aeruginosa. Also, the expression levels of sRNAs AS1974, AS2779, IGR2780 were downregulated in carbapenem-resistant compared to carbapenem-susceptible isolates of P. aeruginosa. The expression level of hfq had significant increase in carbapenem-resistant compared to carbapenem-susceptible isolates of P. aeruginosa (Fig. 1). Statistical comparison and correlation analysis of the gene expression levels of sRNAs AS1974, AS2779, IGR2780, MexAB-OprM efflux pump and Hfq showed significant relationship (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). The obtained results showed the increase expression levels of AS1974, AS2779, IGR2780 led to decrease in the expression levels of MexAB-OprM efflux pump and Hfq.

Discussion

Recent studies have shown that sRNAs play significant roles in the development of antibiotic sensitivity, resistance and modulate gene expression level under specific conditions or in response to environmental stresses [23, 37, 38]. Therefore, these small regulatory RNAs, which are referred to as potential antimicrobial molecules, can be used to develop methods to deal with the challenge of antibiotic resistance in P. aeruginosa [23, 37, 38].

In this study, a total of 80 clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa were examined, and realized that the expression levels of MexAB-OprM efflux pump were upregulated in carbapenem-resistant compared with carbapenem-susceptible isolates of P. aeruginosa, indicating a direct relationship between increased expression level of MexAB-OprM efflux pump and the occurrence of antibiotic resistance. Results obtained were consistent with previous studies by Beig and Arabestani from Iran [39] and Abdallah et al. [40] from Egypt.

Another interesting finding of this study was and the expression levels of three novel sRNAs were significantly downregulated in the carbapenem-resistant compared with carbapenem-susceptible isolates of P. aeruginosa. These observations were in line with a previous study by Law et al. [23] from China. They found that IGR2780 is located at the intergenic region between PA2770 and PA2771. AS1974 and AS2779 are located at the antisense region of mexR and PA2769, respectively [23]. They also showed that the expression levels of three sRNAs were reduced in all drug-resistant strains in comparison with all drug-sensitive strains [23]. AS1974 is capable of modulating gene expression of various pathways, including efflux pumps, biofilm formation, motility, and iron acquisition [23]. AS1974 sRNA downregulate the component of the multidrug efflux system, MexC-MexD-OprJ, which is commonly found efflux pump in P. aeruginosa, and it provide resistance to aminoglycosides, chloramphenicol, and most β-lactam antibiotics except sulbenicillin and carbenicillin [23, 41, 42]. In recent years, few studies have investigated the association between bacterial sRNAs expression and antibiotic resistance. In a previous study by Yu and Schneiders [43], four sRNAs (sYJ5, sYJ20, sYJ75, and sYJ118) were upregulated in the presence of either tigecycline or tetracycline in Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium. In another study by Howden et al. [44], the expressions of some sRNAs in Staphylococcus aureus were related to exposure to tigecycline, linezolid, vancomycin, and ceftobiprole antibiotics. In a recent study by Singh and Dutta [45], the role of a distinct sRNA MTS1338 as a regulatory mechanism in the development of rifampicin resistance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis was demonstrated.

And also, about the significant increase in expression level of Hfq; Since Hfq act as a carrier of sRNAs to bind to their target mRNAs, and affect increase and decrease of gene expression, and considering the results collected from this study, which showed that we saw significant increase in its expression in carbapenem resistant isolates of P. aeruginosa, it seemed to affect the increase in the expression of virulence factors of P. aeruginosa, especially efflux pumps.

And at the end, analysis of the gene expression levels of sRNAs AS1974, AS2779, IGR2780, MexAB-OprM efflux pump and Hfq showed the increase expression levels of AS1974, AS2779, IGR2780 led to decrease in the expression levels of MexAB-OprM efflux pump and Hfq. In other words, they led to decrease in the expression level of genes involved in the occurrence of antibiotic resistance in P. aeruginosa.

Of course, we need more specialized studies such as mutagenesis, RNA-seq, purification of Hfq protein, electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) to assess the interaction between sRNAs and Hfq to prove our results.

Limitation

Lack of investigation of the upstream and downstream regulatory mechanisms of sRNA genes and lack of investigation of the impact of genetic mutations on sRNA regulation of antibiotic resistance were among the limitations of this study.

Determination in sample size due to equipment and laboratory limitations: Due to the aforementioned limitations, the statistical population for gene expression measurement in this study consisted of 20 carbapenem-susceptible and 20 carbapenem-resistant clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa, and the data obtained were the result of statistical analysis of this number of isolates. To rely on the results and generalize them in the community, a larger number of carbapenem-susceptible and resistant clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa is definitely needed.

Conclusion

The expression levels of studied sRNAs were directly related to the expression levels of MexAB-OprM efflux pump and Hfq; the increase expression levels of AS1974, AS2779, IGR2780 led to decrease in the expression levels of MexAB-OprM efflux pump and Hfq. New therapeutic strategies based on neutralizing bacterial virulence factors and reducing bacterial infection without directly killing them have been investigated in recent years, indicating that the use of these strategies leads to a reduction in selective pressure on bacterial survival, thus creating lower probability of inducing drug resistance. Further studies are needed to confirm and determine the detailed background of this regulatory role.
