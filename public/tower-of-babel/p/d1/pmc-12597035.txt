Multi-tissue spatial transcriptomics identified simultaneous responses to oxidative stress and apoptosis in parallel with tissue-specific reprogramming in modeled chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder
Authors: Lainey M Hibbard, Sheng Liu, Yamil G Marambio, Kayleigh N Jennings, Steven S Welc, Jun Wan, Kenneth E White
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMusculoskeletal dysfunction in chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD) is associated with morbidity and mortality. Disease alterations in bone and muscle, and among muscle fiber types, have largely been tested by candidate gene analysis in individual tissues. We undertook a multi-tissue spatial transcriptomics (ST) approach to identify and differentiate tissue-specific and -common genomic reprogramming occurring simultaneously in cortical bone, muscle, and marrow during CKD-MBD. Visium ST was used on femur-muscle histological cross sections from male mice with adenine diet-induced CKD-MBD (0.2%; 4 wk), or casein control diet. The spatial sequencing datasets were analyzed for differential gene expression and pathway analyses. Transcriptional changes were validated using qPCR in the contralateral tissues as well as in the original sections. Uniform manifold approximation and projection analyses paired with hallmark transcript mapping distinguished cortical bone and bone marrow, slow vs. fast twitch muscle fiber cell populations, including 3 fast twitch muscle subtypes (IIa, IIx, and IIb). Upregulation of apoptosis and oxidative stress pathways, including genes P4hb, known to induce apoptosis, and S100a9, associated with responses to inflammation, occurred across all CKD-MBD tissues. Specifically in muscle, atrophy-associated genes (Trim63, Fbxo32) were upregulated by 3-4-fold, and a novel 2-5-fold increase was observed in the mRNA encoding the structural gene Nrap in both CKD-MBD fast and slow twitch skeletal muscle. Fiber subtypes manifested specific disturbances, including a slow-twitch increase in Car3 (3-fold), and fast-twitch muscle enrichment of ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis and RUNX1-driven transcriptional pathways. In bone, CKD-MBD differentially increased pre-osteoblast markers Tnc and Mmp13 but markedly decreased Bglap and Col3a1 (96.6%; 95.3%), whereas marrow downregulated heme biosynthetic pathways along with ~90% suppression of histone gene Hist1h1b, supporting wider genomic changes. Unbiased ST identified transcriptional alterations involving pan-tissue oxidative stress and apoptosis caused by CKD-MBD. Tissue-unique phenotypes were also found, potentially providing novel targets for improvement of musculoskeletal function during CKD-MBD.Keywords: spatial transcriptomics, bone, chronic kidney disease, muscle, bone marrow

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD) is a progressive, multisystem disease that affects 1 in 7 adults in the US alone.1 It is well known that hyperparathyroidism due to reduced 1,25(OH)2 vitamin D (1,25D) and hypocalcemia in CKD-MBD causes osteoclast activation and leads to porous bone.2 Further, the osteocyte-secreted hormone fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) is pathologically elevated in CKD-MBD, often more than 1000-fold higher than normal,3 which contributes to the decreased 1,25D in patients.4,5 Additionally, patients with CKD-MBD have elevated phosphate, which is known to promote inflammation, further contributing to bone loss.6 Altogether, dysregulation of mineral homeostasis during CKD-MBD contributes to changes in bone turnover, mineralization, and increased bone fragility, leading to fracture. As a result, CKD-MBD patients have a higher incidence of fracture and increased mortality compared to the general population.7,8 The musculoskeletal disease of CKD-MBD, particularly for both parallel and unique changes in muscle and bone, remain understudied.

In concert with changes in bone during CKD-MBD, dramatic skeletal muscle wasting is also often seen in patients, further contributing to increased morbidity and mortality.9,10 Multiple mechanisms likely contribute to muscle atrophy in CKD-MBD, including metabolic acidosis, inflammation, micro-RNA activation, and insulin signaling. These pathways, and elevated inflammation and oxidative stress in particular, promote elevated proteolysis and decreased protein synthesis during CKD-MBD, which contribute to atrophy phenotypes.11 Importantly, these additive changes in patient bone and skeletal muscle further increase the risk of fracture. Muscle fiber subtypes are delineated by a primary hallmark myosin heavy chain. In this regard, type I slow-twitch muscle fibers are characterized by Myh7 expression, whereas fast-twitch muscle fibers are separated into the different subtypes IIa, IIx, and IIb, expressing Myh2, Myh1, and Myh4 mRNAs, respectively.12 Typically, fiber types are dissected before genomic analyses to attempt to identify fiber type-specific phenotypes; however, spatial transcriptomics (ST) maintains spatial relationships, allowing study within and between tissues, as well as maintaining the underlying morphology to provide direct associations with transcriptional changes. Additionally, ST has practical advantages including reducing costs and providing additional data, such as pathways that are changed across multiple tissues or potential crosstalk.

Herein, we tested limb cross-sections using ST to provide insight into the poorly understood general, and muscle fiber type-specific, transcriptional changes in a mouse model of CKD-MBD. Further, tissue and cell sub-type specific genomic reprogramming, as well as defects common across bone, bone marrow, and muscle were identified, providing novel, “one-step” insight into the multi-tissue pathogenesis of CKD-MBD.

Materials and methods

Animal studies

Animal studies were approved by, and performed, according to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Indiana University School of Medicine, and complied with the NIH guidelines for the use of animals in research. C57BL/6 mice were purchased (Jackson Labs), group-housed, and acclimated prior to study. Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation/cervical dislocation, and blood was collected by cardiac puncture for serum and plasma (collected in EDTA tubes).

Rodent diets

Mice were fed normal rodent chow prior to experimental diet administration. The adenine diet model was used to induce renal failure in male WT mice according to previous protocols.13 Significant sex effects have been shown with the provision of adenine-containing diets, with males developing disease phenotypes more rapidly.14 At 10 wk of age, mice were randomly assigned and transferred to a diet containing 0.2% adenine (no. TD.160020; Envigo) and euthanized after 4 wk on diet. Male mice fed a casein diet (0.9% phosphate and 0.6% calcium, no. TD.150303; Envigo) for 4 wk were used as the control. Diets and water were provided ad libitum.

Serum parameters

Plasma FGF23 was assessed using a rodent specific commercial ELISA for bioactive, intact FGF23 (“iFGF23”; Quidel, Inc.). Serum TNFα was measured using a mouse specific Quantikine ELISA for TNFα (R&D Systems). Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) was measured in serum collected from the mice fed the respective diets after 4 wk.

Histological sections

Following euthanasia, femurs with the muscles attached were removed from the animals. Tissues were fixed for 24 h in 4% PFA at 4 °C before being transferred to EDTA decalcification media, composed of a final working solution of 1.2% formalin and 10% EDTA. Bone and muscle samples were decalcified for 12 d at 4 °C on a rocker, with 2 solution changes during that time. After decalcification was complete as determined via pin test on the femur, samples were washed in running MilliQ water for 40 min and then stored in 70% ethanol at 4 °C before paraffin embedding and sectioning. Sections were taken from femur midshafts.

RNA extraction and quality assessment

From the paraffin embedded samples, 4 10 μm sections were cut and placed in a microcentrifuge tube. RNA was extracted using the Qiagen RNeasy FFPE kit. Quality of samples was assessed using Agilent RNA Tape Station. The CKD sample had a RINe value of 4.1 and DV200 of 77.56%; Ctrl had a RINe of 2.8 and DV200 of 71.12%. RNA extracted using the same method was also used for RT-qPCR experiments as indicated.

10X Visium CytAssist analyses

Tissue samples used for 10x Genomics Visium CytAssist (n = 1 per group) were paraffin embedded as described above and 5 μm sections were placed on glass microscope slides. The H&E staining was carried out according to the 10x CytAssist protocol. Briefly, slides were heated at 60 °C for 2 h, deparaffinized in xylene for 10 min 3 times, immersed in 100% EtOH for 3 min twice, 96% EtOH for 3 min twice, 70% EtOH for 3 min, and MilliQ water for 20 s. Staining consisted of a 3 min incubation with hematoxylin, followed by 3 washes in MilliQ water, 1 min incubation with bluing buffer, 1 wash, 2 min in alcoholic eosin, and 2 more washes before coverslipping using 85% glycerol. Immediately following imaging (see below), the CytAssist protocol from decrosslinking on was carried out according to the manufacturers protocol, with sections transferred to the Visium 11 mm × 11 mm capture area slide (Visium Spatial Gene Expression for FFPE User Guide CG000407 Rev C, 10x Genomics). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform, with over 93% sequencing saturation for both samples.

CytAssist slide visualization

Brightfield images of the H&E-stained sections were obtained using a Keyence BZ-X810 (Indiana Center for Biological Microscopy) microscope with a 10x objective. Individual captured images were tiled together to obtain the full image.

Histological staining

TUNEL staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Abcam, ab206386) with the following modifications: 15 min H2O2 incubation, counterstained with Mayer’s Hematoxylin Solution (Sigma-Aldrich, MHS16) for 30 s and Scott’s Bluing Reagent (Ricca, 6697-32) for 1 min. Nebulin related anchoring protein (NRAP) immunofluorescence (IF) was performed using 1:200 Proteintech, 25977-1-AP (NRAP) and Tyramide Signal Amplification plus tetramethylrhodamine (TSA TMR) amplification at 1:50 (Akoya Biosciences, SAT702001).

TUNEL analysis

Brightfield images of 10 random regions of the muscle were captured on a Zeiss Axio Observer microscope with a 40x objective. Over 2300 nuclei were counted for both control and CKD groups, and the percentage of apoptotic nuclei was calculated from total nuclei.

NRAP IF analysis

Texas Red signal was captured using a Zeiss Axio Observer microscope with a 10x objective. The average positive intensity of the signal was measured across the entire section (minus bone, marrow, and any folds in muscle) using Halo (v4.0.5107.407). The average intensity of the secondary antibody alone negative control samples was used to remove background signal.

RNA preparation and quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)

Cortical bone from the contralateral femur and tibiae (marrow flushed) and digested bone chips from the mouse used for ST, as well as littermates treated similarly, were harvested and homogenized in 1 mL of Trizol reagent (Invitrogen/ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, then further purified using the Isolate II RNA Mini Kit (Bioline). The flushed marrow was homogenized in 1 mL of Trizol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA from bone-muscle samples was extracted using the Qiagen FFPE RNeasy kit (Qiagen). RNA samples were tested with intron-spanning primers/probe specific for mouse tripartite motif containing 63 (Trim63), F-box protein 32 (Fbxo32), nebulin related anchoring protein (Nrap), bone gamma carboxyglutamate protein (Bglap), secreted phosphoprotein 1 (Spp1), H1.5 linker histone, cluster member (Hist1h1b), and sirtuin 7 (Sirt7) mRNAs. Mouse Gapdh was used as an internal control for RT-qPCR (Applied Biosystems/ThermoFisher Scientific). The TaqMan One-Step RT-PCR kit was used to perform the qPCR reactions under cycling conditions: 30 min 48 °C, 10 min 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s 95 °C and 1 min 60 °C. The data were collected using a StepOne Plus system (Applied Biosystems/ThermoFisher Scientific). The expression levels of mRNAs were calculated relative to casein diet controls, and data analyzed by the 2−∆∆CT method.15

Bioinformatic analyses

Spatial transcriptomics expression analysis

Space Ranger 2.0.0 (http://support.10xgenomics.com/) was utilized to process the raw sequence data generated. The FASTQ files were aligned to the mouse reference genome mm10 with RNAseq aligner STAR16 using the 10X Visium FFPE probe set (version 1). Filtered feature-barcode matrices were generated by removing barcodes corresponding to background noise and retaining only those associated with individual spots. Aligned reads were then traced back to individual spots, where gene expression levels were quantified based on the number of unique molecular indices. Since the provided probe set (version 1) for Space Ranger does not include mitochondrial and ribosomal genes, mitochondrial and ribosomal RNA levels could not be assessed. Only spots with more than 200 unique detected genes were used in downstream analysis. Gene expression normalization was performed using the SCTransform function of Seurat package.17 The first 30 principal components (PCs) from principal component analysis were used to cluster the spots by a shared nearest neighbor modularity optimization based algorithm. Seurat’s anchor-based integration method was used for spatial RNA-seq data integration. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and spatial visualization of the resulting clusters and selected genes were performed with Seurat package. We utilized Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function with the Wilcoxon test to recognize marker genes for each cluster, which were then used to infer major cell types of each spot based on specific cell type markers. The subtypes of muscle cells were determined by specific marker genes, including Myh7, Myh2, Myh1, and Myh4.

Additionally, FindMarkers with Wilcoxon test was applied to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each cell type between control and CKD-MBD sample. Functional analysis of gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway was performed separately on up- and down-regulated DEGs using DAVID.18,19 Significantly enriched GO biological processes with a false discovery rate (FDR) less than 0.05 were visualized by Rrvgo 1.14.220 using treemap. GO terms were grouped and colored according to their unifying parent terms, with the size of each section proportional to their corresponding -log10FDR.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses of the in vivo data presented were performed by Student’s t-test in GraphPad (10.4.1) to assess the differences between control and adenine diet mice. Significant changes were considered when p < .05.

Results

Induction of CKD-MBD and simultaneous ST of muscle, bone, and bone marrow

The effects of CKD-MBD on muscle and bone are incompletely understood and have primarily been studied in individual tissue types. Our goal was to simultaneously test the effects of CKD-MBD across and within multiple tissues, providing a comprehensive mapping of conserved and unique tissue alterations. To this end, CKD-MBD was induced in 10-wk-old male WT C57BL/6 mice by feeding either 0.2% adenine (CKD) or casein control (Ctrl) diet for 4 wk.13 To assure a CKD-MBD phenotype, blood biochemistries were assessed and the mice with CKD vs. controls showed a marked increase in intact FGF23 and TNFα (n = 4) (Figure 1A and B; p < .05). Further, the mice with CKD-MBD had increased BUN (Figure 1C), a hallmark of CKD-MBD.13

To test simultaneous gene expression across musculoskeletal tissues, including fast and slow twitch muscle subtypes, cortical bone, and bone marrow, parallel Visium ST analyses was performed on the upper limb of a control (Ctrl) mouse (Figure 1D) and a mouse with CKD (Figure 1E), and differential gene expression was verified by qPCR on the experimental replicates (n = 3-4). On average, 3882 spots (each spot 55 μm in diameter) were located under the tissue sections. The Ctrl sample had 513 genes/spot and 37 498 mean reads per spot, and the CKD sample had 873 genes/spot and 44 249 reads/spot. Eleven cell clusters were generated from the ST data and mapped on the tissue (Figure 1F and G). One discrete cluster each was found for bone (cluster 7), bone marrow (cluster 9), and slow-twitch (type I) muscle fibers (cluster 10), which highly expressed Myh7 (Figure 1H). The remaining clusters represented fast-twitch muscle and were further separated into fiber types based upon the expression of defining myosin heavy chain (MyHC) mRNAs: muscle fiber subtypes IIa, IIx, and IIb, expressing Myh2, Myh1, and Myh4, respectively21 (Figure 1I and J). Additional muscle fiber mRNA marker genes, along with bone and marrow markers, confirmed the separation of the distinct tissue types (Figure 1H). Along with broader changes across muscle fiber types, this technique can also be used to identify small areas of other muscle types. Indeed, localized expression of Myh11, a gene expressed in smooth muscle,22 and other smooth muscle markers Tagln and Myl9 were found only where vasculature was visible in the tissue (Figure S1). Thus, not only can this multi-tissue approach delineate general muscle fiber types, ST provided a platform for unique sub-tissue analyses.

Tissue-common transcriptional reprogramming but via unique pathways

Common transcriptional reprogramming across major CKD-MBD tissue types

To determine whether common pathologic signaling pathways simultaneously occurred across tissues during CKD-MBD, differential gene expression and pathway analysis was performed. Indeed, an increase in the regulation of intrinsic apoptotic signaling and regulation of oxidative stress-induced intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathways were observed in the CKD-MBD bone, bone marrow, and muscle (Figure 2A and B), with associated DEGs significantly overrepresented in CKD-MBD tissues. In addition to apoptosis responses, all tissues shared a common induction of pathways controlling the detoxification of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Figure 2C), suggesting a general response to elevated oxidative stress, potentially contributing to increased apoptosis. Further, multiple apoptotic process and signaling pathways were enriched with CKD-MBD, contributing to the upregulation of cell death across tissue types (red boxes, Figure 2D). Multiple genes contributed to these altered pathways, including increased expression of glutathione peroxidase 1 (Gpx1) and the calcium- and zinc-binding protein S100a9 in all tissues and muscle fiber types (Figure 2A). Gpx1 is known to protect against oxidative damage, and S100a9 is involved in inflammation and is upregulated by oxidative stress.23 Further, ROS can drive S100a9-induced cell death. Additionally, increased expression of a variety of genes known to protect from mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative damage, such as Gpx3, Sod1, Park7, and Pink1, were found to contribute to identification of these pathways. Systemic TNFα was elevated in the mice with CKD-MBD (see Figure 1B), and it is known that inflammation can increase oxidative stress, potentially contributing to the broad upregulation of these associated pathways. Supporting this increase in apoptotic signaling at the mRNA level, a significant increase in apoptotic nuclei in the CKD muscle was confirmed via TUNEL staining (Figure 2E and F).

Bone and marrow specific pathways

Interestingly, although all tissues showed the presence of apoptotic signals, these pathways were driven by unique gene responses within a specific tissue. In this regard, bone and bone marrow showed upregulation of superoxide anion generation and hydrogen peroxide associated pathways, driven by genes such as the tyrosine kinase binding protein Tyrobp and Cd177. Cd177 (Figure 2G) is expressed on neutrophils and is known to be involved in superoxide generation.24 Consistent with these ROS pathways being specific to the bone and marrow, pathways such as ROS metabolic process and response to oxygen-containing compounds in these tissues were upregulated (purple/indigo and indigo boxes, respectively, Figure 2D).

Muscle specific pathways

In contrast to bone and marrow, muscle phenotypes were characterized by specific induction of genes involved in mitochondrial function, such as Dap3, Ghitm, and Sh3glb1 (Figure 2H), as well as upregulation of apoptotic mitochondrial pathway-related changes (Figure 2I; additional tissue specific alterations described below). The differential presence of these mitochondria driven apoptosis-associated genes support that unique pathways may initiate apoptotic responses within specific tissue types. Therefore, genes that regulate apoptosis and oxidative stress may be a common pathway dysregulated in muscle and bone, contributing to the pathogenesis of CKD-MBD musculoskeletal defects, but occur via unique mechanisms.

Tissue-specific alterations: muscle

Fast-twitch muscle

In accord with known fiber type proportions in mice, fast-twitch muscle (types IIa, IIx, and IIb) comprised the largest area of muscle tissue.21 Using hallmark MyHC gene markers for individual fiber types, multiple muscle fiber subtypes were identified. A small area of type IIa fibers expressing Myh2 localized closer to the bone and near the slow-twitch fibers. Further away from the bone was a larger cluster of type IIx fibers expressing Myh1, and most distant from bone and comprising the largest area of the muscle cross-section was type IIb, expressing Myh421 (see Figure 1J). Across all fast-twitch muscle fibers, an increase in genes associated with muscle atrophy, Fbxo32 and Trim63, encoding MAFbx1/atrogin and MuRF1,25,26 respectively, was observed during CKD-MBD (Figure 3A and B), and confirmed via qPCR (n = 4) (Figure 3C and D). Specifically within type IIx and IIb muscle fibers, an enrichment of pathways related to transcriptional regulation by RUNX1, proteasomal protein catabolic process, and ubiquitin mediated proteolysis were found (see Figure 2I). This observation is consistent with the role of Runx1 involvement in multiple pathways, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and proteolysis.27,28 Induction of protein catabolism related pathways, particularly through ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, are consistent with increased muscle atrophy, known muscle pathologies in CKD-MBD.29,30

Slow-twitch muscle

Slow-twitch muscle fibers (type I) were located closer to cortical bone, and expressed Myh7, Myl2, and Tnnc1 mRNAs (see Figure 1H).31 One of the top upregulated genes in the slow-twitch muscle in the mice with CKD-MBD was Car3, which encodes carbonic anhydrase, an enzyme needed for the reversible hydration of carbon dioxide (Figure 3E). This enzyme is known to be more highly expressed in slow-twitch skeletal muscle fibers.32 It has been shown that Car3 is elevated in sarcopenic muscle from aged mice, suggesting some transcriptional similarities to atrophying CKD-MBD muscle. Additionally, Car3 can act as a ROS scavenger to protect muscle from oxidative damage. Given the enrichment of intrinsic apoptosis pathways was observed in response to oxidative stress in the CKD-MBD muscle, Car3 may also be upregulated to protect the slow-twitch fibers from oxidative stress. Consistent with this, the slow-twitch muscle, similar to fast twitch muscle, also increased expression of S100a9, known to also be upregulated by oxidative stress.23

Common genes shared by slow and fast-twitch muscle

In addition to fiber type-specific changes, we also detected novel, broadly shared changes across muscle fiber types in the mice with CKD-MBD. Expression of nebulin related anchoring protein (Nrap) mRNA increased in all CKD-MBD skeletal muscle subtypes (Figure 3F and G), which has not been previously reported. Nrap is a thin filament chaperone protein expressed during muscle development, with lower expression postnatally. It has previously been shown that overexpression of Nrap in skeletal muscle causes nemaline myopathy and structural changes, as well as being upregulated in dilated cardiomyopathy, where it is thought to correct for disorganized actin filament architecture.33 At the protein level, although NRAP could be detected by IF, there was more variability in protein than in mRNA (Figure S2). While these reported changes in Nrap typically occur alongside nebulin (Neb) deficiency in other disease states, we did not observe Neb deficiency in the CKD-MBD muscle. Contrarily, Neb expression was increased with CKD-MBD in type IIb and IIx fast-twitch muscle with a smaller increase in slow-twitch muscle fibers (log2FC of 0.73). Considering that downregulation of Nrap improves sarcomeric organization and swimming in zebrafish,34 it is possible that the high levels of Nrap in CKD-MBD muscle may cause structural abnormalities. Interestingly, MuRF1, encoded by Trim63, interacts with Nrap and may mediate its ubiquitylation. Deficiency of Klhl41 has also been shown to increase expression of Nrap;33 however, a moderate increase in Klhl41 expression was detected across most CKD-MBD fast-twitch muscle. Klhl41 functions to bind Nrap and promote its ubiquitination and degradation by the proteasome, thus, it may be that Klhl41 expression was potentially upregulated to compensate for increased levels of Nrap in the muscle of mice with CKD-MBD. These differences in expression of regulators of NRAP could contribute to the varying levels of NRAP protein described above.

Cortical bone

Due to the low number of cortical bone cells and the moderate resolution of the Visium platform, mRNAs from the border of cortical bone showed partial overlap with both bone marrow and muscle. To resolve this shortcoming, a previously generated WT mouse bone single cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) dataset (GSE208152)35 was integrated with the ST bone cell population data for pre-osteoblast, osteoblast, and osteoclast marker genes. The improvement in localization of gene expression was especially evident in cortical bone, where integration provided higher resolution. Indeed, within the cortical bone cluster, pre-osteoblast mRNAs were significantly increased in CKD-MBD, such as the extracellular matrix protein tenascin-C (Tnc) (Figure 4A) and matrix metallopeptidase 13 (Mmp13/collagenase-3), whereas expression of other osteoblast genes Bglap and Col3a1 were decreased. The most suppressed gene during CKD-MBD was Bglap (encodes osteocalcin) with a 97% decrease, involved in binding calcium and hydroxyapatite (Figure 4B). We confirmed the decrease in Bglap mRNA via qPCR in the parallel CKD-MBD bone samples (Figure 4C). Col3a1, a component of type III collagen, was the second most downregulated gene in CKD-MBD bone compared to control mice (Figure 4D), with a >95% decrease, which has previously been associated with an osteoporotic phenotype.36 Bglap and Col3a1 are hallmark osteoblast (Ob) genes,36 and decreased expression could suggest decreased osteoblast activity or a reduction in the osteoblast population in CKD-MBD bone. However, increased expression of pre-osteoblast (Pre-Ob) markers Tnc, Mmp13, Serpine2, and Spp1 were also found, supporting that CKD-MBD does not cause a general suppression of osteoblast transcription (Figure 4D). Matrix genes, such as Spp1, which encodes osteopontin, had a 3.47 log2 fold change (11-fold) increase in mRNA expression in the CKD-MBD bone which was confirmed via qPCR (Figure 4E).

Osteoclasts (Oc) are known to be activated in CKD-MBD, and consistent with this, upregulation of markers Acp5, Mmp9, Car2, and Ctsk (Figure 4D) was observed in the CKD-MBD bone. Also, in the CKD-MBD bone, enrichment of osteoclast differentiation and bone remodeling pathways occurred, in parallel with downregulation of multiple glycolysis-related pathways, including NADH regeneration, glucose catabolic processes, and glycolytic processes through fructose-6-phosphate (see Figure 2I). These decreases in glycolysis-associated pathways with CKD-MBD could potentially contribute to decreased osteoblast activity as osteoblasts largely rely on aerobic glycolysis for bone formation.37,38

Bone marrow

In the bone marrow from mice with CKD-MBD, an enrichment of inflammation-related pathways, including chronic inflammation, neutrophil mediated immunity, positive regulation of T cell migration, and inflammatory responses to antigenic stimuli was observed (see Figure 2I). Further, among the most downregulated pathways in marrow were the porphoryin-containing compound metabolic, tetrapyrrole metabolic, and heme metabolic processes. Porphyrins are a component in the biosynthesis of protoporphyrin IX, which along with iron forms heme.39

We also identified differential expression of Hist1h1b, which was decreased in CKD-MBD marrow, and was confirmed by qPCR in marrow samples from the contralateral leg (n = 4) (Figure 4F and G). This member of the histone H1 family has an unknown function in marrow cells, but is known to decrease in aging and during acute kidney injury.40,41 Further, in T cells, decreased expression of histone genes promotes replication stress, leading to activation of inflammatory pathways.42 Along with decreased expression of Hist1h1b, other histone genes, such as Hist1h1b, Hist4h4, and Hist2h2ac, were also downregulated in CKD-MBD, which may contribute to alteration in expression of key components of pathways, such as chromosome organization and structural constituent of chromatin (see Figure 2I).

Among the most downregulated genes in marrow was Sirt7 (88%), which was confirmed via qPCR (Figure 4H and I). Sirt7 is a histone deacetylase, a modification typically associated with transcriptional repression. It has been shown in Sirt7 KO mice that loss of Sirt7 expression increases replication stress and hinders DNA repair.43 This change occurs because Sirt7 is normally recruited to sites of DNA damage, as it is involved in recruiting proteins to double stranded DNA breaks for repair.44 Low levels of Sirt7 expression in CKD-MBD marrow may contribute to genomic instability and impaired DNA repair,43 and reduced expression of Hist1h1b and Sirt7 could potentially drive replication stress.

Among the upregulated inflammation-associated genes in marrow were Vcam1 and Ccl6 (Figure 4J and K). The chemokine Ccl6 is known to be produced during inflammatory disorders, including early in the disease course in mouse models of CKD-MBD.45 Ccl6 acts as a macrophage chemoattractant along with other immune cells, and can induce chemotaxis, possibly contributing to increased inflammation in CKD-MBD marrow.46,47 The role of the cell surface glycoprotein Vcam1 in inflammation and associated leukocyte migration is well known as Vcam1 is induced by TNFα, which is elevated in our CKD-MBD model (see Figure 1B), as well as by ROS.48 Its role in leukocyte migration could potentially lead to increased immune cell infiltration in CKD-MBD, as well as activating pathways involved in increased expression of matrix metallopeptidases (MMPs), expression of which are also elevated in CKD-MBD marrow (data not shown). Thus, using ST in a CKD-MBD model, differences in genes associated with apoptosis and oxidative stress responses were detected across all tissue types, but were associated with tissue-unique transcriptional pathways. Further, specific changes in gene expression associated with CKD-MBD were detected in individual muscle fiber types, as well as in cortical bone and bone marrow.

Discussion

Musculoskeletal genetic reprogramming is a key driver of the pathology of CKD-MBD, and its influence across tissues remains poorly understood. There are currently few treatments to address increased bone fragility and skeletal muscle wasting in CKD-MBD patients.10 Our combined bone, marrow, and muscle ST data distinguished muscle fiber types, and identified both novel gene expression and pathway changes shared across these tissues. Indeed, during CKD-MBD upregulation of apoptotic and oxidative stress pathways occurred. While these changes were common to all tissue types, we detected differences in inflammatory gene expression in bone and marrow, vs. mitochondrial apoptotic changes in muscle. Our muscle findings are consistent with those in patients and mouse models of with uremic sarcopenia, where skeletal muscle mitochondrial damage occurs during CKD-MBD.49 Further, we confirmed the increase in apoptosis by TUNEL staining in the muscle. Thus, unique mechanisms within specific tissues may drive common pathways causing musculoskeletal dysregulation in CKD-MBD.

Mapping expression of fiber types in both CKD-MBD and control muscle specifically designated hindlimb fiber types, as well as transcriptome differences between subtypes. Using this approach, we identified a previously unreported increase in Nrap expression in skeletal muscle during CKD-MBD. While this gene is known to be upregulated in skeletal muscle in nemaline myopathy and cardiac muscle in dilated cardiomyopathy, it has not been implicated in CKD-MBD. In skeletal muscle, upregulation of Nrap contributed to structural disorganization and downregulation of this gene improved muscle structure and swimming function in zebrafish during nemaline myopathy.34 In contrast, it is thought that increased Nrap expression in dilated cardiomyopathy helps to correct disorganized actin filaments,33 which could be a compensatory action in CKD-MBD muscle. In addition to these novel changes in skeletal muscle with CKD-MBD, known changes were detected, including increased expression of muscle atrophy markers Fbxo32 and Trim63, enrichment of proteasome pathways, and dysregulation of extracellular matrix genes and pathways, validating our approach. Further, atrogin-1 and MuRF1, encoded by Fbxo32 and Trim63, are known to be upregulated by oxidative stress. While there are multiple drivers of ROS and oxidative stress in CKD-MBD, elevated TNFα, as seen in the serum of the CKD-MBD mice, likely contributes to ROS generation.11 In particular, the increased ROS drive muscle atrophy through upregulation of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, which we showed was enriched in the CKD-MBD muscle.

In cortical bone, changes in gene expression occurring with CKD-MBD, including increased expression of Spp1 and Mmp13 but decreased osteocalcin (Bglap) and Col3a1. These results parallel those previously published from our group identified using scRNAseq and candidate gene analysis in the adenine model, pinpointing these changes to early and more mature osteoblasts.35 In addition to upregulation of pre-osteoblast markers Tnc and Mmp13, osteoclast genes were also induced during CKD-MBD, a known effect of elevated PTH and potentially contributing to osteoclast differentiation and bone remodeling pathways. In CKD-MBD bone, collagen catabolism was upregulated, in line with results in primary osteoblasts derived from pediatric patients with CKD-MBD.50 Further, in the CKD-MBD bone and particularly the marrow, upregulation of multiple inflammatory pathways was detected. This, combined with elevated systemic TNFα in the CKD-MBD samples, could be contributing to the upregulation of elevated ROS generation in bone and bone marrow and oxidative stress we observed across all tissue types. While the muscle did have similar upregulation of inflammatory genes, whether the inflammatory responses in marrow and bone potentially exacerbate the oxidative stress phenotype in the muscle, and vice versa, remains to be tested.

To the best of our knowledge, ST has not been reported for simultaneous testing of both muscle and bone transcriptional changes during CKD-MBD. Most muscle ST and scRNAseq analyses to date utilized dissected muscle that is known to be mostly fast or slow twitch subtypes. A strength of our current approach is the unbiased testing of muscle fiber types and for making comparisons across tissues with distinct spatial localization. Further, this technique has also proven to be robust in identifying small areas of less prominent muscle fiber types, as shown by localized expression of Myh11 in the vasculature in our tissue samples (see Supplementary information). Typically, these changes could be lost due to dilution of low proportion tissue types within bulk RNA samples. Finally, the ST method may also reduce overall costs in contrast to testing individual tissue samples, as multiple tissues were analyzed per sequencing run. Although the ST approach has important strengths, there are several limitations of this study. First, the cross-section taken represents the changes and proportion of muscle fiber types at a single location in the upper hindlimb and at one timepoint of CKD-MBD progression. Serial sections would allow for multiple areas to be studied. Further, the resolution of ST is still being optimized, as the Visium analysis spots likely cover multiple cells, resulting in masked changes in detectable gene expression. This is less likely to be apparent in muscle tissue due to the larger fiber sizes. We particularly saw the limitations of this at the border of bone-marrow and bone-muscle, where genes known to be expressed in one tissue could potentially overlap with another. This limitation was reduced however, using integration with a bone scRNAseq dataset, greatly improving spatial resolution.

In conclusion, a comprehensive analysis of the collective muscle and bone transcriptomes from mice with CKD-MBD was lacking. Herein, we used ST to identify upregulation of apoptotic and oxidative stress pathways shared across muscle, bone, and marrow in CKD-MBD, potentially driven by elevated inflammation and ROS. Thus, simultaneous tissue-specific and general phenotypes that may influence bone and muscle function in CKD-MBD were resolved, suggesting that multiple pathways may have to be targeted to improve musculoskeletal function in this disease.
