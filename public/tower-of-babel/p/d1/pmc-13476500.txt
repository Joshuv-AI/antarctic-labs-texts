Hepcidin and Cardiovascular Events in CKD With and Without Dialysis
Authors: Yudai Fujimoto, Yuya Matsue, Taisuke Nakade, Yu Suresvar Singh, Yuka Akama, Yutaka Nakamura, Junzhe Cao, Tadao Aikawa, Kentaro Sakamaki, Tohru Minamino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHepcidin’s prognostic role in patients with anemia and chronic kidney disease (CKD), regardless of dialysis requirement, remains unclear.MethodsThis was a post hoc analysis of Anemia Studies in CKD: Erythropoiesis via a Novel Prolyl Hydroxylase Inhibitor Daprodustat–Nondialysis (ASCEND-ND) and Anemia Studies in CKD: Erythropoiesis via a Novel Prolyl Hydroxylase Inhibitor Daprodustat–Dialysis (ASCEND-D), 2 phase 3 trials assessing daprodustat’s efficacy and safety compared with conventional erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs). Patients were divided according to quartiles (Q1–4) of baseline hepcidin levels.ResultsIn ASCEND-ND and ASCEND-D, baseline hepcidin values were available for 3807 and 2881 patients (medians: 106 ng/ml and 176 ng/ml), respectively. Higher baseline hepcidin levels were associated with female sex, lower hemoglobin levels, higher transferrin saturation, higher ferritin levels, and higher C-reactive protein levels in both trials. In nondialysis patients, Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) demonstrated that Q2 exhibited the lowest incidence, followed by Q1 and Q3, with Q4 having the highest. This association persisted after risk factor adjustment (P for nonlinearity: 0.032). In dialysis patients, KM curves for MACE demonstrated no association between baseline hepcidin quartiles and MACE, remaining consistent following adjustment for confounding factors. Temporal changes in hepcidin levels were not independently associated with MACE risk. Daprodustat’s treatment effects were not modified by baseline hepcidin levels in either trial.ConclusionIn nondialysis patients with CKD, a nonlinear relationship was observed between baseline hepcidin levels and MACE, whereas no association was found in dialysis patients. Baseline hepcidin levels did not modify the treatment effect of daprodustat compared with ESAs, irrespective of dialysis status, suggesting limited utility of hepcidin for guiding treatment selection.

CKD is a leading public health issue affecting approximately 10% to 15% of the global population.1 Patients with CKD are characterized by the coexistence of distinct entities, including anemia and iron deficiency, which are associated with MACE.2,3 Hepcidin, a peptide hormone critical in iron metabolism regulation, is overexpressed in patients with CKD because of reduced renal clearance, reduced erythropoietin production, and chronic inflammation, thereby inhibiting intestinal iron absorption and iron release from macrophage stores, leading to anemia.4,5 Although some therapies correct anemia and modulate inflammation,6,7 optimal strategies targeting these 2 entities and subsequently improving the prognosis of patients with CKD remain unclear. In this context, hepcidin has been recognized as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in patients with CKD.8,9

Daprodustat, a novel hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor, is a promising therapeutic option for the treatment of CKD-associated anemia.10 One underlying mechanism is the suppression of hepcidin levels.11 In the 2 trials—the ASCEND-ND and ASCEND-D—hepcidin levels were lower in the daprodustat group than in the ESA group shortly after treatment.12,13 Nevertheless, few studies involving a limited number of patients with CKD have investigated the prognostic role of hepcidin14,15; furthermore, no study has evaluated the difference in the prognostic values of hepcidin between dialysis and nondialysis patients with CKD. Moreover, despite the lack of an overall beneficial effect of daprodustat on MACE, there are no investigations into whether hepcidin levels before treatment modify the prognostic impact of daprodustat. This study assessed the following: (i) the prognostic value of baseline hepcidin levels and their temporal changes in patients with CKD and (ii) the association between baseline hepcidin levels and the efficacy of daprodustat treatment in patients with CKD using data from the ASCEND-D and ASCEND-ND trials.

Methods

The design and primary results of the ASCEND-ND (NCT02876835) and ASCEND-D (NCT02879305) trials have been described previously.12,13,16,17 Briefly, the ASCEND-ND trial enrolled 3872 patients with stages 3 to 5 CKD who were not receiving dialysis or were scheduled to begin within 90 days, had anemia with a hemoglobin level of 8 to 10 g/dl or 8 to 11 g/dl if not receiving or receiving ESA, respectively, and were not iron deficient, as indicated by serum ferritin > 100 ng/ml and transferrin saturation > 20%. Patients who had experienced myocardial infarction, acute coronary syndrome, stroke, or transient ischemic attack within 4 weeks of screening, New York Heart Association functional class IV heart failure, or uncontrolled hypertension were excluded. Those who adhered to the placebo and continued to meet the hemoglobin inclusion criteria during a 4-week run-in period were randomized 1:1 to open-label treatment with ESA darbepoetin or daprodustat. The doses of both agents were adjusted according to a prespecified algorithm to maintain a hemoglobin level of 10 to 11 g/dl. Iron levels were managed according to a prespecified protocol. Participants and their physicians were not blinded to the treatment assignments. The ASCEND-D trial randomized 2964 dialysis patients with CKD receiving ESA and having a hemoglobin level of 8 to 11.5 g/dl. The comparator in the ASCEND-D trial for the conventional ESA was recombinant human erythropoietin. The additional inclusion criteria and trial procedures for the ASCEND-D trial were similar to those of the ASCEND-ND trial.

Serum hepcidin levels were measured by a central laboratory as part of the original ASCEND trial biomarker assessments. Detailed information on the assay platform, isoform specificity, and intra- and interassay coefficients of variation was unavailable in the study documentation for this secondary analysis. In the ASCEND-D trial, laboratory assessments were performed before dialysis for in-center hemodialysis subjects, between dialysis sessions for home hemodialysis subjects at study visits, and at study visits for peritoneal dialysis subjects, as per standard of care. The patients in each trial were stratified into 4 groups based on baseline hepcidin quartiles.

In both trials, outcomes, including death from any cause, myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure hospitalization, and thrombus events, were prospectively adjudicated by the Central Clinical Events Committee, led by the Duke Clinical Research Institute (Durham, NC). To assess the malnutrition-inflammation indices among hepcidin quartile groups, we compared the following 4 indices. The prognostic nutritional index was calculated as 10 × serum albumin concentration (g/dl) + 0.005 × total lymphocyte count (/mm3). The geriatric nutritional risk index was calculated as 1.489 × serum albumin (g/l) + 41.7 × actual body weight/ideal body weight, with the weight ratio capped at 1. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was calculated as neutrophil count divided by lymphocyte count. The fibrosis-4 index was calculated as age (years) × aspartate aminotransferase (U/l) divided by platelet count (109/l) × the square root of alanine aminotransferase (U/l). Herein, the composite of MACE, defined as death from any cause, myocardial infarction, or stroke, was used as the primary end point, as it was among the prespecified coprimary end points in both trials. Combined thromboembolic events (the first occurrence of MACE or thromboembolic events) and combined heart failure events (the first occurrence of MACE or heart failure hospitalization) were assessed as secondary end points, as these were the prespecified common principal secondary outcomes in the ASCEND trials. All-cause mortality was assessed as a secondary end point. All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocols were approved by the local ethics committees and institutional review boards of each site.

Statistical Analyses

Data are expressed as mean ± SD for normally distributed variables and as median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. When necessary, the variables were log-transformed for further analysis. Patients were stratified into 4 groups (Q1: lowest; Q4: highest) based on baseline hepcidin quartiles. Baseline patient characteristics were compared using 1-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. Incidence rates were calculated per 100 person-years of follow-up from the first event occurrence to the end of follow-up. In all time-to-event analyses, the time origin was defined as the date of randomization. Consistent with the primary intention-to-treat analyses of the ASCEND-D and ASCEND-ND trials, patients who did not experience the event of interest were right-censored at the administrative end of the trial or the date of their last known clinical contact, whichever occurred first. KM curves were constructed, and event rates were compared using log-rank tests to evaluate differences in the incidence of each end point across hepcidin quartiles. To assess the association between changes in hepcidin achieved 4 weeks after the allocated treatment and MACE, a landmark analysis was performed to construct KM curves 4 weeks after randomization. Cox regression analysis was performed to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to assess the prognostic value of the hepcidin quartiles. To clarify whether the treatment effect of daprodustat versus conventional ESA on MACE was modified by baseline hepcidin levels, we constructed KM curves for MACE across 4 groups stratified by treatment allocation and median baseline hepcidin levels and assessed the interaction between treatment and median baseline hepcidin levels. Because hepcidin levels are affected by various factors, the following variables known to be associated with both MACE and hepcidin were included as adjustment variables in the adjusted models for both ASCEND-ND and ASCEND-D analyses: age, sex, race, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, history of cardiovascular disease, hemoglobin, transferrin saturation (TSAT), C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin, hypertension, low-density lipid cholesterol, and intravenous iron use. CKD stage, previous ESA use, and oral iron use were included as adjustment variables in the ASCEND-ND trial, whereas dialysis type, duration, and ESA hyporesponsiveness were included in the ASCEND-D trial. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals for each Cox regression model, including tests for the covariate of interest and the global test. To elucidate the effect of hepcidin on MACE, adjusted HRs for MACE were evaluated using linear and nonlinear models with restricted cubic splines with 3, 4, and 5 knots. Goodness-of-fit was compared between the models using analysis of variance, and the model with the best fit was selected. Incremental prognostic value was assessed using time-dependent C-statistics at 2 years and category-free net reclassification improvement. The primary analyses were performed using complete-case datasets. As a sensitivity analysis, missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations under the missing-at-random assumption. Twenty imputed datasets were generated using variables included in the primary analysis model, outcome variables, and clinically relevant auxiliary variables. Cox proportional hazards models were then fitted separately in each imputed dataset, and the resulting estimates were pooled according to Rubin’s rules. Statistical significance was set at a 2-tailed P-value < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Of the 3872 and 2964 patients randomized in the ASCEND-ND and ASCEND-D trials, 3807 and 2881 patients had hepcidin levels measured at baseline, and 3545 and 2664 patients had hepcidin levels measured at 4 weeks after randomization, respectively. In both trials, hepcidin distribution was right-skewed (Figure 1). The median hepcidin levels were 106 ng/ml (IQR: 62–168 ng/ml) and 176 ng/ml (IQR: 109–255 ng/ml) in nondialysis and dialysis patients, respectively. Each study population was categorized into quartiles according to baseline hepcidin levels: Q1 (<62 ng/ml, n = 953), Q2 (62–106 ng/ml, n = 952), Q3 (106–168 ng/ml, n = 952) and Q4 (>168 ng/ml, n = 951) in nondialysis patients, and Q1 (<109 ng/ml, n = 721), Q2 (109–176 ng/ml, n = 722), Q3 (176–255 ng/ml, n =720) and Q4 (>255 ng/ml, n = 718) in dialysis patients. A comparison of the patients’ baseline characteristics among the 4 groups is displayed in Tables 1 and 2. In both nondialysis and dialysis patients, higher hepcidin levels were associated with lower hemoglobin, higher ferritin, higher TSAT, higher iron, lower total iron-binding capacity, and higher CRP levels. In nondialysis patients, higher hepcidin levels were associated with younger age, Asian ethnicity, more advanced CKD stage, less history of cardiovascular disease, and greater ESA use. In nondialysis patients, lower hepcidin levels were associated with a higher fibrosis-4 index, whereas geriatric nutritional risk index and prognostic nutritional index did not differ significantly across hepcidin quartiles. Conversely, higher hepcidin levels were associated with higher neutrophil counts and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio. In dialysis patients, higher hepcidin levels were associated with longer dialysis duration but not with age or history of cardiovascular disease. During a median follow-up of 684 and 888 days in the ASCEND-ND and ASCEND-D trials, 758 and 759 MACEs (primary end points), 890 and 867 combined heart failure events, 844 and 1043 combined thromboembolic events, and 589 and 567 deaths were observed, respectively. In nondialysis patients, the KM curves for MACE demonstrated that the highest quartile (Q4) group exhibited the highest incidence, followed by the Q1 and Q3 groups, and the Q2 group had the lowest incidence (log-rank P = 0.001; Figure 2). Similar trends were observed for all secondary end points, including combined heart failure, thromboembolism, and death (Supplementary Figure S4). Conversely, in dialysis patients, the KM curves for all end points demonstrated no association between the baseline hepcidin quartiles and prognosis (Figure 2 and Supplementary Figure S5). Cox proportional hazard analysis revealed that, in nondialysis patients, Q1 and Q4 were independent prognostic factors for MACE after adjusting for conventional risk factors compared with Q2 (Q1 vs. Q2: HR: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.07–1.66, P = 0.010) (Q4 vs. Q2: HR: 1.40, 95% CI: 1.12–1.74, P = 0.003; Table 3). In dialysis patients, no such association was observed for any of the end points (Table 4). Schoenfeld residual testing did not provide evidence of a clinically meaningful violation of the proportional hazard assumption for hepcidin quartiles or the overall model. In a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation for missing covariates, the adjusted Cox models yielded results consistent with those of the primary analysis in both nondialysis and dialysis patients (Supplementary Table S2). Regarding incremental prognostic values for primary end point, hepcidin quartiles improved net reclassification over a clinical model including ferritin, TSAT, and CRP, in nondialysis patients (net reclassification improvement: 0.148; 95% CI: 0.049–0.242; P = 0.003); however, it did not improve C-statistics (P = 0.219; Supplementary Table S3). Discussion

Using data from the ASCEND-D and ASCEND-ND trials, which included a sizable cohort of patients with CKD both requiring and not requiring dialysis, we found the following: (ⅰ) baseline hepcidin levels demonstrated a nonlinear relationship with MACE in nondialysis patients; (ⅱ) baseline hepcidin levels were not associated with MACE in dialysis patients; (ⅲ) changes in hepcidin from baseline to 4 weeks were not associated with subsequent clinical outcomes in patients with CKD, irrespective of dialysis status; and (ⅳ) baseline hepcidin levels did not modify the treatment effect of daprodustat compared with ESA in patients with CKD, irrespective of dialysis status.

To our knowledge, this is the largest study to investigate the association between serum hepcidin levels and prognosis in patients with CKD. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies in both CKD and non-CKD settings. Regarding the association between high hepcidin levels and poor prognosis in non-dialysis patients with CKD, higher serum hepcidin or hepcidin-25 levels have been shown to be associated with poor outcomes in patients with diabetic CKD not requiring dialysis,18 diabetes mellitus,19 HIV-related tuberculosis,20 and lymphoma,21 although all studies included a limited number of patients. In patients with some cancers, hepcidin overexpression was associated with poor prognosis.22,23 Importantly, these studies included almost no dialysis patients, corroborating our finding that the highest quartile of serum hepcidin was associated with a higher MACE incidence, even after adjustments for CRP and TSAT, especially in non-dialysis patients with CKD. The elevated risk observed in the highest quartile may represent the burden of systemic inflammation. In our analysis of the ASCEND-ND trial, patients with higher hepcidin levels had higher CRP levels and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio. Since hepcidin is strongly upregulated by unmeasured inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6, markedly elevated hepcidin levels in Q4 likely serve as a surrogate for underlying, residual inflammatory states that contribute to adverse cardiovascular outcomes. This finding is not surprising, considering that increased hepcidin concentrations are associated with arteriosclerotic disease by retaining iron in macrophages in the vascular wall, potentially resulting in a pro-atherogenic environment mediated by oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and macrophage apoptosis.9,24 Contrastingly, here, the first (lowest) quartile of serum hepcidin levels exhibited a higher MACE risk than did the second quartile in non-dialysis patients. The elevated risk observed in the lowest quartile potentially reflects a persisting degree of suboptimal iron availability or functional iron restriction. In our analysis, patients with lower hepcidin levels had lower TSAT and lower ferritin levels. Although patients with severe iron deficiency (defined by strict cutoffs of TSAT < 20% and ferritin < 100 ng/ml) were excluded from this analysis, biological iron status exists along a continuous spectrum. Thus, lower hepcidin may represent suboptimal iron availability or functional iron restriction just above these traditional thresholds. Moreover, this inverse association between serum hepcidin levels and clinical outcomes is consistent with reports on alcoholic cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma,25,26 suggesting that hepcidin downregulation or lower serum hepcidin levels may represent a marker of severe liver dysfunction. This hypothesis is supported by our finding that fibrosis-4 index was higher in patients with lower hepcidin levels. Likewise, in patients with community-acquired pneumonia, lower serum hepcidin levels were associated with higher long-term mortality but not with short-term adverse outcomes,27 suggesting that lower hepcidin levels during the acute phase might be linked to poor long-term prognosis, independent of acute disease recovery. Overall, conditions in which a patient requires hepcidin production but cannot produce it sufficiently may represent a risk factor for adverse outcomes. These previous studies and the potential underlying mechanisms would support our findings, which show a nonlinear relationship between serum hepcidin levels and MACE in nondialysis patients.

Another important finding of our study was that no association between serum hepcidin levels and MACE was observed in dialysis patients. To date, few studies have focused on dialysis patients, and these have shown contrasting results. The first study to show an association between hepcidin and clinical events in patients on hemodialysis was an analysis of data from the Convective Transport Study, which included 405 patients on hemodialysis and showed that higher hepcidin-25 levels were related to a higher incidence of cardiovascular events, even after adjustment for CRP.15 Nonetheless, the study included coronary heart disease other than myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular interventions, and lower limb amputation as components of cardiovascular events,15 whereas our study used predefined, objective MACE definitions. Furthermore, hepcidin-25 levels were not associated with all-cause mortality after covariate adjustment, which is consistent with our study findings. A study including 561 patients undergoing renal transplantation demonstrated no association between serum hepcidin-25 levels and all-cause mortality or cardiovascular mortality, whereas CRP levels were significantly associated with mortality.14 Originally, the proposed rationale for the association between hepcidin and cardiovascular events was that higher hepcidin levels may be linked to atherosclerotic plaque progression, with impaired iron efflux from macrophages proposed as 1 possible mechanism.28 This hypothesis was supported by findings that in patients with extensive atherosclerotic plaques, atherosclerotic iron content was associated with pro-oxidant activity within plaque,29 and iron-laden macrophages might exhibit pro-atherogenic activity by the oxidation of low-density lipoproteins.30 Although whether hepcidin directly contributes to atherosclerotic progression remains uncertain,31 in previous studies, higher hepcidin levels were associated with atherosclerotic burden and cardiovascular risk. Considering that in patients on dialysis or with end-stage renal disease, cardiovascular events are mainly driven by vascular intima calcification rather than atherosclerosis, it is reasonable to observe no association between hepcidin and MACE in patients with end-stage renal disease. Another explanation for this null association may be the different distribution of hepcidin levels in non-dialysis and dialysis patients. Hepcidin levels were higher in dialysis patients than in non-dialysis patients, and only a small proportion of dialysis patients had hepcidin levels within the optimal range suggested by the nondialysis cohort.

The discrepancies in observations between non-dialysis and dialysis patients should be interpreted with caution. Low hepcidin levels may reflect impaired hepatic regulation, or severe systemic illness, and high hepcidin levels may reflect inflammation or iron-restricted erythropoiesis; however, these mechanisms were not directly demonstrated herein. Detailed information on alcohol use, liver disease, and broader inflammatory biomarkers was unavailable; therefore, residual confounding remains possible. This issue may be particularly relevant in non-dialysis patients who are clinically more heterogeneous than dialysis patients. Conversely, dialysis patients may have had a more homogeneous burden of kidney failure, vascular pathology, and dialysis-related inflammation, partly explaining the absence of a clear association between hepcidin and outcomes. Thus, the differing findings between non-dialysis and dialysis patients should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence of fundamentally distinct biology but rather as reflecting differences in unmeasured clinical heterogeneity.

The effects of daprodustat treatment on cardiovascular events were not modified by baseline hepcidin levels in patients with CKD, regardless of dialysis status, suggesting that targeting elevated hepcidin levels may not necessarily improve the prognosis of patients with CKD. Our observation further supported this hypothesis that the hepcidin level changes from baseline to 4 weeks were not associated with subsequent MACE. To our knowledge, no previous study has investigated the association between temporal hepcidin level changes and prognosis in CKD or non-CKD settings. Nonetheless, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors have been demonstrated to reduce hepcidin levels in patients with heart failure,32 and a subanalysis of the Dapagliflozin and Prevention of Adverse-Outcomes in Heart Failure trial indicated that dapagliflozin reduced hepcidin by 20% to 30% compared with placebo in patients with heart failure and reduced ejection fraction,6 comparable to the degree of hepcidin reduction observed with daprodustat herein. Additionally, dapagliflozin suppressed circulating hepcidin levels in patients with obesity complicated by type 2 diabetes.33 Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors improved the prognosis in 2 large-scale randomized clinical trials in nondialysis patients with CKD,34,35 whereas our findings showed that hepcidin changes were not associated with subsequent clinical outcomes in patients with CKD, irrespective of dialysis status, suggesting that hepcidin may function primarily as a marker rather than a causal mediator of cardiovascular risk in CKD. Nevertheless, to draw definitive conclusions on whether hepcidin reduction confers a prognostic benefit, dedicated mechanistic and interventional studies directly targeting the hepcidin pathway are warranted.

This study has some limitations. First, detailed assay characteristics for hepcidin measurement, including the assay platform, specificity for hepcidin-25, and intra- and interassay coefficients of variation, were not available. Therefore, we could not determine whether the measured values specifically reflected biologically active hepcidin-25 or total hepcidin, including other isoforms or degradation products. If nonhepcidin-25 isoforms were also detected, hepcidin levels may have been overestimated, reducing measurement specificity. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as associations with centrally measured circulating hepcidin levels, rather than with hepcidin-25 specifically. Second, the 2 trials used different active comparators, and conventional ESA or high erythropoietin levels were associated with a greater risk of cardiovascular events,36 which may have led to an underestimation of the potential risk of cardiovascular events with daprodustat relative to placebo.37 Third, the ASCEND trials enrolled selected patients with renal anemia and excluded those with recent MACE or advanced heart failure; therefore, the generalizability of our findings to broader CKD populations may be limited. Fourth, because hypoxia-inducible factor activates transcription of many cytokines, some of which have oncogenic or other long-term adverse effects, the observation time in these trials may be insufficient to fully characterize the risks. Fifth, several of the primary and subgroup analyses may have been underpowered, despite the large overall sample size; therefore negative findings should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, although baseline hepcidin did not modify the treatment effect of daprodustat versus ESAs, we did not perform comprehensive subgroup-effects modeling using multiple baseline characteristics. Therefore, this study provides limited insight into personalized treatment selection based on baseline variables. Future studies specifically designed to evaluate treatment-effect heterogeneity are warranted to identify patient subgroups that may derive greater benefit from daprodustat or ESAs.

In conclusion, a nonlinear relationship between serum hepcidin levels and MACE was observed in nondialysis patients with CKD, whereas no association was observed in dialysis patients. Baseline hepcidin levels did not modify the treatment effect of daprodustat on clinical outcomes compared with ESA in dialysis and nondialysis patients with CKD.
