Climate anxiety in children and young people: A systematic review of prevalence and measurement methods
Authors: Lucija Furman, Matej Vinko, Simona Perčič, Ema Kobal, Lucija Ačko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionClimate anxiety is a growing concern, especially for young people. However, varied definitions and measurement approaches complicate understanding its scope, leading to inconsistent prevalence estimates. This systematic review assessed climate anxiety prevalence among youth (≤ 29 years) and documented measurement methods to clarify public health relevance and identify research gaps.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, we systematically reviewed PubMed, PsycINFO, and Web of Science for peer-reviewed English-language studies on youth climate anxiety prevalence. Duplicate screening, data extraction, and quality assessment occurred. Narrative synthesis and random-effects meta-analyses were performed.ResultsForty cross-sectional studies, mostly from High-income countries (HICs) and rated moderate risk of bias, were identified that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Definitions of climate anxiety varied widely. Single-item questions were common for prevalence estimation (pooled prevalence from 14 estimates: 63%, 95% CI: 58%-68%). Validated scales (mainly CCAS, Clayton and Karazsia, 2020) assessing levels indicated low level of climate anxiety symptoms on average (pooled mean from 14 studies: 1.66, 95% CI: 1.50-1.82). High heterogeneity was present in both analyses. No significant prevalence difference was found between Low- and Middle-income countries (LMICs) (68%) and High-income countries (HICs) (60%) (p=0.12).ConclusionOur review shows that climate anxiety is widely reported among young people, yet average symptom severity appears to be relatively low. Interpretation of prevalence estimates is limited by methodological heterogeneity, variation in measurement approaches, and insufficient reporting of moderating factors. Inconsistent definitions and geographical research bias also limit our understanding of the issue.

Introduction

The Anthropocene represents a new era of environmental destruction on Earth, primarily driven by human activities. Since the Industrial Revolution, human interactions with and exploitation of the environment have led to catastrophic consequences, including global climate change [1]. Climate anxiety and eco-anxiety—emotional distress linked to the climate and ecological crises—are receiving growing global attention as awareness of the present and future threats posed by a warming planet continues to rise [2]. Growing concerns have been raised about the global impact of climate change on mental health. These impacts can be direct or indirect, as well as short- or long-term. Direct mental health effects include the trauma experienced by individuals exposed to climate-related events such as floods, hurricanes, droughts, wildfires [3,4]. Additionally, climate change events have been associated to increased rates of suicide and burden of mental disorders [[5], [6], [7]]. Indirect consequences encompass economic losses, threats to physical health and community well-being, displacement, and forced migration [[7], [8], [9], [10]].

Climate change poses profound emotional, physical, and existential challenges for young people. They are not only dealing with the immediate impacts of climate change but are also navigating life paths that align with an increasingly unstable and climate-altered world. As a result, young people are at the forefront of learning to cope with the distress of these transformed environments while seeking ways to foster more equitable socio-ecological relationships and futures. Their varied emotional responses to this challenge have been acknowledged in both social research and public discussions. However, as Verlie (2022) [11] describes in the concept of "learning to live with climate change," the shared and interpersonal dimensions of these emotional experiences remain largely unexplored.

Systematic reviews highlight the diverse ways in which climate anxiety and eco-anxiety are defined and measured. For example, van Dijk [12] and co-workers note various operationalizations of climate anxiety, while Coffey [13] and co-workers identify over ten different interpretations of eco-anxiety. Additionally, studies have developed numerous scales to assess climate anxiety. Van Dijk [12] and co-workers, for instance, mention 12 scales used to measure climate anxiety, some of which focus on related concepts like eco-anxiety, climate distress, or environmental concern. Among them, four scales are specifically designed to assess climate anxiety: Climate Change Anxiety Scale, the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory for climate anxiety, the Anxiety about Climate Change Survey, and the Inventory of Climate Emotions—climate anxiety subscale. These scales demonstrate limited overlap in terms of item content. As a result of these varied measurement tools, it's unsurprising that reports on the prevalence of climate anxiety in the general adult population show significant variation. For example, 3% of U.S. adults may be experiencing potentially serious levels of anxiety due to climate change based on the adapted GAD-2 Climate measure [14], while 9.37% of Australian adults were identified as experiencing climate anxiety using the Climate Change Anxiety Scale (with a cutoff score of 3) [15], and 26% of U.S. adults reported being anxious about climate change in another study [16]. Given the varying prevalence estimates and measurement approaches, this review aims to assess climate anxiety prevalence specifically among young people – a group identified as particularly vulnerable – and to document the methods used for its measurement.

While several reviews have examined climate anxiety broadly, youth-specific systematic reviews remain limited. The field is experiencing exponential growth, with 242 publications on climate anxiety in 2024 alone, meaning that even recent reviews quickly become outdated [17]. Existing youth-focused reviews have been constrained by small evidence bases and have consistently highlighted the lack of standardized measurement approaches and prevalence data as critical gaps [18]. Given the rapid evolution of this field and the persistent methodological heterogeneity, multiple independent teams conducting sequential reviews serve an important function: they capture emerging evidence, cross-validate findings across different search strategies and time periods, and provide iterative refinement of our understanding of this phenomenon in vulnerable populations. This review therefore aims to provide an updated synthesis of climate anxiety prevalence and measurement specifically in young people, contributing to the growing evidence base on this critical public health concern.

Method

Methods and findings are reported following guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) Statement [19]. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (International prospective register of systematic reviews) on 21 October 2024 (CRD42024600148).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To be eligible for inclusion, studies must have a focus on climate anxiety among young people aged 29 years or younger and must report estimates of the prevalence of climate anxiety among young people. We defined young people as individuals aged ≤29 years, aligning with Eurostat's definition of youth (15-29 years) used across European social, economic, and health statistics. This definition extends beyond the WHO's young people category (10-24 years) to account for delayed social role transitions such as completion of education, financial independence, and parenthood. We limited our search to quantitative studies (cross-sectional, cohort, etc.) and mixed methods studies. We included peer-reviewed research in English, without time or geographical limitations. We excluded non-peer-reviewed journal articles and books, as well as conference abstracts and proceedings, dissertations, editorials, position papers/perspectives, review articles and grey literature.

Information sources and search strategy

We undertook an extensive search in October 2024 in the following databases: PubMed, PsycINFO, and Web of Science. We used a combination of key terms related to 1) climate change, 2) climate anxiety, and 3) children, adolescents or young people. We used the following search string: ("Climate change" or "global warming" or "environment*change*" or "climate crisis" or "climate disaster" or "ecological crisis" or "environmental disaster" or "environmental problem*" or "environmental issues") AND ("eco-angst" or "eco-grief" or anxious* or stress* or worry or worries or "eco-anxiet*" or "climate anxiety" or "ecological worry" or "climate change anxiety" or "environmental worry" or "environmental distress" or "ecological stress") AND (Youth* or kid* or adolescent* or teen* or "school-aged" or "young people" or "young person" or child*), child*) , which we have adapted to the requirements of each database. An updated literature search was conducted in January 2026 using the same search strategy and databases, with additional searches performed in CINAHL and Scopus.

Study selection

Studies identified in different databases were transferred to Rayyan, a web-based software for conducting systematic reviews [20]. This was followed by the removal of duplicates and a two-step screening process. In the first step, titles and abstracts of all the papers were assessed against the eligibility criteria for this review. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by four review authors in two pairs (i.e. each pair screened half of the identified studies) to identify studies deemed potentially eligible. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion between the review authors. In the second step, the review authors in pairs assessed the full texts of potentially relevant studies for inclusion. Studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded and the reasons for exclusion were recorded. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion between the review authors.

Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

The quality of included studies was assessed by the Quality Assessment Checklist for Prevalence Studies1 [21]. The tool was developed for use in appraising observational cross-sectional studies. Two researchers independently assessed the quality of the included studies, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with consultation with a third researcher if necessary.

Data extraction

Review authors extracted data in duplicate and independently on (1) information about the research conducted, such as year, country/region and research design; in the case of a longitudinal research design, the time frame also was noted; (2) characteristics of the participants, such as age, gender, sample size; (3) the definition of the term "climate anxiety"; (4) the reported outcomes (prevalence, proportion, mean, etc. ) and their estimation with a 95% confidence interval (if mentioned); (5) the measurement instrument used, the psychometric characteristics of the instrument (if given), and whether the instrument has been validated or is a validation study.

Data synthesis

The data were first summarized qualitatively; a narrative synthesis was conducted. In the narrative synthesis, we described the main characteristics and results of the included studies.

As we anticipated considerable between-study heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used to pool estimates. To estimate the pooled climate anxiety prevalence of included studies, both overall and in subgroups, we used a generalized linear mixed-effects model with logit transformation. To estimate the pooled means of climate anxiety we used an inverse variance method with raw means.

We used Knapp-Hartung adjustments [22] to calculate the confidence interval around the pooled effect. Results are presented using forest plots, and heterogeneity was assessed using the Higgins I2 statistic (with values of 25%, 50%, and 75%, representing small, moderate, or large heterogeneity, respectively). The significance of any heterogeneity was examined using Cochran’s Q (χ2) test (p < 0.05). Where appropriate, subgroup analyses were conducted to explore sources of significant heterogeneity, such as country and risk of bias. Where appropriate we used Meta-Analysis Accelerator [23] for statistical data conversion. All data analysis was performed by R [24], packages “meta” and “metafor”.

In the updated search 2,454 records were identified and 115 full-text articles were screened, which resulted in 27 new studies to be included in the review. Characteristics of included studies.

Study characteristics

The majority of studies included in the review was carried out in high-income/developed countries (n=30; e.g. Australia, Canada, Netherlands, Finland, France, Portugal, UK, USA, Italy, Slovenia, Poland, Spain), with a minority in low-middle income/developing countries (n=19; i.e. Turkey, Philippines, China, Brazil, India, Nigeria, Colombia, Mexico, Kenya, Uganda, Indonesia, Pakistan, Malaysia, Bangladesh). All studies were conducted in the last six years and were cross-sectional studies.

Minimum sample size was 66, maximum was 15,793 (median sample size=508.50). 29 studies had a sample size of 1,000 or less. Most studies were conducted on adolescents and young adults between the ages of 16 and 25 (n= 23). The percentage of women in the studies ranged from 33.2% to 86.8%. In a minority of studies data regarding ethnicity (n=4) and place of residence (i.e. urban/rural) were reported (n=7). Data about climate anxiety were seldom reported by socio-demographic characteristic of the participants (n=6), but were reported mainly by gender and age.

Almost all studies included were rated as having moderate risk of bias, primarily due to use of a non-representative sample and nonprobability sampling. Further research will be needed to increase confidence in the levels or prevalence estimate of climate anxiety and may lalter the estimates.

Climate anxiety conceptualization

The reviewed studies show definitional variability regarding climate anxiety; only a few studies used the same definition. Across definitions, the most consistent element is that climate anxiety represents a psychological or an emotional response to person’s awareness of climate change and environmental threats and consequences, e.g., “a constellation of emotions associated with the awareness that climate change is occurring” [25], “a set of negative emotional states characterized by feelings of stress, doom, or despair related to the current and future state of the environment” [26], “a chronic fear of environmental doom” [27]. Authors often describe/conceptualize the emotional response as fear, anxiety, worry, or distress. In some studies, climate anxiety is understood as multidimensional, encompassing emotional, cognitive, physiological, and behavioural responses rather than a single emotional state, e.g. “heightened emotional, mental or somatic distress in response to dangerous changes in the climate system” [28].

Eco-anxiety is often used interchangeably with climate anxiety, although some authors conceptualize it as a broader term than climate anxiety, referring it to distress, fear related to wider ecological crises, including loss of biodiversity and environmental degradation.

Furthermore, some authors frame climate anxiety as a mental condition with sub-clinical features [29], and as a spectrum ranging from adaptive responses that may motivate pro-environmental action to maladaptive responses that can stall individual behavior [30] and impair functioning [31]. In 3 studies [[32], [33], [34]] no definition was given.

Measures used

8 studies (out of 10) that reported data on prevalence or percentage used one-items measures; e.g. Does climate change make you feel anxious? with response options yes or no. No study provided information regarding validity and reliability of the measures used. Wu et al [35], as a part of a validation study, used a Climate Change Anxiety Scale – Short Form, which demonstrated satisfactory reliability and sufficient construct validity.

Studies that reported data on levels of climate anxiety among youth mostly used the Climate Change Anxiety Xcale (CCAS) [36] (n=19) and Hogg Eco-Anxiety Scale (HEAS) [37] (n=9). CCAS is an established questionnaire for measuring climate anxiety, with satisfying psychometric characteristics. In studies included in the review that reported information about reliability the measure demonstrated good reliability; Cronbach α coefficients were above .80, as recommended by Nunnally & Bernstein [38]. HEAS is a brief four-dimensional measure of eco-anxiety; in 6 studies there were no reported data regarding reliability, in remaining studies (n=3) the measure had adequate reliability (α coefficients for all subscales were > .72). In 3 studies other measures were used (i.e. Basic Eco-Anxiety Scale for Children and Adolescents, The Inventory of Climate Emotions, Climate Anxiety Scale), all with good reliability (Cronbach α coefficients were above .80).

Climate anxiety levels and prevalence

A meta-analysis of means/levels

14 studies that used CCAS were included in meta-analysis. Range of means of climate anxiety symptoms was between 1.25 and 2.15 out of 5, with pooled mean estimate of 1.66 (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.50-1.82), indicating that on average adolescents and young people reported low level of climate anxiety. There was considerable heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 99.3 %, Q = 1814.04, p < 0.001), indicating that that most of the observed variance in mean estimates across studies is due to real differences in mean estimates rather than sampling error. To assess robustness of meta-analysis results, analysis of outliers and influential cases was performed. The study by Ediz & Yanik [39] emerged as an influential study (Rstudent = 6.63, Dffits = 1.76, Cook’s distance = .78) showing some impact on mean estimate and between-study heterogeneity. As such it was excluded from the analysis. Discussion

The objective of this systematic literature review was to assess the prevalence of climate anxiety among young people. Studies utilizing single-item measures report a pooled prevalence estimate of climate anxiety among youth at 63%, suggesting a high rate of climate anxiety amongst young people. Additionally, studies employing scale-based measures indicate that, on average, adolescents and young people report low levels of climate anxiety. The discrepancy between a high prevalence but a low level of symptoms may be explained by the distinction of the occurrence of climate anxiety and the intensity of experienced symptoms of climate anxiety. Prevalence estimates capture the proportion of young people who report at least some level of anxiety about climate change, whereas symptom severity reflects the magnitude of experienced distress and potential cognitive-emotional and functional impairment. As can be seen, a large proportion of adolescents and young people may report some degree of anxiety about climate change, thereby increasing prevalence estimates, while at the same time experience these symptoms at relatively low levels, without impairment on daily functioning. Previous studies suggest that climate anxiety often represents a rational and an understandable response to a perceived global threat rather than a pathological mental condition [36,37,43,67]. As such, the high prevalence rates do not necessary imply nor are evidence of elevated psychological distress, but could be seen as an adaptive emotional reaction that is widespread and varies in intensity/severity across individuals. Still, such an interpretation risks oversimplifying the underlying construct. In particular, it assumes that low average mean symptom levels necessarily reflect predominantly mild experiences of anxiety, despite the absence of detailed distributional data. It remains possible that a small but clinically relevant subgroup with elevated symptom severity is obscured within aggregate statistics. As only a limited number of studies categorized climate anxiety into severity levels (e.g., mild, moderate, severe) [61,62], it remains difficult to determine how symptom severity is distributed within the population, including the proportion of individuals experiencing mild versus more severe forms of climate anxiety.

One potential explanation for this discrepancy between high prevalence rates and low levels of anxiety also could lie in the psychometric properties of the instruments used. The Climate Change Anxiety Scale [36], for example, is a psychometrically validated instrument that demonstrates both reliability and content validity. In contrast, studies that used single-item measures (e.g. [43]) generally did not report on the validity or reliability of the tools used, raising questions about whether these instruments accurately and consistently measure the intended construct [68], and how the respondents interpreted the questions. Additionally, the binary response format (i.e., “yes” or “no”) primarily used in single-item measures may contribute to higher prevalence rates. It may lead to acquiescence bias, the tendency to endorse any assertion made in a question, regardless of its content [69]. Rivera-Garrido et al [70] showed that the probability of agreeing with the survey question is higher when using a binary instead of a continuous set of responses. Moreover, individuals who only occasionally experience concern about climate change may still respond affirmatively, thereby being categorized as experiencing climate anxiety. A higher number of response options could allow respondents to differentiate in their responses and thus, to provide more valid or accurate data concerning the issues in question [71,72].

One well-known phenomenon in survey research is overreporting of admirable attitudes and behaviors and underreporting those that are not socially respected, i.e. social desirability bias [69]. In recent years, youth-led and youth-centered climate activism has gained momentum and visibility [73]. It is not unlikely that this heightened awareness may prompt some individuals to bias their responses to achieve a better social impression of themselves, and not on their actual experience.

Future research would benefit from more nuanced measurement approaches, including the use of validated multi-dimensional scales, clear severity thresholds, and analyses on behavioural and psychological correlates that account for the distribution of symptom levels within populations.

Research has shown that various factors can contribute to varying prevalence and degrees of severity of climate anxiety [74,75] consistent with the observed heterogeneity between studies in the present literature review. Direct or indirect experiences with extreme weather events can significantly influence emotional responses to climate change. Lower-income countries are being disproportionately affected by climate change [76], and as such we were interested in differences in prevalence of climate anxiety across regions. Developing countries, particularly those with lower income levels, express greater concern about climate change and reported a stronger impact on daily life compared to wealthier nations [77,78]. This disparity may be attributed to variations in population vulnerability and capacity for climate adaptation [79]. Our subgroup analysis found no statistically significant differences among countries based on income. This could be due to the small sample size of the studies included in the analysis, which meant that no differences could be detected. Moreover, this could be due aforementioned psychometric limitations, and as such could not detect nuances in climate anxiety experienced by young people.

Factors such as individual characteristics, e.g., age, gender, psychological resilience, and coping strategies (see [50], [52], [55]), as well as socioeconomic status [55] and contextual influences such as media exposure [33,49] may moderate symptom severity and be a source of observed between-study heterogeneity. Importantly, studies included in the present literature review rarely reported outcomes stratified by these factors, nor did they consistently provide detailed sample characteristics that would enable moderator or subgroup analyses. For example, studies differed considerably in the proportion of female participants included in their samples. Given evidence that being female is associated with higher reported levels of climate anxiety [[80], [81], [82]], variation in gender composition across samples may contribute substantially to observed variability in pooled estimates. As most of the studies did not assess the prevalence of climate anxiety according to gender, we did not conduct a meta-analysis for gender. Similar limitations apply to other potentially important moderators, such as age distribution, socioeconomic background, and exposure to climate-related events.

Future research and meta-analytic work would benefit from more systematic reporting of these variables and stratified results, which would allow for moderator analyses or meta-regression, and a clearer understanding of sources of heterogeneity in climate anxiety.

Despite lower-income countries being disproportionately affected by climate change and experiencing greater losses [76,83,84], our review shows the majority of existing research continues to be conducted in high-income, developed nations. The latter presents a limitation if we are to fully capture the prevalence of climate anxiety among young people worldwide.

Further findings from our literature review indicate that the research field is still in its early stages of development, but rapidly expanding, with studies of relatively weak quality. The results also highlight a lack of a unified definition of climate anxiety, as well as significant variation in how the concept is understood. Specifically, climate anxiety may be interpreted either as an emotional response—such as fear or anger experienced when thinking about climate change—or as an anxious mental state resembling a psychological condition that arises due to direct or indirect exposure to climate-related threats. The absence of a clearly defined and theoretically grounded conceptual framework represents a significant limitation in the field. A well-defined theoretical foundation—such as a consistent conceptualization of the phenomenon under investigation—is essential for both future empirical research and the development of applied interventions. At present, this conceptual ambiguity may pose a barrier to the advancement of high-quality research in the area of climate anxiety.

Limitations of this review

Our findings should be interpreted in light of weaknesses. Comprehensive quantitative synthesis and generalization of its findings is limited due to the small number of studies included, small sample sizes in several studies, and high heterogeneity; as such pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution. As almost all studies included in the review were rated as having moderate risk of bias, exploring heterogeneity according the level of quality of study was not possible, indicating that future high-quality studies are likely to have an important impact on our confidence in the levels or prevalence estimate of climate anxiety and may lead to changes in the estimates. Additionally, the review was limited to English language articles, which may have led to the exclusion of some relevant studies in other languages. Moreover, the review did not include grey literature, leading to the possibility that some relevant results could have been missed.

We planned to examine the funnel plots for asymmetry and perform Egger’s linear regression test of funnel plot asymmetry as an indication of publication bias. This was not possible for the current review, as observed between-study heterogeneity in our review was high (see [85]).

Conclusions

Previous studies have identified young people as particularly vulnerable group to experience climate anxiety. Our review shows that climate anxiety is widely reported among young people, yet average symptom severity appears to be relatively low. However, interpretation of prevalence estimates is limited by methodological heterogeneity, variation in measurement approaches, and insufficient reporting of moderating factors. Inconsistent definitions and geographical research bias also limit our understanding of the issue. Standardized, validated measures and clearer conceptual definitions are crucial for accurate monitoring and guiding interventions. Robust research across diverse settings is also needed to better understand the nature, distribution, and implications of climate anxiety among youth.
