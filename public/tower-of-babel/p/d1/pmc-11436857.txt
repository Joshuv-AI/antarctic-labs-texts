A framework for spatial-temporal cluster evolution representation and analysis based on graphs
Authors: Ivens Portugal, Paulo Alencar, Donald Cowan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Analysis on spatial-temporal data has several benefits that range from an improved traffic network in a city to increased earnings for drivers and ridesharing companies. A common analysis technique is clustering. However, most clustering techniques consider a constrained representation of clusters that is limited to a single timestamp. Evolving clusters exist through several timestamps and interact with other spatial-temporal objects or clusters, having cluster relationships. When many evolving clusters exist for analysis, a graph can be used to represent cluster evolution. In this article, we propose a framework for graph-based cluster evolution representation and analysis. The framework represents cluster structure and relationships as well as provides a graph representation of cluster evolution for analysis. Evaluation is done in three case studies with spatial-temporal data about taxis that can identify important phenomena or trends in movements in a city for traffic improvement.

Introduction

Spatial-temporal data hold information about the location of an object at regular time intervals1. For example, GPS devices attached to taxis show the location of the taxis in the city at each minute of the day. The analysis of spatial-temporal data has many benefits. For example, ridesharing apps may use analysis techniques to find interesting patterns in traffic data and provide affordable prices, a supermarket can increase profits by predicting the behavior of a new customer, or the city hall of a city may attempt to improve mobility of citizens and visitors by analyzing and improving the traffic network of the city. One way to analyze spatial-temporal data is through clustering methods. Clustering methods such as ST-DBSCAN2 and TraClus3 identify clusters of objects taking into consideration their spatial and temporal dimensions.

However, the clusters of spatial-temporal objects identified by these methods do not move. These clusters are formed based on the similarity of the spatial (location) and the temporal (time) dimensions of spatial-temporal data, but they are limited to a given timestamp. Despite its benefits, representation of and analysis on graph-based cluster evolution is not widespread as most spatial-temporal analysis techniques and frameworks do not offer support. To address this problem, we set up the following research questions:

RQ

How to define a framework to support graph-based spatial-temporal cluster evolution representation and analysis?

RQ1

How to represent graph-based spatial-temporal cluster evolution?

RQ2

How to analyze graph-based spatial-temporal cluster evolution?

The framework proposed in this article allows for the representation of evolving clusters, including their structure and relationships, and allows for a graph-based representation of cluster evolution. The approach also allows for the analysis of these evolving clusters, including changes to their structure and presence of their relationships, with suitable analytical methods and a visualization of how clusters evolve in time.

The evaluation of the proposed approach is done in terms of three case studies. Case study 1 relates to the identification of ever-increasing and ever-decreasing regions as well as the calculation of an average rate of change (AROC) of size of a cluster. These regions are moments in the evolution of a cluster when the number of participants always increases or decreases. Identifying these regions helps in the assessment of the location or time of important phenomena, such as traffic jams or events in a city. Case study 2 relates to the calculation of a similarity value between the evolution of clusters and correlation analysis between this similarity and other metrics such as the number of clusters or evolution paths. This result is useful in the identification of outlier clusters that represent peak demand, for example, or in machine learning tasks. Case study 3 relates to the analysis of the movement represented by the evolution of spatial-temporal clusters. The case study identifies the evolution of clusters that start at specific locations and analyze them based on the distribution by region and day and the time they start. The results can be used, for example, in improvements in city infrastructure related to city planning, traffic flow, or the road network.

This article is structured as follows. Section Related Work describes some research areas related to this study and Section Framework describes the proposed approach. Section Case Studies describes three case studies and Section Conclusion completes the article with future work.

Related work

In this section, we describe the main areas related to the framework proposed in this study. We start with a description of the co-movement pattern extraction and some results in this area. Next, we describe some data analysis methods specifically to the spatial-temporal domain. We continue the description of methods with an overview of clustering techniques, more specifically the one used in the proposed framework. Lastly, we explain the concept of cluster relationships and how they can be represented and we conclude the section with the characterization of cluster evolution in our proposal.

Our approach is novel in the sense that it is founded on graph-based representations, explores graph-based relationships that characterize interactions between spatial-temporal clusters and cluster objects, and analyzes the evolution of clusters based on spatial and temporal dimensions, and cluster relationships. The provided statistical analysis are novel in the sense that they consider cluster evolution aspects.

Co-movement pattern extraction

Co-movement pattern extraction is an area of study within data analysis and time series analysis that focuses on identifying and analyzing the shared movement patterns among multiple time series datasets4. Intuitively, data objects, such as vehicles, move together in space. Occasionally, an object moves away from the rest of the objects, but may return later. The identification and analysis of these patterns allows for new insights in the behavior of vehicles in a road or people in the street. Some popular patterns4 are the flock5, group6, convoy7, swarm8, and platoon9 patterns. They differ in both their spatial and temporal aspects. For example, flock and group are radius-based while convoy, swarm, and platoon are density-based. In addition, flock and convoy require objects to be in the cluster in consecutive timestamps, whereas group and platoon use a parameter to allow a number of data points to temporarily leave the cluster. Swarms do not impose any restriction.

Several studies attempt to either identify or support the identification of co-movement patterns. One study10 proposes a solution to the problem of real-time analysis in this domain by identifying co-movement patterns in streams. However, movement analysis is not based on a graph, as is done in our approach, which focuses on graph-based cluster evolution. Another study11 proposes a data mining and visualization framework, called GeoChron, to identify and represent evolution patterns in streams of spatial-temporal data. The framework calculates a correlation coefficient between two time series within a slice of time and adjacent slices. Then, a relation network is constructed based on the location and correlation values. Next, the Louvain algorithm12 is used to detect communities in the network and the evolution of spatial-temporal data and communities are represented in framework as storylines. However, the final analysis of patterns and evolution has a limited representation of the spatial aspect, which hinders movement analysis based on a graph, as is done in our approach. One last study13 uses a graph for representation of spatial-temporal evolution and then traverses this graph to detect co-evolving patterns. The study, however, does not provide analyses on the detected patterns, which limits the usefulness of the patterns. On the contrary, the framework proposed in our study detects and analyses clusters, their evolution, and demonstrate the usefulness of results through case studies.

Spatial-temporal data analysis

Objects, such as a car or a phone, that move in space are called spatial-temporal objects. When observed, spatial-temporal objects produce spatial-temporal data. Spatial-temporal data refers to data that is described based on location and time14,15. For example, mobile phones with GPS capabilities produce spatial-temporal data about the position of the phone at several moments during the day. Several studies describe spatial-temporal data in an extensive way16,17.

The spatial dimension of spatial-temporal data refers to the location of the spatial-temporal object being observed. Important considerations about the spatial dimension are (i) whether the spatial-temporal object moves, (ii) the physical dimensions of the spatial-temporal object, and (iii) what physical dimension is the spatial-temporal object in. These considerations impact the type of the spatial-temporal data.

The temporal dimension of spatial-temporal data refers to the time at which observations of the spatial-temporal object happen. An important consideration about the temporal dimension is its granularity17. Granularity is specified based on a point in the time axis and a partitioning length18. Some observations can happen every minute, in the case of the trajectories of cars, or every day, in the case of the trajectories of planets.

In general, data analysis refers to the task of using techniques on data to uncover patterns and discover value19. Using these techniques on spatial-temporal data characterizes spatial-temporal data analysis. There are many different spatial-temporal data analysis techniques. One way to arrange them is to separates them into two types based on their primary goal20: the clustering and the classification types. We describe here the classification type. Because the clustering type relates to this study more directly, we explain it in a separate section. Two common spatial-temporal data analysis techniques of the classification type are Markov random field and support vector machine21. Markov random field (MRVs)22 are probabilistic graphical models used to represent dependencies between variables in a structured way. MRVs are represented as undirected graphs where nodes correspond to random variables associated with spatial locations or points in time and edges represent dependencies or interactions between neighboring nodes, capturing spatial proximity or temporal adjacency. MRVs require the set of variables observed to follow the Markov property, that is, future states depend only on the present and not on the past, which may not true for spatial-temporal data in many practical situations. Classification happens with the calculation of probabilities at nodes of interest. One example is the analysis of precipitation at different regions. Support vector machine (SVM)23 is a technique that attempts to create a hyperplane that divides data into a predefined number of classes. In classification tasks, spatial-temporal data (e.g. terrain attributes) is represented in a multidimensional space and SVM separates the space using one (binary classification) or many (multi-class classification) hyperplanes. New spatial-temporal data points are then compared with the space divided by the hyperplanes for classification. One study24 creates a new classifier based on SVM and applies it to the trajectory of tourists.

Although we are describing methods related to the classification type, the main use of spatial-temporal data analysis techniques in our framework involves the identification of clusters. For this reason, none of the classification methods described previously are used in our approach. As stated, spatial-temporal data analysis techniques of the clustering type are explained in the next section.

There are different types of clustering techniques, including centroid-based28, density-based29,30, distribution-based31,32, and hierarchical clustering33. There is a plethora of clustering techniques, but one, Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN)34, is further described here because of its relevance for this study.

Cluster relationships

Spatial-temporal clusters are clusters of spatial-temporal objects. They exist for some period of time and move during existence. During its movement, a spatial-temporal cluster may enter another spatial-temporal cluster, resulting in the end of the first spatial-temporal cluster. Moreover, two spatial-temporal clusters moving in space may meet and merge, creating a third larger spatial-temporal cluster. Situations like the ones presented show that spatial-temporal clusters have relationships with each other. These spatial-temporal cluster relationships are patterns in movement that can be identified, analyzed, and processed to reach useful conclusions.

In general, spatial-temporal relationships refer to relationships that objects have with respect to both their location in space and the moment they exist in time35,36. One study37 identifies seven spatial-temporal relationships: appears, disappears, survives, splits, merges, shrinks, and expands. These spatial-temporal relationships describe patterns on moving object data. Another study38 expands this list of spatial-temporal relationships, identifying eight relationships: appear, disappear, update, exit, join, expire, merge, split. They are described in Table 1. Cluster evolution

Clusters, in general, do not evolve in time, that is, cluster properties such as the number of participants or domain-specific properties such as the average height for a cluster of people stay the same once the clustering technique finishes. A novel perspective considers clusters having some properties changed with time, which characterizes an evolving cluster. Therefore, cluster evolution refers to the idea of clusters having their properties changing with time39. For example, wild animals may group forming clusters and, since animals enter and leave the cluster, this cluster has its properties changed with time and is said to be an evolving cluster. In the case of spatial-temporal clusters, the location of the cluster may change with time, as well as domain-specific properties.

A methodology for the analysis of cluster evolution found in the literature is based on five steps39: clustering per time period; between-period similarity identification; forming cluster trajectories; detection of prominent migration patterns; and detailed migration pattern detection. The same model was followed by other researchers in different domains, such as the academic and the software engineering domains40,41. Here, we review each step briefly and discuss the differences between the methodology and the proposed framework.

The first step relates to clustering per time period. There are different ways to cluster temporal data or time-series data42. One common way is to run a clustering technique at regular time intervals. The main drawback of this approach is that clusters at different time intervals are not easily associated and additional processing is required. In the original study of the methodology39, data about corporate bonds, which are one type of security in the financial domain, are divided into five groups based on the year of measurement. The authors use a k-means clustering algorithm on data for each year to cluster bonds based on their risk. In the spatial-temporal domain, the space is described by latitude and longitude coordinates and time can be arbitrarily small (minutes instead of years, for example) or even irregular, resulting in high volume of data. Unlike the methodology, the framework proposed in this paper has mechanisms to handle a two-dimensional space and data spatial-temporal data expressed at small or even irregular intervals.

The second step relates to the between-period similarity. This is the natural result of running clustering techniques at regular time intervals. The study of the methodology39 describes two ways to compare clusters obtained at different periods, or timestamps: comparing shared objects and comparing cluster characteristics. The former way refers to comparing the participants of each cluster and defining a similarity of these clusters. One common way to define such similarity is to use a Jaccard similarity43. The Jaccard similarity is calculated as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} J(c_1,c_2) = \frac{|c_1 \cap c_2|}{|c_1 \cup c_2|} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}J(c1,c2)=|c1∩c2||c1∪c2|where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_1$$\end{document}c1 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_2$$\end{document}c2 are clusters and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\cdot |$$\end{document}|·| calculates the number of participants in a cluster. The latter way refers to comparing cluster characteristics and checking if they are similar in nature. It is common to use statistical tests such as the T-test44 or the Chi-square45,46 to compare clusters. In the spatial-temporal domain, this is the linkage between the clusters found at each timestamp. However, unlike in the finance domain, changes in the number of objects in clusters can happen rapidly, which poses additional challenges to the linkage task. The proposed framework introduces a new similarity metric based on Jaccard similarity to account for the nature of rapid change in the number of participants in a spatial-temporal cluster.

The third step relates to identifying the formed cluster trajectories. By cluster trajectories, the authors mean the path that objects, or bonds in the financial domain, generate with its movement between clusters. In the study describing the methodology39, the authors build a matrix with the movement of bonds between clusters. The authors use the Generalized Sequential Patterns (GSP) algorithm47 to identify the most common patterns in the movement of bonds and filters the bonds based on user-defined parameters. The authors seek to identify paths that indicate stability or reduced loss of value among the paths that these bonds create when moving from cluster to cluster. In the spatial-temporal domain, this world mean to capture the clusters that all spatial-temporal objects belonged to. However, the feasibility of this interpretation is challenged by the amount of data that is available for processing. In the original study of the methodology39, the paths are identified from year to year for four years. In contrast, in the spatial-temporal domain, spatial-temporal data about objects, such as cars or people, can be based on much smaller regular intervals, usually involving a period of one to a few minutes. Additionally, spatial-temporal data about objects may involve millions of vehicles or people. In our proposed approach, instead, we identify spatial-temporal clusters and observe their behavior based on the relationships that they have, such as gaining or losing objects or exchanging them with other clusters.

The fourth step relates to detecting prominent migration patterns. In the original study39, a migration is when a corporate bond moves from one cluster to another cluster based on risk assessment. The authors seek to identify migration repeated paths that characterize bond deterioration, for example. In the spatial-temporal domain, this relates identifying trends in spatial-temporal objects that leave or enter spatial-temporal clusters. The proposed framework differs from the methodology significantly. Instead of analyzing the migration pattern of spatial-temporal objects, the framework analyzes the clusters themselves and their evolution. The proposed framework thus contains ways to represent clusters and evolution even if clusters undergo major changes, such as a large amount of objects leaving or entering the clusters. In more extreme cases, new clusters emerge from a clusters or a clusters is split. The framework is capable of mapping all of these situations.

The fifth step relates to detailing the detected migration patterns. In the study describing the methodology39, the authors use well-defined risk groups for corporate bonds and create a diagram with bars and arrows indicating the movement of bonds among these risk groups. In the spatial-temporal domain, this relates to detailing the groups of spatial-temporal objects that enter or leave clusters and the trends in their movements. This is not done is the proposed framework because, as explained, the main goal is to analyze the spatial-temporal clusters themselves and their evolution. Therefore, this is another step where the framework diverges from the methodology. Alternatively, the proposed framework represents the evolution of spatial-temporal clusters using a graph, where nodes are clusters at specific timestamps and edges are relationships that these clusters have with other clusters or with objects. This graph is then analyzed, producing novel insights about the situations studied.

The methodology for cluster evolution analysis39 serves as an inspiration for the proposal of this study. The several limitations of the methodology are overcome with novel steps, algorithms, and approaches that consider the spatial and temporal dimensions to represent and analyze graph-based cluster evolution. A detailed description is given in the next section.

Spatial-temporal cluster lifetimes of all spatial-temporal clusters are processed to create a graph representation, in which it is possible to identify the evolution of a spatial-temporal cluster based on the spatial-temporal cluster relationships it has. The resulting graph has nodes representing clusters at particular timestamps and edges representing cluster relationships. The fourth step is to perform cluster evolution analysis. There are many analytical techniques that can be used on graphs for analysis, ranging from visualization tools or calculating relevant metrics, to classification. In this step, analysis methods are used to generate results. An extensive review of analysis methods is out of the scope of this article, but instead we discuss some ideas to illustrate how the framework can be used. Analysis on the variation of the size of a cluster help identify growth and decay values that, together with the location, help identify regions in a city where clusters are more likely to appear or disappear. These clusters can be traffic jam or other significant events. The order or frequency of cluster relationships allows for comparison between two different ways clusters evolved and the calculation of how similar they are. When coupled with cardinal points, analysis on the movement of clusters indicate a trend to a specific direction, which may result in improvements in some regions. Lastly, some clusters may represent points where the demand for transportation services is high or low. Thus, calculating demand and identifying these regions can be done based on analysis on cluster evolution. The result of this step is a set of analysis results that are stored in a repository represented in Fig. 4 by the entity called “Analysis Results”.

Case Studies

We describe three case studies used to evaluate the framework. The case studies are examples of graph-based cluster evolution. In each case study, the workflow of the framework is performed. In all analysis tasks performed in the case studies, data is queried at a regular time interval of one minute for case studies 1 and 2 and of five minutes for case study 3. This means that the time difference between timestamps is of one or five minutes, depending on the case study. The difference is explained by the availability of data in the dataset for case study 3, which contains spatial-temporal data described in time intervals that range from a few seconds to a few minutes. We use a similarity threshold of 0.66 for the Jaccard-based similarity metric and DBSCAN parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon = 50$$\end{document}ε=50 meters and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$min\_cluster = 3$$\end{document}min_cluster=3. This means that one cluster object is at most 50 meters from another, and that a cluster has at least 3 objects.

We use two datasets in the case studies: the Rome Taxis dataset and the T-Drive dataset. The Rome Taxis dataset48 contains spatial-temporal data about the movement of approximately 320 taxis in Rome, Italy, collected over 30 days, from February 1, 2014 to March 2, 2014. These taxis serve passengers, many of whom are tourists, in the center of Rome. The T-Drive dataset49,50 contains spatial-temporal data about the movement of 10,357 taxis in Beijing, China, collected during seven days. These taxis serve passengers in the center of Beijing.

Case study 1

This case study illustrates the identification of ever-increasing or ever-decreasing regions in graph-based cluster evolution. An ever-increasing region in the evolution of a cluster is a region where the size of the cluster either increases or remains the same. An analogous definition applies for an ever-decreasing region. This case study is effective in showing that a sudden increase or decrease in cluster size needs to be analyzed because, for example, a high number of taxis at some location can result in congestion or reflect high demand. It is important to support analyses that assess these situations for management and prevention purposes.

Case study 2

Case Study 2 analyzes cluster evolution paths by calculating their similarity. The section describes what cluster evolution path similarities are, how they can be calculated, and some analysis opportunities that can be considered. The effectiveness of this case study is shown by providing analyses that indicate how the similarity of clusters and the number of cluster evolution paths evolve over time. It is important to support analyses that take into consideration similar changes over time to be able to handle comparable situations taking place at a specific location.

Similarity is a fundamental concept in machine learning, as analysis methods compare features of a new data point under examination with features of other data points for classification or clustering tasks. In general, a similarity value is calculated based on quantifiable measurements about the nature or property of the data points. In the context of graph-based cluster evolution, sequences are derived from cluster evolution paths and their elements are compared. The length of these sequences may may differ. Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)51–53 is an algorithm to calculate the similarity between two temporal sequences of possibly different lengths. One common application of DTW is speech recognition. In graph-based cluster evolution, we use DTW to compare cluster evolution paths based on a property of the clusters at each timestamp, such as size.

Data for this case study comes from the T-Drive dataset, more specifically of cluster evolution paths that start near the Beijing Capital International Airport, around Terminals 1 and 2. To calculate the similarity value between two cluster evolution paths, two new sequences are derived containing the number of spatial-temporal objects that each cluster in the path contains and, then, FastDTW54,55, a fast implementation of the DTW algorithm with optimal linear time and memory complexity, is used to compare these two new sequences. Time is divided into 24 slots, each representing one hour, and comparison happens between cluster evolution paths within the time slots, but regardless of the day.

Some considerations are discussed. In the dataset, there are 244,737 clusters that can start cluster evolution paths. In an attempt to reduce the number of these clusters, cluster evolution paths that exist for just one timestamp and that start in a timestamp and end in the next one are filtered out of the analysis. This reduces the number of clusters that can start cluster evolution paths to 5,404. Filtering by location, that is near the Beijing airport, results in 175 locations. They are shown as yellow dots in Fig. 11 and are selected for the Case Study 2. The calculation of a similarity value between cluster evolution paths allows for several other analysis tasks. For example, given that one driver knows how to avoid traffic jams, or bad roads, or any other traffic problems in a city, the cluster evolution path, that is the sequence of spatial-temporal clusters, in which this driver participates is deemed favorable. Similar cluster evolution paths can then be found, perhaps in other cities, or be discovered. Machine learning tasks, such as classification or clustering, are enabled for cluster evolution as well as other similarity-based analysis methods.

Conclusion

When moving, spatial-temporal objects may group and stay as a group for some time forming a spatial-temporal cluster. Spatial-temporal relationships are the interactions that spatial-temporal clusters have with other clusters or spatial-temporal objects. Spatial-temporal cluster evolution refers to the development of all clusters and their interactions in space and time. The evolution of clusters can be visualized in a connected representation using a graph. Graph-based cluster evolution refers to the representation of the structure and relationships of all spatial-temporal clusters in a graph format. Representing and analyzing graph-based cluster evolution is not a trivial task because of the lack of approaches to represent and analyze clusters that move and their relationships and evolution. We solve this with a framework for graph-based cluster evolution. The framework represents spatial-temporal clusters by clustering data using DBSCAN at several timestamps and by linking them using a Jaccard-based similarity metric. The framework identifies cluster relationships based the number of objects that entered or left the cluster following rules similar to first-order logic. Clusters and relationships are transformed into a graph, generating graph-based cluster evolution. This graph is then imported in Neo4j for graph analysis and insights. Some avenues for future work are discussed in the following paragraphs.

The framework uses DBSCAN as the algorithm to find clusters in timestamps because of its ability to identify clusters based on density. However, DBSCAN has limitations that range from sensitivity to or interpretability of parameters to difficulty in identifying clusters in datasets with varying density. For this reason, other types of density-based clustering algorithms, such as ST-DBSCAN2 and TraClus3, should be supported in an attempt to compensate for deficiencies and improve the versatility of the framework.

Current analyses are limited to the spatial and temporal dimensions of data. However, additional data about clusters can be used for improved results. For example, the velocity, the number of passengers, color, weather, lane, or purpose of the trip are types of information related to the environment in which the spatial-temporal object resides and can enrich the information about the cluster, producing novel insights when analyzing graph-based cluster evolution.

Analysis on graph-based cluster evolution in the framework happens after the spatial-temporal data has been gathered and made available in a dataset. Although important phenomena are captured and identified, the decision-making process is impacted because no immediate action can be taken in a city to improve conditions if necessary. Real-time analysis of graph-based cluster evolution delivers quick results for immediate decision-making, assisting in the handling of changes to the traffic flow or in supply and demand values, which result in better spatial-temporal services. The main challenges include monitoring several vehicles in real time for data gathering the efficient use of real-time graph analysis techniques.

The current insights uncover data relationships that were once hidden. A novel approach is the prediction of such data relationships. In graph-based cluster evolution, machine learning methods for prediction can have results in the prediction of the location of spatial-temporal clusters, the relationships they might have, the flow of spatial-temporal objects in a city, and the traffic conditions based on the number of spatial-temporal objects, the start or end of events, or the weather.
