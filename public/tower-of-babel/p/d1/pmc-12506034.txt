Introduction

Antenatal depression (AD) is increasingly recognised as a critical mental health issue during pregnancy, particularly in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs). Research shows that the prevalence of AD varies significantly, with a global prevalence reported to be 20.7%1 and the pooled prevalence in Africa2 also estimated to be 26.3%. It appears that there are varied prevalence rates across various regions. The prevalence of AD in LMICs can exceed that found in high-income nations, with some studies documenting rates as high as 39% in specific regions.3 This disparity points to the need for region-specific pooled data.

The factors contributing to AD are complex and include a range of elements, including sociodemographic variables, psychosocial stressors and obstetric complications. Studies have found that a history of partner violence,4 high-risk behaviour such as substance use,5 smoking6 and low socioeconomic status7 has been associated with increased likelihood of antepartum depression. The lack of realisation and screening for mental health issues compounds the problem, as many women may fail to recognise their symptoms or associate them with typical pregnancy discomforts.8

In Ghana, regional prevalence shows a similar varying trend. For instance, a population-based cohort study9 reported a prevalence of 9.9% using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9. Another cross-sectional study which reported using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) had 13.5% prevalence. Despite the representativeness of the cohort-based study, one might argue that the use of different tools may potentially influence the reliability of these estimates. There is heterogeneity, including differences in methodology, sample size and measurement tool that may affect applicability to policy interventions.

Depressive disorder, commonly referred to as depression, is a prevalent mental health condition that can affect individuals across various demographics. It is characterised by persistent low mood and a diminished interest or pleasure in activities over extended periods. In a recent national assessment conducted by the Ghana Health Service using the WHO’s Stepwise Approach,10 it was reported that depression and other mental health issues disproportionately affect women. Antenatal depressive episode occurring during pregnancy is often triggered by various stressors such as hormonal changes, personal history of mental health issues, relationship problems or economic difficulties.

Following our preliminary search, there is no nationally representative data on AD. While this appears to be a growing mental health concern among women, single studies have shown heterogeneous results with varying risk factors. Nonetheless, there has been no systematic review to estimate overall prevalence in Ghana. With this study, we anticipate estimating a pooled national prevalence with regional differences accounted for. This would potentially guide stakeholders in implementing context-specific and sensitive strategies to address AD effectively.

The objective of this systematic review with meta-analysis is to estimate the pooled prevalence of AD and its associated risk factors among pregnant women in Ghana.

Research question(s)

What is the pooled prevalence of antenatal depression in Ghana?

What are the risk factors associated with antenatal depression in Ghana?

Methods and analysis

Eligibility criteria

Population

The review will include studies on all pregnant women, from any trimester of pregnancy up to delivery. Any depressive episode present during pregnancy, irrespective of whether onset occurred before or after conception. Women with pre-existing depression that persists into pregnancy will also be included. Studies that examine AD in women with specific health complications such as neural tube defects, intra-uterine growth retardation or early pregnancy loss will be excluded. Studies that report findings for both antepartum and postpartum depression may be considered with reason in the final review.

Condition

The condition of interest is AD, which will be defined as depression occurring during pregnancy. Studies will be included if AD is diagnosed or screened using standardised clinical diagnostic criteria, such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) or by validated screening tools like the EPDS. A screening tool will be considered validated if it has undergone formal psychometric evaluation in any population and is recognised in the literature as an established measure for depression.

Context

The context for this review is studies conducted in Ghana. The review will consider studies conducted in both urban and rural areas in Ghana, as well as studies that encompass a range of settings including facility and community-based. No age, date restrictions will be applied. Only evidence published in English will be included.

Type of evidence sources

Studies will include both peer-reviewed and non-peer-reviewed articles such as dissertations from academic institutional repositories in Ghana. Also, grey literature from the mental health authority, Ghana Health Service and ministry of health websites will be assessed for inclusion. This review will include only observational studies, including cross-sectional studies and cohort studies. If multiple studies report on the same cohort, we will only include the study with the most complete data for our outcome of interest, the largest sample size and most detailed methodology. Other observational studies with alternative designs, such as case–control studies, that may provide useful prevalence or risk factor information for AD will also be considered for inclusion. This review will exclude interventional studies, case reports and any other study design that does not report evidence on prevalence and associated risk factors.

Search strategy

A preliminary search has been conducted on the PUBMED database which revealed a number of studies that may be eligible for the review. These studies will be assessed for key words that will inform the search strategy for the review. The search strategy (online supplemental appendix 1) combined keywords in the review topic. This search strategy will be refined using MeSH terms, relevant truncations, limits and Boolean operators to retrieve all eligible studies for the full search which will be developed in line with the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies11 checklist. All relevant studies published up to date will be considered.

During the time of the search, there was no published or ongoing review on the topic following a preliminary search conducted on 29 January 2025 in PROSPERO, Open Science Framework and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence Synthesis. As recommended by JBI,12 the three-step search technique will be employed. The first step has been conducted involving the preliminary search relevant to the topic.

The next stage, which will involve screening the title and abstract of papers following a full search. This search will be conducted across the following databases: PubMed, SCOPUS, African Index Medicus (AIM), Web of Science (WoS), Science Direct and Google Scholar. Also, the African Journal Online (AJOL) will be handsearched for studies not indexed in selected databases.

The final phase will involve searching the reference list of included studies. Additionally, a targeted grey literature search will be conducted to identify non-indexed literature. This will include the repository of the following tertiary institutions; University of Cape Coast, University of Ghana, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology and University for Development Studies. Also, the Ghana Health Service, Mental Health Authority and Ministry of Health websites will be handsearched for potential studies using search terms. This will also employ a somewhat structured approach for grey literature recommended for systematic reviews.13 The final included studies will be stored in a reference management software where duplicates will be deleted.

Source of evidence selection

This review will include studies published up until February 2025. The estimated time span for the review will be between February 2025 and December 2025. Literature that meets the eligibility criteria will be selected and screened for possible exclusions. Studies from bibliographic databases will be imported into EndNote V.20.5 (Bld 16860) to remove potential duplicates. It will then be exported to screening software Rayyan,14 where further duplicates will be excluded. If the included study is inaccessible, the corresponding author will be contacted via email for full text over a period of 4 weeks consisting of 3 consecutive follow-ups. If the study is still not accessible, it will be excluded and reported in the final review. All available included studies will be followed by a title and abstract screening.

Following our established eligibility criteria, two reviewers will screen independently, allowing a third reviewer to resolve disagreement. This will be immediately followed by a further analysis of full text for final inclusion in the review. Reasons for exclusion at this stage will be documented. Having completed this step, a citation analysis15 will be conducted to identify further relevant studies for review. Disagreement will be resolved through discussion with the third reviewer.

Eligible grey literature will also be reported as part of studies identified via other methods and reported in the extended Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram (online supplemental appendix 2).

Patient and public involvement

We did not involve patients or members of the public in the design or conduct of this systematic review. However, the research question was informed by the public health relevance of AD in Ghana, and the findings will be disseminated to stakeholders, including policymakers and healthcare providers, to support improved maternal mental healthcare.

Data extraction

Extraction of data will be conducted by two independent reviewers using the extraction table that will be developed by the review team. Data will be stored securely in Excel. Consensus will be built on areas of disagreement using a third reviewer. The following data will be extracted: study information (authors, publication year, study design, setting/area), population (sample size, age range, trimester distribution), participant demographics (age, gestational age, parity, marital status, education level, employment status, income, social support), condition (assessment tool, prevalence, CI), risk factors (OR with their 95% CI, p values), study quality, method of assessment (tool vs interview).

Data synthesis

Two main approaches will be used: quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) and qualitative synthesis, depending on data homogeneity. For quantitative synthesis, if the studies are sufficiently homogeneous regarding population characteristics, diagnostic criteria and risk factors, a meta-analysis will be performed. Data will undergo analysis using StataMP V.17 (64-bit) statistical software.

The first step will involve assessing the homogeneity of the studies based on design, population characteristics, measurement methods and reported prevalence rates. The primary outcome will be the pooled prevalence of AD, with estimates expressed as percentages and accompanied by 95% CIs. Where multiple time points are reported in cohort studies, we will prioritise the estimate closest to delivery, as depressive symptoms are most likely to peak in late pregnancy.

For studies reporting risk factors, a meta-analysis will synthesise the strength of associations, that is, the OR, across variables. A random-effects model will be used based on the heterogeneity, and forest plots will be generated to visually represent the pooled estimates. The I² statistic will also be used to assess heterogeneity. If heterogeneity is high, a subgroup analysis will be conducted based on the study design (cross-sectional vs cohort) setting (rural vs urban, facility based vs community based), diagnostic method (DSM-5, EPDS) or sociodemographic variables.

In cases where meta-analysis is not feasible due to high heterogeneity or the nature of the data, a qualitative synthesis will summarise the key findings from the studies. A sensitivity analysis will be conducted by excluding studies with a high risk of bias or low quality, to allow for reassessment of the pooled prevalence and associations. If sufficient studies are available for meta-analysis, publication bias will be evaluated through funnel plots and Egger’s test.

Finally, the synthesis will integrate findings into a coherent narrative, by presenting the overall pooled prevalence of AD in Ghana, the most significant risk factors, an assessment of the quality of evidence and study limitations.

Quality appraisal

The quality of the included studies will be assessed using an adapted version of the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies.16 Bias will be evaluated at the study level across key domains including selection, measurement and reporting. The overall quality rating will categorise studies as either good quality (most criteria met), fair quality (some criteria met) or poor quality (few criteria met). Study quality will not be used to weight studies statistically in the primary meta-analysis. Instead, the potential influence of study quality on pooled estimates will be explored through sensitivity analyses in which studies judged to be at high risk of bias will be excluded.

Strengths and limitations

This review relied on the use of multiple databases and grey literature sources, thereby enhancing the likelihood of capturing relevant studies and minimises publication bias. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for selecting studies were clearly defined, focusing on observational studies that provide prevalence and risk factor information for AD.

A possible limitation would be the variability in study designs and diagnostic criteria used to assess AD which may account for significant heterogeneity in the meta-analysis results. While plans for subgroup analysis are in place, the potential for significant variance cannot be overlooked.

Ethics and dissemination

Given that this study involves no primary data collection, an ethical review is not necessary. The findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal, presentations at academic conferences and key national stakeholder events. Data will be extracted using a standardised form, checked independently for accuracy, and managed in secure, well-organised files to ensure transparency and reproducibility, and will be made available in supplementary materials.
