Embolization of a pediatric choroid plexus papilloma as a stand-alone treatment strategy: illustrative case
Authors: Lucas P Mitre, Ishav Y Shukla, Bradley Weprin, Rafael De Oliveira Sillero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Choroid plexus papilloma (CPP) is a benign WHO grade 1 intraventricular tumor arising from the choroid plexus epithelium, accounting for 0.4%–0.6% of all brain tumors and 2%–4% of pediatric CNS tumors.1–3 It most commonly presents in children younger than 5 years, usually in the lateral ventricles, with hydrocephalus and increased intracranial pressure as the typical presenting features.1–3 Resection is the definitive treatment and is generally curative when gross-total resection is achieved, with reported 5-year survival approaching 100% and no need for adjuvant therapy after complete resection.2–5

Because CPPs may be highly vascular, preoperative embolization has been used as an adjunct to surgery in selected cases to reduce perioperative blood loss, decrease CSF production, and facilitate resection, particularly in infants with large tumors supplied by the anterior or posterior choroidal artery.1,6–8 However, embolization has generally been described as a preoperative adjunct rather than a definitive treatment strategy, and stand-alone embolization is not typically performed.6,9

We report a case of CPP successfully treated with stand-alone embolization, illustrating a rare treatment strategy in a tumor classically managed with resection.

Illustrative Case

Initial Presentation

A 14-month-old boy was referred for neurosurgical evaluation after neuroimaging obtained for macrocephaly and developmental delay identified an intracranial lesion. His medical history was notable for gross motor delay, congenital right-sided ptosis previously evaluated by pediatric ophthalmology and considered familial without associated pathological findings, and reflux in early infancy that resolved with acid-suppressive therapy. He was born at term without perinatal complications. There was no history of seizures, focal neurological deficits, failure to thrive, major febrile illness, or trauma.

Developmentally, he had delayed rolling until approximately 11 months of age, followed shortly by crawling. At the time of initial neurosurgical evaluation, he had recently begun pulling to stand, was taking early steps, and had started physical therapy. His parents denied seizures, abnormal eye movements, opisthotonus, spell-like events, feeding difficulty, or other subjective focal neurological deficits.

At initial neurosurgical evaluation, approximately 3 weeks before embolization, he was well appearing, interactive, smiling, and able to sit upright, reaching for objects, and giving a high five symmetrically. Head circumference was approximately 48–48.2 cm. The anterior fontanelle was sunken and cranial sutures were well apposed. Despite the reported history of macrocephaly, he was not felt to be macrocephalic on examination, and head shape was normal. Neurological examination was notable for congenital right-sided ptosis. Pupils were equal and reactive, gaze was conjugate, extraocular movements were full without nystagmus, facial movement was symmetric, and he moved all extremities spontaneously with normal bulk, tone, and apparent strength.

Neuroimaging and Preoperative Evaluation

Initial brain MRI examination with and without contrast demonstrated a 2.6-cm lobulated, avidly enhancing intraventricular mass centered in the temporal horn of the left lateral ventricle with extension into the atrium (Fig. 1). There was no definite restricted diffusion. Mild enlargement of the lateral and third ventricles was present without severe hydrocephalus. There was no significant mass effect, no additional parenchymal lesion, and no abnormal leptomeningeal enhancement to suggest disseminated disease. The corpus callosum was normally formed. Overall, the imaging appearance was considered most consistent with a CPP.

Initial axial T2-weighted (left) and T1-weighted postcontrast (right) MR images showing an enhancing tumor in the left temporal horn.

Diagnostic cerebral angiography performed with theraupetic intent demonstrated a hypervascular lobulated tumor blush. Selective angiography of the left internal carotid artery showed an enlarged left anterior choroidal artery (AChA) supplying the lesion, and tumor vascularity was confirmed on superselective AChA injection (Fig. 2). Selective left vertebral angiography showed no definite posterior circulation contribution to tumor supply. Overall, the lesion appeared to be supplied predominantly, and likely exclusively, by the left AChA.

Lateral (A) and anteroposterior (B) views of selective internal carotid injection cerebral arteriograms. Lateral (C) and anteroposterior (D) dedicated views of superselective AChA injection cerebral arteriograms showing a hypervascular tumor blush.

Endovascular Procedure

The patient underwent preoperative endovascular embolization under general endotracheal anesthesia. Using ultrasound-guided right common femoral artery access, we performed selective catheterization of the left internal carotid and left vertebral arteries. A 4-Fr sheath, 1.3-Fr microcatheter (Headway Duo, Terumo Neuro), and microwire were used for the intervention. After superselective catheterization of the left AChA, Onyx-18 was injected under fluoroscopic guidance for tumor devascularization. Postembolization angiography demonstrated no residual tumor blush, consistent with complete angiographic devascularization.

Postprocedural Course and Follow-Up

Following embolization, the patient was admitted to the ICU. During the admission, he developed cough, congestion, and intermittent fever, and respiratory viral testing was positive for rhinovirus/enterovirus. In this setting, a planned postembolization sedated MRI and subsequent resection were deferred because of concern for anesthesia-related respiratory complications. A nonsedated rapid MRI obtained 1 day following the endovascular procedure demonstrated a stable similar appearance of the known lesion, with decreased signal intensity, and no evidence of complications. On postoperative day 2, the patient was subsequently transferred from the ICU to the neurosurgical floor and discharged home.

Serial follow-up MRI demonstrated progressive treatment response without evidence of dissemination or new complication. MRI at 1 month postembolization showed interval decrease in lesion size to 2.4 × 1.4 × 1.2 cm, marked reduction in intralesional enhancement with only thin irregular peripheral enhancement remaining, increased central apparent diffusion coefficient, and decreased perfusion relative to adjacent choroid plexus (Fig. 3A and C). MRI at 4 months of follow-up demonstrated a further decrease in tumor size to approximately 1.8 × 0.9 × 1.0 cm, with significantly regressed peripheral enhancement and susceptibility artifact related to embolic material (Fig. 3B and D). MRI at 6 months showed continued decrease in size of the residual lesion, measuring approximately 1.3 × 1.0 cm in axial dimensions. Subsequent MRI studies at the 10- and 14-month follow-ups demonstrated stable residual treated tumor within the temporal horn of the left lateral ventricle, with slightly diminished enhancement on the most recent study (Fig. 3E and F). Mild persistent prominence of the left temporal horn and atrium and adjacent treatment-related periventricular change remained stable. Throughout follow-up, there was no restricted diffusion, hemorrhage, mass effect, or abnormal leptomeningeal enhancement, and the patient remained neurologically stable.

Discussion

Choroid Plexus Supply

Understanding the vascular anatomy of choroid plexus tumors is central to safe embolization planning. In lateral ventricular CPPs, which represents the most common location for these tumors, arterial supply is typically derived from both the AChA and the lateral posterior choroidal artery (LPChA) (Fig. 4). The AChA often divides into medial and lateral trunks, whereas the LPChA demonstrates variable anatomy and principally supplies the glomus and posterior choroid plexus. Anastomoses between these systems are well described, underscoring the potential complexity of tumor vascularization.10 A key landmark is the plexal point, where the AChA enters the choroidal fissure; proximal to this point, the cisternal segment gives off perforators to eloquent structures including the posterior limb of the internal capsule, optic tract, and cerebral peduncle, whereas the distal plexal segment supplies the choroid plexus itself.11,12 CPPs characteristically recruit enlarged choroidal feeders and show intense, persistent tumor blush on angiography.7,13 In the present case, angiography demonstrated a hypervascular lesion in the atrium/temporal horn of the left lateral ventricle supplied predominantly, and likely exclusively, by an enlarged left AChA, without definite posterior circulation contribution. This pattern is consistent with the known vascular anatomy of lateral ventricular choroid plexus tumors and likely contributed to the technical feasibility of superselective embolization through a single dominant pedicle.7,10,13

Indications for Embolization

Current guidance from the American Heart Association scientific statement and Society of Neurointerventional Surgery (SNIS) supports preoperative tumor embolization when it may reduce blood loss, shorten operative time, control surgically inaccessible feeders, improve resection conditions, prevent hemorrhage in unresectable lesions, or serve a palliative role.9,14 In CPPs, the best candidates appear to be large hypervascular tumors in very young children, especially infants, in whom limited estimated blood volume makes even moderate intraoperative hemorrhage potentially life-threatening.1,6 In fact, there is only one additional case report in the literature, from Wind et al., where a CPP was managed with stand-alone embolization.15 The authors reported on a 3-month-old patient in whom embolization was utilized as a strategy to allow for a more feasible operative setting and ended up with an outstanding tumoral involution.15

In the largest comparative series, embolized patients had lower estimated blood volume loss and a markedly higher rate of gross-total resection at the first operation than nonembolized patients.1,6 Other reasonable indications include tumors in which an initial surgical attempt was limited by bleeding and lesions supplied by deep choroidal arteries that may be difficult to secure early during resection.9,13,16 It is important to note that no high-quality comparative evidence exists to guide the decision on the extent of the embolization of deeper feeders combined with resection. Haliasos et al. proceeded with superselective embolization, representing the largest comparative series on CPPs.17 Yamao et al. reported on a combined approach for large hypervascular lateral ventricular tumors, with LPChA feeder embolization followed by microscopical AChA feeder occlusion.18 These reports allow some degree of inference on the technical necessity of hybrid approaches in cases of unfavorable AChA anatomy in standard-behvaing CPP postembolization procedures.

Observations

An additional consideration unique to CPP is the effect of embolization on CSF physiology. Haliasos et al. reported reduced postoperative CSF production and shorter duration of external ventricular drainage in embolized patients, which may be particularly relevant in tumors associated with hydrocephalus from CSF overproduction.17 In the present case, embolization was initially planned as an adjunct before resection because of the expected vascularity of the lesion and the anticipated consequences of blood loss in a toddler. Although surgery was deferred, the case still fits the profile of a lesion for which embolization would be expected to provide perioperative benefit based on published indications.1,6,9,15,17

Embolization Technique and the Choice of Embolic Agent

The technical cornerstone of CPP embolization is superselective catheterization of the feeding choroidal artery, with particular care during AChA embolization to position the microcatheter beyond the plexal point before injection. This principle is critical to avoid reflux or inadvertent embolization of cisternal perforators supplying eloquent brain structures.8,19 In the current case, embolization was performed through the left AChA with a microcatheter system that achieved selective access and complete angiographic devascularization, without immediate ischemic or hemorrhagic complication. With respect to embolic agent selection, the literature favors liquid embolics over particulate agents in CPP embolization. N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA) has the longest published track record and provides immediate, permanent occlusion; in the largest CPP series, NBCA was used with high technical success.6,20 Wang et al. specifically favored NBCA over particles because of concern for procedure-related bleeding and delayed tumor hemorrhage.20 Ethylene-vinyl alcohol copolymer–based agents such as Onyx have also been used successfully in this setting and offer the practical advantage of slower, more controlled injection with the ability to pause and resume, potentially allowing more deliberate penetration of the tumor vascular bed.8,16 More recently, squid has also been reported as a successful liquid embolic option for a giant choroid plexus carcinoma via the AChA.19 By contrast, particulate agents such as polyvinyl alcohol or embospheres have been associated with a higher risk of incomplete occlusion and delayed intratumoral hemorrhage.14,20 In the study by Wang et al., one patient experienced intraprocedural bleeding and another developed delayed hemorrhage 4 hours after particle embolization with resultant hemiparesis.20 For that reason, the use of Onyx-18 in this case is supported by the available literature favoring permanent liquid embolic agents for choroid plexus tumors,8,14,16,20 with a lower risk of catheter retention.

Timing of Embolization

The ideal interval between embolization and surgery remains unsettled. SNIS guidelines, drawing largely from broader tumor embolization experience, state that resection is generally performed 1–8 days after embolization, with very early surgery potentially limiting the full effects of devascularization and later surgery carrying some risk of recanalization.14,21 Histopathological data from the meningioma literature suggest that thrombus formation and inflammatory change occur within the 1st week, with maximal tumor softening around 7–9 days and recanalization seen in approximately 30% of embolized vessels thereafter.21 For CPPs specifically, however, several authors have advocated same-session embolization and surgery, particularly in infants and particularly when permanent liquid embolic agents are used, to minimize the risk of interval hemorrhage.20 Conversely, staged management has also been described. Mangham et al. reported delayed resection after embolization in a 3-month-old with a third ventricular CPP, allowing interval tumor involution before surgery.1 Thus, the available literature supports either immediate or delayed surgery, with the decision influenced by agent choice, clinical stability, and logistical considerations.1,15,20,21 A practical way to frame the timing question is whether the embolization goal is near-total upfront devascularization or staged risk reduction before resection. Lessons from the pediatric arteriovenous malformation (AVM) literature suggest that a more aggressive upfront strategy is reasonable when the vascular anatomy is simple (as described in the Indications for Embolization section), the lesion is compact, there are one or a few dominant catheter-accessible feeders, and a high degree of devascularization can be achieved without jeopardizing eloquent branches.22 By contrast, a staged approach is better suited to lesions with multifocal or bilateral supply, high-flow shunting, difficult access through an eloquent arterial territory, or concern for postembolization swelling or hemorrhage. Extrapolated to pediatric tumors, the same principle likely applies most clearly to hypervascular lesions with discrete pedicles, including other choroid plexus tumors, hemangioblastomas, and selected intraventricular meningiomas, whereas infiltrative or diffusely supplied tumors are less amenable to aggressive embolization as an upfront strategy. Our case adds an unusual perspective to this discussion. Surgery was postponed because of an acute rhinovirus/enterovirus infection after embolization, yet serial MRI demonstrated progressive reduction in size and enhancement of the lesion with durable radiographic control through follow-up. While embolization is generally described as an adjunct rather than definitive treatment for CPP,6,23 this case suggests that in selected circumstances, complete angiographic devascularization with a permanent liquid embolic may be followed by sustained involution when resection is deferred. Still, this should be interpreted cautiously, as the standard treatment for CPP remains excision.

Risks and Complications

The major concern during CPP embolization is ischemic injury, particularly AChA territory infarction. Because the cisternal AChA supplies the posterior limb of the internal capsule, optic tract, lateral geniculate body, and cerebral peduncle, embolization proximal to the plexal point can produce devastating deficits including contralateral hemiplegia, hemisensory loss, and hemianopia.11,24 The importance of distal catheterization is reinforced by choroidal artery embolization experience in AVMs, where infarctions are common on MRI and symptomatic events are associated with embolization from the cisternal segment.24 Other recognized risks include intratumoral hemorrhage, especially with particulate agents; vessel perforation or dissection during navigation of small, tortuous choroidal arteries in infants; postembolization tumor swelling with possible worsening hydrocephalus or herniation; failed embolization due to the inability to achieve safe distal catheterization; and errant embolization through collateral pathways causing stroke or cranial nerve injury.6,9,14,20,25 Despite these concerns, CPP-specific series suggest an overall favorable safety profile when embolization is carefully selected and technically successful, with no procedure-related mortality reported in the largest series.6 In the present case, embolization through the AChA was completed without immediate thromboembolic, hemorrhagic, or neurological complication, and follow-up imaging showed no diffusion restriction, hemorrhage, or radiographic evidence of infarction. This favorable course highlights that, although technically demanding and anatomically unforgiving, AChA embolization can be performed safely when meticulous attention is paid to superselective positioning and embolic control.8,19

Lessons

Resection remains the standard treatment for CPP. In this rare case, however, embolization alone achieved complete angiographic devascularization and was followed by sustained radiographic response and clinical stability during follow-up. Stand-alone embolization may therefore represent a feasible option in highly selected patients when resection is deferred or not immediately possible, although careful long-term surveillance remains essential.
