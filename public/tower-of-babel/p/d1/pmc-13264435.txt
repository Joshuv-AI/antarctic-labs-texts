Clinical and molecular features associated with the presence of brain metastases and survival in patients with advanced thyroid cancer
Authors: Carly C Barron, Thais Baccili Cury Megid, Yangqing Deng, Vijithan Sugumar, Sara Ladak, Aruz Mesci, Michael Yan, Jelena Lukovic, Monika K Krzyzanowska, Anna M Sawka, Ozgur Mete, Lucy X Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain metastases in advanced thyroid cancer may be underrecognized due to lack of routine brain imaging. We characterized the clinical and molecular features, risk factors, and survival outcomes of patients with non-anaplastic follicular cell-derived thyroid carcinoma (FCDTC) with brain metastases.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients with advanced non-anaplastic FCDTC treated in the Medical Oncology Department at Princess Margaret Cancer Centre between 2007 and 2022. Clinicopathologic and treatment data were extracted from electronic records, and all tumors underwent targeted next-generation sequencing. Risk factors for brain metastasis were assessed using logistic regression, and overall survival was analyzed with Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsOf 187 patients with advanced non-anaplastic FCDTC, 32 (17%) were diagnosed with brain metastases. Dedicated brain imaging was performed in 56% of patients, and most metastases (78%) were asymptomatic and incidentally detected. Lung metastases were associated with higher risk of developing brain metastases (aOR 8.27, 95% CI 1.87-36.46). ARID2 mutations were enriched in patients with brain metastases (9% vs 1%, P = .02). Survival did not differ between patients with and without brain metastases. Patients with brain metastases who had an ECOG performance status ≥2 (aHR 6.30, 95% CI 1.61-24.64) or ≥4 brain metastases (aHR 4.92, 95% CI 1.45-16.64) had worse survival. Molecular alterations were not predictive of survival.ConclusionBrain metastases occurred more frequently than previously reported. Lung metastases were associated with higher risk of developing brain metastases, and performance status and lesion number were associated with survival. These findings support consideration of brain metastasis screening in advanced thyroid cancer and may inform future prognostic and treatment strategies.Keywords: thyroid neoplasms, brain neoplasms, mutation, prognosis, retrospective study

Brain metastases are traditionally considered rare in advanced non-anaplastic follicular cell-derived thyroid carcinoma; however, their true incidence may be underestimated due to the lack of routine screening. In this cohort, 17% of all patients and 30% of those who underwent dedicated brain imaging had brain metastases. These findings support consideration of brain imaging in advanced disease. The integration of clinical and molecular data from this study provides a foundation for developing more refined prognostic models and therapeutic strategies. Larger, genomically informed studies are needed to guide more personalized management for advanced thyroid cancer with brain metastases.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer is the seventh most common cancer globally, with an estimated 821 214 new cases diagnosed in 2022.1 Follicular cell-derived thyroid cancer (FCDTC) includes papillary, follicular, oncocytic, poorly differentiated, and anaplastic subtypes. Surgery remains the primary treatment for most well-differentiated thyroid cancers, and distant metastases are uncommon, occurring in fewer than 10% of patients.2 The lungs and bones are the most frequent sites of metastasis, found in approximately 70% and 20%-30% of patients with metastatic disease, respectively.3–7 The development of brain metastases is rare, ranging from <1% to 3% in well-differentiated thyroid cancer,3–5,8 with higher rates seen in poorly differentiated disease (up to 4.1%).9–11 Patients with thyroid cancer and brain metastases have been reported to have poor outcomes, with median survival ranging from 8.8 to 19 months following diagnosis, depending on the series.8,12–14

Predictive models for brain metastases based on clinical and pathologic factors have been developed for cancers such as lung, breast, and melanoma.15–18 Molecular alterations, such as HER2 positivity in breast cancer or EGFR mutations in lung cancer, have been used to identify patients at higher risk of developing brain metastases.19,20 These findings have influenced screening practices for high-risk patients to attempt to detect brain metastases earlier and genomic data has also been used to inform treatment paradigms in these disease sites.21

In contrast to advances in other cancers, less is known about the risk factors for developing brain metastases in advanced non-anaplastic FCDTC, and no formal guidelines exist to direct brain imaging screening. Although computed tomography (CT) of the head and neck is included in routine restaging, it visualizes only the skull base and lower intracranial regions, and dedicated brain imaging with CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is not routinely obtained. Moreover, there is a paucity of studies including patients with thyroid cancer with brain metastases that incorporate genomic data, which could potentially predict the development of brain metastases and associated survival. Unlike prior studies, all patients in our cohort underwent comprehensive next-generation sequencing. To address some of these gaps, we conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study to characterize the clinical and molecular features of patients with non-anaplastic FCDTC with brain metastases, identify potential risk factors, and evaluate survival outcomes.

Methods

Patient cohort

This was a single-center retrospective analysis that included all patients with advanced (defined as unresectable locally advanced or distant metastatic disease) non-anaplastic FCDTC seen by medical oncology for consideration of systemic therapy at the Princess Margaret Cancer Center from 2007 to 2022. A diagnosis of non-anaplastic FCDTC was confirmed by pathological examination. Brain metastases were diagnosed by CT or MRI imaging. Electronic medical records were reviewed to collect clinicopathologic and treatment data. Clinical variables included age, sex, histology, performance status as assessed by Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status,22 and sites of distant metastases as per AJCC 8th edition.23 The management of brain metastases was at the discretion of the treating physicians. Treatment variables included systemic therapy, surgery, and radiation for brain metastases. Variables were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature. This study was approved by the University Health Network (UHN) Research Ethics Board (ID# 19-5888).

Next-generation sequencing

All patients had tumor next-generation sequencing (NGS) by a targeted gene panel as part of the Integrated Molecular Profiling in Advanced Cancers Trial (IMPACT; NCT01505400) or Ontario-wide Cancer TArgeted Nucleic Acid Evaluation (OCTANE; NCT02906943) clinical trials which have previously been described24,25 or the Access to Genetic Advanced Testing (AGATE) program which was an institutional NGS initiative for patients with advanced solid tumor cancers. The details of these panels are described in the Supplementary Data File 1. Although gene content varied across platforms, all assays consistently captured key thyroid cancer driver genes, including BRAF, RAS (NRAS, HRAS, KRAS), RET, and PI3K pathway alterations (PIK3CA, PTEN). Informed consent for participation was obtained and NGS was performed on archival tumor samples from previously collected biopsies or surgical specimens.

Statistical analysis

Cohort demographics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Comparisons were made between patients with and without brain metastases using the Wilcoxon rank sum, χ2 or Fisher exact test. Risk factors for the development of brain metastasis were identified using univariable and multivariable logistic regression analysis. Overall survival (OS) from initial medical oncology consultation was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method log-rank test. Survival was measured from initial medical oncology consultation to account for differing lengths of time between diagnosis and development of advanced disease as the natural history of thyroid cancer is variable. Survival from brain metastases was measured from the date of diagnosis on imaging to death from any cause or last follow-up and univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate predictors of survival. Age was dichotomized into <60 and ≥60 years based on previous prognostic indices in the brain metastases literature.26 Odds ratios (OR), adjusted odds ratios (aOR), hazard ratios (HR), adjusted hazard ratios (aHR), and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported where applicable. All statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.4.1) by a biostatistician, and a P value less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Overall cohort

A total of 187 patients with advanced non-anaplastic FCDTC were included (Figure S1, see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). The clinicopathological factors of patients with advanced non-anaplastic FCDTC with and without brain metastasis are reported in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis of thyroid cancer of the full sample was 56.2 years and 55% were male. The majority of patients (81%) had a differentiated thyroid carcinoma and 19% had poorly-differentiated disease. One-hundred seventy-eight (95%) patients had prior radioactive iodine treatment and 81% had evidence of distant metastases. The median follow-up time, estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method, was 27.8 months (95% CI 18.4–117). At the time of last follow-up, 107 patients (57%) had been treated with systemic therapy. At last follow-up, 105 (56%) patients had dedicated brain imaging in the form of CT or MRI brain at any time. Of these, 32 (30%) patients were found to have brain metastases.

Patients with brain metastases

Of the 32 patients with brain metastases, the median age at diagnosis of non-anaplastic FCDTC in patients with brain metastases was 58.2 years and 53% were male (Table 1). The median interval from initial thyroid cancer diagnosis to detection of brain metastases was 7.1 years (IQR 4.9-12.9 years). Tumor histologies were differentiated thyroid carcinoma in 27 patients and poorly differentiated in 5 patients.

The presentation and management of patients with brain metastases can be seen in Table 2. Most patients had a good performance status at the time of brain metastases diagnosis with 26 (81%) with an ECOG Performance Status of 0-1. Seven (22%) patients had symptoms associated with brain metastases which included neurological deficits (n = 3), headaches (n = 2), dizziness (n = 1), seizures (n = 1), confusion (n = 1) and ataxia (n = 1). The remainder of patients were asymptomatic and brain metastases were discovered incidentally on restaging CT head and neck (n = 12), MRI (n = 5) or CT brain (n = 2) as part of staging, or MRI spine (n = 1). The mean number of brain metastases was 3 with a range of 1-10.

Five (16%) patients underwent resection and 27 (84%) patients received radiation. Sixteen patients received stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), 9 received whole brain radiotherapy (WBRT) and 2 received both at different time points. At the time of brain metastases diagnosis, most patients (75%) were not receiving systemic therapy (Table S1), reflecting the absence of a clinical indication for initiation of systemic therapy at that time. After development of brain metastases, 16 patients were started on a new systemic therapy of which 69% (n = 11) received lenvatinib. The most common multimodal management was radiation and initiation of new systemic therapy in 47% (n = 15) of patients, followed by surgery and radiation in 13% (n = 4 patients) (Table 2).

Molecular alterations

The frequency of molecular alterations in patients with and without brain metastases can be seen in Figure 1. Only sequence variants classified as “Oncogenic” or “Likely oncogenic” are described. Genes with alterations occurring in 5 or more patients are presented. Among patients with brain metastases, BRAF-like molecular signatures were identified in 18 (56%) patients, whereas RAS-like signatures were observed in 9 (28%). TERT promoter mutations co-occurred with BRAF p.V600E mutations in 5 patients, and with NRAS mutations in 3, with 1 patient lacking either alteration.

Molecular alterations based on NGS sequencing in patients with and without brain metastases.

The frequency of ARID2 mutations was higher in the group with brain metastases compared to those without (9% vs 1%, P = .02). These three patients had a median age of 65 years (range 57-71) and included 1 male and 2 females. All had papillary histology and prior radioactive iodine (RAI) treatment. All had metastatic involvement of lungs, 1 patient had pleural disease. Co-occurring genomic alterations included BRAF p.V600E mutations in two patients. There were no other statistical differences in the frequency of molecular alterations.

Factors predicting development of brain metastases

Univariable analysis results suggested that presence of distant disease involving the lymph nodes and lung were significant risk factors for the development of brain metastases (Table S2). ARID2 mutations were a significant predictor of brain metastases (OR 15.93, 95% CI 1.96-328.25).

Due to the limited number of patients with ARID2 mutations and the broad OR confidence interval, this was not included in multivariable analysis. Other significant factors from the univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model and adjusted for age at diagnosis and sex (Table 3). The variance inflation factors calculated were close to 1, indicating the absence of multicollinearity among the predictor variables in the regression model. Patients with distant disease to the lungs (aOR 8.27, 95% CI 1.87-36.46) had greater risk of the development of brain metastases.

Survival in patients advanced thyroid cancer with brain metastases

The median OS was 6.1 years (95% CI 3.92, NR) in patients with brain metastases and 6.6 years (95% 5.83, NR) in patients without brain metastasis (P = .07) (Figure 2). At the time of analyses, 15 of the 32 patients with brain metastases had died. The median survival after diagnosis of brain metastases was 3.7 years. The univariable analysis of factors associated with survival in patients with brain metastases can be seen in Table 4. There were no molecular alterations that were associated with survival in patients with brain metastases. Significant factors from the univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model (Table 5). Patients with brain metastases that had an ECOG performance status of 2 or greater (aHR 6.30, 95% CI 1.61-24.64) or 4 or more brain metastases (aHR 4.92, 95% CI 1.45-16.64) had worse survival.

Discussion

Brain metastases in advanced non-anaplastic FCDTC are described as rare, and the affected patient population is not well characterized. In this retrospective, single-center study, we found approximately half of the patients with advanced non-anaplastic FCDTC underwent dedicated brain imaging, and 30% of these patients were diagnosed with brain metastases, indicating a high prevalence in this population. The presence of distant disease to the lungs was associated with an increased risk of developing brain metastases. Characteristics associated with survival in patients with brain metastases included performance status and the number of lesions. While this is the first study of patients with non-anaplastic FCDTC with complete molecular data, molecular alterations were not associated with brain metastases development or survival.

The proportion of patients with brain metastases in our study is greater than previously reported.3–5,8 For example, in the previous studies by Osborne et al.7 and Gomes-Lima et al.8 the prevalence ranged from 0.3% to 0.5%. Several factors could explain this. In the study by Osborne et al.7, only 34% of patients had distant metastases at the time of original diagnosis, compared to 81% of the patients at the beginning of our cohort. In the study by Gomes-Lima et al.8, most patients had well-differentiated disease, whereas our cohort included a higher proportion of PDTC (37%), further supporting a higher-risk population. Accordingly, our cohort represents a highly selected population of patients with more advanced disease, who are inherently at increased risk for developing brain metastases. Additionally, in the study by Gomes-Lima et al.8, a greater proportion of patients had poor performance status (ECOG 3-4) at the time of brain metastases diagnosis, potentially reflecting more symptomatic intracranial disease. In contrast, most patients in our cohort were asymptomatic, with brain metastases incidentally detected on routine or restaging imaging. Taken together, prior studies likely reflect a lower-risk, symptom-driven population, whereas our cohort represents a higher-risk population with earlier and more frequent detection of asymptomatic brain metastases, which may account for the observed differences in prevalence.

Current guidelines for management of advanced thyroid cancer do not recommend routine screening imaging for brain metastases.2,27 The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines consider brain MRI in only select circumstances for melanoma and lung cancers.28,29 The prevalence of brain metastases at baseline in pivotal phase III trials for the treatment of advanced FCDTC is not consistently reported,30,31 and without routine dedicated brain imaging, the prevalence in real-world settings is also unclear. To better understand this population and develop prognostic models and treatment paradigms similar to those used for other cancer types, studies that include brain imaging in advanced non-anaplastic FCDTC are needed. Although imaging is often recommended for patients with symptoms suggestive of CNS involvement, the majority (78%) of patients in our study were asymptomatic, with most brain metastases being incidentally discovered during routine restaging with head and neck CT scans. This suggests that physicians may face challenges in accurately identifying which patients are most at risk for developing brain metastases.

To improve prediction accuracy, we analyzed a number of independent risk factors to try to identify patients at greatest risk of developing brain metastases. We found that evidence of metastases to the lungs at the time of medical oncology consultation was significantly associated with developing brain metastases. This is expected as previous work in other cancers has shown populations that develop brain metastases generally have a higher proportion of patients with lung, liver and bone metastases as well as more advanced pT and pN stages.15 The extent of metastatic disease in patients with non-anaplastic FCDTC at the time they are being considered for systemic therapy may prompt physicians to consider brain imaging. Future research focusing on this population with advanced disease could offer greater insights into thyroid cancer brain metastases.

There were no differences in overall survival between patients with and without brain metastases in our study cohort. Historically, patients with advanced thyroid cancer with brain metastases have been reported to have a poor prognosis with shorter median survival compared to metastasis in other organs.32 Possible explanations for the lack of difference include the long median time from seeing a medical oncologist to brain metastases diagnosis (7.1 years), a higher baseline risk of dying from complications of disease in the non-brain metastases group, management of brain metastases with radiation or surgery, and subsequent lines of systemic therapy.

A limited number of previous studies have explored prognostic factors associated with the survival of patients with thyroid cancer brain metastases. Consistent with our findings, factors such as an increasing number of lesions and poorer performance status have been identified as negative prognostic indicators.7,14,33,34 These factors are also incorporated into the widely used Graded Prognostic Assessment, which estimates survival for patients with brain metastases across various cancers.26 Prior retrospective studies have shown longer survival in patients with local management of their brain metastases including surgery or stereotactic radiosurgery.8,35–38 In our cohort, most patients received treatment aimed at local lesion control. However, no differences in survival were observed between patients treated with or without surgery and those receiving different radiation modalities, although our analysis was limited by the small numbers of patients.

Our cohort is the first study of patients with non-anaplastic FCDTC with brain metastases with complete molecular data, as a previous report described only a subset with genomic analysis.7 As in the previous study, BRAF p.V600E and TERT promoter mutations were the most common molecular alterations in affected patients with brain metastases. We also found a subset of patients (9%) with ARID2 mutations, a tumor suppressor encoding a component of the PBAF chromatin-remodeling complex of the SWI/SNF family. Somatic mutations in ARID2 have been reported in solid tumors including melanoma,39–42 hepatocellular carcinoma43,44 and non-small cell lung45 and colorectal cancer.46 ARID2 mutations have been described to be enriched in melanoma metastases, including brain metastases.39–41

Loss of ARID2 disrupts the PBAF chromatin remodeling complex, leading to redistribution of BAF occupancy and activation of invasion-related transcriptional pathways.47 These epigenetic changes, enriched in approximately 14% of melanoma brain metastases, cooperate with integrin-mediated adhesion (VLA-4) and PI3K/AKT pathway activation to enable melanoma cells to breach the blood-brain barrier.42,47

ARID2 mutations in patients with advanced non-anaplastic FCDTC and brain metastases have not been previously described and require further characterization in larger cohorts. Although these findings are hypothesis-generating, they support further investigation into the role of chromatin remodeling in the development of brain metastases in thyroid cancer.

Although FCDTC with TERT promoter mutations, and the coexistence of TERT and BRAF p. V600E mutations, represent an aggressive subgroup with poor outcomes,48,49 our study did not find these molecular alterations to be predictive of brain metastases occurrence or survival outcomes. This could be due to the small number of patients with brain metastases and clinical variability within our cohort. Molecular alterations are used to inform the management of brain metastases in other cancers. For patients with EGFR-mutated lung cancer with brain metastases, upfront treatment with a third-generation EGFR inhibitor, rather than radiation therapy, may be used in select patients with successful intracranial control.16 The selective RET inhibitor selpercatinib has shown intracranial response rates of greater than 80% in RET-mutated lung cancers.50 Seventy-three percent of patients in our study were not receiving any systemic therapy at the time of brain metastases diagnosis. Improved understanding of the molecular changes in non-anaplastic FCDTC that has metastasized to the brain, along with improved blood–brain barrier penetration with systemic therapies, may allow for the integration of systemic therapy into the management of thyroid cancer brain metastases alongside surgical resection and radiation treatment.

Our study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective nature and limited number of patients with brain metastases. The number of patients with brain metastases may be underestimated as only 56% of patients had dedicated brain imaging. Furthermore, many patients had a CT which is not as sensitive as MRI to detect brain metastases. Panel composition, and inclusion of structural variants, copy number variations, and tumor mutation burden, was not consistent across platforms and represents a limitation of this study. As previously mentioned, our single-center cohort is a highly selected population of patients with thyroid cancer with advanced disease and high-risk features that were treated in a tertiary care Medical Oncology clinic, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Notwithstanding these limitations, our study provides valuable clinical insight into the characteristics of patients with advanced non-anaplastic FCDTC that manifested with brain metastases, and possible risk factors and predictors of survival. Future prospective and multi-center studies are warranted to gain further insights into this uncommon group requiring multidisciplinary management.

Conclusions

This retrospective study of patients with advanced non-anaplastic FCDTC found that 30% of those who underwent dedicated brain imaging had brain metastases, a higher proportion than previously reported. Lung metastases were associated with an increased risk of brain involvement, supporting consideration of screening with brain imaging in advanced thyroid cancer. Poor ECOG performance status and the presence of ≥4 brain metastases were associated with worse survival outcomes. Although this is the first study to include complete next-generation sequencing in patients with brain metastases, molecular alterations did not appear associated with brain metastasis risk or survival. However, the role of ARID2 mutations warrants further investigation in larger studies. Future studies involving larger populations of patients with advanced thyroid cancer and brain metastases, coupled with comprehensive genomic data, are needed to refine prognostic models and develop disease-specific treatment paradigms, ultimately guiding more personalized and effective management strategies.
