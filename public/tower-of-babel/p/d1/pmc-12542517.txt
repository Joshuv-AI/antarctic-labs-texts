The effectiveness of endoactivator, EDDY, passive ultrasonic irrigation, and XP-Endo finisher r in removing filling materials from retreated root canals
Authors: Zeynep Sena Güngördü, Pelin Tufenkci, Merve Sarı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundsThe aim of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of supplementary techniques, including conventional needle irrigation (CNI), EDDY (ED), EndoActivator (EA), passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI), and XP-Endo Finisher R (XP-FR), in removing residual filling materials from oval-shaped root canals during non-surgical endodontic retreatment.MethodsSixty human mandibular premolar teeth were initially prepared using an R25 instrument and filled with gutta-percha points and Bioserra sealer. The initial filling material removal was performed with PTUR D1 (30/0.09) and D2 (25/0.08) files, and the root canals were finally shaped with an R40 instrument. The samples were randomly divided into 5 groups (n = 12) according to the irrigation activation techniques. Residual filling materials in the apical, middle, and coronal thirds were examined using a scanning electron microscope. Statistical analyses were performed using Kruskal-Wallis tests, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.ResultsIn the ED and XP-FR groups, residue scores decreased from apical to coronal (p < 0.05). ED, PUI, and XP-FR showed significantly lower residue scores in all root thirds compared to CNI (p < 0.05) and in the middle third compared to EA (p < 0.05). EA showed lower scores only in the coronal third compared to the CNI group (p < 0.05).ConclusionED, PUI, and XP-FR did not show statistically significant differences, all activation techniques demonstrated better efficacy than the EA and CNI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06877-4.

Introduction

The aim of nonsurgical endodontic retreatment (NSRT) is to effectively clean the infected root canal system due to inadequate disinfection and/or coronal or apical leakage, and to foster periapical healing [1, 2]. The inability to remove root filling materials may prevent irrigation solutions from sufficiently reaching the persistent microorganisms within the canal system. This can negatively affect the success of root canal therapy [2, 3].

Calcium silicate-based sealers (CSBS) are biocompatible materials that, once set, are considered physically and chemically stable, exhibiting excellent sealing properties. However, their removal from root canals during NSRT is challenging and significantly prolongs clinical procedure time [4, 5]. In teeth with complex root anatomy, conventional mechanical shaping alone is insufficient to eliminate residual root filling materials and smear layers from inaccessible canal walls, isthmuses, lateral canals, and dentinal tubules [6]. Additionally, rotary instruments themselves tend to displace debris into these anatomical irregularities, necessitating a chemomechanical cleaning approach [7, 8]. As it has become evident that complete debridement of root canals cannot be achieved through conventional needle irrigation (CNI) alone, various irrigation activation techniques have been developed to enhance the delivery and efficacy of irrigating solutions [9–11].

EndoActivator (EA; Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) is a sonic activation device that operates at a frequency of 2.000–10.000 cycles per minute (cpm) (33–167 Hz) and offers polymer tips in different sizes [12]. Due to its flexible tip design, it can be used even in curved canals and maintains its effectiveness as long as it does not come into direct contact with the canal walls [13].

EDDY (ED; VDW, Munich, Germany) is a sterile, single-use sonic activation tip with a 25/0.04 polyamide tip, designed to operate with an air-driven unit. Similar to ultrasonic activation, it functions based on cavitation and acoustic streaming principles. Studies have demonstrated that ED, which operates at 6.000 Hz, enhances debris removal and smear layer elimination [14–16].

Passive ultrasonic activation (PUI) occurs when the energy generated by an ultrasonic device at a frequency of 25–32 kHz is transmitted to the irrigation solution within the root canal via an ultrasonic tip. This process induces circular acoustic microstreaming and bubble formation around the ultrasonic tip, followed by bubble collapse. This phenomenon is known as the acoustic cavitation principle [17].

XP-Endo Finisher R (XP-FR; FKG Dentaire; La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland) is an extremely flexible file made of a specialized titanium alloy (Martensite-Austenite Electropolish-Flex). Due to the Max-Wire alloy properties, the file remains in the martensitic phase at room temperature, maintaining a straight shape. However, upon exposure to 37 °C within the root canal, it transitions to the austenitic phase, adopting a spoon-like curvature with a depth of 1.5 mm. This shape transformation facilitates the activation of irrigation solutions, allowing the file to reach difficult areas within the root canal without altering dentin or the canal shape. The file has an ISO #30 tip size, zero taper, and expands up to a 6 mm diameter when used in rotation within the canal [3, 18].

One of the clinical challenges in the NSRT of oval-shaped canals is the persistence of residual material, particularly on the buccal and lingual walls, which often resists complete debridement [19]. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has comprehensively compared the effectiveness of various supplementary irrigation activation techniques—CNI, EA, ED, PUI, and XP-FR—in removing residual filling materials from oval-shaped root canals during NSRT. Given the difficulty of eliminating calcium silicate-based sealers from such anatomies, the present study aims to assess the performance of these activation methods under standardized NSRT conditions. The null hypothesis (H₀) assumes that there is no significant difference among the tested techniques.

Materials and methods

Priori power analysis

The priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.4 software (Heinrich Heine University, Dusseldorf, Germany). Five study groups were formed based on different supplementary irrigation activation techniques. To assess whether there was a significant difference in residual filling material among the activation techniques, a sample size calculation was performed using the F-test family and a One-way ANOVA analysis. The calculation was based on 5% α error, 80% power, and an effect size of 0.5 20, resulting in a required total sample size of 55.

The study protocol was reviewed by the local ethics committee and approved with the project number 2021/09.

Sixty human mandibular premolars with a single root and a single oval-shaped canal (long-to-short diameter ratio ≥ 2), extracted for periodontal reasons, were selected. The desired morphology was confirmed by evaluating the buccolingual and mesiodistal periapical radiographs taken from the teeth. Only teeth with fully developed roots, no caries or restorations, no fractures, cracks, calcification, or internal/external resorption, and with an apical foramen equal to or smaller than a size 15 K-file and root curvature not exceeding 10° according to Schneider’s method [21]were included in the study. The selected teeth were stored in distilled water until the time of the experiment.

Soft tissue remnants and debris on the root surface were cleaned using periodontal curettes. The coronal portion of the teeth was sectioned with a diamond separator to achieve a standard root length of 14 ± 1 mm.

Initial root canal treatment

An access cavity was created with a diamond fissure bur, and the root canals were identified. A #15 K-file (Diadent Group International, Burnaby, BC, Canada) was placed into the canal to measure the working length (WL) and was advanced until the tip became visible at the apical foramen. The measured length was then reduced by 1 mm. Root canal shaping was performed using X-Smart Plus endodontic motor (Dentsply) with an R25 (25/0.08) file (VDW, Munich, Germany) in the Reciproc ALL program. Shaping was carried out with in-and-out movements from the coronal to the apical region. Irrigation was performed with 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) (Cermaked, Stalowa Wola, Poland) using a 30G side-vented needle (Medic; Shanghai Carelife, China). Final irrigation was performed sequentially 5 mL of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; Kemiger, Ankara, Turkey), and 5 mL of distilled water. The root canals were then dried using sterile paper points (DiaDent) until they remained slightly moist.

Following chemomechanical shaping, the root canals were filled using the single-cone technique with Reciproc R25 (25/0.08) gutta-percha and injectable CSBS (Bioserra; Dentac, Turkey). After confirming the tug-back of the gutta-percha cone, the sealer was injected into the canal until it became visible at the canal orifice. The gutta-percha cone was coated with sealer and placed into the canal, after which excess material was removed from the canal orifice using a heated hand instrument. The access cavity was cleaned with alcohol-moistened cotton pellets and temporarily restored with Cavit-G filling material (3 M Espe, Seefeld, Germany). Periapical radiographs were taken to evaluate the quality of the root canal filling. To ensure complete setting of the sealer, the specimens were stored in a 100% humid environment at 37 °C for two months.

Retreatment procedure

After the removal of the temporary filling material, the initial root filling was removed using ProTaper Universal Retreatment files (Dentsply Sirona, Pennsylvania, USA). The D1 file (30/0.09) was used for the coronal third, while the D2 file (25/0.08) was used for the middle and apical thirds. Subsequently, final shaping was performed with an R40 (40/0.06) file (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flowchart demonstrating the study protocol

A total of 15 mL NaOCl was used, with 5 mL applied to each root third. For the final irrigation phase, the specimens were randomly assigned into five groups (n = 12) based on the activation method (www.random.org).

Experimental groups

Group 1: Conventional Needle Irrigation Group–Control Group

In this group, irrigation was performed in three stages using a 30G side-vented irrigation needle. In the first stage, 5 mL of 5.25% NaOCl solution was introduced into the canal, with the irrigation needle positioned 1 mm short of the WL and agitated with 3–4 mm amplitude for 1 min. In the second stage, 5 mL of 17% EDTA solution used for 1 min. In the third stage, 5 mL of 5.25% NaOCl solution was used in the same manner as in the first stage. Following these three stages, final irrigation was completed with 5 mL of distilled water.

Group 2: endoactivator group

In this group, root canals were filled with 2.5 mL NaOCl solution using a 30G side-vented irrigation needle. A polymer tip (25/0.04) was positioned 1 mm short of the WL and activated at 10.000 cpm for 30 s using gentle 3 mm in-and-out motions. This procedure was repeated twice, resulting in a total activation time of 1 min with 5 mL of NaOCl. Similarly, 5 mL of EDTA was activated for a total of 1 min, followed by another activation cycle with NaOCl. Final irrigation was performed with 5 mL of distilled water.

Group 3: EDDY group

EDDY, a polyamide tip (25/0.04), was used with the Micron TA-200 Air-Scaler (TA-200-S2H, Micron, Japan). It was positioned 1 mm short of the WL and activated with 3–4 mm amplitude, following the same protocol described for the EA group.

Group 4: passive ultrasonic irrigation group

In this group, the NSK Various 970 lx ultrasonic device (NSK Nakanishi Inc., Kanuma, Tochigi, Japan) was used with an ultrasonic irrigation tip (NSK; U-files, 25/0.02). The tip was positioned 1 mm short of the WL and activated following the same protocol as in the EA and ED groups.

Group 5: XP-Endo finisher R group

The XP-FR file was used with a torque-controlled X-Smart Plus motor, set to 1000 rpm and 1 N/cm torque, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The file was positioned 1 mm short of the WL and moved slowly in an up-and-down motion with a 7–8 mm amplitude. The irrigation activation cycle followed the same protocol as described previously.

After the irrigation activation procedures, all specimens were dried using sterile paper points.

Preparation and evaluation of specimens for SEM analysis

Following the removal of the previous root filling using different activation techniques, the specimens were embedded in pink cold acrylic using molds measuring 2 × 3 × 2 cm to facilitate longitudinal sectioning. A precision cutting machine (Accutom-10; Struers LLC, USA) was used at 1250 rpm, and specimens were sectioned longitudinally into two halves using a diamond disc (MOD15; Struers ApS, Denmark) with dimensions of 152 × 0.4 × 12.7 mm.

One half of each specimen was selected for further analysis. The root surfaces were marked at 4 mm (apical), 8 mm (middle), and 12 mm (coronal) levels using a fine-tip marker. The marked specimens were then coated with a platinum-gold layer and examined under a scanning electron microscope (Quanta 650 Field Emission SEM; FEI, USA) at ×2000 magnification (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Representative images of the experimental groups. 1: Conventional needle irrigation; 2: Endo Activator; 3: EDDY; 4: Passive ultrasonic irrigation; 5: XP-Endo Finisher R. Representative images of the experimental groups. 1: Conventional needle irrigation; 2: Endo Activator; 3: EDDY; 4: Passive ultrasonic irrigation; 5: XP-Endo Finisher R. A: Coronal third; B: Middle third; C: Apical third

The obtained SEM images were scored by two calibrated researchers based on the following criteria [22]: Score 0: No residue; Score 1: Minimal residue (≤ 20% of the examined surface); Score 2: Moderate residue (20–60% of the examined surface); Score 3: Extensive residue (> 60% of the examined surface).

Statistical analysis

The data obtained were statistically analyzed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Inter-observer agreement was assessed using the Kappa test. The normality of the data was checked using the Shapiro-Wilks test. As the data did not follow a normal distribution, the effect of different activation techniques on the residual filling material scores in canals filled with CSBS was compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Additionally, the residual scores in different regions of the root canals were also evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test. The significance level was set at p < 0.05, and Bonferroni correction was adjusted for multiple comparisons.

The analysis revealed an inter-observer agreement of 80% (r: 0.80). Statistically significant differences were also observed between the apical, middle, and coronal thirds of the root canal. In all activation groups, the residue scores decreased from apical third to coronal third. However, this decrease was statistically significant only in the ED and XP-FR groups (p < 0.05).

Discussion

In retreatment procedures, researchers have reported that root filling materials cannot be entirely removed from the root canal system, which may lead to treatment failure due to microorganisms embedded in the residual filling materials [3, 23–27]. The removal of filling materials is particularly challenging in the apical regions of oval-shaped root canals [23, 28]. Therefore, the use of supplementary activation systems is recommended to eliminate residual filling materials [3, 13, 14, 25, 29]. In this study, which compared the retreatment efficacy of supplementary activation techniques—CNI, EA, ED, PUI, and XP-FR—in oval canals filled with Bioserra sealer using SEM analysis, a statistically significant difference was observed among the activation techniques. Consequently, the H₀ hypothesis was rejected.

In numerous studies on endodontic retreatment, examinations of cross-sections taken from the root canals have revealed that, regardless of the type of sealer, the filling technique, or the imaging method, the amount of residual material in the apical thirds is higher than in the coronal and middle thirds [25, 30–33]. This reduction in irrigation effectiveness in the apical third can be attributed to factors such as the decrease in the diameter and number of dentinal tubules from coronal to apical, as well as the increased presence of calcifications and sclerotic areas in the apical third [34]. This condition, which also negatively affects the removal of the smear layer, makes it more challenging to eliminate filling materials [35].

In this study, although residual scores decreased from the apical to the coronal third in all groups, a statistically significant reduction was observed only in the ED and XP-FR groups. A SEM-supported study evaluating the removal of debris and the smear layer in curved canals found that the ED technique was significantly more effective in eliminating the smear layer in the coronal third compared to the apical third. In contrast, the PUI and EA techniques showed no significant differences in effectiveness across different root thirds [35]. It has been suggested that the polyamide structure of the ED sonic activation tip allows high-frequency oscillations to be freely transmitted without damaging the dentin, generating rapid fluid movement through three-dimensional oscillation in the coronal third, which may account for its greater effectiveness [36]. This finding has been explained for PUI by the higher flow rate of ultrasonic devices, which are generally less affected by root canal curvature [37] For EA, it has been suggested that its low-frequency but high-displacement tip may have reduced its effectiveness in the coronal third due to its contact with the canal walls. Elnaghy et al. [38] found that the EA device exhibited similar effectiveness at different root thirds. The present study supports these findings.

Düzgün et al. [39] compared the effectiveness of CNI, PUI, EA, and manual dynamic activation in removing CSBS from standardized artificial grooves created in maxillary central incisors. Among the activation techniques, PUI demonstrated superior efficacy. Since sealer removal efficiency in standardized grooves is particularly dependent on the hydrodynamics of the irrigation solutions, it has been suggested that the higher velocity irrigation flow generated by PUI may have contributed to its greater effectiveness.

The potential effectiveness of ED, a sonic device operating at 6000 Hz, in the removal of root canal filling materials has been previously demonstrated [14, 40–42]. In the present study, ED and PUI exhibited similar effectiveness across all root thirds. The efficiency of irrigation increases as the irrigant makes greater contact with the filling materials targeted for removal [43]. In this context, despite operating at a lower frequency than PUI, ED’s flexible polyamide tip enables three-dimensional oscillation, generating a powerful and rapid fluid flow without losing energy. This suggests that, like PUI, ED has the ability to induce acoustic streaming and cavitation within the irrigant solution [35, 41].

The PUI, ED, and XP-FR groups demonstrated superior effectiveness compared to the CNI group across all root thirds, whereas the EA group was significantly more effective than CNI only in the coronal third. Grischke et al. [31] conducted a study comparing the efficacy of EA, PUI, and hydrodynamic devices in removing sealer from canal surfaces and simulated irregularities using visual scoring. Their findings showed that while the EA device did not exhibit superiority over the control group, PUI demonstrated the highest effectiveness among all groups. Consistent with our study, the lack of superiority of the EA group over the control group may be attributed to its low frequency (2.000–10.000 cpm), which could limit the penetration and effectiveness of irrigants into dentinal tubules, particularly in the apical third, where increased sclerosis is commonly observed [44].

XP-Endo Finisher R is designed to optimize cleaning efficacy in root canal retreatment procedures [33] its effectiveness did not show a statistically significant difference from the ED and PUI groups in this study. De-Deus et al. [33] and Silva et al. [45] compared the effectiveness of PUI and XP-FR as supplementary activation systems in removing AH Plus sealer from single-rooted oval canals during retreatment. Micro-Computed Tomography (Micro-CT) images from these studies showed that both activation systems significantly reduced the residual filling volume, with XP-FR removing more canal filling material than PUI. Similarly, in a retreatment study on mandibular premolars filled with calcium silicate-based sealer iRoot SP, Micro-CT analysis revealed significantly less residual material in the XP-FR group compared to the PUI group at all root levels [25]. The discrepancy between these findings and those of our study may be attributed to methodological differences, such as the final diameter and taper of the root canals during the retreatment protocol, as well as the type of root canal sealer used. Moreover, the use of micro-CT for detecting residual filling material may also have influenced the assessment outcomes.

Crozeta et al. [23] reported that PUI was more effective than XP-FR in removing filling materials from oval-shaped canals filled with EndoSequence BC sealer. This finding contradicts our study and previously mentioned studies. The discrepancy may be attributed to the specific R2 Flatsonic tip used in that study, which features a 2 mm-long, flat equilateral triangular shape with a lower taper but a larger diameter compared to the tip used in our study. This design allows for better adaptation to the oval canal anatomy, enhancing contact with areas where debris tends to accumulate and facilitating the removal of residual filling material.

The XP-FR, made from MaxWire alloy, expands at body temperature, and its elliptical portion is compressed by the canal anatomy during its movement. This mechanical effect, along with the tip being pushed toward the canal walls, allows it to make contact with even hard-to-reach areas during canal irrigation, forcing the sealer off the canal walls [45, 46]. PUI, which operates at high frequencies, generates cavitation in the irrigation solutions, positively impacting the removal of sealer from dentin tubules. Similarly, the high-frequency ED device also activates the irrigation solution with a comparable effect. The hydrodynamic phenomenon created by the polymer tip of EA in the irrigation solution has been noted to be less effective in removing filling materials [47].

A limitation of this study is the inability to standardize the root canal morphology, size, volume, and amount of sclerotic dentin in extracted human teeth. The study was conducted using only single-oval canals to maintain standardization. The SEM used as the imaging method allows for the visualization of the smear layer and dentinal tubules; however, its limited field of view at high magnifications, the need for the samples to be divided into two parts prior to imaging, and the requirement for a gold-palladium coating may reduce the efficiency and accuracy of the data obtained from the samples. The use of other imaging devices, such as Micro-CT, which allows for quantitative evaluation of residual filling materials, and confocal laser scanning microscopy, which provides histotomographic images of the examined samples, would enable a more thorough assessment of the samples before and after root canal filling. Additionally, the study exclusively used Bioserra sealer, which does not represent all CSBS and fails to account for the characteristics of other sealers, such as epoxy resin-based sealers. Future research could benefit from exploring variations in sealer types and tooth anatomy.

Within the limitations of this study, the results indicate that all tested activation techniques performed better than CNI in removing calcium silicate-based sealer residues from oval-shaped root canals. Although no statistically significant differences were observed among the ED, PUI, and XP-FR groups, they consistently yielded the lowest residue scores across all root thirds, particularly in the apical region where debridement is most challenging. These findings support the clinical use of ED, PUI, and XP-FR as effective supplementary activation systems during NSRT procedures. If a sonic activation device is to be selected, ED may be preferred over EA, as it demonstrated superior cleaning efficacy. Ultimately, clinicians should consider using advanced activation techniques—especially ED, PUI, and XP-FR—to improve disinfection and filling material removal in complex root canal anatomies.
