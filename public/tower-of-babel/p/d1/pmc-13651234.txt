Background

A renowned antitussive and edible perennial herb, Fritillaria unibracteata P. K. Hsiao & K. C. Hsia belongs to the Fritillaria section of the Liliaceae family. It is mainly distributed in northwestern Sichuan, southeastern Qinghai, and southern Gansu in China [1, 2]. F. unibracteata is one original plant of the traditional Chinese medicine Chuanbeimu [3, 4]. The desiccated bulbs of F. unibracteata have the potential for heat-clearing, nourishing the lungs, reducing phlegm, dissolving lumps, and especially antitussive effects [5, 6]. Numerous medicines have been formulated from the dried bulbs of F. unibracteata based on pharmacological results that have anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, anti-aging, anti-asthmatic, and rejuvenating properties [7, 8, 9]. To date, various compounds have been isolated and analyzed. Of these, isosteroidal alkaloids and their intermediates comprise the main active ingredients, including ebeiedinone, ebeienine, imperialine, isoverticine, and verticine [10]. The non-alkaloid group has also been widely studied, and includes adenosine, saponin, succinic acid, sterols, terpenoids, and thymidine [11, 12, 13]. Owing to their beneficial properties, the bulbs of F. unibracteata form building blocks in medication dosages used in Chinese traditional medicine and Indian medicine [3, 9, 14]. In 2021, F. unibracteata was included on the medication list for treating or taking precautions against COVID-19 [15]. However, wild germplasm resources have shrunk rapidly due to overexploitation. Currently, this high-altitude plant is a growing concern for the people of the Himalayas and trans-Himalayas [16]. The genomic data provide valuable genetic resources for fritillary germplasm enhancement and garden variety breeding. The plastome of F. unibracteata has been assembled [17]. However, mitochondrial genome analysis currently lags.

Mitochondria are the energy factories that manufacture ATP. Mitochondria play a key role in the processes of lipid transformation, ion homeostasis, and coping with stress. They also participate in metabolism that yields reducing equivalents for the architectures and carbon skeletons of amino acid metabolites [18]. The mitochondrial genome contains a semi-autonomous machinery independent of the nucleus and exhibits an intricate conformation in structure and size [19]. In terrestrial plants, the largest mitochondrial genome is 18.99 Mb in Cathaya argyrophylla. The smallest is 66 kb in Viscum scurruloideum, a semiparasitic plant [20, 21]. In angiosperms, the gene number of the mitogenome generally falls within the range of 50 to 60, including 24 pivotal genes with conserved function [22, 23]. Mitochondrial DNA usually attaches to the inner membrane and is predominantly circular and naked without protein binding. Several plant mitochondrial genome configurations have been deciphered, including circular, linear, branched, multi-ring, and mixed architecture [24, 25]. The mechanisms controlling this complexity have been poorly explored, impeding genetic and evolutionary research on mitogenomes. Furthermore, the shortage of data on intra- and inter-species variation increases the difficulty of elucidating the evolutionary process of the formation mechanism of these changeable mitochondrial genome structures.

The intricate conformation of the angiosperm mitogenome, featuring variable structure, size, gene number, and rich repetitive sequences, is ideal for probing genome complexity [26]. Complex conformations hinder successful mitogenome sequencing and assembly. Herein, the mitogenome of F. unibracteata was sequenced using Illumina and Oxford Nanopore sequencing techniques. The mitochondrial genome architecture, size, gene content, and features were analyzed. To investigate the mechanisms responsible for shaping the miscellaneous mitogenome architecture of F. unibracteata, various analyses were performed. This included repetitive sequences, codon usage preferences, RNA editing, and gene transfer events between organelles. In this study, comparison of the mitogenome of F. unibracteata with that of other angiosperms was also been carried out to explore gene gain or loss, clarify the phylogenetic position, and analyze the selection pressure of PCGs. This paper offers preliminary data for studies furthering our understanding of the phylogenetic relationships of Fritillaria taxa, aids in fritillary germplasm enhancement and horticultural variety breeding, and contributes to an in-depth understanding of the mechanism and evolution of multi-chromosomal mitogenomes.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and genome sequencing

In florescence, the samples of F. unibracteata were collected in Hongyuan County, Sichuan, China. Fresh leaves and bulbs were stored in liquid nitrogen for DNA extraction. The samples were authenticated by Dr. Song Lu, and specimens were deposited in Emeishan Biological Station under the voucher number: 23-AB-0608. To obtain the mitogenome of F. unibracteata, a hybrid sequencing strategy was performed on the Oxford Nanopore PromethION sequencer and Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform, where the long-reads were sequenced and corrected with short-reads, respectively.

Assembly and annotation of the mitogenome

After the long-fragment library was established with a mean length of 9 kb (Table S1), the real-time single molecule sequencing (SMART) was performed on the Nanopore platform. Then, raw data of the third generation were filtered using Filtlong (v0.2.1, https://github.com/rrwick/Filtlong) with the parameters: --min_length 1000, --min_mean_q 7. As for second-generation sequencing, after the short-reads library was constructed, the quality control of raw data was carried out by fastp (version 0.23.4) [27]. Adapters and primer sequences were trimmed, reads with an average Phred quality score below Q5 or more than five N bases were filtered out, and then clean reads were retained for downstream analysis. Single stretches of the chloroplast and nuclear contigs were manually removed and visualized using Bandage. Next, two sets of data were assembled according to the following strategy: (1). Minimap2 (v2.1) was utilized to compare and screen the third-generation data with the seed sequences which contained more alignment genes and with overlap greater than 1 kb [28]. (2). After the third-generation data were calibrated and corrected in Canu (v2.0, parameters: -correct genomeSize=500k useGrid=false -nanopore-ra) [29], bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1) was used to align the second-generation data to previous products [30]. (3). The mixed comparison and assembly of two sets of data were performed using Unicycler (v0.4.8, default set) with manual regulation [31]. (4). Then, the mitochondrial concatenation of F. unibracteata was generated. The annotations of protein coding genes and rRNA genes were performed by BlastN with the published sequences as references, and then manual adjustments were carried out according to the sequences of related species (OP973783.1-OP973810.1); tRNA genes were detected in tRNAscanSE (http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/); ORFs were annotated using OpenReading Frame Finder (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gorf/gorf.html); PmtREP was used to predict RNA editing sites. OGDRAW (1.3.1) was used to visualize the mitogenome of F. unibracteata [32].

Codon usage analysis

The phenomenon of codon degeneracy exists in the mitochondrial genome, and that results in the disequilibrium of codons for amino acids with the usage number spanning from minimum 1 to maximum 6 [33]. Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) was the result of genetic drift, gene mutation, and natural selection. A self-coding Perl script was written to detect coding genes and calculate the codon numbers (http://112.86.217.82:9919/#/tool/alltool/detail/214).

RNA editing sites of F. unibracteata

RNA editing sites of protein-coding genes in F. unibracteata were forecast using two complementary methods. First, PREP-Mt was used to predict the editing events including deletion, insertion, and substitution sites [34]. To improve accuracy, a second method was employed using transcriptome data as the reference [35]. The transcriptome data were mapped to the mitogenome using BWA [36]. Then, a self-coding Perl script was used to identify consistent nucleotide differences between DNA and RNA sequences supported by high read coverage (http://112.86.217.82:9919/#/tool/alltool/detail/336).

Analysis of repeats

To detect simple sequence repeats in the mitochondrial genome of F. unibracteata, MISA v1.0 (parameters for the thresholds: 10 − 1, 5 − 2, 4 − 3, 3–4, 3–5, 3–6) was used [37]. Tandem Repeats Finder v4.09 (parameters: 2 7 7 80 10 50 2000 -f -d -m) was used to identify tandem repeats. Dispersed repeats were identified using the REPuter web server (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer) [38], with a Hamming distance of 3, a minimum repeat length of 27 bp, and an E-value cutoff of 1e− 5. Circos v0.69–5 was used to visualize these repeats [39].

Migration analysis of chloroplast-to-mitochondrial DNA

The online software BlastN (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) was used to screen homologous sequences between the plastome and mitogenome of F. unibracteata. The criteria were set as: E-value of 1e− 5 and similarity threshold of 70%. Results were visualized in Circos v0.69–5 [39].

Comparative analysis of mitogenome structure

The mitogenomes of nine medicinal plants between neighboring families were matched using CGView (http://stothard.afns.ualberta.ca/cgview_server/) with default settings and the mitochondrial genome of F. unibracteata was set as reference (Table S2). The dot-plot synteny analysis was performed using Nucmer v4.0.0 beta2 (– maxmatch parameter) [40].

Phylogenetic analysis

The mitochondrial Bayesian inference (BI) phylogenetic tree was constructed based on PCGs of 23 mitogenomes. Firstly, a string of shared PCGs was aligned and concatenated in MAFFT v7.427 (auto mode), and then the sequences were trimmed with trimAl v1.4. rev15 (parameter:-gt 0.7) [41, 42]. The prediction of optimal model was performed using jModeltest v2.1.10 [43]. Finally, the BI phylogenetic analysis was carried out using MrBayes v3.2.7 with a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis over 1,000,000 generations, sampling every 100 generations and discarding 25% of the phylogenetic trees as burn-in [44]. A consensus tree was summarized with all compatible clades, and posterior probability (PP) values were annotated on each node. The final tree was visualized in Figtree v1.4.43 [45].

Analysis of synonymous and nonsynonymous substitution ratio and nucleotide variability (Pi)

Nonsynonymous and synonymous substitution ratios of coding genes in the mitogenome of F. unibracteata were calculated. The extraction and global alignment of homologous sequences were performed in Mafft v7.427 with auto mode [41]. Then, the computation of Ka and Ks values for each gene pair was carried out with KaKs Calculator v2.0 applying the MLWL model [46]. Then the Ka and Ks values were subjected to a significant test, and these gene pairs with p-value less than 0.05 were selected. The Ka/Ks ratios for all gene pairs were compiled and the result was visualized in a box-plot map. The Pi value of each gene was calculated in DnaSP v5.10.1 [47].

Results

Mitochondrial genomic features of Fritillaria unibracteata

After sequencing and filtering, 69,845,590 clean reads with 20.95 Gb were obtained from the Illumina platform, and the average coverage was about 157×. Besides, 3,992,035 clean reads with 35.74 Gb were generated from the Nanopore sequencer, and the average coverage was around 249×. The mitochondrial genome of F. unibracteata was assembled as 22 discrete circular molecules spanning from 18,718 bp to 82,085 bp, with a total size of 865,248 bp (Fig. 1). The GC content for each chromosome ranges from 44.07% to 45.99% (Table 1). Chromosome 1 acts as the primary ring DNA with the longest length. The F. unibracteata mitogenome contains 60 genes, including 44 protein coding genes (PCGs), 13 tRNA genes, and 3 familiar rRNA genes (Table S3 and Table S4). The GC contents of PCGs, tRNA, and rRNA are 43.27%, 52.17%, and 53.21%, respectively (Table S5). Besides, 53 ORFs encoding hypothetical proteins hits were identified and sparsely spread in 18 chromosomes, and 13 ORFs located in the master ring (Table S3 and Table S6). The 36 PCGs are categorized into 9 groups, comprising 9 NADH dehydrogenases, 8 small ribosomal proteins (SSU), 5 ATP synthases, 4 cytochrome C biosynthetic enzymes, 4 small ribosomal proteins, 3 cytochrome C oxidases, 1 ubiquinol cytochrome c reductase, 1 mature and 1 transport membrane protein (Table S7). Normally, in terrestrial plants, their mitogenomes usually consist of 3 rRNA genes. F. unibracteata is mainly distributed in alpine meadows and shrubby grasslands, and it also contains three rRNA genes in the mitogenome, to wit, rrn18 (1,869 bp), rrn26 (3,277 bp) and rrn5 (118 bp). Additionally, nine different transfer RNA genes (5 copies for trnM-CAT) were annotated with the length ranging from 70 to 74 bp (Table S7). Moreover, 19 introns were identified and situated in 6 cis-spliced genes (ccmFc, nad4, nad7, rpl2, rps3, and rps10), 2 trans-spliced genes (nad1 and nad5), and 1 tRNA gene (trnI-TAT). The exons of nad1 and nad5 reside on different chromosomes (Tables S3 and S8). Their full-length functional transcripts are generated through sequence ligation mediated by trans-splicing. During this process, exons from separate chromosomes are independently transcribed into pre-mRNAs, and partial intron sequences fold into an intact secondary structure. Assisted by nuclear-encoded proteins such as maturases, two successive transesterification reactions take place: the bulging adenosine within the intron first attacks the 5’ splice site to generate a lariat intermediate, followed by covalent ligation of adjacent exons. After multiple rounds of trans-splicing and cis-splicing, the discrete exons are assembled into full-length mature mRNAs.

Repeat sequences in the mitogenome of F. unibracteata

The dispersed repeats are discontinuously scattered in the mitochondrial genome. A gross of 1,077 dispersed repeats were detected with the length spanning from 27 bp to 24,186 bp (Fig. 2, Table S9). The total length is 82,099 bp, which accounts for 9.49% of the whole mitogenome. Of these, including 606 palindrome repeats and 471 forward repeats, 94.34% of them (1,016) are short repeats with length less than 100 bp, and only one forward repeat is longer than 700 bp (Fig. 3-A, Table S9). The highest abundance of dispersed repeats falls in the span of 27–49 bp (Fig. 3-B).

SSRs or microsatellites refer to the short tandem DNA repeats (1–6 bp) being extensively applied for their codominance, polymorphism, and duplicability. As shown in Table S10 and Figs. 3-B and 175 SSRs were identified. Tetrameric, dimeric, and monomeric repeats accounted for 37.71%, 24.57%, and 18.86% of the total, respectively. Most monomeric repeats are A/T types (Fig. 3-B). In dimeric repeats, AG/CT and AT/AT types account for 46.51% and 51.16%, respectively. Besides, the AAG/CTT repeat accounts for 77.78% in trimeric types. Overall, 15 tandem repeats with the size ranging from 8 to 37 bp were detected in 10 chromosomes with the match greater than 82% (Table S11).

Analysis of codon usage

The RSCU values of PCGs in F. unibracteata mitogenome were measured (Table S12). There are 64 common codons that happen 10,621 times corresponding to 21 amino acids or stop codons. Similar to other mitogenomes in angiosperms, most amino acids consist of degenerate codons except methionine and tryptophan. Except the start and stop codons, the highest synonymous codon value is 1.59, belonging to GCT, which encodes alanine. Then the second is CAA encoding glutamate with the value of 1.51, and the third is TAT encoding threonine. Among 32 codons with RSCU > 1, 28 codons (87.5%) demonstrate obvious A/T bias that end in A/T bases comprising of 16 T-ending bases and 12 A-ending bases. Additionally, as shown in Table S7, Table S12, and Fig. 4, most PCGs use ATG as the start codon, while nad1, nad4L, and rps10 also use ACG. The usage rate of TAA is the highest in three stop codons, which accounts for 48.72%.

Substitution rates of protein-coding genes

Synonymous substitution or silent mutation does not generate a new amino acid due to the degeneracy of codons. However, non-synonymous mutations change the amino acid sequence and may alter protein structure and function or lead to premature termination. Ka/Ks of PCGs reflects selection pressure in sibling species. As shown in Fig. 5, all average Ka/Ks ratios of 36 PCGs in F. unibracteata mitogenome are less than 1 after the significant test. The result shows that purifying selection (Ka/Ks < 1) in mitogenomic genes is common, and it connotes conservatism in the evolution of F. unibracteata because negative selection helps to eliminate detrimental variations.

Comparative mitogenome analysis

Collinearity analysis was performed between nine mitogenomes of tracheophyta medicinal plants: Lilium tsingauense, Allium cepa, A. fistulosum, Asparagus officinalis, Chlorophytum comosum, Crocus sativus, Hemerocallis citrina, Luzula sylvatica, and F. unibracteata (Table S2). As shown in the dot-plot result (Fig. S1), the homology between F. unibracteata and L. tsingauense, which belong to the same family, is the highest with a similarity percentage of 57.71%. The homology is lower when compared with other seven mitogenomes, with the similarity spanning from 3.60% to 7.64%. The homologous collinear blocks between F. unibracteata and L. tsingauense mainly exist in chromosomes 3, 4, 6, 11, 15, and 16. Nearly all fragments in chromosome 11 of F. unibracteata replicate with those in chromosome 15 of L. tsingauense, indicating higher conservativeness of these sequences. Yet, significant genome differences exist between the mitogenomes of F. unibracteata and other 7 species, illustrating extensive genome rearrangements and variable structures in the evolutionary process. Additionally, gene content comparison in nine species showed higher homology in core genes of mitochondrial genome and obviously variable in others. Seven species, including F. unibracteata, contained all 9 nad genes. F. unibracteata retained all ribosomal large subunit (LSU) genes and most small subunit (SSU) genes, lost rps1, rps2, and rps11 (Fig. 6-A, Table S7). The sdh4 remains intact only in Hemerocallis citrina, while it was lost or pseudogenized in the rest eight.

Phylogenetic analysis

To clarify the evolutionary placement of the F. unibracteata mitogenome, a phylogenetic tree was constructed in MrBayes using 21 publicly available plant mitogenomes, with Nicotiana tabacum L. and Cannabis sativa L. as outgroups. Nearly all topological relationships received full bootstrap support (100%). Phylogenetic reconstruction showed that F. unibracteata grouped with Lilium tsingtauense within the Fritillaria-Lilium clade, matching the taxonomic result of APG IV (Fig. 6-B). This phylogenetic affinity highlights the necessity of further comparative genomic analyses focusing on fritillary relatives.

Nucleotide diversity

High variable regions are helpful in mining tabs for genetic analysis. Nucleotide diversity (Pi) of 34 PCGs and 3 rRNA genes in nine species of Tracheophyta is illustrated in Fig. S2 and Table S13. The Pi values of 37 genes are in the scope of 0.023 to 0.162, and most of them are lower than 0.07. The variability of atp8 (Pi = 0.162) is the highest, followed by rps19 (Pi = 0.098), cox2 (Pi = 0.086), and atp9 (Pi = 0.082). Contrarily, the most conservative PCGs are cob (Pi = 0.023) and nad7 (Pi = 0.024).

Prediction of RNA editing sites

In F. unibracteata mitogenome, 518 RNA editing sites were predicted in PCGs. These sites irregularly distribute in genes with a variable number from 2 to 46 (Fig. S3, Table S14). After editing, 45.95% of amino acids alter their polarities from hydrophilic to hydrophobic, while 9.46% of them convert in reverse. Also, 31.85% and 11.97% amino acids do not change their characteristics and remain hydrophobic or hydrophilic after editing, respectively.

Among the 30 types of codon transfer which relate to 14 changeable amino acids, TCA = > TTA is the most popular editing with 67 sites. It also predicates that, of all amino acids, the conversion from others to leucine is the highest, which accounts for 42.08%. Furthermore, all RNA editing types are C to T, 64.98% (347) of them reside on the second place, and the rest 35.02% (187) reside on the first position of the triplet codons. A special occurrence is that both the first and second bases are replaced, and the event leads to translation from proline (CCC or CCT) to phenylalanine (TTC or TTT). Particularly, four termination codons are generated in the rpl16, atp9, rps10, and ccmFc genes.

Horizontal gene transfer in the mitogenome of F. unibracteata

The migration process of plastid genomic fragments to the mitogenome is common in plants. In the mitochondrial genome of F. unibracteata, 12 of the 22 chromosomes contain plastid-derived sequences, encoding 1 intact tRNA gene and 2 partial rrn genes (Fig. 7 and Table S15). Sixteen homologous fragments exist between two organelle genomes. The size of these plastid-origin sequences falls in the scope of 32 bp to 3,692 bp with high similarity from 73.67% to 100% (Fig. 7 and Table S15).

Discussion

The structure, size, and gene content of the F. unibracteata mitogenome

As the main original source of the antitussive medicinal herb Chuanbeimu or Songbeimu, the increasing interest in F. unibracteata has always been a key research focus. Research efforts have primarily focused on its pharmacology, pharmacodynamics, genetic diversity, and phylogeny [3, 4, 16]. Genomic data are critical for exploring these processes. Most of the sequenced mitogenomes in angiosperms are mapped as a major circular chromosome or a collection of minor subgenomes [48, 49]. Here, the mitogenome of F. unibracteata had a sophisticated multi-chromosomal configuration with one master circular chromosome and 21 sub-master chromosomes [50]. Multicircular chromosome architecture is common in the family Liliaceae, such as Lilium tsingtauense Gilg (27 molecules) and Cardiocrinum giganteum (Wall.) Makino (19 circular molecules) [51, 52]. Repeat fragments mediate recombination and can result in dynamic configurations in plant mitogenomes by forming subchromosomal structures and pervasive genomic isomerization [53, 54]. The F. unibracteata mitogenome has a high number of repetitive fragments, indicating a high probability of intermolecular recombination in its evolutionary history [55]. Only one long repeat was identified on chromosome 1, which may have generated a reversible variation. Short repeats were predominant, especially those with lengths less than 100 bp. Although low-frequency recombination mediated by short repetitive fragments is seldom detected in multi-chromosomal mitogenomes, it exists in some cases. This includes Glycine max, a short repetitive element facilitates intramolecular homologous recombination [56]. Rare recombination events between two duplicates trigger deletion or inversion of the intervening segment. This yields multiple subgenomic architectures that coexist in a dynamic state among tissues and individuals [51, 56]. No recombination induced by short repeats was identified in the F. unibracteata mitogenome, and the result was verified by long-read mapping. However, the potential occurrence could not be entirely excluded.

Genome size variations have substantial effects on species divergence, phenotypic evolution, and ecological adaptation across plant lineages [51]. The total size of F. unibracteata mitogenome was within the normal range for most angiosperms (200 kb to 2,400 kb) [50]. Coding genes usually account for a small proportion (< 1%) of the expanded mitogenome of terrestrial plants [57]. In F. unibracteata, the length of the coding regions accounts for 4.62% of the whole mitogenome, which is far less than that of the intergenic non-coding regions. The mechanism of long non-coding regions remains obscure, and the expansion of these sequences is not in line with their phylogenetic patterns [58]. Repetitive sequences constitute a key element of non-coding sequences. Dispersed repeat regions in F. unibracteata mitogenome accounted for 9.49% of its total length. The high proportion of these fragments contributed to the enlarged F. unibracteata mitogenome more than the coding genes and chloroplast-derived fragments. Foreign DNA sequences from the plastome or nuclear genome can be recombined into the mitogenome to form sources of enlarged genomes [59]. In many flowering plants, chloroplast-derived DNA accounts for 3–11.5% of mitochondrial DNA [60]. In F. unibracteata, 16 homologous fragments were detected in both organellar genomes (Table S14, Fig. 7). During the ongoing evolution history, gene transfer events between organelles and the nucleus are part of genome evolution in eukaryotes [61–63]. The rbcL gene in the plastid has been inserted into the mitochondrial DNA on numerous occasions, but all have lost function in the evaluated angiosperms [64]. In F. unibracteata, sequence migration from the plastome contributed to a low level of mitogenome enlargement. The same phenomenon was observed in other Liliaceae species, such as Cardiocrinum giganteum (0.45%) and Lilium tsingtauense (0.25%) [51, 52].

The mitogenomes of angiosperms usually have similar gene contents and have a conserved GC level [22, 23]. The mitogenome of F. unibracteata had a lower gene density, and the coding genes and ORFs devoid of function were unevenly spread across different chromosomes (Fig. 1, Table S4 and Table S6). The uneven distribution of coding genes and ORFs across multiple chromosomes also reflects a flexible mitogenomic architecture that may facilitate the adaptive regulation of gene expression under distinct environmental conditions. This is unlike the more evenly organized mitogenomes of fungi and animals. Dozens of silent or empty chromosomes comprising of unfunctional sequences occur in plant mitogenomes [49, 65]. Additionally, GC levels play an important role in mitogenomic evolutionary processes and affect codon usage preferences in a wide spectrum of organisms, including viruses [66–69].

Codon usage preference and RNA editing sites prediction

Codons are indispensable for information conversion. Codon usage preference is usually considered to be the equilibrium result of natural selection [33]. In the F. unibracteata mitogenome, the codon usage bias is in accordance with the model for most terrestrial plants that use ATG as an initiator codon and shows an obvious A/T bias in the third site of all PCGs [33]. Most codons had RSCU values higher than one end in A/T and only four with A/T ending were less than one, which resided in Ala (GCA, 0.94), Ter (TAA, 0.931), Leu (CTA, 0.888), and Ile (ATA, 0.734).

RNA editing is a transcriptional process that inserts, deletes, or substitutes bases in coding regions. This results in transcriptome variation by changing RNA nucleotides or retrotransposons [70]. The analysis of RNA editing events helps in understanding the expression profiles of plant organellar genes, allowing for a reliable inference of evolutionary dynamics. In F. unibracteata mitogenome, 518 RNA editing sites were similar to those in Lilium tsingtauense (510) and fewer than those in Cardiocrinum giganteum (595) and Polygonatum sibiricum (609) of the family Liliaceae [51, 52, 71]. Although the RNA editing sites differed among the 36 protein-coding genes, NADH dehydrogenase and cytochrome c biogenesis genes were the most prevalent, which was similar to P. sibiricum. Here, RNA editing yielded mostly non-synonymous mutations and generated enzymes through the introduction of hydrophobic amino acid codons. Many sites rest in the coding regions that convert hydrophilic serine to hydrophobic leucine or phenylalanine, which influences the segment direction of transmembrane complexes (Table S14). These alterations facilitate modification of the physicochemical properties of proteins, thereby changing their stability.

Mitochondrial genomic comparison and evolutionary diversification

Plant mitogenomes have complex configurations that vary in length. Here, a comparative analysis of nine mitochondrial genomes was performed to explore gene gains or losses. Core genes were stable in terrestrial plants, although mitogenomic size, configuration, and gene content differed among the different species. This model is common in monocots [72]. The mitogenome of F. unibracteata contained 29 of the 43 core genes. In eight of the nine analyzed mitogenomes, the gene sdh originating from a eukaryotic ancestor and encoding succinate dehydrogenase that acts as a hub connecting the tricarboxylic acid cycle and the respiratory chain, was pseudogenized or lost.

Measuring the diversity of nucleotide sequences can provide valuable metric markers among heterogeneous groups and help elucidate genetic relationships [73, 74]. The atp8, rps19, cox2, atp9, and atp1 genes, which have high Pi values, may play a pivotal role in tracking the evolutionary history of the genus Fritillaria. Developing valuable markers aids in the authentication of medicinal materials and controlling their quality, particularly in the pharmaceutical market. A substantial number of repeat sequences and genes with high mitogenomic diversity can offer this resource.

In eukaryotes, plant mitochondria have undergone special evolutionary processes [75]. Paternal leakage of the organellar genome occurs in plants [76, 77]. Mitochondrial genes usually have slower nucleotide substitution rates than those in the nucleus and chloroplast [78, 79]. In F. unibracteata, all the genes were conserved under negative selection pressure, with a Ka/Ks ratio of less than 1. This result is in accordance with previous reports on flowering plants [57, 58, 80].

Mitogenome structure and size exhibit extensive variations across angiosperms. Multiple factors affect their evolutionary diversity, such as frequent homologous recombination, horizontal gene migration, repetitive sequence expansion, and non-coding region accumulation. Error-prone DNA repair mechanisms in mitochondria, relaxed selective constraints, and ecological factors further enhance mitogenomic heterogeneity. At the organismal level, the configuration of plant mitochondrial genomes maintains dynamic homeostasis among various tissues and developmental periods. However, the balance mechanism remains poorly understood [52]. At the population level, mitogenomes have a high number of genetic polymorphisms. Mutation rates and the scale of species assemblages influence mitogenomic evolution [51]. In the mitogenomes of soybeans, wild types tend to harbor a monocircular configuration. Meanwhile, bicircular arrangements predominate in cultivated accessions [56]. Low frequency illegitimate recombination mediated by short repeats forms the core driver for the interconversion between monomeric and dimeric genomic conformations [56]. The limited effective population of wild F. unibracteata weakens the power of natural selection and makes genetic drift more influential. The wild population of F. unibracteata, which was listed in the “Endangered” (EN) class on the IUCN Red List, has decreased recently owing to over-exploitation. As the population contracts and the effective size decreases, the evolutionary bottleneck effect and genetic drift have a far more pronounced effect on the mitogenome than on the nuclear genome [51]. Subsequent studies with more extensive sampling are imperative to further explore the mitogenomic evolutionary mechanisms of endangered F. unibracteata.

Conclusions

In this study, the inaugural mitogenome of F. unibracteata was sequenced and annotated. The genome possesses the typical characteristics of plant mitogenomes, including low gene density, conserved core genes, a considerable number of introns in genes, lots of RNA editing sites, and sequence migration from plastome. Besides, repeat sequences contribute to the enlarged mitogenome size more than coding genes and chloroplast-derived fragments in F. unibracteata. The results further our understanding of the features and organization of multi-chromosome mitogenomes. Also, it yields helpful information for further research in conservation, breeding, phylogenetic, and evolutionary studies.
