Ly96-mediated activation of TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma and its potential for nanoparticle-based therapy
Authors: Yixin Zhang, Sui Zheng, Xiaoqing Su, Wanrong Luo, Haifeng Tang, Shiyu Xiong, Min Tan, Baoming Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) continues to pose a chief threat to the global healthcare landscape and is characterized by scarce therapeutic options and poor clinical outcomes, especially in advanced-stage disease. Although lymphocyte antigen 96 (LY96) is associated with immunogenic cell death, its specific role in HCC progression and therapeutic potential remains unclear. To identify prospective therapeutic targets in HCC, by combining the cancer-immunity cycle score with WGCNA and systems biology methods, we identify pivotal molecular interactions. By integrating the cancer-immunity cycle score with WGCNA and systems-level approaches, we systematically identify potential therapeutic targets in HCC. We evaluate LY96 expression at the transcriptomic and proteomic levels in HCC tissues and explore its prognostic relevance by drawing upon information from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) repository. The functional role of LY96 is delineated through a panel of cellular assays conducted in vitro, complemented by in vivo tumorigenesis models. To identify the downstream signaling cascades associated with LY96, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) is performed to elucidate the implicated pathways, which are then confirmed via experimental validation. Furthermore, we employ a lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticle (NP) platform to facilitate the systemic delivery of an LY96 inhibitor and demonstrate its potential as a newly proposed intervention strategy for HCC. Clinically, marked LY96 overexpression occurs in HCC samples, where elevated LY96 expression is strongly associated with reduced overall survival (OS) among liver cancer patients. LY96 facilitates the progression of HCC via complementary in vitro and in vivo approaches. Mechanistically, LY96 induces the activation of the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling axis in HCC. For therapeutic applications, we develop a liposome-based nanoparticle system that delivers the LY96 inhibitor L6H21 to tumor cells and effectively suppresses HCC progression through a combination of in vivo and in vitro studies. Taken together, the current observations identify LY96 as a promising diagnostic indicator and a viable intervention for therapeutic modulation to improve HCC treatment. Keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, LY96, TGF-β1, Smad2/3, immunogenic cell death

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the predominant histological subtype of primary liver cancer, continues to constitute a formidable global medical concern, with projections indicating that the annual incidence of HCC will surpass one million cases worldwide by 2025 [1]. Despite notable advances in prevention and treatment, HCC continues to rank among the foremost causes of cancer-associated deaths, driven by frequent recurrence and limited therapeutic outcomes [ 2– 4]. Following the guidelines set forth by the 2022 Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) consortium, surgical strategies, including tumor resection and liver transplantation, remain central to the current therapeutic paradigm for HCC. This method is complemented by multimodal approaches, including transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), radiofrequency ablation (RFA), and systemic therapies [ 5– 9]. This multimodal approach has demonstrably extended patient survival [10]. However, there remains a substantial gap between treatment efficacy and long-term prognosis, particularly in advanced HCC, where malignancy is more aggressive [11].

The progression of HCC is frequently accompanied by complex molecular alterations, including the dysregulation of multiple genes that not only drive tumor development but also offer opportunities for prognostic assessment and targeted therapeutic strategies [12]. Consequently, a critical unmet need persists in uncovering robust biomarkers and delineating the mechanistic basis of HCC progression to advance individualized and efficacious therapeutic strategies.

Despite their clinical adoption, the therapeutic efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade modulators (ICIs) for the care of patients with advanced HCC across both frontline and subsequent lines of therapy continues to be critically examined. Despite extensive evaluation, phase III clinical trials have largely fallen short of confirming a meaningful survival advantage of ICIs in patients with unresectable HCC [ 13, 14]. When administered as monotherapy, ICIs yield an objective response rate of only 15–20% [15], a limitation attributed to the intricate interplay of multiple immune checkpoint pathways within the immunological milieu of HCC, the paucity of intertumoral immune cell infiltrates, and the excessive exhaustion of immune effector cells [ 16, 17]. Nonetheless, findings from several phase III trials indicate that compared with sorafenib monotherapy, combined administration of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 agents or antiangiogenic therapies confers superior survival benefits for unresectable HCC patients [ 18, 19]. Collectively, these insights highlight the critical importance of elucidating cancer-immune dynamics in HCC to enable the identification of innovative targetable molecules and the rational design of combination therapies.

The multistep cancer–immunity cascade, first conceptualized by Chen and Mellman in 2013, delineates a sequential, cyclic process by which the immune system identifies and eradicates malignant cells through seven key steps: (1) dissemination of tumor-associated antigens during cancer cell lysis, (2) APC-mediated presentation of tumor-derived antigens, (3) initiation and subsequent stimulation of T lymphocytes, (4) migration of T cells to tumor sites, (5) trafficking into the tumor microenvironment, (6) detection of tumor-specific epitopes, and (7) cytolytic destruction of cancer cells [20]. Therefore, identifying key regulators that impede the cancer-immunity cycle holds great promise for enhancing immunotherapeutic responses in HCC. Within this framework, we investigated immune regulatory molecules to identify potential inhibitory targets that may contribute to the disruption of the cancer-immunity cycle in HCC.

Throughout the previous ten years, the Cell Death Nomenclature Committee has systematically characterized and refined the mechanisms of cell death, scrutinizing them via an integrative approach encompassing morphological, biochemical, and functional criteria [21]. Notably, early studies of anticancer therapies laid the groundwork for the principle of immunogenic cell death (ICD), predominantly in animal models, which underscored the pivotal role of tumor-specific immunity in modulating responses to anticancer therapies [22]. The function of ICD involves triggering the host immune system, largely through the release of DAMPs, which are essential for the mobilization and stimulation of antigen-presenting cells [ 23, 24]. The induction of DAMP-mediated ICD relies on the interplay between multiple innate immune receptors, whose cooperative interactions with DAMPs are essential for eliciting ICD and mounting an effective antitumor immune response [25]. Thus, further elucidation of the relationship between ICD-associated biomarkers and HCC prognosis may provide novel insights for therapeutic intervention and prognostic assessment in HCC.

Therefore, on the basis of the cancer-immune cycle score combined with ICD-related markers, this study systematically investigated the HCC-related ICD inhibitory target LY96 in vivo and in vitro via a strategy that combines bioinformatics methods with experimental techniques. LY96, also known as myeloid differentiation factor 2 (MD-2), functions as a critical coreceptor for Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), orchestrating early immune defense mechanism activation. It facilitates the recognition of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and initiates NF-κB-mediated proinflammatory signaling pathways downstream [ 26, 27]. In addition to its well-characterized role in microbial recognition, the binding of LY96 to endogenous ligands, notably high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), has been shown to intensify inflammatory signaling and potentially contribute to immune evasion mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment [ 28, 29]. In our study, functional experiments revealed that LY96 is involved in governing the growth, motility, invasiveness, and dissemination of HCC cells. In addition, LY96 overexpression facilitates changes in EMT-related molecule expression by triggering the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling axis. Finally, we encapsulated the LY96 inhibitor via a lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticle platform (L6H-Lipo) and found that the nano formulation significantly inhibited HCC tumor growth. Overall, our data emphasize the central involvement of LY96 in HCC progression and demonstrate that an inhibitory strategy against LY96 may provide a new direction for HCC treatment.

Materials and Methods

Datasets and samples

Using the GDC data portal for retrieval, we retrieved transcriptomic and patient data related to liver cancer (TCGA-LIHC) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database ( https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). FPKM values, which represent transcript abundance, were employed in subsequent correlation analyses. Thirty-two ICD-related genes were retrieved from the relevant literature at the same time [ 29, 30]. The processing of RNA-seq data from the TCGA-LIHC cohort included the following steps: (1) Data transformation: Conversion of probe IDs to the gene symbol format; (2) Data exclusion: Samples lacking sufficient follow-up information were excluded. Following meticulous preprocessing, our analysis incorporated a comprehensive set of samples: 421 HCC samples from the TCGA dataset, encompassing 50 normal samples and 371 tumor samples. Twenty pairs of clinical patient samples were collected from 2018 to 2024. The study protocol complied with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University (ethics number: SYSU-IACUC-2024-89101).

Building weighted gene coexpression networks and key gene discovery

Using the ‘WGCNA’ R package, a weighted gene coexpression network was generated to reveal pivotal molecular relationships. Standardized transcriptomic expression data from the LIHC cohort were used as the input for WGCNA. The cancer–immunity cycle scores corresponding to the HCC samples were obtained from the TIP database ( http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/TIP/) and were incorporated into the analysis of phenotypic traits. A soft-thresholding power (β) of 5 was chosen to achieve the resulting network exhibiting scale-free properties. Initially, the dataset was reformatted into an adjacency matrix for network construction, which was subsequently used to generate a TOM (topological overlap matrix). Hierarchical clustering via average linkage, informed by topological overlap, was employed to cluster genes. Following the hybrid dynamic tree-cutting algorithm, modules were defined with a minimum gene count of 30. Module eigengenes were computed, and hierarchical clustering was conducted with a merging threshold of 0.25. Genes that remained unclassified within distinct modules were assigned to the gray module, representing an uncharacterized gene set. Correlation analyses were performed by associating module eigengenes with clinical traits, and a module-phenotype correlation heatmap was generated through GlueGO plug [31]. The modules with the strongest correlations were chosen for in-depth investigation.

Using Cytoscape (version 3.2.1), a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was systematically constructed for the target module, and the topological heterogeneity of individual nodes was subsequently assessed. Node degree values were used as a metric to assess their relative importance. Nodes with the highest degree values were identified as principal elements of the network. Concurrently, gene-module correlations were analyzed, and genes with correlation coefficients greater than 0.4 were selected. Genes that satisfied both criteria were designated hub genes. Venn diagram was generated using the online tool available at the VIB Bioinformatics Core ( http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/).

The association between LY96 expression and overall survival, as well as promoter methylation levels in HCC patients, was analyzed using the GEPIA2 online platform ( http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/), which integrates data from TCGA projects.

Cultivation of cell lines

THLE-2, a normal human liver cell line, was obtained from Procell (CL-0833; Wuhan, China). The HCC cell lines Huh7, HepG2, SK-Hep1 and MHCC97H were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). THLE-2 cells were maintained in THLE-2-specific culture medium (CM-0833; Procell) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The remaining cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Excell, Shanghai, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin and maintained under standard conditions at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. These cell lines were maintained under optimal culture conditions to preserve viability and ensure reproducibility in subsequent experiments.

Cell transfection

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) specifically targeting LY96, along with corresponding negative control siRNAs, were procured from IGE Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). Huh7 and HepG2 cells were plated in six-well plates and transfected with 50 nM siRNAs via the Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) following the recommended procedure. For overexpression studies, Huh7 and HepG2 cells were transfected with the pCDNA3.1-Homo-LY96-EFla-copGFP plasmid via Lipofectamine 3000. The specific sequences of the siRNA constructs are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Gene silencing via lentiviral infection

Lentiviral short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting LY96 (shLY96) were obtained from ICG Technology (Guangzhou, China). Liver cancer cell lines were infected with lentiviruses designed for LY96 overexpression or shRNA-mediated knockdown. Stable cell lines were established through selection with 5 μg/mL puromycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). The specific sequences of the shRNA constructs are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Quantification of gene expression via qRT-PCR

Using an RNA extraction kit (EZBioscience, Shanghai, China), total RNA was isolated and purified. Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized via reverse transcription via the Color Reverse Transcription Kit (EZBioscience) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was conducted via the use of 2× Color SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (EZBioscience) on a real-time fluorescence detection system (LightCycler 480 System; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) to ensure precise amplification and quantification. The qPCR cycling and melting curve programs were set as per the supplier’s guidelines. The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table 1.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular and tissue proteins were isolated via radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (CWBIO, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Ncmbio, Suzhou, China). A BCA protein assay kit (CW0014; CWBIO) was used to quantify the total protein content. Thirty micrograms of protein per sample were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes with a transfer system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA), with the transfer time adjusted to align with the molecular weight of the specific protein. The membrane was then blocked with 5× protein-free fast blocking buffer (ES-8715; ECOtop Corporation, Guangzhou, China) for 15 min, and then incubated with primary antibodies, including antibodies against LY96 (YN2063, 1:800; Immunoway, Plano, USA; 11784-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), TGF-β1 (YM8257, 1:1000; Immunoway), Smad2/3 (YT4332, 1:1000; Immunoway), p-Smad2/3 (YP0362, 1:1000; Immunoway), β-actin (AP0060, 1:1000; Bioworld, City of Dublin, USA), and GAPDH (BS0023-T, 1:10,000; Bioworld), followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG or goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (RM1002, RM1003; Ray Antibodies, Shanghai, China). Protein bands were detected via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Ncmbio), and images were captured via a Syngene G: BOX Chemi XT4 imaging system (Syngene, Cambridge, UK).

Immunocytochemistry (IHC) assay

Tissue sections embedded in paraffin were processed through conventional deparaffinization and rehydration steps. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (C1032; Solarbio, Beijing, China) via microwave heating, after which the samples were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) to block endogenous peroxidase. To reduce nonspecific binding, the sections were blocked with normal goat serum before being incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4°C. After the samples were returned to room temperature the next day, appropriate amounts of secondary antibodies were added to completely cover the tissues, which were subsequently incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The staining intensity was visualized via DAB chromogen (DA1010; Solarbio) under a microscope. Then, nuclear staining was performed, and the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min, rinsed in running water for 1–2 min, and returned to blue. Dehydration was performed via the addition of increasing concentrations of ethanol, followed by clearing in xylene. The primary antibodies used were anti-LY96 (11784-1-AP, 1:100) and anti-Ki-67 (ab15580, 1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) antibodies.

Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Yongjin Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China), the cells on glass slides were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Biosharp, Hefei, China). After a 30-min blocking step with normal goat serum at room temperature, the blocking solution was discarded, and 150 μL of fluorescent primary antibody was added, followed by incubation at 4°C overnight. The next day, the cells were incubated with 150 μL of fluorescent secondary antibody for 1 h. The cells were subsequently counterstained with 1× DAPI staining solution (C0065; Solarbio) for 5–10 min in the dark to stain the nuclei. Finally, the cell surface was covered with an anti-fluorescence quencher, and images were acquired via a fluorescence microscope. The antibodies used included those against N-cadherin (ET1607-37; HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), E-cadherin (20874-1-AP; Proteintech), SNAIL (ER1706-22; HUABIO), and SLUG (HA722828; HUABIO).

Cell viability assay

The Huh7 and HepG2 cell suspensions were adjusted to densities of 5000 and 2000 cells/mL, respectively, and plated evenly into 96-well plates at 100 μL per well. Following a 24-h incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 (C0005; TargetMol, Shanghai, China) was added to each well. The samples were incubated at 37°C for 4 h to facilitate cell attachment and growth (Huh7 cells) or 2 h (HepG2 cells). Measurements of cell absorbance at 450 nm were performed with a SYNERGY H1 Gen5™ multifunction microplate spectrophotometer (Bio-Tek, Winooski, USA).

Assessment of clonogenic potential

Each well of a six-well plate was seeded with 1000 cells and maintained in 2 mL of DMEM containing 10% FBS. The plates were cultured for 10–12 days at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ humidified environment, with the culture medium refreshed every two days. The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature following a 10-day incubation period and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Under microscopic examination, colonies exceeding 50 cells were enumerated via light microscopy.

In vitro scratch assay

The migratory potential of the cells was examined via a wound healing assay. The cells were seeded at a density near 3 × 10 5 cells per well for subsequent assays, according to the experimental design. Once the cells reached confluence, a vertical scratch was made across the well via a 200-μL sterile pipette tip. The medium was then discarded, and detached cells were removed by gently washing the cultures two to three times with PBS. New serum-free medium was carefully added to the cultures, and images were captured at the 0-h time point via an inverted fluorescence microscope. At the desired time point (24 h), the scratch width was examined under an inverted microscope (IX73; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and photographed. ImageJ software was used to measure the scratch distance.

Evaluation of cell migration and invasion capabilities

Transwell chambers (BIOFIL, Guangzhou, China) and Matrigel (Corning, Corning, USA) were used to assess the migratory and invasive capacities of the cells. The cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and carefully loaded into the upper chamber, with the lower chamber containing 15% FBS-enriched medium serving as a chemoattractant. The upper chamber’s lower surface was pretreated with a 1:10 dilution of Matrigel in serum-deprived medium for the invasion assay. After 48 h of incubation and washing with PBS, the Transwell inserts were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min. The underside of the membrane was examined under a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) to visualize migratory and invasive cells, which were subsequently quantified via ImageJ.

Apoptotic cell analysis

An annexin V-FITC/PI dual-staining assay kit (E-CK-A211; Elabscience Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) was used to assess apoptosis. Huh7 and HepG2 cells were subjected to treatment following the experimental protocol to induce apoptosis. Following centrifugation, the cells were washed twice with PBS and immediately stained with 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and PI reagents. The cell suspension was gently mixed and then incubated in a dark room. Apoptotic cell populations were then analyzed via flow cytometry.

In vivo mouse model

Male BALB/c nude mice (aged 4–6 weeks and weighing 20–25 g) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University and kept in SPF facilities. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines set forth by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University (Ethics Number: SYSU-IACUC-2023-002051). For the subcutaneous xenograft model, mice were randomly assigned to four groups and inoculated subaxillary with Huh7 cells transfected with sh-NC, sh-LY96, vector, or overexpression-LY96 (OE-LY96). After four weeks, the mice were euthanized, and the tumor tissues were excised to determine the tumor weight. For the orthotopic liver tumor model, a 25-μL suspension of cells (5 × 10 6 cells/mL) was injected beneath the liver capsule of each mouse. After six weeks, the mice were sacrificed, and the livers were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and prepared for H&E staining and subsequent evaluation.

Preparation of lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticles for LY96 inhibitor (L6H21) delivery

L6H21-loaded liposomal nanoparticles were fabricated via thin-film hydration in this study. A total of 36.88 mg of DPPC, 3.11 mg of S-lysoPC (16:0), 1.21 mg of cholesterol, and 8.8 mg of DSPE-mPEG2000 were dissolved in 5 mL of chloroform within a 50 mL round-bottom flask by vortexing. Three milligrams of L6H21 (L90004; Saitong Biotechnology, Yantai, China) was dissolved in 5 mL of chloroform and subsequently added to the lipid mixture. The combined solution was subjected to ultrasonic agitation for 30 s to ensure homogeneity. The mixture was then evaporated via a rotary evaporator (N-1300D-WB; EYELA, Tokyo, Japan) under vacuum at 37°C and 15.4 g, forming a uniform, opalescent lipid membrane film along the flask’s inner wall. To ensure the complete removal of residual organic solvents, the film was further dried under vacuum overnight. Twenty-four hours later, the flask was removed, and 2 mL of ultrapure water (pH 7.4) was added to hydrate under gentle stirring. The flask was then transferred to an ultrasonic cell disruptor and ultrasonicated at 300 W for 10 min to generate drug-loaded multilamellar liposomes. The resulting suspension was filtered through a 220-nm membrane and extruded via a liposome extruder (LF-1; Avestin, Ottawa, Canada) to obtain nanoparticles with a uniform size distribution. To remove unencapsulated drug residues, the suspension was subjected to dialysis against ultrapure water through a 3.5-kDa molecular weight cutoff membrane (3306577, Spectra/Por®; Spectrum Labs, Los Angeles, USA) for 48 h, with the water replaced every 4 h. The final L6H21-loaded liposomes (L6H-loaded liposomes) were stored at 4°C until further use.

For fluorescence-based tracking studies, Cy5 dye was co-encapsulated into L6H21-loaded liposomes. Specifically, 1.5 mg of Cy5 (GC35769; Glpbio, Montclair, USA) was dissolved in 5 mL of chloroform and thoroughly mixed with DPPC, S-lysoPC (16:0), cholesterol, DSPE-mPEG2000, and L6H21 prior to film formation. The resulting lipid mixture was subjected to rotary evaporation and vacuum drying to yield a uniform lipid film. Hydration, sonication, membrane filtration, and extrusion were then performed following the previously described protocol to generate Cy5/L6H21-coloaded liposomes (Cy5/L6H21-lipo). The final formulation was stored until further use in the cellular uptake and biodistribution assays.

Physicochemical characterization of L6H21-loaded nanoparticles

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis of L6H21-loaded nanoparticles was performed at 25°C using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK) to determine the particle size and distribution. Zeta potential measurements were also conducted to assess the surface charge characteristics. The morphological structure of the nanoparticles was visualized via a Talos F200S G2 transmission electron microscope (TEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cellular uptake assay

The cells (Huh7) were dispensed in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cy5-labeled L6H-lipo NPs or free Cy5-labeled L6H21 were then added at an L6H21 concentration of 10 μM. The cells were then incubated with Cy5-labeled L6H21 or L6H-lipo NPs for 0.5, 2, or 4 h. Following incubation, the harvested cell populations were evaluated via flow cytometry on a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, USA).

Assessment of encapsulation efficiency

The encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of L6H-lipo was evaluated by quantifying the free drug fraction via centrifugal ultrafiltration. Drug release profile analysis

To evaluate the pH-responsive release behavior of L6H21 from liposomes, dialysis-based in vitro release experiments were conducted. L6H-lipo samples containing equivalent amounts of L6H21 were enclosed within dialysis tubes with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 3.5 kDa and immersed in 25 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4, 6.5, or 5.0. The system was maintained at 37°C with constant shaking. At specified intervals (0–72 h), an aliquot of 1 mL of the release medium was withdrawn and immediately replenished with an equal volume of fresh buffer. The amount of L6H21 released was quantified via HPLC, and the cumulative percentage of L6H21 released was subsequently calculated.

Tumor inhibition study in a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model

Mice maintained under SPF conditions were used to establish a subcutaneous xenograft model. Huh7 cells (5 × 10 6) suspended in 150 μL of a PBS/Matrigel mixture (1:1) were inoculated subcutaneously into the right thigh of each mouse. When the tumors reached approximately 100 mm³ in size (approximately Day 7), the animals were randomly allocated into three experimental groups, with five mice per group: a control group receiving PBS, a naked drug L6H21 group, and a treatment group receiving L6H21-loaded nanoparticles (NPs). The L6H21 nanoparticles were administered via intravenous injection through the tail vein at 20 mg/kg every two days for a total of three rounds. Caliper measurements were performed every four days, and the tumor volume was estimated via the formula (length × width 2)/2. Five days after the end of treatment, the mice were euthanized, and the tumor tissues were excised for further analysis. Concurrently, the primary organs of the mice were excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Following paraffin embedding, tissue sections were prepared and subsequently stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (G1120; Solarbio), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The whole blood sample was incubated at room temperature for 2 h, centrifuge at 15.6 g for 15 min at 4°C. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected and the biochemical markers ALT (R01502), AST (R01702), creatinine (R05602), and urea (R02902) (Rayto, Shenzhen, China) were analyzed accordingly.

Data analysis and statistics

The data were statistically analyzed via R (v4.2.3) and GraphPad Prism (v9.0). The data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between two groups were analyzed via either paired or unpaired Student’s t tests, as appropriate, whereas comparisons across multiple groups were made via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

LY96 is a candidate target for modulating immune suppression in HCC

To identify immunoregulatory targets that may contribute to immune evasion in HCC, we conducted a WGCNA using cancer immune cycle scores and HCC transcriptomic data. Hierarchical clustering classified all genes into 25 distinct modules, revealing two major clusters with distinct patterns of gene expression ( Figure 1A). Analysis of correlations revealed a positive association between Cluster 1 and cancer immunity cycle steps 1–4, whereas Cluster 2 exhibited a negative correlation with steps 5–7 ( Figure 1B). Among these modules, the green and blue modules in Cluster 1 and the blue module in Cluster 2 presented the most significant differences in expression. Gene Ontology annotation analysis [32] of the green module revealed enrichment in immune-related signaling pathways, particularly those linked to the early stages of immune activation (steps 1–4) in the cancer-immunity cycle ( Figure 1C). Given its strong association with immune activation, we focused on this module to identify potential therapeutic targets. Further screening for core immunogenic cell death (ICD) genes led to the identification of CXCR4 and LY96 as key candidates ( Figure 1D–F) Previous studies have demonstrated that CXCR4 blockade, in combination with PD-1 inhibition, enhances intratumoral dendritic cell activation and promotes antitumor immunity in HCC [33]. Additionally, the accumulation of aged CXCR4 + neutrophils has been linked to increased immunosuppressive effects and poor patient outcomes [34]. Given these findings, we focused on LY96 as a potential immunosuppressive regulator.

To validate the immunological relevance of LY96, we performed RT-qPCR on 10 mouse HCC samples, which revealed positive correlations between LY96 expression and both CD4 (R = 0.8909, P < 0.001) and CD8 (R = 0.6822, P = 0.0297) levels ( Figure 1G–J). However, these markers do not distinguish between functionally active ( e.g., IFN-γ⁺, Granzyme B⁺) and exhausted ( e. g., PD-1⁺, TIM-3⁺) T-cell subsets.

Next, we performed correlation analyses between LY96 and multiple immune exhaustion markers. As revealed in Figure 1K, LY96 expression is strongly and positively correlated with HAVCR2 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.559, P = 7.4e–42) and moderately correlated with LAG3 (ρ = 0.201, P = 9.8e–05) and PDCD1 (ρ = 0.332, P = 5.54e–11). These findings suggest that although LY96 expression may coincide with increased T-cell infiltration, these T cells are likely to be in an exhausted or immunosuppressed state, providing mechanistic support for the protumor role of LY96 within the HCC immune microenvironment.

In summary, our integrative bioinformatics analysis, combined with preliminary experimental validation, identified LY96 as a potential target in liver cancer. Its correlation with CD4/CD8 markers further implicates LY96 in the regulation of the tumor immune microenvironment, highlighting its potential role in immune evasion.

Elevated LY96 expression in HCC predicts adverse outcomes

To elucidate the involvement of LY96 in HCC, we first examined its expression across various tumor types and observed significant upregulation in LIHC relative to matched neighboring nontumorous tissues ( Figure 2A). This discovery was subsequently validated via the use of independent datasets, which consistently demonstrated elevated LY96 mRNA expression in LIHC samples ( Figure 2B,C). Next, we assessed the prognostic significance of LY96 in HCC through Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of TCGA data. Patients with high LY96 expression had significantly poorer survival outcomes than did those with low LY96 expression ( Figure 2D), indicating a potential association between LY96 expression and poor prognosis. In light of the critical function of DNA methylation in regulating gene expression, we further examined LY96 methylation levels in LIHC. Our analysis revealed a general reduction in LY96 DNA methylation in LIHC, with the most pronounced decrease observed in stage IV tumors ( Figure 2E), suggesting a potential epigenetic mechanism contributing to LY96 overexpression in advanced HCC. To substantiate these findings through protein-level analyses, we conducted IHC analysis in both HCC patient samples and mouse orthotopic tumor models. The IHC results aligned with those of RT-qPCR, reinforcing the reliability and consistency of our findings ( Figure 2F–I). Overall, our integrative analysis highlights the upregulation of LY96 in HCC and its link to unfavorable clinical outcomes, shedding light on its potential use as a dual marker of prognosis and a target for therapy.

LY96 knockdown suppresses the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cells

We first assessed its expression in physiologically normal livers and four HCC cell lines. LY96 was highly expressed in Huh7 and HepG2 cells ( Figure 3A,E), which were selected for further study. LY96 knockdown via siRNAs (si-LY96-1 and si-LY96-2) was confirmed via western blot analysis ( Figure 3B–D). Functionally, LY96 knockdown markedly inhibited cell proliferation, as evidenced by the results of the CCK-8 and colony formation assays ( Figure 3F,G,L,M). Wound healing and Transwell assays further indicated that LY96 silencing reduced both the migration and invasion capacities of HCC cells ( Figure 3H–K). Additionally, LY96 knockdown increased the percentage of apoptotic cells ( Figure 3N,O), indicating its role in promoting tumor cell survival.

Tumor growth was significantly slower in the LY96-knockdown group than in the control group, with a significant reduction in tumor size and mass ( Figure 3P–R). Further analysis of EMT markers in tumor tissues revealed that LY96 knockdown decreased SLUG, N-cadherin and SNAIL expression levels while increasing E-cadherin level ( Figure 4A–E), indicating that EMT was suppressed. Additionally, Ki67 staining revealed a lower proportion of proliferative cells in LY96-knockdown tumors ( Figure 4F,G).

LY96 overexpression promotes proliferation, migration and invasion in HCC

To further investigate the role of LY96 in HCC, we transfected Huh7 and HepG2 cells with a pCDNA3.1-based plasmid encoding full-length LY96 to induce overexpression. The significant increase in LY96 expression in the examined cell lines was validated by RT-qPCR and western blot analysis ( Figure 5A,B). Functionally, LY96 overexpression significantly increased HCC cell viability and proliferation, as supported by the results of the CCK-8 and colony formation assays ( Figure 5C,H). Transwell assays further revealed that LY96-overexpressing cells presented increased migration and invasion abilities ( Figure 5D–G). Moreover, the level of apoptosis was lower in LY96-overexpressing cells than in control cells ( Figure 5I), indicating that LY96 plays a role in promoting tumor cell survival. To validate these findings in vivo, we established a mouse xenograft model using LY96-overexpressing and control cells. Tumors derived from LY96-overexpressing cells exhibited significantly faster growth, along with increased tumor volume and mass upon resection ( Figure 5J–L). Overall, these results confirm that LY96 overexpression enhances HCC proliferation, migration, and invasion, supporting its oncogenic role in liver cancer progression.

LY96 affects the malignant proliferation of HCC cells by influencing the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling axis

To clarify the role of LY96 in the mechanism of HCC progression, HCC samples were stratified into high and low LY96 expression groups according to the median LY96 expression level. GSEA revealed significant enrichment of LY96 in the TGF-β1 signaling pathway, suggesting that this pathway may be involved in LY96-mediated biological effects ( Figure 6A). To test this hypothesis, we examined TGF-β1 pathway-related protein expression in liver cancer cells following LY96 knockdown or overexpression. Western blot analysis revealed that in both Huh7 and HepG2 cells, LY96 knockdown reduced the expression levels of TGF-β1 and p-Smad2/3, whereas LY96 overexpression increased their levels. Notably, there were no observable changes in the total protein levels of Smad2 and Smad3 ( Figure 6B–F). These data demonstrate that LY96 may mediate its biological functions via the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling axis.

TGF-β1 overexpression rescues the antitumor effects induced by LY96 knockdown

To further validate the role of the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 pathway in LY96 knockdown-mediated tumor suppression, we performed a rescue experiment by overexpressing TGF-β1 in LY96-knockdown HepG2 cells via transfection with a TGF-β1 overexpression plasmid. Western blot analysis confirmed that LY96 knockdown led to a reduction in p-Smad2/3 activation, while TGF-β1 overexpression effectively restored Smad2/3 activation ( Figure 6G). Although TGF-β1 overexpression partially restored the viability and invasive potential of LY96-deficient cells, LY96 knockdown still significantly inhibited colony formation, migration, and invasion ( Figure 6H–L). Collectively, these findings indicate that the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling pathway is a pivotal mediator of LY96-driven HCC tumor progression.

Nanoparticle-based LY96 inhibitor delivery for HCC treatment

While small-molecule inhibitors hold promise for cancer therapy, their clinical application is often hindered by poor solubility, rapid clearance, and off-target effects. To improve the therapeutic potential of LY96 inhibition in HCC, we developed a lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticle (L6H-lipo) encapsulating the LY96 inhibitor L6H21 for targeted drug delivery ( Figure 7A). The self-assembled L6H-lipoform exhibited a uniform spherical morphology (~140 nm) ( Figure 7B,C) with a stable negative zeta potential ( Figure 7D), ensuring effective drug loading and prolonged stability under physiological conditions ( Figure 7E). Following the incorporation of varying amounts of L6H21 during liposome formulation, the encapsulation efficiency stabilized at approximately 45% ( Figure 7F). This relatively moderate encapsulation rate may be attributed to transient disruption or flipping of the phospholipid bilayer during the hydration and ultrasonication steps. In parallel, in vitro release assays demonstrated markedly enhanced release of L6H21 under acidic conditions (pH 5.0), indicative of the pH-responsive behavior of the nanoparticles within the acidic tumor microenvironment ( Figure 7 G).

Cellular uptake studies demonstrated that Cy5-labeled L6H-loaded L6H was efficiently internalized by HCC cells, with significantly greater fluorescence intensity than free L6H21 ( Figure 7H). The results of functional assays revealed that L6H-loaded liposomes had superior cytotoxic effects on HCC cells, significantly reducing cell viability but effectively downregulating LY96 expression ( Figure 7I,J). In vivo biodistribution analysis further confirmed the enhanced tumor accumulation of L6H-loaded liposomes, highlighting their tumor-targeting ability ( Figure 7L–M). To assess the antitumor efficacy of L6H-lipo, we administered L6H-lipo via systemic injection to a liver cancer mouse model ( Figure 7K). Compared with the control and free L6H21 treatments, L6H-Lipo markedly reduced tumor growth, with no adverse effects on body weight, suggesting good tolerability. Furthermore, histopathological examination and blood analysis confirmed minimal systemic toxicity, with no apparent abnormalities in major organs or alterations in liver and kidney function markers ( Figure 7N–Q).

Overall, our findings demonstrate that L6H-loaded liposomes effectively enhance LY96 inhibitor delivery, improving both its cellular uptake and therapeutic efficacy while maintaining its biocompatibility. This nanoparticle-based strategy offers a compelling strategy for targeted HCC management.

Discussion

In the present study, LY96 was significantly overexpressed in HCC, a conclusion reinforced by further investigation of independent clinical cohorts. Mechanistically, LY96 was shown to activate the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling axis, thereby promoting the progression of HCC. From a translational therapy perspective, we employed a nanoplatform to demonstrate that LY96 may serve as an effective target for therapeutic intervention for HCC treatment.

Myeloid differentiation factor 2 (MD2), encoded by the LY96 gene, is a low-molecular-weight glycoprotein that is primarily localized in macrophages and dendritic cells [26]. As an indispensable coreceptor for TLR4, MD2 is essential for receptor activation and the initiation of downstream signaling cascades [35]. LY96 is overexpressed in multiple cancer types, such as prostate, thyroid, and kidney cancers. Its dysregulated expression is frequently correlated with poor patient prognosis, highlighting its potential role in tumor progression and disease outcomes [ 36– 38]. Moreover, multiple studies have indicated a connection between LY96 expression and the initiation and progression of tumors [39]. In cases of gastric cancer, LY96 has been shown to trigger macrophage-induced activation of the NF-kB and STAT3 pathways, promoting the advancement of tumors [40]. The LY96-TLR4 complex promotes increased release of proinflammatory cytokines and adhesion factors, leading to expedited progression of colon cancer and metastasis to the lungs [41]. Therefore, dysregulated LY96 expression can drive cancer progression by promoting tumor cell proliferation, invasion and metastasis. This study revealed that increased LY96 expression is associated with poor prognosis in HCC patients and is positively related to HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Furthermore, LY96 expression was significantly associated with increased levels of TGF-β1 and phosphorylated Smad2/3, suggesting a potential regulatory interaction. Collectively, these results indicate that LY96 facilitates HCC progression, at least in part, by activating the TGF-β1 signaling pathway. However, the mechanism at the molecular level through which LY96 regulates the TGF-β pathway remains unclear. LY96 functions as a coreceptor for TLR4 and participates in innate immune signaling. Activation of the LY96/TLR4 complex can trigger downstream NF-κB or AP-1 signaling, which has been shown to transcriptionally upregulate TGF-β1 expression in various pathological contexts, such as liver injury, pulmonary fibrosis, and tumor progression. We therefore hypothesize that LY96 may promote TGF-β1 expression via the TLR4/NF-κB axis, subsequently activating Smad2/3 phosphorylation and mediating protumorigenic effects.

The TGF-β family constitutes the most diverse and widely distributed group of cytokines in the human body, with expression found across nearly all tissue and cell lineages [42]. This superfamily comprises 33 multifunctional factors, with TGF-β isoforms I–III as representative members; among them, TGF-β1 is the most abundant and extensively studied [43]. The TGF-β receptor family consists of three isoforms, types I, II, and III, where types I and II are single-pass transmembrane glycoproteins possessing serine/threonine kinase activity, whereas type III is a proteoglycan devoid of serine kinase activity function [44]. TGF-β plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis, exhibiting a well-characterized dual function in cancer, a phenomenon also evident in HCC [45]. In HCC, the reduced activity of the TGF-β signaling axis may indicate a loss of its tumor-suppressive role, whereas its increased activity can promote fibrosis and inflammation. Approximately 40% of HCC patients harbor somatic mutations in at least one component of the TGF-β signaling pathway [46]. Increasing evidence indicates that TGF-β1 signaling plays a crucial role in cancer pathogenesis by modulating cell migration, the tumor microenvironment, and metastatic potential [ 47, 48]. In this study, LY96 upregulation led to increased expression of TGF-β1 and p-Smad2/3, accompanied by elevated N-cadherin level and a reduction in E-cadherin expression. These findings suggest that LY96 exerts its oncogenic effects, at least in part, by modulating the TGF-β1/p-Smad2/3 axis in HCC.

Interestingly, LY96 expression was positively correlated with CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration. Our subsequent analyses revealed that these infiltrating T cells were not indicative of effective antitumor immunity in HCC tissues. Instead, LY96 expression was concurrently associated with elevated levels of immunosuppressive molecules such as PD-1, CTLA-4, and TIGIT, suggesting that the infiltrated T cells may be in a functionally exhausted or suppressive state. This implies a paradoxical scenario in which LY96 may facilitate immune cell recruitment but impair their effector functions through the induction of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Moreover, LY96 has been reported to enhance TLR4-mediated signaling, which, in the context of chronic activation, can contribute to immune escape via the upregulation of IL-10, TGF-β, and regulatory myeloid populations. These findings collectively support a dual role for LY96 in promoting tumor progression despite its association with the presence of T cells, highlighting the complexity of immune regulation in HCC.

Our analyses revealed a positive correlation between LY96 expression and CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration in HCC tissues. However, our experiments consistently demonstrated a tumor-promoting role of LY96, characterized by increased proliferation and migration and reduced apoptosis of HCC cells. This discrepancy suggests that increased immune infiltration does not necessarily indicate effective immune activation or tumor suppression. We hypothesize that tumor-infiltrating CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells may be functionally exhausted, spatially excluded from tumor nests, or skewed toward regulatory/suppressive subtypes. Future single-cell or spatial transcriptomic profiling is needed to clarify immune cell functionality and localization relative to LY96 expression. LY96 acts as a coreceptor of TLR4 and is implicated in LPS-induced NF-κB activation and cytokine production. Aberrant activation of this pathway can foster a chronic inflammatory environment that promotes tumor progression, angiogenesis, and immune evasion. These findings imply that although LY96 expression coincides with immune infiltration, its tumor-promoting effect may stem from immune dysfunction rather than activation. These findings highlight LY96/TLR4 as a potential therapeutic axis. Targeting LY96 may reshape the immune microenvironment and enhance responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors or TLR4 antagonists.

Despite the promise of transformational therapy, effective pharmacological options remain limited. The clinical efficacy of targeted therapies for advanced liver cancer is often hindered by undesirable side effects and the development of drug resistance [42]. Recent advancements in liposomal nanomedicines, characterized by their robust efficacy and safety profiles, highlight the potential of liposome-based nanoparticle platforms for the targeted delivery of LY96 inhibitors.

Lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticles combine the benefits of lipid-like materials and polymers, offering distinct advantages. Unlike drug‒lipid complexes or polymer‒drug complexes, the solid polymer‒cationic lipid core of our hybrid NPs ensures a stable structure for LY96 inhibitor encapsulation, providing better protection against degradation. Moreover, the PEG shell extends the circulation time of the hybrid NPs in the bloodstream, potentially enhancing their accumulation at tumor sites through EPR-mediated accumulation. In this study, we constructed a liposome-based polymer nanoplatform that is capable of delivering the LY96 inhibitor drug L6H21 to liver cancer cells, thereby successfully inhibiting liver cancer growth and metastasis in vivo. These findings indicate that LY96 is an effective target for liver cancer treatment and has potential for achieving clinical translational treatment.

While this study presents important insights, we acknowledge its limitations with respect to equal candor. Although our findings demonstrate that L6H21-loaded nanoparticles effectively suppress LY96 expression and attenuate HCC progression, the underlying mechanism by which L6H21 downregulates LY96 expression remains to be elucidated. It is currently unclear whether this regulation occurs at the transcriptional level through posttranslational modifications such as enhanced protein degradation or via the modulation of upstream signaling pathways. In future investigations, we aim to dissect these possibilities and delineate the specific molecular mechanisms governing LY96 suppression by L6H21. This mechanistic understanding will be critical in refining LY96-targeted strategies and advancing their translational application in HCC.

Additionally, we adopted a coencapsulation strategy involving Cy5 and L6H21 to enable fluorescence-based tracking while avoiding potential alterations in the physicochemical properties of the small-molecule drug caused by covalent conjugation. Although this method offers good biocompatibility and structural preservation, Cy5 is not directly linked to L6H21, and its intracellular release and localization behaviors may differ. Therefore, the fluorescence signal from Cy5 primarily reflects the overall uptake and distribution of the liposomal formulation rather than the precise dynamics of L6H21 itself. In future studies, alternative strategies such as click chemistry or FRET-based approaches may be considered to improve tracking specificity and resolution.

In conclusion, LY96 is a promising therapeutic target for HCC, and elevated LY96 expression is associated with poor prognosis and enhanced tumor progression. LY96 drives HCC progression by activating the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling axis, influencing key processes in HCC. Additionally, the use of a liposome-based nanoplatform for the targeted delivery of LY96 inhibitors demonstrates its potential for effective liver cancer treatment. These findings highlight the critical role of LY96 in HCC pathogenesis and highlight its clinical translational potential.
