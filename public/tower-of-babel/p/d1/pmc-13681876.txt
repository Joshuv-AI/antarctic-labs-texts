Frailty transitions and the risk of multisystem burden of chronic disease: a prospective cohort study in China
Authors: Jian-Cheng Wu, Tingting Weng, Qiang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty, defined by a decline in physiological reserve, is a key determinant of adverse health outcomes in older populations. While baseline frailty has been shown to predict the development of chronic diseases, the impact of changes in frailty status over time, particularly in rapidly ageing populations like China, remains poorly understood. This study aimed to assess how baseline frailty and its longitudinal transitions influence the incidence of chronic diseases across multiple organ systems.MethodsWe analysed data from 17,708 adults (aged ≥ 40) in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) collected between 2011 and 2018. Frailty was assessed using the Rockwood Frailty Index at baseline and at two-year follow-up, categorised as stable, improved, or worsened. We applied Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the onset of 13 major chronic diseases spanning cardiovascular, metabolic, respiratory, neurological, psychiatric, digestive, renal, and musculoskeletal systems.ResultsThe study included 9,807 to 16,882 participants across 13 disease-specific subgroups, with a median follow-up period of 6.2–6.8 years for baseline frailty status and 4.4–5.0 years for frailty transitions. Frail individuals were typically older, more likely to be female, unmarried, with lower education, higher BMI, and reported fewer hours of sleep. Baseline frailty was associated with significantly higher risks for all 13 chronic diseases, with the strongest association observed for psychiatric problems (HR 5.17, 95% CI 3.24–8.24). Worsening frailty over time was linked to elevated risks, particularly for asthma (HR 3.91, 95% CI 1.28–11.91), while improvements in frailty showed reduced risks, notably for kidney diseases (HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.22–0.63). The association was particularly strong in adults aged 40–65 years, indicating greater potential for risk modification in midlife.ConclusionsFrailty is a dynamic and modifiable predictor of multisystem chronic disease risk. Changes in frailty status offer valuable insights into future disease development, especially among middle-aged adults. Routine frailty monitoring in clinical settings may offer a strategic opportunity to delay or prevent the onset of chronic diseases through timely interventions. These findings emphasize the need for incorporating frailty-targeted strategies into public health and clinical care policies to enhance ageing-related disease prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13690-026-01970-8.

Background

As the global population ages, frailty has emerged as a critical biomarker of biological aging, characterized by diminished physiological reserves and heightened vulnerability to disability, hospitalization, and mortality [1–3]. Frailty is well documented to broadly increase the risk of adverse health outcomes, including falls [4], disability [5], all-cause mortality [6, 7], and to predict multiple age-related diseases [8]. While frailty has been linked to poor outcomes in specific diseases, emerging evidence suggests that it is a reversible state, which can improve recovery from physical illness [9, 10].

However, most existing evidence relies on static assessments of frailty, overlooking its dynamic nature and temporal evolution. A critical unresolved question is whether frailty functions as a precursor that actively drives chronic disease development, or as a dynamic marker reflecting recovery capacity and physiological resilience. This distinction is fundamental: if frailty precedes disease onset, it may represent a modifiable upstream determinant; if it reflects adaptive reserve, it may instead capture the organism’s response to accumulating pathology. Disentangling these roles requires longitudinal modeling that separates frailty progression from incident disease, thereby clarifying directionality and minimizing reverse causation. Emerging evidence indicates that frailty and pre-frailty are already prevalent among middle adults (37–65 years) and are associated with increased risks of multimorbidity and mortality [7]. Moreover, whether these dynamics differ across midlife and older adulthood remains insufficiently understood.

By adopting a longitudinal approach, this study enables a more precise delineation of the temporal dynamics of frailty and its health consequences, while reducing the risk of reverse causation by disentangling frailty progression from disease onset [11]. Additionally, the unique socio-economic and healthcare context in China, where disparities in healthcare access, regional differences, and familial support systems significantly influence frailty and chronic disease trajectories, further highlights the need for region-specific research [12].

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) provides an unprecedented opportunity to address these gaps through its nationally representative cohort of 17,708 adults with repeated frailty assessments [13]. This study pioneers a multidimensional approach to examine frailty status and it’s evolving relationship with 13 categories of chronic diseases spanning multiple organ systems (Fig. 1), including cardiovascular diseases (heart disease, hypertension), respiratory diseases (asthma and other chronic lung diseases), endocrine and metabolic disorders (diabetes, dyslipidemia), digestive system diseases (digestive diseases, liver disease), urinary system disorders (kidney disease), nervous system conditions (memory-related diseases, psychiatric problems, and stroke), as well as musculoskeletal diseases (arthritis). Using joint modeling of longitudinal frailty trajectories and time-to-event data, we analyze in three key ways: 1) To quantify the prognostic value of baseline frailty severity for subsequent disease risk; 2) To evaluate the impact of longitudinal changes in frailty on the risk of disease onset; and 3) To identify age-specific windows of vulnerability (40–65 vs > 65 years) for effective frailty intervention using stratified survival analyses. Our findings aim to reconceptualize frailty not merely as a static outcome marker, but as a dynamic modulator of disease risk over time. By providing empirical evidence to support age-stratified prevention strategies, this study contributes to a shift towards precision public health. Methods

Study design and population

The CHARLS is a prospective, nationally representative cohort study conducted in China [13], with detailed study designs outlined in the supplementary information. In this study, wave1 (2011) of CHARLS was regarded as the baseline. Wave2 (2013) of CHARLS was regarded as the second survey. We used data from the wave1 and the wave2 to evaluate the dynamic transitions of frailty status. Subsequent follow-up surveys were used to follow the outcome until the final follow-up survey which was wave4 (2018) of CHARLS. The CHARLS study was approved by the Ethics Review Committees of Peking University, and informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Supplementary Figure illustrates the study population selection process using a flow diagram. First, we excluded 123 participants with missing frailty index (FI) data at baseline. For each of the 13 disease-specific analyses, we then excluded participants who had the specific disease at baseline or were lost to follow-up. Consequently, the number of participants excluded at this stage ranged from 232 to 4,959 across the different diseases, reflecting the varying baseline prevalence of each condition. To mitigate reverse causality, we applied a 1-year landmark analysis by further excluding 435 to 1,276 participants who experienced the outcome event within the first year of follow-up, as well as those aged < 40 years. This resulted in a final sample size ranging from 11,719 to 16,882 eligible participants for the baseline frailty status analyses across the 13 diseases. For the analysis of frailty status transitions, we additionally excluded 1,912 to 2,180 participants with missing FI data at wave2, leading to a final sample size ranging from 9,807 to 14,702 participants for these longitudinal analyses.

Calculation of FI

We used the FI based on the Rockwood cumulative deficit model [14], because it conceptualizes frailty as a multidimensional accumulation of health deficits across multiple systems, making it well-suited for studying multisystem chronic disease burden and detecting subtle changes in frailty over time. FI was calculated by accumulating multiple age-related health deficits, following standard procedures described in previous studies [14, 15]. A total of 30 items were used to construct the FI (Supplementary Tables S1), including disease, symptoms, disability, physical function, depression, and cognition. For analyses involving conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, stroke, arthritis, chronic lung disease, psychiatric problems, and memory-related diseases, the corresponding disease items were excluded, and the remaining 29 items were used to calculate the FI. Items 1–3 were categorized as ordinal variables with the following scores: Excellent = 0; Very good = 0.25; Good = 0.5; Fair = 0.75; Poor = 1. Items 4–29 were dichotomized as 1 (presence of the deficit) or 0 (absence of the deficit) based on corresponding cut-off values.

Item 30 (cognitive score) was a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1, with a higher score indicating worse cognition. For each participant, the FI was calculated by summing the present health deficits and dividing by 30 (or 29 items when excluding disease-specific items), resulting in a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1. A higher FI indicated a higher level of frailty. As suggested by previous studies [2, 16, 17], frailty status was categorized into three groups: robust (FI ≤ 0.10, indicating few or no deficits), pre-frail (0.10 < FI < 0.25, mild-to-moderate deficit accumulation), and frail (FI ≥ 0.25, indicating a clinically significant burden that increases vulnerability). Changes in frailty status were assessed by comparing baseline frailty status with that from the second survey, which was conducted two years after the baseline. Additionally, we characterized longitudinal frailty patterns using the average FI across two timepoints and the change in FI over time. The average FI was calculated as the mean of the baseline and follow-up FI scores, while the change in FI was derived by subtracting the baseline value from the follow-up measurement.

Outcome ascertainment and follow-up

The study outcome was defined as the first occurrence of incident diseases during the follow-up period. Disease diagnoses were based on self-reported physician-diagnosed conditions. The specific diseases included in the analysis were: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, asthma, chronic lung diseases, liver diseases, cardiovascular diseases, kidney diseases, digestive diseases, psychiatric problems, memory-related diseases, stroke and arthritis. The year of self-reported first diagnosis was recorded for each disease. Details regarding the specific assessment of chronic diseases in CHARLS can be found in the supplementary information.

To minimize reverse causation, a 1-year landmark analysis was applied consistently across all baseline frailty and frailty transition analyses, excluding participants who experienced health events within the first year of follow-up. The 1-year threshold was selected a priori to balance the exclusion of early events potentially reflecting sub-clinical disease while retaining sufficient statistical power. For baseline frailty status analyses, follow-up commenced from wave1; for frailty transition analyses, follow-up began at wave2 to ensure correct temporal sequencing and avoid immortal time bias. In both analyses, follow-up time was calculated from the respective landmark time (1 year after wave1 for baseline frailty analyses and 1 year after wave2 for frailty transition analyses) to the first occurrence of a health outcome, death, loss to follow-up, or censoring, whichever came first.

Statistical analyses

Cox proportional hazards models were employed to examine the associations of baseline frailty status and its longitudinal changes with disease risk, using follow-up time as the time scale. Proportional hazards assumptions were tested using Schoenfeld residuals [18], and no significant violations were observed (global test P > 0.05 for all models, Supplementary Tables S2-S7).

For the baseline frailty status analysis, participants classified as robust served as the reference group for estimating hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for pre-frail and frail categories. Stratified analyses by age (middle-aged: 40–65 years; older: ≥ 65 years) were also performed. For the frailty status transition analysis, we examined the risk associated with each possible transition pattern between wave1 and wave2. To enable meaningful comparisons among transitions originating from the same baseline state, separate reference groups were used based on the wave1 status: (1) participants who remained robust (stable robust) served as the reference for those transitioning to pre-frail or frail; (2) participants who remained pre-frail (stable pre-frail) served as the reference for those transitioning to robust or frail; and (3) participants who remained frail (stable frail) served as the reference for those transitioning to robust or pre-frail.

Sensitivity analyses focused on changes in frailty status using average FI and delta FI (divided into tertiles, lowest tertile as reference) were also conducted. To test the robustness of the landmark analysis findings, we repeated the baseline frailty status and frailty transition analyses using alternative landmark times of 6 months and 2 years. Multivariable-adjusted Cox models controlled for age, sex, sleep hours, marital status, education, smoking status, drinking status, and BMI. Missing data for covariates were summarized in the supplementary table (Supplementary Tables S8-S33). Missing data for covariates were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (random forest method) with five imputations, and results were combined using Rubin’s rules [19]. To account for multiple comparisons across 13 outcomes, the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Corrections were applied separately by analysis module: baseline frailty and frailty index analyses were corrected for all comparison groups (n = 2 × 13), and frailty transition analyses for all pattern-disease combinations (n = 6 × 13). Statistical significance was defined as an FDR-adjusted P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software [20] (Version 4.4.3).

Covariates

Covariates included in all analyses were age, sex, sleep hours, educational level, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, and BMI. Marital status was dichotomized as married/partnered or other (separated, divorced, unmarried, widowed). Education was categorized as below senior middle school, senior middle school, or college/higher. Smoking and drinking status were each classified as never or ever (former and current included in “ever”).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

For the baseline frailty status analyses, participants from 13 chronic disease-specific subgroups were included, with sample sizes ranging from 11,719 (48.9% female; mean age 58.8 years) to 16,882 (52.2% female; mean age 58.7 years). For the frailty transition analyses, subgroup sizes ranged from 9,807 (48.8% female; mean age 58.9 years) to 14,702 (52.5% female; mean age 58.8 years). Baseline characteristics of all participants are summarized in the supplementary table (Supplementary Tables S34-S46), including characteristics of those with non-imputed data. At baseline, frail individuals were generally older, more likely to be female, unmarried or unpartnered, had lower educational attainment, higher levels of BMI, and reported fewer sleep hours compared with their robust counterpart. The median follow-up duration was 6.2–6.8 years for baseline frailty status analyses, during which 1,948 participants died. For frailty transition analyses, the median follow-up was 4.4–5.0 years, with 1,653 deaths recorded during follow-up. Furthermore, we also describe the baseline characteristics of participants, which were used to analyze changes in frailty status, average frailty index, and delta frailty index (Supplementary Tables S47-S85). All analyses were conducted using non-imputed data.

Association between baseline frailty status and incident chronic diseases

Baseline frailty status was strongly and consistently associated with increased risk of incident chronic diseases across multiple organ systems. Frail participants exhibited significantly higher risks of developing multiple chronic diseases over the one-year follow-up period compared with their robust counterparts (Supplementary Tables S86-S98).

The strongest associations were observed for psychiatric problems (HR 5.17, 95% CI 3.24–8.24), asthma (HR 3.38, 95% CI 2.21–5.17), and memory-related diseases (HR 3.28, 95% CI 2.34–4.59). Elevated risks were also noted for kidney diseases (HR 2.48, 95% CI 1.91–3.23), liver disease (HR 2.27, 95% CI 1.64–3.12), heart diseases (HR 2.25, 95% CI 1.84–2.75), diabetes (HR 2.17, 95% CI 1.75–2.69), and stroke (HR 2.05, 95% CI 1.59–2.65). Additional associations were found for chronic lung diseases (HR 1.95, 95% CI 1.55–2.46), digestive diseases (HR 1.75, 95% CI 1.41–2.17), dyslipidemia (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.36–1.93), arthritis (HR 1.60, 95% CI 1.27–2.01), and hypertension (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.01–1.34). Similar, though attenuated, associations were also observed among pre-frail participants when compared with robust individuals. Increased risks were noted for psychiatric problems (HR 2.68, 95% CI 1.76–4.09), asthma (HR 2.43, 95% CI 1.70–3.49), heart diseases (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.58–2.17), stroke (HR 1.64, 95% CI 1.33–2.01), diabetes (HR 1.63, 95% CI 1.37–1.94), memory-related diseases (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.19–2.20), liver diseases (HR 1.60, 95% CI 1.24–2.08), chronic lung diseases (HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.38–1.97), arthritis (HR 1.50, 95% CI 1.28–1.75), dyslipidemia (HR 1.45, 95% CI 1.27–1.65), digestive diseases (HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.25–1.73), kidney diseases (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.12–1.74), and hypertension (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.06–1.28). These associations demonstrated a clear gradient, with progressively higher risks observed from robust to pre-frail and frail individuals across all chronic disease outcomes (Fig. 2). Furthermore, the 6-month and 2-year milestone analyses, performed as sensitivity analyses, produced results consistent with the primary findings (Supplementary Information, Supplementary Tables S99-S124). Transitions in frailty status and risk of incident diseases

Changes in frailty status over the one-year period were strongly associated with subsequent disease risk, with deterioration increasing risk and improvement conferring protective effects (Supplementary Tables S125-S137).

Compared with individuals who remained robust, those transitioning from robust to pre-frail had significantly elevated risks of heart diseases (HR 2.27, 95% CI 1.72–3.00), digestive diseases (HR 1.81, 95% CI 1.38–2.39), arthritis (HR 1.75, 95% CI 1.36–2.26), diabetes (HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.22–2.23), chronic lung diseases (HR 1.61, 95% CI 1.17–2.20), dyslipidemia (HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.27–1.97), stroke (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.09–2.26) and hypertension (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.17–1.59) (Fig. 3a). Conversely, improvements in frailty status-such as transitions from frail to pre-frail or robust and pre-frail to robust were associated with lower risks of several health outcomes.

Transitions from pre-frail to robust significantly lowered the risks of memory-related diseases (HR 0.36, 95% 0.18–0.71), kidney diseases (HR 0.37, 95% 0.22–0.63), heart diseases (HR 0.52, 95% 0.39–0.68), stroke (HR 0.54, 95% 0.36–0.80), diabetes (HR 0.57, 95% 0.42–0.78), arthritis (HR 0.73, 95% 0.56–0.95) and dyslipidemia (HR 0.76, 95% 0.61–0.96) (Fig. 3d). The 6-month and 2-year milestone sensitivity analyses yielded results largely consistent with the primary findings (Supplementary Information, Supplementary Tables S138-S163).

Association between delta frailty index and disease risk

Short-term changes in frailty severity, quantified by the delta frailty index, were associated with differential risks of subsequent chronic diseases. The difference between the FI assessed at the second and first follow-up evaluations was defined as the delta frailty index and was analyzed using tertile analysis. Compared to participants in the lowest tertile of frailty transitions (T1), those in the intermediate (T2) and highest (T3) tertiles exhibited distinct risk patterns (Supplementary Tables S177-S189).

Individuals in the T2 tertile (slight transition in frailty) showed a reduced risk of asthma (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.44–0.9) and heart diseases (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.67–0.95), but the protective effects were modest and the statistical significance was relatively weak (Fig. 4b).

Individuals in T3 (significant increase in frailty) had a significantly higher risk of arthritis (HR 1.3, 95% CI 1.1–1.54), stroke (HR 1.45, 95% CI 1.18–1.78), digestive diseases (HR 1.3, 95% CI 1.10–1.53), (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.10–1.54), hypertension (HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.14–1.41) and heart diseases (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.07–1.45) (Fig. 4b).

Association of baseline frailty status with disease risk by age group

Age-stratified analyses revealed that the impact of frailty on disease risk was substantially stronger in midlife (40–65 years) than in older adults. In the 40–65 age group, individuals classified as pre-frail and frail exhibited significantly increased risks for various chronic diseases compared to those classified as robust (Supplementary Tables S190-S202).

Discussion

In this large-scale prospective cohort study of middle-aged adults across multiple chronic disease-specific populations, we found that baseline frailty status was strongly associated with increased risks of a wide range of chronic conditions. Individuals classified as frail at baseline exhibited substantially elevated hazards of 13 major chronic diseases, with a clear dose–response gradient observed from robust to pre-frail to frail states. Importantly, longitudinal changes in frailty status provided further insights into the dynamic patterns of risk. Transitions reflecting worsening frailty were associated with a significantly higher incidence of all included chronic diseases. In contrast, improvements in frailty status were linked to notable reductions in risk across several diseases, including memory-related diseases, kidney diseases, heart diseases, stroke, diabetes, arthritis, dyslipidemia, and psychiatric problems. These findings underscore the critical role of frailty as a dynamic and modifiable risk factor, highlighting that both baseline status and longitudinal changes in frailty are strongly predictive of future chronic disease burden across multiple organ systems.

The robust predictive capacity of frailty status revealed in our study carries significant clinical implications, particularly regarding the staggering magnitude of risk elevation across distinct morbidity categories. Most alarmingly, frail individuals demonstrated a 417% higher risk (HR = 5.17) of developing psychiatric problem compared to robust counterparts—a risk ratio exceeding that of many established psychosocial risk factors. This finding resonates with a recent Mendelian randomization (MR) study, which demonstrated a potential causal relationship between FI and psychiatric problems [21]. This profound mental health vulnerability likely stems from the vicious cycle between physical frailty and psychological distress, where mobility limitations and chronic inflammation may disrupt neuroendocrine regulation while simultaneously eroding coping resources. Additionally, frailty status increased the risk for multiple other diseases, varying from 238% for asthma (HR = 3.38) to 17% for hypertension (HR 1.17). These results represent the first comprehensive analysis of the association between the FI and major chronic diseases within a Chinese population. Previous research on this topic has been conducted across diverse countries and populations, using varying methodologies. Cross-sectional studies have explored the relationships between FI and conditions such as hypertension [22], chronic lung diseases [23], neurodegenerative diseases [24], gastrointestinal disorders [25], dyslipidemia [26], and stroke [27]. Longitudinal studies have focused on how FI levels influence diseases like diabetes [28], heart diseases [29], arthritis [30], and liver diseases [31]. Additionally, MR has been employed to investigate the potential causal relationships between FI and diseases like asthma [32], stroke [33], arthritis [34], and kidney disease [35]. However, these studies have typically focused on individual diseases, utilized different methods to construct the FI, and involved diverse populations, limiting the ability to directly compare results.

Furthermore, we pioneered the investigation of frailty status transitions as a predictor of multiple system chronic diseases. Notably, frailty status transitions were independently associated with an increased risk of developing chronic diseases. Among these, the transition from robust to frail was associated with the highest risk increase, notably a 291% increase in the risk of asthma (HR 3.91). In contrast, improvement in frailty, such as transitions from frail to robust, offered significant protection against certain conditions, including a dramatic reduction in the risk of diabetes (HR 0.18), reflecting an 82% decrease in risk. This aspect of frailty research has been largely underexplored in prior studies, likely due to the complex link between frailty, chronic inflammation [36], and metabolic dysregulation [37]. As frailty improves, homeostasis within the body is likely restored, reducing disease risk and contributing to better long-term health outcomes. Sensitivity analyses using both average and delta frailty indices confirmed the robustness of these associations, showing a clear dose–response relationship and emphasizing frailty deterioration as an independent predictor of disease onset. Notably, individuals with worsening frailty were particularly vulnerable to arthritis, heart diseases, hypertension, digestive disorders, and stroke. These findings were consistently supported across different analyses. In addition, landmark sensitivity analyses using alternative cut-points (6-month and 2-year) yielded broadly similar effect estimates for both baseline frailty status and frailty transitions across multiple outcomes, including memory-related disease and psychiatric problems, supporting the temporal robustness of our findings. Nevertheless, for outcomes with insidious onset and extended preclinical trajectories, subclinical disease activity may still partially contribute to the observed associations, although the overall pattern remained stable in extended landmark analyses.

Age-stratified analyses refined our understanding of frailty’s impact across the lifespan. In adults aged 40–65, even pre-frailty was associated with a significantly increased risk of chronic diseases, emphasizing the need for earlier surveillance and intervention. While statistical significance was less pronounced in those aged 65 and above—likely due to smaller sample sizes and a higher baseline prevalence of chronic illness—frailty still notably increased the risk of kidney diseases. These findings highlight the importance of age-specific interventions, which is consistent with a similar conclusion reported in a UK Biobank study [7], further reinforcing the importance of frailty interventions in the 40–65 age group.

As China faces rapid population ageing, understanding health from an integrative, multidimensional perspective is critical [38]. Frailty, as a summary measure of both physical vulnerability and psychosocial decline, offers a powerful lens through which to identify individuals at high risk for chronic disease and functional deterioration. Yet, in contrast to countries like the UK [7], US [39], and Japan [40], where national frailty data are well-established, research in China remains limited. This study provides large-scale longitudinal evidence from the nationally representative CHARLS cohort, using a comprehensive frailty index to analyze multiple diseases across systems by examining dynamic changes in frailty status.

We recommend the routine use of a comprehensive frailty assessment in clinical settings, particularly for middle-aged and older adults. Such assessments could be feasibly integrated into annual health check-ups or primary care visits for at-risk populations, allowing systematic identification of individuals in pre-frail or frail states. Monitoring transitions between robust, pre-frail, and frail categories may facilitate early detection of high-risk individuals and timely preventive intervention. Notably, the pre-frail stage may represent a critical window for action, given its greater potential for reversal. Even individuals classified as robust may benefit from periodic reassessment to anticipate adverse risk trajectories.

Given the potential reversibility of frailty, targeted interventions—nutritional [41], physical [42], pharmacological [43], or behavioral [43]—could meaningfully alter health trajectories. However, further clinical trials are needed to determine the most effective, safe, and context-appropriate approaches for preventing or reversing frailty, especially across diverse populations.

This study has several limitations. First, reliance on self-reported data for certain chronic conditions may lead to imprecise disease definitions and introduce recall or reporting bias. Second, although we adjusted for multiple covariates, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded. Third, we did not apply competing risk regression models; thus, while the Cox models estimate cause-specific hazards, they do not account for the impact of competing mortality on cumulative incidence, and our findings should be interpreted as associations with relative risk rather than absolute disease probability. Fourth, although CHARLS is a nationally representative cohort of community-dwelling adults in China, it may not fully capture institutionalized individuals or the most severely frail older adults, potentially leading to underrepresentation of extreme frailty. Finally, although our study provides valuable evidence from a large, nationally representative Chinese population, its generalizability may be limited by cultural, socioeconomic, and healthcare system differences across countries. Nonetheless, the findings provide strong evidence for frailty as a key modifiable factor in chronic disease prevention and support the integration of frailty-focused strategies into public health and clinical frameworks in ageing societies.

Conclusion

Frailty represents a dynamic and modifiable risk factor closely linked to the incidence and accumulation of chronic diseases across multiple organ systems. Our large-scale, longitudinal analysis underscores the critical importance of early identification, especially during the pre-frail stage, when targeted intervention may be most effective in mitigating long-term disease risk. Routine incorporation of frailty assessment into primary care could enable more accurate risk stratification and guide preventive strategies tailored to individual health trajectories. In the context of China’s accelerating population ageing, prioritizing frailty prevention through integrated and proactive health management offers a strategic pathway to reduce chronic disease burden and support healthier ageing at the population level.
