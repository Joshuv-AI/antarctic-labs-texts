Crystal structures of free-state tRNALeu reveal conformational flexibility of type-II tRNAs
Authors: Gyuhyeok Cho, Jaehun Yoo, Kiroo Shin, Jangmin Lee, Jihyo Lim, Jungwook Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransfer RNAs (tRNAs) are classified into type-I and type-II based on the length of their variable loops, with type-II characterized by an extended variable loop. While structures of type-I tRNAs have been well-documented, standalone structures of type-II tRNAs have not been reported. Here, we present the first crystal structures of two free-state type-II tRNAs, specifically tRNALeu from Bacillus subtilis and Escherichia coli. Our structures reveal that the B. subtilis tRNALeu anticodon stem–loop (ASL) retains its canonical conformation. The variable loops in both structures are well-defined, displaying a distinctive tetranucleotide loop conformation. Comparisons with type-I tRNA and biomolecule-bound tRNAs highlight the flexibility of the ASL, variable loop, and terminal CCA residues in type-II tRNAs, suggesting that this structural plasticity is crucial for their biological interactions and function. These findings provide new insights into the structural dynamics and functional roles of type-II tRNAs.Keywords: tRNALeu, crystal structure, variable loop, type-II tRNA, free-state

INTRODUCTION

Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) play a critical role in translating the genetic code into proteins, regulating transcription, and participating in other cellular processes. Moreover, derivatives of tRNA, such as tRNA fragments (tRFs), participate in the regulation of cell viability, differentiation, homeostasis, and the development of human diseases (Fu et al. 2023). The conformation of all mature cytosolic and most mitochondrial tRNA can be described as an ensemble of five domains, that is, acceptor stem, D-loop, TψC-loop, anticodon stem–loop (ASL), and variable arm. Although tRNA is often described as a two-dimensional clover-leaf shape, the first X-ray crystal structure of yeast tRNAPhe revealed a distinctive L-shaped conformation, which provided molecular insights into codon recognition and peptidyl transfer on the ribosome (Kim et al. 1974; Robertus et al. 1974). Subsequent studies have determined numerous structures of tRNA molecules in both unbound and complexed states, including those bound to ribosomes (Selmer et al. 2006), tRNA synthetases (Rould et al. 1989), tRNA-modifying enzymes (Ishitani et al. 2003), and riboswitches (Zhang and Ferré-D'Amaré 2013). Analyses of these structures reveal that the elbow region, composed of D- and TψC-loops, is rigid and highly conserved, whereas the acceptor stem, variable loop, and anticodon loop regions exhibit greater flexibility. This conformational plasticity is important for the specific interactions with various binding partners, serving as a critical determinant of tRNA's biological function.

Depending of the length of variable loop, tRNA can be classified into two groups: type-I and type-II. For example, tRNASer, tRNATyr, and tRNALeu belong to type-II tRNAs, which contain long variable loops composed of 10–24 nt (Biela et al. 2023). It has been shown that the longer variable loop of type-II tRNAs serves as an important determinant for recognition by their respective tRNA synthetases (Biou et al. 1994; Yaremchuk et al. 2002; Tukalo et al. 2005). As of this writing, there are 77 structures containing full-length type-II tRNA reported in the Protein Data Bank (PDB): 47 for tRNALeu, 12 for tRNASer, and 18 for tRNATyr. However, these structures have only been determined in complexed forms, bound to the ribosome (Selmer et al. 2006), tRNA synthetases (Biou et al. 1994; Yaremchuk et al. 2002; Tukalo et al. 2005), tRNA pseudouridine synthase A (TruA) (Hur and Stroud 2007), tRNA [guanine(37)-N1]-methyltransferase (Trm5) (Goto-Ito et al. 2009), tRNA 5-carboxymethoxyuridine methyltransferase (CmoM) (Yoo et al. 2023), tRNA N(3)-methylcytidine methyltransferase (METTL6) (Throll et al. 2024), or ribonuclease P (RNase P) (Wan et al. 2019; Bhatta et al. 2021). The absence of structures representing free type-II tRNA hampers our understanding of the conformational changes induced by these interactions, particularly those involving the variable loops in tRNA dynamics.

Here, we present two crystal structures of unbound tRNALeu from Escherichia coli and Bacillus subtilis. As a member of type-II group, the common secondary structure of bacterial tRNALeu comprises a 3–5 bp variable stem with a 4–5 nt loop. Our findings highlight the structural details of the unusual conformational state of the extended variable loops, which may influence their interactions with LeuRS, ribosome, and other interacting proteins. Additionally, understanding these structural features can provide insights into the evolutionary conservation and diversification of tRNA molecules.

RESULTS

Overall structures of free tRNALeu

B. subtilis tRNALeuUAA (BstRNALeuUAA) and E. coli tRNALeuUAG (EctRNALeuUAG) were expressed and purified from E. coli. Initially, we attempted co-crystallization of the tRNA with B. subtilis CspR and E. coli TrmJ, which install a methyl group in the anticodon loop of the respective tRNA. However, only the tRNA was observed in the crystal structures, with no discernible electron density for the proteins. The tRNA crystals diffracted to resolutions of 2.5 and 2.9 Å, respectively (Table 1). Each asymmetric unit contains a single tRNA molecule, both displaying the canonical L-shaped conformation with extended variable loops (Fig. 1A,B).

For BstRNALeuUAA, electron density is observed for 87 out of 89 nt, excluding U16 and U17 in the D-loop, likely due to disorder. For EctRNALeuUAG, 81 out of 85 nt are resolved, with U16 and U17 in the D-loop, and U34 and A35 in the anticodon stem–loop (ASL) not observed. Given that the tRNAs were recombinantly expressed in E. coli, modifications by tRNA-modifying enzymes present in the expression host are expected. However, no modification-related electron density could be discerned, even for the bulkiest modifications like 5-carboxymethylaminomethyluridine at position 34 (cmnm5U34) and 2-methylthio-N6-isopentenyladenosine at position 37 (ms2i6A37) (Cappannini et al. 2024). Consequently, the structures have been modeled as unmodified tRNAs. Notably, all residues in the variable loop and acceptor arm are clearly resolved, possibly aided by interactions with neighboring molecules (Supplemental Figs. 1, 2). Indeed, both BstRNALeuUAG and EctRNALeuUAA form crystal contacts involving the variable loop, anticodon stem, acceptor arm, D- and TψC-loop, and the G19-C56 tertiary base pair. Both structures are highly similar, aligning with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 1.70 Å across 1122 atoms, with a moderate sequence identity of 63% (56 out of 89 nt). The “elbows” or cores of the L-shaped tRNA molecule, which comprises the D- and TψC-loops, are well-aligned, while differences are observed in the acceptor stem, ASL, and variable loop (Fig. 1C).

Anticodon stem–loop

The ASL of free BstRNALeuUAA is fully resolved (Fig. 2A,B). The anticodon U34-A35-A36 trinucleotide stack is stabilized within the classical U-turn motif (ASHRAF et al. 1999): The 2′-O of invariant U33 interacts with the N7 of the A35 base, while the uracil base with the phosphate oxygen of A35 and A36. Additionally, the carbonyl oxygen O4 of U33 interacts with N4 of C32, and O2 of C32 is in contact with N6 of A38. A37 is inserted between the noncanonical C32-A38 base pair and the U34-A35-A36 stack. In contrast, the anticodon stem of EctRNALeuUAG bends slightly from the core, resulting in a 7° angle difference when aligned with that of BstRNALeuUAA (Fig. 1C). The anticodon loop of EctRNALeuUAG displays greater conformational flexibility, with U34-A35 completely disordered (Supplemental Fig. 3). In this distorted anticodon loop, the nucleobases of G37 and U33 form stacking interactions. Meanwhile, the base of G36 does not participate in base-pairing or stacking and swings out toward solvent, with its 2′-O of ribose is in contact with base of G19 from a neighboring tRNA.

Variable arm

The variable arm of BstRNALeuUAA is 14 nt long, consisting of a five Watson–Crick base-pair stem (G45-C47k, G46-C47j, U47-A47i, A47a-U47h, and G47b-C47g) and a canonical GNRA tetranucleotide loop (G47c to A47f) (Fig. 3A–C; Jaeger et al. 1994; Correll and Swinger 2003). Within the loop, the Hoogsteen edge of G47c and the sugar edge of A47f form a trans base pair (Leontis et al. 2002), where N2 of G47c forms hydrogen bonds with N7 and phosphate oxygen of A47f (Fig. 3C). Additionally, the 2′-O of G47c interacts with N7 and O6 of G47e. U47d, G47e, and A47f stack similarly to an anticodon triplet. Meanwhile, the 10 nt long variable arm of EctRNALeuUAG consists of a three-base-pair stem, including two Watson–Crick base pairs (C46-G47f and C47-G47e) and one wobble base pair (G45-U47g), and a canonical GNRA tetranucleotide loop (G47a to A47d) (Fig. 3D–F). The short variable loop of EctRNALeuUAG exhibits a similar hydrogen bonding network as observed in BstRNALeuUAA (Fig. 3F). Specifically, N2 of G47a bonds with N7 and phosphate oxygen of A47d, while the 2′-O of G47a interacts with N7 of A47c. The trinucleotides C47b, A47c, and A47d exhibit stacking interactions analogous to those of U47d, G47e, and A47f within BstRNALeuUAA.

Comparison with type-I tRNA

To highlight the conformational feature of type-II tRNA, the structure of BstRNALeuUAA was superimposed with that of yeast tRNAPhe (PDB: 4TNA), a representative of type-I tRNA, which share only 40% sequence identity (36 out of 89 nt) (Fig. 4A). Overall, the ASL, TψC-loop, and the acceptor stem display similar folding, except for the terminal CCA residues. The largest changes are observed in the variable loop, followed by the D-loop due to the addition of 12 and 1 nt, respectively (Fig. 4B,C). The nucleotide residues 12–15 in the D-loop of BstRNALeuUAA shift 4 Å toward the variable loop compared to yeast tRNAPhe (Fig. 4C). Similarly, EctRNALeuUAG exhibits structural changes in the terminal CCA, variable loop, and D-loop when aligned with tRNAPhe, with which it shares 51% sequence identity (43 out of 85 nt) (Fig. 4D–F). The ASL of EctRNALeuUAG is tilted by ∼4° compared to the ASL of tRNAPhe. Analogous to BstRNALeuUAA, the variable loop displays the largest conformational change, containing 8 more nucleotides, followed by the D-loop which is one nucleotide longer than in tRNAPhe (Fig. 4E,F).

Despite these variations, the core architecture of tRNALeu remains conserved when compared to the type-I tRNA (Fig. 5A–C). The TψC-loop is structurally conserved between BstRNALeuUAA and EctRNALeuUAG, with only minor base-pair substitutions, specifically, C51-G63 and G52-C62 in B. subtilis are replaced with G51-C63 and A52-U62 in E. coli. Notably, key base pairs in the TψC-loop such as G53-C61, U54-A58, G18-U55, and G19-C56 are conserved across all three structures. In the D-loop, sequence differences between B. subtilis and E. coli tRNALeu include purine-to-purine and pyrimidine-to-pyrimidine substitutions, such as U11, G13, C20, and A24 in BstRNALeuUAA being replaced by C11, A13, U20, and G24 in EctRNALeuUAG. Nevertheless, important base triplets involved in structural stabilization are preserved (Fig. 5D,E), including U8-A14-A20a, G/A13-G21-A22, and G9-G12-C23. In contrast, the corresponding triplets in tRNAPhe differ: U8-A14-A21, C13-G22-m7G46, and A9-U12-A23 (Fig. 5F). Notably, A20a in tRNALeu appears to occupy the structural position of A21 in tRNAPhe, while m7G46 in tRNAPhe occupies a cavity absent in tRNALeu due to the lack of an insertion like A20a (Tukalo et al. 2005). The triplet G9-G12-C23 in tRNALeu is stabilized by an extensive network of six to seven hydrogen bonds, whereas the corresponding triplet A9-U12-A23 in tRNAPhe is supported by only four hydrogen bonds, suggesting a more rigid and stabilized tertiary interaction in tRNALeu.

Comparison with LeuRS-bound tRNALeu

To assess conformational changes upon biomolecular binding, we compared our free-state tRNALeu structures with LeuRS-bound complexes. Leucyl-tRNA synthetase (LeuRS) recognizes tRNALeu by binding to its D-loop face and interacting with several structural features, including the variable arm, the discriminator base A73, the A15-U48 Levitt base pair, and the G19-C56 tertiary base pair (Tukalo et al. 2005). To evaluate structural differences upon LeuRS binding, we compared our free-state tRNALeu structures with LeuRS-bound counterparts containing fully resolved variable arms. A Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) search (Altschul et al. 1990) identified E. coli tRNALeuUAA (EctRNALeuUAA) in complex with LeuRS in two crystal structures (PDB: 4ARI and 5ONH) as the closest sequence matches to BstRNALeuUAA and EctRNALeuUAG, showing 72% (64 out of 89 nt) and 65% (55 out of 85 nt) identity, respectively. These two structures represent the editing (4ARI) and aminoacylation (5ONH) states of LeuRS.

Structural alignment revealed that the overall conformation of free and LeuRS-bound tRNALeu is largely conserved, with RMSD values of 1.23 Å (editing) and 1.54 Å (aminoacylation) for BstRNALeuUAA and 2.04 Å (editing) and 1.46 Å (aminoacylation) for EctRNALeuUAG (Fig. 6A,B). In the LeuRS-bound state, the D-loop face is stabilized, allowing resolution of nucleotides U16 and U17, which are disordered in the free state. Conversely, the anticodon loop remains disordered in both LeuRS-bound structures, as LeuRS does not directly recognize the anticodon. The D- and TψC-loops remain rigid with and without LeuRS. Notable conformational changes are observed in the acceptor stem, ASL, and especially in the variable arm upon LeuRS binding. In both LeuRS-bound structures, the variable arm is bent inward by more than ∼7–11 Å relative to the free-state structures (Fig. 6C,D). This bending appears to be mediated by multiple salt bridges formed between K809 and R811 of LeuRS and the phosphate backbones of G47g, C47h, and U47i in the variable arm. These interactions likely induce and stabilize the bent conformation of the variable arm.

LeuRS binding also induces significant conformational changes in the terminal CCA residues (Fig. 6E). The discriminator base A73, a key identity element for LeuRS recognition (Asahara et al. 1993; Soma et al. 1999; Tocchini-Valentini et al. 2000), adopts different conformations depending on the tRNA state. In the aminoacylation complex (5ONH), A73 is tightly recognized by LeuRS through extensive contacts, whereas in the editing complex (4ARI), A73 is displaced from the recognition pocket (Fig. 6F,G and Supplemental Fig. 4A). In our free-state structures, A73 remains stacked on the G1-C72 base pair in EctRNALeuUAG (Fig. 6H), resembling the conformation observed in the editing state. In contrast, in BstRNALeuUAA, A73 is unstacked and forms a kink between C72 and A73, subsequently adopting a new stacking interaction with the terminal triplet C74-C75-A76 (Fig. 6I). This conformational shift in BstRNALeuUAA may be influenced by contacts with a crystallographically neighboring molecules (Supplemental Fig. 1), potentially orienting A73 toward the editing domain of LeuRS (Supplemental Fig. 4B). These observations highlight the conformational flexibility of the CCA terminus in tRNALeu, which can be modulated by external interactions.

Comparison with ribosome-bound tRNALeu

We further compared the free-state tRNALeu structures with ribosome-bound counterparts in three functional states, A-, P-, and E-sites. Among 23 ribosome-bound tRNALeu structures in the PDB, six contain tRNALeu in the A-site, 16 in the P-site, and one structure includes tRNALeu simultaneously in the A-, P-, and E-sites. For the A-site, we selected two EctRNALeuGAG structures (PDB: 4WSM and 4V8B), which exhibit well-resolved electron density covering the full-length tRNA. In the A-site, key interactions are observed between the ribosome and the D- and TψC-loops, ASL, and acceptor arm of tRNALeu (Fig. 7A). The selected EctRNALeuGAG shares 62% sequence identity with BstRNALeuUAA (55 out of 89 nt) and 67% with EctRNALeuUAG (57 out of 85 nt). Structural alignments reveal moderate similarity, with RMSD values of 2.19–2.21 Å (vs. BstRNALeuUAA) and 2.77–2.80 Å (vs. EctRNALeuUAG) (Fig. 7A). Major conformational differences are observed in the variable arm and acceptor arm. Ribosome binding induces a displacement of the variable arm by 6–10 Å, similar to the bending observed upon LeuRS binding (Fig. 7B). Notably, the closest approach between the variable arm and ribosome occurs ∼9–10 Å from residue C884 of 23S rRNA. At the CCA terminus, steric hindrance from a loop containing 23S rRNA residues C1942 and U1943 compresses the acceptor stem, displacing it by 4–9 Å (Fig. 7C).

For the P-site, we selected two EctRNALeuUAG structures (PDB: 7OII and 7OT5) based on complete electron density. Major interactions occur at the G19-C56 tertiary base pair, ASL, and acceptor arm (Fig. 7D). The selected ribosome-bound tRNALeu shares 63% sequence identity with BstRNALeuUAA (56 out of 89 nt). Structural alignments reveal high similarity, with RMSD values of 1.98 Å (7OII and 7OT5) for BstRNALeuUAA and 1.72 Å (7OII) and 1.65 Å (7OT5) for EctRNALeuUAG. As in the A-site, ribosome binding induces conformational changes in the acceptor arm, ASL, and variable arm. However, the P-site imposes tighter spatial constraints on the variable arm (Fig. 7E,F), displacing it by 9–10 Å. Contacts with the ribosome at the acceptor arm shift the tRNA by 2–6 Å to properly position the terminal CCA trinucleotide into the peptidyl transferase center (Fig. 7G).

Only one available structure contains tRNALeu in the E-site (PDB: 4WSM), corresponding to EctRNALeuGAG. This tRNA exhibits poor electron density around the variable loop, suggesting high flexibility. Major contacts at the E-site involve the G19-C56 tertiary base pair, ASL, and acceptor arm (Fig. 7H). Comparisons with free BstRNALeuUAA and EctRNALeuUAG yield moderate structural similarity, with RMSD values of 3.80 Å and 3.48 Å, respectively. Unlike the P-site, the E-site does not tightly constrain the variable loop, consistent with its unresolved density (Fig. 7I). The CCA terminus is significantly displaced, by more than 20 Å, due to interactions with 23S rRNA residues G2421, A2422, C2394, and the 50S ribosomal protein L35 (notably residue R30) (Fig. 7J).

Comparison with TruA-bound tRNALeu

tRNA pseudouridine synthase A (TruA) modifies uridine at position 38, 39, and 40 in the ASL (Hur and Stroud 2007). TruA interacts with the D-stem, G19-C56 tertiary base pair, and anticodon stem of tRNA. Among the three TruA-complexed structures, the one with EctRNALeuUAA shows the highest sequence homology with BstRNALeuUAA at 72% (64 out of 89 nt) (PDB: 2NQP). TruA recognizes the D-stem, G19-C56 tertiary base pair, and anticodon stem, maintaining overall structure of the cognate tRNA (Hur and Stroud 2007). When structures of free BstRNALeuUAA and TruA-bound EctRNALeuUAA are superimposed, they display similar overall conformations with an RMSD of 2.58 Å (Fig. 8A). Although a large portion of the variable loop is unresolved in the TruA-complexed structure, the conformational change of the stem can still be compared, showing that the variable stem is shifted by 9.1 Å, without any interactions with nearby molecules (Fig. 8B).

EctRNALeuGAG bound to TruA (PDB: 2NR0) exhibits 67% sequence identity (57 out of 85 nt) compared to our EctRNALeuUAG. However, the structural alignment displays a substantial diversity, with an RMSD of 3.72 Å (Fig. 8C). Significant differences are observed in the terminal CCA residues, variable arm, and ASL. Notably, the ASL undergoes a large conformational change, swinging by ∼14.1°, due to TruA binding to the ASL stem. Furthermore, the variable arm shifts by 6.9 Å upon this binding (Fig. 8D).

Comparison with Trm5-bound tRNALeu

tRNA (guanine(37)-N1)-methyltransferase (Trm5) methylates G37 in the ASL and recognizes the D-stem, G19-C56 tertiary base pair, and anticodon arm (Goto-Ito et al. 2009). When comparing BstRNALeuUAA with the tRNALeuUAG complexed with Trm5 from Methanocaldococcus jannaschii (PDB: 2ZZM), significant conformational changes are observed in the acceptor stem, anticodon loop, and variable loop (Fig. 8E). These tRNAs share only 53% sequence identity (47 out of 89 nt). Trm5 binding tilts the ASL by 8.5°, positioning the 37th nucleotide into its active site (Supplemental Fig. 5; Goto-Ito et al. 2009). Although Trm5 does not directly recognize the variable loop, this loop bends toward the protein, shifting by 9.0 Å compared to the free BstRNALeuUAA (Fig. 8F). Finally, we compared the structures of free EctRNALeuUAG with Trm5-bound tRNALeu (PDB: 2ZZM) (Fig. 8G), where these two tRNAs share a sequence identity of 59% (50 out of 85 nt). Notably, Trm5 binding induces bending of the ASL axis by ∼3.6°. The variable arm shifts by 11.0 Å (Fig. 8H).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we report the crystal structures of two free-state type-II tRNAs, tRNALeuUAA from B. subtilis and tRNALeuUAG from E. coli, providing novel insights into the conformational flexibility of tRNAs, particularly within the variable loop and ASL regions. Notably, only seven full-length tRNA structures in a free-state have been previously reported, including tRNAPhe (Kim et al. 1974; Robertus et al. 1974), initiator tRNAMet (Schevitz et al. 1979; Woo et al. 1980), tRNAAsp (Moras et al. 1980), tRNALys (Bénas et al. 2000), tRNASec (Itoh et al. 2009), tRNAGly (Grigg and Ke 2013), and tRNAVal (Jeong and Kim 2021; Ohira et al. 2022). Thus, our structures contribute significantly to the limited pool of unbound tRNA molecules available.

A major finding of this study is the structural plasticity of the variable loop in response to molecular recognition by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and the ribosome. In the free state, the variable arm is stabilized in a relatively compact conformation. However, upon binding to LeuRS or the ribosome, this region undergoes notable displacement and bending. In particular, comparisons with LeuRS-bound structures in both the editing and aminoacylation states (Fig. 6) displayed similar conformational changes. In the both states, the variable loop is bent inward and engages in direct interactions with basic residues (e.g., K809, R811) in the LeuRS C-terminal domain. These interactions likely involved in recognition of type-II tRNA for LeuRS. Similarly, comparisons with ribosome-bound tRNALeu across the A-, P-, and E-sites (Fig. 7) show that the variable loop exhibits different extents of displacement depending on the ribosomal context. In the A-site, the loop is moderately shifted; in the P-site, steric restriction forces a larger displacement; and in the E-site, the loop appears highly disordered and partially unresolved. These structural changes are paralleled by repositioning of the acceptor arm and terminal CCA, which is critical for tRNA engagement with the ribosomal peptidyl transferase center.

We also observed that in both free-state structures, the 3′ CCA terminus adopts a conformation oriented toward the editing domain of LeuRS. This is consistent with previous proposals that the editing state represents a lower-energy, more relaxed binding conformation, while the aminoacylation state involves a more strained, high-energy configuration (Palencia et al. 2012; Chopra et al. 2013; Hernandez et al. 2013). Structural comparisons show that the overall conformation of free-state tRNALeu more closely resembles the editing-bound state, supporting this model. Interestingly, the A73 discriminator base in BstRNALeuUAA flips outward in a manner that aligns with the aminoacylation conformation, suggesting local flexibility or potential packing influence (Supplemental Fig. 4B).

Another significant finding is the subtle distortions observed in the ASL of both free-state tRNAs. The flexibility of the ASL has been previously noted in complex structures with tRNA-modifying enzymes such as Trm5 (Supplemental Fig. 5; Goto-Ito et al. 2009). Trm5 induces substantial conformational changes in the ASL to accommodate the substrate nucleotide G37 into the active site. Similar ASL flexibility is evident during ribosome binding and enzyme interactions, such as with TruA. Although nucleotide flipping within the ASL was not observed in the free state, the structural distortions in EctRNALeuUAG suggest an inherent flexibility even without enzyme binding.

Our findings provide deeper insights into the evolutionary conservation and diversification of tRNA molecules. Studies on type-I tRNAs complexed with biomolecules reveal that the tRNA core, specifically the elbow region composed of the D- and TψC-loops, is rigid and highly conserved, whereas the acceptor stem, variable loop, and anticodon loop exhibit greater flexibility. We observed similar features in type-II tRNAs. By comparing the free-state tRNALeu structures with their complexed counterparts, we confirmed that the D- and TψC-loops remain rigid, while the acceptor stem, variable loop, and anticodon loop show high flexibility.

Importantly, our structural comparison between type-I and type-II tRNAs also reveals functional implications regarding tRNA identity and recognition by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases. Type-II tRNAs, including tRNALeu, are defined by their long variable arms (>10 nt), which form critical identity elements recognized by their cognate synthetases (Asahara et al. 1993; Soma et al. 1999; Tocchini-Valentini et al. 2000; Giegé and Eriani 2023). In the case of LeuRS, the variable arm engages in extensive interactions with the enzyme, contributing significantly to aminoacylation fidelity. This mode of recognition is thought to be a general feature among synthetases that interact with type-II tRNAs (Giegé and Eriani 2023). In contrast, type-I tRNAs possess shorter variable arms and rely more heavily on alternative recognition elements, such as the acceptor stem, anticodon loop, or discriminator base. This divergence in recognition strategies reflects an evolutionary adaptation tailored to the structural features of each tRNA type, further emphasizing the functional relevance of the structural distinctions observed in our study.

Our findings also underscore the potential of heterologously expressed tRNAs as valuable tools for structural studies. These tRNAs can undergo post-transcriptional modifications by the modifying enzymes present in the expression host. Depending on the availability of specific tRNA modifying enzymes, the extracted tRNAs can be either fully modified or hypomodified, making them ideal substrates for enzyme kinetic studies or for determining complex structures. For instance, our laboratory has used this approach to prepare specifically hypomodified tRNAs and to solve the structure of a Michaelis complex between cmo5U34-containing tRNA and CmoM, which methylates the 5-carboxymethoxy group of wobble uridine (Yoo et al. 2023).

In summary, the crystal structures of free tRNALeu presented in this study reveal distinct structural features that differ from their complexed counterparts. Notably, we resolved the long variable arm of type-II tRNA in its free state, providing deeper insights into the functional implications of tRNA conformational plasticity in this region. These insights could significantly enhance our understanding of how tRNAs adapt to interact with various biomolecules, including aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, ribosome, and modifying enzymes, thereby advancing our knowledge of tRNA biology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

tRNA preparation

The insert sequences for BstRNALeuUAA and EctRNALeuUAG were synthesized by IDT (USA). These inserts were amplified by PCR using primers with overhang sequences containing restriction sites for EcoRI and BamHI. After purification, the PCR products were digested with EcoRI and BamHI and cloned into EcoRI/BamHI-treated pBSTNAV vector (Addgene #45801, a gift from Luc Ponchon). The resulting plasmids were used to transform E. coli ΔtrmL (for BstRNALeuUAA) or ΔcmoB (for EctRNALeuUAG) strains, which were provided by NBRP E. coli Strain (Japan). The cells were cultured at 37°C for 24 h to overexpress the tRNAs. The cultures were harvested by centrifugation, and the in vivo transcribed tRNAs were extracted using the acidic phenol method. Briefly, the cell pellets were lysed in 15 mL of buffer A (0.3 M sodium acetate, pH 4.5, and 10 mM EDTA) and mixed with 16.7 mL of acid-saturated phenol. After rocking at 4°C for 30 min, the aqueous phase was separated by centrifugation, and 14 mL of the supernatant was transferred to a new 50 mL conical tube. tRNA was precipitated by adding 35 mL of ethanol and incubating at −86°C for 2 h. The precipitated tRNA was collected by centrifugation, washed three times with 70% (v/v) ethanol, dried at 60°C for 10 min, and dissolved in autoclaved water. Further purification was performed using gel electrophoresis. The tRNA was run on a 9% (w/v) acrylamide and 7 M urea denaturing gel. RNA bands were visualized under UV light on a silica plate, excised, and eluted from the gel using electroelution with Elutrap. The tRNA was denatured at 95°C for 10 min and renatured at room temperature prior to crystallization.

Crystallization and structure determination

For crystallization of BstRNALeuUAA, a tRNA–protein solution was prepared by mixing 0.08 mM in vivo transcribed BstRNALeuUAA, 0.04 mM B. subtilis CspR, and 0.5 mM sinefungin. An 0.8 µL aliquot of this solution was mixed with an equal volume of mother liquor (0.072 M sodium chloride, 0.036 M sodium cacodylate trihydrate pH 7.0, 27% [v/v] [+/−]-2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol, 0.0108 M spermine tetrahydrochloride, and 0.5% [v/v] ethyl acetate). The mixture was equilibrated against 60 µL of the mother liquor. Rhombic crystals formed at 20°C using the sitting drop vapor diffusion method. For EctRNALeuUAG crystallization, a mixture of 0.15 mM EctRNALeuUAG, 0.1 mM E. coli TrmJ, 0.5 mM sinefungin, and 1.5 mM MgCl2 was prepared. Rhombic crystals were obtained using the same method as for BstRNALeuUAA, with a different mother liquor (0.25 M ammonium sulfate, 0.1 M sodium acetate trihydrate, pH 4.6, and 32% [w/v] polyethylene glycol monomethyl ether 2000).

The crystals were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen without cryoprotectant. Diffraction data were collected at the 7A beamline of the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory (PAL) with a wavelength of 0.9793 Å. BstRNALeuUAA data were indexed, integrated, and merged using autoPROC (Vonrhein et al. 2011) with XSCALE (Kabsch 2010) for scaling (Table 1). EctRNALeuUAG data were indexed, integrated, and scaled with XDS (Kabsch 2010). Due to strong anisotropy in the EctRNALeuUAG data, with reflections observed to 2.685 Å in the strongest direction and 5.114 Å in the weakest, anisotropic resolution cutoff was applied using STARANISO (Tickle et al. 2016). The structures were determined by molecular replacement using MOLREP (Vagin and Teplyakov 1997) with AlphaFold3-predicted tRNA structures as search models (Abramson et al. 2024). As no electron density corresponding to proteins was observed in either data set, only the tRNA molecules were iteratively built using Coot (Emsley et al. 2010) and refined with REFMAC5 (Murshudov et al. 2011), aided by PDB-REDO (Joosten et al. 2014). The final models were visualized using PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, version 2.5.5, Schrödinger, LLC).

MEET THE FIRST AUTHOR

Meet the First Author(s) is an editorial feature within RNA, in which the first author(s) of research-based papers in each issue have the opportunity to introduce themselves and their work to readers of RNA and the RNA research community. Gyuhyeok Cho is the first author of this paper, “Crystal structures of free-state tRNALeu reveal conformational flexibility of type-II tRNAs.” Gyuhyeok is a postdoc at the Gwangju Institute of Science and Technology (GIST) in Korea. His research focuses on the structural and biochemical characterization of RNA-modifying enzymes.

What are the major results described in your paper and how do they impact this branch of the field?

This study presents the first reference structures of type-II tRNAs in their free state. While free-state structures of type-I tRNAs (with short variable arms) have been reported since the 1970s, the structure of type-II tRNAs (with long variable arms) has remained elusive. Our crystal structures reveal notable conformational flexibility in both the variable loop and the acceptor arm, offering new insight into the intrinsic dynamics and structural features of this important class of tRNAs.

What led you to study RNA or this aspect of RNA science?

RNA undergoes a wide range of post-transcriptional chemical modifications. During my PhD, I realized that the enzymes responsible for many of these modifications had yet to be identified. The approaches and validation tools used by other groups to uncover similar enzymes seemed feasible within our lab environment. This motivated me to pursue the challenge of characterizing previously unknown RNA-modifying enzymes.

What are some of the landmark moments that provoked your interest in science or your development as a scientist?

This question related to the previous one. Early in my PhD program, when I browsed the UniProt database, there were a large number of entries annotated as “uncharacterized proteins.” Even in E. coli, one of the most well-studied organisms, hundreds of proteins still lacked functional annotation. I thought, “If these proteins are conserved in the genome, they must have a function.” That realization sparked my curiosity. It felt like a puzzle waiting to be solved. I set a personal goal for my PhD: to identify and name a previously uncharacterized protein and update in the UniProt database. Under the guidance of Professor Jungwook Kim and in collaboration with Dr. Jangmin Lee, we predicted and validated the function of a previously unknown tRNA-modifying enzyme in B. subtilis. We confirmed its activity through biochemical assays and named it MnmM. I later contacted UniProt to update the entry to reflect our findings (see UniProt: O34614). The experience of building a hypothesis, validating it through experiments, and contributing new knowledge to the field deeply reinforced my passion for science. Since then, I've realized that there are still many such enzymes waiting to be discovered. Time was the only constraint. Now, as a postdoc in Professor Kim's lab, I continue to pursue this interest. While I'm currently focused on characterizing a new tRNA-modifying enzyme, I keep a growing list of uncharacterized proteins with expected functions. I hope to return to this list as an independent researcher in the future.

What are your subsequent near- or long-term career plans?

I completed my PhD in the lab of Professor Jungwook Kim at GIST, where I built a strong foundation in structural biology and biochemistry. I also had the opportunity to collaborate with Dr. Lothar Hennighausen at the NIH. Currently, I am seeking a postdoc position where I can continue investigating biologically important macromolecules using structural, biochemical, and bioinformatic approaches. Through reading many papers during our lab's literature seminars, I've come to realize that numerous enzymes remain uncharacterized. In many cases, the reactions they catalyze are known, but the enzymes themselves have not yet been identified. I am interested in joining a lab focused on discovering and characterizing such enzymes. My interest is not limited to RNA-modifying enzymes, but includes any proteins involved in catalyzing essential biological reactions.
