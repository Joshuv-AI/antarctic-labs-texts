Cascaded regulatory network composed of small RNAs involves in the symbiosis of Panax notoginseng and fungus Acremonium sp. D212
Authors: Baolin Yao, Haiyan Zhu, Xie He, Wenjing Yang, Chongyu Luo, Yan Li, Ao Yang, Yinhui Zhang, Lihui Jiang, Yaqiong Li, Liwei Guo, Xiahong He, Yunlong Du, Changning Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPanax notoginseng (P. notoginseng) is a valuable traditional Chinese medical herb with multiple pharmacological effects, and Acremonium sp. D212 is a symbiotic fungus. The symbiotic relationship between fungi and plants is widely present in nature, but the molecular mechanism of the symbiotic relationship between P. notoginseng and its symbiotic microorganisms is unknown. This study found that Acremonium sp. D212 altered the gene expression of P. notoginseng under different lights, and this change was associated with the miRNAs transferred from Acremonium sp. D212 to P. notoginseng. Transferred miRNAs can not only regulate P. notoginseng target genes, but also trigger the production of phased siRNAs to regulate more target genes, thereby forming a cascaded regulatory network. P. notoginseng target genes are associated with trans-membrane transporting and plant hormone metabolism, suggesting that these small RNAs may have potential influence on the growth and development of P. notoginseng. This study discovers an underlying molecular mechanism of the interactions between P. notoginseng and its symbiotic fungus Acremonium sp. D212, and provide a new perspective for further research on the gene interaction between plants and symbiotic fungi.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40644-x.Keywords: Panax notoginseng, Acremonium sp. Introduction

Panax notoginseng is a valuable traditional Chinese medicinal herb widely used to stop bleeding, resolve blood stasis, and relieve pain associated with swelling1–3. Light conditions critically influence its development: as a shade-preferring crop, both light quality and intensity strongly affect its growth, photosynthetic performance, and saponin biosynthesis4–6. Endophytic fungi can enhance host plant photosynthesis and health7, and diverse endophytes have been reported from P. notoginseng; some reduce root damage caused by pathogens8. Acremonium sp. D212 is an endophytic fungus isolated from P. notoginseng that colonizes the root and promotes plant growth and saponin production9. However, how different light regimes modulate the symbiotic interaction between P. notoginseng and Acremonium sp. D212 remains poorly understood.

Small non-coding RNAs (sRNAs) are important regulators of epigenetic and post-transcriptional processes in plants10. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are especially potent because they can move systemically and silence target genes at a distance; notably, plant-derived sRNAs can transfer to interacting fungi and contribute to pathogen resistance11. Phased small interfering RNAs (phasiRNAs) are 21- or 24-nt small RNAs typically generated following miRNA-directed cleavage12; they have established roles in processes such as rice fertility and reproduction13–15 and in responses to drought16. Despite their regulatory importance, the involvement of miRNAs and phasiRNAs in the P. notoginseng-Acremonium sp. D212 symbiosis has not been explored.

In this study, we assembled the genome of Acremonium sp. D212 and profiled mRNA and small RNA transcriptomes of both P. notoginseng and the fungus under white, red, and blue light conditions. We show that Acremonium sp. D212 alters host gene expression in a light-dependent manner and that fungal miRNAs are transferred to P. notoginseng. These transferred miRNAs can trigger phasiRNA biogenesis in the plant, creating a multilayered regulatory cascade that varies with light quality. Functional enrichment of the predicted plant targets implicates multiple key biological pathways. Our results provide a foundation for mechanistic studies of how light shapes plant-endophyte interactions and highlight a role for cross-kingdom small RNA signaling in that process.

Symbiosis of P. notoginseng and Acremonium sp. D212 grown under three lights

To detect the symbiosis of P. notoginseng seedlings and endophytic Acremonium sp. D212 grown under different light conditions, the symbiont were grown under white light, red light and blue light (Fig. 1a–c). Compared with the control seedlings inoculated without Acremonium sp. D212 (Fig. 1d–f), the P. notoginseng seedlings inoculated with Acremonium sp. D212 did not show disease phenotype (Fig. 1g–i). When we detected the colonization of Acremonium sp. D212 in the roots of P. notoginseng seedlings, compared with the seedlings grown under white light condition, the colonization of Acremonium sp. D212 was decreased under red light, but increased under blue light condition (Fig. 2a). It suggests that the symbiosis of P. notoginseng and Acremonium sp. D212 was affected by different light conditions. When we further detected the morphogenesis of Acremonium sp. D212 grown under different light conditions, it showed that the sporulation of Acremonium sp. D212 was decreased under red light and blue light compared with the fungus grown under white light condition (Fig. S1a). However, the fungus growth was increased under red light and blue light compared with the white light condition (Fig. S1b). It showed that the growth of fungus Acremonium sp. D212 is responded to different light conditions.

Genome resource announcement of Acremonium sp. D212

To find close related species of Acremonium sp. D212, a phylogenetic tree was constructed by MEGA717 based on the maximum likelihood method. As shown in Fig. S2, Acremonium sp. D212 showed a more close relationship with the Acremonium sclerotigenum species.

Genome assembly was conducted by wtdbg218. Compared to other fungi, Acremonium sp. D212 had a smaller genome size, which was consistent with N50, and the longest scaffold (Fig. 2b). Acremonium sp. D212 showed a higher GC content and genome completeness than other four species (Fig. 2b), suggesting that the Acremonium sp. D212 genome was well assembled (Fig. S3). To explore the similarity of Acremonium sp. D212 and other fungal strains on genome levels, other fungal organisms were selected for comparison. The N50 of Acremonium sp. D212 genome was 4.44 Mb, while N50 in other 4 fungal organism genomes ranged from 0.167 to 7.202 Mb (Fig. 2b). Two other Acremonium species were selected for further comparison, much more details were presented in Fig. 2b (Table 1).

P. notoginseng transcriptome analysis and differential gene expression profiling

To determine whether the observed transcriptional changes were attributable to Acremonium sp. D212 rather than to light quality, we performed RNA-seq on P. notoginseng samples grown under white, red, or blue light with and without Acremonium sp. D212 inoculation. Within each light condition, inoculated and non-inoculated samples were compared to identify Acremonium sp. D212-dependent differentially expressed genes. We obtained high-quality transcriptome data from 18 samples (total 787,766,760 clean reads), with Q30 values of 92.6–95.1% and per-sample mapping rates of 89.3–94.6% (Table 1). These data provided sufficient depth and quality for downstream differential expression and functional analyses (see Materials and Methods). There were strong correlations between biological replication samples, except for PSL_2 and PS_2W, while there was a difference in correlation between samples transfected with Acremonium sp. D212 or those not inoculated with Acremonium sp. D212 (Table S1). Furthermore, we selected 13 genes related to plant hormone metabolism for Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR) experiments, the experiment confirmed the effectiveness of RNA-seq experiments (Fig. S4a, b).

Inoculation with Acremonium sp. D212 under different light conditions significantly affected gene expression in P. notoginseng, resulting in numerous differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Investigating the samples transfected with Acremonium sp. D212 under three lights, it was found that 22 DEGs were up-regulated under all three lights, 27 DEGs were up-regulated under both white and red light, 68 DEGs were up-regulated under both white and blue light, and 230 DEGs were up-regulated under both red and blue light (Fig. S5b). On the other hand, down-regulated DEGs were also found. There were 24 DEGs down-regulated under all three lights, 228 DEGs down-regulated under both white and red light, 57 DEGs down-regulated under both white and blue light, and 74 DEGs down-regulated under both red and blue light (Fig. S5a). Taken together, when samples were transfected by Acremonium sp. D212, gene expression levels in P. notoginseng were changed significantly under all three lights. Intriguingly, genes related to jasmonic acid and saponin biosynthesis showed different expression trends under different lights: they were all down-regulated under white light transfected with Acremonium sp. D212 (Fig. 3a). Under red light, genes related to jasmonic acid biosynthesis were down-regulated, while genes related to saponin biosynthesis were up-regulated (Fig. 3b). The results under blue light were more complicated, more genes were up regulated, including genes associated with jasmonic acid and saponin biosynthesis, however, saponin biosynthesis related genes were also found down-regulated (Fig. 3c).

To further investigate the possible functions of DEGs detected in P. notoginseng samples inoculated with Acremonium sp. D212 under different lights, GO enrichment analysis were conducted. GO enrichment of down-regulated DEGs (Supplementary Fig. S6) revealed clear, light-dependent suppression patterns. Under white light, down-regulated genes were mainly associated with primary metabolic and biosynthetic processes (Supplementary Fig. S6a). Under red light, suppressed genes were enriched for ion transport and carbohydrate catabolism–related functions (Supplementary Fig. S6b). Under blue light, down-regulation predominantly affected phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, fungal-response pathways and membrane‑related components (Supplementary Fig. S6c). Full GO term lists for each condition are provided in Supplementary Fig. S6.

The GO enrichment results in up-regulated DEGs are different. Under white light, up-regulated DEGs were enriched in biological processes and molecular functional categories, and most DEGs are involved in “calcium ion transmembrane transport”, “calcium ion transport”, “oxidoreductase activity”, “ω-6 fatty acid desaturase activity” and “calcium ion transmembrane transport protein activity”(Fig. 3d); Under red light, up-regulated DEGs were enriched in biological processes, such as “cell response to nitrogen compounds”, “nuclear cytoplasmic transport” and “nuclear transport”(Fig. 3e); Under blue light, up-regulated DEGs were enriched in all three categories, including “reaction to auxin”, “auxin-activated signaling pathway”, “pigment binding”, “water transmembrane transport protein activity”, and “plastid beads” (Fig. 3f).

Identification of small RNAs translocated from Acremonium sp. D212 to P. notoginseng

To investigate the role of small RNAs in the P. notoginseng-Acremonium sp. D212 interaction, we performed deep sequencing of small RNA libraries from both organisms. After quality filtering, we obtained a total of 61,104,070 high-quality reads for P. notoginseng and 9,464,297 for Acremonium sp. D212. Annotation of these reads identified 443 putative miRNAs in P. notoginseng and 240 in Acremonium sp. D212 (Tables 3 and 4).

We compared read abundances of the annotated small-RNA classes across light conditions. In all three light treatments, miRNAs constituted the largest proportion among annotated small RNAs detected in plant libraries. Notably, the proportion of fungal-derived miRNAs recovered from plant samples was higher under red and blue light than under white light (Fig. 4a–c), suggesting that red and blue light favor fungal miRNA transfer or stability in the host.

We analyzed length distributions of reads mapped to Acremonium and detected in P. notoginseng. Under white light, both annotated small RNAs and unannotated reads showed a dominant peak at 24 nt, and the proportion of 24-nt reads was higher among unannotated reads than among annotated small RNAs (Fig. 4d,g). Under red light, peaks at both 21 nt and 24 nt were observed in annotated and unannotated reads, with an elevated fraction of 24-nt reads among unannotated reads (Fig. 4e,h). Under blue light, annotated and unannotated reads also showed peaks at 21 nt and 24 nt, but the proportion of 24-nt reads was lower in the unannotated fraction compared with annotated small RNAs (Fig. 4f,i). Overall, length distributions in blue-light samples differed from those in white- and red-light samples, suggesting light-dependent differences in the populations of transferred small RNAs.

miRNA qPCR validation and miRNA target analysis in P. notoginseng

Validation and expression patterns of pre-miRNAs

Validation by cloning and sequencing showed that the amplified precursors (pre-miRNA1, pre-miRNA2, pre-miRNA5, pre-miRNA6, pre-miRNA8, and pre-miRNA10) were identical to the in silico predictions (Table S2). Expression profiling under white, red, and blue light revealed light-dependent patterns for all six precursors, with consistently higher abundance in stems than in leaves. Levels were lowest under white light, whereas blue light both increased the number of cloned precursors recovered and produced markedly higher expression (Fig. S7; Fig. 5a).

Target prediction and GO enrichment

Predicted targets of Acremonium-derived miRNAs were obtained using miRanda, psRNATarget and TargetFinder (see Materials and Methods), and were subsequently used for GO enrichment and differential-expression analyses (Fig. S8a). Gene Ontology enrichment of the predicted targets revealed over-representation of cellular component terms related to membranes, including “cell envelope”, “cell outer membrane” and “outer membrane”, suggesting these transferred miRNAs may preferentially target membrane-associated functions (Fig. S8b). Transcript-level analysis under white, red and blue light showed that many of these predicted targets were down-regulated in P. notoginseng (Fig. 5b-d). qRT-PCR validation confirmed the down-regulation observed in the transcriptome data (Fig. 5e–g), supporting the conclusion that miRNAs from Acremonium sp. D212 can reduce expression of specific genes in P. notoginseng.

DEGs among targets and functional implications

DEGs among the predicted miRNA targets were detected under all three light conditions. Under white light, two DEGs were identified and annotated with GO terms including “plasma membrane”, “proton transmembrane transport” and general “metabolic process” (Table S3). In red light, eight DEGs were found, enriched for terms such as “mechanosensitive ion channel activity”, “response to stress” and “plant organ development” (Table S4). Under blue light, twenty‑seven DEGs were detected and associated with functions including “motor activity”, “extracellular region” and various “intracellular” processes (Table S5). Taken together, GO annotation of these targets suggests that Acremonium sp. D212-derived miRNAs preferentially affect membrane‑ and transport‑related transcripts, which could influence molecular exchange between the fungus and P. notoginseng and thereby impact the host’s growth and development.

A cascading network consists of fungus-derived small RNAs and mRNAs in P. notoginseng

We next examined whether Acremonium sp. D212-derived miRNAs trigger phasiRNA biogenesis in P. notoginseng and whether these phasiRNAs affect host gene expression. Using PhaseTank, we identified phasiRNAs across six P. notoginseng small-RNA libraries. In total, 1,932 distinct 21-nt phasiRNAs were detected originating from 281 PHAS loci, and 1241 distinct 24-nt phasiRNAs were detected from 286 PHAS loci. Notably, a small subset of these phasiRNAs appears to be initiated by fungal miRNAs: four 21-nt phasiRNAs (from four PHAS loci) and four 24-nt phasiRNAs (from four PHAS loci) were predicted to be produced following cleavage by Acremonium-derived miRNAs.

We quantified phasiRNA abundance under the three light conditions with and without fungal inoculation (Fig. 6). The overall abundance of 21-nt phasiRNAs did not differ appreciably between inoculated and control samples under white or blue light, but increased upon Acremonium inoculation under red light (Fig. 6a). In contrast, 24-nt phasiRNAs were more responsive to both inoculation and light: their abundance decreased after inoculation under white and red light but increased under blue light (Fig. 6b). These patterns indicate that 24-nt phasiRNAs are more strongly influenced by light regime than 21-nt phasiRNAs.

To predict potential regulatory effects, we identified phasiRNA targets using three complementary algorithms (miRanda, psRNATarget, and TargetFinder) and examined overlaps between tools. Pairwise intersections yielded 6,472 targets predicted by miRanda and TargetFinder, 7590 targets predicted by TargetFinder and psRNATarget, and 6196 targets predicted by miRanda and psRNATarget (Fig. S8c). GO enrichment analysis of the consensus target sets revealed recurring categories: in the cellular component domain, targets were over-represented in terms such as “cell envelope”, “periplasmic space”, and “cell outer membrane”, suggesting potential effects on membrane-associated processes; in the molecular function domain, enriched terms included cofactor/coenzyme binding (Fig. S8d). Intriguingly, several fungus-derived phasiRNA targets mapped to plant hormone metabolism pathways (auxin, abscisic acid, and ethylene), implying possible impacts on hormone-related processes.

We then examined expression changes of phasiRNA target genes following Acremonium inoculation. Under white light, 289 phasiRNA targets were down-regulated and 298 were up-regulated after inoculation (Fig. S9a). Under red light the numbers were 411 down- and 352 up-regulated (Fig. S9b), and under blue light 600 down- and 417 up-regulated (Fig. S9c). Focusing on the down-regulated gene sets, GO enrichment revealed context-dependent patterns: under white light, down-regulated phasiRNA targets were enriched for DNA replication–related terms (e.g., “DNA replication”, “replication fork”, “DNA topoisomerase activity”) (Fig. S10a); under blue light they were enriched for terms linked to binding and energy metabolism (e.g., “vitamin binding”, “energy reserve metabolic process”, “glycogen metabolic process”) (Fig. S10b); under red light no GO terms reached enrichment significance.

To validate selected predictions, we performed qRT–PCR on a subset of P. notoginseng genes targeted by fungus-derived miRNAs and by the resulting miRNA-triggered phasiRNAs. Under white light, eight predicted miRNA target genes showed reduced expression following Acremonium inoculation (Fig. 6c). Several of these miRNA-targeted transcripts gave rise to phasiRNAs; of those, six phasiRNA target genes were confirmed by qRT-PCR to have lower expression after inoculation (Fig. 6d). Comparable validation results were observed under red and blue light (Fig. S11a–d).

Finally, to assess the direct regulatory capacity of the small RNAs, detached P. notoginseng leaves were treated with synthetic miRNA or phasiRNA solutions. Application of the miRNA mixture reduced expression of both the predicted miRNA targets (Fig. 7b) and downstream phasiRNA target genes (Fig. 7c). Treatment with phasiRNA solution alone also led to decreased expression of phasiRNA target genes (Fig. 7d). Together, these results support a model in which fungal miRNAs can initiate phasiRNA production in the host and thereby modulate host gene expression in a light-dependent manner.

Discussion

Understanding the molecular dialogue between P. notoginseng and its root endophyte Acremonium sp. D212 is important for both basic biology and the crop’s medicinal value. In this study, we show that inoculation with Acremonium sp. D212 induces light-dependent changes in the P. notoginseng transcriptome. We further demonstrate that specific fungal-derived miRNAs, such as miR5 and miR10, are transferred into the plant. Inside the host, these miRNAs are presumed to be loaded into AGO complexes to down-regulate predicted target transcripts (Fig. 8d). Some of these targets appear to be PHAS loci, and their cleavage initiates phasiRNA biogenesis, producing a multilayered small RNA cascade that correlates with altered expression of downstream genes. Collectively, these observations reveal a hierarchical small RNA-mediated network linking fungal signals to host transcriptional responses and lay the groundwork for deeper mechanistic investigation.

Schematic model illustrating the cascading network initiated by fungus-derived miRNAs. The pre-miRNA5 and pre-miR10 are transported form the nucleus to the cytoplasm to produce mature miRNAs in Acremonium sp. D212, then these mature miRNAs are transported again to the P. notoginseng cytoplasm through the phospholipid bilayer membrane of P.notonotoginseng. AGO recruit miRNA5 and transcripts in P. notoginseng to assemble into a complex, transcripts are cleaved into phased secondary small interfering RNAs (phasiRNAs) with a length of 21nt or 24nt, the process of phasiRNAs made by miRNA10 is similar to miR5. PhasiRNAs finally trigger the degradation of their target transcripts with AGO by forming a phasiRNA-induced silencing complex. PhasiRNA-induced gene expression silencing influence diverse biological process in P. notoginseng, including material transportation, plant growth and so on. When miR5 and miR10 do not take part in producing phasiRNAs, both these miRNAs also affect the material transportation and plant growth in P. notoginseng.

MiRNAs are small RNAs that can influence gene expression in plants and animals23–25. Our finding that Acremonium-derived miRNAs suppress host gene expression is consistent with reported cross-kingdom RNA interactions, for example, cotton miR166 and miR159 can move into Verticillium dahliae and reduce expression of fungal virulence genes11. The route by which fungal miRNAs enter P. notoginseng, however, remains unresolved. Previous studies suggest several possible mechanisms: direct uptake of naked small RNAs potentially facilitated by RNA-binding proteins (RBPs)26, and vesicle-mediated transfer, as supported by reports of plant-derived extracellular vesicles delivering small RNAs to fungi27,28 and by observations of vesicle-like structures at the fungal plasma membrane in Arabidopsis thaliana29. Moreover, fungal miRNAs can modulate transport processes; for instance, miR399 and miR397 influence lipid transport from arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi to maize30. Targeted experiments—such as labeled-RNA tracking, disruption of vesicle formation, or functional tests of candidate RBPs—will be required to determine the transfer mechanism in the Acremonium-P. notoginseng system.

We also observed heterogeneous responses among phasiRNA targets: some showed down-regulation consistent with direct phasiRNA action, while others were up-regulated. Similar mixed patterns have been reported in rice31 and likely reflect integration of multiple regulatory inputs. A gene’s steady-state expression is shaped not only by phasiRNAs but also by transcription factors, other small RNAs, feedback circuits, and post-transcriptional regulation; thus, the net outcome can vary depending on the broader regulatory context. Disentangling these relationships will benefit from perturbation studies (loss- and gain-of-function of specific miRNAs/phasiRNAs), time-course profiling, and network modeling.

More broadly, our data extend known miRNA-TF regulatory modules in plants (e.g., Arabidopsis)32 by revealing that fungal miRNAs and derived phasiRNAs can participate in and modulate host co-expression networks that influence diverse biological processes, including plant hormone signaling and material transport. Previous work has also documented regulatory relationships among miRNAs, phasiRNAs and their targets in Arabidopsis thaliana33, consistent with the multilayered interactions we report here. Importantly, the light-dependence of these effects suggests that environmental cues modulate the composition or efficacy of cross-kingdom small RNA signaling, with potential consequences for host growth and secondary metabolism.

Conclusions

We show that Acremonium sp. D212 can deliver miRNAs into P. notoginseng, where these fungal miRNAs directly repress host mRNAs or trigger phasiRNA production that amplifies regulatory effects on additional targets. This light-dependent small RNA exchange establishes a hierarchical regulatory network that contributes to host transcriptome remodeling. Our results provide a molecular framework for future mechanistic studies aimed at (i) defining the route and machinery of small RNA transfer between fungus and plant, (ii) experimentally validating key miRNA/phasiRNA–target interactions and their physiological consequences, and (iii) mapping the spatial–temporal dynamics of these interactions under varying light regimes. Ultimately, elucidating these pathways may inform strategies to harness beneficial endophytes for improving growth or secondary metabolite production in P. notoginseng, but translation to application will require rigorous functional validation.

Methods

Identification of Acremonium sp. D212, Permission for collecting the species, voucher specimens and the source or origin of the plant material

The strain Acremonium sp. D212 used in this study was identified by Professor Yunlong Du. The strain has been reported in the Journal of Integrative Plant Biology34. It has been deposited in the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC, http://www.cgmcc.net) under the accession number 12374.

As a common endophytic fungus of P. notoginseng, this strain can be isolated from the plant. It functions as a beneficial endophyte that promotes the growth of P. notoginseng9 and can be isolated and cultured in standard laboratories. We have deposited this strain in the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC, http://www.cgmcc.net). Additionally, we have uploaded the deposit certificate documents.

In this study, Panax notoginseng seedlings were obtained from the Daheqiao Farm of Yunnan Agricultural University (103° 16′ 49″ E, 25° 31′ 2″ N) (Han et al., 2020). Through tissue culture methods, we obtained P. notoginseng tissue-cultured seedlings and developed relevant experiments for this study.

Plant and fungus growth conditions

To detect the effects of red, blue, and white light on the colonization of P. notoginseng by Acremonium sp. D212, P. notoginseng tissue culture seedlings were grown on MS medium. After inoculating the mycelium of fungus Acremonium sp. D212 on the stem base of P. notoginseng tissue culture seedlings, they were co-cultured for 12 days under red (640.1 nm), blue (457.4 nm), and white light (528.5 nm) for 12 h under light, 12 h under dark conditions, and 22 °C.

To assess the effect of miRNAs and phasiRNAs on the expression levels of miRNA and phasiRNA target genes (Table S6), the P. notoginseng leaves were treated with miRNA or phasiRNA solution following previously published reports35,36. The two-year old P. notoginseng seedlings were grown in a greenhouse at 25 °C. Twenty microliters of miRNAs (20 ng/μL) and phasiRNA (20 ng/μL) (Table S7) were dropped onto the surface of P. notoginseng seedlings leaves, respectively, and 24 h later, the leaves treated with miRNAs or phasiRNAs were collected to analyze the expression levels of miRNA and phasiRNA target genes.

To detect the colonization of endophytic fungus Acremonium sp. D212 inoculated with the roots of P. notoginseng tissue cultured seedlings grown under red light, blue light, and white light, isolation of fungal genomic DNA in the P. notoginseng roots and expression of the fungal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of the ribosomal DNA (rDNA) gene amplified with the primer pairs ITS1 and ITS437 (Table S8) was referred to previous report34.

To test the spores of fungus Acremonium sp. D212, spores of 15-day old colony of Acremonium sp. D212 grown on PDA medium under white light, red light and blue light, respectively, were washed and collected with sterilized water, and counted with hemocytometer using microscope. To test the fungus growth, the Acremonium sp. D212 grown on PDA medium under white light, red light and blue light, respectively, the cross plot method was used to measure the fungus colony diameter at a fixed time every day.

Acremonium sp. D212 genome assembly and assessment

Canu v1 was used for the correction of filtering reads38, then genome assembly was conducted by wtdbg218. BUSCO v3 was used for the evaluation of genome completeness39. To predict de novo gene, Augustus v2 was conduct for ab initio based prediction40, GeMoMa v1 for the prediction based on homology41, TransDecoder v2 for RNA-seq based Unigene sequence’s prediction (http://transdecoder.github.io), and integrated by EVM v142. Furthermore, tRNAscan-SE43 and Infernal 144 were conducted for predicting tRNA and rRNA. De novo genes were undergo further analysis for function annotation with databases, including Gene Ontology19, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes20, NCBI’s non-redundant (Nr) database21, carbohydrate activate enzyme (CAZyme)45, Transport Classification DataBase for membrane transport (TCDB)46, and Pathogen Host Interactions (PHI)47 databases. Single peptide prediction was conducted by the software SignalP v448. Transmembrane proteins were predicted by the software Tmhmm49.

Transcriptome and small RNA-seq analysis of P. notoginseng seedlings and fungus Acremonium sp. D212

The stems of P. notoginseng tissue cultured seedlings inoculated with Acremonium sp. D212 for 12 days, as well as the Acremonium sp. D212 mycelium growing at the base of the stem of P. notoginseng tissue cultured seedlings grown under red light, blue light, and white light were used for transcriptome and small RNA-seq. The isolation, measurement, quantification of total mRNA and small RNA, construction of the RNA library, and the paired-end sequencing of sample cDNA and small RNA sequencing from the P. notoginseng stems and Acremonium sp. D212 were performed by the Novogene Co., LTD (Beijing, China). The stems of P. notoginseng tissue cultured seedlings treated with red light, blue light, and white light but not inoculated with Acremonium sp. D212, as well as Acremonium sp. D212 mycelium grown on 1/2MS medium, were used as controls.

The transcriptome date (accession no. CRA014807) and small RNA-seq data (accession no. CRA014827) from the P. notoginseng stems and Acremonium sp. D212 were submitted to the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) public database (http://gsa.big.ac.cn). Raw reads were quality-checked with FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and trimmed for adapters and low-quality bases using Trim Galore (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/). RNA libraries from 18 samples yielded a total of 787,766,760 clean reads. Clean reads had Q30 values ranging from 92.6 to 95.1%. Reads were aligned to the the reference genome using HISAT250, and transcript assembly/quantification was performed with StringTie51. Per-sample mapping rates ranged from 89.3 to 94.6% (see Table 1). Differential expression in P. notoginseng across light conditions and after Acremonium sp. D212 exposure was assessed with DESeq2 using expression data from all unigenes52. Genes with p-values below 0.05 and absolute log2 fold-change ≥ 1 were considered DEGs. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess biological replicate agreement (Formula1).

With n unigenes, Xi and Yi represent the expression of unigene i in sample X and sample Y, respectively; and are the average expression levels across unigenes for the two samples. The Pearson correlation Rx, y lies between − 1 and 1, where a value near + 1 implies strong agreement and that the datasets are reliable replicates.

The enrichment analysis employed the R package clusterProfiler53, applying a hypergeometric distribution (Formula 2).

Small RNAs identification and small RNAs target prediction

Small RNA annotation in P. notoginseng was performed as follows. Reads corresponding to known structural and housekeeping small RNAs (tRNAs, snRNAs, sRNAs, and rRNAs) were annotated by comparison to the Rfam database using BLASTN with parameters optimized for short sequences. Plant miRNAs in P. notoginseng were predicted using the plant-specific miRNA prediction tool miRDeep-P254.

To detect potential miRNA transfer between organisms, we first removed all small RNA reads that mapped to the P. notoginseng genome. The remaining reads were then mapped to the Acremonium sp. D212 genome, and candidate fungal-derived miRNAs were predicted with mireap (http://sourceforge.net/projects/mireap/). An analogous workflow (removing reads mapping to the Acremonium genome and mapping the remainder to the P. notoginseng genome) was used to identify potential miRNAs transferred from P. notoginseng to Acremonium sp. D212.

Phased secondary siRNAs (phasiRNAs) of 21 nt and 24 nt length in P. notoginseng were identified using PhaseTank55; the phased score reported by PhaseTank was used as the primary criterion for calling PHAS loci. Predicted miRNA and phasiRNA targets were identified using three complementary target-prediction programs: miRanda56, TargetFinder57, and psRNATarget58. Predictions supported by multiple algorithms were considered more reliable.

Identification and expression of miRNA in fungus Acremonium sp. D212

To identify the miRNA of endophytic fungus Acremonium sp. D212, the total RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis of the of Acremonium sp. D212 grown in MS medium were referred to previous report59. The primer pairs of pre-miRNA were designed according to the small RNA-seq data of Acremonium sp. D212. The 20 μL PCR reaction mixture contained 1 μL of cDNA template, 10 μL 2 × Hieff Canace ® Glod PCR Master Mix (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai), 2 μL of a 10 μM primer pairs and 7 μL sterilized water. The following primer pairs: miRNA1-FP1 and miRNA1-RP1, pre-d212-miR2 -FP and pre-d212-miR2 –RP, pre-d212-miR5 -FP and pre-d212-miR5-RP, pre-d212-miR6-FP and pre-d212-miR6-RP, pre-d212-miR8 -FP and pre-d212-miR8 –RP, pre-d212-miR10 -FP and pre-d212-miR10 -RP (Table S9) were used to amplify the miRNAs pre-miRNA1, pre-miRNA2, pre-miRNA5, pre-miRNA6, pre-miRNA8 and pre-miRNA10, respectively. The PCR was performed under the following conditions: denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 50 s, 52 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 90 s, and 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified PCR products of the pre-miRNA genes were cloned into the vector pBluescript sk (-) and transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α, the plasmid of positive single colony screened by PCR were sequenced by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Kunming, China).

To detect the expression of miRNAs in the Acremonium sp. D212 grown on MS medium under white light, red light and blue light, the relative quantitative gene expression levels were determined using an ABI QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The 10 μL PCR reaction mixture contained 5 μL 2 × NovoStart SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (Novoprotein Scientific lnc, Shanghai), 0.2 μL ROX Reference Dye Ⅰ,0.5 μL of cDNA template, 3.5 μL H2O and 0.8 μL of a 10 μM primer pairs solution. The following primer pairs: miRNA1-FP1 and miRNA1-RP1, miRNA2-FP1 and miRNA2-RP1, miRNA5-FP1 and miRNA5-RP1, miRNA6-FP1 and miRNA6-RP1, miRNA8-FP1 and miRNA8-RP1, miRNA10-FP1 and miRNA10-RP1 (Table S8) were used to amplify the miRNA genes pre-miRNA1, pre-miRNA2, pre-miRNA5, pre-miRNA6, pre-miRNA8 and pre-miRNA10, respectively. The Acrem-actin(GenBank: JN836730) gene of fungus Acremonium sp. D212 acted as the internal control, and was amplified using the primer pair Acrem-actin-rFP and Acrem-actin-rRP (Table S8). qRT-PCR amplifications were carried out with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 45 s, annealing at 54–60 °C for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. Three independent biological replicates were included. Relative expression was calculated by the comparative Ct (2^−ΔΔCt) method and reported as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test in SPSS v19.060; *P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and **P < 0.01 highly significant.
