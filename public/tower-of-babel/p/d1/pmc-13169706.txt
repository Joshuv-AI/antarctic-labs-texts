Cutaneous adverse drug reactions in patients with congenital heart disease: a systematic review with focus on perioperative outcomes

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCutaneous adverse drug reactions (CADRs) are uncommon but clinically significant complications among patients with congenital heart disease (CHD), particularly those with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) or perioperative hemodynamic instability. The complex pharmacologic regimens required in CHD—often involving prostaglandins, vasodilators, antibiotics, and antiepileptics—predispose patients to idiosyncratic and immune-mediated reactions. Despite increasing recognition of CADRs in cardiovascular medicine, systematic evidence focusing on CHD populations remains limited.MethodsA systematic search of MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Web of Science, and CENTRAL was conducted from database inception to October 31, 2024, covering all types of congenital heart disease, both cyanotic and acyanotic, isolated and syndromic forms. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials, observational studies, and case reports that reported CADRs in patients with structural CHD, regardless of age or surgical status. Extracted outcomes included type of CADR, implicated drug, temporal association, histopathology, management, and clinical outcome. Study quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for case reports and case series.ResultsThirteen studies encompassing 22 patients met the inclusion criteria. Most patients (68%) were neonates or infants with duct-dependent congenital heart disease receiving prostaglandin E₁ (alprostadil) for lesions such as transposition of the great arteries, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, and Taussig–Bing anomaly. The most frequently reported CADRs were prostaglandin-associated vasomotor eruptions, including migratory urticaria and erythematous patches (n = 4, 18%), as well as radiographically detected brown fat necrosis and soft-tissue calcification following prolonged prostaglandin infusion (n = 9, 41%). Severe hypersensitivity reactions—including drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) and Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS)—occurred in patients receiving antiepileptics or antibiotics (n = 3, 14%), typically in postoperative or intensive care settings. Additional reports described bosentan-associated indurated erythema in an adult with repaired tetralogy of Fallot and amoxicillin-associated morbilliform exanthem in pediatric patients after surgical repair. Overall, prostaglandin analogs accounted for the majority of implicated drugs (77%), followed by antiepileptics (9%), antibiotics (9%), and pulmonary vasodilators (5%). Methodological quality of the included studies was generally low according to the Joanna Briggs Institute appraisal tools due to descriptive study designs and limited follow-up.ConclusionsCADRs, though infrequently reported, occur across the therapeutic continuum of CHD management—from neonatal prostaglandin therapy to adult antimicrobial and antiepileptic use. The predominance of prostaglandin-associated reactions underscores the importance of dermatologic and metabolic surveillance during infusion therapy, while severe whereas severe hypersensitivity reactions underscore the interface between cardiology and dermatology. Larger multicenter studies are warranted to clarify incidence, risk factors, and outcomes, guiding safer pharmacologic management in congenital cardiology.

Introduction

Cutaneous adverse drug reactions (CADRs) are among the most frequent adverse drug events encountered in clinical practice, ranging from mild exanthematous eruptions to life-threatening severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) such as Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), and drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) [1, 2]. While the general incidence of CADRs in hospitalized patients has been estimated at 1–3%, SCARs occur at a much lower frequency, approximately 2–7 cases per million person-years, yet carry mortality rates exceeding 20% in TEN and 10% in DRESS [3–5]. In patients with congenital heart disease (CHD), CADRs may pose an even greater clinical challenge due to frequent polypharmacy, altered pharmacokinetics, and the need for lifelong cardiovascular medications.

Eisenmenger syndrome (ES), representing the extreme end of pulmonary arterial hypertension secondary to unrepaired CHD, constitutes a particularly vulnerable subgroup [6, 7]. Patients with ES are often exposed to a broad range of cardiopulmonary therapies including endothelin receptor antagonists, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, anticoagulants, and diuretics, each of which carries potential risk for hypersensitivity or dermatologic toxicity [8, 9]. Importantly, ES patients have fragile hemodynamics and limited cardiopulmonary reserve, such that the occurrence of a severe CADR requiring hospitalization, systemic corticosteroids, or interruption of therapy may significantly increase perioperative and long-term mortality [10, 11]. Beyond hemodynamic fragility, cyanotic and hypoxemic states alter hepatic drug metabolism and tissue oxygenation, amplifying oxidative stress and endothelial permeability that predispose to dermatologic and systemic hypersensitivity responses.

Perioperative outcomes in CHD, especially in patients undergoing corrective or palliative cardiac surgery, are known to be adversely influenced by comorbid conditions and drug intolerance. Large surgical registry analyses indicate that operative mortality in adult CHD patients undergoing cardiac procedures ranges from 3 to 8%, with higher rates in those with pulmonary hypertension or cyanotic lesions [12, 13]. Eisenmenger patients remain at particularly high risk: historical series report perioperative mortality rates exceeding 20% in this group, and contemporary outcomes, while improved, continue to reflect excess risk compared with other CHD subgroups [14, 15]. The additive impact of CADRs in this perioperative context has not been systematically examined, despite case reports linking perioperative drug exposure (e.g., antibiotics, anticoagulants, anesthetic adjuncts) with severe dermatologic reactions complicating recovery [16]. Recent pharmacovigilance studies have identified multiple risk factors for ADRs in congenital heart disease, including high medication burden, dose intensity, and comorbid metabolic or hepatic dysfunction [Health Sci Rep. 2023;10.1002/hsr2.70835]. A recent scoping review of ADRs in CHD further emphasized the need for systematic drug safety evaluation across cardiovascular therapies [Eur J Clin Pharmacol. 2024;10.1007/s40272-024-00644-8]. Unlike these broad analyses, the present review specifically focuses on cutaneous and perioperative adverse drug reactions, aiming to define their clinical patterns and perioperative relevance.

Despite the recognized vulnerability of patients with CHD to multisystem complications and pharmacologic sensitivity, there remains no comprehensive synthesis of CADR within this population. Existing evidence is scattered across isolated case reports, pharmacovigilance data, and small observational studies, which limits the ability of clinicians to estimate incidence, identify drug-specific risk factors, or anticipate clinical outcomes. A systematic review consolidating this fragmented evidence provides an opportunity to (i) map the epidemiology and phenotypic spectrum of CADRs in CHD, and (ii) evaluate their perioperative and clinical implications on morbidity and mortality. By integrating data from case series, observational studies, and perioperative reports, this review aims to clarify the clinical significance of CADRs in CHD and generate actionable insights for cardiologists, dermatologists, anesthesiologists, and multidisciplinary congenital heart teams.

Methods

Protocol and reporting

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.1 The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD420251161840).

Eligibility criteria

We considered eligible for inclusion all studies that reported CADR in patients with CHD, including both cyanotic and acyanotic lesions, as well as postoperative or perioperative settings. Eligible study designs included randomized controlled trials, non-randomized interventional studies, observational cohorts, case–control studies, case series, and individual case reports, provided that primary patient data were available. Narrative reviews, editorials, commentaries, and studies lacking original data were excluded. Patients of any age with structural or functional congenital heart defects were eligible. Drug exposures of interest encompassed cardiovascular and perioperative medications commonly used in CHD populations, including prostaglandins, pulmonary vasodilators, anticoagulants, antiarrhythmics, diuretics, and antibiotics. The primary outcome of interest was the occurrence of CADRs—ranging from benign exanthematous eruptions and urticaria to severe manifestations such as DRESS, SJS/TEN, and skin necrosis—while secondary outcomes included their clinical course, management strategies, perioperative implications, and mortality. No restrictions on publication year or language were applied.

Information sources and search strategy

We searched MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from inception to 31 October 2024. In addition, reference lists of all included studies and pertinent review articles were manually screened to identify further eligible publications. The search strategy incorporated both controlled vocabulary (MeSH/Emtree terms) and free-text keywords related to “cutaneous adverse drug reaction,” “drug eruption,” “Stevens–Johnson syndrome,” “toxic epidermal necrolysis,” “DRESS,” “skin necrosis,” “photosensitivity,” and “drug hypersensitivity,” combined with terms for “congenital heart disease,” “cyanotic heart disease,” “pulmonary arterial hypertension,” “perioperative cardiac surgery,” and “congenital cardiac repair.” An example of the PubMed search strategy is as follows: (“cutaneous adverse drug reaction” OR “drug eruption” OR “Stevens–Johnson syndrome” OR “toxic epidermal necrolysis” OR “DRESS”) AND (“congenital heart disease” OR “cyanotic heart disease” OR “Eisenmenger syndrome” OR “perioperative cardiac surgery”).

Study selection

All records were imported into a reference manager, and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers (R.M.J. and K.K.) independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Full texts were retrieved for all potentially relevant articles. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (S.S.). Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented. A PRISMA flow diagram illustrates the selection process.

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (R.M.J. and K.K.) using a predesigned extraction form. Extracted variables included: study characteristics (author, year, country, design, sample size), patient demographics, CHD subtype (with Eisenmenger or cyanotic physiology noted), drug exposure (name, dose, indication), CADR characteristics (latency, morphology, severity), management strategies (drug withdrawal, systemic therapy, perioperative adjustments), and outcomes (hospitalization, perioperative complications, mortality). For RCTs or cohort studies, denominators and numerators for dermatologic adverse events were extracted where available.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated according to study design. RCTs were assessed with the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool.2 Non-randomized studies were evaluated using the ROBINS-I tool.3 Case series and case reports were appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists for case reports and case series.4 Two reviewers (R.M.J. and K.K.) performed assessments independently, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Studies with higher JBI scores were given greater interpretative weight when summarizing mechanistic patterns, whereas those rated low were included descriptively without inferential emphasis.

Data synthesis and analysis

Although a random-effects meta-analysis was initially planned in the protocol, quantitative pooling was not performed because all included studies were descriptive case reports or small case series lacking comparable denominators. General characteristics of the included studies

Thirteen studies [17–29] encompassing a total of 22 individual patients with CHD were included in this review. Across the reports, patients ranged from neonates (n = 15) to adults (n = 7), reflecting the broad vulnerability of both pediatric and adult CHD populations to drug-induced dermatologic reactions. The majority of reports originated from tertiary care cardiac or neonatal intensive care centers, underscoring the complexity and fragility of these cases. All included studies underwent methodological quality assessment using the JBI Critical Appraisal tools appropriate for case reports and case series. Consistently, the overall JBI appraisal results were rated as low, reflecting inherent limitations in evidence strength due to single-patient observations, incomplete follow-up, and lack of standardized diagnostic confirmation across cases. Of the 22 patients, 15 (68%) were neonates or infants and 7 (32%) were adolescents or adults. Reporting heterogeneity was marked, with neonatal cases detailing dosing parameters, whereas adult postoperative reports emphasized immune findings and systemic complications.

The distribution of underlying cardiac lesions was diverse, with TGA and hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) being the most common [18–21, 26–29], followed by tetralogy of Fallot [1], single ventricle physiology [28], and atrioventricular canal defect [23, 24]. A small number of adult patients had complex post-repair CHD with implanted devices or pulmonary hypertension [17, 24].

The culprit drugs implicated were mainly prostaglandin analogs (PGE₁ or PGE₂) used for ductal patency maintenance (n = 17), followed by antiepileptics (n = 2; oxcarbazepine and carbamazepine), antibiotics (n = 2; amoxicillin and vancomycin), and bosentan, an endothelin receptor antagonist (n = 1). These agents were administered in critical-care contexts—often as life-saving measures—emphasizing the clinical tension between therapeutic necessity and adverse risk in CHD management.

Overall, cutaneous manifestations occurred between 6 h and 8 weeks after drug initiation, with variable severity. The mortality rate was approximately 23% (5 of 22 patients), primarily driven by systemic complications of DRESS or cardiac deterioration rather than dermatologic causes. Across all studies, prostaglandin-associated reactions represented the predominant category, followed by hypersensitivity to antiepileptic and antibiotic agents. The temporal clustering of prostaglandin-induced reactions in the preoperative phase and immune-mediated syndromes in the postoperative phase reflects pharmacologic exposure patterns inherent to CHD management.

Incidence of CADRs in pre- and postoperative settings

Among the included cases, 17 of 22 (77%) reactions occurred in the preoperative phase—primarily related to prostaglandin infusion for ductal-dependent lesions—whereas 5 (23%) reactions occurred postoperatively, mainly following antibiotic or antiepileptic administration. Preoperative reactions were mostly vasomotor and transient, while postoperative events were immune-mediated hypersensitivity syndromes with higher morbidity and mortality. This temporal distribution highlights distinct pharmacologic exposures before and after surgery and underscores the importance of phase-specific surveillance.

Prostaglandin-induced eruptions and brown fat necrosis

Acute vasomotor eruptions [18–21]

The most frequently described reactions were acute erythematous or urticarial eruptions developing within days of prostaglandin initiation. These lesions typically appeared as transient, migratory, and polycyclic erythema, involving the trunk, face, and proximal limbs, with sharp demarcation and rapid evolution over hours. All patients were neonates with ductal-dependent lesions, including transposition of the great arteries or complex coarctation. The rash intensity was tightly correlated with infusion rate—exacerbating with higher doses and resolving when prostaglandin was tapered or withdrawn.

No mucosal involvement, hypotension, or systemic signs were observed. Histology, when performed, revealed minimal dermal inflammation and vasodilatation, consistent with a non-immunologic mechanism. The pharmacologic vasodilatory action of PGE₁ likely induces capillary hyperpermeability, explaining the blanching nature and dose dependency. This vasomotor hypersensitivity is likely specific to neonatal dermal microvasculature, where endothelial reactivity and thermoregulation are immature.

These findings emphasize that not all cutaneous eruptions in CHD neonates imply allergic hypersensitivity; rather, vascular tone dysregulation in a prostaglandin-dependent circulation can mimic dermatologic inflammation. Recognizing this prevents unnecessary cessation of life-sustaining therapy.

Brown fat necrosis and soft tissue calcification [26–29]

A distinct subset of prostaglandin-associated lesions was brown adipose tissue (BAT) necrosis presenting as calcific densities on radiographic imaging in the neck, axilla, and scapular regions. This phenomenon was described in four individual case reports and one case series (nine patients). All affected neonates had cyanotic CHD requiring prolonged prostaglandin infusion for systemic oxygenation or postoperative stabilization.

The latency between therapy initiation and calcification detection ranged from two to eight weeks, often after cumulative prostaglandin doses exceeding 5,000 µg/kg. The pattern of calcification corresponded anatomically to brown fat depots, suggesting ischemic necrosis secondary to vasomotor and metabolic stress. Notably, in the case series of nine infants [12], hypercalcemia and hypertriglyceridemia were present in over half of the patients, supporting a metabolic basis for tissue injury.

Autopsy and histologic data in several reports revealed dystrophic mineralization with preserved dermal structures and no inflammatory infiltrate, distinguishing BAT necrosis from panniculitis or calciphylaxis. The temporal relationship between prostaglandin use and calcification regression after discontinuation supports causality.

Pathophysiologically, BAT necrosis likely arises from prostaglandin-mediated vasodilation superimposed on chronic hypoxia, leading to local ischemia within high-metabolic adipose depots. While self-limiting radiographically, this entity serves as an early biomarker of systemic oxidative stress and energy imbalance in prostaglandin-dependent neonates. None of the deaths in these cases were directly attributable to the skin lesions; mortality stemmed from cardiac failure or multi-organ dysfunction.

Vasodilator-associated indurated dermatitis [17]

An adult female with surgically corrected tetralogy of Fallot and Eisenmenger physiology developed indurated erythematous plaques after one month of bosentan therapy for pulmonary arterial hypertension. Histopathology revealed mild perivascular lymphocytic infiltration without vasculitis, aligning with a delayed hypersensitivity reaction. The lesions resolved following bosentan withdrawal without recurrence.

Although bosentan is known for hepatic toxicity, cutaneous hypersensitivity remains exceedingly rare, particularly in CHD-associated PAH. This case demonstrates that endothelin receptor antagonism can elicit immune-mediated dermal inflammation, possibly through endothelial or cytokine modulation. Given the expanding use of targeted PAH therapies in congenital cardiology, awareness of such idiosyncratic reactions is clinically pertinent.

Antiepileptic-induced severe cutaneous adverse reactions [22, 25]

Two pediatric CHD patients experienced severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) secondary to antiepileptic therapy. In the first [22], a post-operative patient developed SJS following sequential exposure to oxcarbazepine and levetiracetam, characterized by confluent erythematous macules, mucosal ulceration, and systemic fever. Withdrawal of the agents and corticosteroid therapy led to full recovery.

In the second (case 9), carbamazepine triggered DRESS syndrome with eosinophilia, hepatic failure, and vanishing bile duct syndrome, resulting in fatal multiorgan failure despite corticosteroid and antiviral therapy.

These cases highlight the heightened immune reactivity in CHD, possibly potentiated by altered hepatic metabolism, systemic hypoxia, and impaired detoxification. Notably, both patients were negative for HLA-B*1502, suggesting that factors beyond genetic predisposition contribute to SCAR risk in this cohort. The findings underscore the need for cautious drug selection and early dermatologic consultation in post-operative CHD patients requiring anticonvulsants.

Antibiotic-related reactions [23, 24]

Three cases documented antibiotic-associated cutaneous reactions in patients with congenital heart disease. Two young children with repaired atrioventricular canal defects developed morbilliform drug eruptions after 7–9 days of amoxicillin for otitis media. The exanthems were non-pruritic, self-limited, and resolved upon drug discontinuation, with no systemic sequelae.

Conversely, an adult male with complex repaired CHD and device infection developed vancomycin-induced DRESS syndrome after 24 days of therapy, presenting with fever, eosinophilia, hepatitis, and lymphadenopathy. Clinical improvement followed withdrawal of vancomycin and corticosteroid initiation.

Together, these reports illustrate the wide severity spectrum of antibiotic-related cutaneous hypersensitivity in CHD—from benign exanthematous eruptions to life-threatening systemic reactions. Repeated antibiotic exposure in these patients—due to endocarditis prophylaxis or device infections—likely promotes immune sensitization and reactivation of latent viruses (e.g., HHV-6), which can amplify DRESS pathogenesis.

Temporal dynamics and clinical outcomes

Latency from initial drug exposure to rash onset ranged widely:6–48 h for prostaglandin-induced urticaria,2–8 weeks for brown fat necrosis, and3–5 weeks for delayed hypersensitivity reactions such as SJS or DRESS.

Clinical and research implications

The synthesized evidence underscores that cutaneous adverse drug reactions in CHD are not isolated dermatologic curiosities but rather systemic reflections of cardiovascular and metabolic instability.

For clinicians, early identification of these rashes should prompt consideration of drug withdrawal or dose adjustment, but also serve as a warning sign of endothelial dysfunction or immune dysregulation.

For researchers, the heterogeneity of manifestations across prostaglandins, antibiotics, and antiepileptics suggests a need for prospective pharmacovigilance registries focused on CHD populations.

Furthermore, imaging recognition of brown fat necrosis could be developed as a noninvasive biomarker for metabolic stress in neonates on prolonged prostaglandin therapy. Similarly, systematic immunogenetic profiling could clarify the determinants of hypersensitivity in CHD patients beyond traditional HLA risk alleles.

Discussion

This systematic review consolidates currently scattered evidence on CADR among patients with CHD, a population characterized by profound physiological vulnerability and frequent polypharmacy. From the thirteen reports encompassing twenty-two individual patients, several consistent pathophysiologic themes emerge that bridge the fields of dermatology, cardiology, and pharmacology. Although the reported cases differ in age, underlying anatomy, and therapeutic exposure, they collectively highlight how circulatory instability, metabolic stress, and immune dysregulation interact to produce distinct but interrelated dermatologic manifestations.

The majority of reactions involved prostaglandin analogs (PGE₁ or PGE₂) used in neonates with ductal-dependent cyanotic CHD. In this setting, two characteristic cutaneous patterns were observed: transient erythematous or urticarial eruptions and delayed-onset BFN. The former typically appeared within hours of initiating prostaglandin infusion and was closely correlated with dose intensity, often subsiding upon tapering or discontinuation [18–21]. These lesions likely represent vasomotor reactions driven by prostaglandin-mediated endothelial dilation rather than immunologic hypersensitivity. In contrast, BFN manifested radiographically after prolonged prostaglandin therapy, presenting as calcific densities within axillary, supraclavicular, and paraspinal soft tissues—regions rich in metabolically active brown adipose tissue [26–29]. Histopathologic and biochemical findings across several reports support an ischemic, rather than inflammatory, mechanism: prostaglandin-induced vasodilatation superimposed on chronic hypoxemia and high oxygen consumption in brown fat creates a microenvironment conducive to adipocyte necrosis and dystrophic calcification. Recognition of this entity is clinically significant, as BFN may serve as a radiologic biomarker of systemic oxidative stress and metabolic exhaustion in prostaglandin-dependent neonates, rather than a benign or incidental finding.

Beyond prostaglandin-related events, the review identifies severe immune-mediated reactions among CHD patients treated with other pharmacologic agents. Two postoperative pediatric patients experienced life-threatening hypersensitivity syndromes following antiepileptic therapy—one with Stevens–Johnson syndrome after oxcarbazepine and levetiracetam [22] and another with fatal DRESS following carbamazepine [25]. These cases occurred despite negative HLA-B*1502 testing, implying that hemodynamic compromise, hepatic congestion, and systemic hypoxia can independently modulate immune reactivity and drug metabolism, predisposing CHD patients to exaggerated immunologic responses. Similarly, an adult patient developed vancomycin-induced DRESS with eosinophilia, hepatitis, and HHV-6 reactivation [24], illustrating how chronic inflammation and polypharmacy can precipitate delayed hypersensitivity in the presence of impaired antigen clearance. A milder but mechanistically related reaction was observed in a patient receiving bosentan for CHD-associated pulmonary hypertension, who developed indurated erythematous plaques consistent with delayed-type drug hypersensitivity [17]. Collectively, these immune-mediated reactions reveal that CHD itself may act as an immunometabolic amplifier, increasing susceptibility to T-cell–mediated drug toxicity even in the absence of classical pharmacogenetic risk factors.

When interpreted together, these cases delineate a continuum from vascular to metabolic to immune mechanisms of cutaneous injury. At the vascular end, transient prostaglandin-induced erythema reflects a direct pharmacodynamic effect on dermal circulation [18–21]. At the metabolic midpoint, brown fat necrosis embodies ischemic stress and tissue hypoxia within an energetically active environment [26–29]. At the immune end, hypersensitivity syndromes represent maladaptive immune activation leading to multiorgan dysfunction [17, 22, 24, 25]. Each mechanism can coexist or sequentially evolve within the same patient, given the overlapping pathophysiologic substrates of CHD—namely endothelial dysfunction, chronic hypoxia, and systemic oxidative stress. Thus, dermatologic reactions in CHD are not isolated phenomena but visible extensions of underlying cardiovascular physiology.

Clinically, the recognition of CADRs in CHD has important implications for early diagnosis and therapeutic decision-making. In neonates dependent on prostaglandin therapy [18–21, 26–29], the appearance of erythema, induration, or calcific deposits should prompt metabolic and hemodynamic assessment, not necessarily discontinuation of therapy. Radiologic monitoring may aid in detecting early signs of brown fat necrosis, allowing timely adjustments to drug dosage before irreversible tissue injury occurs. In older children and adults, vigilance for hypersensitivity markers—such as rash accompanied by eosinophilia, lymphadenopathy, or hepatic enzyme elevation—is essential, especially in the postoperative period or during prolonged antibiotic or antiepileptic use [22–25]. Multidisciplinary coordination between cardiologists, dermatologists, and clinical pharmacologists is critical to ensure prompt identification and management of these reactions, balancing drug efficacy with patient safety.

The findings of this review also advance a broader conceptual understanding: congenital heart disease is not merely a structural defect but a systemic disorder influencing pharmacokinetics, immune regulation, and tissue oxygenation. The convergence of hypoxia, hepatic congestion, and inflammation enhances susceptibility to both pharmacologic and immune-mediated injury. Recognizing the skin as a diagnostic mirror of systemic physiology opens new perspectives for integrated monitoring—where dermatologic changes can serve as early indicators of metabolic distress or inflammatory activation in complex cardiac patients. Beyond clinical recognition, these findings underscore the need for system-level strategies to enhance drug safety in CHD. Integration of electronic health record–based pharmacovigilance, standardized perioperative drug protocols, and personalized dosing algorithms can mitigate risk. Policy frameworks promoting machine-learning–driven ADR detection and equitable access to dermatologic consultation have been advocated in recent policy briefs [Health Serv Insights. 2025;10.1177/00469580251335805; Front Digit Health. 2024;10.1177/2333794X241291398], and their adoption could transform reactive case reporting into proactive prevention.

Limitations

The present review is limited by the nature and quality of the available data. All included studies were single case reports or small case series [17–29], lacking standardized diagnostic criteria, control groups, and quantitative causality assessment such as the Naranjo scale. Reporting bias likely favored publication of severe or unusual cases, while mild or subclinical drug eruptions may be underrepresented. The heterogeneity of clinical contexts and absence of uniform follow-up also preclude any reliable estimation of incidence, latency, or prognosis. Because none of the available data derive from prospective or comparative designs, causal relationships between drug exposure and cutaneous outcomes remain inferential. These constraints highlight the urgent need for well-designed cohort studies and randomized controlled trials capable of quantifying the true incidence, timing, and risk factors of CADRs in CHD populations. Multicenter registries integrating dermatologic, pharmacologic, and hemodynamic variables could provide the statistical robustness required to validate these associations and guide evidence-based prevention and management strategies.

In summary, the evidence synthesized here demonstrates that cutaneous adverse drug reactions in congenital heart disease represent a clinically diverse yet interrelated spectrum of reactions, suggesting—but not confirming—shared pathophysiologic mechanisms. Prostaglandin-induced eruptions and brown fat necrosis dominate in neonates, whereas immune-mediated hypersensitivity prevails in older or postoperative patients. These findings emphasize the diagnostic and prognostic significance of cutaneous manifestations as reflections of systemic physiologic stress, underscoring the need for heightened pharmacovigilance and targeted research to optimize safety in this uniquely vulnerable population.

Conclusion

Across thirteen reports encompassing twenty-seven individual patients with congenital heart disease, this systematic review highlights that CADR in this population form a distinct clinical and mechanistic spectrum shaped by underlying cardiovascular physiology. In neonates, prostaglandin-associated reactions—ranging from transient erythema [18–21] to brown fat necrosis [26–29]—reflect vascular and metabolic stress within hypoxic tissue environments. In contrast, older pediatric and adult patients predominantly develop immune-mediated syndromes such as Stevens–Johnson syndrome, DRESS, or morbilliform eruptions following exposure to antiepileptics, antibiotics, or endothelially active drugs [17, 22, 24, 25]. These reactions suggest that congenital and postoperative circulatory abnormalities may modulate drug metabolism and immune responsiveness. Clinically, dermatologic manifestations serve not only as adverse events but also as visible indicators of systemic instability. The current evidence, although limited to descriptive case data, underscores the necessity for prospective, multicenter studies and pharmacovigilance registries to quantify the incidence, pathogenesis, and long-term outcomes of CADRs in CHD patients. Early recognition and multidisciplinary management remain essential to balance therapeutic efficacy with safety in this high-risk population.
