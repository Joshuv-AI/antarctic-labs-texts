The outcomes between cystic decompression and marsupialisation methods in odontogenic cysts and cyst-like tumours: A retrospective comparative study
Authors: Kannika Rungsaeng, Pisha Pittayapat, Sappasith Panya, Paksinee Kamolratanakul
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Context:Marsupialization and decompression are treatments designed to reduce hydrostatic pressure which results in reducing the cystic size and increasing the bone density of the lesion.Aims:To compare the percentage of cystic size reduction and the percentage of change in radiographic density from marsupialisation and two types of decompression: tube drain and stent.Settings and Design:Retrospective analysis of panoramic films was conducted to evaluate cystic size and intralesional density.Materials and Methods:Data were analysed from 40 cases, including 31 cases of odontogenic cyst and 9 cases of cyst-like tumour, treated at Chulalongkorn University’s Faculty of Dentistry from 2009 to 2019. The percentage of cystic size reduction and percentage of change in radiographic density were analysed before the procedure and three months after treatment. The density and size of the odontogenic cysts were determined using Image J software version 1.43.Statistical Analysis:Data were analysed using Kruskal–Wallis test.Results:The percentage of cystic size reductions when using marsupialisation, decompression with a tube drain and decompression with a stent were 31.49 ± 7.43, 35.42 ± 10.58 and 30.99 ± 7.16, respectively (P = 0.366). The percentage of change in radiographic density was 25.21 ± 7.04, 27.09 ± 7.96 and 24.94 ± 5.35, respectively (P = 0.661). There was no statistically significant difference in outcomes between the groups.Conclusions:Decompression with a tube drain yielded both the highest percentage of size reduction and the highest percentage of change in radiographic density in the cyst, although this better performance was not statistically significant. We recommend decompression with a tube drain, as it effectively maintains an open drainage pathway and prevents collapse of the cyst opening. This continuous release of hydrostatic pressure is beneficial in promoting gradual bone healing and regeneration.

INTRODUCTION

Intraosseous odontogenic cysts are defined as cysts that arise from the odontogenic epithelium and occur in the tooth-bearing region of the jaw.[1] Intracellular products make the lumen hypertonic, which transudates fluid into the lumen. This in turn creates hydrostatic pressure, producing bone resorption, clinical expansion and sometimes mild paraesthesia.[2] Treatment options for intraosseous odontogenic cyst may be performed with various modalities, depending on multifactorial factors including cystic size, location and recurrent rate of the lesion. For aggressive odontogenic cysts, definitive management, including total enucleation or resection, with or without the use of adjunctive therapeutic modalities, is recommended. However, total enucleation and resection may cause large defect and deformity especially in large lesion or any lesions that are associated with vital structure. Conservative treatment plays a role in order to reduce cystic pressure, reduce cystic size resulting in preservation of vital structure. To reduce cystic pressure, marsupialisation and decompression can be applied in order to decrease intracystic pressure and promote shrinkage of the cyst as well as new bone formation.[3] In both marsupialisation and decompression, a surgical window is created in the wall of the cyst, evacuating the contents of the cyst and maintaining continuity between the cyst and the oral cavity.[3] Marsupialisation surgery usually involves making a bone window or stoma (opening) in the wall of the cyst, partial debridement with excision of the top portion of the cyst and suturing the edges of the remaining cyst with the surrounding soft tissue.[4] A small opening is surgically created in the cyst, and its patency is maintained using an appropriate drainage device to facilitate continuous decompression.[5] Decompression is typically achieved using a drainage device, which is usually a tube or a stent.

A thorough search of existing literature reveals no studies comparing outcomes after treatment with marsupialisation or decompression using different devices. Thus, the purpose of this study is to compare treatment outcomes after odontogenic cysts and cyst-like tumours are treated with either marsupialisation or decompression using a tube drain or a stent.

Statistical analysis

The sample size calculation was performed using the N4 studies version 1.4.1 for estimating a finite population. According to a previous study by Park (2014), a sample size (N) of 32 was calculated with a one-tailed alpha level (α) of 0.05, a standard deviation (SD, σ) of 2.90, and a margin of error (d) of 0.5.[9] [Figure 3]. Based on the software’s calculations, the minimum required sample size was 26.[9]

To ensure accuracy and reliability, two examiners, including an experienced radiologist, calculated the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) and calibrated bone density and cystic size.

All analyses were performed using SPSS software version 20 (IBM, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The distribution of data was verified by Shapiro–Wilk test (P < 0.05). Since the data did not follow a normal distribution, Kruskal–Wallis test was used to analyse the difference between groups. Probability values lower than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Forty cases were included in this study. ICC values for intra-observer agreement in this study were 0.944, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.902–0.970. For inter-observer agreement in this study, the coefficient was 0.996, with a 95% CI ranging from 0.98 to 0.999.

DISCUSSION

Intracystic lesions include intracellular products, which cause the lumen to become hypertonic, mark higher osmotic pressure than the surrounding environment. This results in the inflow of tissue fluids into the cystic cavity, leading to an increase in the size of the cyst and the displacement or destruction of the surrounding structure.[101112] Therefore, the method for the treatment such as marsupialisation or decompression in combination with or without enucleation has been used frequently for large odontogenic cysts, cysts that are located proximal to the inferior alveolar nerve, maxillary sinus or inferior border of the mandible in order to preserve those structure and to perform conservative treatment in the young or elderly.[4] The objective of the conservative treatment for cystic lesions of the jaw is to decrease the pressure within the intraluminal space, resulting in developing healing and reducing the volume of the lesion.[13]

In a previous study, panoramic views were used to measure the cysts before and after decompression with a tube drain. The relative velocities of shrinkage were calculated by measuring the change of size before, during and after decompression. That study showed only the result of decompression with a tube comparing three different kinds of cystic lesions. The relative velocities of shrinkage were found to be 6.1 ± 2.9%/month in dentigerous cysts, 4.7 ± 2.9%/month in odontogenic keratocysts and 4.6 ± 5.6%/month in unicystic ameloblastomas. No statistically significant difference in the relative velocity of shrinkage was found between the three different cystic lesions.[9] In our study, different methods – including marsupialisation and decompression using either a tube or a stent – demonstrated varying degrees of effectiveness in reducing cystic size. Although the differences between these methods were not statistically significant, the cystic size reduction using decompression with tube drain was 35.42% within three months post-treatment. This rate of reduction suggests a trend that may favour gradual new bone formation.

Zhao et al.[7] demonstrated that marsupialisation in odontogenic keratocysts promoted bone formation, as evidenced by a 46.07% increase in bone density three months after treatment. A different study by Ling Gao et al.[14] investigated varies types of odontogenic cystic lesions of the jaw that underwent decompression with a customised thermoplastic resin stent, and the results showed that the increase in bone density was significantly higher in radicular cysts compared to odontogenic keratocysts or ameloblastomas. Our study compared cysts treated with either marsupialisation or decompression using a tube drain or a stent and found no significant differences in the percentage of change in radiographic density between the use of marsupialisation and the two decompression methods. However, bone density in our study indicated at a rate of 24.94%–27.09% in the three months after treatment which suggested that significant bone regeneration had occurred in the lesion.

Decompression and marsupialisation are both conservative treatment modalities that involve creating an opening drainage in order to reduce intracystic pressure and induce bone formation. All these procedures provide surgical patency drainage to lesions with low incidences of post-operative morbidity and perioperative complications, maintain pulp vitality of intracystic teeth, shrink the lesion volume and reduce the rate of recurrence.[15]

The major disadvantage of marsupialisation is that pathological tissue is left in situ, so there is a possibility of a more aggressive lesion developing in the residual tissue and a risk of lesion recurrence.[16] Hence, marsupialisation may require further closed follow up and pathological tissue excision.[317]

Decompression requires a smaller window by creating an opening into the cystic cavity and then suturing a device (plastic tube or stent) at the periphery of the cyst.[15] Plastic tube decompression maintains the opening drainage and prevents collapse of the opening. The drainage tube can also be adaptable for patient comfort. Moreover, insertion and removal are easy.[15] However, plastic tube may potentially dislodge. Obstruction of the tube entrance may result in difficult irrigation leading to infection.[1]

Customised thermoplastic resin stent maintains the balance between intraluminal and interluminal pressures and prevents food debris from being trapped inside the surgical opening.[14] The stent can also be carved, trimmed and smoothed to adapt to the surgical opening of the cyst cavity. Retention is achieved by the clasp which engages the undercuts within the cyst cavity to prevent the risk of aspiration.[18]

Schlieve and colleagues investigated the potential histological changes following decompression of odontogenic cysts and cyst-like lesions. Their findings indicated that decompression does not alter post-decompression histological diagnosis across all cystic and cyst-like lesions when compared to initial histological characteristics. In conjunction with our results, this suggests that decompression may not impact the histological features, thereby allowing for appropriate definitive treatment following decompression.[19]

The difference in window size between decompression (a smaller window) and marsupialisation (a larger window) has no effect on the reduction of the cystic cavity.[20] Regardless of the size of the surgical opening, the positive pressure gradient disappears during marsupialisation, and the maintenance of a similar pressure inside the intraosseous cyst and outside in the oral cavity.[13]

The results of this study suggest that all patients should have a follow-up appointment three months after decompression or marsupialisation. The results of this study indicate that three months will provide enough time to show a decrease in the size of the cyst and an increase in the bone density. This is consistent with a previous study that suggested that the increase in bone density was more significant at three months after treatment compared to six months after treatment.[7] An ideal period of decompression is around 3–14 months, resulting in a size reduction of up to 50%–60%.[21] In this study, the patients diagnosed with odontogenic cysts or cyst-like tumours underwent decompression followed by definitive surgical intervention. After three months of marsupialisation or decompression, all cases were performed enucleation for odontogenic cysts and resection for cyst-like tumours.

The results obtained from the computer-assisted method used for the evaluation of the healing processes on panoramic radiographs were almost the same as those from CT scans.[7] Panoramic radiography exposes patients to lower levels of radiation and results in lower treatment costs compared to computed tomography (CT) scans.[21] When using a panoramic view instead of CT, this study represented the area of the cystic lesion using the mesiodistal and the vertical dimensions. Although it is true that CT scans, unlike panoramic views, can also provide more information about bucco-lingual dimension, odontogenic cysts rarely expand bucco-lingually in the posterior mandible.[22] So when a lesion is located in the posterior mandible, the panoramic radiography is effective. One disadvantage of the panoramic view is that it can be more difficult to assess vital structure involvement, which requires an investigation using a CT scan.[721]

There are several factors related to treatment outcomes. The previous study suggested that the size of a cystic lesion before marsupialization may affect the speed of shrinkage in odontogenic keratocysts and radicular cysts. Therefore, we enrolled cases with cystic size ranging from 15 to 50 mm. Our study included patients aged 15 to 60 years. According to a previous study, the reduction in size of radicular cysts, odontogenic keratocysts, and unicystic ameloblastomas was not influenced by age following decompression.[1422] Therefore, decompression is recommended for all patients with large cysts, especially for older patients, to reduce any potential surgical complications.[14] Multiple lesions possibly result from syndromic conditions that may impact cystic healing. Additionally, the presence of multiple lesions can complicate accurate localisation, complicate decompression procedures and precise measurement of lesion size.

CONCLUSION

Decompression and marsupialisation methods were effective in reducing odontogenic cystic lesions of the jaw and increasing bone density. Decompression with a tube drain yielded both the highest percentage of cystic size reduction and the highest percentage of change in radiographic density in the cyst, although statistical significance was not found. Decompression with tube drain may promote better healing in intrabony odontogenic cystic lesion. Moreover, conservative treatments such as decompression may provide better treatment outcomes with or without definitive treatment and result in the preservation of vital structure. Higher percentage of cystic size reduction and improvement of radiographic density in intralesion may preserve upcoming deformity of the large defect. We recommend decompression with a tube drain, as it effectively maintains an open drainage pathway and prevents collapse of the cyst opening. This continuous release of hydrostatic pressure is beneficial in promoting gradual bone healing and regeneration. However, additional factors such as the surgeon’s experience, the cost of the device and patient comfort should be considered when choosing between the two different devices for decompression or marsupialisation.

Limitation

The number of the cases treated with marsupialisation or decompression with tube or with stent was limited. Moreover, this study utilised panoramic radiography to determine the reduction in cystic size and increase in density. Randomised controlled trial study may be suggested. Since measurements were taken using panoramic radiographs, lesions located in the anterior region of the mandible may be superimposed with surrounding structures, potentially impacting the clarity of the assessment which becomes our limitation.
