Raman of 2D‐MoS2: Disentangling the Metallic Phase Conundrum
Authors: Alaeddin Maddouri, José Ramón Durán‐Retamal, Sergio Humberto Domingues, David Llorens Rauret, Alba Garzón‐Manjón, Jordi Arbiol, Ouassim Ghodbane, Fathi Touati, Rosa M González‐Gil, Leandro N Bengoa, Salah Kouass, Pedro Gómez‐Romero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract2D Molybdenum disulfide (2D‐MoS2) is thermodynamically stable and hence easily synthesized in its semiconducting 2H phase. In contrast, the metallic‐phase 1T‐MoS2 is a highly sensitive, metastable, and complex phase that is not naturally occurring. This heightened sensitivity and instability have resulted in a widespread misrepresentation in academic literature. Based on the current understanding, the majority of Raman investigations incorrectly identify the spectrum of MoO3 as 1T‐MoS2 due to the samples oxidation when exposed to intense laser irradiation in the presence of air, as commonly performed. Therefore, this work focuses on conducting a comprehensive Raman analysis of 1T‐MoS2 by investigating the impact of oxidizing atmosphere, laser power, irradiation time, and temperature changes on the sample. Controlling the Raman conditions has allowed to introduce a novel spectrum that signifies a new phase triggered by laser heating. This has led to further confusion in the identification of the metallic MoS2 polymorphs. This study seeks to expose the current challenges in characterizing the metallic phase of MoS2, especially when using Raman spectroscopy, which, till now, is considered the most reliable technique for this purpose. These results provide guidance to the next experimental Raman studies and possible practical applications for materials engineering.Keywords: 2D‐MoS2 , metallic MoS2 , MoO3 , phase transitions, phases instability, raman spectroscopy, semiconductor 2H‐MoS2

Introduction

Molybdenum disulfide has been one of the strongest newcomers in the era of post‐graphene 2D materials. Commonly, MoS2 can exist in three distinct phases: 2H and 3R, which are semiconducting; and the metallic phase, which has various polymorphs 1T,[ 1 ]1T’,[ 2 ] 1T″, and 1T′″.[ 3 , 4 ] This metallic phase is perhaps the most fascinating form of MoS2 due to its properties, performance, and wide range of applications, including energy storage,[ 5 ] sensors,[ 6 ] catalytic,[ 7 ] and, in particular, as a catalyst for hydrogen evolution reaction, with the potential to replace noble metals.[ 8 , 9 ] Moreover, the local phase transformation of monolayer 2H‐semiconducting into 1T‐metallic has enabled a new paradigm to reduce the contact resistance of MoS2 transistors.[ 10 ]

On the other hand, Raman spectroscopy is widely recognized as one of the key tools to properly characterize this growing category of 2D structures. Extensive research undertaken by Alexandar D. Marinov et al. utilizing the Web of Science Expanded API method demonstrates that Raman spectroscopy is the most used technique for analyzing MoS2.[ 11 ] The study encompasses a broad spectrum of works published from 2011 to 2022, showcasing more than 4000 hits for this technique across a span of 10 years. However, when it comes to making sense of the whole body of literature dealing with Raman spectra of the metallic phase of MoS2, the different pieces do not fit together.

The metallic phase of MoS2 has been proven to be thermodynamically unstable under ambient conditions, as it tends to quickly transform into 2H‐MoS2.[ 12 ] Thus, previous studies have proposed various methods to stabilize the 1T phase. These include thermal treatments,[ 13 ] high magnetic fields,[ 14 ] water‐coupled methods,[ 15 , 16 ] defects engineering,[ 17 ] the use of supports,[ 18 ] and alkali metal intercalation.[ 19 ]

However, few researchers have considered the durability of this sensitive phase during the characterization process. In particular, Raman spectroscopy needs to focus a laser beam on the sample for a certain time with a given power to get the required spectra. And yet, it has been shown that phase transitions in MoS2 can be easily induced by laser irradiation.[ 20 , 21 , 22 ] Susmitha B. et al. are among those who have critically reported Raman‐induced changes in MoS2.[ 23 ] Thus, they documented the transformation of the thermodynamically stable phase 2H‐MoS2 to MoO3 by using a 532 nm laser with a power of 5 mW for their Raman experiments. Therefore, it may be expected that the metastable 1T phase could undergo phase transitions even more easily and faster, requiring less power precisely due to its instability.

The transformation of MoS2 into MoO3 under a Raman laser can be the source of serious misinterpretations. Indeed, in their excellent critical review on hydrothermal synthesis of 1T‐MoS2, Strachan et al. estimated that “90% of the current reports in the literature present, in place of 1T‐[MoS2], the spectrum of MoO3”.[ 24 ]

We believe this is in part due to the trend in recent abundant literature to limit the analysis of Raman spectra to the range 100–500 cm−1 when studying the metallic phase of MoS2. A broader range of wavenumbers up to 1000 cm−1 would show peaks (at 663, 820, and 995 cm−1) which could be unambiguously assigned to MoO3.[ 25 , 26 ]

The confusing wealth of recent literature concerning 2D‐MoS2 is probably due to a combination of factors, including fast and limited data analysis, but also a lack of proper standard conditions for trustworthy measurements, leading to correct interpretations. Thus, apart from limiting the scale and chopping it off at 500 cm−1, recent Raman tests on MoS2 have been missing important data, like the laser power, the test duration, and the local temperature reached. Recently, Bhatnagar et al. showed that the temperature of the test is a critical factor.[ 27 ] In addition, previous studies have measured the local temperature of MoS2 samples when using laser irradiation from a Raman spectrometer, showing that a temperature of ≈600 K can be easily reached even at a laser power of 5 µW, which is sufficient to enhance the risks of phase transitions and material oxidation.[ 28 , 29 ]

But the oxidation of MoS2 to MoO3 is but one of the many transformations taking place when 2D‐MoS2 is activated by a Raman laser. We have systematically studied and reported here a whole series of processes taking place under different conditions that include: i) 1T to 1T’ polymorph stretch, ii) 1T’ to 2H phase transition, iii) oxidation of 2H‐MoS2 to MoO3, and iv) oxidation of 1Tn‐MoS2 to MoO3.

Based on this line of reasoning, using Raman spectroscopy in air to characterize a metastable sulfide phase such as 1T‐MoS2 could likely result in misleading information regarding the desired phase, polymorph, and material. Therefore, we are positive that the definitive validation of the true Raman spectra of the metallic phase of MoS2 has not yet been well established due to the sensitivity of the phase and the constraints imposed by the instruments used. That is why ensuring a clear and controlled verification of information is crucial for the metallic MoS2 and its polymorphs.

In this paper, we analyze and disentangle the ambiguity between the many similar Raman spectra of different material phases of MoS2 (2H‐MoS2, 1T’‐MoS2, 1T‐MoS2, and 1T″′‐MoS2) and α‐MoO3. To accomplish that, we have carried out systematic measurements under different atmospheres, controlling different key parameters such as nominal power, absorbed power, and exposure time. Our results make evident the need for well‐controlled experimental conditions during Raman measurements and show, for the first time, the path toward reliable and reproducible data measurement and analyses for these materials.

Thus, we propose an improved approach to perform more reliable Raman analyses, including a broader frequency range, together with the control of the atmosphere, the duration, and the power for every single spectrum to contribute to the unification of the literature.

Finally, the elimination of oxygen, which is key to avoiding the oxidation of sulfides, has allowed us, for the first time, to detect a new Raman pattern induced by laser treatment under specific power and time conditions, adding even more variety to this conundrum.

Results

Preliminary characterization of the synthesized materials was carried out before using Raman spectroscopy, including XRD, STEM, and XPS. We decided to use these techniques to provide information on the actual materials phase and structure before any Raman laser‐induced change.

Figure 1a shows the X‐ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the as‐prepared 1T@2H‐MoS2 powder. The peaks were indexed according to the literature,[ 30 , 31 ] with the crystal structure of the sample matching the PDF (JCPDS Card No. 73 1508) prepared by annealing the 1T@2H‐MoS2. Based on the main diffraction peak at 9.2°, the interlayer distance is 0.94 nm, which is larger than the 2H‐MoS2 interlayer (≈0.64 nm). However, for this kind of amorphous sample produced via hydrothermal synthesis, XRD analysis cannot reliably distinguish between individual phase‐specific peaks due to the peak broadening caused by small crystallite sizes and structural defects. Additionally, the characteristic peaks of 1T‐MoS2 largely overlap with those of 2H‐MoS2, further complicating their differentiation.[ 32 ] This makes it difficult to conclusively assert the production of a pure 1T phase.

Characterizations of the 1T@2H‐MoS2. a) XRD pattern. b) The intensity profile taken along the directions represented by the red arrow in the corresponding direction from the HAADF‐STEM interlayer spacing. c) TG‐DSC curves. d) XPS signals of Mo 3d.

To further examine the 1T@2H‐MoS2 phase of the prepared powder, we investigated the lattice structure by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). The HAADF‐STEM image (Figure 1b) clearly shows the coexistence of the 1T and the 2H phase. By looking at the interlayer distance, we identified a distance ≈9 Å for the 1T‐MoS2 and 6.4 Å for the 2H‐MoS2 phase. The coexistence of the octahedral 1T phase and the trigonal prismatic 2H phase was further verified by the iDPC‐STEM image (Figure S2a, Supporting Information). The precise nanostructures of the metallic 1T phase and semiconducting 2H phase were easily visible from the zoomed‐in images (Figure S2b for the 2H and Figure S2d for the 1T, Supporting Information). The 2D atomic structure of both phases was pinpointed in the geometric structure viewed from top (Figure S2c for the 2H and Figure S2e for 1T‐MoS2, Supporting Information). We have also performed an elemental quantitative analysis of 1T@2H‐MoS2 using EDS mapping (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The concentrations of Molybdenum and Sulfur are 35% and 65%, respectively, confirming the material stoichiometry and purity.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) were employed to monitor the transformation of MoS2 precursors during the annealing process. The samples were heated in air from 25 to 900 °C at a controlled rate of 10 °C min−1 (Figure 1c). The TGA curve reveals a continuous weight loss beginning ≈100 °C and stabilizing near 400 °C, indicating progressive oxidation of MoS2. This process is further supported by a net mass reduction of ≈10%,[ 33 ] which is identical to the expected value for the substitution of S by O atoms from MoS2 to MoO3. Resulting in the reaction:

At high temperatures, the sample shows the same thermal profile as that of MoO3, and the mass loss grows due to the sublimation of the MoO3.[ 34 , 35 ]

The DSC shows a distinct exothermic peak with a maximum at 232°C (505 K) corresponding to the metallic to semiconducting phase transition (1T‐MoS2 to 2H‐MoS2). This does not correspond to any weight loss since the chemical formula remains unchanged.[ 36 ] It should also be noted that this temperature is for a bulk powder sample, but it might differ for thinner 2D layers. All the results and conclusions from the TGA‐DSC are later confirmed with the in situ XRD study described below in the results.

X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) tests were conducted to verify the phase conversion in this deliberate procedure and measure the amount of 1T phase that was transformed. As depicted in Figure 1d, the Mo 3d3/2 and Mo 3d5/2 peaks were observed at binding energy levels of ≈231.4 and 228.2 eV, respectively. These values are ≈0.9 eV lower than the equivalent peaks of the 2H phase MoS2.[ 37 , 38 ] This indicates that the solvothermal‐treated MoS2 consists of a mixture of the 1T and 2H phases. Furthermore, the 1T phase exhibits a larger peak area compared to the 2H phase, suggesting that a significant portion of the 2H phase was converted into the 1T phase. The phase percentages were quantified by peak area fitting using CasaXPS. In addition, the phase conversion to 1T reaches over 60%. The fitting also shows a pair of additional peaks that are associated with Mo6+ species, which could be the result of some superficial oxidation of the material during handling.[ 39 ]

To start with the Raman characterization, we delved deep into the literature to discuss this contentious misrepresentation. The Raman characterization of the metallic MoS2 phase depends on the presence of three main characteristic peaks labeled J1, J2, and J3, which were first coined by Jimenez et al. in 1991.[ 40 ] These features were attributed to a superlattice in the basal planes (2a0 x a0), which corresponds to the 1T' polymorph in the present literature. Nowadays, those three peaks are systematically claimed as the main evidence for the presence of another polymorph, which is the 1T‐MoS2 with a lattice of a0 x a0.[ 41 , 42 ]

Next, we characterized the vibrational modes of the as‐synthesized solvothermal 1T@2H‐MoS2 powder by Raman spectroscopy. We used a 532 nm laser and carried out a series of measurements to determine the effect of the laser powers (Figure 2a). Initially, these measurements were performed in ambient air. At nominal laser powers below 335 µW, the fundamental vibrational Raman modes of 2H‐MoS2 are observed, namely the in‐plane E1 2g mode at 374 cm−1 and the out‐of‐plane A1g mode at 404 cm−1. In addition, a weak, asymmetric broad peak appearing between 110 and 220 cm−1 reveals the characteristic Raman signature of the metallic phase of MoS2. Detection of this broad feature is only possible when the laser power is kept sufficiently low (below 335 µW), as shown in Figure 2b. The most intense peak from the 1T′‐MoS2 phase (labeled J2), which was identified in previous simulations,[ 43 ] is integrated within this broad feature and has also been reported in previous studies that performed the Raman test using minimal laser power.[ 44 , 45 ] Nevertheless, as the laser power increases, the intensity ratio of the J2 peak to the A1g peak decreases noticeably (Figure 2b), suggesting that the 2H phase becomes dominant in the spectra. Accordingly, we speculate that the metastable 1T phase detected in the XRD, TEM, and XPS measurements rapidly transforms first into the 1T′ phase and then into the semiconducting 2H phase due to local laser‐induced heating, even at low power. This is reasonable since the 1T′ phase is energetically more stable than the 1T phase but less stable than the 2H phase.[ 43 , 46 ] Therefore, it is crucial to maintain the laser power as low as possible to detect the 1T′ phase before it is overtaken by the more stable 2H phase during Raman measurements, as has also been observed in other studies.[ 47 , 48 ]

As the nominal laser power increases beyond 335 µW, new peaks begin to emerge across the entire Raman spectrum and continue to intensify up to 932 µW (Figure 2a). Simultaneously, the characteristic peaks of the 2H phase gradually diminish until they completely disappear, indicating a full phase transformation. Too frequently, this evolving Raman pattern has often been misattributed to the metallic phase of MoS2, as summarized in Table S1 (Supporting Information), which reviews recent 2024 literature on the metallic phases of MoS2.

It can be demonstrated that this Raman spectrum corresponds to the characteristic modes of the orthorhombic Molybdenum trioxide (α‐MoO3) phase.[ 25 , 26 ] We further confirm this correspondence by performing a series of power‐dependent Raman measurements of commercial MoO3 powder in air (Figure S4, Supporting Information) and comparing it with the emerging MoS2 pattern in Figure 2a. Up to now, this emerging Raman pattern has been misassigned to the metallic phase of MoS2.

To get further insight into the time evolution of laser‐induced heating, we performed a time‐dependent Raman analysis under Air (See Video S01, Supporting Information‐the time‐dependent Raman analysis of MoS2 under Air). Figure 2c presents the heatmap plot of the irradiated sample with a fixed laser power of 380 µW in air for 3600s. Figure 2d shows the progress of the Raman spectrum at different instants extracted from the heatmap. The Raman spectrum at 0 s resembles the spectrum recorded at low power shown in Figure 2b, displaying the typical E1 2g and the A1g peaks at 374 and 404 cm−1 and the broad peak at 110–220 cm−1 (the pattern of the metallic phase). As the time increases, we observe three new features. First, the weak and broad peaks start to disappear. Second, the intensity of the E1 2g and A1g decreases over time. Third, the new set of MoO3 peaks emerges after just 60 s, reaching a maximum after 1200 s, and then starts to decrease. This confirms the oxidation of MoS2, with weak Mo─S bonds[ 49 ] being replaced by Mo─O bonds due to the higher electronegativity of oxygen.[ 50 ]

When studying the metallic MoS2 Raman spectra, it is customary to limit the scale to 500 cm−1. Thus, we strive to demonstrate the differences within this limited frequency range. Figure S5 (Supporting Information) shows both the spectra of the oxidized MoS2 and commercial MoO3. All the peaks match fairly well, except for the presence of the 2H characteristic peak (A1g) at 402 cm−1, which will eventually disappear if the laser power is high enough, as we presented in Figure 2a, where the total oxidation of the material is shown.

The other difference is the shifting of the so‐called J1, which in our spectrum appears at 147 cm−1 for the oxidized MoS2 and at 157 cm−1 for commercial MoO3. The time evolution of the oxidized MoS2 peak at (147 cm−1) supposedly identified as J1 is displayed in (Figure S6, Supporting Information: blue line). It is important to note that the intensity of this mode changes in an unexpected way.

In our experiments, the intensity of this misassigned mode increases with higher laser power (Figure 2a) and with longer irradiation times (Figure 2d), both of which are associated with a rise in temperature. This behavior contrasts sharply with that of the true J₁ mode of the 1T phase, whose intensity is known to decrease as temperature increases.[ 51 ] This is further evidence that the emerging pattern in Figure 2a does not belong to the metallic MoS2.

We have also found another revealing difference in this misassigned spectrum (even if a limited range of frequencies was reported (only 140–500 cm−1)). It concerns the intensity of the J2 peak (at 220 cm−1). This peak has been proven in studies of the MoS2 monolayer,[ 52 , 53 ] the intensity calculation,[ 43 ] controlled low laser power,[ 44 , 45 , 54 , 55 ] and this work, as represented in Figure 2b, to be the most intense peak among those of the 1T’ phase. However, all the references reported in Table S1 (Supporting Information) presented the J2 peak as the least intense of the J series (notably, MoO3 presents three weak peaks in this frequency area, all of which could be (and have been) mistaken for J2). Accordingly, it is noteworthy that the low‐frequency peaks have frequently been wrongly assigned to the metallic phase of MoS2 (Table S1, Supporting Information: the recent literature on the metallic phase of MoS2).

In cases where mixtures of any of the MoS2 phases and MoO3 are reported, such as MoO3/2H‐MoS2,[ 56 ] MoO3/1T‐MoS2,[ 57 ] and MoO3/1T’‐MoS2.[ 58 ] The confusion of assigning Raman peaks still involves the misrepresentation of MoO3 peaks as belonging to some metallic MoS2 phase. Thus, all three combinations referenced above present essentially the same Raman pattern. Yet, they differ in some peak assignments, which are claimed to be J1, J2, and J3 for MoO3/metallic MoS2 in one paper but are assigned as belonging to MoO3 in others (MoO3/2H MoS2). This underscores the serious uncertainty in making the right assignments in order to understand true phase relationships in this system.

All the Raman results are supported by in situ XRD experiments on the synthesized powder before and after different treatments. The powder was kept at 300 °C under an air atmosphere, and XRD patterns were recorded every hour (Figure S7, Supporting Information). The first change was a phase transition from the metallic to the semiconductor 2H (Figure S7a, Supporting Information) with the (002) peak shifting from 9.2° to 14.4°, suggesting the change of the interlayer distance to 0.64 nm (2H). After 2 h, new peaks, which were assigned to MoO3, began to develop (Figure S7b, Supporting Information). We ended up with a total material change to MoO3, ascertained by comparison with the XRD of commercial MoO3 (Figure S7c, Supporting Information). The XRD results are also consistent with the TGA‐DSC conclusions, which indicate that the first weight loss of 14% is related to the substitution of sulfur for oxygen atoms, since we steer clear of the second weight loss temperature. Concerning the MoS2 phase transition (1T to 2H), this will not be detectable in the TGA since the chemical formula remains unchanged. Instead, we detected it in the DSC with an exothermic peak (Figure 2c) at 232 °C.

To prevent MoS2 from oxidation, we carried out a new set of power‐dependent and time‐dependent Raman measurements under a nitrogen (N2) flow environment within a compact enclosure that eliminates any potential reaction caused by oxygen (Figure S1, Supporting Information). Figure 3a shows the power dependence ranging from 140 to 3500 µW in N2, revealing the absence of MoO3 peaks (i.e., 663, 820, and 995 cm−1). Therefore, we confirm that the N2 environment is effective in preventing the MoS2 from oxidation into α‐MoO3.

On the other hand, the evolution of 2D‐MoS2 under Raman laser irradiation is still very complex and interesting even in the absence of oxygen. Thus, at low power (141–240 µW), peaks indicating a mixture of 2H and 1T phases (with the former predominating) are detected. This is not different from the starting point of the series of experiments under air. However, at powers between 335 and 380 µW, a hitherto unreported new phase transition occurs above as seen clearly in Figure 3a.

The peaks at each power are highlighted by different colors (black, red, green, blue, cyan, and purple), and the dashed lines indicate the minimum and maximum Raman shift reached by each peak as the power is increased. The peaks highlighted in black, red, green, and blue, located ≈at 125, 190, 215, and 340 cm−1, respectively, are attributed to a newly formed Mo–S phase that emerges at laser powers above 380 µW. This new Mo–S phase is currently under further investigation.

On the other hand, the peaks highlighted in cyan and purple correspond to the E1 2g and A1g characteristic peaks of 2H‐MoS2, respectively, which vanish with powers above 1800 µW. Whereas, the cyan and purple dashed lines indicate that the E1 2g and A1g peaks have fluctuated from 396 and 407 cm−1, and 360 and 377 cm−1, respectively, while the power was increased.

It is noteworthy that the peaks shift between the minimum and maximum is not monotonic, due to the competition between both phases at different powers, which coexist in different locations of the sample under the spot of irradiation, due to the powder nature of the sample.

As the power is further increased and the 2H phase finally disappears completely between 1800 and 3500 µW, a photoluminescence (PL) tail can be detected at the high range frequencies, which could be relevant to understanding the structural evolution of the phase(s) (under study).

To verify the stability of the laser‐modified structure, we performed Raman tests before and after the phase change. Figure 3b illustrates the results of these tests conducted with a laser power of 240 µW. The first spectrum indicates the existence of the characteristic peaks of the 2H phase at ≈379 and 401 cm−1. Subsequently, a phase transition was deliberately induced until the distinctive peaks of the 2H phase disappeared entirely by increasing power above 1 mW. Then, we repeated the test using the same laser power of 240 µW before the transition for 30 s. The 2H peaks did not reappear, and the peaks of the new phase were preserved with just a slight shift that can be attributed to the variation in temperature due to laser heating.[ 59 , 60 ]

We also performed a time‐dependent series of measurements in N2 (See Video S02, Supporting Information‐The time‐dependent Raman analysis of MoS2 under Nitrogen), with a fixed laser power of 380 µW, which is just slightly above the power triggering of the phase transition observed in the power dependence. Figure 3c shows the time evolution of the Raman spectra in a heatmap plot. The plot reveals a phase transition after ≈400 s from the start, without any signature of MoS2 oxidation. To capture the evolution of the vibrational modes involved in the transition, Figure 3d shows the Raman spectra at relevant times. First, in the initial state at 0 s, we observe again the characteristic E1 2g and A1g of the 2H at 369 and 398 cm−1, respectively, as well as the broad peak starting from that start ≈220 cm−1, which was previously shown and discussed in Figure 2b. Then, a phase transition starts ≈400 s, which is characterized by the emergence of peaks at 124, 191, 220, and 340, 480 cm−1, as well as by the disappearance of the E1 2g peak and the decrease of the A1g peak. Then, the A1g eventually vanishes after 1000 s, whereas the other peaks are sustained along the time until 3600 s without major changes. Therefore, we conclude that a completely new phase can be obtained in N2 environment under low‐controlled power irradiation (380 µW), which remains stable over time.

Discussion

The most intriguing fact concerning the Raman spectra of 2D‐MoS2 is the absence in all the literature of the 1T‐MoS2 Raman characteristic peaks at 259 and 360 cm−1, which have been proven by simulation.[ 36 , 61 ] This is despite the confirmation of the presence of even high percentages of the 1T metallic phase in a variety of materials, as shown by other techniques (TEM, XRD, and XPS). Instead, using Raman Spectroscopy, all the literature reported the peaks of 1T’ (J1, J2, and J3) as we can see in (Table S3, Supporting Information), which corresponds to the calculation of the Raman phonon mode of the 1T’‐MoS2. This is well explained by M. Calandra when he studied the stability of MoS2 different phases and polymorphs.[ 43 ] He demonstrates that the phonon dispersion of the 1T‐MoS2 phase displays dynamical instability (not stable even in ambient), and it will directly stretch evolving to the structure of 1T’‐MoS2. Another metastable polymorph of the MoS2 metallic phase that can easily transform to 2H with heating and laser irradiation.[ 36 ] This makes things even more complex, considering that the 1T’‐MoS2 exhibits a great deal of similarity with the MoO3 phonon mode, making it difficult to distinguish between the two using low‐frequency Raman (140–500 cm−1), so revealing a larger scale (0–1000 cm−1) is a must.

There is growing evidence that many Raman spectra reported as 1T′‐MoS2 in the literature are actually those of MoO3. Table S1 (Supporting Information) shows just how common this mistake has become. In some cases, this misidentification has been repeated so often that it is now accepted as fact and even cited as confirmation.[ 62 ] We believe this is a serious issue that needs immediate attention to prevent the continued spread of this error in the scientific community.

Motivated by these concerns, our systematic investigation aims to clarify and ultimately correct the widespread mischaracterization of MoS2 phases in Raman studies. Through a comprehensive series of controlled experiments, we demonstrate that the unintended oxidation of MoS2 is not a random occurrence, but rather the result of a combined influence of three key factors: i) the presence of oxygen in the measurement environment, ii) localized heating caused by laser irradiation, and iii) the duration of exposure during spectral acquisition. Our findings clearly indicate that even moderate laser power, when applied over extended periods in an oxygen‐rich atmosphere, can initiate a phase transformation, often leading to the formation of MoO3 mistakenly attributed to the metallic phase in many studies. Therefore, minimizing or eliminating oxygen from the testing environment is essential for preserving the true structural identity of MoS2 during characterization. Additionally, careful optimization of laser power and acquisition time is necessary to avoid thermal damage and ensure accurate, reproducible Raman spectra.

However, recent literature often overlooks these critical factors, as shown in Table S1 (Supporting Information). Even when laser power is reported, the values are frequently too high, given the known metastability of the phase and our current findings. Addressing these overlooked variables is essential for accurate characterization, guiding future studies in the right direction and helping to correct existing misinterpretations in the literature.

Through an extensive review of the literature, we identified significant ambiguity in the reported Raman shifts associated with the metallic phase of MoS2. Figure 4a,b illustrates the wide dispersion of Raman peak positions (J₁, J2, and J3), which vary depending on experimental conditions (Tables S1 and S2, Supporting Information) and theoretical predictions (Table S3, Supporting Information). Specifically, we observed shifts of up to 78 cm−1 for J₁, 51 cm−1 for J2, and 59 cm−1 for J3. Such variability has led to frequent misassignments, especially by researchers who may not be fully aware of the phase complexity and sensitivity of the system. Moreover, a noticeable difference in both peak positions and intensities was observed between studies that tightly controlled laser power and those that did not (Table S2, Supporting Information). Given these findings, we focused particular attention on MoS2 in powder form (Table S1, Supporting Information), which appears to be the most susceptible to this type of mischaracterization.

While all the literature shows the direct stretch between the metallic MoS2 polymorphs 1T to 1T’, we follow up and demonstrate the phase transition from 1T’ to 2H. Then, if oxygen is present, the material changes to MoO3 as this work shows.

Finally, we show a new Raman spectrum under N2 corresponding to a new Mo‐S phase. But deciphering this new phase is a daunting challenge full of uncertainties due to the plethora of phase transitions that take place during Raman measurements of 2D‐MoS2. Indeed, this Raman pattern has been equivocally assigned to different polymorphs of the metallic phase of MoS2, like, for example, 1T‐MoS2 and 1T’‐MoS2.[ 63 , 64 ] Conversely, the new spectrum exhibits a significant resemblance to the phonon modes simulation of 1T′″‐MoS2.[ 65 ] Notably, the spectra show a single peak ≈190 cm−1 and share the same peak shifts and intensity for both our work and the calculation. This raises an obvious question: What is the true nature of the new MoS2 phase giving rise to this Raman spectrum?

Accordingly, this new phase shows some interesting aspects like the induced PL, the Raman peaks shifting, and the lattice strain. Ongoing efforts are being carried out to characterize this new phase and exploit its properties.

Conclusion

We have carried out a systematic study on the Raman spectra of 2D‐MoS2, demonstrating that the presence of oxygen from air leads to the formation of MoO3 due to the high local temperature reached even under low‐power lasers. Examining the various Raman test parameters, we determined the importance of laser power, test duration, local temperature, and controlled atmosphere. Paying attention to these Raman conditions has allowed us to present a novel Raman pattern of a newly laser‐induced Mo‐S phase. These largely overlooked experimental conditions severely affect the results and hence their conclusions. Therefore, establishing the standards for future assertions regarding the Raman fingerprint confirmation of these metastable phases is necessary.

This work has tried to put some order concerning the Raman characterization of the multiple phases and polymorphs of 2D‐MoS2. We have detected a widespread misinterpretation of the Raman spectra of the MoS2 metallic phase, being frequently contaminated by MoO3, resulting from the decomposition of the primary phase itself. With our present work, we would like to raise awareness of both the confusion in the literature and the difficulty in using Raman to characterize this increasingly important material. Since the interest in the metallic phase of MoS2 is most likely to keep growing, it is of the utmost importance to be able to unambiguously identify each of its polymorphs. This will allow proper selection of the right phase for each application and consider possible transformation even during operation.

Going beyond the negative side of mischaracterization, we could consider a positive side of our first results on laser‐induced MoS2 oxidation to be extended to most TMDs (Transition Metal Dichalcogenides) as a simple, cost‐efficient, and fast method for the formation of extended TMO (Transition Metal Oxide)‐TMD heterostructures, which are potentially interesting for different application like p‐type transistors,[ 66 ] gas sensors,[ 67 ] etc.

Experimental Section

Synthesis

N,N‐dimethylformamide (DMF) was reported to be one of the best polar solvents to induce the formation of the metallic 2D‐MoS2.[ 68 ] This phase was prepared by a modified solvothermal method. Thus, 100 mg of 2H‐MoS2 powder was dispersed in 20 mL of DMF and ultrasonicated for 15 min for proper dispersion. This dispersion was then transferred to a 50 mL Teflon‐lined autoclave and kept in an oven at 200°C for 24 h. The sample was collected by centrifugation, washed several times with the same solvent, and dried in a vacuum at 45 °C overnight to get the powder of 1T@2H‐MoS2.

Material Characterization

Powder XRD characterization was performed using the Malvern Panalytical X'pert Pro MPD (Materials Powder Diffractometer). The X‐ray source is a ceramic X‐ray tube with Cu Κα anode (λ = 1.540 Å). The detector used is an X'Celerator which is an ultra‐fast X‐ray detector based on Real Time Multiple Strip (RTMS) technology.[ 46 ] The morphology and size of the nanostructured materials were characterized using high‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF‐STEM) and integrated differential phase contrast (iDPC)‐STEM imaging on a double‐corrected and monochromated ThermoFisher Spectra 300 microscope operated at 300 kV. Energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS)‐STEM elemental mapping and quantification were conducted on the same microscope, equipped with a Super‐X EDS system, using a probe convergence angle of 19.5 mrad. For EDS analysis, the L lines of S and the K lines of Mo were used. TGA analysis was carried out using the NETZSCH‐STA 449 F1 Jupiter thermal analysis system. A heating rate of 10°C min−1 from 25 to 900 °C in air. The X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using a Phoibos 150 analyzer (SPECS GmbH) under ultrahigh vacuum conditions (5 × 10−10 mbar) with a monochromatic aluminum K alpha X‐ray source (1486.74 eV), peaks fitting for high‐resolution spectra was performed using CASA‐XPS.

In Situ XRD

For the in situ XRD, the TTK 600 Low‐Temperature Chamber was used, which is a non‐ambient attachment for powder X‐ray diffraction studies from −190 to 600°C. Different sample holders allow investigating samples in reflection and transmission geometry. Samples can be measured in vacuum, air, or inert gases.

Raman Spectroscopy

For the Raman spectroscopy studies, a Witec alpha300 Raman system was used, equipped with a laser of 532 nm (2.33 eV). For Raman spectra, a 1800 lines mm−1 grating with a spectral resolution of 1 cm−1 was used. The samples in both air and N2 were focused with a x50‐long working distance objective lens of NA of 0.55. The Raman instrument was adopted to illuminate the samples for 30 s. The measurements in N2 were conducted in a homemade chamber designed for flowing N2 and with a 1 mm‐thick optical window. Additionally, the power was measured at the sample level with a photodiode sensor (PM16‐120 from Thorlabs).

Power Density Calculation

A laser with a wavelength of λ = 532 nm focused on the sample through a long‐distance focusing lens Zeiss LD EC Epiplan‐Neofluar Dic with a numerical aperture of NA = 0.55, a focal length of LF = 9 mm, and an objective magnification of M = x50 was used. Therefore, the diameter of the Airy disc (also called spot size) will be D = 1.22*λ/NA = 1.2 µm. Nevertheless, if the full‐width half maximum (FWHM) of the Gaussian beam was considered, where the diffraction pattern intensity falls by half, then the FWHM = 0.51*λ/NA = 0.49 µm, ≈500 nm. On the other hand, the power measured at the sample height was recorded by a photodiode sensor (PM16‐120 from Thorlabs). So the power density was calculated by dividing the power on the sample by the area of the spot size, for which either the beam diameter or the FWHM was considered. Each laser power used in the Raman experiments is listed in Table S4 (Supporting Information), along with the corresponding calculated power densities based on both the beam diameter and the FWHM. In addition, Figure S8 (Supporting Information) illustrates the relationship between the power on the sample and the resulting power density, using either the beam diameter or the FWHM for calculation. For example, a laser power of 1 mW on the sample corresponds to a power density of 0.91 mW µm−2 when calculated using the beam diameter, and 5.23 mW µm−2 when using the FWHM.

Statistical Analysis of Raman Data

The spectra presented correspond to the original data collected. Each curve of the power series depicted in Figures 2a,b, and 3a,b, corresponds to the average of 60 spectra integrated during 0.5 s each spectrum. Similarly, each spectrum of the time series of 3600 spectra depicted in heatmaps (Figures 2c and 3c) corresponds to an integration time of 1 s. However, for a clearer presentation of the data, each spectrum shown in Figures 2d and 3d corresponds to an average of 10 spectra after the specific time shown in each spectrum. Spikes arising from cosmic rays were removed by the native software Project Five of the Witec Raman system. It is noteworthy that the background signal was not removed due to the layer of as‐synthesized MoS2 powder being thick enough to block any contribution of the underlying Silicon substrate. In addition, due to the large differences in intensity as the irradiation power increases, some spectra are magnified by a constant, as indicated by a label over the spectra (i.e., the spectra in Figure 2d at 0 s were multiplied by three fold, x3). Whereas the shift of some spectrum in the cascade plots is manually modified for clearer presentation. Furthermore, the wavenumber (Raman shift) of each peak was extracted either by the function find_peaks of the scipy signal python library or by fitting with a Lorentzian function in the spectral range of interest via the LorentzianModel function of the lmfit.models python library. Finally, no further pre‐processing or statistical analysis was done with the data.
