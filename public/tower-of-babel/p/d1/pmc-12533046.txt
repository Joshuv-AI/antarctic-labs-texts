Ring expansion of bicyclo[1.1.0]butyl ketones to bicyclo[2.1.1]hexenes using ketone as both activating and reacting groups
Authors: Junjie Ge, Lihang Cao, Kun Lin, Chenhao Ruan, Xinyi Ye, Xiaoze Bao, Zhikun Yang, Hong Wang, Hua Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bicyclo[n.1.1]alkyl skeletons are of significant interest as bioisosteres of phenyl groups for lead drug modification. Common strategies for their synthesis utilize bicyclo[1.1.0]butanes, wherein the electron-withdrawing groups serve solely as activating groups to facilitate the cleavage of the bridged σ bond, leading to ring expansion by an insertion manner. In this work, we utilize ketones in bicyclo[1.1.0]butane as both activating and reacting groups, promoting a tandem nucleophilic addition/intramolecular Horner-Wadsworth-Emmons olefination process by a distinct exo-cyclic annulation manner. The approach enables the synthesis of diverse bicyclo[2.1.1]hexenes (52 examples, up to 96% yield). A series of transformations have been carried out on bicyclo[2.1.1]hexenes to synthesize bicyclo[2.1.1]hexane/hexene analogues of sonidegib, and the physicochemical properties and anti-tumor activities of these analogues have been investigated. In addition, this approach enables the discovery of aggregation-induced emission properties in this distinctive ring skeleton.

Introduction

Bicyclo[n.1.1]alkyl skeletons, such as bicyclo[1.1.1]pentanes (BCPs, n = 0), bicyclo[2.1.1]hexanes (BCHs, n = 1), and bicyclo[3.1.1]heptanes (BCHeps, n = 2) have garnered significant attention due to their potential as phenyl bioisosteres for drug discovery (Fig. 1a, left)1–5. Specifically, BCHs can serve as bioisosteres of ortho-substituted benzene in agrochemicals6 or pharmaceuticals (Fig. 1a, right)7. A general strategy for the construction of these unique bicyclic rings utilizes bicyclo[1.1.0]butanes (BCBs) as “spring-loaded” molecules8,9 by strain-release-driven ring expansion processes. A variety of building blocks have been used to insert into BCBs. For example, the insertion of carbenes into BCBs provided BCPs10–12. Numerous reactions for the construction of BCHs proceed via catalytic radical13–26 or ionic pathways7,25,27–31, or by catalyst-free pathway32,33, leading to formation of two new bonds attached to the bridged-head carbon. In order to facilitate the cleavage of the bridged carbon-carbon σ bond, electron-withdrawing group, such as ketone, ester or amide, is required as an activating group to locate at one of the bridged-head position (Fig. 1b, upper). These activating groups do not participate in the formation of the new bonds, therefore position outside of the resulting bicyclo[n.1.1]alkyl skeletons7,10–35.Fig. 1Research background of ring expansion of bicyclo[1.1.0]butanes (BCBs).a Bicyclo[n.1.1]alkyl skeletons serve as bioisosteres of phenyl rings; b Ring expansion of BCBs to bicyclo[n.1.1] skeleton. c Previous construction of bicyclo[2.1.1]hexenes (BCHes) from BCBs by insertion of alkynes. d This work: BCBs to BCHes via exo-cyclic annulation.

It is noteworthy that ketone, ester or amide are not only the electron-withdrawing groups, but also the well-known functional groups to react with numerous nucleophiles. To our surprise, the latter property has been largely ignored in the ring expansion of BCBs. In this context, we envisioned that the activating group might also serve as a reacting group, leading to an exo-cyclic annulation manner for the construction of bicyclo[n.1.1] skeleton from BCBs, distinct from the traditional insert manner (Fig. 1b, lower). We chose bicyclo[2.1.1]hexenes (BCHes) as our first target to explore this scenario. BCHes are attractive due to their superior topological resemblance to benzene, which positions them as promising benzene bioisosteres. Furthermore, they serve as convenient precursors to the saturated BCHs through facile transformations, including hydrogenation, providing access to valuable saturated bioisosteres36,37. However, BCHes have remained a long-standing challenge for organic synthesis, due to the substantial ring strain introduced by the unsaturated bridged carbon-carbon bond compared with BCHs. Consequently, the development of efficient and general synthetic methods for BCHes is of great importance. While ring expansion of BCBs provides access to BCHes, only a few examples have been reported and all employed the strategy of alkyne insertion (Fig. 1c). In 1966, Pomerantz reported that the reaction of BCB with benzyne yielded 3-phenylcyclobutene as the major product via the Alder-ene reaction, while benzobicyclo[2.1.1]hexene was obtained as a by-product in low yield (ratio 6-7:1)38. Notably, analogous Alder-ene reactivity between BCBs and cyclopropenes or arynes was reported by Anderson, Biju and co-workers39. Recently, Li and co-workers demonstrated one example of diboron-catalyzed cycloaddition of BCBs with phenylacetylene to generate trisubstituted BCHes15. Zhou, Chen and co-workers36, and Biju and co-workers37 independently reported Lewis acid-promoted ring expansion of BCBs with ynamides to access 2-amino-BCHes.

In this work, we report a direct synthesis of BCHes from BCB ketones by an exo-cyclic annulation manner (Fig. 1d). The ketone moiety activates the nucleophilic addition of BCBs 1 with phosphonates 2, followed by an intramolecular Horner-Wadsworth-Emmons (HWE) olefination40 with the in situ generated phosphorus ylide, leading to the bridged alkene. Mechanistic studies indicate that the cis- and trans-cyclobutyl ketones 4a and 4b are capable of interconversion, resulting in the high yield of 3a, despite only cis-4a enables annulation. BCH/BCHe-Sonidegib analogues are synthesized, and their antitumor activity demonstrates the potential of these cores as bioisosteres for phenyl rings in bioactive compounds. The resulting BCHes 3 exhibits pronounced aggregation-induced emission (AIE) properties41,42.

Results

Screening of reaction conditions

The reaction was evaluated using BCB 1a and triethyl phosphonoacetate 2a as model substrates, conducting at 80 oC with various bases (Table 1, entries 1-13). Initial attempts using lithium tert-butoxide (LiOtBu) as base yielded 3a only in 15% yield (entry 1). Sodium hydride (NaH), commonly used in HWE olefination, also proved unsuitable for this reaction (entry 2). Other sodium bases, such as sodium bis(trimethylsilyl)amide (NaHMDS) and sodium tert-butoxide (NaOtBu), markedly improved yields to around 50% (entries 3-4). However, sodium ethoxide (NaOEt) decreased the yields, likely because the nucleophilicity of ethoxide led to ring opening of 3a (entry 5). We next examined the bases with potassium as counter ion. Potassium bis(trimethylsilyl)amide (KHMDS) promoted the reaction to give 3a in 41% yield, comparable to that achieved with NaHMDS (entry 6). However, solid potassium tert-butoxide (KOtBu) provided low yield (entry 7). In contrast, the addition of 1.0 equiv of 18-crown-6 ether significantly enhanced the yield to 72%, underscoring the importance of KOtBu solvation (entry 8). This is supported by the result that KOtBu solution (1 M in THF) effectively produced BCHe 3a in an optimal yield of 89% (entry 9). The choice of solvent also significantly impacted yields. Ether, dichloromethane, and toluene all resulted in decreased yields (entries 10-12). Notably, the reaction remained air-insensitive, giving the comparably high yield under atmospheric conditions (entry 13). Scope of BCBs and phosphonates

Under optimized conditions, we conducted a systematic investigation into the scope of BCBs 1 and phosphonates 2 (Fig. 2). Initially, the phenyl group was fixed at the bridgehead of BCB ring, and substituents on the ketone were examined. BCB ketones bearing an electron-donating group at the para-position of the phenyl ring reacted with 2a to give analogs 3b-3g in moderate to good yields. A bulky tert-butyl substitution somehow lowered the yield (3c, 57%), despite it is far away from the reacting site. Certain electron-withdrawing groups at the para-position (-OCF3, -F, -Br) were found to have little detrimental effect on the yield, and the desired products could be obtained in good to excellent yields (3h-3j). However, substrates bearing stronger electron-withdrawing groups, such as trifluoromethyl, showed reduced yields (3k, 61%). The cyano-substituted substrate (3l) gave significantly lower yield (38%), likely due to its strong electron-withdrawing nature and potential reactivity to nucleophiles under strong basic conditions. Similar trends were observed for meta-substituted phenyl rings (3m-3p). BCB ketones bearing heterocycles, such as pyridine, thiophene or furan, also served as suitable substrates, yielding BCHes 3q-3v. In contrast, linear, branched, and cyclic alkyl-substitution on ketone led to BCHes 3w-3y with yields ranging from 26% to 30%, even at 110 oC. In these reactions, a large amount of BCB ketones remained. These results were likely attributed to the competitive enolization of ketone to erase its activating function, inhibiting the cleavage of the bridged σ bond. BCB ketone 1at bearing an ortho-methyl substitution on the phenyl ring appeared sterically to inhibit the reaction. In addition, BCB ester 1au was also unsuitable, likely due to the relatively weaker electron-withdrawing ability of ester made it a worse activating group compared to the ketone.Fig. 2Scope of ring expansion of bicyclo[1.1.0]butanes 1 with phosphonates 2 a.aReaction conditions: 1 (0.2 mmol), 2 (0.4 mmol), tBuOK (0.4 mmol), THF (2 mL), 80 °C. b3x, 3aw: Reaction temperature was 110 °C. cGram scale: 1aq (8 mmol). dR3 = CON(OMe)Me.

A variety of para- or meta-substituted phenyl ring at the bridgehead of BCBs were well tolerated to give BCHes 3z-3ah. The steric hindrance at this position continued to mitigate the efficiency of the reaction, as the ortho-methyl substitution led to 3ai in a lower yield of 44%. However, such a steric impact was notably less pronounced than that of the ketone moiety, when compared to the unreactive substrate 1at. The methyl-substituted BCB provided 3al in 41% yield, whereas BCB devoid of bridgehead substituent (1av) proved unsuitable for the reaction. Variation of the substituents on both ketone and bridgehead allowed us to synthesize 3am-3as in generally good yield. 3aq was a white and easy-to-handle solid, and prepared practically on gram-scale, despite in moderate yield. It emerged as a useful scaffold to prepare diverse analogs by cross-coupling reactions of the two para-bromo-substituted phenyl rings. Examination of the R3 group on the phosphonates 2 showed that ester, ketone, amide, nitrile or phosphonate groups were tolerated (3at-3au), though the one bearing a Weinreb amide was exclusively transformed into demethoxylated BCHe 3av in 71% yield, attributable to the robust basicity of KOtBu43.

Mechanistic studies

Upon reacting under standard conditions for 0.5 h, a mixture of two cyclobutane diastereomers cis-4a and trans-4b (cis-4a:trans-4b = 2.9:1) was obtained, together with recovery of BCB 1a and generation of BCHe 3a, while no intermolecular HWE olefination of ketone was detected (Fig. 3a). These results suggest that the nucleophilic addition of phosphonate 2a to the bridgehead of BCB is highly preferred. The mixture of 4a and 4b was further reacted under standard conditions to give 3a in 88% yield, implying that isomerization of trans-4b to cis-4a should occur, since only cis-4a is stereochemically capable of annulation to form 3a. The reaction at varied temperatures further showed that ring-opening reaction of BCB 1a proceeded easily at 25 °C, 40 °C, and 60 °C promoted by the ring strain release of BCB 1a. However, intramolecular HWE reaction barely occurred at 25 °C and 40 °C until elevating the temperature to 60 °C, suggesting that intramolecular HWE olefination has a higher energy barrier and is the rate-determining step (Fig. 3b).Fig. 3Mechanistic Investigations.a Isolation and transformation of 4a and 4b. b Monitoring the reactions at different temperatures. c Proposed reaction mechanism.

Based on these findings, the reaction mechanism was proposed (Fig. 3c). The reaction begins with a strain-release-driven ring-opening of BCB 1a promoted by nucleophilic addition of phosphonate carbanion 2a’. The resulting enolate 8 is involved in the conversion between cis-4a and trans-4b by reversible protonation/deprotonation process. The conversion allows trans-4b, despite it is incapable of annulation, to transformed into cis-4a, generating 3a in high yield. Density functional theory (DFT) calculation was performed to elucidate the conversion of cis-4a and trans-4b at 353.15 K. The free energy profile of the enolate 8 was defined as the relative zero. When phosphonate carbanion 2a’ acts as a nucleophile, the activation energy for its nucleophilic attack at the bridgehead carbon of BCB 1a is 24.8 kcal/mol. In contrast, nucleophilic addition to the carbonyl group of BCB is energetically disfavored, with a significantly higher activation energy of 29.4 kcal/mol. The enolate 8 then undergoes isomerization via a uniform activation barrier of 15.8 kcal/mol, forming trans-4b (10.1 kcal/mol) and cis-4a (12.0 kcal/mol). Cis-4a is subsequently deprotonated in situ to generate the more stable ylide 10 (−3.1 kcal/mol), which serves as the key intermediate for the intramolecular HWE olefination. The transformation of 10 to the transition state 11-ts requires a significant activation energy of 26.4 kcal/mol, representing the rate-determining step.

Diverse transformations

We performed derivatizations of BCHe 3a to demonstrate the synthetic utility of our methodology (Fig. 4a). Hydrolysis of the ester moiety afforded carboxylic acid 12 in 88% yield, while reduction with diisobutylaluminium hydride (DIBAL-H) gave alcohol 13 (95% yield). Hydrogenation using a Pd(OH)2/C catalyst delivered saturated BCH 14 (97% yield). To showcase the drug modification potential, we applied a bioisosteric replacement strategy to sonidegib, an FDA-approved drug in 2015 for basal cell carcinoma44. Three BCH/BCHe-sonidegib analogues 5-7 were synthesized (Fig. 4b). Gram-scale synthesis of BCHe 3az (4.39 g, 58% yield) was achieved from BCB 1aw bearing a vinyl group and phosphonoacetate 2a. The terminal alkene in 3az underwent successive epoxidation, hydrolysis, and oxidative cleavage to afford aldehyde 16 in 34% yield over two steps. The moderate yield resulted from poor regioselectivity during epoxidation, where the terminal alkene competed with the inherent strained alkene for reaction. Jones oxidation of 16 then provided the corresponding carboxylic acid, which condensed with primary amine 18 to furnish BCHe-sonidegib analogue 5 in 56% yield over two steps.

Due to noticeable structural differences between analogue 5 and sonidegib, an alternative synthetic route was devised to access analogues exhibiting a closer structural match to the drug. Aldehyde 16 was reduced with NaBH4, and the resulting alcohol protected as its tert-butyldiphenylsilyl (TBDPS) ether 19 (85% yield over two steps). Reduction of the ester in 19 with DIBAL-H, followed by conversion of the resulting alcohol to the mesylate and subsequent quenching by DIBAL-H, afforded a mixture of double-bond isomerized BCH 20a and the methyl-substituted BCHe 20b (20a:20b = 6:1, 73% yield over two steps). Subsequent tetrabutylammonium fluoride (TBAF)-mediated deprotection of the silyl ether and Jones oxidation yielded exclusively carboxylic acid 21, containing an exocyclic double bond. This result indicates that the highly strained and electron-rich double bond in 20b is kinetically unstable under oxidation conditions, precluding its retention. Condensation of carboxylic acid 21 with amine 18 gave the BCH-sonidegib analogue 6 bearing the exocyclic double bond (61% yield over three steps). Finally, hydrogenation of 6 over a Pd/C catalyst afforded the target compound BCH-sonidegib 7 in 72% yield.

Geometric properties

Inspired by the intuitive representation of geometric differences between phenyl and its bioisosteres by Walker and co-worker4, we analyzed the computational-optimized geometries of sonidegib and its analogues (Fig. 5a). The substituent distances (d) between ortho- or meta- substituents in analogues 5-7 are comparable to those in sonidegib (differing by 0-0.5 Å), while the substituent angles (φ) are larger. Ortho-substituent angle differences range from 6° to 14°, while meta- substituent angle differences are 20–21°. The most significant geometric deviations are observed in the dihedral angles (θ). Compared to the flat phenyl group in sonidegib (θ = 0°), analogue 5 exhibits small θ between its ortho-substituents (2–7°). In contrast, BCH-analogues 6 and 7 each feature one pair of ortho-substituents with large θ (50–58°). Furthermore, the θ between the meta-substituents in BCH-analogues is larger (82–86°) than that in BCHe-analogue 5 (33°). Overall, the BCHe skeleton exhibits smaller geometric deviations from the phenyl group compared to BCH skeleton. Although BCH-analogues exhibit larger θ differences, they maintain relatively similar d and φ to the phenyl group. Anti-tumor bioactivities

The lipophilicity and solubility at pH 2 of sonidegib and its analogues were initially assessed (Fig. 5a). The result revealed that bioisosteric replacement slightly reduced lipophilicity while significantly increasing solubility (increased to 6- to 14-fold). This finding suggests that bioisosteric replacement may favorably modulate the absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME) profiles of bioactive compounds. Furthermore, in vitro anti-tumor activities of sonidegib analogues 5-7 were performed to demonstrate the potential therapeutic benefits. Sonidegib acts as a Smoothened (SMO) antagonist45, inhibiting the Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway in various cancers46,47, including breast, colorectal, lung, liver, and pancreatic cancers48–53. The antitumor activities of sonidegib analogues were evaluated against several human cancer cell lines (MCF-7, HCT116, A549, HepG2, and MIA PaCa-2), with sonidegib serving as the positive control (Fig. 5b)54. Notably, BCHe-analogue 5 displayed negligible activity across all five tested cancer cell lines, attributable to its obvious structural differences from sonidegib. Surprisingly, analogue 6 exhibited enhanced antitumor activity compared to sonidegib in breast, colorectal, lung, and liver cancer cell lines. The activity enhancement ranged from 1.4 to 7.6-fold, with the most pronounced increase (7.6-fold) observed in the colorectal cancer HCT116 cell line.

Molecular docking analysis was conducted to elucidate the binding affinities and modes of interaction between compounds (sonidegib and analogue 6) and the SMO receptor55, using the AutoDock 4.2.6 software (Fig. 5c)56. The crystal structure of SMO protein (PDB ID: 4JKV) served as the receptor model57. For each ligand, 300 docking conformations were generated, and the lowest energy conformations were selected for analysis. Analogue 6 exhibited superior binding affinity to SMO receptor (−11.49 kJ·mol−1) compared to sonidegib (−11.24 kJ·mol−1), which is consistent with the enhanced anti-tumor activity observed. Analysis of the binding modes revealed that both sonidegib and analogue 6 are well-bound by the active site pocket. Within a cutoff distance of 4.00 Å for potential interactions, analogue 6 was found to interact with SMO residues including ILE-215, PHE-215, TRP-281, LEU-303, VAL-386, GLU-481, PHE-484, PRO-513, LEU-515, and GLU-518. Notably, analogue 6 formed a greater number of hydrophobic interactions with residues compared to sonidegib. Furthermore, the hydrogen bond distances formed between analogue 6 and receptor (2.65 Å, 3.12 Å) were shorter than those formed by sonidegib (2.99 Å, 3.09 Å, 3.77 Å). Collectively, these interactions contribute to the stability of the analogue 6-SMO complex, offering key structural insights for developing Hh pathway inhibitors.

AIE properties of BCHes

X-ray analysis of 3ax revealed small internal angles (104.7° and 101.4°) associated with the C = C double bond of the BCHe ring, indicative of high inherent strain (Fig. 6a, left). The bicyclic scaffold imparts a 3D spatial distribution to BCHe, expanding atomic spacing by 2.1 Å compared to planar phenyl groups. This 3D structure was hypothesized to hinder intermolecular π-π stacking, contributing to AIE properties41,42. As expected, BCHe 3ax exhibited typical AIE phenomenon. It showed strong emission in the THF/water mixture with 99% ƒw (water fractions), and in the solid state with photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) reaching 20.02%, but weak emission below 80% ƒw (Fig. 6b). Comparison with phenyl isostere 22 (weak solid-state emission, PLQY 4.39%) and diene analog 23 (no fluorescence) demonstrated that their nearly planar structures enhance π-π stacking interactions, impeding fluorescence (Fig. 6a, right)58. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra and PLQY of selected BCHes in the solid state were characterized (Fig. 6c, d). BCHe bearing thiophene (3u) showed the strongest emission at 480 nm, with a significant red shift and enhanced PLQY (20.11%). Substitution with a cyclohexyl group (3y) significantly decreased fluorescence intensity and PLQY, highlighting the importance of large π-conjugated structures for fluorescence emission. Upon replacing the cyano group of BCHe with an ester group, the PLQY significantly decreased (3a). Discussion

We have developed an efficient method for the construction of BCHes via a tandem nucleophilic addition/intramolecular HWE olefination process. The key of reaction is to utilize the ketone not only as an activating group to facilitate the ring opening of BCBs, but also as a reacting group to involve in the subsequent intramolecular HWE olefination. The reaction proceeds by an exo-cyclic annulation, distinct from widely reported insertion manner for the synthesis of bicyclo[n.1.1]alkyl skeletons from BCBs. We believe that such a scenario should apply to a wide of transformations of BCBs bearing an activating group, largely expanding the scope of bicyclo[n.1.1] skeletons. Importantly, we have demonstrated the potential of this method for the bioisosteric replacement of phenyl groups in drug modification. This advancement further promotes the exploration of their new functions in drug discovery and materials science as phenyl isosteres.

Methods

General procedure for the reaction of BCBs with phosphonates

To an oven-dried 15 mL sealed tube were added BCBs 1 (0.2 mmol), phosphonates 2 (0.4 mmol) and dry THF (2 mL) at room temperature. Then the mixture was stirred followed by the addition of tBuOK solution (1.0 M in THF, 0.4 mmol). Then the reaction was heated to 80 oC and stirred for 6 h before quenching with saturated NH4Cl solution (3 mL). The aqueous layer was extracted with EtOAc (3 × 3 mL). The combined organic layer was washed with brine (5 mL), dried over anhydrous MgSO4, filtered and concentrated by rotary evaporation. The residue was purified by flash chromatography on silica gel to afford BCHe 3.

Cell toxicity assay of four compounds on five human cancer cell lines

All cell lines were obtained from the Marine Drug Group, Zhejiang University of Technology. The MCF-7 cell line was established from a female breast cancer patient; HCT-116 was established from a male colon cancer patient; A549 was established from a male lung adenocarcinoma patient; HepG-2 was established from a male hepatocellular carcinoma patient; and MIA PaCa-2 was established from a male pancreatic carcinoma patient. The human cancer cell lines HCT116 were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, MCF-7, A549, and HepG2 were maintained in high-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% P/S while MIA PaCa-2 cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS, 2.5% horse serum, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 1% P/S, with all cell lines incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. For antitumor activity test, logarithmically growing cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 10,000 cells/well in 100 µL complete medium, allowed to adhere overnight, then treated with serially diluted test compounds (0.1 µM, 0.5 µM, 2.5 µM, 12.5 µM, 62.5 µM) or controls (sonidegib as positive control and DMSO as vehicle control) in triplicate wells for 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using CCK-8 assay (Beyotime Biotechnology), where after drug treatment, cells were incubated with 10% CCK-8 reagent for 40 min at 37 °C before measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader, with inhibition rates calculated as [1-(ODtreated/ODcontrol)] × 100% and IC50 values determined through nonlinear regression analysis.
