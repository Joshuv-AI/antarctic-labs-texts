Association between health locus of control and self-management in stable COPD patients: the mediation effect of coping styles
Authors: Xiaohe Ren, Yingying Cai, He Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with stable chronic obstructive pulmonary disease have poor self-management ability. Health locus of control and different coping styles can affect their self-management. This study aims to explore the relationship between health locus of control, coping styles and self-management, which investigate the mediating role of coping styles in the relationship between health locus of control and self-management in Chinese patients with stable chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.MethodsIn the cross-sectional study, a total of 306 patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were assessed on health locus of control, coping styles and self-management by self-reported questionnaires in China. Pearman’s correlation was used to test the links between variables and to verify the indirect effects between variables by constructing a structural equation model.ResultsA total of 306 valid questionnaires were collected. The total scores of health locus of control, coping styles and self-management were 65.69 ± 14.19, 50.75 ± 8.21 and 176.37 ± 34.19, respectively. Pearson’s correlation analysis showed that the health locus of control of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease revealed that the total score was positively correlated with self-management, positively correlated with the level of coping styles. Coping styles positively correlated with the level of self-management. Coping styles are a partial mediator of health locus of control and self-management behaviors, and the mediating effect accounts for 46.9% of the total effect.ConclusionsThis study identified coping styles as mediators in the relationship between health locus of control and self-management. These findings enhance the understanding of this mechanism and support the development of comprehensive self-management interventions. In clinical practice, nurses should assess the health locus of control and coping styles of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Encouraging patients to adopt adaptive coping strategies may contribute to improved self-management.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common, preventable and treatable disease, mainly characterized by persistent airflow limitation and recurrent respiratory symptoms. Due to its severe impact on lung function and exercise tolerance, COPD has become the third leading cause of death worldwide after ischemic heart disease and cancer [1]. Under the combined influence of smoking, population aging and other related chronic diseases, the prevalence of COPD continues to rise, posing a major public health challenge. Currently, the number of COPD patients worldwide exceeds 250 million, and the incidence rate is increasing year by year, making it one of the major causes of death globally [2, 3]. In China, the disease burden of COPD is particularly prominent, with a total of nearly 100 million patients and a prevalence rate of 13.7% among people over 40 years old, making it the third leading cause of death among Chinese residents [4]. It is predicted that the prevalence of COPD will continue to increase in the next four decades. It is estimated that by 2060, the number of deaths caused by COPD and related diseases may exceed 5.4 million each year [5]. The large number of patients not only seriously affects the quality of life of patients, but also brings heavy pressure on family and social medical resources.

At present, COPD cannot be cured completely, and the clinical intervention goals are mainly focused on stabilizing the condition and alleviating symptoms [6]. Against this backdrop, self-management, as an economical and effective model, is crucial for improving the quality of life and prognosis of patients [7]. Self-management refers to the comprehensive ability of patients to actively manage their symptoms, treatment, physical and psychosocial impacts, and adapt to lifestyle changes, which is a key factor in improving the health outcomes of COPD patients [8]. A systematic review by Schrijver et al. [9] reported that self-management interventions for COPD patients are associated with improved health-related quality of life and reduced probability of respiratory-related hospitalizations. A systematic review found that self-management interventions can improve lung function in patients with COPD [10]. It can be seen that effective self-management has been proven to enhance the physical functions of COPD patients, improve health outcomes, improve quality of life, and reduce the social and economic burdens associated with chronic diseases [11, 12].

According to the Health Promotion Model (HPM) [13], health promotion behaviors integrate three core elements: personal characteristics, behaviors, and environment. It emphasizes that health promotion is a dynamic process influenced by both internal and external factors. Health locus of control as a personal characteristic, refers to the belief that individuals’ health relies on the control of internal factors (including the individual’s behaviors and actions) or other factors (chance and health-care staff, such as doctors and nurses). It is of vital importance for maintaining healthy beliefs and behaviors. According to the multidimensional health locus of control theory, the three belief dimensions comprise one internal dimension (internal HLC, IHLC) and two external dimensions, namely, chance HLC (CHLC) and powerful others HLC (PHLC) exert distinct influences. IHLC refers to an individual’s tendency to attribute health outcomes to their own behavior and abilities. CHLC reflects an individual’s belief that health status is primarily determined by luck, fate, or chance. PHLC represents an individual’s belief that their health is more heavily influenced by authority figures such as doctors [13]. A strong internal HLOC is consistently associated with proactive health behaviors and better self-management in chronic conditions, including COPD. Conversely, a dominant chance HLOC is frequently linked to passive coping, poorer adherence, and adverse clinical outcomes. The role of powerful others HLOC is more nuanced; while trust in healthcare providers can facilitate initial treatment acceptance, an over-reliance on external control may potentially undermine long-term self-management autonomy in stable chronic disease phases. Specifically, a stronger IHLC is generally associated with more positive health habits and greater motivation to engage in self-management. In contrast, beliefs emphasizing chance or external control may correlate with less adaptive outcomes [14]. Research shows that the establishment of an IHLC for patients after a stroke is beneficial for their self-management of the disease [15]. A systematic review also investigated the correlation between HLOC and self-management behaviors in adults with ischemic heart disease [16]. However, previous studies mainly focused on exploring the cardiovascular disease population.There is insufficient evidence regarding the influencing factors of self-management in the COPD population and the underlying mechanisms between these factors.

Coping is defined as the cognitive and behavioral strategies individuals employ when facing setbacks and stress [17]. Previous studies have shown that patients who adopt positive coping strategies are more likely to actively seek disease-related information and assistance from others, thereby improving their self-management behaviors [18]. Conversely, negative coping styles weaken patients’ ability to adapt to their illness, making it difficult for them to cope with the lifestyle changes and long-term treatment associated with the disease. This gradually leads to psychological issues such as anxiety and frustration, which in turn result in reduced treatment adherence and ultimately affect their self-management behaviors [19]. A cross-sectional study also found that coping styles in patients with chronic diseases are independently associated with self-management [20]. Coping styles are not only independently associated with patients’ self-management capabilities but may also be influenced by deep-seated psychological beliefs (such as health locus of control). Specifically, Magdalena’s study demonstrated that stress coping styles among adults with chronic somatic diseases are related to their health locus of control [21]. Subsequent research further deepened this understanding, finding that among patients with coronary heart disease, a facing coping style mediates the relationship between an internal health locus of control and health-promoting behaviors [22]. For patients with COPD, this mediating mechanism holds particular clinical significance. As a progressive and incurable disease, the management of COPD during its stable phase relies primarily on long-term self-management outside the hospital setting, including adherence to medication, recognition of symptom changes, conservation of energy, and avoidance of triggers. However, due to the disease’s long duration, fluctuating symptoms, and heavy treatment burden, patients are highly prone to feelings of helplessness or treatment fatigue. In such circumstances, patients’ beliefs regarding the HLOC profoundly influence how they cope with the disease. For example, patients with an internal locus of control are more likely to proactively adopt problem-focused coping strategies to adapt to the disease, whereas those with an external or chance-based locus of control may resort to avoidance or resignation due to a perceived lack of control over their condition, thereby directly influencing their daily self-management behaviors. Therefore, a thorough exploration of the mediating role of coping styles between the HLOC and self-management in COPD patients is crucial for understanding the behavioral mechanisms of this vulnerable population.

Based on this, the present study aims to construct and validate a hypothetical model (Fig. 1) to elucidate the underlying mechanisms through which coping styles influence the relationship between perceived health control and self-management behaviors in elderly COPD patients. The findings are expected to provide a critical pathway for developing more targeted intervention strategies in clinical nursing practice, thereby effectively improving patients’ overall health and self-management capabilities.

Methods

Participants and procedures

A cross-sectional design was used to investigate health locus of control, coping styles, and self-management in patients with stable COPD. Accordingly, from May 2021 and June 2022, 306 patients with stable COPD were recruited through the convenience sampling method from a tertiary general hospital respiratory department in Shandong, China. Participants were considered eligible if they were: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for stable COPD as defined by the 2021 Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of COPD; (2) having Grade I or II pulmonary function; (3) possessing basic literacy and comprehension skills; and (4) providing informed consent voluntarily. Exclusion criteria included: (1) critically ill or acutely exacerbated patients; and (2) those with a documented history of psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairment.

As a cross-sectional survey, the sample size was determined based on Kendall’s recommendation that the minimum sample size should be 5 to 10 times the number of variables [23]. Accounting for a 15% invalid response rate, a final sample size of 306 was established. Prior to the commencement of the survey, the researchers obtained approval and cooperation from the target hospital and relevant department heads. Data were collected through on-site, face-to-face questionnaire surveys. To ensure consistency and accuracy in data collection, all questionnaires were distributed and collected by investigators who had received standardized training. Uniform instructions were used throughout the process to explain the purpose of the study and guide participants on how to complete the questionnaires. The questionnaires were collected on the spot and reviewed one by one by the investigators to ensure completeness and the absence of missing responses.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the affiliated hospital (Approval No: KYLL-QDQl2020084). In accordance with internationally recognized ethical standards for human research, this study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki .

Measures

General information questionnaire

The researchers developed a self-administered general information questionnaire to collect sociodemographic data, including gender, age, educational level, occupation, household income, marital status, and living arrangement.

Mental health locus of control scale

The Mental Health Locus of Control Scale (MHLC) was developed by Wallston et al. in 1987. The scale consists of three dimensions: Internal Health Locus of Control (IHLC), Powerful Others Health Locus of Control (PHLC), and Chance Health Locus of Control (CHLC) [24]. This questionnaire consists of 18 items. A six-point Likert scale is used for scoring, with higher scores indicating a stronger external or internal locus of control, depending on the subscale [25]. In this study, Cronbach’s α values for the IHLC, PHLC, and CHLC subscales were 0.80, 0.77, and 0.84, respectively.

Coping styles

The Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire (MCMQ) assessed coping styles.This 20-item Chinese version is grouped into three subscales: confrontation, avoidance, and acceptance–resignation [26]. Responses were recorded using a 4-point Likert scale (from never to all the time). Higher scores represented a greater frequency for participants who adopted certain coping strategies. The internal consistency reliability of each dimension ranged from 0.60 to 0.76 [27].

Self-management

The COPD Self-Management Scale (CSMS) consists of 5 dimensions: symptom management, daily life management, emotional management, information management, and self-efficacy, with a total of 51 items [28]. It uses a Likert 5-point scale with scores ranging from 1 to 5, with a total score between 51 and 255. The higher the score, the stronger the self-management ability. According to the calculation standard of self-management scores, patients with scores exceeding the calculation standard (> 180 points) are considered to have a high level of self-management ability, those with scores below the calculation standard (< 124 points) are considered to have a low level of self-management ability, and those with scores between 124 and 180 points are considered to have a medium level of self-management ability [29]. In the validation study, CSMS demonstrated good reliability and validity.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered using Excel software by two persons. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 29.0 and AMOS 27.0. Firstly, common method bias test, descriptive statistical analysis and correlation analysis were performed using SPSS 29.0. Secondly, structural equation modeling was carried out using AMOS 27.0 to analyze the mediating role of coping styles in the relationship between the health control points and self-management behaviors of COPD patients. Statistical significance was indicated by p < 0.05.

Results

Descriptive analysis

A total of 310 questionnaires were distributed, with 306 valid responses obtained (effective rate: 99.35%). The participants had a mean age of 71.93 ± 7.22 years; 79.1% were male. Nearly half were overweight (BMI 24.0–27.9), and the vast majority were married. Most resided in urban areas and over 85% lived with their spouse. Approximately 48.0% had a monthly household income per capita between RMB 3,000–5,000. Educational attainment was generally low, with 77.4% having junior middle school education or below. Workers and farmers were the dominant occupations. Regarding health behaviors, 40.9% were former smokers and 43.79% had quit drinking. Most patients were covered by health insurance and 61.8% had a disease course exceeding 10 years. Detailed data are presented in Table 1.

Disscussions

In the current cross-sectional study conducted in China, we investigated the relationship between health locus of control and self-management, and explored the mediating role of coping styles. The results indicated a significant positive correlation between health locus of control and self-management, with coping styles partially mediating this relationship among elderly COPD patients. These findings highlight the potential mechanism by which health locus of control can enhance self-management behaviors through the adoption of positive coping styles. Insights gained from this study may provide valuable evidence for developing targeted interventions to improve self-management in elderly COPD patients, especially considering the importance of chronic disease management and the increasing emphasis on enhancing patients’ self-health management capabilities in the Healthy China 2030 initiative.

This study found that the self-management ability score of elderly patients with COPD was 176.37 ± 34.19, which was at a medium to low level. This finding is consistent with the research results of Zhao et al. [6]. Although our study did not directly test associations with demographic factors, it is possible that patient characteristics common in our sample, such as rural residence and limited educational background, may contribute to these low scores. Previous research has suggested that patients from rural areas often face restricted access to ongoing medical services due to limited healthcare infrastructure and economic constraints [30]. Additionally, lower educational levels may make it more challenging for patients to comprehend and act upon complex health information [30]. The lack of systematic assessment schemes for chronic disease self-management [31] may further compound these difficulties by hindering the personalization of medical interventions. Therefore, strengthening discharge planning through channels such as Internet platforms and providing accessible health information may represent promising strategies for broadening patients’ knowledge channels and improving rehabilitation outcomes. Future research should empirically test the relationships between socioeconomic factors, healthcare access, and self-management to confirm these potential associations.

In this study, the health locus of control score of COPD patients was 50.75 ± 8.21, which was at a medium to low level, similar to the results of previous studies [32]. One possible explanation for this finding relates to the clinical characteristics of COPD. Patients with this condition typically experience symptoms such as severe dyspnea, cough, and sputum production, which can substantially limit daily activities. It is plausible that these persistent physical limitations may weaken patients’ confidence in their ability to influence their own health outcomes, potentially leading them to attribute health outcomes to external forces. However, as this relationship was not directly examined in the present study, this interpretation should be viewed as a hypothesis warranting further investigation.

Unlike previous studies, an unexpected finding in our correlation matrix was the moderate positive association between internal HLOC and chance HLOC. This contrasts with much of the existing literature, where these constructs are typically orthogonal or negatively related. One plausible interpretation is that in the context of managing a chronic, progressive, and sometimes unpredictable condition like COPD, patients may develop a more integrated or complex belief system [33]. They might acknowledge the importance of their own self-management efforts while simultaneously feeling that exacerbations or disease progression can occur despite their best efforts, attributing these events to ‘bad luck’ or factors beyond their control. This co-existence of beliefs could reflect a realistic appraisal of the illness experience rather than a mutually exclusive psychological construct. What’s more, both avoidance coping and acceptance-resignation were positively correlated with all self-management dimensions. While counter to theories that link such strategies to poor adaptation, these findings may reflect a unique dynamic within our sample of stable COPD patients. In the context of a chronic, incurable, and progressively limiting disease, a certain degree of strategic avoidance may be a functional, self-protective behavior that is accurately reported and contributes to symptom stability. Similarly, acceptance-resignation in this population might not represent maladaptive surrender, but rather a form of emotional acceptance—a realistic coming to terms with the illness’s permanence. This psychological acceptance could reduce disease-related distress and free up cognitive-emotional resources, thereby paradoxically facilitating better adherence to daily self-care routines that do not require active problem-solving but rather consistent execution. These interpretations suggest that the functionality of a coping strategy is highly context-dependent and may be qualitatively different in stable chronic disease management versus acute crisis or early adjustment phases [34]. Future qualitative and longitudinal research is needed to unpack the nuanced meanings and behavioral correlates of these coping subtypes in COPD populations.

This study found a significant positive correlation between health locus of control and self-management in patients with COPD, indicating that patients with a stronger internal locus of control tend to engage in better self-management behaviors. This finding aligns with previous research documenting similar associations in stroke survivors [33], diabetic patients [34], and those with ischemic heart disease [35], suggesting that the relationship between health locus of control and self-management may be consistent across various chronic disease populations. The theoretical underpinning for this relationship can be understood through the lens of self-management frameworks. Individuals who believe their health outcomes depend primarily on their own actions are more likely to invest effort in disease monitoring, treatment adherence, and lifestyle modifications. In contrast, those who attribute health outcomes to external forces such as chance or powerful others may adopt a more passive approach to their daily disease management. For COPD patients specifically, this distinction carries particular clinical relevance, as effective self-management requires sustained daily engagement with complex medication regimens, symptom monitoring, and behavioral adaptations. From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that assessing health locus of control could help identify patients at risk for poor self-management who might benefit from additional support. Interventions designed to strengthen internal control beliefs, while respecting patients’ individual circumstances and cultural contexts, may complement traditional skill-based self-management education. For example, educational modules that emphasize personal agency, tailored tools that reinforce the connection between daily actions and health outcomes, and community-based programs that provide structured guidance and social support could collectively help translate this psychological construct into improved self-management behaviors. Such approaches align with broader population health strategies, including national initiatives focused on health literacy enhancement [36].

The mediating role observed for coping styles suggests a potential psychological pathway linking health locus of control to self-management behaviors in patients with stable COPD. The consistency of our findings with prior research across various chronic conditions points to the possible relevance of this psychological process across disease contexts [37, 38]. Our study extends this established paradigm to the COPD population, suggesting that the association between control beliefs and coping adaptability may be a relevant factor in disease management. This mechanism can be understood within the framework of the transactional model of stress and coping [39]. Patients with a strong internal health locus of control may be more likely to appraise the challenges of COPD management as controllable demands [40]. This cognitive appraisal may predispose them to adopt problem-focused coping strategies, such as actively seeking health information, diligently adhering to exercise plans, and proactively managing their symptoms. These constructive efforts may, in turn, translate into better self-management. Conversely, patients with an external health locus of control, who perceive their health outcomes as being determined by fate or powerful others, may be more prone to view disease management as an insurmountable threat [33]. This appraisal may foster emotion-focused or avoidant coping styles, characterized by denial, resignation, or behavioral disengagement, which could subsequently undermine their engagement in essential self-care activities. Therefore, in clinical practice, nursing staff working with patients who exhibit negative coping strategies might consider providing disease information and emotional support, along with targeted intervention strategies to encourage changes in coping approaches, with the goal of supporting better self-management. It should be noted, however, that these proposed pathways are based on cross-sectional data; longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the temporal sequence of these relationships and to rule out reverse causality, such as the possibility that better self-management leads to more internal control beliefs or more adaptive coping styles.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it is noteworthy that coping styles served as a partial mediator in the relationship between health locus of control and self-management, with the effect size being modest. This finding does not preclude the existence of other significant mediators, such as self-efficacy, social support, or illness perception, which may also help explain the observed associations [41]. Future research with larger samples is required to test a more comprehensive multiple-mediation model. Second, and most importantly, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference regarding the relationships between health locus of control, coping styles, and self-management. The directional paths proposed in our model should be interpreted with caution, as reverse causality cannot be ruled out. Future longitudinal or interventional studies are needed to establish temporal precedence and causal relationships. Third, due to the single-center design and the use of a convenience sampling method, the generalizability of our findings to all COPD patients in other settings should be approached with caution. Fourth, all data were self-reported using questionnaires, which may introduce issues of recall bias and social desirability bias. The use of a common method for measurement might also lead to common method variance. Finally, the structural equation model used in this study analyzed single-dimensional total scores for health locus of control and coping strategies, failing to distinguish potential differential effects across their internal dimensions. Conclusions

In conclusion, this study found that the self-management level of elderly patients with COPD needs to be improved. Coping styles play a significant partial mediating role between health control points and self-management, and the mediating effect accounts for a high proportion of the total effect. This result reveals an important mechanism by which psychological factors influence behavior: patients’ health control beliefs not only directly promote self-management, but also play a more significant role through the indirect path of coping strategies. The research findings suggest a correlation between health locus of control, coping styles and self-management in patients with COPD. In clinical practice, medical staff may consider assessing these psychological factors alongside routine health education. Interventions that guide patients toward adaptive coping strategies could potentially support better self-management outcomes, although the cross-sectional design of this study cannot establish causality. Future longitudinal or interventional research is needed to confirm whether changes in health control beliefs and coping styles precede improvements in self-management.
