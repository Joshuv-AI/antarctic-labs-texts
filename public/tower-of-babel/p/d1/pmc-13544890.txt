Subtle Signals of Severe Disease: Central Nervous System Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis
Authors: BC Rohan, Supriya Kushwah, Shikha Jain, Rupali Rokade, Abhijit Choudhary, Meenakshi Girish, Vishvdeep Khushoo
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by aberrant immune activation. While systemic HLH is well-documented, isolated central nervous system HLH (CNS-HLH) remains underrecognized and diagnostically challenging. The familial variant, particularly those involving PRF1 mutations, can present with exclusive neurological involvement. These cases often mimic more common demyelinating disorders or vasculitis, leading to significant diagnostic delays. Through this case report, we aim to highlight the importance of considering CNS-HLH in the differential diagnosis of atypical neurological presentations to ensure timely intervention.

A previously healthy 15-year-old boy, with an unremarkable developmental and scholastic history, presented with a one-month history of progressive left-sided hemiparesis, gait instability, and dysarthria. He was initially treated elsewhere and later presented to us with one episode of generalized tonic-clonic seizure. Notably, there was no history of consanguinity or prior systemic illness. On examination, internuclear ophthalmoplegia with left hemiparesis was noted. Brisk deep tendon reflexes were observed in all four limbs, along with positive cerebellar signs. The remainder of the examination was unremarkable. Limited sequences from outside brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed multiple patchy hyperintense demyelinating lesions in the bilateral cerebral hemispheres, cerebellar hemispheres, and brainstem. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed lymphocytic pleocytosis, with normal CSF culture and viral polymerase chain reaction results. Extensive workup for infectious (human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis, tuberculosis) and immune-mediated etiologies was negative. The child was initially diagnosed with acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), and intravenous pulse methylprednisolone (MPS) was administered for five days. Due to incomplete recovery, progressive ataxia, and new-onset tinnitus, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (2 g/kg) was also administered. Following symptomatic improvement, he was discharged on a three-month oral steroid taper. Although he improved with treatment, recovery remained incomplete, and the child was subsequently lost to follow-up. Seven months after disease onset, the child presented with progressive headache, diplopia, worsening ataxia, and tinnitus. Neurological examination revealed dysarthria and extensive cranial nerve involvement, with persistence of internuclear ophthalmoplegia and facial palsy. Positive cerebellar signs and an ataxic gait were observed. Owing to worsening illness following partial improvement with steroids, possibilities including multiple sclerosis, chronic lymphocytic inflammation with pontine perivascular enhancement responsive to steroids (CLIPPERS), CNS lymphoma, and CNS-HLH were considered [Table 1]. MRI of the brain with contrast showed multiple patchy, fluffy fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintense lesions in the subcortical and deep white matter of the bilateral cerebral and cerebellar hemispheres, along with involvement of the corpus callosum and brainstem [Figure 1]. Contrast enhancement and microhemorrhages were observed in the cortex, brainstem, and cerebellum. Comprehensive workup, including repeat blood and CSF analyses, was normal, with no malignant cells or oligoclonal bands detected. Autoimmune and vasculitis workup remained negative. Systemic HLH markers—including serum ferritin, fibrinogen, triglycerides, and platelet counts—were within normal limits.

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) ultimately confirmed compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in the PRF1 gene: c.394G>A (p.Gly132Arg) and c.386G>C (p.Trp129Ser). These variants are associated with Familial HLH type 2 (OMIM#603553). In silico prediction tools classified both variants as damaging. The child was planned for treatment according to the HLH-1994 protocol, with ongoing plans for bone marrow transplantation based on human leukocyte antigen (HLA) matching.

CNS-Familial HLH remains an uncommon manifestation that frequently mimics other CNS inflammatory diseases, including CLIPPERS, ADEM, and small-vessel CNS vasculitis. Moshous et al. reported primary necrotizing lymphocytic CNS vasculitis resulting from perforin deficiency, while Algahtani et al. highlighted Familial HLH presenting as a demyelinating disease.[12] MRI can help differentiate close mimics. Symmetrical periventricular lesions are commonly associated with HLH.[34] HLH also has a posterior fossa predilection, with abnormal progressive microhemorrhage foci, while contrast studies show nodular and focal patterns of enhancement. Hemorrhagic features within demyelinating lesions should raise suspicion for conditions such as CNS vasculitis and HLH.[56]

Isolated CNS involvement is a rare and misleading presentation of Familial HLH, often mimicking disorders such as ADEM or CLIPPERS. Recurrent or steroid-dependent neuroinflammation with atypical radiological features should prompt suspicion of underlying immunological dysregulation. Early consideration of genetic testing, particularly WES, is crucial in refractory cases to initiate prompt HLH-directed therapy and prevent disease progression.
