Linear and Unilateral Basaloid Follicular Hamartoma Along Blaschko’s Lines in an Elderly Patient: A Rare Presentation
Authors: Pratiksha Mishra, Swatismita Sahoo, Subhransu K. Hota, Lity Mohanty, Siddhartha Dash, Alaka Senapati
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Basaloid follicular hamartoma (BFH) is a rare hamartoma of follicular origin which occurs due to abortive growth of secondary hair germs with limited differentiation towards the upper follicular portion of hair shaft.[1] Clinically, BFH can appear as solitary or multiple lesions having a generalized or localized unilateral linear distribution. It may be congenital, familial, sporadic, or acquired.[1] Herein we report a case of late onset linear and unilateral basaloid follicular hamartoma along Blaschko’s lines in an elderly male patient.

A 68-year-old male presented with multiple asymptomatic skin-colored to brown papules and plaques in a linear pattern for eight years. There was no history of muscle weakness, joint pain, or photosensitivity. Family history was unremarkable. General examination revealed no abnormality. On cutaneous examination, there were multiple asymptomatic skin-colored to brown papules and plaques in a blaschkoid pattern over his anterior chest, left shoulder and axilla, and extending to left arm and left posterior chest [Figure 1a and b]. Few lesions had verrucosity over their surface. Rest of the mucocutaneous and systemic examination were within normal limits. Dermoscopy (Dermlite DL4 with polarized light under 10X magnification) showed reddish brown globules and blotches surrounded by accentuated reticular pigment network along with comedo-like opening in few areas [Figure 2a and b]. Differential diagnosis of blaschkitis and blaschkolinear lichen planus were kept and tissue was sent for histopathological examination after taking informed consent from the patient. The biopsy sample consisted of scanty skin structure measuring 0.5 cm in diameter. Histopathological examination revealed normal epidermis with a well-circumscribed tumor located in the deep dermis, having focal connections to the epidermis. The tumor was characterized by a basaloid and squamoid proliferation of epithelial cells arranged in strands and branched cords around the hair follicles in the deep dermis [Figure 3a and b]. Majority of the tumor exhibited basaloid proliferation, with a few scattered squamoid cells. Dermis showed periappendageal and perivascular chronic lymphocytic infiltrates. There were no signs of peripheral palisading, mitosis, necrosis, or nuclear atypia. Biochemical, hematological, and radiological investigations were within normal limits. Based on clinical and histopathological examination, a final diagnosis of linear and unilateral basaloid follicular hamartoma (BFH) was made and the patient was planned for radio-frequency ablation of the lesions.

The term BFH was first coined by Mehregan and Baker in 1985.[1] Abnormal PTCH signalling pathways contribute to the pathogenesis of BFH, however, the aberrant expression is lower as blaschkolinear compared to basal cell carcinoma (BCC).[1] Clinically, BFH presents as solitary or multiple lesions, and can have generalized or localized linear unilateral distribution. Solitary BFH presents as smooth plaque or papule mostly over face and scalp. Generalized BFH can be sporadic (without systemic disease), acquired (associated with alopecia and autoimmune diseases like myasthenia gravis and systemic lupus erythematosus), familial (autosomal dominant form with or without hypotrichosis, hypohidrosis, and palmoplantar pitting), and congenital (associated with hypotrichosis, punctate palmoplantar keratotic pits, and cystic fibrosis).[1] In generalized acquired form, lesions appears mostly over face and periorificial areas, and in generalized familial form, lesions appear over the face and genitals.[1] Generalized basaloid follicular hamartoma syndrome is an autosomal dominantly inherited disorder that presents with congenital or early onset BFH with comedones, milia, dermatosis papulosa nigra, and achrochordons.[1]

The linear and unilateral BFH may be present at birth or appear during childhood or puberty, and presents as hypopigmented to hyperpigmented macules, papules, or plaques with or without comedones. It has no systemic association; however, sporadic association has been reported like Grave’s disease, congenital dorsal scoliosis, and Happle-Tinschert syndrome. Multiple BCCs and trichoblastomas has been reported to occur within lesions of linear and unilateral BFH.[1] In the index case, verrucosity was present over few lesions and there was an unusual late onset of the above-described variant without any systemic associations. The clinical differential diagnoses include lesions having blaschkolinear distribution such as blaschkitis, blaschkolinear lichen planus, linear verrucous epidermal nevus, linear trichoepithelioma, linear follicular Darier’s disease, linear eccrine spiradenoma, linear poroma, linear syringoma, and others.[1]

Microscopically, BFH shows well delineated, anastomosing islands and strands of basaloid and squamoid tumor cells often connected to the epidermis. Surrounding stroma is usually loose, cellular or fibrocellular with scant inflammatory infiltrates. The tumor cells are often limited to the superficial dermis. Histopathological differential diagnosis includes basal cell carcinoma (BCC) because of the presence of basaloid tumor cells. However, BCC may involve deeper tissues, present in interfollicular dermis showing purely basaloid tissues and may show nuclear atypia, mitosis, and necrosis.[2] Fibroepithelial tumor of pinkus may also show thin anastomosing strands and cords of basaloid or squamous cells intermixed with plump bright cells of melanophages, however peripheral palisading is common.[2] Trichoepithelioma shows superficial anastomosing nests of basaloid cells but with prominent keratin horn cysts.[2]

Currently there is no standard treatment protocol for BFH and requires excision due to cosmesis. Identification at correct time is needed for adequate intervention for periodic follow up to prevent malignant transformations. When associated with systemic autoimmune diseases, treatment of the comorbidities associated may show regression in skin lesions.[3]
