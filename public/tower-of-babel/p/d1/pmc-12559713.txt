Impact compression test and dynamic triaxial unified strength criterion for concrete under freeze–thaw cycle action
Authors: Wenbiao Liang, Yan Li, Xiaohong Guo, Jinlong Si, Lintao Li, Kai Ding, Yi Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Directing at the concrete material, a set of freeze–thaw (FT) cycle tests with a temperature range of − 20 °C to 20 °C and FT cycles from 0 to 50 times were carried out in this paper, series of uniaxial and triaxial impact compression tests with high strain rate (about 101 s−1–103 s−1) were completed on the Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) system, and the dynamic compression strength, failure mode and the influencing rules were analyzed. According to the test results, the rate-dependent expression of concrete strength parameter cohesion was derived based on the unified strength theory and the rate-dependent physical mechanism of concrete strength thermal activation and cohesive coexisting and competing. Through developing the classical static unified strength criterion into a dynamic unified strength criterion, the dynamic triaxial unified strength criterion of concrete under different FT cycles was established, and its correctness and applicability were proved from both the necessity analysis and the experimental verification. On this basis, the influence characteristics of intermediate principal stress effect and strength criterion on the calculation of concrete dynamic compression strength were discussed.

The cold regions in China are widely distributed. The seasonal and permanent cold areas account for more than three quarters of China’s national territory. Concrete is the most extensively utilized building material globally, and China’s consumption of concrete has surpassed that of all other countries combined, representing 60% of the world’s total1. In cold region construction, concrete is subjected to prolonged exposure in freeze–thaw (FT) cycle environments. The residual moisture within the capillary pores of the concrete continues to undergo freezing and thawing, leading to the progressive development of internal voids. This phenomenon can significantly impact the structural integrity of concrete, resulting in issues such as cracking, deformation, and even catastrophic failure. Moreover, engineering structures in cold regions face additional threats from dynamic loads including earthquakes, strong winds, explosions, and shocks. Therefore, investigating the dynamic mechanical properties of concrete under FT cycles holds substantial theoretical significance and practical value for engineering applications, particularly concerning dynamic strength under multi-axial complex stress states. Such research can provide valuable insights for mechanism analysis, prediction, evaluation, and prevention strategies related to freeze–thaw damage in projects located in cold region.

There have been many studies on the dynamic properties of concrete under various conditions. These studies are mainly focused on mechanical properties, constitutive models, microscopic composition, aggregate characteristics, numerical simulation analysis and other related aspects2–5. The establishment and selection of the dynamic strength criteria is the key to study the dynamic strength of materials. Most concrete materials in engineering structures are in the state of complex stress, and have the basic characteristics such as tension and compression strength inequality, intermediate principal stress effects and intensity interval. These are the basic issues that must be considered when establishing their dynamic strength criteria. The dynamic strength test of concrete provides the basis for the establishment of dynamic strength criteria. In general, there are two main methods for establishing the dynamic multiaxial strength criterion of concrete. One method involves directly establishing the relationship between concrete multi-axial strength and strain rate through the fitting of experimental results6–8. The strength criterion expression derived from this method is an empirical formula. However, its limitation lies in the restricted scope of application, which cannot be broadened. The other method is based on the existing static multi-axis strength criterion, and the static strength criterion is developed into the dynamic strength criterion by giving the strain rate correlation expression of the static strength parameter9,10. For example, based on the Mohr–Coulomb strength criterion, Qian established a Mohr–Coulomb constitutive model considering the effect of strength-strain rate under complex stress state through the correlation expression of cohesive force11,12.

At present, the study of dynamic strength criteria for brittle materials such as concrete and rock is based on classical yield criteria such as Tresca, Mohr–Coulomb, Hoek–Brown and Drucker-Prager13,14. However, the Tresca,

Mohr–Coulomb, and Hoek–Brown strength criteria do not consider the influences of the intermediate principal stress effect and the intensity interval. Although the Drucker-Prager strength criterion has considered the influence of the intermediate principal stress effect, it does not consider the inequality characteristic of tension and compression, so it can only be applied to the materials with equal tensile and compression strength. In contrast, unified strength theory15 can comprehensively reflect the basic strength characteristics including tensile and strength. The compression strength inequality, intermediate principal stress effect and intensity interval of brittle materials suchas concrete, and embody the influence of different strength criteria on the material mechanical properties. It has been widely used in solving complex stress state problems of concrete and rock materials16–19. However, the research based on the unified strength theory for concrete and rock materials mostly focuses on the static mechanical problems, and the dynamic mechanical research is relatively rare20–22. Although Wei21 and Wang22 had analyzed the anti-penetration performance of rock targets according to the unified strength theory, they did not consider the influence of strain rate effect on the dynamic strength of materials, which has certain limitations. Studies have shown that the strain rate effect has an important impact on the impact resistance of concrete materials23–25. Therefore, in allusion to the problem of concrete dynamic strength under complex stress conditions, it is necessary to establish a dynamic multi-axis strength criterion that reflects the basic strength characteristics of brittle materials and considers the strain rate effect in accordance with the actual conditions of the project.

In conclusion, the existing studies on the dynamic characteristics of concrete have paid less attention to the coupling effect of FT cycles and multi-axial dynamic loads. Thus, investigating the dynamic mechanical properties of concrete under FT cycles, especially the multi-axial dynamic strength criteria, is of great theoretical and practical significance. It not only helps reveal the damage mechanism of concrete under the combined action of FT cycles and dynamic loads but also provides a scientific basis for the design, maintenance, and disaster prevention of cold-region infrastructure, which is vital for improving the durability and safety of engineering structures in harsh environments.

In this paper, a series of FT cycle tests and high strain rate26,27 uniaxial and triaxial impact compression tests using the SHPB system were conducted on C35 concrete, since C35 concrete is commonly used in the concrete engineering in China. The purpose of the tests is to explore the influence laws of factors such as freeze–thaw cycle degradation, strain rate, confining pressure and intermediate principal stress on the dynamic strength of concrete. Combining with the test results, based on the unified strength theory and the coexisting-competing mechanism of thermal activation and cohesion, the rate-dependent expression of concrete cohesion was derived. By developing the classical static unified strength criterion into a dynamic unified strength criterion, the dynamic triaxial unified strength criterion of concrete under different FT cycles was established. This criterion considers strain rate effects, FT degradation, intermediate principal stress effects, and tension–compression strength inequality, and the correctness of the criterion is verified through theoretical derivation and experimental data. These findings provide a new theoretical framework for evaluating the dynamic performance of concrete in cold-region engineering.

For the static compressive strength test, three standard concrete cubic specimens were cast. After undergoing standard curing, and their 28-day compressive strengths were measured as follows: 45.3 MPa, 45.9 MPa, 46.5 MPa, with an average value of 45.9 MPa.

The concrete was processed into cylindrical specimens with dimensions of Φ50mm × 25 mm. Both end faces of the specimens were polished to ensure that non-parallelism and non-perpendicularity were maintained within 0.02 mm28. After numbering the specimens and measuring their volume and mass, they were placed in a drying oven for 48 h. Subsequently, the specimens were transferred to a water-filled vessel. Distilled water was injected into the vessel while continuously pumping out air at a pressure of 0.1 MPa for four hours until no air bubbles appeared on the surface of the specimens. The specimens were then allowed to stand in water for over 24 h, resulting in saturated concrete samples.

In the FT cycle test, the saturated concrete specimens were initially placed in an automatic low-temperature FT test chamber (Model: CLD-1; Temperature measurement accuracy: ± 0.5℃; Temperature range: + 50℃ to -40℃). The FT temperature was set to vary from -20℃ to + 20℃ for different FT cycles. The process of one complete freezing and thawing cycle is as follows: firstly, the temperature is reduced from + 20℃ to -20℃ over a period of 120 min, followed by maintaining this constant temperature for 240 min. Subsequently, the specimen is rapidly warmed back to room temperature (+ 20℃) within 30 min and held at this constant temperature for another 240 min. The total duration of one FT cycle amounts to 630 min, with 10 FT cycles defined as a single testing period.

Impact compression test

In this paper, we focus on concrete specimens subjected to various FT cycles (0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50). Uniaxial and triaxial compression tests were conducted at different strain rates using the Φ50mm split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) system under active confining pressures of 5 MPa and 10 MPa (as shown in Fig. 1).Fig. 1SHPB system.

SHPB system.

In order to prolong the duration before the incident pulse reaches its peak and maintain a constant strain rate, it is essential to consider waveform shaping operations. This ensures that the specimen has sufficient time to achieve stress uniformity prior to failure29. In this study, a series of experiments were conducted on various sizes of copper shapers, and their effects on waveform shaping were analyzed. At impact rates of 5.4 m/s, 8.8 m/s, and 11.3 m/s, shapers with a thickness of 1 mm and diameters of 25 mm, 20 mm, and 15 mm were selected accordingly. Prior to conducting the SHPB test, molybdenum disulfide was uniformly applied to both end faces of the specimen in order to minimize friction between the specimen and pressure bars as well as reduce energy loss due to end face friction.

After different freeze–thaw cycles, the internal defects of concrete specimens propagated, expanded, gathered and penetrated continuously, and eventually developed from microscopic damage to macroscopic fracture. Table 2 shows that after experiencing 10 freeze–thaw cycles, the surface deterioration characteristics of the concrete specimen were mainly reflected on the slight peeling off the edge of coarse aggregate and cementing materials, and cracking phenomenon began occurring on the specimen surface at this time. After suffering 20 freeze–thaw cycles, the cementing material fell off slightly at the edge of specimens, mainly concentrating near the coarse aggregate, and obvious cracks appeared at the interface between coarse aggregate and cementing material. After undergoing 30 freeze–thaw cycles, cracks on the surface of specimens developed continuously, and the number and width of cracks had increased. The aggregation of cracks led to a large crack along the axial direction of the specimen, accompanied by a small amount of spalling of edge aggregates and colloids. After 40 freeze–thaw cycles, the surface cracks of specimens increased and widened significantly, and the axial cracks further extended and penetrated, with new cracks generated along the aggregate-colloid interface. The growth and connection of multiple cracks increased the peeling area of the specimen edge. After being frozen and thawed 50 times, the main cracks of concrete specimens were connected, and the aggregate peeled seriously from the edges. The concrete specimen had begun to take on an incomplete form. When freeze–thaw cycles reached 60 times, several main cracks were connected through the specimen interior, the bulks of the specimen peeled off, and the appearance deteriorated seriously, and as a result, the specimen lost its integrity and could not perform the mechanical test. Therefore, the upper limit number of freeze–thaw cycles in the follow-up study of this paper was 50 times.

According to the above analysis of the apparent deterioration characteristics and damage evolution processes of concrete under the freeze–thaw cycle actions, the mechanism of freeze–thaw cycle deterioration of concrete is as follows: the concrete is a kind of non-homogeneous material and there exists internal microcracks and micro pores. The internal freeze–thaw damage of concrete specimens starts from the freezing and thawing of the water in internal initial pores of materials, and the external apparent degradation begins at the aggregate-colloid interface. As the cracks grow and extend continuously, the damage develops further to the spalling of aggregates, colloids, and even concrete blocks. This is because after the water inside the concrete freezes, the specimen volume expands and the frost heaving force causes continuous development of internal pores; the water in the pores further increases, then the specimen volume continues to expand, resulting in the more severe deterioration and the loss of integrity.

It can be seen from Table 3, Fig. 2 and Table 4 that the freeze–thaw cycles significantly influenced both the dynamic compression strength and failure modes of concrete. Within the strain rate range of 101.31 to 102.68 s⁻1, as the number of freeze–thaw (FT) cycles increased from 0 to 50, the uniaxial compressive strength of concrete decreased from values of 55.10 MPa, 68.00 MPa, and 79.10 MPa to levels of 16.30 MPa, 27.30 MPa, and 29.70 MPa respectively. Similarly, the triaxial dynamic compression strength diminished from initial measurements of 77.60 MPa, 118.30 MPa, 129.80 MPa, and 161.50 MPa to 69.50 MPa, 92.50 MPa, 78.10 MPa, and108.80 MPa correspondingly. Throughout this transition, there was a continuous increase in the degree of impact fracture within the concrete, specifically, uniaxial impact failure morphology evolved progressively from strip-shaped fragments and large blocks into fine uniform particles. In terms of triaxial impact failure morphology, it transitioned gradually from apparent damage to longitudinal splitting before ultimately reaching an impacted crushing state. In essence, with an increasing number of FT cycles, the dynamic failure mode shifted systematically from splitting failures towards crushing failures. This phenomenon can be attributed to lower internal crack density at fewer FT cycles, resulting in reduced damage levels within specimens. Under dynamic loading conditions, the existing internal cracks propagated directly along their original paths leading to minimal fracturing or flaws,and thus exhibiting a splitting failure mode. The measured dynamic compression strengths during this phase were consequently higher. As the number of FT cycles escalated, the distribution density of axialand transverse crackswithin thespecimen intensified,resulting in greater overall specimen damage.Under subsequent dynamic loading conditions,specimens rapidly lost stability becoming fragmented into smaller particles which ledto severe breakage manifesting as acrushingfailure mode.The corresponding measured dynamic compression strengths atthis stage exhibited a marked decrease.

It is evident from Table 3, Fig. 2 and Table 4 that confining pressure also significantly influenced the dynamic compressive strength and failure mode of concrete. Within the strain rate range of 101.31 to 102.68 s-1, under identical freeze–thaw cycle conditions, an increase in confining pressure markedly enhanced the dynamic compressive strength of concrete while substantially reducing the extent of fracture. This phenomenon can be attributed to the active confining pressure, which constrained the initiation and propagation of localized cracks. Specifically, the confining pressure increased the normal stress on the concrete slip surface, thereby raising the critical failure strength and inducing a transition in failure mode from uniaxial brittle failure to triaxial plastic failure.

Furthermore, Table 3, Fig. 2 and Table 4 indicate that concrete materials exhibit a significant strain rate effect. As the strain rate increases, both the uniaxial and triaxial impact compressive strengths of concrete generally increase, with the degree of fracture becoming progressively more severe. This phenomenon can be attributed to the enhanced cohesion of concrete materials due to increased axial loading strain rates, leading to higher dynamic compressive strength. Additionally, higher strain rates result in greater impact energy, causing specimens to absorb more energy upon failure, thereby exacerbating the severity of fractures. However, as observed in Table 3, some concrete specimens demonstrated an increase in dynamic compressive strength despite approximately equal or slightly decreased strain rates, such as those under FT10 and FT30 conditions with 10 MPa confining pressure, and FT40 conditions with 5 MPa and 10 MPa confining pressures. Two possible explanations for this phenomenon are: (1) High-strain-rate impact tests, particularly multi-axial impact tests, are challenging to conduct successfully, resulting in measured strain rates that exhibit some degree of variability. (2) Under triaxial stress states, the damage extent of concrete specimens varies across different working conditions. For instance, some specimens exhibited only superficial cracking or peeling, as seen in condition FT0-10–1 with 0 FT cycles, 10 MPa confining pressure, and a strain rate of 101.52 s-1, while others experienced severe crushing, as in condition FT50-10–1 with 50 FT cycles, 10 MPa confining pressure, and a strain rate of 101.49 s-1. The diversity in failure modes and extents led to varying critical breaking strengths measured in the tests.

According to the analysis presented above, it can be concluded that the dynamic mechanical properties of concrete materials in a specific freezing and thawing environment are the result of the combined influences of freeze–thaw cycle degradation effects, confining pressure enhancement effects, and strain rate strengthening effects. Therefore, it is crucial to establish a dynamic multiaxial strength criterion for concrete that comprehensively considers these factors.

Rate-dependent physical model of concrete dynamic strength

In different strain rate ranges, physical mechanisms of the rate correlation of concrete dynamic strength are different. When the strain rate is in the lower range, the deformation heat activation mechanism plays a leading role, and the concrete strength changes slowly with the increase of the strain rate in this phase. When the strain rate is higher, the viscous damping mechanism turns to a leading role, and the concrete dynamic strength changes drastically in this stage. When the strain rate is very large, the thermal activation mechanism becomes the dominant role again, and the concrete dynamic strength tends to be flat1,11,12. Discussion

The unified strength criterion parameter b serves as an indicator of the influence exerted by intermediate principal stress effects and embodies various strength criteria. By setting the strain rate at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\varepsilon }$$\end{document} = 102 s-1 and varying b to values of 0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 respectively, the dynamic compression strength of concrete under different fatigue cycle actions is illustrated in Fig. 5.Fig. 5Dynamic compression strength of concrete under different working conditions with the variation of strength criterion parameter b.

Dynamic compression strength of concrete under different working conditions with the variation of strength criterion parameter b.

As depicted in Fig. 5, it is evident that the envelope curve of the dynamic triaxial unified strength criterion expands outward continuously as b increases from 0.0 to 1.0. This observation indicates that accounting for intermediate principal stress effects can enhance the material’s strength potential; furthermore, a higher confining pressure amplifies this effect significantly. In practical engineering applications, concrete materials typically experience complex three-dimensional stress states; thus, neglecting the impact of intermediate principal stress on material strength would be unwise.

Additionally, Fig. 5 suggests that as b transitions from 0.0 to 1.0, the envelope curve shifts from an inner boundary corresponding to the dynamic Mohr–Coulomb strength criterion toward an outer boundary aligned with the dynamic twin shear strength criterion. This transition signifies that while calculations based on the dynamic Mohr–Coulomb strength criterion yield a lower limit for dynamic compression strength, those derived from the dynamic twin shear strength criterion provide an upper limit value for such measurements.

Moreover, this analysis underscores that selecting an appropriate strength criterion is crucial for accurately predicting the dynamic compression strengths of concrete materials in practice; therefore it is imperative to choose suitable criteria during calculations in order to optimize design efficiency and conserve materials effectively.

Conclusion

In this paper, freeze–thaw tests, as well as uniaxial and triaxial impact compression tests were conducted on concrete materials. The following conclusions could be drawn:The results of freeze–thaw tests show that freeze–thaw has a significant deterioration effect on concrete materials. After 50 freeze–thaw cycles, through cracks form in the concrete specimens, and the edges of the specimens spall seriously. When freeze–thaw cycles reached 60 times, several main cracks were connected through the specimen interior, the bulks of the specimen peeled off, and the appearance deteriorated seriously.The results of impact compression tests indicate that freeze–thaw (FT) cycle action, strain rate, and confining pressure significantly influence the dynamic compression strength and failure modes of concrete materials. With an increasing number of FT cycles, the dynamic failure mode shifted systematically from splitting failures towards crushing failures. Meanwhile, the dynamic compressive strengths were significantly reduced. The increase in confining pressure significantly improves the dynamic compressive strength of concrete and greatly reduces the degree of concrete fracture. As the strain rate increases, both the uniaxial and triaxial impact compressive strengths of concrete generally increase, with the degree of fracture becoming progressively more severe. In a specific freeze–thaw environment, the dynamic mechanical properties of concrete are a comprehensive outcome resulting from the interplay of FT cycle degradation effects, strain rate strengthening effects, and confining pressure enhancement effects.The dynamic triaxial unified strength criterion developed in this study comprehensively addresses various dynamic mechanical characteristics, including strain rate effects, intermediate principal stress effects, tension–compression strength inequality, and intensity intervals.
