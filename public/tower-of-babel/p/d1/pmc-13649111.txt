532‐nm KTP Laser Treatment of Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor Inhibitor–Associated Paronychia: A Case Report
Authors: Emilee M. Dreifus, Aditi Chokshi, Ariel E. Eber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesParonychia is a common and often painful cutaneous adverse event of epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitor therapy that can impair function and quality of life. We report the first known case of amivantamab‐associated paronychia successfully treated with 532‐nm potassium‐titanyl‐phosphate laser.Materials and MethodsA 67‐year‐old man with metastatic non‐small cell lung cancer receiving amivantamab developed painful periungual inflammation with pyogenic granuloma‐like changes involving multiple fingers and both great toes after failing multiple topical therapies. A representative lesion on the left fourth digit was selected for treatment and underwent two sessions of 532‐nm KTP laser therapy. Treated and untreated digits were compared for clinical response.ResultsFollowing two treatment sessions, the treated digit demonstrated marked improvement in erythema, pain, and friability. Untreated digits remained inflamed and symptomatic, serving as a within‐patient comparator. No adverse events were observed.ConclusionGiven its superficial vascular targeting mechanism and broad clinical availability, the 532‐nm KTP laser may represent a practical and effective therapeutic option for refractory EGFRI‐associated paronychia. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of successful treatment of amivantamab‐associated paronychia with a 532‐nm KTP laser.

Introduction

Paronychia is one of the most common dermatologic adverse events associated with epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitor (EGFRI) therapy, typically presenting as painful periungual inflammation and granulation tissue [1]. Paronychia can lead to discomfort, functional impairment, and reduced quality of life, and in severe cases may necessitate decreasing the dose or interruption of oncologic therapy [1, 2]. Commonly used therapies, including topical corticosteroids, topical beta blockers, and topical or systemic antibiotics, often provide incomplete clinical improvement and do not adequately target the vascular features characteristic of these lesions. Among treatment options, pulsed dye laser (PDL) has been reported as an effective option for refractory cases [3].

However, access to PDL may be limited in some settings, and data regarding the use of alternative vascular lasers for this indication remain sparse. The 532‐nm potassium‐titanyl‐phosphate (KTP) laser similarly targets hemoglobin and is widely used for the treatment of vascular lesions. Given its established safety profile and vascular target, the KTP laser represents a plausible therapeutic option for EGFRI–associated paronychia. Here, we present a case of amivantamab‐associated paronychia successfully treated with the 532‐nm KTP laser following failure of topical therapies.

Case Report

A 67‐year‐old man with a history of stage IIB non–small cell lung cancer (adenocarcinoma of the left lower lobe; pT2bN1Mx) harboring an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) exon 20 insertion mutation experienced disease progression to stage IV with brain metastases. He was subsequently initiated on systemic therapy with the EGFRI amivantamab.

Following initiation of amivantamab, the patient developed painful periungual inflammation involving both great toes and multiple digits of the bilateral hands (Figure 1a). The affected areas demonstrated erythema, edema, and friable vascular overgrowth clinically consistent with pyogenic granulomas in the setting of EGFRI‐associated paronychia. Given the characteristic clinical presentation in the setting of amivantamab therapy and the patient's desire to avoid additional procedures, histopathologic confirmation was not pursued. The lesions were associated with tenderness and functional limitation, affecting hand use and driving abilities, significantly altering his quality of life. The patient reported no history of preceding trauma or infection.

Prior treatments included topical clobetasol ointment, topical timolol, fusidic acid, and betamethasone valerate, prescribed during travel in Europe, applied to the toes and periungual regions without clinical improvement. Given persistent symptoms and impact on quality of life, laser therapy was pursued.

The patient underwent two treatment sessions using a 532‐nm KTP laser targeting a representative lesion on the left fourth digit of the hand. During the first session, an initial pulse was delivered with a fluence of 6.5 J/cm2, pulse duration of 1.5 ms, and spot size of 8 mm. Additional pulses were administered to surrounding areas at a reduced fluence of 5.5 J/cm2 with the same pulse duration and spot size, resulting in transient purpura and duskiness. A second treatment session was performed 2 weeks later using a fluence of 6.5 J/cm2, pulse duration of 2.0 ms, and spot size of 8 mm. Following two sessions, the treated digit demonstrated marked improvement in erythema and pain, with decreased friability and improved patient‐reported comfort. All other affected fingers and toenails remained inflammatory and painful while the single digit that received 532‐nm KTP laser had marked improvement (Figure 1b). Of note, the patient was subsequently started on 20 mg of isotretinoin daily for EGFRI‐associated acneiform eruptions unresponsive to topical therapies after the onset of paronychia and periungual pyogenic granuloma formation. No adverse events were observed. At 30 weeks following the second treatment session, the treated digit remained improved with continued healing and no evidence of recurrence (Figure 2). In contrast, new periungual pyogenic granulomas continued to develop and progress on other untreated digits during follow‐up.

Discussion

Although EGFR‐targeted therapies have transformed the treatment landscape for multiple malignancies, their associated cutaneous adverse effects remain a significant clinical challenge. Among these, paronychia associated with EGFRI therapy can be debilitating, resulting in severe pain and functional limitations. Despite its prevalence, current treatment strategies are largely extrapolated from data on drug‐induced paronychia related to other systemic agents, including high‐dose systemic retinoid therapy and indinavir [4]. In addition, EGFRI frequently causes acneiform eruptions that may require systemic isotretinoin, which can induce or exacerbate paronychia, further highlighting the need for more targeted treatments [5]. Although isotretinoin may contribute to periungual granulation tissue formation, the lesions in our patient preceded isotretinoin therapy, making it less likely to have been the primary inciting factor.

Anticancer drug‐induced paronychia is thought to arise from the disruption of key signaling pathways, resulting in impaired keratinocyte proliferation and migration and compromised barrier integrity. This epithelial dysfunction is followed by chemokine‐mediated inflammation, aberrant angiogenic signaling, and the development of friable granulation tissue with vascular proliferation [3, 6]. These features suggest that EGFRI‐associated paronychia represents not only an inflammatory process, but also a vascular‐reactive disorder characterized by vascular congestion [3].

The KTP laser emits light at a 532‐nm wavelength, which is strongly absorbed by oxyhemoglobin and preferentially targets superficial vasculature. Compared with longer vascular wavelengths, such as 595 nm, the 532‐nm wavelength exhibits greater hemoglobin absorption at shallow tissue depths, resulting in more superficial vessel targeting [7]. This absorption profile may be particularly well suited for periungual vascular overgrowth, where inflammation predominantly involves superficial vessels with associated vascular congestion [3].

This report represents the first documented case of successful treatment of amivantamab‐associated paronychia with a 532‐nm KTP laser, expanding the spectrum of laser‐based interventions for this condition. While a prior study has demonstrated the efficacy of PDL therapy, access to PDL may be limited and not all dermatology practices have this modality readily available. In contrast to the PDL study, which involved 4 weekly treatment sessions, our patient achieved significant clinical improvement after only two treatments, with restoration of normal nail growth and sustained reduction in periungual inflammation despite continuation of amivantamab. This may suggest that fewer treatment sessions are sufficient to achieve a meaningful clinical response. Although prior studies have also shown efficacy with deeper penetrating 1064‐nm lasers, the more superficially targeting 532‐nm KTP wavelength may allow for selective targeting of superficial vascular changes and broaden the applicability of vascular laser therapy beyond a single device or wavelength [8]. Future comparative studies evaluating other 532‐nm laser platforms, including diode lasers, may help establish the most effective approach for this condition. Notably, our patient's untreated digits remain inflamed, reinforcing the efficacy of the KTP laser.

In addition, laser therapy offers several practical advantages for oncology patients. It is noninvasive, associated with minimal downtime, and can be performed without interruption of systemic cancer therapy. In this case, treatment was well tolerated and resulted in marked improvement in pain and periungual inflammation, translating into substantial improvement in patient‐reported quality of life. Although no recurrence was observed at 30 weeks of follow‐up, longer‐term observation is needed to better characterize recurrence rates. While this single case does not establish a standard of care, it contributes insight into the broader feasibility and potential utility of vascular laser strategies beyond PDL alone. Prospective comparative studies evaluating different wavelengths, pulse duration parameters, and lesion severities will be needed to refine parameters and establish guidelines for laser therapy for this condition.

Conclusion

Our case establishes the 532‐nm KTP laser as a novel, non‐invasive, and safe treatment option for EGFRI‐associated paronychia. As the first reported case of this wavelength in the setting of amivantamab therapy, this report adds to the emerging evidence that targeting the vascular component of this drug‐related side effect can lead to improved quality of life and clinical improvement.
