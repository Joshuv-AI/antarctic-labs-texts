“I am struggling to find an explicit task”—a midterm evaluation of patient research partner involvement in HIPPOCRATES translational research
Authors: Maarten de Wit, Oliver FitzGerald, Anne Barton, Heidi Bertheussen, Laura C. Coates, Owen Davies, Christine Huppertz, David Trigos, Michaela Köhm, Sina Mackay, Denis O´Sullivan, Stefan Siebert, Wendy Wagenaar, Stephen R. Pennington, Diana Rodrigues, Katie I. Gallacher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe HIPPOCRATES project is a 5-year, international research partnership dedicated to studying and treating psoriatic disease. Integrating Patient Research Partners (PRPs) into large scientific groups, particularly those heavily focused on preclinical or laboratory-based science, is challenging. This paper details a midterm evaluation of how effectively PRPs have been engaged in the HIPPOCRATES project.MethodsOur evaluation used a mixed-method approach comprising 7 dedicated reflective PRP meetings, 2 midterm surveys among PRPs and researchers, a modified World Café discussion, and 5 structured dialogue meetings. We used descriptive statistics and pragmatic constant comparative analysis.ResultsSubstantial PRP input occurred early during the project’s presubmission phase, which resulted in a formal strategy for patient involvement. However, 3 years after approval, significant differences emerged between researcher and PRP perspectives. Compared with researchers, PRPs reported more challenges, had differing expectations, and perceived a lower overall impact. Although PRPs were highly motivated at the start and contributed significantly to clinical components, motivation decreased for some regarding the laboratory components. This was attributed to highly technical language, long contract negotiations, and a lack of clear opportunities for input or tangible results. Consortium members collaboratively agreed upon a set of specific changes during the 5 dialogue sessions.ConclusionsDespite the early and substantial involvement of PRPs in the initial study design, the PRPs themselves rated their influence on HIPPOCRATES lower than the researchers did. Further evaluation over the next 2 years will show whether the jointly developed solutions successfully enhance their impact on future research outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Patient and public involvement (PPI) in rheumatology and musculoskeletal diseases (RMD) research has become the norm, and much research has been conducted to evaluate its benefits in clinical research [1,2]. Although there is compelling evidence for the benefits of PPI, a paucity of knowledge exists of the processes and effects of such involvement in the context of basic science and translational research [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. In addition, there is a limited body of knowledge on researcher-patient research partner (PRP) collaboration in international, large-scale research initiatives [7]. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a responsive evaluation of PRP involvement in HIPPOCRATES, a public-private partnership funded by the Innovative Health Initiative (IHI) for the period 2021–2026 (GA No. 101007757).

The HIPPOCRATES consortium comprises 27 academic and industry partners with a strong desire to identify and validate predictive biomarkers in psoriatic disease, a condition that covers both skin psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis (PsA) [8]. PRP involvement has been an integral part of HIPPOCRATES since the project design stage. Three patient organisations were invited as partners: European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology – People with Arthritis/Rheumatism in Europe (EULAR PARE), European Federation of Psoriasis Patients’ Associations(EUROPSO), and Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis-EU office (GRAPPA-EU). They nominated 13 PRPs to get involved. The HIPPOCRATES project followed the EULAR recommendations for PRP involvement in rheumatology research [9].

Two PRPs took part in early conversations in 2017 about a potential IHI (then IMI) application [10]. Four PRPs joined the first preapplication stage consortium meeting held in Amsterdam (2019) and subsequently in remote (on-line) meetings necessitated by the Covid-19 pandemic. The PRPs reviewed the research priorities and developed an ambitious PRP strategy (Fig 1). A decision was made to recruit at least 2 PRPs for each work package (WP). Initially, 1 PRP joined the HIPPOCRATES executive team (HET). All PRPs together formed the Patient Advisory Council (PAC). It is notable that during the final evaluation of the project proposal, the IHI grant reviewers highlighted and praised the PRP involvement strategy: ‘The European psoriasis patient organisation has representatives that will take part in the proposed project, and this is a very positive aspect.’ In addition, the reviewers noticed: ‘A strength of this proposal is that patient representative organisations seem highly integrated in the proposal and will be important avenues for dissemination’.

Since previous studies of public-private partnerships have highlighted several challenges of PPI engagement [7,11,12], monitoring and evaluation of the process and impact of PPI is necessary [13]. We planned a responsive evaluation of PRP involvement in HIPPOCRATES with the aim of exploring the perceptions, challenges, and experiences of the researchers and PRPs regarding PRP involvement during the first 3 years of the project. Because much of HIPPOCRATES’ research covers the evaluation and validation of candidate biomarkers, we focused in particular on the PRP involvement in translational research activities. This was where we encountered the greatest challenges and hence could learn the most.

METHODS

This responsive evaluation [14,15] followed an emergent longitudinal research design, employing mixed methods [16,17]. The study was coled by EULAR and the University of Glasgow and received ethical approval from the Glasgow MVLS College Ethics Committee (project no: 200230447). Data were collected through surveys, reflective PRP meetings, 1 modified World Café session, and 5 dialogue meetings (Fig 2). The mixed methods approach allowed us to triangulate data from different sources and across multiple time points [18].Figure 2Flowchart Midterm evaluation. Surveys

Early in 2022, PRPs completed a short questionnaire, asking about their experiences of receiving invitations to WP meetings and HIPPOCRATES emails, and whether they felt included in the project. In 2024, 2 comprehensive, mirrored EULAR questionnaires, based on the Public and Patient Engagement Evaluation Tool [19], were sent to PRPs (39 questions, of which 9 were open) and researchers (38 questions, 9 open) to collect data regarding their experiences and perceptions of PRP involvement in HIPPOCRATES during the first half of the project (Supplementary material 1 and 2). Survey data were subjected to descriptive statistical analyses.

Reflective meetings

Regular reflective PRP meetings (n = 7) were held to enable PRPs to share their views on what was promoting or inhibiting effective PRP contributions during online WP meetings and the in-person annual plenary meetings (APMs). Meetings were recorded and transcribed.

World Café session

At the start of the second HIPPOCRATES APM (2023, Frankfurt), a short ‘modified World Café session’ [20] was held to generate ideas and feedback on the challenges of PRP involvement in the WPs. Eight groups were formed, comprising 7 participants of whom at least 1 was a PRP. The groups addressed questions on whether HIPPOCRATES was effectively addressing the PRP challenges in engaging with the WPs and how PRP involvement was influencing HIPPOCRATES research.

Dialogue meetings

In the first quarter of 2025, the PAC leadership expressed concerns about the level of engagement with PRPs, which sparked the initiation of 5 dialogue sessions for PRPs and non-PRP consortium members. The objective was to learn about the research progress in the 5 WPs, discuss expectations of PRP involvement in this research, and plan potential PRP contributions until the end of the project.

An overview of reports of recorded meetings that were included in the qualitative analyses is provided in Supplementary material 3. A member check of all meeting reports took place after each meeting. The study's findings were regularly discussed in detail with stakeholders representing various perspectives, including the PAC, Steering Committee, HET, and the full WP7 team. The core group (MW, DR, and KG) met regularly to interpret findings, applying an approach of pragmatic constant comparative analysis [21,22] with a focus on iteratively formulating actionable insights. Triangulation was used both to verify findings (by comparing survey results and document analysis) and to enrich data collection by synthesising study findings with feedback from consortium members, based on their memories and real-world experiences. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion within the core group.

RESULTS

Here, we provide a report, in chronological order, of the expectations, challenges and experiences of PRPs and other consortium members during the first 3 years of HIPPOCRATES (2021–2024). We also report the changes in strategy that the HET agreed to implement during the remainder of the period (2024-2027), a decision that was ratified by the whole consortium.

Successful PRP involvement at the start

After successful involvement of PRPs during the presubmission stage (n = 4 PRPs) and at the 2 kick-off meetings (n = 12 PRPs and 11, respectively), the participation in WPs was initially well sustained. Despite the COVID-19 pandemic, PRPs stayed active in the project and developed a PRP role description (Supplementary material 4) agreed upon at their first PAC meeting, and subsequently presented at the first APM (Basel, 2022). At the end of the first year, a short 3-item survey showed that 9 out of 10 PRPs confirmed being invited to their assigned WP meetings and that they felt well included. Seven out of 10 confirmed receiving regular emails related to their WP(s), whereas 3 were unsure. At the second reflective PRP meeting, it was reported that PRP attendance at online WP meetings had been good; however, some PRPs reported that not many PRPs had spoken during these meetings.

Active involvement in clinical studies

At the World Café session during the second APM (Frankfurt, 2023), consortium members were positive about PRP involvement and reported many benefits (Supplementary material 5). They were particularly positive about PRP involvement in the HIPPOCRATES Prospective Observational Study (HPOS), an ambitious patient-driven online cohort study, with the aim of identifying risk factors for the development of PsA in people with psoriasis. Similarly, at one of the reflective PRP meetings at the end of 2023, it was clear that several PRPs had contributed significantly to the design of HPOS. They attended codesign sessions and focus groups, actively discussed the number of questionnaires to prevent overburdening of participants, and suggested adding some patient-reported outcomes (PROs) only when respondents passed a particular threshold. For instance, only participants with a high score on the Psoriatic Arthritis Impact of Disease score-item fatigue would be invited to fill in an optional fatigue questionnaire [23]. The PRPs also assisted in the development of a single-item question to assess itching. Finally, they reviewed the layout of the online platform and highlighted the need for participants to receive feedback on their answers. This resulted in an attractive dashboard where PRO scores were displayed in colourful figures. At a later stage, some PRPs became actively involved in translating patient-facing materials and developing recruitment strategies as HPOS enrolment expanded to individual EU countries.

Workshop on treatment response

Another successful clinical initiative was the setup of a workshop on treatment response from the perspective of patients, organised by WP4 leadership at the end of the second year. It was well attended by 7 PRPs and made clear what outcomes matter to patients when analysing data from omics studies as carried out in WPs 1-5 (Table 1). Decreased participation in WP sessions

Although most PRPs did continue to participate actively in the APMs, for some, the motivation to attend online WP meetings decreased over time (Fig 3). During reflective PRP meetings, they reported ‘being dropped from e-mail lists’ and receiving less frequent email invitations to online meetings. Attendance at meetings was unmonitored, therefore no follow-up contact was made after nonattendance. A difference in engagement was noticed between PRPs recruited through the PRP networks of EULAR and GRAPPA-EU and the PRPs recruited from the EUROPSO board. EUROPSO PRPs reported feeling positive about their involvement and contributions, although the frequency of attendance at WP meetings was limited. This prompted the consortium leadership to discuss potential reasons for the lower involvement of EUROPSO at the International Federation of Psoriasis Associations (IFPA) World Congress (2024, Stockholm) and explore solutions. EUROPSO confirmed that they saw a role in assisting in the recruitment of participants for the HPOS study. Although EUROPSO was not a beneficiary partner in the consortium, GRAPPA-EU offered funding to EUROPSO PRPs to attend the APM, which has been used to a limited extent. It is unknown whether the lack of a dedicated budget might explain the difference in involvement and expectations compared with EULAR and GRAPPA-EU PRPs.Figure 3Overview of PRP participation numbers in online and in-person meetings. A minority of PRPs continued to engage in the research as the project progressed. Some PRPs reported that their participation was hampered by the technical language and complex nature of laboratory research, and the lack of opportunities for meaningful input: 1 reported, ‘I am struggling to find an explicit task’. This might explain why some PRPs kept silent during online meetings. Previous suggestions to try to address these challenges, such as providing lay summaries and sharing the agenda and the presentation slides ahead of WP meetings, were inconsistently implemented.

In addition, not all PRPs appreciated the consortium achieving a major success by getting all partners to sign up to a data sharing agreement. They wondered why these negotiations take so much time, causing significant delays in tangible results. Not being able to share any results with their communities made PRPs feel that they were not having an impact on the research. A discussion took place about potentially supplementing or replacing PRP participation in separate WPs with regular plenary PAC meetings with patient-relevant research items on the agenda. Since the group expected that more opportunities for input would appear soon after completion of the data sharing agreement, they decided not to change the PRP involvement strategy.

Divergent perspectives

The questionnaires sent out to PRPs and other consortium members at the end of the third year revealed significant differences in experiences (Table 2). Researchers found PRP involvement worthwhile, although time consuming and at times difficult due to the knowledge gap and the variability in engagement. Despite an online webinar on the topic of collaborative research, available on the consortium’s platform, 44% of the researchers indicated that they did not have access to training or knowledge about PRP involvement when needed. PRPs reported more challenges with the collaboration than researchers (58% vs 28%). When asked whether challenges were resolved satisfactorily, 25% of PRPs answered in the affirmative vs 44% of the researchers. Although 83% of the PRPs reported that the project was worth the time spent, half (50%) reported that their engagement in HIPPOCRATES was less than expected, vs 20% of the researchers.

When asked whether they were satisfied with the PRP initiative in HIPPOCRATES, almost all researchers answered ‘yes’ (96%) vs 75% of PRPs. When asked whether PRPs had made a difference to the HIPPOCRATES initiative, again 96% of researchers and 75% of PRPs answered ‘yes’.

During a reflective PRP meeting at the end of the third year, it became apparent that PRPs were rarely involved as coauthors of peer-reviewed publications. A review of publications on the HIPPOCRATES website revealed that only 2 out of 35 papers included 1 or 2 PRPs as coauthors.

Dialogue meetings

Following discussions around the concerns of PRPs, the HET, together with the PAC leadership, organised 5 dialogue meetings between PRPs and WP team members. Deliberative dialogue is a participatory research methodology wherein stakeholders with diverse backgrounds, experiences, and interests come together to engage in discussions to build consensus for collaborative decision-making [24]. The aim of these meetings was to learn about the current state of the research, discuss expectations of PRP involvement in the research, and align on suggestions to expand the opportunities for PRPs to actively contribute to the remainder of the HIPPOCRATES project. These meetings were well attended with an average of 13 participants (5 PRPs and 8 researchers, including several young investigators). The continued commitment to PRP involvement of the leadership was demonstrated by the project coleads (SRP and OF) attending all 5 dialogue meetings.

The meetings resulted in a set of changes in the PRP involvement strategy. Two PRPs were added to the working group that plans and prepares the APM. A second PRP was added to the HET and WP leads were encouraged to involve PRPs at an early stage of the planning of publications. The focus on PRP participation in separate WP meetings shifted towards the organisation of PAC meetings, with presentations in plain language by WP leads to create more opportunities for PRPs to ask questions and give feedback. One PRP with a background in laboratory research preferred to continue participation in the online meetings of several WPs. For a full summary of outcomes from the dialogue meetings, see Supplementary material 7.

Realistic expectations

The WP presentations about research progress during the dialogue meetings created more realistic expectations among the PRPs. Participants agreed that, due to the COVID-19 pandemic early in the life of the project, they had not been able to meet in person to exchange, discuss, and address mutual expectations. The potential of a 1-year no-cost extension of HIPPOCRATES into 2027 supported the belief that more results were soon to be expected. The PRPs had accepted that their role in most WPs during the first 3 years had been limited due to discussions around identifying cohorts and negotiations on data sharing and contracts. Towards the end of the project, they expected that their contribution could become valuable in the interpretation of findings and dissemination of results. There was a willingness to ensure that PRP perspectives were included in publications and in other initiatives related to the sustainability of the project after its completion.

Triangulation

To increase the validity of our study, we triangulated the findings originating from various sources to cross-check data from different angles for initial trends and emerging patterns. The response shift in experiences of PRPs, expressed during reflective PRP meetings, was confirmed by the results of the survey. However, the perspectives of the researchers did not change and were positive from the start and throughout the first 3 years. Both the World Café session and the survey revealed great satisfaction with the involvement of PRPs and the contributions they provided.

Triangulation of findings supported concerns regarding a gradual decline in PRP attendance at WP meetings, a shortfall in PRP authorship on papers, and overall limited outputs with meaning for patients. This was not the case, as noted previously, for the HPOS study, for which PRPs provided substantial input into the development of the online platform and recruitment of study participants. Also, the workshop on defining treatment response from a patient perspective resulted in valuable insights. Nevertheless, the concerns of PRPs prompted the IHI reviewers to make a request in their midterm review report (March 2024) for the project to ‘increase the participation of the patient community; although actively engaged thus far, the patient community could potentially participate more proactively and deepen its role in the project’.

The reflective PRP meetings enhanced the bonding between PRPs and offered an opportunity to exchange mutual experiences. Many lessons learned were shared and reported (Table 3). However, the meetings were less effective in optimising PRP involvement since the lessons learned and recommendations did not lead to sustainable improvements. For instance, the PRP role description developed at the start of the project was not fully implemented across all WPs, and therefore divergent views emerged on the tasks and responsibilities of PRPs. Moreover, the absence of clearly defined objectives and an agreed role description early in the research process resulted in PRPs and researchers feeling lost. DISCUSSION

This evaluation reports the experiences and challenges of PRP-researcher collaboration during the first 3 years of HIPPOCRATES. We learned that PRP involvement right from the start and the full support of the project leadership alone cannot prevent serious challenges regarding meaningful participation of PRPs, particularly in basic and translational research. The COVID pandemic interfered with early HIPPOCRATES in-person meetings, limiting opportunities to get to know each other and to discuss mutual expectations of the years ahead.

We have found that the added value of PRP involvement in clinical endeavours is evident but is currently limited in translational research. Concrete, well-defined opportunities to influence translational research in HIPPOCRATES were scarce during the first half of the project, although a more prominent role for PRPs is expected when more results become available. Managing expectations in basic science research [25], especially during large international projects [26], is therefore key.

Building sustainable partnerships requires time, commitment, and respectful communication [27]. Our study confirmed many challenges reported by other RMD consortia [7,11,12] relating to communication between researchers and PRPs. These reports emphasise the need for the use of nontechnical language, management of expectations, and the benefits of a PRP coordinator. One study recommended including psychosocial research in lab-based focused research initiatives, facilitating more meaningful opportunities for PRPs to contribute to all aspects of the research [7]. In HIPPOCRATES, this was difficult as a large part of the data came from retrospective studies that did not collect psychosocial information.

If PPI is not carefully done, there is a risk of tokenistic involvement of PRPs, leading to significant differences in perceptions and experiences [27,28]. Although we report critical experiences of PRPs, the overall satisfaction with the PRP initiative is relatively high, despite the different ratings of PRPs and researchers (75% and 96%, respectively). Varying expectations at the start of HIPPOCRATES and unequal understanding of the PRP role may explain these different levels of satisfaction. Also, the variety of experiences and competencies between different consortium partners caused diverging perspectives. For instance, PRPs were nominated by patient organisations with different cultures, attitudes, and affinity to research, and not all PRPs had received equal training and support. We hypothesise that the clinical researchers were satisfied with the valuable input from experienced PRPs, while the laboratory-based researchers might have had lower expectations and were therefore satisfied with any kind of PRP involvement.

Another important lesson learned was that taking personal and professional background and interests of PRPs into account when allocating PRPs to WPs can aid more meaningful involvement. For instance, a PRP with professional knowledge and experience in medical laboratory work became a highly valued PRP in several basic science WPs, in which she felt confident and able to contribute. Although this kind of knowledge may benefit some PRPs in terms of how well they are able to contribute to areas of basic and translational science, it does not mean that PRPs without this background are unable to make valued contributions in these areas. Diversity increases the likelihood of a project achieving its goals. The great value of PRPs participating in a project is that they provide their different lived experiences of the disease to the project and to those working on it. Adopting an inclusive approach, including researchers explaining their studies in understandable language, will enable PRPs to provide that meaningful input.

Strengths of this study are the longitudinal data collection and an emergent research design, allowing us to triangulate findings from different sources and to compare data from different stages in the project. The high response to the 2 questionnaires and the number of PRPs and researchers in the World Café and dialogue sessions enhances the validity of our findings, although we did not explore the nonresponse.

A limitation of this evaluation study is that our data collection did not start from the beginning and was not linked to any milestone or delivery. For EULAR, HIPPOCRATES was the first experience of participating in a research consortium as a beneficiary partner with responsibilities for organising the PRP involvement and disseminating results to patient audiences and the public. One staff member was responsible for coordinating and supporting the PAC, but with limited knowledge or experience of PRP involvement in a primarily translational research initiative such as HIPPOCRATES. It is unknown to what extent PRP coordinators should have this kind of scientific knowledge. The PRP coordinator is responsible for adequate communication between PRPs and researchers, matching PRPs with WPs and ensuring continuity and sustainable partnerships. However, the EULAR recommendations for PRP involvement warn that the presence of a PRP coordinator should never replace the responsibility of the research team to ensure adequate support for PRPs or the direct dialogue between researchers and PRPs [9].

New consortia in which EULAR participated benefitted from the lessons learned in HIPPOCRATES. They received higher budgets dedicated to coordinating and facilitating patient involvement, and more prominence was given to the role of PRP coordinators with the update of the EULAR PRP recommendations [9]. When the concerns of the PRPs became manifest, the concept of dialogue meetings was introduced as an alternative to the reflective PRP meetings. The structured dialogue meetings in lay language, bringing PRPs and researchers together, seemed an effective way to collectively evaluate and discuss the concerns raised by PRPs.

This study is part of a longitudinal responsive evaluation. To this end, focus group meetings with PRPs and researchers at the fourth APM (2025, Amsterdam) took place with the purpose of gaining more in-depth knowledge on some of the issues identified through the survey. A second round of surveys and additional interviews or focus groups at the end of the HIPPOCRATES project should demonstrate whether the dialogue sessions have resulted in re-engagement of the PRPs.
