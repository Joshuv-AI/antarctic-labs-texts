High-risk human papillomavirus genotypes and associated clinical pathological factors among patients with oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma in north-western Tanzania
Authors: Oscar Ottoman, Fidelis David, Michael James, Caroline Arnold Minja, Crispin Mukerebe, Benson R. Kidenya, Peter F. Rambau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) is a significant global health concern, with incidence rising as the prevalence of high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) increases. Understanding HPV genotypes and their associated clinicopathological factors is essential to improving OPSCC diagnosis and management, particularly in resource-limited settings. This study investigated the prevalence of high-risk HPV genotypes and associated factors in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks from patients with OPSCC at Bugando Medical Centre, Tanzania.MethodsA total of 85 FFPE tissue blocks from histologically confirmed OPSCC cases, collected between July 2023 and January 2025, were analysed. Genomic DNA was extracted, and high-risk HPV genotypes were detected using the Anyplex™ II HPV28 real-time polymerase chain reaction assay. Demographic, behavioural, and clinicopathological data were collected via structured questionnaires and analysed using Stata version 15.ResultsThe mean age of patients was 55.3 ± 17.6 years (range, 24–92 years), with a slight male predominance (57.6%). Most participants were unaware of HPV’s role in the causation of OPSCC, 65/85 (77.3%). HPV DNA was detected in 38/85 (44.7%) of cases, with HPV-16 the most prevalent, 27/85 (31.8%), followed by HPV-33, 8/85 (9.4%), and HPV-18, 7/85 (8.2%). HPV-positive tumours were significantly associated with favourable histopathological features, including a pushing border, less than 50% keratinisation, absence of perineural invasion, and a dense population of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (p < 0.001). Additionally, HPV-positive OPSCC was associated with better prognostic indicators, including lower rates of nodal involvement.ConclusionHigh-risk HPV plays a critical role in OPSCC. HPV-positive tumours are associated with favourable histopathological and prognostic features, supporting the clinical value of routine HPV testing for prognostic stratification and patient management. These findings also underscore the need for greater public awareness, expanded HPV vaccination programmes, and strengthened preventive strategies to reduce the burden of HPV-associated OPSCC.

Introduction

Oropharyngeal cancers occur in the tonsils, lips, base of the tongue, soft palate, and posterior pharyngeal wall. Like other head and neck cancers (HNSCC), over 95% of oropharyngeal cancers originate from squamous cells in the mucosal epithelium and are classified as oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) [1, 2]. Globally, OPSCC incidence varies, with an increase reported in developed countries such as the United States since 1973, particularly among males and younger populations [3]. In sub-Saharan Africa, OPSCC remains a significant health concern, exhibiting unique epidemiological patterns compared to other regions. One study done in a tertiary care centre in Sub-Saharan Africa reported the prevalence of HPV on OPSCC to be 5% [4]. A study conducted at BMC in Tanzania on HNSCC revealed that oropharyngeal cancers were the most common head and neck malignancy encountered, with a prevalence of 55.6%, and squamous cell carcinoma was the common histological type [5].

Historically, OPSCC has been associated with tobacco and alcohol consumption [3]. However, growing evidence suggests that high-risk HPV plays a crucial role in the increasing incidence of OPSCC [6, 7]. The incidence of OPSCC has risen markedly, primarily due to HPV infections, while cases linked to tobacco use have declined [8]. This increase is particularly observed among middle-aged white men with oral sexual behaviour, identified as a significant risk factor [2, 8]. HPV-positive OPSCC represents a distinct subset of head and neck cancers, characterised by unique epidemiological, clinical, and molecular features that differentiate it from HPV-negative cases [2].

Regarding HPV-related OPSCC, its incidence in developed countries has risen from 16% to 70%, with projections indicating that it could surpass cervical cancer cases in the United Kingdom by the end of 2016. In contrast, the incidence of HPV-negative OPSCC has declined dramatically [9, 10]. Several studies have reported that among head and neck cancers, HPV-induced tumorigenesis predominantly affects the oropharynx. In contrast, the fraction of HPV-positive cancers remains markedly lower at non-oropharyngeal subsites [11, 12]. Moreover, HPV-positive OPSCC is more common among white males and non-smokers [12]. Although an increasing trend has been observed among women and non-white populations [13]. Studies also suggest that patients with HPV-positive OPSCC who have a history of tobacco exposure are at a higher risk of recurrence [14]. Exposure to high-risk HPV can lead to both cutaneous and mucosal cancers, including skin carcinomas [15]. The mucosal subtype, particularly HPV genotypes 16 and 18, is a known risk factor for various cancers, including cervical cancer, OPSCC, and penile carcinoma [15]. OPSCC is notably prevalent in high-risk regions [16–18]. East Africa has the highest estimated HPV infection prevalence rates globally (33.6%). However, the exact prevalence of HPV in the general Tanzanian population remains unclear [19].

In Tanzania, HPV screening and detection programs primarily focus on cervical cancer in young schoolgirls and HIV-positive individuals, who are at a higher risk of developing cervical carcinoma [20]. Routine HPV DNA detection in mucosal lesions is not well established. However, molecular detection methods have been employed in cervical carcinoma screening programs due to the high disease prevalence in the country [20]. Similarly, in OPSCC, the probability of detecting HPV DNA in tissue samples is high in individuals aged under 55 years [21, 22]. The role of HPV as a risk factor for oropharyngeal carcinoma in Tanzania remains poorly defined. Current HPV preventive efforts are largely focused on females to prevent cervical cancer, leaving a gap in preventive strategies for males. Therefore, this study aims to assess the prevalence of high-risk HPV genotypes in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks from OPSCC cases in Mwanza, Tanzania.

Materials and methods

Study population, study area, and inclusion criteria

This was a hospital-based study involving 85 cases of oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC). The study was conducted between July 2023 and January 2025 and investigated samples collected over six years, from January 2017 to December 2024. The FFPE tissue blocks for patients diagnosed with oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma were retrieved from the histopathology laboratory of Bugando Medical Centre (BMC), a tertiary referral hospital in Northwestern Tanzania. Demographic, clinical, and associated factors were retrieved from medical records and the oncology clinic during patient follow-up, and a scheduled interview was conducted for those who were missing important clinical information. Laboratory procedures were conducted at the Histopathology Laboratory of BMC, the Molecular Biology Laboratory of the Catholic University of Health and Allied Sciences (CUHAS), and the National Institute of Medical Research Laboratory in Mwanza. Only blocks which were double-verified by pathologists and had complete demographic and clinical information were included. Ethical clearance was granted by the joint CUHAS/BMC Research and Ethics Review Committee (NO. 633/2022) and the Lake Zone Institutional Review Board (NO: MR/53/100/727).

Laboratory procedures

Histopathological procedures

The retrieved tissue blocks were cut into thin sections of 2–3 µ using a Rotary Microtome. The tissues were then stained with routine Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E). Deparaffinization was performed in a hot-air oven at 60 °C, and the slides were then placed in two xylene containers for 10 min each. This was followed by rehydration, during which the tissue slides were dipped in a series of absolute ethanol (99.8%) and 95% ethanol for 10 min, then in clean tap water for 5 min. Then the tissue slides were immersed in Hematoxylin for 10 min, and then blued in clean running tap water for 5 min. The section was immersed in an eosin stain solution for 5 min, then dehydrated in 95% and 99.8% ethanol for 10 min each. After this, the tissue slides were immersed in xylene 10 times in two containers to clear the ethanol [23]. Lastly, the slides were mounted in Dibutyl phthalate Polystyrene Xylene (DPX) for histological evaluation by two registered pathologists who were blinded to the HPV status of the tissue samples. An Olympus CX21 light microscope was used to review OPSCC diagnosis, its subtypes, and to establish histological prognostic factors [24–26]. All images of Histomorphological prognostic factors were obtained using a digital microscope camera with a 10MP CMOS sensor and a resolution of 3648 × 2736.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from an FFPE tissue section using the adopted cost-effective manual DNA extraction protocol based on the salt-out method [27, 28]. This method involves 4 steps: removing paraffin from tissue sections; lysing cells with proteinase K; precipitating proteins and removing contaminants by salting out; and finally recovering DNA by alcohol precipitation [27, 28]. Deparaffinization was carried out by adding 1 ml of xylene to the microtubes containing tissue sections, vortexing for 15 s, then spinning at 14,000 rpm for 3 washes. Two washes in absolute ethanol were performed to remove xylene; the pellet was then vortexed and centrifuged for 10 min at 14,000 rpm, and finally dried in air for 5 min. Tissues were dried at 55 °C and digested overnight at 55 °C in 100 µl of TEN buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 20 mM NaCl) containing 20 mg/ml proteinase K, followed by Proteinase K inactivation at 95 °C for 10 min to complete tissue digestion. Finally, undigested tissue remnants were pelleted by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant containing extracted DNA was transferred to a new microcentrifuge tube [27, 28, 29]. DNA quantity and concentration were assessed with a Qubit meter, followed by quality analysis using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, with genomic DNA extracted from oropharyngeal tissue samples (see Fig. 1A) and PCR amplification of HPV DNA produced distinct bands at expected sizes for consensus (~ 450 bp) and genotype-specific primers (HPV-16 at ~ 216 bp and HPV-18 at ~ 390 bp), confirming the presence of high-risk HPV types, with HPV-16 and HPV-18 prominently detected. Multiple bands in some lanes indicated co-infection with more than one genotype (See Fig. 1B) [28, 30].

PCR amplification

High-risk HPV genotypes were identified using the Real-Time PCR Anyplex™ II HPV28 PCR System on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Dx real-time PCR platform (manufactured in Singapore; serial number 787BR19205). The assay is designed to detect human papillomavirus DNA, including 19 high-risk HPV genotypes (16, 18, 26, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 69, 73, and 82) and nine low-risk genotypes (6, 11, 40, 42, 43, 44, 54, 61, and 70). In this study, the system was specifically utilised to detect high-risk HPV genotypes [31].

The kit included three known positive control tubes (5 µL each) containing HPV28 PC1, PC2, and PC3. Additionally, 5 µL of RNase-free water was used as a negative control for all 85 cases tested for high-risk HPV genotypes. For PCR reaction preparation, the Anyplex™ II HPV Master Mix was used. This mix includes multiplex PCR primers targeting 28 HPV types, a universal internal control (UIC) to monitor PCR performance, and real-time PCR enzyme mixes for amplification and detection. The PCR thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, PCR amplification (50 cycles), including denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing & extension at 60 °C for 1 min, and a final extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Detection and interpretation were performed using Tagging Oligonucleotide Cleavage & Extension (TOCE) technology for genotyping. Data analysis was conducted using a closed, automated system in the Seegene Viewer Software, which provides the presence or absence of 28 high-risk genotypes for each tissue sample. (see Fig. 2). The internal control had to be detected to ensure the validity of the results [31, 32].

Data analysis

All information obtained was recorded in Microsoft Excel. The data were cleaned and exported to STATA software version 15 for analysis. Frequency distribution tables and bar plots were generated to determine the prevalence and distribution of high-risk HPV genotypes. To identify factors associated with the acquisition of high-risk HPV genotypes, we used Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s Exact test, as appropriate. We employed Pearson’s Chi2 test to assess the significance of differences in distribution across clinical risk factors, sociodemographic factors, and histological prognostic factors between the two groups: high-risk HPV-positive and high-risk HPV-negative OPSCC. In all analyses, the significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The mean age of the patients whose samples were analysed was 55.3 ± 17.6 years, and 48 of 85 (56.5%) samples were obtained from patients aged below 60 years. The age range spanned from 24 to 92 years. The majority of the FFPE blocks, 49 out of 85 (57.6%), were from male patients, while 34 out of 85 (40.0%) were from individuals residing in the Mwanza region. Additionally, most patients in this study, 65 out of 85 (77.3%), were unaware that HPV could be a causative factor for OPSCC (Table 1).

Patient characteristics among 85 adults with OPSCC

In this study, the minority of participants had a history of smoking (25/85, 29.4%), and 45 out of 85 participants (52.9%) reported consuming alcohol daily. Regarding HIV status, 26 out of 85 participants (30.6%) tested positive, 37 out of 85 participants (43.5%) tested negative, while the status of 22 out of 85 (25.8%) remained unknown. Additionally, 39 out of 85 cases (45.8%) reported engaging in regular oral sex, with the majority (34/39, 87.1%) engaging in the practice more than once per week. Only 17 out of 85 participants (17.6%) reported a previous history of oral wart lesions. The most commonly reported anatomical site of OPSCC was the soft palate extending into the oral cavity, accounting for 28 out of 85 cases (32.9%), then followed by the posterior 1/3 base of the Tongue alone, accounts for 19 cases (22.4%), while the least one is from posterior pharyngeal wall of the oropharynx account 5 cases (5.9%) (Table 3).

The majority of the cases (46/85, 54.1%) were diagnosed with invasive squamous cell carcinoma, keratinising type, with grade I (46/85, 54.1%) (Table 2). Regarding tumour stage, most cases (23/85, 27.0%) were stage III (Table 3). The majority of cases (40/85, 47.0%) had complete involvement of the surgical resection margin (Fig. 3A). The degree of keratinisation was greater than 50% in 43 of 85 OPSCC cases (50.5%) (Fig. 3C). Additionally, 42 out of 85 cases (49.6%) of OPSCC did not report nerve invasion. Other histological prognostic factors, including host immune status, nodal status, and invasion pattern, were assessed and documented (Table 2).

The prevalence of high-risk HPV DNA and the pattern of its distribution among FFPE tissue blocks with oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma

In this study, 38 of 85 (44.7%) FFPE OPSCC tissue blocks tested positive for high-risk HPV DNA. Genotype-specific prevalence analysis showed that HPV16 was the most frequently detected genotype, occurring in 27 cases (31.8%) (Table 3). Other detected HR-HPV genotypes included HPV33 in 8 cases (9.4%), HPV18 in 7 cases (8.2%), HPV35 in 4 cases (4.7%), and HPV73 in 3 cases (3.5%) (Table 3; Fig. 4). Of the HPV-positive cases, 27 (71.1%) harboured a single HR-HPV genotype, whereas 11 (28.9%) exhibited multiple HR-HPV infections. HPV16 remained the dominant genotype regardless of infection pattern, accounting for the largest proportion of HPV-associated OPSCC cases (Table 3).

Discussion

Human papillomaviruses (HPV) are the first viruses recognised to induce carcinogenesis. They are associated with cancers of the uterine cervix, anogenital tumours, and head and neck malignancies [33]. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of high-risk HPV genotypes in oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) and their associated clinicopathological factors by comparing HPV-positive and HPV-negative OPSCC cases. Our study identified high-risk HPV DNA in 44.7% of oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) cases, with Human papillomavirus type 16 being the most prevalent genotype in OPSCC in north-western Tanzania. This finding is consistent with global epidemiological data that recognise HPV 16 as the dominant genotype in OPSCC [34].

HPV16 was the predominant genotype, followed by HPV33, HPV18, HPV35 and HPV73. The predominance of HPV16 is consistent with its established role as the principal oncogenic genotype worldwide, responsible for the overwhelming majority of HPV-attributable head and neck cancers globally [35]. The observed 44.7% prevalence falls within the broader African pattern of marked geographic variability. Recent analyses report HR-HPV attribution of approximately 56% of OPSCC cases in North America, around 39–52% across Western Europe, Japan and Australia, but only about 20% across sub-Saharan Africa, with some South African and Nigerian series reporting detection rates of 0–5% [36]. More closely aligned with the present data, recent evidence from Muhimbili National Hospital in Tanzania also identified HR-HPV as a meaningful, though not Western-comparable, etiologic contributor to OPSCC and stressed the need for additional region-specific data to inform vaccination policy and clinical management guidelines [37]. Comparable data from neighbouring Uganda confirm the importance of HPV 16/18 and have identified HIV infection as a key cofactor for HR-HPV detection in oral and oropharyngeal cancers in East Africa [38]. The additional HR-HPV genotypes detected in the present cohort (HPV33, HPV35, HPV73) reinforce the well-documented regional variability of HPV genotype distribution. These differences may reflect variation in sexual behaviour, oral HPV transmission, tobacco and alcohol exposure, HIV prevalence, vaccination coverage, host genetics, and laboratory detection methods [36, 39, 40]. Together, the present findings provide important baseline epidemiological evidence on the prevalence and genotype distribution of high-risk HPV-associated OPSCC in Tanzania and support the expansion of HPV vaccination and routine HPV testing.

In this study, Human papillomavirus type 18 had a relatively higher prevalence than in reports from North America and Europe, where its prevalence typically ranges from 1.3% to 7% [41]. In contrast to Western populations, the epidemiological distribution of HPV 18 in oropharyngeal carcinomas shows marked geographic variation. Meta-analyses from Asian populations have reported HPV 18 prevalence rates as high as 54.2% in certain subsets of oral squamous cell carcinoma [42]. Similarly, a study conducted in Uganda identified HPV 18 in 21% of oral and oropharyngeal cancer cases, either as a single infection or in co-infection with HPV 16 [38]. These findings underscore that although HPV 16 remains the primary global driver of OPSCC, the contribution of non-HPV 16 genotypes, particularly HPV 18, may be substantial in specific geographic and clinical settings [43]. In this patient sample, 23 out of 85 had HPV-associated tumours that may have been prevented by the quadrivalent HPV vaccine, which covers only HPV genotypes 6, 11, 16, and 18.

Furthermore, our analysis demonstrated that HPV positivity was significantly associated with several histopathological characteristics, including histological grade II tumours, advanced tumour stage (AJCC stage III–IV), complete tumour-free surgical margins, keratinisation ≤ 50%, absence of perineural invasion, pushing patterns of invasion, dense tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, and negative nodal status. Collectively, these findings indicate that HPV-positive OPSCC exhibits a distinct biological and histomorphological phenotype compared with HPV-negative disease [44]. Although HPV-positive tumours in our cohort were more frequently diagnosed at advanced AJCC stages, they also showed several favourable histopathological features associated with improved treatment response and prognosis. This apparent paradox has been described previously in HPV-associated OPSCC [45], where advanced clinical stage does not necessarily reflect aggressive tumour biology but rather the unique natural history of HPV-driven disease and the limitations of conventional staging systems in predicting prognosis [46]. Furthermore, the strong association between HPV positivity and dense tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes observed in the present study supports the hypothesis that enhanced host immune responses contribute to the favourable biological behaviour and increased treatment sensitivity of HPV-associated tumours [47]. These findings further emphasise the importance of determining HPV status in patients with OPSCC, as it provides valuable prognostic information and may facilitate risk stratification and individualised treatment planning [44, 48].

The favourable histopathological features observed in HPV-positive OPSCC in the present study have important prognostic implications. Although the present study was cross-sectional and did not directly assess survival outcomes, the association of HPV-positive tumours with the absence of perineural invasion, favourable patterns of invasion, and dense tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes aligns with the recognised biological behaviour of HPV-driven OPSCC [34]. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients with HPV-positive OPSCC have significantly improved overall survival, disease-specific survival, and treatment response compared with those with HPV-negative disease [34–51]. These favourable outcomes have been attributed to the distinct molecular characteristics of HPV-associated tumours, including preserved apoptotic pathways, lower genomic instability, enhanced radiosensitivity, and a stronger anti-tumour immune response [49, 50]. Gupta et al. further demonstrated that HPV-driven head and neck cancers possess distinct molecular and stem-cell characteristics that may contribute to improved therapeutic responsiveness and reduced tumour recurrence [50]. Similarly, landmark clinical studies have consistently reported superior survival among patients with HPV-positive OPSCC, despite frequent presentation with cervical nodal metastases [34, 50]. Collectively, these findings support the incorporation of routine HPV testing into the diagnostic evaluation of patients with OPSCC, as HPV status provides valuable prognostic information and may assist in risk stratification and individualised patient management in Tanzania.

Regarding sociodemographic and clinical factors, we found that the mean age of OPSCC patients was 55.3 years, with an almost equal distribution between patients aged 55 years or older and those aged 55 years or younger. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that OPSCC prevalence increases with age, particularly in the fifth and sixth decades of life [52]. However, a large review study on HPV-related OPSCC in Nigeria reported that HPV-related OPSCC occurs predominantly in younger populations and is more common in males [53]. The gender distribution in our study showed a slight male predominance, and this is consistent with global trends, where OPSCC incidence is higher in men, likely due to behavioural risk factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption [54]. Additionally, a significant proportion of participants were unaware of the role of HPV in OPSCC aetiology. This lack of awareness highlights the need for targeted public health interventions to educate at-risk populations, as other studies have reported similar gaps in HPV knowledge, particularly in low-resource settings [55]. In our study, most participants had no history of smoking, but more than half reported daily alcohol consumption, which is a well-established risk factor for OPSCC [56]. The prevalence of regular oral sex practices in nearly half of the participants aligns with studies linking oral sexual behaviours to HPV transmission and an increased risk of OPSCC [57]. Furthermore, 17.6% of participants reported a history of oral warts, which have been associated with persistent HPV infection.

Regarding histopathological prognostic factors, we found that most OPSCC cases were diagnosed as invasive squamous cell carcinoma, with more than half being keratinising. The predominance of keratinising carcinoma has also been observed in other studies, where it is typically associated with poorer outcomes compared to non-keratinising variants [58].

Notably, HPV-positive tumours were strongly associated with lower histological grade and distinct patterns of tumour differentiation. A significantly higher proportion of HPV-positive cases was observed in grade II tumours, whereas grade I tumours were predominantly HPV-negative. This aligns with established findings that HPV-related tumours often present with an immature, “basaloid” or non-keratinising appearance, which can be misclassified as poorly differentiated in conventional grading systems despite their distinct biology [59]. These differences underscore the divergent carcinogenic pathways between HPV-driven and non-HPV-driven tumours [60].

Tumour stage also showed a significant association, with HPV-positive tumours more likely to present at advanced stages (III–IV) than at early stages (I–II). This clinical pattern is well documented in HPV-associated OPSCC, which often presents with extensive regional nodal involvement even when the primary tumour is small or inconspicuous [61]. Despite presenting at an advanced stage, these tumours are associated with significantly better clinical outcomes than HPV-negative disease [62].

Histomorphological features further reinforced the distinct phenotype of HPV-associated tumours. HPV positivity was significantly associated with non-keratinising morphology, absence of perineural invasion, and specific patterns of invasion characterised by pushing borders and tumour islands. These findings are consistent with the non-keratinising histological type, which typically displays smooth-edged tumour nests and lacks the intense desmoplastic stromal reaction seen in tobacco-related SCC [59, 60]. Furthermore, the non-aggressive tumour invasive front, characterised by pushing rather than infiltrative borders, is a recognised hallmark of the HPV-positive phenotype [60].

Additionally, a strong association was observed between HPV positivity and the host immune response, with dense lymphocytic infiltration significantly more common in HPV-positive tumours. This finding is supported by several studies on host immune defence, which show that HPV-positive tumours exhibit stronger activation of immune signalling pathways and higher expression of genes associated with tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, particularly CD8 + T cells. This enhanced immune activation is associated with better prognosis and treatment outcomes in HPV-driven OPSCC [63].

In this study, the majority of HPV related OPSCC were significantly associated with negative nodal involvement. However, overall survival studies on OPSCC suggest that tumour nodal involvement is an independent intermediate-risk factor for survival, regardless of HPV status [14]. Also, it has been reported that patients with HPV-positive tumours had a 3-year overall survival rate of 82.4%, significantly higher than the 57.1% observed in HPV-negative patients [14]. This improved survival is attributed to the distinct biological behaviour of HPV-positive tumours and their enhanced response to therapy. These findings emphasise the importance of considering HPV status in OPSCC prognosis and management, as it influences both the tumour’s immune environment and clinical outcomes.

In contrast, no significant associations were observed between HPV status and demographic or behavioural factors, including age, sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, HIV status, oral sexual practices, and number of sexual partners. Although many studies suggest that HPV-positive patients are typically younger and have lower tobacco/alcohol exposure [64, 65], such No associations were observed in the present study. Furthermore, although the ≥ 90-year age group showed an equal distribution of HPV-positive and HPV-negative cases (50% each), this finding should be interpreted with caution because it was based on only two patients. The apparent 50% prevalence in this age category reflects the very small sample size rather than a true estimate of HPV prevalence in the elderly population. Therefore, these percentages are statistically unstable and should not be generalised. Larger studies with adequate representation of older patients are needed to provide more reliable age-specific estimates of HPV-associated OPSCC in Tanzania. The absence of significant demographic associations in this cohort suggests that histopathological markers and tumour biology may be more reliable indicators of HPV status than traditional risk profiles [66].

This study has some limitations. The small sample size and cross-sectional study design limit the ability to establish causal relationships and assess survival rates, as OPSCC is relatively rare in our setting and poses challenges for patient follow-up. Additionally, a multivariate model was not performed due to the limited number of OPSCC cases, which precluded full statistical adjustment. Furthermore, viral DNA genome sequencing and the expression of oncoproteins or viral mRNA were not profiled in HPV-positive cases due to budget constraints. However, a major strength of this study is its inclusion of all 28 high-risk HPV genotype primers, enabling comprehensive reporting of the prevalence and genotype distribution of high-risk HPV in OPSCC patients—crucial information for vaccination and public health prevention strategies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that high-risk HPV is an important etiological factor in oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) in north-western Tanzania, with HPV16 being the predominant genotype. HPV-positive tumours were associated with favourable histopathological and prognostic features, underscoring the clinical importance of routine HPV testing for prognostic stratification and individualised patient management. Furthermore, the detection of less common high-risk genotypes, including HPV33, HPV35, and HPV73, emphasises the need for broad HPV vaccination strategies and continued molecular surveillance in our setting. Future multicentre prospective studies are warranted to evaluate long-term treatment outcomes and survival among patients with HPV-positive and HPV-negative OPSCC in Tanzania.
