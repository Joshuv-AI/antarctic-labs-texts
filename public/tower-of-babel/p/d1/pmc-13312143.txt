Evaluation of the study carried out by ANSES on the effectiveness of sanitary shucking of queen scallops (Aequipecten opercularis) contaminated by okadaic acid toxins
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Ana Gago‐Martínez, Arjen Gerssen, Isabelle P Oswald, Jose Cortiñas Abrahantes, Anita Radovnikovic, Salomon Sand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing a request from the European Commission, EFSA evaluated the effectiveness of sanitary shucking of queen scallops (Aequipecten opercularis) contaminated with okadaic acid (OA) group toxins. Specifically, EFSA was tasked to evaluate a study conducted by the French Agency for Food, Environmental and Occupational Health & Safety (ANSES), to assess the appropriateness of a proposed threshold of 4000 μg OA eq/kg in whole scallops, intended to ensure compliance of the edible part after shucking, and to provide recommendations on sampling schemes that would allow detection of non‐compliant lots/areas above the threshold of 80 μg OA eq/kg. The assessment was supported by structured literature searches on the hazard profile of OA group toxins and contamination events. The ANSES study was assessed previously in the EFSA CONTAM Panel opinion, but it was not possible to retrieve a safe shucking level of OA for A. opercularis. No new occurrence data were submitted for the current assessment. While the ANSES study results indicate that shucking was effective and provided compliant edible part under the specific conditions studied, the following limitations were identified: restricted number of samples, representativeness across contamination scenarios and concentration ranges. The proposed limit of 4000 μg OA eq/kg is substantially higher than the highest whole‐scallop level observed in the study (approximately 900 μg OA eq/kg), and it is unknown whether the relationship between OA levels in muscle vs. whole scallops would remain unchanged at higher concentration levels. Consequently, it is concluded that the proposed threshold of 4000 μg OA eq/kg in whole scallops cannot be endorsed based on ANSES study alone. With regard to sampling schemes for shucked scallops, the number of individual scallops required in a pooled analytical sample to reliably classify batches as compliant or non‐compliant depends strongly on the observed mean contamination level and the variability.Keywords: Aequipecten opercularis, dinophysistoxins, lipophilic marine biotoxins, okadaic acid, scallops, shucking

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Background

In 2020 (Ares (2020) 1170730 of 25.02.2020) EFSA was requested to provide a scientific opinion on shucking of certain species of scallops contaminated with domoic acid and lipophilic toxins exceeding the limits established by the European legislation.

In particular, EFSA was asked to provide a scientific opinion on the practice of shucking in certain species of scallops (Pecten maximus, Pecten jacobaeus, Chlamys islandica, Aequipecten opercularis and Mimachlamys varia) containing domoic acid and the lipophilic toxins covered by the Union legislation in the whole body, or in any part edible separately, exceeding the level authorised under the current EU rules and its possible public health impact. On 27 January 2021, a first opinion on the evaluation of the shucking of scallops contaminated with lipophilic toxins was adopted by the EFSA CONTAM Panel (doi: 10.2903/j.efsa.2021.6422). After the adoption of the 2021 EFSA opinion, France asked the Commission to assess the appropriateness of the study carried out by ANSES (the French Agency for Food, Environmental and Occupational Health & Safety) on the effectiveness of sanitary shucking of queen scallops (Aequipecten opercularis) contaminated by okadaic acid (OA) toxins (https://hal.science/hal‐04938795v1/document) (HAL Id: hal‐04938795). France moreover asks to evaluate the appropriateness of the maximum threshold of the okadaic acid toxins of 4000 micrograms of okadaic acid equivalents per kilogram under which sanitary shucking can be authorised based on the results of the above cited study.

Interpretation of the terms of reference

Following the terms of reference (TOR) from the European Commission (EC), the assessment will focus on the scallops Aequipecten opercularis and OA toxins group only. A brief summary of the toxicological effects of these toxins and the results of the structured literature search on the potentially relevant new information from the last 10 years is part of the statement. Following the specific TORs provided by the European Commission and limited time for the assessment, hazard identification and characterisation, including re‐evaluation of toxic equivalency factors (TEFs) of OA toxins, exposure assessment, and risk characterisation are outside of the scope of this assessment.

The statement will also address the request to provide scientific advice on appropriate sampling scheme of shucked scallops (TOR 2) which includes a batch‐based approach that would allow the detection of non‐compliant batches or lots with respect to a target threshold of 80 μg OA equivalents per kilogram (in edible tissue). This target threshold corresponds to half of the current EU regulatory limit of 160 μg OA equivalents per kilogram and is intended as a precautionary measure, to ensure that the entire batch complies with the regulatory limit.

Additional information

Terminology

The terminology used in the current statement has been adopted from the previous EFSA opinion (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2021). Under the term ‘Scallop’ will be considered both an exoskeleton and internal soft tissue. The phrase ‘entire animal’ is used to refer to all soft tissues, even though this is not the entire scallop with the exoskeleton. The term ‘gonad’, colloquially ‘roe’, refers to the anatomically distinguishable reproductive organ. The phrase ‘edible parts’ for the species under assessment (A. opercularis) will be considered ‘adductor muscle’ only, since gonad is usually not marketed for this species due to its small size.

EU food safety legislation does not define shucking. In English language, the verb ‘shuck’ means the ‘removal of the shell or natural covering from something that is eaten’. In scallop production, ‘shucking’ generally means removal of some soft tissues in addition to removing the shell. In the current assessment, the term shucking with reference to scallops will be considered as:

opening of outer shell, and detaching soft tissues from both shells;

opening of outer shell, and detaching soft tissues from both shells;

evisceration to remove and discard hepatopancreas, intestines and mantle;

evisceration to remove and discard hepatopancreas, intestines and mantle;

leaving adductor mussel and gonad, or just adductor muscle alone; these parts are actively rinsed with water and may be replaced in the half‐shell for commercial presentation.

leaving adductor mussel and gonad, or just adductor muscle alone; these parts are actively rinsed with water and may be replaced in the half‐shell for commercial presentation.

The 2021 EFSA Opinion on the evaluation of the shucking of certain species of scallops contaminated with lipophilic toxins with a view to the production of edible parts meeting the safety requirements foreseen in the Union legislation (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2021) notes that the procedure of shucking kills the bivalve mollusc, if it had been alive, thereby moving the product from the legal category of ‘live bivalve mollusc’ to the category of ‘fishery product’. In addition, the EU food legislation appears to accept the labelling of scallop adductor muscle alone or adductor muscle with gonad, within or without a half‐shell, i.e. shucked scallops, quite simply as ‘scallop’ even though much of the animal is not present.

EU food safety legislation recognises the risk of biotoxins in live bivalve molluscs (LBMs) and establishes a specific regulatory risk management framework. In particular, Regulation (EC) No 853/2004, Annex III, Section VII, Chapter V defines the health standards for LBM and states that LBM must not contain marine biotoxins in total quantities (measured in the whole body or any part edible separately) that exceed the limits listed in Table 1.

Of interest to this assessment are the limits established for the OA group, which consist of OA and its derivatives dinophystoxins, lipophilic marine biotoxins.

Legislation on shucking

Scientific knowledge on accumulation of biotoxins in target organs and tissues of the scallops suggests the possibility of a regimen where biotoxin‐safe parts may be marketed for human consumption, following the removal of biotoxin‐unsafe parts. In the previous EFSA opinion (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2021), it has been emphasised that ‘EU legislation acknowledges the potential for an edible parts‐only regimen where safe compliant food may be extracted from animals which had exceeded a biotoxin limit when assessed as an entire animal’.

One such example is the EU permission for shucking of Pecten maximus and Pecten jacobaeus scallops under strict conditions, when the level of domoic acid (DA) in the whole scallop exceeds the regulatory limit of 20 mg/kg, but is below 250 mg/kg. The conditions for the restricted harvesting regime are described in Commission Decision 2002/226/EC. 1 It can be initiated if two consecutive analyses of samples, taken between one and no more than seven days, show that DA concentration in whole mollusc is lower than 250 mg/kg and that the DA concentration in the parts intended for human consumption, which have to be analysed separately, is lower than 4.6 mg/kg. After total removal of hepatopancreas, soft tissues and any other contaminated part, the adductor muscle and/or gonads intended for human consumption must not contain DA exceeding 20 mg/kg. The EU decision requires that the analyses of the entire body is performed on a homogenate of 10 molluscs, while the analysis on the edible parts is performed on a homogenate of 10 individual parts. This regulatory exception is not applicable to other scallop species or toxins.

Previous assessments

EFSA carried out an assessment of the toxicity of lipophilic toxins, in particular OA and its derivatives in 2008 (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2008). OA and its derivatives dinophysistoxin‐1 (DTX1), dinophysistoxin‐2 (DTX2) and the esterified forms of OA, DTX1, DTX2 (DTX3) cause diarrhoeic shellfish poisoning (DSP) in humans, which is characterised by vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea and nausea, usually appearing shortly after consumption of contaminated shellfish.

The mode of action of OA toxins indicated in the earlier EFSA assessment (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2008) is the inhibition of serine/threonine phosphoprotein phosphatases (PPs). DTX3 cannot directly bind to PPs and a hydrolysis step is necessary to transform DTX3 into its corresponding parent compound (OA, DTX1 or DTX2). At the time of the assessment, no long‐term toxicity/carcinogenicity studies were available for OA and its derivatives. However, OA was identified as a tumour promoter in rodents. The Panel also concluded that ‘OA appeared to be not mutagenic per se, but induced changes at the chromosome level and was aneugenic in vitro’. The Panel noted that these effects may be related to cytotoxicity of OA. The Panel considered that ‘in vivo relevance of the positive in vitro genotoxicity findings was unclear’. No genotoxicity data were available for DTX1, DTX2 and DTX3. There were insufficient data on chronic effects of OA to retrieve a tolerable daily intake (TDI). Based on a lowest observed adverse effect level (LOAEL) in humans of 0.8 μg OA eq/kg body weight (bw) for adults and by applying an uncertainty factor (UF) of 3, an acute reference dose (ARfD) of 0.3 μg OA eq/kg bw was established.

Based on LD50 experiments following intraperitoneal injection in mice, the Panel established the TEFs: OA = 1, DTX1 = 1, DTX2 = 0.6. For DTX3, the TEF values are equal to those of the corresponding unesterified toxins (OA, DTX1, and DTX2) (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2008).

In addition, the Panel noted that the consumption of a 400 g portion of shellfish (high portion size) containing OA‐group toxins at the EU regulatory limit of 160 μg OA eq/kg shellfish meat would result in a dietary exposure of 64 μg OA eq/person, which is approximately three times higher than the ARfD and in the range of the LOAEL for DSP in humans (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2008).

Previous assessments on shucking of scallops

In 2008, the French Food Safety Agency, Department of Risk Evaluation (former AFSSA, currently part of ANSES) published an opinion on the reduction of the algal toxin risk in Pectinidae by the introduction of an evisceration procedure (AFSSA, 2008). AFSSA assessed the potential for extending the shucking procedure that is already in place for DA (hydrophilic toxin) contamination in two species of scallops (Commission Decision 2002/226/EC) to lipophilic or paralytic shellfish poisons (PSP) toxins. It concluded that additional information on the occurrence levels coming from new contamination episodes are needed to draw conclusions related to potential shucking conditions for lipophilic and PSP toxins.

In 2015, ANSES completed an investigation study in P. maximus scallops from three different fishing areas, following a natural DSP event in 2014 (ANSES NRLMB, 2015). In 2017, ANSES published a report on the DSP contamination event from 2016 and the effect of shucking on levels of OA group toxins in A. opercularis (ANSES NRLMB, 2017). The study is described in detail in Section 3.3.1.

In 2021, EFSA published an Opinion on the evaluation of the shucking of certain species of scallops contaminated with lipophilic toxins (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2021). Suitable data on concentrations of the toxins of concern in the whole scallops and body parts were only available for two scallop species A. opercularis and P. maximus. These data came from the ANSES investigations, mentioned above, following up contamination events in 2014 and 2016 (ANSES NRLMB, 2015; ANSES NRLMB, 2017) and from another contamination event in Ireland and not from a national monitoring scheme of products on the market. The CONTAM Panel calculated the ratio between the toxin levels in the edible parts (muscle, gonad or the sum of muscle and gonad) and in the entire scallop. Regarding the OA toxins group in P. maximus the data allowed a statistical approach and the calculation of a threshold level in whole scallops that should ensure with 99% certainty that levels in edible parts are below the regulatory limit. The following values were retrieved for the whole scallops (P. maximus):

342 μg OA eq/kg to ensure compliant levels in adductor muscle plus gonad

342 μg OA eq/kg to ensure compliant levels in adductor muscle plus gonad

256 μg OA eq/kg to ensure compliant levels in gonads based on French data and 165 μg OA eq/kg considering the Irish data.

256 μg OA eq/kg to ensure compliant levels in gonads based on French data and 165 μg OA eq/kg considering the Irish data.

For the adductor muscle only (P. maximus), data were all left censored and not suitable for such an assessment. The same statistical approach was not applicable for A. opercularis, since lipophilic toxin levels (OA and yessetoxin group) in adductor muscles were all below the detection limits (LODs) or below the quantification limit (LOQ). At the time, the Panel expressed uncertainty if the result reached in the assessment can be extrapolated to other scallop species.

Hazard characterisation was not within the TOR of the 2021 EFSA assessment, but the CONTAM Panel recommended updating the risk assessments on OA, YTX and AZA group toxins including a re‐evaluation of the relative potencies of their different analogues.

Okadaic acid and its derivatives

OA and its derivatives (DTX1, DTX2 and DTX3) are polyether compounds and have been characterised in previous EFSA Opinions (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2008, 2021) (Figure 1). OA toxins have lipophilic properties and may accumulate in the filter‐feeding bivalves.

Chemical structures of OA and DTX1, 2, 3. (Adapted from Larsen et al., 2007a,b)

The toxins are thermostable and when frozen (−20°C to −80°C) they are stable for several months. At temperatures above 100°C, they start to degrade significantly. In general, OA, DTX1 and DTX2 are products of toxin‐producing marine plankton (e.g., dinoflagellates), while DTX3 is suggested to be a metabolic product of OA, DTX1 and DTX2 produced within the shellfish through acylation of the parent toxins. Suzuki et al. (1999) demonstrated the transformation of DTX1 to 7‐O‐acyl‐DTX1 in the scallop Patinopecten yessoensis. It is important to recognise that a significant portion of the toxin content is present as the DTX3 form. The most predominantly described acyl moiety for DTX3 detected is the hexadecanoic (palmitic, C 16:0) acid. The lipophilicity of DTX3 is higher as compared to the unacylated toxins (OA, DTX1 and DTX2). The stability of the DTX3 is comparable to OA, DTX1 and DTX2. Under alkaline conditions, at elevated temperatures or in the presence of enzymes such as lipase and cholesterol esterase, DTX3 is deacylated to the parent toxin, i.e. OA, DTX1 or DTX2 (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2021).

DATA AND METHODOLOGIES

The present Statement was developed by applying a structured methodological approach as described hereafter. The information provided in the mandate, together with data from other sources, such as previous risk assessments by EFSA or other risk assessment bodies, peer‐reviewed scientific literature, other scientific reports and experts' knowledge was used to deliver the present output.

Supporting information for the assessment

ANSES study

The current assessment is focused on evaluating the study report provided by ANSES, on the effectiveness of sanitary shucking of queen scallops (A. opercularis) contaminated by OA toxins, with the aim of producing edible parts meeting EU safety requirements (ANSES NRLMB, 2017). This study was in the public domain at the time of the assessment. 2 In the 2021 EFSA assessment, the ANSES study from 2017 also included results for the yessotoxin (YTX) group, which were not resubmitted for the current assessment. The YTX toxins levels that were reported for the entire animal in 2021 Opinion were 0.006–0.025 mg YTX eq/kg, while the levels in the adductor muscles were all left censored (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2021).

Literature search

Two structured literature searches have been carried out. The first search focused on the occurrence studies and information on any OA toxins marine contamination events that could provide information on the partitioning of OA toxins in different parts of A. opercularis (without time limit). The second search targeted studies on the hazard characterisation of OA toxin group in the last 10 years. This time frame was considered appropriate given the constrained timelines of the mandate and that it sufficiently captures the most up‐to‐date knowledge on hazard characterisation, improved study designs and relevant toxicological endpoints. The aim of this search was to identify if there was any new relevant information that could modify the hazard profile of OA group toxins established in the first EFSA assessment in 2008 (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2008). The databases used were Web of Science 3 and PubMed. 4 References were retrieved and managed by Endnote. The search terms and Boolean operators applied for the searches in both databases are available in the Appendix A. The outcome of the literature search is described in more detail in Sections 3.1 and 3.2.

Statistical methodologies

Ratio between concentrations in the edible part and the entire animal

The 2021 EFSA Opinion (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2021) applied a statistical approach to estimate the highest levels of lipophilic toxins in the whole scallop, that would ensure that levels in edible parts comply with the respective regulatory limit. This approach was only possible in cases where toxins were detected in edible parts. For each sample, the ratios between edible parts (muscle, gonad or the sum of these) and entire animals were calculated both for lower bound (LB) and upper bound (UB) levels. For each sample, this provided an interval in which the true ratio could be. Subsequently, a distribution function was estimated for the whole set of samples from which mean and median ratios were derived. Also, the resulting levels in the whole scallop that would guarantee that levels in the edible parts would be below the regulatory limit with high probability (99% certainty) could be estimated.

Sample size estimation

The methodology used to calculate the sample size in the present assessment follows the same principles used in the 2021 EFSA Opinion (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2021). It considers an adaptation of the approach proposed by Matsushima et al. (2018a); Matsushima et al. (2018b) assuming that the concentration of toxins in scallops follows a log‐normal distribution instead of a normal distribution as assumed by the authors. It is expected that toxin concentration only takes positive values and generally shows skewness, where the lower concentrations are more likely to be observed than very high ones. The approach considers the variation observed in four French 5 sampling areas (coefficient of variation (CV) ranging from 0.37 to 0.5) that were studied by EFSA in 2021. The CV estimated for the study submitted by ANSES under consideration in the present statement was 0.37. The approach proposed by El‐Shaarawi and Lin (2006) was used to estimate the confidence interval for a log‐normal random variable which was then used to calculate the number of samples needed to classify the area/batch as compliant or non‐compliant. More details on the approach used can be found in Appendix B from 2021 EFSA Opinion (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2021).

ASSESSMENT

Literature search on the hazard profile of OA and its derivatives

The literature search resulted in 12 relevant studies. Seven of these papers concern in vivo experiments dealing with the compared toxicity of OA, DTX1 and DTX2. Five papers were from the same research group.

There was one in vivo study that investigated the subchronic toxic effects of OA, with a focus on its impact on the liver using a mouse model with 120 days exposure. According to the authors, intraperitoneal exposure (0.2, 2, 10 μg OA/kg bw) to OA damaged the liver by disrupting metabolism and cellular stress pathways, leading to oxidative stress, mitochondrial failure, and increased apoptosis, which may contribute to long‐term disease risk including cancer (Wang et al., 2021).

Comparative toxicity – in vivo studies

Several studies compared in vivo toxicity of OA, DTX1 and DTX2. The first study compared OA and DTX1 toxicity using the mouse bioassay (intraperitoneal administration). Both toxins induced dose‐dependent mortality; however, DTX1 showed higher acute toxicity, with a lower LD50 (~150 μg/kg) compared to OA (~186 μg/kg), and caused faster mortality. These findings suggest that DTX1 may be more toxic than OA under acute systemic exposure conditions (Suzuki & Okada, 2018).

Abal et al. (2017, 2018) established acute oral toxicity values for diarrhoeic shellfish toxins in mice using a standardised up‐and‐down procedure. The oral LD50 values demonstrated clear differences in potency, with DTX1 (487 μg/kg) being more toxic than OA (760 μg/kg), while DTX2 showed markedly lower toxicity (2262 μg/kg). Based on these findings, the authors proposed revised TEFs: OA = 1.0, DTX1 = 1.5, DTX2 = 0.3.

Using a voluntary feeding model in mice, that better reflects human exposure, Rodríguez‐Santos et al. (2025) compared the acute oral toxicity of OA, DTX1, and DTX2. DTX1 and OA showed similar high toxicity, with DTX1 being slightly more potent, while DTX2 was significantly less toxic. DTX1 induced earlier and more severe effects, including diarrhoea, weight loss and gastrointestinal damage, and was eliminated more slowly. The study proposed updated TEFs (OA = 1, DTX1 = 1.1, DTX2 = 0.25), supporting a toxicity ranking of DTX1 ≥ OA > > DTX2.

The last study assessed both acute and repeated oral toxicity of OA and DTX1 in mice (Park et al., 2023). Acute oral LD50 values were similar, although slightly lower for DTX1, indicating marginally higher acute potency. However, repeated oral exposure revealed that OA induced more severe cumulative toxic effects, including greater body weight loss, increased diarrhoea scores, intestinal alterations and ascites formation. The results suggest that OA is at least as toxic as DTX1 under repeated exposure conditions.

Comparative toxicokinetics – in vivo studies

Louzao et al. (2021) investigated the toxicokinetics of OA, DTX1 and DTX2 following oral administration in mice. Twenty‐four hours post‐administration, the highest concentration of OA and DTX1 were detected in tissues from the stomach, the large intestine, the small intestine and the liver. In contrast, DTX2 was present at much lower levels in these tissues and at negligible levels in plasma. The findings indicate that the substantially lower toxicity of DTX2 is primarily due to poor bioavailability rather than reduced intrinsic potency, highlighting the importance of toxicokinetics in risk assessment.

Rodríguez‐Santos et al. (2024) evaluated the toxicokinetics and sublethal toxicity of OA, DTX1, and DTX2 following oral exposure of mice at realistic doses. OA and DTX1 produced more severe gastrointestinal symptoms, including persistent diarrhoea and tissue damage, and showed greater accumulation in digestive organs. DTX2 exhibited limited tissue distribution, faster elimination, and only mild, transient effects. The results confirm that differences in toxicity are largely driven by absorption, distribution, and elimination, with DTX1 and OA posing higher toxicological concern than DTX2.

Comparative toxicity – in vitro

Hégarat et al. (2020) compared the genotoxic effects of OA, DTX‐1 and DTX‐2 in the human liver cell line HepaRG. All three toxins induced DNA damage and mitotic arrest in a dose‐dependent manner as measured by γH2AX and pH3 biomarkers. However, DTX‐1 was the most potent toxin, followed by OA, while DTX‐2 showed the weakest effects. Using a BMD approach, they calculate relative potency factors and determined that DTX‐1 was approximately twice as potent as OA, while DTX‐2 showed comparable or lower potency than OA.

Jiménez‐Cárcamo et al., 2020 investigated the effects of OA, DTX‐1 and DTX‐2 on non‐cancerous and cancerous colon cell lines (CCD841, SW480 and SW620). All toxins reduced cell viability, with normal cells being more sensitive. In contrast, cancer cells were more resistant but showed increased migration and activation of adhesion‐related signalling.

Ikehara et al. (2021) compared OA, DTX‐1 and DTX‐2 in the neuroblastoma cell line (Neuro2A) using both a cytotoxicity assay and a protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) inhibition assay. The relative potencies of the three toxins determined by the PP2A inhibition assay were 1.0:1.6:0.3, whereas the cytotoxicity assay yielded relative potencies of 1.0:1.5:0.5.

Zhao et al. (2025) compared the cytotoxic effects of several marine toxins – including OA, DTX1, and DTX2 – across three cell lines (the neuroblastoma cell line Neuro‐2a, the neuroblasoma x glioma hybrid cell line NG108‐15, and the breast cancer cell line MCF‐7) at two exposure time points. Cytotoxicity varied both by cell line and duration of exposure. Overall, DTX1 was the most toxic compound, OA the least toxic, and DTX2 showed intermediate toxicity. An exception was observed in MCF‐7 cells after 48 h of exposure, where DTX2 exhibited the lowest toxicity among the tested compounds.

Summary

Across these studies, DTX1 generally showed higher acute toxicity; OA showed lower toxicity than DTX1 except upon repeated exposure; DTX2 was substantially less toxic, largely due to poor absorption and low bioavailability. Toxicokinetics seems to play a central role in determining toxicity differences. Overall, this limited assessment indicated relative potencies that are slightly different than those developed in 2008, which suggests that a re‐assessment of TEFs might be warranted. However, this is not within the scope of the current TOR.

Literature search on contamination events

The literature search provided nine studies that were potentially relevant for the assessment. Four of these studies mention a combination of OA group toxins and A. opercularis. However, none of the studies describe in detail a contamination event involving A. opercularis with corresponding OA group toxin levels, or provide relevant information for this specific study. The most relevant finding is that OA group toxins were mainly present in esterified forms (DTX3) in A. opercularis (Dhanji‐Rapkova et al., 2018). This is of importance since for a correct quantification DTX3 needs to be hydrolysed to OA, DTX1 and DTX2.

Evaluation of the ANSES study

Description of the study

The study was conducted in response to an episode of contamination of queen scallops (A. opercularis) by lipophilic marine biotoxins that occurred during the summer of 2016 in French waters (ANSES NRLMB, 2017). The affected harvesting area was located in the north west of the Cotentin Peninsula. The objective of the study was to evaluate the effectiveness of sanitary shucking – defined as the separation of the adductor muscle from the remaining flesh – with the aim of producing the edible part compliant to the EU regulation on maximum levels of marine biotoxins.

Two batches of scallops were sampled from this area, Batch 1 on the 16 August 2016 and Batch 2 on 24 August 2016. Each batch consisted of 80 scallops. Shucking was carried out within an accredited facility on 17 August and on 24 August, respectively. For each scallop, the adductor muscle was separated from the remaining flesh and each part was packed in individual bags and frozen. Samples were kept frozen at < −18°C in the National Reference Laboratory for Marine Biotoxins (NRLMB) from the reception (31 August 2016) to the analyses, which were carried out between March and May 2017. In total, results from 122 scallops were available: 43 from Batch 1 and 79 from Batch 2, with both parts (‘adductor muscle’ and the ‘remaining flesh’) analysed for OA toxins for each individual scallop. The analysis was performed using an accredited LC–MS/MS method (ANSES PBM BM LSA_INS‐0147), corresponding to the harmonised protocol of the EU Reference Laboratory for Marine Biotoxins for the analyses of lipophilic toxins in live bivalve molluscs. The analyses included detection of both free and total forms of lipophilic toxins after alkaline hydrolysis. Reported results were expressed as μg OA eq/kg using TEFs. The concentration of the OA toxins in the whole scallops was calculated based on the weights of the parts and concentrations of OA eq in each of the parts. The LOD and LOQ of the analytical method for the OA toxin group were determined following in‐house validation and were 3.9 and 10 μg/kg, respectively.

The results indicated that only OA and DTX2 were present, predominantly in bound form. OA and DTX2 toxins were almost exclusively concentrated in the ‘remaining flesh’. The levels of OA acid toxins in adductor muscles were below LOD in all tested samples, apart from one, which was below the LOQ. Table 2 shows a summary of the reported results.

In addition, ANSES carried out statistical analyses on samples from batch 2 to assess the probability that levels of OA toxins in the muscle would exceed the LOQ or the regulatory limit of 160 μg/kg. Their calculation indicated an extremely low probability (5 × 10−13) of exceeding LOQ, and the probability of exceeding the compliance limit was too low to estimate.

The uncertainty analysis in 2021 EFSA assessment indicated limitations, such as the lack of recovery data. In a clarification, ANSES explained how recovery was addressed in their validation study. The method was validated for official control by spiking shellfish meat (mussel, oysters and scallops) prior extraction. Overall, the recoveries of all OA toxins ranged between 90% and 109%. The validated method is also used for this specific study. The validated range of the methodology applied is up to levels of 240 μg OA eq/kg.

It is unknown whether the extraction efficiency, as well as other parameters, such as the hydrolysis conditions, perform similarly at the proposed limit of 4000 μg OA eq/kg in the whole scallop. Therefore, in case there would be any regulatory limit of 4000 μg OA eq/kg, the laboratories involved in this type of analyses would need to validate the method at that level.

Appropriateness for establishing a threshold

In the data set from France, OA was only detected in the remaining tissue (or in ‘hepatopancreas, mantle and gonad’) and not in the adductor muscle (i.e. all levels below the LOQ/LOD). Estimated toxin equivalents in muscle based on LOD/LOQ varied between 7.1 and 11.7 μg OA eq/kg, and the estimated UB levels in the entire animal ranged between 191 and 942 μg OA eq/kg, which is above the EU regulatory limit of 160 μg OA eq/kg (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2021).

As discussed in Section 2.2, the statistical approach used for modelling the levels in whole scallop that would guarantee (with high certainty) that the level in edible parts is compliant could only be performed in cases where toxins were in fact detected. Since OA toxins in edible parts were not detected in the French study, EFSA CONTAM Panel (2021) noted that a potential cut‐off for whole scallops could then only be based on LOD/LOQs of the methods. Using the UB levels in whole scallops (191–942 μg OA eq/kg) this resulted in an average ratio of ‘muscle’ to ‘whole animal’ of 0.017, and the ratio ranged from 0.008 to 0.037.

This approach is conservative if considering that the actual levels in edible parts are ≤ LOD/LOQ. However, the representativeness of the ANSES study with respect to other contamination events is uncertain, and the number of samples is limited (N = 122). While the proposed limit of 4000 μg OA eq/kg in whole scallops results from using a conservative ratio, it seems unnecessary high as a regulatory shucking limit since this level is much higher than the highest observed concentrations in the ANSES study (≈ 900 μg OA eq/kg). It is not clear if the relation between OA levels in muscle vs. whole scallops is independent of the absolute concentration levels, i.e., at levels that are higher than those observed in the ANSES study. More data are needed on individual scallops and distribution of toxins in different body parts to obtain a better understanding of above mentioned limitations, as already pointed out in the previous assessment on shucking of scallops contaminated by lipofillic toxins (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2021). Based on these considerations, it is considered that the proposed shucking threshold of 4000 μg OA eq/kg in whole scallops cannot be supported on the basis of the ANSES study alone.

Recommendations on sampling scheme

Sample size determination relies on key statistical parameters, primarily the expected mean and variance. When employing a lognormal distribution, the CV – which represents the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean – serves as a crucial measure of sample variability. The sample size is calculated using a range of expected mean values, with corresponding standard deviations derived directly from the CV.

To ensure a well‐informed selection of potential variability, data from additional three French sampling areas, as referenced in the 2021 EFSA Opinion, submitted by France on OA, were considered. This comprehensive approach allows for a broader understanding of how observed variability can influence sample size calculations. Accordingly, the process was not limited to the CV observed in the ANSES (2017) study (CV = 0.37; N = 122 samples); it also considered other variations noted in different regions, even when these involved different scallop species. This method provides a more robust and representative basis for estimating the required sample size across diverse sampling scenarios.

For the OA toxins group, the data used for sample size estimation correspond to different French areas and time periods where a contamination episode allowed a more detailed study on individual scallops. For each area, the CV was calculated, ranging from 39% and 50% for P. maximus and 37% for A. opercularis. Using these CVs, it was estimated for several hypothetical mean levels around a precautionary threshold of 80 μg OA eq/kg in the edible part (adductor muscle), how many individual scallops should be included in the sample to ensure with a 95% certainty that the sample tested is below or above 80 μg OA eq/kg. As shown in Figure 2, in the range of 70–90 μg OA eq/kg, this would require the pooling of more than 30 scallops in an analytical sample when considering the largest CV of 0.5. If instead considering the CV = 0.37 estimated for the area with A. opercularis, the required pooling will require less than 30 scallops. For levels < 70 and > 90 OA eq/kg, respectively, 11–16 (CV of 0.5), and 7–10 (CV of 0.37) whole scallops per pooled sample would be sufficient. Sample sizes of < 10 would give a 95% correct classification at actual levels of ≤ 60 or ≥ 100 μg OA eq/kg in the edible part. The details of the calculations are presented in Appendix B.

With regard to sampling schemes for shucked scallops, the assessment showed that the number of individual scallops required in a pooled analytical sample to reliably classify batches as compliant or non‐compliant depends strongly on the mean contamination level and the data variability. In particular, when mean concentrations are close to the precautionary target threshold of 80 μg OA eq/kg in the edible part, relatively large sample sizes, above 30 would be required to minimise misclassification.
