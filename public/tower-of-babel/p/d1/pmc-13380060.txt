GFRα2 identified in TBI-induced bone healing is a novel therapeutic target for osteoporosis via osteogenesis and angiogenesis
Authors: Huibo Ti, Zhenyu Zhang, Huaxin Kang, Yuechun Chen, Keyue Zhang, Shuwen Shi, Xian Wu, Zixin Luo, Xingchen Yao, Xielin Yan, Junjie Wu, Zhengdong Yuan, Hao Nie, Feng-lai Yuan, Xia Li

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a prevalent skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mass, microarchitectural deterioration, and increased fracture risk [1]. It poses a significant public health burden, particularly in the elderly, due to debilitating fractures and high associated mortality. While current therapeutic strategies primarily target osteoblasts and osteoclasts to restore bone homeostasis [2], the need for novel approaches remains critical.

Intriguingly, recent clinical observations have revealed a seemingly paradoxical phenomenon: patients suffering from traumatic brain injury (TBI) often exhibit accelerated fracture healing and enhanced callus ossification [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. This stands in stark contrast to OP, where the core pathophysiology involves an imbalance favoring bone resorption over formation. The remarkable osteogenic response triggered by TBI suggests that the nervous system, particularly in the context of injury, may harbor potent pro-osteogenic signals. Indeed, emerging evidence points to complex bidirectional communication between the skeletal and nervous systems [7,8]. The skeleton is densely innervated, and neurological disorders such as stroke, spinal cord injury, Parkinson's, and Alzheimer's disease are frequently associated with OP development [9,10]. Furthermore, neurotransmitters and neuropeptides are recognized as key mediators of bone metabolism.

A critical insight from TBI research is the altered secretion of neural-derived factors following injury. For instance, dynamic changes in the levels of neurotrophic factors like nerve growth factor (NGF), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and GDNF have been documented in both patients and animal models, correlating with injury type, progression, and intervention [11,12]. Nevertheless, the exact mechanisms through which these neural factors induced by TBI, along with their specific receptors, facilitate bone formation are still predominantly unclear. Most importantly, their possible function in regulating bone metabolism in the context of OP, a disorder characterized by compromised bone formation, has yet to be investigated.

Among these factors, GDNF and its receptor GFRα2 have emerged as particularly compelling candidates. Notably, mice lacking the relevant functional element associated with GFRα2 exhibit significantly reduced bone mass [[13], [14], [15], [16]], and GDNF signaling holds therapeutic promise for neurodegenerative conditions. Based on the compelling link between TBI-induced osteogenesis and the known importance of GFRα2 in bone mass regulation, we hypothesized that the GDNF-GFRα2 axis is a key mediator of the enhanced bone healing observed after TBI and represents a novel, promising target for mitigating skeletal degenerative disorders like OP.

In this study, we investigated this hypothesis by: (1) Demonstrating that GFRα2 is highly expressed specifically within bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) in the callus of TBI mice; (2) Showing through sequencing analysis that GFRα2 knockdown inhibits BMSC osteogenic differentiation, while high GFR2 expression enhances it; (3) Identifying FTH1 and VEGFD as downstream mediators linking GFRα2 signaling to BMSC osteogenesis via sequencing; (4) Establishing the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis by demonstrating that in vivo overexpression of Gfrα2 (OE-Gfrα2 plasmid) protects against ovariectomy (OVX)-induced OP.

Results

TBI promotes bone formation and is accompanied by an increase in GFRα2 expression

Clinical data show faster fracture healing in patients with femoral fractures and concurrent TBI, often linked to heterotopic ossification. [6,16,17]. To explore the mechanism, a murine femoral fracture model with TBI or sham surgery was developed (Fig. 1A and B). Micro-CT and histomorphometric analysis at 14 days post-fracture revealed significantly higher bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and bone mineral density (BMD) in the TBI group versus sham controls (Fig. 1C and D). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Safranin O/Fast Green staining confirmed enhanced new bone formation in the callus (Fig. 1D and E). These findings confirm that TBI enhances bone formation and speeds up fracture healing.

Brain injury enhances fracture healing via neurotrophic factors [16]; GDNF signaling through GFRα2 suggests this pathway's critical role in bone metabolism. We hypothesized GDNF-GFRα2 signaling contributes to TBI-mediated bone formation. To test this, we assessed GFRα2 expression: fracture site samples from TBI patients showed significantly elevated GFRα2 protein, and immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and Western blot confirmed markedly increased GFRα2 in the bone marrow of TBI model mice (Fig. 1G, H, I). In brief, these results show that TBI enhances bone formation and fracture healing in both humans and mice, along with a marked increase in GFRα2 expression at the fracture site.

Given the established role of GFRα2 in promoting bone formation in mice, we focused on investigating its expression pattern and potential role in mediating bone mass increase. To clarify the cellular localization of GFRα2 within the bone microenvironment, particularly in the context of fracture healing, we performed immunofluorescence staining on fracture callus tissue. The results revealed that GFRα2 was specifically co-localized with BMSCs markers, including CD90 and CD105 [18], within cells in the fracture area. This confirmed the expression of GFRα2 in BMSCs. Furthermore, compared to the sham-operated group, we observed a concurrent increase in both GFRα2 expression and new bone formation within BMSCs in the fracture region. This correlation suggests that GFRα2 upregulation in BMSCs may contribute to the enhancement of bone mass and fracture healing.

GFRα2 regulates osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which GFRα2 regulates bone mass in BMSCs, we designed three experimental groups for comparative analysis and performed transcriptome sequencing: the control group (untreated BMSCs), the siRNA-NC group (BMSCs treated with siRNA-Negative Control), and the siRNA-Gfrα2 group (BMSCs with Gfrα2 knockdown via siRNA). After stringent data quality control, only one differentially expressed gene was identified between the control and siRNA-NC groups (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the siRNA-Gfrα2 group exhibited significant differences compared to the siRNA-NC group, with 352 upregulated and 531 downregulated genes (Fig. 2B). Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis confirmed that Gfrα2 knockdown significantly affected osteoblast differentiation processes and extracellular matrix formation-related pathways (Fig. 2C).

To further clarify the association between Gfrα2 knockdown and osteogenic differentiation, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment of osteogenic differentiation signature gene sets in the siRNA-Gfrα2 group (P < 0.001, FDR<0.001, ES = −0.559) (Fig. 2D). Heatmap analysis visually demonstrated altered expression of key osteogenic differentiation-related mRNAs (Fig. 2E). At the protein level, Western blot results showed a significant reduction in GFRα2 protein expression in the siRNA-Gfrα2 group, confirming efficient siRNA interference. Concurrently, the protein levels of key osteogenic markers (RUNX2, OSX, and COL1A1) were significantly downregulated, indicating that Gfrα2 knockdown suppresses the osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs (Fig. 2F and G). Combined with weakened trends in immunofluorescence staining, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity staining, and Alizarin Red S (ARS) mineralization nodule staining, these findings collectively validated that Gfrα2 silencing leads to significant reductions in the mRNA and protein levels of key osteogenic factors in BMSCs (Fig. 2H).

Having established that Gfrα2 knockdown inhibits the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs, we further verified its functional role through overexpression experiments (Fig. 3A–C). Compared to the vector-transfected control group, Gfrα2-overexpressing BMSCs exhibited significantly enhanced ALP staining intensity, and ARS staining demonstrated a marked increase in the number and area of mineralized nodules (Fig. 3J). Following osteogenic induction, the mRNA and protein expression levels of key osteogenic factors were significantly upregulated in the overexpression group. These results collectively demonstrate that GFRα2 significantly enhances the osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs by promoting the expression of osteogenesis-related genes (Fig. 3D–I). These findings provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying bone mass regulation and suggest GFRα2 as a potential therapeutic target for bone-related diseases.

GFRα2 drives osteogenesis via FTH 1-dependent iron homeostasis in BMSCs

To systematically elucidate the molecular mechanism by which GFRα2 regulates osteogenic differentiation, we conducted an in-depth analysis of RNA-seq data. Using a screening threshold of P < 0.05 and |log2FC|>1, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were visualized via volcano plots (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, the top 100 most significantly DEGs ranked by P-value were selected for heatmap construction (Fig. 4A). Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis of the DEG sets revealed that biological processes including osteoblast differentiation, extracellular matrix organization, and collagen-containing extracellular matrix were significantly enriched among downregulated genes, whereas upregulated genes were predominantly associated with immune system processes and extracellular space components (Fig. 4C and D).

To validate the reliability of transcriptome sequencing results, the top five downregulated genes ranked by P-value (Vegfd, Fth1, Igfbp3, C3, and Nid2) were selected for qPCR verification (Fig. 4E). Compared with the control group, the mRNA levels of these genes were significantly downregulated in the siRNA-Gfrα2 group. Western blot analysis further confirmed that the protein expression levels of the top two candidates, Vegfd and Fth1, were consistently and significantly reduced, demonstrating good concordance with transcriptomic data (Fig. 4F–G).

To identify key downstream proteins mediating GFRα2-regulated osteogenic differentiation, specific siRNAs targeting Vegfd, Fth1, Igfbp3, C3, and NidD2 (siRNA-Vegfd, siRNA-Fth1, etc.) were designed and transfected into BMSCs, followed by osteogenic induction culture (osteogenic differentiation medium containing dexamethasone, β-glycerophosphate, and vitamin C). After 10 days of culture, quantitative analysis via alizarin red staining demonstrated that the siRNA-Fth1 group exhibited significantly reduced mineralization nodule formation capacity, whereas knockdown of other genes did not show significant inhibitory effects on osteogenesis (Fig. 4G).

FTH 1, a core iron metabolism protein, regulates iron homeostasis and reactive oxygen species levels by chelating intracellular free iron ions. In BMSCs, FTH 1 expression positively correlates with osteogenic differentiation potential, potentially through mechanisms involving iron overload-induced oxidative stress suppression of osteogenic gene expression [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. WikiPathways enrichment analysis further confirmed that Gfrα2 knockdown significantly affected the iron homeostasis pathway (Fig. 4I and J). Consistent with the proposed mechanism, further experiments revealed that siRNA-mediated knockdown of Gfrα2 significantly increased intracellular labile iron levels and ROS production. These changes were highly similar to those observed following Fth1 knockdown. Moreover, treatment with an iron chelator effectively reversed these effects, resulting in reduced levels of both Fe2+ and ROS. Collectively, these findings indicate that GFRa2-mediated osteogenic differentiation is closely associated with FTH 1-dependent iron homeostasis.

PI3K/AKT signaling mediates GFRα2-Induced FTH1 expression and osteogenic differentiation

To verify whether GFRα2 mediates osteogenic differentiation through FTH1, an ex vivo functional rescue experiment was designed. BMSCs were divided into: control group (transfected with siRNA-NC + OE-NC plasmids), experimental group A (transfected with OE-Gfrα2 plasmids + siRNA-NC), and experimental group B (transfected with OE-Gfrα2 plasmids + siRNA-Fth1) (Fig. 5A and B). After 4 days of osteogenic induction, experimental group A showed significantly higher protein expression of osteogenic markers, ALP activity, and ARS-quantified mineralization nodules compared to controls. These indicators in experimental group B reverted to control levels due to Fth1-specific knockdown, with qPCR results validating this trend at the mRNA level (Fig. 5A–E). These findings together indicate that FTH 1 serves as a critical downstream effector of GFRα2 in regulating osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.

We next sought to identify the downstream signaling pathways governed by GFRα2. WikiPathway pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 4H), suggesting its key involvement in GFRα2-mediated biological processes. To validate this finding, we examined the activation status of the PI3K/Akt pathway and its downstream targets via Western blot. Gfra2 overexpression markedly increased the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and Akt, which was accompanied by elevated expression of FTH 1 and the key osteogenic transcription factor RUNX2. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of PI3K with LY294002 effectively reversed these changes, suppressing PI3K/Akt activation and downregulating both FTH 1 and RUNX2 expression in GFRα2 overexpressing cells(Fig. 5F).

Taken together, these results, supported by both transcriptomic enrichment and pharmacological validation, demonstrate that GFRα2 promotes osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs, at least in part, through the canonical PI3K/Akt/FTH1 signaling axis.

GFRα2 in BMSCs drives angiogenesis via VEGFD secretion

Based on GO functional enrichment analysis, the differentially expressed genes were significantly associated with angiogenesis and artery morphogenesis (Fig. 4C). To investigate this, a Transwell co-culture model was established. BMSCs were divided into three groups: PBS-treated, transfected with OE-NC plasmid, and transfected with OE-Gfrα2 plasmid (Fig. 5F), and seeded in the upper chamber. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC-C) [23] were seeded in the lower chamber. Tubule formation and scratch wound healing assays were performed to observe and quantitatively analyze the tubulogenic and migratory capacities of HUVEC-C in the co-culture system, aiming to validate the effect of differential GFRα2 expression in BMSCs on vascular formation. The experimental results demonstrated that after 24 h of co-culture, the complexity of the tubule network formed by HUVEC-C in the OE-Gfrα2 group was significantly increased compared to the control groups (Fig. 5G and 6I). The scratch wound healing assay further confirmed an enhanced cell migration rate in the OE-Gfrα2 group (Fig. 5H). For VEGFD, the most significantly differentially expressed gene, ELISA revealed the concentrations of VEGFD in the culture media of the upper and lower chambers in the OE-Gfrα2 group, respectively, which were significantly higher than those in the OE-NC and PBS groups (Fig. 5I). Furthermore, in our in vivo experiments, we observed that the OE-GFRα2 group exhibited more abundant intraosseous blood vessels(Fig. 6H). These findings suggest that Gfrα2 overexpression promotes VEGFD secretion by BMSCs, thereby enhancing angiogenic capacity.

GFRα2 ameliorates OP via BMSC osteogenic promotion and angiogenesis

To determine the potential of GFRα2 in regulating bone health, we constructed Gfrα2 overexpression plasmids and siRNA-Gfrα2, which were delivered to mice via tail vein injection as a mixture with transfection in vivo reagents. The experiment comprised six groups (n = 5 per group, totaling 30 mice): OVX + siRNA-Gfrα2, OVX + siRNA-NC (negative control), OVX + OE-Gfrα2 (overexpression), OVX + OE-NC, OVX (ovariectomized), and SHAM (sham-operated) (Fig. 6A). Mice were subjected to biweekly tail vein injections for 4 weeks. At the study endpoint, micro-CT imaging was performed to analyze the subchondral trabecular bone of the right femoral growth plate, with 3D reconstructions generated for qualitative assessment of bone mass and microarchitecture. Quantitative analysis of trabecular parameters was conducted (Fig. 6B).

Compared with the SHAM group, OVX mice exhibited osteoporotic alterations in femoral bone mass and microstructure, characterized by significant reductions in bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), connectivity density (Conn.D), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th). Notably, OVX + siRNA-Gfrα2 mice demonstrated further deterioration in these indices. In contrast, OVX + OE-Gfrα2 mice showed attenuated trabecular bone loss and increased bone mass relative to the OVX + OE-NC group, with 3D analyses confirming improvements in Tb.Sp (trabecular separation), BV/TV, and Tb.N (Fig. 6C).

For in-depth evaluation, histological assessments were performed using H&E staining, and calcein labeling (Fig. 6F and G). These results corroborated the enhanced bone mass and microstructure in the OVX + OE-Gfrα2 group, revealing greater new bone formation compared to OVX + OE-NC. Meanwhile, serological tests showed that osteogenesis-related indicators significantly increased in the OVX + OE-Gfrα2 group, while there was no significant difference in bone resorption indicators (Fig. 6I). Concurrently, immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry confirmed markedly elevated GFRα2 expression in BMSCs of the OVX + OE-Gfrα2 group (Fig. 6D and E).

In summary, our in vivo study demonstrates that GFRα2 effectively ameliorates postmenopausal OP by promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Our data identify a novel therapeutic target for OP-related disorders, offering translational relevance.

Discussion

Bone, as a dynamically remodeling organ, undergoes a tightly regulated process mediated by osteoclast-driven bone resorption and osteoblast-driven bone formation, with the balance between these processes being fundamental to maintaining skeletal health [[24], [25], [26]]. Although current therapeutic strategies primarily target osteoblasts and osteoclasts to restore bone homeostasis, the need for novel treatment approaches remains critical [27,28].

In this study, we identified a unique subpopulation of BMSCs with high Gfrα2 expression in a TBI-fracture comorbidity mouse model, which exhibited remarkable osteogenic differentiation potential. Through multifaceted investigations, including functional cell assays, molecular mechanism exploration, and animal model validation, we demonstrated that GFRα2 exerts a dual regulatory role in BMSCs by simultaneously promoting osteogenic differentiation and angiogenesis, thereby presenting a promising therapeutic target for OP.

In studies investigating the mechanisms underlying TBI-enhanced fracture healing, Thompson et al. reported that “extracellular vesicles (EVs) post-TBI promote bone repair,” while others attributed this phenomenon to β2-adrenergic receptor-mediated sympathetic signaling activation. However, the precise molecular mechanisms governing its role in bone metabolism and pathology remain to be elucidated. Notably, none of these studies have thoroughly explored post-TBI alterations in BMSCs. Our findings in a TBI-fracture comorbidity mouse model revealed sustained high GFRα2 expression in BMSCs, and its downregulation significantly impaired their osteogenic differentiation capacity. These observations suggest that GFRα2 expression in bone tissue is associated with bone formation and may, to some extent, reflect osteogenic potential [29,30]. Nevertheless, In most existing studies, these pathways are viewed as acting independently and in parallel to promote osteogenesis and bone repair. However, based on our identification of the GFRα2-FTH 1/VEGFD axis as a key regulator of TBI-enhanced bone formation, we propose that these mechanisms may not be mutually exclusive. They could operate either as parallel complementary pathways or as a hierarchical cascade in which sympathetic activation and EVs act upstream to regulate GFRα2 expression in BMSCs. We therefore hypothesize a dual-relationship: on one hand, sympathetic signaling, EVs, and the axis may function as parallel pathways that cooperatively enhance osteogenesis and angiogenesis. On the other hand, it is equally plausible that TBI-induced sympathetic excitation and EV secretion serve as upstream triggers that upregulate GFRα2 in BMSCs, thereby activating the downstream FTH 1-iron homeostasis and VEGFD-angiogenesis programs to amplify bone regeneration. Elucidating these potential relationships will be highly meaningful for future studies.

In this scenario, the GFRα2-FTH 1/VEGFD axis may act either as an independent parallel module or as a core downstream effector that integrates multiple pro-osteogenic signals. This concept provides a more comprehensive framework for understanding the complex network driving accelerated bone healing after TBI. Previous studies have primarily localized GFRα2 to the nervous system, where it functions as a critical coreceptor for neurotrophic factors in both central (cerebral cortex, hippocampus, spinal cord) and peripheral (sensory neurons, sympathetic neurons) neural tissues. By binding to GDNF family ligands, GFRα2 activates the RET receptor tyrosine kinase signaling pathway, playing a central role in neural differentiation, survival, and related physiological processes [31]. This mechanism underscores its significance in neurodevelopmental research and interventions for neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson's disease. Intriguingly, Gadomski et al. identified stable GFRα2 expression in osteocytes, with GFRα2-deficient mice exhibiting a pronounced low-bone-mass phenotype. Our study extends the functional scope of GFRα2 to BMSCs and delineates its molecular mechanism in synergistically driving osteogenesis and angiogenesis via the FTH 1-iron homeostasis and VEGFD-angiogenesis pathways.

Given the established roles of GFRα2 in both neural and osteogenic differentiation, as well as its potential therapeutic value in neurodegenerative diseases, we further explored its applicability in treating skeletal degenerative disorders. In vivo injection of OE-GFRα2 plasmids significantly elevated GFRα2 expression and effectively improved bone mass. However, while this approach enhances target protein expression in bone tissue, its targeting specificity remains limited. Thus, future efforts should focus on developing more precise drug delivery systems to minimize complications. Additionally, comprehensive long-term safety assessments are necessary to evaluate whether sustained systemic GFRα2 activation may induce ectopic calcification or vascular abnormalities. Notably, angiogenesis-osteogenesis coupling represents a core biological process in skeletal development and regeneration. Previous studies have demonstrated that factors secreted by the bone microenvironment, such as PDGF, VEGF, and EGFL, mediate the interactive regulation between blood vessels and bone. Furthermore, pericytes play a crucial role in the process of angiogenesis, and their biological characteristics are highly similar to those of the cells investigated in the present study [32,33].Three distinct pericyte subtypes (NG2+, CD146+, and PDGFR-β+) are not only involved in angiogenesis, vascular maturation, and the maintenance of vascular homeostasis but also possess multilineage differentiation potential toward osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic lineages. Given the substantial biological similarities between these pericyte subtypes and the cells used in this study, we hypothesize that GFRα2 may exert a synergistic regulatory role in pericyte function. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the specific regulatory mechanisms and modes of action involved [34].

In summary, Our data identify a subpopulation of BMSCs with high GFRα2 expression in a TBI-fracture comorbidity model, highlighting GFRα2's dual role in promoting osteogenic differentiation via the FTH 1-iron homeostasis pathway and angiogenesis through the VEGFD pathway. These findings extend GFRα2's known functions beyond the nervous system to skeletal biology, establishing it as a promising therapeutic target for OP, as evidenced by improved bone mass following in vivo OE-GFRα2 plasmid injection. While our work links sustained GFRα2 expression in BMSCs to osteogenic potential, complementing previously described TBI-healing mechanisms involving EVs and sympathetic signaling, the upstream regulators and precise pathway connections require further elucidation. Challenges include enhancing delivery specificity and long-term safety assessments for ectopic calcification/vascular risks. These insights bridge neural and skeletal biology, offering new directions for skeletal degenerative disorders.

Materials and methods

Animal models and procedures

TBI + fracture model mice

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) was induced using a controlled cortical impact (CCI) device. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and secured in a stereotaxic frame. After exposing the skull, a 5-mm-diameter craniotomy was performed on the left hemisphere to accommodate a 3-mm-deep T-shaped impactor tip. A 20-g weight was then dropped from a height of 20 cm onto the target area. Finally, the incision was closed with sutures.

Ovariectomized (OVX) surgery was performed on 4-month-old female C57BL/6J mice to establish an OP model, while sham surgery was conducted in the control group.

Drug intervention

On postoperative day 22, tail vein injections were administered to the following groups: OVX + siRNA-Gfrα2 group, OVX + siRNA-NC group, OVX + OE-Gfrα2 group, OVX + OE-NC group. The injected solution consisted of a mixture of siRNA/plasmid and an in vivo transfection reagent (Invigentech, America).

Micro-CT analysis

At the end of the experiment, the mice were euthanized, and bone tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Subsequently, the femurs were scanned using a micro-CT system (Skyscan system, Bruker, Belgium) with an isotropic resolution of 9 μm. Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of the bone tissue was performed using CTvol software. The scanned data were analyzed and quantified using CTan software to further evaluate parameters such as bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV), and trabecular number (Tb.N), thereby assessing changes in bone microstructure.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Detection of GFRα2 Expression and Localization in Mouse Bone Tissue.

Histological Staining of Bone Tissue.

Bone samples were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 24 h. Decalcification was performed using 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution at room temperature (25°C) for 3 weeks. After decalcification, the joint tissues were embedded in paraffin and subjected to conventional histological analysis with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. To evaluate fracture healing, sections were stained with Safranin O/Fast Green. Stained sections were observed and imaged using an Olympus DP70 microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

Double calcein labeling

Ten days and three days prior to euthanasia, mice from different groups were intraperitoneally injected with 0.1% calcein (20 mg/kg, Sigma, USA) at a 7-day interval. Femurs were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 days. After dehydration, the samples were embedded and sectioned. Double calcein labeling was detected using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Jena, Germany).

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized by heating at 60°C for 15 min, followed by rehydration through a series of xylene and graded alcohol washes. For antigen retrieval, heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) was performed using a vegetable steamer: slides were incubated in 10 mmol/L citrate buffer at 60°C for 40 min, followed by PBS washes. Cell permeabilization was achieved by incubating the sections in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min at room temperature. Subsequently, appropriate primary antibodies (see Table S1) were applied and incubated overnight. The next day, corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies and DAPI were added, and samples were observed under a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Jena, Germany).

Cell experiments

Isolation and culture of BMSCs

The femur and tibia of mice were harvested, and the bone marrow cavity was flushed to prepare a single-cell suspension, which was then filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. The isolated cells were seeded in T25 culture flasks, and passage 3-4 cells were used for subsequent experiments.

Osteogenic differentiation assay

For osteogenic induction, after 12 h of special cell treatment, the culture medium was replaced with differentiation medium (containing β-glycerophosphate, ascorbate-2-phosphate, and dexamethasone). Mineralized nodules were detected by Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining, and osteogenic genes (e.g., Runx2, Bglap) were analyzed via qRT-PCR.

Transient transfection assay

For transient transfection, 200 μL of Opti-MEM medium (Gibco) was added to each of two sterile 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes labeled Tube A and Tube B. Transfection reagent was added to Tube A, while the transfection product (siRNA, GenePharma) was added to Tube B, followed by gentle mixing for 5 min to ensure thorough dispersion. Subsequently, the dispersed transfection product from Tube B was slowly added to the transfection reagent in Tube A, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min with gentle agitation. Within 60 min, the mixed solution was added dropwise to the target cells, which had been pre-cultured in 2 mL of high-glucose DMEM medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS). For RNA-based experiments, transfection was performed for 24 h, whereas for protein-based experiments, transfection lasted 72 h. GP-transfect-Mate transfection reagent (GenePharma) was used at 6.5 μL per tube for primary human chondrocytes, with 9 μL of siRNA. The corresponding sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Non-contact coculture model

A Transwell system (0.4 μm pore size) was employed to coculture BMSCs (upper chamber) with HUVECs (lower chamber) for investigating intercellular signaling.

Molecular biology and proteomics methods

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates from tissues or cells were prepared using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in TBS-T containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). After TBST washes, membranes were incubated for 2 h with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG or anti-mouse IgG. Immunocomplexes were visualized using an ECL detection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and detected via chemiluminescence.

Total RNA Extraction, cDNA Synthesis, and Quantitative Reverse Transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from tissues or cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA quantity and quality were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit with RNase Inhibitor (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). For mRNA analysis, RT-qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 12K Flex System (Applied Biosystems) using gene-specific primers and SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). GAPDH served as the normalization control. Data were analyzed via the comparative Ct (ΔΔCt) method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Tube formation assay

The effect of conditioned medium on endothelial progenitor cell (EPC) tube formation was evaluated using Matrigel matrix (Corning Life Sciences). Prior to cell seeding, Matrigel was applied to wells and polymerized at 37°C for 30 min. HUVECs were plated on polymerized Matrigel and incubated for 12 h at 37°C in respective conditioned media. Tube length was examined under an optical microscope and quantified using ImageJ software from three wells per group across six independent replicates.

ELISA

Serum levels of procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (PINP), C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX), and VEGF-D in culture media were quantified using ELISA kits (Elabscience, China) per manufacturer's protocols.

Immunofluorescence staining

On day 1, sterile coverslips (pre-treated with 75% ethanol and air-dried) were placed in 24-well plates. Cells were seeded and cultured overnight until 30-60% confluency. After experimental treatments, culture medium was aspirated, and cells were washed twice with PBS. Fixation was performed at room temperature for 15 min, followed by three 5-min PBS washes (with gentle shaking). Permeabilization was achieved using 0.2% Triton X-100 (5 min, RT), followed by three PBS washes. Blocking was performed for 60 min, after which primary antibodies were applied (60 min at RT or overnight at 4°C with shaking). On day 2, primary antibodies were removed, and cells were washed three times with PBS. Fluorescent secondary antibodies were incubated for 40 min at RT (light-protected, with shaking). After DAPI counterstaining (5 min, RT) and four PBS washes, coverslips were mounted using antifade mounting medium. Images were acquired via fluorescence microscopy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using data expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments, as detailed in the figure legends. For pairwise comparisons, unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests were employed. When comparing more than two groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for multiple-group comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using Prism software (version 9.0, GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were repeated at least three times, with representative results presented.
