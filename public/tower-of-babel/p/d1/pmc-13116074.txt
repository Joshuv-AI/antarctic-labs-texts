Age‐Related Differences in Short Term Spatial Memory in Rhesus Macaques
Authors: Lili R. Lurye, Daniel L. Smith, William A. Liguore, Steven G. Kohama, Alison R. Weiss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrevious studies revealed deficits in working memory among old macaques (> 20 years old) compared to younger macaques (< 14 years old) but have not thoroughly assessed these metrics in middle‐aged macaques (14–19 years old). Here, we sought to develop a high‐throughput adaptation of the 2‐choice delayed response task of spatial working memory suitable to query middle‐aged macaques in comparison to young‐adult and very old rhesus macaques. We administered our manual 3‐choice delayed response task to male and female monkeys ranging in age from 7 to 27 years, and included 3 choice locations to avoid floor effects and improve task sensitivity. Critically, Acquisition was limited to 15 blocks of 30 trials and used a short 1‐second delay. Spatial memory was then probed using variable delays of 5, 10, 30, and 60 s. Retrieval accuracy (percent correct) significantly decreased with increasing delay length and age, as expected. In addition, we developed a new method to quantify the temporal and spatial distribution of responses and assess perseverative responding. The results identified age‐dependent differences in memory, learning and perseveration, suggesting a decline in cognitive flexibility in late adulthood contributed to the memory deficits measured here. This task refinement therefore provides a novel approach to quantify errors in this age‐sensitive short‐term spatial memory task.

Introduction

The aging population in the United States is growing exponentially. By 2050, the number of people in the United States aged 65 and older is projected to reach 85.5 million (US Census Bureau 2010). Normal aging is characterized by measurable declines across cognitive domains including memory, attention, executive function, and learning (Jahn 2013; Murman 2015). Age‐associated neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD), accelerate these changes due to the accumulation of neuropathology (amyloid beta plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles) that impact brain function, particularly affecting short‐term memory and learning abilities (Grober and Kawas 1997). Due to the growing aging population, the prevalence of ADRD is projected to increase by 128% in the United States between 2020 and 2060 (Rajan et al. 2021). It is well known that this increase will pose a strain on the economy and health care systems (Li et al. 2022; Takizawa et al. 2015). Furthering research on cognitive aging is vital for mitigating the burden on our medical system and improving the health of an aging population. Yet, a notable challenge for studies in human populations pertains to the need for long‐term studies, spanning multiple decades, to capture the progression of ADRD and normative aging longitudinally from midlife to advanced old age.

Comparative research on nonhuman primates (NHPs) such as marmosets, great apes and macaques has informed our understanding of cognitive and biological aging processes (De Castro and Girard 2021; Glavis‐Bloom et al. 2022; Lacreuse et al. 2020; Chiou et al. 2020). More specifically, the rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta) is a prime model for aging due to their phylogenetic similarity to humans and their accelerated aging trajectory, typically living ~25 years in laboratory environments (Huber et al. 2025). Rhesus macaques also share a variety of lifespan characteristics with humans such as a prolonged adolescence, a similar endocrine system and reproductive anatomy, and the spontaneous development of age‐related brain volumetric loss, increased white matter diffusivity, neuroinflammation, and mild amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques (Alldritt et al. 2024; Appleman et al. 2023; Darusman et al. 2014; Darusman et al. 2013; Dash et al. 2023; Rapp and Amaral 1989; Rapp et al. 1997; Shamy et al. 2011; Simmons 2016; Stonebarger et al. 2021; Weiss et al. 2025). These characteristics establish the rhesus macaque as an indispensable model for aging research.

Given that age‐related cognitive impairment is one of the most commonly reported factors impacting quality of life, macaques' proficiency in executing complex cognitive tasks dependent on the prefrontal cortex (PFC) further position them as an ideal model for aging research. Previous investigations have documented that old macaques suffer from deficits in memory and other cognitive functions (Baxter et al. 2023; Gray and Barnes 2019; Rapp and Amaral 1989). In the context of aging, old macaques (aged 20 years and over) display significant deficits in short‐term recognition memory and memory capacity, commonly assessed using delayed recognition tasks (Moss et al. 1997; Vanderlip et al. 2025).

The spatial delayed response (SDR) task has a rich history in the world of comparative psychology and neuroscience, with its earliest applications dating back to the start of the 20th century (Hunter 1914; Tinklepaugh 1932; Meyer et al. 1951; Pribram et al. 1952; Riopelle 1959; McDowell et al. 1960). SDR tasks have captured age‐related differences in macaques; aged macaques have been found to have lower retrieval accuracy (% correct) compared to younger subjects, particularly during longer delays (Bachevalier et al. 1991; Baxter et al. 2023; Darusman et al. 2014; Rapp and Amaral 1989). These findings parallel evidence from human studies (from participants ranging between 17 and 80 years old), which found that performance was worse in older adults on a SDR task (Lyons‐Warren et al. 2004). SDR tasks are of interest in this context for their use in measuring short‐term spatial memory, which is highly impacted in ADRD (Rodriguez and Paule 2009). Furthermore, performance on versions of the SDR task have been assessed in conjunction with age‐associated changes in physical function, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) volume, and synaptic density, along with hormone replacement therapy in aged rhesus macaques (Dumitriu et al. 2010; Motley et al. 2018; Shamy et al. 2011) and aged vervet monkeys (Frye et al. 2021). Taken together, these and other studies provided a foundation for the utility of the SDR task in understanding age‐related memory impairment.

However, investigating cognitive aging in NHPs poses unique challenges, particularly due to the logistical challenges and costs associated with managing this species, leading to relatively limited sample sizes in many studies. To overcome these limitations, cognitive tasks should be carefully designed to meet several criteria. First, they must be sensitive to age‐related changes to effectively measure memory across lifespan. Second, they must be high throughput enough to obtain substantial data sets, allowing for greater numbers of animals to be screened in a reasonable timeframe. Lastly, they should measure performance without first training the animals to reach an arbitrary performance criterion, such as scoring 80% accuracy, to reduce attrition and better control against unequal opportunities for practice. This approach enables the tracking of age‐related differences in acquiring the task, and prevents overtraining. Here, we report a variation of the classic SDR task specifically tailored to address these challenges in studying cognitive aging in NHPs.

Description

Task

Building on paradigms used in previous research, we developed new behavioral metrics to assess not only retrieval accuracy (% correct) but also the trajectories of learning and patterns of errors. To commence a trial, the experimenter raised an opaque slide and baited the left, right, or center well with a food reward in view of the subject. Well location was predetermined in a pseudorandomized sequence, so that each well was baited an equal number of times. The opaque slide was then lowered to obstruct the subject's view for 1, 5, 10, 30, or 60‐seconds depending on the test phase (preventing visual access), and subsequently re‐opened to allow the animal to select one well by moving the plaque to reveal its contents. If an animal uncovered more than one well before the experimenter could lower the slide, the first well the animal uncovered was noted. If the animal did not respond within 1 min, that trial was considered a “time‐out,” marked as omitted, and the experimenter proceeded to the next trial.

To link this work with the well‐established body of SDR literature that focuses on aging (reviewed in Table 1), our primary dependent variable was retrieval accuracy (% correct). In addition, several novel behavioral measures were calculated for both the Acquisition and Performance phase (defined in Table 2): 1) a Well Preference Index (WPI) was calculated to measure the degree of spatial bias in each animal's choices, regardless of accuracy, by quantifying how responses deviated from an equal distribution across the three possible locations (left, center, right). WPI scores could range from 0 to 133, where 0 indicates perfectly balanced responding (i.e., equal use of all wells) and 133 indicates exclusive preference for a single location; 2) the percentage of perseverative errors, defined as two consecutive incorrect responses in the same location (“lose‐stay” strategy); 3) “time‐outs,” which occurred when an animal did not make a choice within 1 min; and 4) “lagged correct” responses, which captured another form of perseveration, occurred when an animal incorrectly chose a well that was the correct location in the preceding trial (“win‐stay” strategy). In addition to assessing memory, this task served as a self‐paced and enriching activity for the animals by stimulating foraging instincts. Therefore, if an animal appeared to become satiated or disengaged with the task based on experimenter judgement, testing was halted and continued the following day.

Example

Animals

Twenty‐one rhesus macaques (11 females, 10 males), aged 7‐27 years, participated in this study. Subject demographic information is detailed in Table 3. All animals resided at the Oregon National Primate Research Center (ONPRC) where they were singly housed indoors with visual access to other rhesus macaques, or housed with continuous full contact, protected contact, or intermittent contact with a socially compatible partner when available. Animals were fed a standard diet of LabDiet formula 5000 primate chow, received fresh fruit or vegetable enrichment daily, and water was provided ad libitum. No food restriction was involved, and subjects' AM meals were provided when the animal returned from completing cognitive tasks each morning. The animals in this data set have participated in previous experiments, but all were naïve to cognitive tasks, including the one in the present study.

Apparatus

Animals were transported from their home housing environment to a dedicated behavioral testing space. Testing took place in a Wisconsin General Test Apparatus (WGTA), a figure of the apparatus can be seen in Weiss et al. (2022) figure 1A). A white noise machine was used to obscure any external sounds. The cognitive testing apparatus consisted of an enclosure (0.7 × 0.6 × 0.9 m) modified with an opaque screen (43 × 27 cm) that could be raised and lowered via a pulley system. Attached to the apparatus behind the screen was a plexiglass tray (40 × 15 cm) consisting of three wells (6.3 cm in diameter) spaced 9 cm apart with moveable covers tethered to the tray by metal cording. The well tray was secured to the WGTA with wingnuts and could be easily removed for cleaning purposes.

Acclimation

Prior to testing, animals were familiarized with test apparatus over five to eight daily sessions. During acclimation, the experimenter presented high value treats such as mini‐M&Ms, grapes (quartered), or jellybeans into the food tray, only concealing the wells partially so the animal could see a food reward. The animal was free to manipulate the plaques and explore the wells to retrieve food rewards. This was repeated 30 times each session. In subsequent acclimation sessions, wells were covered completely after being baited, and the animal was still free to search all the wells. Introduction of the moving door was incremental: first, the door was lowered halfway each trial for one session. If the animal continued to take treats from the wells, the door was fully lowered (for less than 1 s) during the next session. Task acquisition concluded upon habituation to the moving door and covered wells, signified by the animal continuing to take food rewards.

Acquisition Phase

Data collected during the Acquisition phase was used to assess learning. During this phase, a 1‐second delay was implemented after baiting the well. The two wells that were not baited remained covered for the entirety of the trial and were never manipulated by the experimenter. For every trial, the experimenters had a visual target centrally placed above the eyeline of the monkeys, so as to prevent potential visual cueing. The first well that the animal uncovered was recorded. If the subject did not select the baited well, the slide was lowered, and the experimenter recorded the location chosen. All animals completed a total of 450 trials structured to take place in sessions of 30 trials with the left, center, and right wells each baited 10 times in a predetermined pseudorandom sequence over 15 sessions. However, sessions occasionally ended early if an animal appeared satiated or exhibited signs of frustration and did not engage with the task. In such instances, animals required more than 15 sessions to complete 450 trials. For this reason, we will refer to sets of 30 trials as “blocks” rather than sessions.

Performance Phase

Upon completion of the Acquisition phase, memory was probed using identical procedures but instead variable delays of 5, 10, 30, and 60 s. Delay duration and reward placement in the center, left, and right wells were equally distributed in a pseudorandom sequence in each block, which consisted of 3 sessions with 32 trials (for a total of 96 trials). Like Acquisition, the first well that the animal uncovered was recorded, and trials were marked as omitted if the animal did not respond within 1 min. Data from the Performance phase was combined across blocks, such that the total number of trials from each delay were analyzed together (with 24 trials in total at each delay).

Data Analysis

Linear mixed models were fit to examine the effect of age and block number, and their interaction, on each behavioral measure (retrieval accuracy, WPI, lagged correct responses, perseverative errors, and trials omitted). The same analytic approached were applied to both the Acquisition and Performance phases. In each of these models, age, delay duration, and block number were entered as fixed effects and continuous variables. When appropriate according to variance estimates, a random intercept for each subject was included to model subject‐specific baseline values for each behavioral measure. Detailed results of linear mixed models are reported in Supporting Information Tables S11 and S12.

All statistical analysis was performed using R Studio software (Version 2024.04.2 + 764) (R Studio Team, 2022). All data used in these analyses are reported in Supporting Information Tables S1–S12, and the R Studio markdown file is provided in Supporting Information. All figures were generated using R Studio software and include fit lines and 95% confidence intervals.

Results

Acquisition—Retrieval Accuracy

The effect of age was found to be significant, as each additional year of age led to a reduction in retrieval accuracy (b = −0.88, SE = 0.27, t(30.55) = − 3.24, p = 0.003). Block was not a significant predictor of retrieval accuracy, though a slight positive slope trended toward significance (b = 0.54, SE = 0.31, t(292) = 1.76, p = 0.079) (Figure 1), suggesting an upward trend in retrieval accuracy as each block progressed. The age x block interaction did not yield significance (b = 0.03, SE = 0.02, t(292) = 1.76, p = 0.080).

Scatterplot showing the relationship between age and retrieval accuracy in Acquisition. Each point is averaged across all blocks. Solid line indicates linear regression fit with 95% confidence interval. These results demonstrate poorer spatial memory in older monkeys, measured by the total percent correct.

Acquisition—Well Preference Index (WPI)

A significant main effect of age was found (b = 1.13, SE = 0.26, t(70.01) = 4.32, p < 0.001), such that older subjects had higher spatial bias. The main effect of block was not significant (b = −0.19, SE = 0.45, t(292) = − 0.42, p = 0.671). Importantly, the age x block interaction was significant (b = −0.05, SE = 0.02, t(292) = − 2.24, p = 0.026), suggesting that age‐related differences in spatial bias diminished across blocks (Figure 2).

Scatterplots showing the relationship between age and WPI at each block in Acquisition. Solid lines indicate linear regression fit with 95% confidence interval. Statistical models include all 15 blocks, but for the ease of visualization blocks were grouped by 5. Overall, older subjects had higher spatial biases across blocks.

Acquisition—Perseverative Errors

There was a significant main effect of age, indicating that older animals made more perseverative errors (b = 0.70, SE = 0.20, t(141.68) = 3.45, p < 0.001). The main effect of block was not significant (b = 0.20, SE = 0.41, t(292) = 0.50, p = 0.616) suggesting that perseveration was relatively stable across blocks. The age x block interaction was significant (b = −0.04, SE = 0.02, t(292) = − 2.08, p = 0.038), indicating that age‐related differences in perseverative errors decreased across blocks (Figure 3).

Scatterplots showing the relationship between age and perseverative errors at each block in Acquisition. Solid lines indicate linear regression fit with 95% confidence interval. Statistical models include all 15 blocks, but for the ease of visualization blocks were grouped by 5. Older animals made more perseverative errors than younger animals, but made fewer as blocks progressed.

Acquisition—Timeouts

The mixed‐effects model predicting trials omitted revealed no significant main effects of age (b = 0.14, SE = 0.10, t(27.91) = 1.45, p = 0.159) or block (b = 0.07, SE = 0.10, t(292) = 0.64, p = 0.522), and no age x block interaction (b = −0.01, SE = 0.01, t(292) = − 1.07, p = 0.286) (Figure 4).

Scatterplot showing the relationship between age and timeout trials in Acquisition. Each point is averaged across all blocks. Solid line indicates linear regression fit with 95% confidence interval. There were no significant main effects of age nor block on the number of timeout trials.

Acquisition—Lagged Correct

There was no significant main effect of age on lagged correct responses (b = −0.85, SE = 0.61, t(149.89) = − 1.38, p = 0.169). The main effect of block was significant (b = −2.96, SE = 1.25, t(292) = − 2.38, p = 0.018), pointing to a decrease in lagged correct responses as blocks progressed. Additionally, the age x block interaction was significant (b = 0.15, SE = 0.06, t(292) = 2.33, p = 0.021), suggesting that while lagged correct responses declined across blocks, this decline was attenuated in older animals (Figure 5).

Scatterplots showing the relationship between age and lagged correct responses at each block in Acquisition. Solid lines indicate linear regression fit with 95% confidence interval. Statistical models include all 15 blocks, but for the ease of visualization blocks were grouped by 5. There was no significant main effect of age or block, but younger animals made less lagged correct responses over time.

Performance—Retrieval Accuracy

Retrieval accuracy was lower in older animals (b = − 2.10, SE = 0.33, t(60.77) = − 6.30, p < 0.001), indicating overall poorer memory performance in older monkeys. Retrieval accuracy also declined as the delay increased from 5 to 60‐seconds (b = −1.04, SE = 0.18, t(61) = − 5.78, p < 0.001), suggesting greater forgetting at longer retention intervals. The age x delay interaction was significant as well (b = 0.02, SE = 0.01, t(61) = 2.17, p = 0.034), indicating that age‐related differences in retrieval accuracy were attenuated when delay duration increased (Figure 6).

Scatter plot showing retrieval accuracy by age in Performance, with data points and linear regression lines grouped by delay intervals (5, 10, 30, and 60 s). Shaded areas indicate 95% confidence interval. All animals performed worse as delays increased, and age‐related differences in retrieval accuracy became less evident with delay increases.

Performance—Well Preference Index (WPI)

There was a significant main effect of age on WPI (b = 1.76, SE = 0.61, t(36.32) = 2.89, p = 0.007), indicating stronger spatial bias in older monkeys (Figure 7). WPI also significantly increased as delays lengthened (b = 0.50, SE = 0.25, t(61) = 2.03, p = 0.047) (Figure 8). The age x delay interaction was not significant (b = −0.00, SE = 0.01, t(61) = − 0.39, p = 0.698).

Comparison and Critique

Previous research has demonstrated that old macaques display deficits in short‐term working memory during SDR tasks (Bachevalier et al. 1991; Baxter et al. 2023; Darusman et al. 2014; Rapp and Amaral 1989). Although this research clearly demonstrates impaired short‐term spatial memory in old macaques (> 20 years old) compared to young macaques (7–13 years), there has yet to be a robust sample of middle‐aged (14–19 years) animals included in delayed‐response experiments that can provide insight into learning and memory during mid‐life. To address this gap, our study deliberately included animals in this age range (14–19 years old). As expected, we found overall poorer memory in older animals in both Acquisition and Performance, reflected by lower retrieval accuracy. Further, our novel behavior measures allowed us to examine the type of errors animals made. Most notably, the number of perseverative errors (“lose‐stay” strategy) and spatial biases (quantified by the WPI) were significantly higher in older animals during the Acquisition and Performance phases. The tendency to continuously select a previously correct response, or to seemingly “default” to one position may be indicative of impaired cognitive flexibility. Previous studies have reported monkeys over the age of 12 demonstrate more perseverative responding than young‐adult monkeys in set‐shifting tasks (Moore et al. 2006). Older animals had slightly more timeout trials in both phases, but this age effect did not reach significance and was driven by a small subset of subjects.

Furthermore, data relating to the Acquisition (learning) of the delayed response task has been reported minimally and primarily focused only on the number of trials to reach a criterion, potentially overlooking vital information related to the types of errors animals make. Here, all animals performed an equal number of trials during the Acquisition phase, allowing the measurement of age‐related differences in learning, and preventing overtraining. Throughout blocks in Acquisition, the effect of age on perseverative responding and spatial biases decreased significantly. This reflects older monkeys' spared abilities to learn the task and optimize their strategies with repeated opportunities for practice. However, reductions in lagged correct responses across blocks were attenuated in older animals, suggesting potential deficits in cognitive flexibility due to inability to disengage from previously reinforced responses.

The Performance phase probed spatial memory by increasing the time between encoding and retrieval. As expected, longer delays significantly attenuated retrieval accuracy, as did older age. However, age‐related differences in retrieval accuracy were most pronounced at shorter delays and became less distinct as memory demands increased, potentially due to floor effects impacting young and older animals equally. Interestingly, younger monkeys adhered more to the “win‐stay” strategy by choosing the previously reinforced response (measured by lagged correct responses), whereas older monkeys made more “lose‐stay” perseverative errors, which may represent changes in heuristic strategies during aging.

Previous experiments using the SDR task typically provide two choices for the subject to select between, which also creates potential floor effects limiting variability in performance, and thus reducing sensitivity to detect age‐related differences. Some of the limitations in these prior studies are summarized in Table 1, highlighting gaps in the existing literature and underscoring the need for more comprehensive data on how learning and memory processes unfold across the lifespan, particularly during middle age.

The task's simplicity, requiring minimal training and relying on manual administration with basic equipment, allows for flexible deployment across various environments and avoids the need for complex technological setups with computer equipment and extend animal acclimation. In the present study, we demonstrated that this refined method enables a nuanced assessment of cognitive aging across young adult, middle‐aged, and old rhesus macaques. With the establishment of this task, our goal is to facilitate the generation of large‐scale datasets with which predictive biomarkers of cognitive decline can be tested and validated. A larger sample size may be required to examine any potential sex‐related effects on SDR performance, particularly in post‐menopausal females. Although we administered the task outside of the social housing environment, given the self‐pacing of this task design we feel that it is also appropriate for group‐housed and/or free‐ranging animals (with modifications to the testing apparatus). We believe this is feasible, as a similar 3‐choice delayed response task was implemented by the ManyPrimates project through a large‐scale, collaborative framework. Subjects from numerous species, housing environments and group settings were successfully tested with consistent methodology, although were generally limited to younger animals and therefore could not comprehensively examine the relation between age and delay (ManyPrimates, Altschul et al. 2019). In contrast, our sample of rhesus macaques included a wider age spectrum which captured an age*delay interaction. Taken together, these applications support the strength of the SDR task with robust sample sizes and administration in a wide variety of captive NHPs.

It is important to note, however, that as implemented here and in other studies it is possible that monkeys could rely on multiple mnemonic or attentional strategies other than active maintenance or rehearsal during the delay period. Canonical definitions of working memory emphasize rehearsal and cognitive control processes (e.g., Brown and Hampton 2020), which this task does not explicitly isolate. Therefore, interpretations related specifically to working memory in the context of this and other SDR tasks should be made with appropriate caution. Regardless, the clear sensitivity of this task to age‐related differences provides strong support for its continued applications in the context of future studies of brain aging. Areas ripe for follow up include a systematic evaluation of sex differences, hormonal influences on task performance through hormonal profiling and manipulations, integration of multimodal neuroimaging (MRI, DTI, resting‐state fMRI, PET) and electrophysiological measures with behavioral measures to elucidate neural substrates of age‑related differences, systematic assessment of pharmacological (e.g., cholinesterase inhibitors) and lifestyle (e.g., exercise, sleep) interventions for mitigation of memory impairments, and cross‑validation of macaque‑derived biomarkers with human clinical datasets to strengthen translational relevance and drive precision‑medicine strategies for cognitive aging.
