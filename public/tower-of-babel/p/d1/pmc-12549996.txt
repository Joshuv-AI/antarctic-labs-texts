Comprehensive insight into gut microbiota and their interaction with fatty acid profiles in Channa striata during the spawning season
Authors: Bayasis M. Sharma, Madhusmita Mohapatra, Rajesh Kumar, Pragyan Paramita Swain, Ipsita Iswari Das, Lakshman Sahoo, Manohar Vasam, Diptiranjan Bag, Jackson Debbarma, Sarika Jaiswal, Mir Asif Iquebal, U. B. Angadi, Anil Rai, Dinesh Kumar, Jitendra Kumar Sundaray
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Striped murrel (Channa striata) is renowned for its nutritional benefits, such as antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-obesity, and anti-inflammatory properties, attributed to its rich content of short-chain and polyunsaturated fatty acids (SCFAs and PUFAs). Unlike many food fish, it successfully breeds in captivity, adding to its value. Despite its nutritional significance, specific composition of its gut microbiota and its influence on fatty acid (FA) profiles remain underexplored. This study investigated sex-specific differences in gut bacterial communities and their association with FA profiles across two rearing conditions: fish maintained in water tanks (T1) and those reared in pond-simulated habitats (T2). Gut tissue samples underwent high-throughput 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing (V3-V4 region) and muscle tissue samples were analyzed for FA profiling. Significant variations were observed across habitat and sex. Habitat influenced FA and microbiota composition, with females from T2 showed higher palmitic (44.32%) and linoleic acids (12.01%), while T1 females had greater oleic acid (32.32%). Pseudoalteromonadaceae (Pseudoalteromonas) and Salmonella enterica were enriched in T1 than in T2. Females exhibited distinct FA profiles dominated by palmitic, oleic, and linoleic acids and sequencing results showed greater species richness and shannon diversity in their gut microbiota, with a higher abundance of Alphaproteobacteria. Males, by contrast, had higher levels of Clostridia and Fusobacteriia. Functional predictions revealed that females exhibited higher enrichment in energy metabolism, lipid biosynthesis, and xenobiotic biodegradation, while males showed greater enrichment in nucleotide and amino acid metabolism. Key microbial taxa such as Methylobacterium organophilum, Pseudomonas stutzeri and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii contributed to PUFA synthesis and antioxidant production. Co-occurrence networks in female displayed stronger interactions and connectivity compared to male. Notably, Cetobacterium somerae, Clostridium perfringens, and M. organophilum were key taxa in females, while Propionibacterium acnes, M. organophilum, and Plesiomonas shigelloides were prominent in males. Overall, the findings highlight sex- and habitat-specific gut microbiome dynamics that shape the FA composition of C. striata, offering insights for aquaculture improvement and nutritional optimization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20869-y.

Introduction

Aquaculture, being a boom food-producing sector in the world, is not anything less than the key source of the world’s food fish supply1. Aquaculture has evolved as a boon for human society in providing food and nutrition to the world. Fish species such as Indian major carps (Labeo catla, Labeo rohita, and Cirrhinus mrigala) and Chinese carps (silver carp: Hypophthalmichthys molitrix, grass carp: Ctenopharyngodon idella, and common carp: Cyprinus carpio) and Channa striata serve as significant candidates in aquaculture2,3. A better strategy for the improvement of fish production is aquaculture diversification, in which other indigenous species are added to system-specific aquaculture. In this context, C. striata, known as the Striped Snakehead murrel, is a freshwater fish species widely distributed across Southeast Asia, with significant ecological, economic, and cultural importance, making it a prime candidate for large-scale food production due to its abundant nutritive values4. As an apex predator in many aquatic ecosystems, it is an air-breathing and tropical carnivorous freshwater fish, belonging to the family Ophiocephalidae or Channidae5 that can sustain low dissolved oxygen levels and hence suitable for climate-resilient aquaculture6. C. striata plays a critical role in maintaining ecosystem balance and biodiversity. However, increasing anthropogenic activities and environmental pressures have led to habitat degradation and population declines, necessitating comprehensive research efforts to understand and mitigate these threats.

Due to the high nutritive value of C. striata, it is necessary to examine one of the significant aspects that shape every organism’s physiology, i.e. its microbial composition, especially the gut microbiome. The gut microbiota is central to nutrient digestion and absorption, lipid metabolism, and the synthesis of bioactive compounds such as polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), vitamins, and antioxidants, factors directly linked to flesh quality and health benefits7,8. For instance, microbial-mediated PUFA biosynthesis can enhance the fish’s functional food value, while certain gut microbes contribute to immune modulation and stress resilience. Understanding these microbial communities in C. striata can therefore guide feed optimization, improve aquaculture yields, and maintain the fish’s nutritional profile. Symbiotic microbial colonies have provided various additive values to the host, such as essential vitamins and regulation of various physiological pathways, throughout evolution, making the study crucial for nutritionally important fish species.

Gut microbiome consisting of diverse microbial communities residing within the gastrointestinal tract, influences host physiology, immunity, growth, and overall development9. Its composition can be shaped by trophic level, diet, and habitat10. C. striata inhabits the bottom of water bodies containing soil and floating hydrophytes4. Recent studies have highlighted the dynamic nature of gut microbiomes and their susceptibility to environmental changes, dietary shifts, and host-specific factors. Importantly, microbial communities influence host lipid metabolism through multiple mechanisms: (i) fermentation of dietary substrates into short-chain fatty acids i.e. SCFAs (e.g., acetate, propionate, butyrate), which serve as energy sources and regulate lipid synthesis; (ii) modulation of bile acid metabolism, thereby affecting lipid emulsification and absorption; and (iii) biosynthesis or bioconversion of PUFAs, which are essential for membrane structure, anti-inflammatory functions, and overall health7,8. In aquatic organisms, including C. striata, the gut microbiome has emerged as a key determinant of overall fitness, stress resilience, and reproductive success11, and its metabolic processes can directly influence muscle fatty acid composition and quality, thereby shaping the fish’s nutritional value and health benefits to consumers.

There are countless health benefits in the consumption of this fish, and studies also suggest that the consumption of 300–600 g of fillet of this fish daily showed a lower level of blood pressure, lower plasma lipid concentration, and an increase in eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and decosahexaenoic acid (DHA) levels in blood plasma12. During the spawning season, C. striata undergoes physiological and behavioral changes associated with reproduction, making this period particularly sensitive to environmental perturbations13. Habitat manipulation, including alterations in water quality, substrate composition, and vegetation cover, can significantly impact fish behavior, reproductive physiology, and habitat utilization patterns14. Furthermore, sex-specific differences in C. striata physiology and behavior may further modulate responses to environmental stressors and influence gut microbiome dynamics between males and females. Understanding these links is essential for developing aquaculture strategies that optimize both fish health and nutritional quality.

The present study aimed to address significant queries regarding the gut bacterial communities of C. striata fishes in response to different environmental stimulations by employing Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) techniques, specifically high-throughput 16S rRNA gene (V3-V4) amplicon sequencing. Specifically, we investigated the influence of habitat manipulation on gut microbiome composition and diversity during the spawning season. Additionally, we explored potential sex-specific variations in gut microbiome dynamics and metabolic pathways in male and female fish. Our objectives were three-fold: (i) to examine the effects of habitat manipulation on the gut microbiome composition and diversity of C. striata during the spawning season. (ii) To identify key microbial taxa and functional pathways associated with habitat manipulation and sex-specific differences in C. striata gut microbiomes and evaluate their relevance to host health, reproductive success, and ecological interactions. (iii) To investigate the potential relationship between fatty acid (FA) profiles and gut bacterial communities in C. striata inhabiting diverse aquaculture habitats. Furthermore, we hypothesized that habitat type and sex interact to shape the gut microbiome of C. striata during the spawning season, influencing both microbial diversity and functional potential. Furthermore, we posited that variations in gut microbial communities are linked to differences in muscle fatty acid composition, and that these microbiota–lipid associations contribute to the nutritional and physiological traits of the species. This study lays a foundation for understanding the essential inter-relationship between the host and the symbiotic microflora, which is crucial for maintaining health and other physiological functions.

Methods

Experimental setup

Two concrete tanks, each with a volume of 15 m3, were utilized to create different habitats for the rearing of adult C. striata. The two experimental habitats were as follows. Habitat 1, Tank 1 (T1) contained only the fish. Habitat 2, Tank 2 (T2) was designed to resemble a pond environment (hereafter, simply referred as pond-simulated habitat) where murrels are reared, containing fish, soil at the bottom, and aquatic macrophytes on the water surface. The experiment was conducted at the ICAR-Central Institute of Freshwater Aquaculture (ICAR-CIFA), Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India (20°11′38.3" N 85°51′17.4" E) (Fig. 1) over a 45 days period during the spawning season (June–August) 2021. The reproductive status of the fish was confirmed by gonadal inspection. The fish were maintained in the same hatchery environment and were fed twice a day with a commercial diet [Growel feed, Nutrila (6 mm); Crude protein-40%, Crude fat-10%, Crude fiber-3% and Moisture-12%] with a feeding amount equivalent to ~ 2% of the fish body weight. A total of 16 fish, with an average weight of 367.5 ± 17.8 g (mean ± standard error), were collected from the rearing pond of ICAR-CIFA and randomly distributed into concrete tanks. Each tank contained 4 fish, with two replicate tanks for each habitat (T1 and T2), resulting in 8 fish per habitat (4 males and 4 females). The sample size was determined considering the large body size and territorial nature of C. striata, which necessitate lower stocking densities to minimize aggression and stress, maintain stable water quality, and comply with animal welfare regulations. The tanks were placed in a shaded area with continuous aeration to maintain optimal conditions. This experimental design aimed to compare the growth and behavior of C. striata in two different rearing environments, examining the impact of substrate and aquatic vegetation on the fish.

Growth measurements and collection of samples

Following the end of the 45-day experimental period, fish were collected from both experimental tanks and immediately transferred to the laboratory. Individual body weight (g) and total length (cm) were recorded at the start (day 0) and end (day 45) of the experiment (Table 1). Body weight was measured using an analytical balance with an accuracy of 0.1 g, and total length was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a large measuring scale suitable for adult fish. Growth performance indices were calculated as follows: Weight gain rate (WGR, %) = (Wf − Wi)/Wi × 100, where Wf = final body weight (g) and Wi = initial body weight (g). Specific growth rate (SGR, % day⁻1) = [ln (Wf) – ln (Wi)]/t × 100, where ‘t’ is the experimental duration (45 days). Length increment (cm) = final total length − initial total length.

During handling, fish were anesthetized using 2-phenoxyethanol, with a dosage of 0.30 ml/l15. Subsequently, the fish were surface-sterilized using 70% ethanol. Fish were dissected using sterile scissors to access the abdominal cavity. Gut samples were collected by gently squeezing out the contents, after which only the foregut portion was selected and washed three times with PBS (pH 7.2) to remove undigested food and allochthonous bacteria, following the method of Parivallal et al.16. Muscle samples were obtained by carefully removing the skin. Both gut and muscle tissues were stored at − 80 °C in a deep freezer for future analyses. All experimental procedures were conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the Institute Animal Ethical Committee (IAEC), ICAR-Central Institute of Freshwater Aquaculture, Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India. Animal experiments followed the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines, covering study design, sample size, randomization, and statistical analysis. All the methods are in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Histological analysis of gonadal sections of C. striata

Gonadal tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for over 24 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 4 μm thickness were cut using a microtome, floated on water when necessary, and mounted on albumin-coated glass slides. The slides were fixed on a slide warmer at 60 °C and incubated for 12 h to ensure proper stretching. Standard hematoxylin and eosin (H & E) staining was performed, involving dewaxing in xylene, rehydration through graded ethanol, nuclear staining with hematoxylin, and cytoplasmic counterstaining with eosin. The slides were sealed with resin and examined under a Nexscope RXLr-5 microscope equipped with a Radical Procam camera. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Determination of fatty acids

The total lipids from the fish muscles were extracted by using chloroform–methanol solvent mixture (2:1, v/v) containing 0.05% butylated hydroxytoluene (BTH) as an antioxidant. For transmethylation, a methanol-hydrochloric acid-dimethoxy propane mixture (40:4:1.6, v/v/v) was used. Two microliters of each lipid extract were injected into a GC–MS system (Thermo Fisher Trace) equipped with a capillary column. Helium gas was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate was 1.2 ml/min. The injector temperature was set at 290 °C. The oven temperature was programmed as follows: Initial temperature: 80 °C, which was then increased to 120 °C at a rate of 10 °C per minute, increased to 250 °C at 5 °C/per min, increased to 270 °C at 2 °C per minute and maintained for 4 min. The temperature of the ion source was set at 280 °C, and the ionization voltage was 70 eV. Fatty acids were identified by comparing retention times with known standards.

DNA extraction, amplification, and sequencing of 16S rRNA genes (V3–V4 region)

DNA was extracted from foregut samples using a Qiagen DNeasy Power Soil Pro kit (Cat no. 47014, Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quantification and quality of the extracted DNA were checked using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific; 2000) and by agarose gel electrophoresis. The 16S rRNA gene amplicon library preparation was performed using the extracted DNA. The V3-V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene were targeted for amplification using region-specific targeting proprietary primers by Genotypic Technology Pvt. Ltd., Bangalore, India. Genomic DNA was amplified in 26 cycles using the KAPA HiFi HotStart PCR Kit (KAPA Biosystems Inc., Boston, MA, USA). The forward and reverse primers were each used at a concentration of 5 µM. Amplicons were verified via 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The amplicon libraries were prepared using the NexteraXT v2 Index Kit (Illumina, USA) as per the 16S rRNA gene sequencing library preparation protocol. The sequences for the Illumina adapters were as follows: Forward adapter: 5’-AATGATACGGCGACCACCGAGATCTACAC[i5]TCGTCGGCAGCGTC and Reverse adapter: 5’-CAAGCAGAAGACGGCATACGAGAT[i7]GTCTCGTGGGCTCGG. The libraries were then normalized to equimolar concentrations, pooled, and subjected to multiplexed sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq V2 sequencer (Illumina, CA, USA). Paired-end sequencing was performed for 300 cycles according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The obtained fish gut microbiota sequence data have been deposited to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive which can be accessed through bioproject number: PRJNA917215 (https://dataview.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/object/PRJNA917215).

Sequence data processing, taxonomic, and functional prediction

Raw sequencing reads were processed using TrimGalorev.0.4.0117 to remove adapters and low-quality bases, with a Phred score threshold set at 30. High-quality reads containing primer sequences were stitched using Fastq-join18. These stitched reads were further analyzed using the QIIME pipeline19. Sequence clustering was performed using the UCLUST method20, employing a closed-reference operational taxonomic units (OTU) picking approach. Taxonomic classification of the clusters was assigned based on ≥ 97% sequence similarity to the Greengenes v13.8 reference database21, which is free of chimeric 16S rRNA gene sequences. This process generated a biom file, which was then used for further advanced analysis and visualization.

The generated OTU table underwent a filtering process, followed by the summarization of taxonomic annotations. From this, the final OTU abundance count table, relative abundance table, and α-diversity metrics were derived. The α-diversity indices calculated included observed species (Sobs), Shannon (H’), Simpson, and Chao1 indices. A Venn diagram was generated to compare microbial species between groups. The β-diversity represents the variance within microbial communities across samples in a given habitat, quantified by the distance between them22. In this study, overall microbial diversity among groups was assessed using Bray–Curtis distance matrices, which compare taxonomic β-diversity.

The biom file which was generated using the Greengenes database was used for functional analysis using the Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States i.e., PICRUSt tool23. PICRUSt predicts gene family abundances in host-associated and environmental communities, incorporating quantifiable uncertainty. The OTU table was normalized by adjusting each OTU value with its known or predicted 16S rRNA gene copy number, then multiplied by corresponding gene family abundance profiles for each taxon. This step yielded an annotated table of predicted gene family counts for each sample, where gene families represented as orthologous groups or other identifiers, such as Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Orthology (KOs)24,25, www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html).

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA was performed on the fatty acid data using SPSS software v.23 (IBM), keeping the threshold p-value at 0.05. Growth parameters were computed and evaluated using one-way ANOVA with F-statistics, followed by a Monte Carlo permutation test to determine significant differences between habitat treatments. Patterns in bacterial communities in habitat (T1 and T2) and sex-specific (male and female) type were analyzed using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) using Bray–Curtis distances in MicrobiomeAnalyst26. Differences in microbial community composition (i.e., Bray–Curtis distances) were assessed by an Analysis of Similarity (ANOSIM) test with 999 permutations to assess significance of associations between the measured variables and microbiota structure. The R value close to zero suggests minimal separation among the samples. To analyze the differentially abundant taxonomic groups across habitats, and sex, a multi-factor analysis (MFA) was conducted using MicrobiomeAnalyst26, applying criteria of Log2 fold change (FC) > 2 and p-value < 0.05. Differences among sample groups were further evaluated using similarity percentages (SIMPER) and pairwise PERMANOVA in PAST v. 3.18.

To better understand the relationship between fatty acid composition and bacterial communities, Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) was performed using PAST (v. 3.18) software. Additionally, a heat map was created based on Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r), calculated with SPSS 20.0 (IBM, USA) at a significance level of p-value < 0.01. For these analyses, microbial taxa were included based on a relative abundance threshold of ≥ 1% across samples, ensuring both statistical robustness and biological relevance in evaluating their relationships with FA variables. Statistical analysis of functional microbial profiles was performed using the Statistical Analysis of Metagenomic Profiles i.e., STAMP v 2.1.3 software package27. Microbial co-occurrence network analysis was conducted using the Spearman correlation inference algorithm through MetagenoNets with default settings28. The analysis only considered significant correlations (|r|> 0.7, p-value < 0.01), derived from bootstrapping with 100 iterations. Bacterial taxa were filtered using a maximum prevalence cut-off of ≥ 0.0001 and had to be present in at least 10% of the samples. Network visualization was carried out using network plots on the Gephi v. 0.10.1 platform29. The network’s topological features, including nodes, edges, average degree, clustering coefficient, betweenness centrality, and closeness centrality were compared to analyze changes in bacterial co-occurrences and identify keystone taxa.

Results

Histological examination of Gonads in C. striata

Histological examination of C. striata gonads revealed distinct structural characteristics between female and male specimens. In females, the ovary displayed well-organized ovarian lobules filled with oocytes at varying stages of development (Supp. Figure 1a). The majority of oocytes were in the vitellogenic stage, characterized by the presence of abundant yolk granules occupying the cytoplasm and a distinct peripheral nucleus. Pre-vitellogenic oocytes with basophilic cytoplasm and centrally located nuclei were also evident, indicating asynchronous oocyte development. There were no signs of atresia or degeneration. In males, the testicular architecture consisted of numerous seminiferous lobules containing germ cells at different developmental stages (Supp. Figure 1b). Spermatogonia, primary and secondary spermatocytes, spermatids, and spermatozoa were visible within the lobules. The interstitial tissue between lobules was minimal, and there were no histopathological abnormalities.

Fatty acids content in muscles

The fatty acid composition of the muscle tissues (Table 2), reveals the presence of a total of 27 distinct fatty acids. The predominant fatty acids identified were palmitic acid (C16:0), oleic acid (cis n-9, C18:1), and linoleic acid (cis n-6, C18:2). Among saturated fatty acids (SFA), palmitic acid was most abundant, with the highest concentration observed in muscle tissues of females from the T2 habitat (T2F) at 44.32%. For monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), oleic acid was most prevalent, with a notable concentration of 32.32% in the muscle tissues of females from the T1 habitat (T1F). Linoleic acid, the major PUFA, was most abundant in the muscle tissues of T2F (12.01%). Additionally, significant differences were observed in the levels of palmitoleic acid and heptadecenoic acid (C17:0) between the male and female muscle tissues, highlighting sex-specific variations in muscle fatty acid profiles. Overall, females showed relatively higher proportions of DHA (0.73 ± 1.56) and EPA (0.85 ± 2.34) compared to males (0.70 ± 0.24 and 0.20 ± 0.30, respectively), although these differences were not 16S rRNA gene sequence data and bacterial diversity

The sequencing depth of the sampling was determined by rarefaction curves, which were generated for the number of species i.e., species richness (Supp. Figure 2). To study the gut microbial community of C. striata based on its surrounding environment, metadata were generated using the Illumina MiSeq platform. A total of 2,934,407 raw reads were acquired including habitat T1 and T2 and sex-specific male and female groups were 1,224,682 and 1,709,725, respectively (Supp. Table 1). After quality control, a total of 2,723,791 high quality sequences were obtained. The quality-filtered reads were clustered into OTUs at a 97% of sequence identity, which resulted in several distinct OTUs, representing sequences from each group (Supp. Table 1).

Due to variations in the sequencing depth within the samples, the sequences were subjected to species richness and diversity estimates with the help of Sobs, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson (Fig. 2). The estimated richness (Sobs and Chao1 index) and diversity (Shannon, and Simpson) for T1-treatment was higher compared to T2. Significant differences in the alpha diversity indices were not observed between T1 and T2 (One-way ANOVA, p-value > 0.05). However, the estimated richness for the female gut bacterial group (Sobs: 107.13 ± 1.73 versus 163.25 ± 2.64; Chao1: 140.83 ± 1.95 versus 216.47 ± 2.67) was significantly (One-way ANOVA, p-value < 0.05) lower compared to male group (Fig. 2a,b). In addition, the Shannon diversity of the female gut bacterial group (2.63 ± 0.20 versus 2.29 ± 0.28) was significantly (One-way ANOVA, p-value < 0.05) higher compared to male group (Fig. 2c). T2 females had a higher Shannon diversity index of 2.72 as compared to other gut samples. Functional prediction of C. striata gut microbial communities based on KEGG Orthology (KO)

The functional prediction of the C. striata gut microbiota, based on KO analysis, identified a total of 6,034 KEGG genes. These genes were categorized into six broad functional modules at Level 1 of functional annotation: metabolism (46.47%), environmental information processing (EIP) (15.97%), genetic information processing (GIP) (15.62%), cellular processes (4.99%), human diseases (1.32%), and organismal systems (0.69%) (Fig. 5a). At Level 2, within the EIP module, membrane transport (13.22%) and signal transduction (2.58%) were highly represented (Fig. 5b). Additionally, the GIP module at Level 2 displayed significant representation in replication and repair (6.98%), translation (4.13%), transcription (2.32%), and folding, sorting, and degradation (2.23%) (Fig. 5b). The study revealed significant sex-specific differences in C. striata gut microbiota functions. Comparisons of bacterial genes, based on identified KEGG genes, demonstrated that significant differences in the relative abundance of these KEGG genes were observed between male and female gut microbiomes (STAMP, p-value < 0.05) (Fig. 5c; Supp. Figure 4). No significant differences were detected at KEGG pathway Level 1, 2, and 3 when comparing habitat-specific groups of gut microbiota. However, at KEGG pathway Level 1, GIP showed significant differences between sex-specific groups (Fig. 5c). Further comparisons of the mean relative abundance of dominant pathways at KEGG pathway Level 2 between groups revealed significant differences between male and female gut microbiomes (p-value < 0.05). For instance, within the metabolism module at KEGG pathway Level 2, nucleotide metabolism was more enriched in the male gut microbiota, whereas pathways related to energy metabolism and xenobiotics biodegradation and metabolism were significantly higher in the female gut microbiota. In the GIP module at KEGG pathway Level 2, pathways associated with replication and repair, translation, and transcription were significantly enriched in the male gut microbiota.

The analysis of metabolic functions at KEGG Level-2 indicated that amino acid metabolism was the most prevalent (Supp. Figure 3). KEGG Level-3 pathways associated with amino acid-related enzymes, arginine and proline metabolism, glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism were prevalent in both male and female C. striata gut microbiota. Notably, glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism were more abundant in the female gut microbiome (Supp. Figure 4). Other amino acid metabolism categories with heightened activity included phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis, and lysine biosynthesis, which favored the male gut microbiota. Conversely, valine, leucine, and isoleucine metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, lysine degradation, and tryptophan metabolism were more prevalent in the female gut microbiota.

The study also observed significant differences in carbohydrate metabolism at Level 3 (Supp. Figure 4). Pathways such as amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, fructose and mannose metabolism, galactose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, pentose phosphate pathway, and starch and sucrose metabolism were significantly enriched in the male gut microbiota. In contrast, ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, inositol phosphate metabolism, and propanoate metabolism were significantly enriched in the female gut microbiota.

Lipid metabolism pathways also showed distinct differences between male and female C. striata gut microbiota (Supp Fig. 3). Arachidonic acid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, lipid biosynthesis proteins, and steroid biosynthesis were more enriched in the female gut microbiota. Conversely, glycerolipid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, and sphingolipid metabolism were significantly enriched in the male gut microbiota. Pathways associated with xenobiotics biodegradation and metabolism, including aminobenzoate degradation, atrazine degradation, DDT degradation, caprolactam degradation, drug metabolism (cytochrome P450), metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450, nitrotoluene degradation, and styrene degradation were significantly higher in the female gut microbiota (p-value < 0.05; Supp Fig. 3). Further analysis of KEGG Level 3 functional pathways identified genes related to replication and repair (DNA repair and recombination proteins, homologous recombination, mismatch repair), membrane transport (phosphotransferase system), signal transduction (mTOR signaling pathway), transcription (RNA polymerase), and translation (ribosome and translation factors) as being more abundant in the male gut microbiota than in the female gut microbiota. In this study, we identified microbial taxa associated with PUFA and antioxidant synthesis in the gut microbiota. Our results revealed a significant presence of M. organophilum and P. stutzeri in the gut microbiota, particularly linked to PUFA metabolism (Supp. Figure 5), while Methylobacterium mesophilicum and F. prausnitzii contributed to antioxidant pathways such as glutathione and butyrate synthesis (Supp. Figure 6).

Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into the gut microbiota of C. striata, revealing significant influences of habitat and sex on bacterial diversity and community composition. The findings demonstrate that while habitat did not result in distinct clustering of microbial communities, specific bacterial taxa exhibited differential abundances between the two habitat groups, T1 and T2. In contrast, sex-specific differences were more pronounced, with clear segregation of gut bacterial communities between male and female fish. These results are consistent with previous studies highlighting the role of host-specific factors, including habitat and sex, in shaping the gut microbiota of aquatic organisms30,31.

Influence of habitat complexity on growth performance

Fish reared in pond-simulated habitats (T2) containing substrates and aquatic vegetation exhibited comparatively lower growth than those in the simpler T1 habitat. This reduced growth may be attributed to higher energy expenditure associated with navigating complex environments and increased competition for food resources in enriched habitats32,33. Although structural enrichment is known to enhance welfare and stimulate natural behaviors, it can also limit foraging efficiency and feed intake, potentially reducing growth rates33. Conversely, fish in the less complex T1 habitat may have directed more energy toward somatic growth due to reduced locomotor demands and easier access to feed34.

Gonadal maturation and reproductive status in C. striata

The active reproductive status and asynchronous oocyte development in female C. striata, as well as peak spermatogenic activity in males during the spawning season, are well-supported by histological studies. Research has documented multiple oogenesis phases, including vitellogenic and pre-vitellogenic oocytes in females, indicating asynchronous ovarian growth and the potential for multiple spawning events. In males, abundant mature spermatozoa within seminiferous lobules during the spawning season confirm peak spermatogenic activity and reproductive readiness. These patterns are consistent with observations in other teleosts, highlighting the synchrony between gonadal maturation and seasonal reproductive cycles35,36.

Influence of reproductive status on fatty acid composition in fish

Reproductive status strongly influences fatty acid composition in fish, with notable variations between sexes and habitats. During the spawning season, energetic demands of gametogenesis, spawning behavior, and reproductive investment drive extensive mobilization of lipid reserves, particularly PUFAs, to support gonadal development, hormone synthesis, and offspring provisioning37. Females often show higher levels of DHA and EPA, essential for egg quality, embryonic development, and larval survival, while males may exhibit enrichment of fatty acids critical for sperm function. Habitat factors, including diet availability, composition, and environmental conditions, further shape these lipid profiles, influencing both their qualitative and quantitative composition37,38.

Recent research across multiple fish species, including both marine and freshwater taxa, consistently demonstrates that reproductive phase–driven metabolic demands and tissue-specific lipid allocation are major determinants of fatty acid profiles. The differences in fatty acid composition observed between sexes and habitats in this study may therefore be partially attributed to the reproductive status of the fish, as all specimens were collected during the spawning season. During this period, reproductive activity is associated with heightened metabolic demands for gonadal development, steroid synthesis, and energy mobilization, which can influence lipid deposition patterns in muscle tissue39,40. This is consistent with previous studies reporting that spawning-phase fish often exhibit shifts in fatty acid profiles to meet reproductive requirements39–41. Such findings underscore the importance of integrating reproductive biology into nutritional and ecological assessments of fish populations, particularly for species of commercial and aquaculture relevance.

Habitat-specific and sex-specific variations in gut bacterial communities

Our study revealed that habitat exerted limited influence on the overall gut microbial community structure of C. striata, as revealed by NMDS analysis, which showed no significant clustering between T1 and T2 treatments. However, habitat-specific shifts were evident in certain bacterial lineages. For instance, members of the family Pseudoalteromonadaceae, particularly the genus Pseudoalteromonas were more abundant in the T1 group, indicating that certain bacterial taxa are sensitive to environmental conditions such as water quality and nutrient availability, consistent with earlier findings in fish microbiota research42,43. Similarly, the higher abundance of Salmonella and its associated lineage Salmonella enterica in the T1 group may reflect habitat- driven exposure to particular pathogens or environmental stressors that select for these bacteria.

In contrast, sex had a more pronounced effect on gut microbial composition. NMDS and ANOSIM analyses revealed clear segregation between male and female gut bacterial communities with females showing higher diversity and enrichment of Alphaproteobacteria, including Methylobacterium and Faecalibacterium, which belongs to the class Firmicutes. These bacteria are known for their roles in organic carbon cycling and SCFAs production, which are essential for maintaining gut health and energy balance44. The higher prevalence of these beneficial taxa in females may suggest a sex-specific adaptation to optimize metabolism and immune regulation, aligning with previous reports of sex-related microbiome differences in fish45–47. The enrichment of Alphaproteobacteria, including genera such as Methylobacterium and Faecalibacterium, in female gut communities is particularly noteworthy. The higher abundance of F. prausnitzii in females is notable given its role in postbiotics i.e. butyrate production, which supports epithelial health, reduces inflammation, and contributes to antioxidant capacity48–50. Functional predictions (PICRUSt) further indicated enrichment of genes related to fatty acid metabolism in females, suggesting stronger links between sex, microbiota, and host lipid metabolism.

By contrast, male gut communities were enriched with Photobacterium species, including P. rosenbergii and P. angustum, which have been linked to pathogenicity and histamine production51–53. This may indicate a sex-specific susceptibility to microbial infections or divergent metabolic requirements. Together, these results demonstrate that sex is a key determinant of microbial composition, consistent with observations in other vertebrates where microbiota is shaped by host physiology, reproductive cycles, and immune function46,47. Such findings highlight the need to consider sex as a critical biological factor when studying fish microbiota.

The findings from the Venn diagram analysis suggest that species unique to a specific habitat or sex might be linked to habitat-specific adaptations or sex-specific physiological processes47. The differential enrichment of certain species in either sex underscores the role of sex-specific factors in shaping gut microbiota composition in C. striata. This emphasizes the need to consider host-specific factors when investigating gut microbial ecology54,55.

Beyond sex and habitat effects, our study identified dominant bacterial phyla included Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Fusobacteria, Cyanobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes. The predominance of Proteobacteria in the gut of C. striata, consistent with recent findings on fish gut microbiota43,56,57. Proteobacteria are involved in various biogeochemical cycles, including carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling, which may explain their dominance in the gut of C. striata58. However, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes play established roles in polysaccharide breakdown and energy harvesting8,59,60. Within Firmicutes, F. prausnitzii, is regarded as a next-generation probiotic, produces butyrate e.g. SCFA, whereas Actinobacteria degrade macromolecules and produce antibacterial compounds, enhancing host health61–63.

Moreover, the presence of another beneficial taxa such as Pseudomonas spp., widely used probiotics in aquaculture, further emphasizes the potential of leveraging microbiota to support fish health, including their growth, and reproductive performances64–67. These species are known to produce vitamin B12, which supports normal growth and physiological functions in the host68–70. Pseudomonas also contributes to a pollutant-free environment by producing compounds toxic to other species71. Beneficial microbes protect aquaculture species by killing pathogens or inhibiting the expression of pathogenic virulence factors through quorum sensing inhibition70,72,73. The higher abundance of these taxa in female fish suggests that the host may interact with its microbiota to maintain well-being, even when inhabiting environments different from its natural habitat. Given these findings, there is potential for developing new probiotic formulations that include P. stutzeri and F. prausnitzii to enhance microbial balance in C. striata. Administering such probiotics through diet could improve fish growth performance, reduce disease incidence, modulate immune responses, and maintain or restore normal physiological states. This approach would contribute to increased productivity and health in aquaculture practices, promoting sustainable and efficient fish farming.

Habitat-specific effects were also evident when considering species-level differences. For example, C. somerae, a vitamin B12-producing gut bacterium known to promote lipid and carbohydrate metabolism through the production of acetic and propionic acids via the fermentation of peptone74–77, was more abundant in females from T1 compared to T2. This difference suggests that females in T1 may depend more on water to fulfill their dietary needs, potentially consuming more suspended organic matter or prey from the water column, which could naturally promote the enrichment of C. somerae populations. In contrast, the microbial communities in T2, which mimicked a natural pond ecosystem with soil and macrophytes, did not show significant differences. This stability in microbial composition might be due to the more diverse and natural rearing conditions provided in T2, which likely offer a broader range of nutrients and environmental interactions for the fish. These findings support the idea that habitat complexity can influence gut microbiota composition in fish, with simpler environments like T1 driving certain bacteria to dominate, while more natural, biodiverse environments like T2 foster a balanced microbial community. This understanding could be valuable for aquaculture practices, suggesting that habitat manipulation could be used to modulate gut microbiota and enhance fish health and productivity.

Functional prediction analysis revealed a significant richness of functional genes associated with various metabolic processes, GIP, EIP, cellular processes, and human diseases. The contribution of intestinal microbiota to the carbohydrate metabolism of the host has been reported in many fish species56,78,79. Consistent with these findings, our study identified a high abundance of genes related to carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism in the gut microbiota of female fishes, aligning with observations from previous research7,10,56. Major amino acids such as glycine, proline, asparagine, glutamine, and lysine are positively correlated with the phylum Proteobacteria, and EPA and DHA are also associated with Proteobacteria in the case of bighead carp (Aristichthys nobilis)80. Major amino acids such as lysine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, arginine, leucine, alanine, valine, threonine, and glycine are found in the fillet of striped murrel along with fatty acids such as palmitic acid, stearic acid, arachidonic acid, oleic acid DHA and EPA, making the fish a highly valued food50,81. We therefore focused on lipid metabolism to identify microbial genes associated with these pathways, given its central role in fish physiology and relevance to C. striata. Recent studies on the fish gut suggest that the amino acids and fatty acids present in the muscles of the fish might be correlated with the gut microflora of the fish82.

Influence of gut microbiota on muscle fatty acid composition and metabolism in C. striata

The relationship between gut microbiota and muscle fatty acid composition in C. striata through CCA analysis offers valuable insights into how specific bacterial species might influence lipid metabolism and overall fish health. The correlation analysis between gut microbiota composition and long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) revealed significant associations that underscore the roles of specific bacterial species in fatty acid metabolism. Notably, C. somerae and P. damselae exhibited positive correlations with elevated levels of DHA and EPA, two essential omega-3 fatty acids critical for maintaining various physiological functions in fish. C. somerae demonstrated a strong correlation with the first axis of our CCA analysis, suggesting that this bacterium plays a central role in the metabolic processing of LCFAs within the gut. The positioning of C. somerae along this axis indicates that its presence is significantly linked to the increased synthesis or accumulation of DHA and EPA, which are vital for membrane fluidity, neural function, and anti-inflammatory processes83. This correlation supports previous findings that C. somerae is integral to lipid and carbohydrate metabolism74, reinforcing its role in facilitating the biosynthesis and regulation of key fatty acids within the host.

P. damselae, on the other hand, was positively positioned along the second axis of the CCA, indicating a distinct but complementary role in fatty acid metabolism. The genus Photobacterium is known for its diverse metabolic capabilities, including the potential to influence fatty acid composition43. The positive association of P. damselae with DHA and EPA suggests that this bacterium may contribute to the elongation and desaturation processes that are essential for the formation of these LCFAs. The distinct positioning on the second axis highlights its unique contribution to the microbial ecosystem and its potential influence on host lipid profiles. The positive associations of both C. somerae and P. damselae with increased levels of LCFAs like DHA and EPA provide insight into the complex interactions between gut microbiota and host fatty acid metabolism. These findings underscore the importance of specific gut bacteria in the synthesis and regulation of essential fatty acids, which are crucial for maintaining the health and well-being of the host. The results also suggest potential avenues for future research, including exploring the mechanisms through which these bacteria influence LCFA metabolism and their implications for nutritional strategies aimed at enhancing fish health and productivity.

Heat-map analysis results revealed significant correlations between the gut bacterial community at the species level and various muscle fatty acids, suggesting potential metabolic interactions. M. organophilum, a methylotroph known for its ability to utilize one-carbon compounds such as methanol and methane84, enriched in C. striata females. Methylobacterium species are often used for SCP production due to their metabolic versatility, sustainability, reduced competition with food resources, energy efficiency, reduced environmental footprint, adaptability, and high protein content85,86. Studies have shown that M. organophilum contains unique fatty acids, including C18:1ω7c as a major component, with other saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids present in lower amounts87,88. These findings support the observed positive correlation between M. organophilum abundance and specific fatty acids (e.g., lauric acid, pentadecenoic acid, heptadecenoic acid, nervonic acid, and linolelaidic acid), indicating a role in the biosynthesis or regulation of these fatty acids, possibly through its involvement in lipid metabolism pathways that utilize C1 compounds as substrates87,88. Interestingly, this species was negatively correlated with myristic acid and α-linolenic acid (n-3), suggesting a complex regulatory mechanism where certain fatty acids are either consumed or produced as by-products of microbial metabolism88.

Role of gut microbiota in PUFA Synthesis

Based on current literature, several microbial communities are recognized for their pivotal roles in the accumulation of PUFAs within the host. Methylotrophic bacteria, including Methylobacterium species, are recognized for their unique fatty acid profiles and metabolic capabilities, which can influence the host’s lipidome87,88. Additionally, broader studies highlight the importance of microbial metabolism in shaping the fatty acid composition of host tissues, including the synthesis and modification of PUFAs89. Predictive functional analysis indicates that M. organophilum is associated with stearoyl-CoA desaturase, an enzyme responsible for introducing double bonds at the delta-9 position of fatty acids, a key step in PUFA synthesis90. Additionally, P. stutzeri contributes to this process through the provision of linoleoyl-CoA desaturase, which facilitates the introduction of a double bond, thereby enhancing the desaturation of long-chain fatty acids. The presence of these bacterial species and their associated enzymes suggests a critical role for the gut microbiota in modulating the fatty acid composition of the host.

Additionally, PUFAs, crucial for maintaining cell membrane fluidity and function, were found to be negatively correlated with Prevotella copri. P. copri is typically associated with carbohydrate metabolism, and its negative correlation with PUFAs may suggest an inhibitory effect on the production or retention of these essential fatty acids in C. striata muscle tissue91. This finding could indicate a competitive metabolic interaction where the presence of P. copri might limit the availability or synthesis of PUFAs, which are vital for various physiological processes in fish. Furthermore, P. shigelloides was positively correlated with linoleic acid, a PUFA known for its role in inflammation and immune responses92. This positive association suggests that P. shigelloides might enhance the production or accumulation of linoleic acid in C. striata muscle, potentially influencing the fish’s inflammatory response or overall health. Meanwhile, P. stutzeri showed a significant association with heptadecenoic acid, a lesser-known fatty acid, which might indicate this bacterium’s role in the synthesis or modification of specific fatty acids within the fish’s gut. These findings underscore the complex and potentially symbiotic relationships between gut bacteria and fatty acid metabolism in C. striata. Understanding these interactions could be crucial for developing strategies to enhance the nutritional value of fish through microbiome management.

Role of gut microbiota in antioxidant synthesis

Glutathione, a major antioxidant present in all higher organisms, plays a crucial role in neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS) by directly quenching hydroxyl free radicals93,94. Our study identified key enzymes involved in glutathione synthesis in the gut microbiota of T1 males, indicating a potential role in glutathione biosynthesis and oxidative stress regulation. Glutathione synthase is linked with M. mesophilicum, a microorganism present in relatively higher abundance in the gut of T1 males compared to other groups, suggesting a potential link between this microbe and enhanced antioxidant capacity in this group95. Furthermore, SCFAs (e.g., butyric acid), which act as antioxidants, are produced as metabolic byproducts of bacterial fermentation. F. prausnitzii is particularly notable for its association with butyrate synthesis, primarily through the acetyl-CoA pathway96,97. Our findings also indicate that the enzyme butyrate kinase is associated with F. prausnitzii. Interestingly, the relative abundance of F. prausnitzii was observed to be higher in T1 males and T2 females, suggesting a potential link between gut microbiota composition and antioxidant capacity. The presence of F. prausnitzii and the corresponding enzymatic pathway in these groups suggests that butyrate production may play a significant role in maintaining gut health and providing antioxidant protection. Overall, the observed differences in microbial composition and the associated functional pathways between the different groups highlight the potential roles of gut microbiota in PUFA and antioxidant synthesis, which could have important implications for host physiology and health.

Role of gut microbiota in xenobiotic degradation

The present study demonstrates a significant enrichment of metabolic pathways associated with xenobiotic biodegradation in the gut microbiota of female C. striata. Pathways involved in degradation of aminobenzoate, atrazine, DDT, caprolactam, nitrotoluene, styrene, drug metabolism via cytochrome P450, metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450 were found to be significantly higher in females compared to males. The cytochrome P450 enzyme system plays a crucial role in the detoxification of pollutants and drugs through oxidation reactions, which facilitate their excretion from the host98. The higher abundance of pathways related to drug metabolism and xenobiotic degradation via cytochrome P450 in the female gut microbiota implies a more active role in detoxification, potentially protecting the host from harmful substances. Moreover, the presence of pathways for aminobenzoate and atrazine degradation indicates the microbiota’s capacity to break down specific organic pollutants. Atrazine, a widely used herbicide, has been shown to induce oxidative stress in fish99. The enhanced degradation of atrazine by the gut microbiota of females could mitigate its harmful effects. The elevated degradation of DDT and nitrotoluene in the female gut microbiota may highlight an evolved mechanism to reduce the toxic load from such pollutants, especially in aquatic environments where these chemicals persist. Pseudomonas sp. strain was reported capable of degrading DDT100. Interestingly, pathways related to styrene degradation, a process associated with breaking down plastic-related pollutants, were also enriched in females. This finding aligns with recent concerns over plastic pollution in aquatic ecosystems and the role of microbial consortia in biodegrading such contaminants101. The gut microbiota’s capacity to process these xenobiotics may also contribute to mitigating the effects of microplastic ingestion, which is increasingly recognized as a threat to aquatic organisms. Overall, the enhanced xenobiotic degradation capacity of the female gut microbiota in C. striata underscores its role in maintaining host health and resilience in contaminated environments. Understanding these microbial-mediated detoxification processes can inform aquaculture management practices, especially in areas prone to pollution, and may guide future strategies aimed at mitigating the impact of xenobiotics on fish health and productivity.

Influence of habitat and sex on the gut bacterial community network structure in C. striata

This study used co-occurrence network analysis to investigate habitat and sex-specific variations, revealing crucial ecological patterns within the gut microbiota. These differences in gut bacterial communities are likely driven by both mutualistic and antagonistic interactions among the taxa102. In the T1 habitat and female fish, the co-occurrence networks demonstrated stronger potential interactions and a higher level of connectedness. This was evidenced by a greater number of nodes, edges, average degree, and clustering coefficient. These metrics suggest that the microbial communities in the T1 habitat and female fish are more robust and diverse, potentially contributing to enhanced ecosystem functionality and resilience. The higher clustering coefficients observed in these networks may indicate metabolic interdependence among taxa, driven by various degradation pathways, habitat filtering, or environmental heterogeneity69. Additionally, the increased number of positive and negative correlations in the T1 habitat further emphasizes the complexity and intricacy of microbial interactions within this environment. The negative interactions observed within the networks may be attributed to cooperative and competitive dynamics among taxa102. For example, P. shigelloides (Gammaproteobacteria) was the most active bacterium in the male network, although some of its interactions were also observed in female fish. These findings align with previous studies on sex-specific gut bacterial co-occurrence patterns, such as those observed in Siamese fighting fish103.

Various factors (e.g., stage of maturity and age) can influence gut bacterial communities, subsequently affecting bacterial functionality within the gut104. The structure of gut microbiota is often shaped by both habitat and species-specific factors, as highlighted in the previous study105. In the present study, host habitat and sex-specificity were found to play dominant roles in shaping the community networks within the gut of C. striata. In the T1 habitat, certain bacterial associations such as the negative correlations between P. damselae and M. organophilum, and the positive correlations among P. shigelloides, S. marcescens, and M. organophilum were particularly noteworthy. These unique relationships, absent in the T2 habitat, may reflect habitat-specific interactions that are crucial for understanding the ecological roles of these taxa105,106. The absence of such correlations in the T2 habitat may indicate a less dynamic or less stable bacterial community, possibly due to different ecological pressures or resource availability in that environment. Conversely, the T2 habitat exhibited strong positive correlations between M. organophilum and P. copri, and a strong negative correlation between P. damselae and S. marcescens. These relationships suggest distinct ecological interactions and potentially different functional roles for these bacteria in the T2 habitat compared to T1. Sex-specific bacterial relationships were also observed, with strong negative correlations between C. somerae and F. prausnitzii in female fish, and positive correlations between M. organophilum and P. acnes in male fish. These patterns may reflect differences in ecological niches or functional requirements between sexes107. The reduced interactions observed in male networks could be due to lower microbial diversity or differences in community assembly processes. Keystone taxa, which play a critical role in maintaining ecological connectivity within networks and driving community assembly and stability, were identified in both habitats and sexes108. In the T1 habitat, M. organophilum, P. damselae, and P. acnes emerged as key taxa, whereas in the T2 habitat, M. organophilum, F. prausnitzii, and P. copri were prominent. These findings underscore the variability in keystone taxa based on habitat and sex, highlighting their role in shaping community interactions and ecosystem functions106.

Conclusions

This study provided a comprehensive comparative analysis of the gut microbial community compositions, taxonomic co-occurrence networks, predicted metabolic profiles, fatty acid composition, and growth performance of C. striata using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, biochemical analysis, and advanced bioinformatics tools. The results demonstrate significant habitat and sex-specific variations in both the fatty acid composition and gut microbiota of C. striata. Female muscle tissues across different habitats exhibited distinct fatty acid profiles, predominantly featuring palmitic acid, oleic acid, and linoleic acid. High-throughput sequencing revealed a diverse gut microbial community, with notable differences in species richness and diversity between males and females. Females displayed a higher Shannon diversity index and distinct bacterial communities compared to males. Across habitats, Proteobacteria and Firmicutes were the dominant phyla, with sex-specific variations observed at the phylum and genus levels. Females had a higher abundance of Alphaproteobacteria and related taxa, whereas males showed a greater prevalence of Clostridia and Fusobacteriia. Functional predictions using KEGG Orthology analysis uncovered sex-specific differences in gut microbiota functions, particularly in pathways related to metabolism, genetic information processing, and environmental information processing. Females were more enriched in energy metabolism, lipid biosynthesis, and xenobiotic biodegradation, while males showed increased nucleotide and amino acid metabolism. Additionally, key microbial taxa such as M. organophilum, P. stutzeri, and F. prausnitzii were associated with PUFA synthesis and antioxidant production, underscoring their potential metabolic roles in C. striata. Co-occurrence network analysis further highlighted the significance of potential ecological interactions and/or associations in structuring the gut microbiome composition.

Our findings suggest that the gut microbial diversity of C. striata reared under different conditions is not only distinct in composition but also varies in abundance, with significant sex-specific differences. This study highlights the association of gut microbes with various physiological aspects that contribute to the fish’s nutritive value and immunity. Given the biomedical significance of C. striata, the presence of symbiotic gut microbes that aid in the synthesis of medically important compounds such as butyric acid, EPA, and DHA further emphasizes the crucial role of gut symbionts. These insights pave the way for future research in this field. Further investigations are needed to explore the connections between gut bacterial communities and the biochemical composition of C. striata. Future studies should explore the functional implications of the microbial shifts, particularly how dominant taxa influence fish health, growth, and reproduction under different environmental conditions. Additionally, investigating the microbial-metabolic pathways activated in these habitats could provide insights into how environmental conditions influence gut microbiota function. Understanding the complexities of microbial ecosystems through such studies could have practical applications in aquaculture and contribute to species conservation efforts.
