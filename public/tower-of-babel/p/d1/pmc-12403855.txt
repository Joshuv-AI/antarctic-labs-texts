Antidermatophytic activity of Curcuma longa against Trichophyton spp.: compound identification and molecular docking to lanosterol 14α-demethylase
Authors: A. B. Z. Naimur Rahman, Md. Fahad Patwary, Shinjon Ahmed, Mehraz Hossen, Md Hasibul Hossain, Md. Shariful Islam, Rifat Rahman, Swagato Dutta, Khandokar Fahmida Sultana, Mohammad Salim Hossain, Firoz Ahmed, Sabuj Baran Dhar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The increasing incidence of dermatophytic infections and rising resistance to conventional antifungal agents necessitates the exploration of alternative therapies. This study investigates the antifungal potential of Curcuma longa rhizome extract against dermatophytes, particularly Trichophyton species, through a combination of in vitro and in silico techniques. The methanolic extract of Curcuma longa was evaluated for its antifungal efficacy using the disc diffusion method against Trichophyton mentagrophytes, Trichophyton indotineae, and Trichophyton interdigitale. The extract demonstrated significant antifungal activity, with inhibition zones ranging from 10 to 25 mm. Comparative analysis with fluconazole revealed that Curcuma longa extract was effective even against fluconazole-resistant strains. GC–MS analysis identified eleven major compounds, with Curlone (44.19%) as the dominant component. Molecular docking studies targeted lanosterol 14α-demethylase, a key enzyme in ergosterol biosynthesis, revealing strong binding affinities of Curlone, α-Curcumene, 3-Isobutylidene-6,7-dimethyl-3H-isobenzofuran-1-one, and 1-Bisabolone. These compounds demonstrated favorable interactions, suggesting their role in inhibiting fungal growth. Further, ADME and Toxicity analysis highlighted Curlone’s superior pharmacokinetic and toxicity profile, making it a promising candidate for antifungal therapy. This study concludes that the extract of Curcuma longa shows promising potential as a natural alternative to traditional antifungal agents, and further investigation through in vivo studies is recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05049-9.

Introduction

Dermatophytes are a group of pathogenic fungi that may invade the host’s hair, skin, and nails by infecting keratinous tissue [1]. They are primarily classified as Microsporum, Trichophyton, and Epidermophyton, with 30 species (including the most important, Trichophyton indotineae, Trichophyton rubrum, Trichophyton interdigitale, Trichophyton mentagrophytes, Microsporum gypseum, Microsporum canis, and Epidermophyton floccosum) that cause dermatophytosis in humans [2]. Dermatophytes infect people by colonizing the keratinized outermost layer of the skin, often without invading live tissue. The infections involve scalp infection (tinea capitis), foot infection (tinea pedis), groin infection (tinea cruris), and nail infection (tinea unguium or onychomycosis) [3, 4]. The condition, commonly referred to as tinea or ringworm may result from the host’s inflammatory response to the enzymes released by the fungus during its invasion [5, 6]. The protein of dermatophytes, lanosterol 14α-demethylase, encoded by the CYP51 gene, is a critical enzyme in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway, which is essential for maintaining fungal cell membrane integrity [7–9]. As in other harmful fungus, in dermatophytes this enzyme’s suppression disrupts membrane formation, causing cell death [10]. This makes it a well-validated target for development of antifungal drugs. Especially among the most conserved members of the cytochrome P450 (CYP450) group, CYP51 shows great substrate specificity [11]. The larger CYP450 family also participates in the metabolism of primary and secondary substances, therefore highlighting its central importance in pathogen survival and adaptation [11, 12]. Lanosterol 14α-demethylase is a rational and important target for new antifungal medicines against dermatophytic infections given its essential role and conserved character.

All azole drugs share a common mechanism of action, which involves inhibiting the activity of the protein Cytochrome P450 14α-demethylase. These azoles, such as Ketoconazole, Itraconazole, and Fluconazole, possess a potent ability to impede the growth of dermatophytes, particularly those belonging to Trichophyton spp. [13, 14]. In fungal cell membranes, the major sterol component is ergosterol, which plays a pivotal role in regulating essential fungal functions like membrane fluidity and integrity [15]. The suppression of ergosterol production and the accumulation of alternative sterols in the fungal cell membrane lead to enhanced membrane rigidity and permeability. This impairs the function of membrane-bound enzymes, ultimately resulting in the suppression of pathogen proliferation and, finally, cellular demise [16, 17].

In recent times, dermatophytes are a major and highly prevalent disease. This is due to the antifungal drug resistance of the organism and the adverse effects associated with the medications employed to treat fungal infections [18–20]. Therefore, there is a significant need for a more effective, alternative, and safer treatment. There has been a growing focus on utilizing various herbal materials as alternative treatments for certain fungal infections, with numerous natural product compounds being specifically aimed at combating pathogenic fungi [21–23]. Medicinal herbs may serve as a potential alternative for addressing dermatophytosis. Herbal medicines are considered safe and typically do not have side effects [24]. Another significant factor contributing to this resurgence is the widely acknowledged efficacy of numerous traditional medicines. Given this renewed interest, the present study aims to assess herbal plants’ ability to combat dermatophytosis through their antifungal qualities. Following a careful analysis of the literature and taking into account its traditional medical use, Curcuma longa (C. longa) was selected for its potential antifungal activity [25–27]. Prior research has demonstrated that methanol extract of C. longa is efficacious in addressing many fungal infections [28]. Based on this, we decided to use methanol extract in varying doses for our research. This study employed a combination of in vitro methods and a computer-aided drug discovery approach to identify compounds capable of inhibiting fungal proteins, with the goal of discovering potential antifungal agents.

Materials and methods

Plant collection

The plant Curcuma longa was procured from Torabgonj (22°49′01.8"N 90°51′59.4"E), situated around 19 km from Laxmipur, Chittagong, Bangladesh. The plant was authenticated by Dr. Mehede Hassan Rubel, Associate Professor, Department of Agriculture at Noakhali Science and Technology University, Noakhali, with the voucher no. NSTU/AGR/Herb/2023/045. Exclusively rhizome of Curcuma longa was collected for the extract solution. Further, the rhizome was washed well and dried under shade for one week. The plant materials were powdered in a mechanical grinder after drying thoroughly.

Plant extract preparation

Curcuma longa extracts were prepared using a standard methanol extraction method, followed by rotary evaporation [28, 29]. The powdered plant material (25.0 g) was first put in a Methanol solution (250 ml). The extracts underwent vacuum filter filtration, were concentrated in a rotary evaporator, and then desiccator drying. The extraction yielded a reddish-brown solid residue weighing 5.750 g (23.0% w/w). With this extraction technique, higher extract yields were obtained. By using solvent evaporation to get the dry weight of the plant extracts, the concentration in mg/ml was ascertained. The extracts were then stored for further use in sterile flask at 2 to 4 °C in a refrigerator.

Fungal culture

A total of 4 fungal cultures, Trichophyton mentagrophytes (GenBank accession no. PQ394944), Trichophyton indotineae (GenBank accession no. PQ394963, PQ357663), and Trichophyton interdigitale (GenBank accession no. PQ357286) were collected from the fungal laboratory of the Department of Microbiology, Noakhali Science and Technology University.

Determination of antifungal activity

The fungal samples were grown on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) at 30 °C for 5–7 days. A combination of mycelium and conidia was obtained by probing the colonies with the tip of a sterile Pasteur pipette after the conidia and hyphae from SDA cultures were collected in 1 ml of sterile normal saline. After being moved to sterile tubes, the fungal solutions were given 30 min to settle. Disc diffusion test, which was reported by Sing et al. following the CLSI guidelines for antifungal susceptibility testing, was used to assess the in vitro antifungal activity of crude extract and its constituents [30]. Blank sterile discs were placed onto the surface of the blank plate. Three different concentrations of crude extracts, 20 mg/disc, 30 mg/disc and 40 mg/disc, impregnated on the paper disc. The discs were then dried for a few hours. After that, the aseptic swabs were equally spread over the whole Sabouraud dextrose agar plate after being dipped into the fungal solution. The paper discs were then placed on the lawn of fungus. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

GC–MS analysis

To find out whether bioactive chemicals were present in the extracts, GC–MS Analysis was employed to perform phytochemical screening. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis was carried out on a PerkinElmer GC–MS (Model: GC–Clarus 690 and MS–SQ8C) using an Elite–5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mmID × 0.25 µm) (Cat# N9316282) at the Bangladesh Council of Scientific & Industrial Research (BCSIR), Dhaka. The column temperature initially held at 120 °C for 2.00 min, and then temperature was raised to 320 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C/min and held for 10 min. The temperatures of the injector, interface, and ion source were maintained at 220 °C, 180 °C, and 200 °C, respectively. At a rate of 1 ml/min, helium was employed as the carrier gas. An electron ionization mode with an ionization energy of 70 eV was used for mass detection (Scan mode; m/z ratio was 50 amu to 450 amu). The delay in the solvent was two (2) minutes [31].

Pharmacoinformatics elucidation (ADMET profiling)

The ADMETlab 2.0, admetSAR 2.0 and pkCSM online description servers were used to assess the pharmacokinetic characteristics, such as absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) profile of the active components of C. longa rhizome [32–34]. The adsorption, metabolism, and excretion of such chemicals were analyzed using ADMETlab 2.0, admetSAR 2.0 and their distribution characteristics were assessed using pkCSM. Graph-based signatures are employed by the pkCSM to define ADMET characteristics and support drug development. The ProTox-II server was used to calculate the drugs’ toxicity parameters, including mutagenicity, immunotoxicity, carcinogenicity, and hepatotoxicity [35]. ProTox-II service uses pharmacophores, chemical similarities, and several machine-learning algorithms to predict different toxicity endpoints. Additionally, SwissADME [36] and ADMETlab 2.0 servers were utilized to assess factors relevant to topical skin administration.

Protein preparation and binding site prediction

The structure of lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51) (PDB ID: 4LXJ) was obtained using the protein database (https://www.rcsb.org/), a database that contains details on nucleic acid and experimental protein structures. PyMOL, an open-source application for making molecular visualizations, was used to eliminate the water molecules. The amino acids involved in the creation of active pockets were identified using the Computed Atlas for Surface Topography of Proteins (CASTp). In order to align the mesh box prior to docking, the position must be determined.

Ligand preparation

The three-dimensional (3D) structure of the selected active compounds of this plant was taken from the PubChem database in the SDF (Structure Data File) format. In addition, the compounds’ two-dimensional structures were obtained using PubChem. The structures were initially called up in SDF format, and PyMOL was then used to transform them into PDB format.

Molecular docking

PyRx with Autodock Vina was used for the molecular docking investigation of the compounds in order to determine the most stable binding conformations [37]. The grid box for each compound was set with dimensions of X = 30.06, Y = − 4.19 and Z = − 22.94, with a grid spacing of 1.0. PyMOL Molecular Graphics System 2.0 and Discovery Studio 4.5 were used to further depict the docked compounds with greater binding affinities (measured in kcal/mol). To validate the docking protocol, we performed a redocking analysis using the co-crystallized ligand present in the crystal structure of lanosterol 14α-demethylase (PDB ID: 4LXJ). The co-crystallized ligand was extracted and re-docked into the same binding pocket using identical grid coordinates and docking parameters as those applied to the test ligands. The resulting redocked pose was superimposed onto the original crystallographic pose, and the root mean square deviation (RMSD) was calculated to assess pose similarity. An RMSD ≤ 2.0 Å was considered acceptable for protocol validation [38].

In vitro antifungal activity

The Curcuma longa rhizome extract demonstrated notable in vitro antifungal activity against several species of Trichophyton, a group of dermatophytes known to cause skin infections. At concentrations of 20 mg, 30 mg, and 40 mg per disc, the methanol extract was evaluated against Trichophyton mentagrophytes (PQ394944), Trichophyton indotineae (PQ357663, PQ394963), and Trichophyton interdigitale (PQ357286) (Fig. 2 and Table 1). Results showed that the extract inhibited fungal growth, producing inhibition zones ranging from 10 to 25 mm, depending on the concentration and species tested. For example, the inhibition zone increased from 20 mm at 20 mg to 25 mm at 40 mg against Trichophyton indotineae (PQ394963), demonstrating a dose-dependent response. In contrast, at the greatest extract concentration, Trichophyton mentagrophytes (PQ394944) demonstrated a maximum inhibition of 23 mm. As a control, fluconazole, a common antifungal, produced varying inhibition zones ranging from 0 to 23 mm, demonstrating the efficacy of the C. longa extract against fluconazole-resistant strains. Protein–ligand interactions

Protein–ligand interactions of C. longa extracts were characterized by their van der Waals contacts, electrophoresis, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions. The binding affinities between ligands and proteins are largely determined by these interactions. Every chemical showed a wide variety of binding and unbound interactions with several residues either within the site or outside of it. As determined, 1-Bisabolone exhibited a single conventional hydrogen contact with the residue Arg A:98, as well as seven hydrophobic interactions (p-Alkyl and Alkyl interactions) with Leu A:95, Leu A:96, Pro A:238, Phe A:241, Val A:242, Tyr A:72, and His A:381 (Fig. 4). Ten hydrophobic interactions, such as p-Alkyl and Alkyl contacts, p-p Stacked/T-Shaped interactions, and Pi-Cation and Anion interactions, were generated by α-Curcumene in place of conventional hydrogen bonding, with the following amino acid residues: Val A:242, Tyr A:72, Leu A:95, Phe A:241, Phe A:384, Ala A:125, His A:381, Leu A:96, His A:405 and Pro A:238 (Fig. 5). The compound 3-Isobutylidene-6,7-dimethyl-3H-isobenzofuran-1-one showed a single typical hydrogen contact with the residue Arg A:98. Six hydrophobic connections were also generated by it, including p-Alkyl and Alkyl contacts, as well as p-p Stacked/T-Shaped Interactions, with Leu A:95, Pro A:238, Leu A:96, Phe A:241, Ala A:125, and Phe A:384 (Fig. 6). Curlone demonstrated eight hydrophobic contacts and five Vander Waals interactions in total (p-Alkyl and Alkyl contacts) with the following amino acids: Leu A:96, Val A:242, Leu A:95, Tyr A:72, Met A:509, Phe A:384, Phe A:241, and His A:381 (Fig. 7). The positive control, Fluconazole exhibits multiple non-covalent interactions, including van der Waals forces, halogen (fluorine) bonds, carbon-hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic Pi-alkyl and Pi-sigma interactions. Key interacting residues include ILE205, TYR229, LEU221, and ALA226. ADME and toxicity analysis

Following the administration of the drug via any route to the human body or in an animal model, it experiences processes of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, leading to either active or passive transport to the target site. In order to meet these criteria, the analysis of in-silico pharmacokinetics provides insights on the behavior of a molecule within the body. The evaluation of the drug-likeness of the compounds was mainly conducted through Lipinski’s rule of five, which considers factors including molecular weight, topological polar surface area (TPSA), hydrogen bond acceptors, hydrogen bond donors, and the number of rotatable bonds (Nb). According to the molecular docking data, all of the drugs that underwent pharmacokinetic analysis performed exceptionally well in terms of Lipinski’s rule of five and other criteria. In terms of ADMET properties, every drug exhibited positive results for the proportion unbound in plasma, BBB permeability, MDCK permeability, CaCO2 permeability, CNS permeability, volume distribution, and human intestine absorption.

Regarding adsorption, curlone exhibited superior human intestine absorption compared to the other three chemicals. 3-Isobutylidene-6,7-dimethyl-3H-isobenzofuran-1-one exhibited superior caco2 permeability and MDCK. In terms of distribution, α-Curcumene had a higher volume of distribution and CNS permeability whereas curlone had better result for fraction unbound in plasma and BBB permeability (Table 5).

Human cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzyme is crucial in phase I oxidative metabolism and is responsible for the majority of drug biotransformation [39]. Surprisingly, the curlone compounds had very few negative effects on the activity of CYP450 enzymes (Table 5). However, compounds such as 3-Isobutylidene-6,7-dimethyl-3H-isobenzofuran-1-one, alpha curcumene, and 1 bisabolone showed significantly higher inhibition against CYP450 enzymes (specifically CYP1A2 and CYP2C9). The clearance and half-life (t1/2) parameter demonstrated favorable outcomes, with curlone exhibiting a superiority compared to the other chemicals (Table 5).

The skin permeability, as indicated by the logarithm of the skin permeability coefficient (Log Kp), showed considerable variation among the compounds studied. α-Curcumene exhibits the highest predicted skin permeability, while 3-Isobutylidene-6,7-dimethyl-3H-isobenzofuran-1-one shows the lowest. Solubility assessments revealed that all compounds except α-curcumene exhibited good solubility; α-curcumene showed only moderate solubility. Notably, none of the compounds in this study were categorized as insoluble. Transdermal absorption depends on the polar surface area (PSA), with a lower PSA indicating greater skin permeability. α-Curcumene, possessing a PSA of 0.00 Å2, aligns with its high permeability profile. In contrast, 3-Isobutylidene-6,7-dimethyl-3H-isobenzofuran-1-one has a significantly higher PSA of 26.30 Å2, supporting its lower permeability. Curlone and 1-Bisabolone had middle-range PSA values of 17.07 Å2, showing that they can moderately pass through the skin, which is better than 3-Isobutylidene-6,7-dimethyl-3H-isobenzofuran-1-one but not as good as α-curcumene. All four compounds demonstrated potential for poor skin sensitization (Table 5).

Fluconazole was used as a control in this study for comparison with the compounds detected in our results. In terms of absorption, fluconazole demonstrated similar results to human intestinal absorption, showing poor Caco-2 permeability and a negative result for F 20%, similar to curlone. In terms of distribution, fluconazole exhibits poor brain barrier permeability, lower volume of distribution (Vd) (L/kg), and inability to penetrate the CNS. In terms of metabolism, fluconazole performs well and similarly to Curlone for CYP2C9-sub, but is not as effective as α-Curcumene and 1-Bisabolone. Fluconazole displays hepatotoxicity, which is not present in the other four compounds. Furthermore, fluconazole demonstrated reduced skin permeability, indicated by a more negative Log Kp value and relatively high topological polar surface area (TPSA) of 81.65 Å2 (Table 5).

Discussion

The present investigation combined in vitro methods with computer-aided drug development to investigate the antifungal potential of Curcuma longa rhizome extract. The notable in vitro antifungal activity of Curcuma longa rhizome methanol extract against multiple pathogenic Trichophyton species revealed the extract’s potential as a natural therapeutic agent for dermatologic illnesses. Fungal growth was shown to be inhibited by the extract in a dose-dependent manner, with bigger inhibition zones appearing at higher doses. The extract shown noteworthy efficacy in combating fluconazole-resistant strains, implying that it could serve as a substitute or adjunctive therapeutic approach. These findings align with previous studies, such as Khan et al., who reported broad-spectrum antifungal activity for Curcuma longa extract and its nanoparticles, with inhibition zones ranging from 13.5 to 31 mm against Aspergillus niger at concentrations between 5 and 20 mg/ml [26]. Although their focus was on Aspergillus rather than Trichophyton, the comparable inhibition zones support the wide-spectrum activity of turmeric-derived compounds. Our study expands this understanding by demonstrating the extract’s efficacy specifically against dermatophytes involved in dermatophytosis, with inhibition zones reaching up to 25 mm at 40 mg/disc Similarly, Fuloria et al. emphasized the antifungal activity of curcumin and its derivatives but did not quantify inhibition zone sizes or focus on dermatophytes [27]. In contrast, our results suggest curlone, a compound less frequently reported as a dominant contributor to antifungal activity, especially against Trichophyton spp. These results validate Curcuma longa’s historic use in Ayurvedic medicine for treating skin ailments and call for more research into the plant’s active ingredients and mode of action in order to create cutting-edge antifungal treatments [40].

To better understand the bioactive components responsible for this antifungal activity, GC–MS analysis was conducted. Eleven compounds were identified in the methanolic extract, with Curlone (44.19%), 3-Isobutylidene-6,7-dimethyl-3H-isobenzofuran-1-one (17.93%), and Tumerone (17.29%) being the major constituents (Fig. 3, Table 2). Curlone, in particular, is well known for its therapeutic properties and is believed to contribute significantly to the extract’s antifungal potential [41].

To further explore the potential of these compounds, molecular docking studies were conducted against lanosterol 14α-demethylase, a crucial enzyme in fungal sterol biosynthesis. Four major compounds- Curlone, α-Curcumene, 3-Isobutylidene-6,7-dimethyl-3H-isobenzofuran-1-one, and 1-Bisabolone- exhibited strong binding affinities, ranging from −8.2 to −8.7 kcal/mol (Table 3), indicating potential inhibitory effects. These compounds establish permanent contacts with important residues in the enzyme’s active site, according to the molecular docking data. With more specificity, the most conserved protein in the CYP (CYP450) superfamily is lanosterol 14a-demethylase, which is encoded by the CYP51 gene. CYP450s are involved in many of biological activities, including the pathogen’s synthesis of primary and secondary products [12]. Ergosterol is the primary steroid of the fungal cell membrane, controlling the natural processes of the fungus, including fluidity and integrity (Fig. 8A) [15]. The suppression of ergosterol production and the buildup of other sterols on the cell membrane cause the membrane to become stiffer and more permeable, disrupt enzymes that are attached to the membrane, stunt the growth of pathogens, and ultimately cause the cell to die [16, 17]. Furthermore, pharmacokinetic and toxicology analyses were performed to appraise the compounds’ drug-like qualities. ADMET analysis revealed that curlone exhibited excellent pharmacokinetic properties, including superior intestinal absorption and minimal interaction with human cytochrome P450 enzymes, particularly CYP1A2 and CYP2C9 (Table 5). This minimizes the potential for drug-drug interactions, enhancing bioactive compound- curlone safety profile. The compound also displayed favorable toxicity profiles, with no predicted hepatotoxicity, carcinogenicity, or mutagenicity, further supporting its candidacy as an antifungal agent (Table 5).

These findings underscore the potential of C. longa rhizome extract, particularly its major constituent curlone, as a natural source for antifungal therapies. In previous studies curlone was established as one of the major compound in C. longa rhizome. However, its broader therapeutic potential, particularly in antifungal therapy, remains underexplored. In this study, the in vitro antifungal efficacy of curlone has been investigated and the gap in understanding of the mechanism of action has been fulfilled. This study also shows how effective curlone is against both azole sensitive and azole resistant dermatophyte strains. The in vitro antifungal activity, combined with the strong in silico binding affinities and favorable ADMET profiles, suggests that C. longa methanol extract could be developed into a safe and effective treatment for dermatophytosis. To confirm these results and investigate the therapeutic potential of these molecules in practical applications, more in vitro studies like: ergosterol quantification, CYP51 enzymatic assays, or transcriptomic/proteomic analyses, in vivo research and clinical trials are needed.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the significant antifungal potential of Curcuma longa rhizome methanol extract, particularly against dermatophytes, including fluconazole-resistant strains. The methanolic extract exhibited strong in vitro antifungal activity, with key compounds such as curlone showing high binding affinity for lanosterol 14α-demethylase, a crucial enzyme in fungal ergosterol biosynthesis. The molecular docking studies ratified the strong interaction between the enzyme and curlone and demonstrated a potential mode of action in a similar vein to existing known azole antifungals. The chemical curlone’s advantageous ADMET features were further underlined by pharmacokinetic and toxicology tests, confirming its safety and effectiveness as a possible antifungal drug. These results together suggest that C. longa methanolic extract, and more specifically curlone, could be beneficial natural substitutes for the treatment of dermatophytosis, especially resistant fungal infections. However, although the in vitro results are encouraging, further in vivo studies and well-designed clinical trials are required to establish the therapeutic efficacy, safety, and pharmacological mechanisms of this extract in a clinical setting. Further studies along these lines may ultimately pave the way for the development of novel, plant-based antifungal drugs with little or no risk of resistance and side effects.

Limitations of the study

A key limitation of this study is the exclusive reliance on in-silico techniques to characterize the interactions between C. longa phytochemicals and fungal proteins. Although molecular docking provided useful insights into binding affinities, the absence of molecular dynamics simulations in vitro ergosterol quantification, CYP51 enzymatic assays, or transcriptomic/proteomic analyses to identify additional targets, and in vivo experiments weakens the overall conclusions about the compound’s efficacy and stability. Additionally, while in vitro antifungal activity was assessed, in vivo studies are necessary to confirm the pharmacological relevance and therapeutic potential of these compounds in clinical applications. Finally, although in silico ADMET and toxicity predictions using SwissADME and ProTox-II provided initial insights into the pharmacokinetics and safety profiles of the identified compounds, they do not substitute for experimental validation. In vitro cytotoxicity assays using mammalian cell lines are necessary to confirm these predictions and will be considered in future studies.
