Multiyear experience with blood culture results from a tertiary care hospital in Saudi Arabia: a retrospective study
Authors: Abdulaziz H Alhazmi, Ahmed Mohammed Dighriri, Nabil Soliman Dhayhi, Abdulaziz Omar Bamarouf, Saud Mohammad Erwi, Amany Ibrahim Mashi, Moayad Haddad, Abdulmajeed M Talea, Hassan M Khobrani, Layla A Makeen, Ebtehal Y Abutalib, Alallah F Khawaji, Sarah Ibrahim Shubayli, Zahra Mohammed Sherfan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBloodstream infections (BSI) are a significant etiology of morbidity and mortality in hospitalized patients, particularly among critically ill patients in intensive care units (ICU). ICU patients are at a higher risk of BSI due to invasive procedures, prolonged hospitalization, and the prevalence of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs). Blood culture remains the gold standard for diagnosing BSI and its associated resistance pattern. However, contamination during sample collection often limits its utility, and diagnostic accuracy leading to unnecessary antibiotic use, prolonged stay in the ICU, and increased healthcare costs. Understanding trends in blood culture contamination rates and factors associated with BSI in ICU patients is vital for optimizing infection control measures and antimicrobial stewardship programs. This study aims to analyze blood culture contamination rates and explore ICU-associated factors influencing BSI at a tertiary hospital.MethodsThis three-year retrospective study (2018,2022,2023) analyzed 27,544 blood culture samples from a tertiary hospital. Data were retrieved from the medical record system and included bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns. The contaminated blood culture rates over years were calculated and factors associated with BSI among ICU patients in 2022 and 2023 were analyzed using the Chi-square test.ResultsThe study revealed a decrease in the contamination rate between 2018, 2022, and 2023 with a result of 6.3%, 3.7%, and 4.2%, respectively. Patients’ age and hospital wards are the variables associated with the contaminated blood culture. Factors associated with positive blood culture for the ICU admissions in 2022 and 2023, included patient age, results of Gram stain, identified bacteria, resistance pattern, and length of stay. Gram-negative bacteria predominated in positive blood culture with Klebsiella spp. being the most frequently isolated organism across all the studied years. ESBL-producing bacteria remained the most common resistance pattern throughout the study period.ConclusionsThis study reports a reduction in blood culture contamination rates during the studied years, suggesting improvement in clinical practices related to sample collection and microbiological techniques. However, the persistent predominance of ESBL-producing bacteria and Klebsiella spp. indicates the urgent need for further enhanced antimicrobial stewardship programs to combat antimicrobial resistance. Further national studies should focus on hospital-wide interventions to reduce contamination and improve patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11908-0.Keywords: Bloodstream infections, Blood culture, Blood culture contamination, Saudi Arabia, Jazan

Introduction

Bloodstream infections (BSI), including bacteremia, fungemia, and sepsis, are a major source of morbidity and mortality in hospitalized patients worldwide [1, 2]. The blood culture remains the gold standard technique for detecting BSI and guiding the antimicrobial treatment for patient management [1, 2]. A true positive blood culture (i.e., noncontaminated) provides a conclusive diagnosis, enables targeted antimicrobial chemotherapy, and offers prognostic benefits [1, 2]. However, false-positive results due to contamination frequently limit its clinical utility.

Blood culture contamination occurs when microorganisms from the skin or surrounding environment inadvertently enter during the collection process leading to results that do not represent a true BSI [1, 3]. Contaminated blood cultures are a significant quality and safety issue in healthcare settings, as they increase unnecessary diagnostic tests, admissions, antimicrobial exposure, and overall healthcare costs [1, 3]. Various factors have been related to blood culture contamination, such as patient age, busy hospital wards, and the increasing use of catheters and other indwelling vascular access devices has further complicated blood culture interpretation [1, 4–6]. The blood culture contamination rate usually ranges from 0.6 to 6%; however, according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 (2018) recommendations, the acceptable contamination rate in hospitals should not exceed 3% [1]. Some studies report rates as high as 40%, with up to 60% of patients receiving unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotics due to contamination, contributing to increased antimicrobial resistance and financial burdens [7, 8]. Contaminated blood cultures have been associated with additional pharmacy costs (between $210 and $12,611 per patient), additional total laboratory charges (between $2,397 and $11,152 per patient), and prolonged hospital stays (from 1 to 22 days) [7–9].

Despite the clinical and economic impact of blood culture contamination, studies about true BSI and contamination rates are scarce in the region, and none was conducted in the Jazan Province, where about two million people reside. Therefore, this study aims to describe our experience with positive blood culture results at a tertiary hospital in Jazan, Southwestern Saudi Arabia. In this work, we present the rate of blood culture contamination over three years (2018,2022,2023) and assess the prevalence of pathogens detected in BSI and resistance pattern. This study holds particular significance as it provides baseline data for future infection control strategies in the region and is aligned with the planned development of healthcare services in Saudi Arabia under Saudi Vision 2030.

Materials and methods

Study design and area

This is a retrospective study conducted between 2018, 2022, and 2023, at a tertiary hospital in Jazan Province, Saudi Arabia. This tertiary hospital is located in the Southern region of Saudi Arabia, with a capacity exceeding 500 beds. Serving as the region’s central hub for periphery hospitals, it receives a high volume of cases.

Sample size, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

We included non-duplicate samples of positive blood culture results between 2018,2022 and 2023. Clinical samples were collected from patients suspected of infections, and positive cultures underwent processing following the guidelines set by the CLSI [10]. Data were extracted from the microbiology laboratory and encompassed complete bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility results and patterns. All the data was meticulously retrieved, verified, and subjected to statistical analysis. All positive blood culture samples processed in the microbiology laboratory during the study period were included, and duplicate registrations were eliminated from the processed data to ensure that each infection episode was counted only once.

Contamination can occur during the sample collection, processing, or handling, leading to non-pathogenic or environmental bacteria growth. These bacteria include the majority of staphylococcus coagulase-negative species, micrococcus, and other skin flora [7].

Data collection process

The microbiology laboratory employed an electronically completed results system to collect and store data. Demographic information about patients with positive culture results for bacterial infections was extracted from the microbiology laboratory’s computer system. The information included the patient’s age group, hospital ward, admission and discharge date, collection date, isolate identification, and antibiotic susceptibility profile for all isolates. Samples were processed following the guidelines established by the CLSI. Clinical specimens were inoculated onto various agar plates (provided by Saudi Prepared Media Laboratory Company, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia) using sterile disposable loops, depending on the sample type. The culture plates were then incubated at 35–37 °C for 24–48 h.

Bacterial identification

Primary identification of bacterial growth was conducted using manual methods such as colony morphology, Gram staining, and plate culture characteristics. Subsequent identification of bacterial species was performed using the VITEK® 2 automated system (BioMérieux, Craponne, France) following the manufacturer’s instructions. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the identified bacteria were subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing using the VITEK® 2 automated system (BioMérieux, Craponne, France). Data regarding antimicrobial resistance patterns were presented, and the proportion of susceptible isolates was calculated by comparing the total number of susceptible organisms to the total number of organisms tested. Multidrug resistance was defined as resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes. MRSA, CRE, and VRE isolates were confirmed via molecular tests (GeneXpert, Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) for the data collected in 2022 and 2023.

Ethical approval

The research study received approval from the Jazan Research Ethics Committee, part of the Ministry of Health Directorate of Jazan Health Affairs, Saudi Arabia. The study was assigned registry number 117/2019, and approval number 012–2019 was granted on June 16, 2019. This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration and the local guidelines established by the National Committee of Bioethics in Saudi Arabia. The Jazan Research Ethics Committee agreed to waive the informed consent of the participants due to the nature of the study as a retrospective study in which the data used were fully anonymized. The data utilized in this research were collected for clinical purposes and were obtained from patient laboratory databases. Strict confidentiality was maintained for all collected data, ensuring privacy and protection.

Statistical analysis of collected data

The collected data were presented in tabular format, and descriptive analyses were carried out, involving calculating means and generating frequency tables. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS v.23 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Univariate analysis was conducted to investigate individual variables, employing statistical tests such as the chi-squared test for categorical variables. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing values were evaluated, and cases with incomplete demographic or microbiological data were excluded from the final analysis. No imputation methods were applied, and only complete records were used to ensure data reliability.

Results

Pediatric patients contributed to a larger proportion of contaminated cultures in 2022 (48.37%) and 2023 (46.42%) compared to 2018 (27.30%). In contrast, adults had a higher proportion of true positive BSI cultures in 2022 (51.63%) and 2023 (53.58%). The distribution of organisms in positive blood cultures shifted over the years, with a decline in Klebsiella spp. (12.16% in 2018 to 3.25% in 2023), Pseudomonas spp. (6.46% to 1.74%), and ESBL-producing bacteria (10.29% to 3.25%). However, Gram-positive bacteria became more dominant, increasing from 34.69% in 2018 to 86.98% in 2023.

Among the 922 positive blood cultures in 2022 and 2023, 24% (n = 220) were true BSI, while 76% (n = 702) were identified as contamination (Table 2). The ICU had a significantly higher proportion of true BSI (33.64%) compared to contaminated cultures (18.09%) (p-value = 0.0001). Similarly, adult patients had a higher rate of true BSI (62.27%) than contamination (49.57%) (p-value = 0.001). Although the length of stay did not significantly differ between BSI and contamination cases (p = 0.075), longer hospital stays were more frequent in contaminated samples. Seasonal variations in contamination rates were observed in 2018 but not in 2022 or 2023.

Discussion

This study evaluates blood culture results, contamination rates, and resistance patterns based on data from a laboratory of a tertiary hospital in Jazan, Southwestern Saudi Arabia. Blood culture is crucial in diagnosing BSI, guiding appropriate antibiotic therapy, and improving patient outcomes. However, the reliability of this diagnostic tool is hindered by contamination rates, consequently causing unnecessary antibiotic use, resource wastage, and increased healthcare costs [1]. This study revealed a contamination rate of 6.3% in 2018, followed by a notable decline in the recent years of 2022 and 2023 with a rate of 3.74% and 4.25%, respectively (Fig. 1; Table 1). This reduction resulted in rates close to the CLSI recommendation of < 3% [1, 10] and can be attributed to initiatives that were implemented after 2018 such as improved infection control measures, stricter adherence to aseptic blood culture collection techniques, increased staff training and awareness programs, the implementation of dedicated phlebotomy teams, and strengthen antimicrobial stewardship policies. Lessons learned from the COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed to improved hand hygiene compliance, stricter aseptic techniques in sample collection, and increased vigilance in antimicrobial stewardship efforts [11]. These rates that were reported in 2018, 2022, and 2023 are comparable to what has been reported in other regional and international studies [1, 12–20]. Regional studies from Saudi Arabia found higher contamination rates as reported by Hemeg et al. who conducted a prospective study in 2017 in Almadina, Western Saudi Arabia, and reported a 6.6% rate of positive blood culture contamination [1]. Similarly, Al-Hamad et al. observed a contamination rate of 8% in the Eastern region of the country in 2016, which significantly decreased, 6 months later, from 8% to 5% after implementing an educational intervention program targeting nurses [19]. In other international settings, Ramos (2013) in Texas and Bowen et al. (2016) at Stanford reported a contamination rate of 6% and 4%, respectively [13, 14]. The consistent challenges seen across these studies reinforce the need for continuous quality improvement initiatives. Other key findings in our study include reduced ESBL prevalence (10.29% to 3.25%) and decreased Klebsiella spp. and Pseudomonas spp. rates. Despite progress, the slight increase in 2023 indicates the need for continued vigilance and standardized collection protocols.

Our study’s analysis of factors associated with a high rate of contaminated blood cultures revealed significant associations with patient age group and department [Table 1]. Similar findings have been reported by Hemeg et al., who observed a higher likelihood of contamination in samples from elderly patients [1]. Additionally, overcrowded emergency departments have been identified as a hotspot for contamination, attributed to difficulties in establishing intravenous lines in patients of extreme age by inexperienced nursing staff, lack of dedicated phlebotomy teams, and improper aseptic techniques during blood sampling [13–25].

Unlike 2018, no seasonal variation was observed in 2022 and 2023. Hemeg et al. identified two seasonal peaks in contaminated blood culture [1]. These peaks were linked to periods when senior nurses were on leave during the Hajj season and school vacations, which led to higher contamination rates due to crowded conditions. In contrast, the absence of seasonality in 2022 and 2023 that we observed likely reflects enhanced infection prevention and control measures, standardized blood culture collection protocols, and improved staffing policies. These improvements align with Saudi Arabia’s national CLABSI reduction initiative, launched in 2022, which led to a 48.8% decline in CLABSI rates. Key interventions, including clinician training, real-time infection surveillance, and stricter aseptic techniques, have contributed to improved blood sampling practices and reduced contamination rates [12].

Studying positive blood cultures that reflect true BSI in highly critical settings such as the ICU is crucial in healthcare. It serves as a key diagnostic tool for detecting and identifying BSI, guiding effective antibiotic treatment, and enhancing patient outcomes. We compared BSI in ICU patients vs. other patients for the data collected in 2022 and 2023 as they are more recent, robust, and reflect current infection control practices, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and clinical management strategies [Table 3]. Our findings confirm that Gram-negative bacteria predominated in ICU patients, particularly Klebsiella spp. aligning with previous reports that found higher Gram-negative infection rates in ICUs compared to general wards [26–28]. Further, Gram-negative resistance patterns, including ESBL and CRE, were more common in ICU patients, highlighting the urgency of strengthing antimicrobial stewardship in critical care settings.

Conversely, MRSA was more common in non-ICU patients, suggesting a potential reservoir of resistant bacteria in the community. This aligns with existing reports attributing community-acquired MDROs to person-to-person transmission and misuse of antibiotics [26–29]. The emergence of community-acquired CRE is an alarming trend in the Middle East and globally, emphasizing the need for expanded surveillance and targeted infection control strategies [28, 29].

This study indicates the urgent importance of implementing robust hospital-wide antimicrobial stewardship programs to address the challenges posed by MDRO in both ICU and non-ICU settings. The decline of ESBL from 10.29 to 3.25% suggests that current interventions are effective, but sustained efforts are necessary and further studies are required to assess the impact of MDROs at the patient care and public health levels [29–31]. Antimicrobial stewardship programs are essential for ensuring the responsible use of antimicrobials, safeguarding patient safety, and maintaining the effectiveness of antibiotics. These programs employ a multidisciplinary approach to encourage judicious prescribing practices, reduce unnecessary antimicrobial use, and combat the rise of resistant microorganisms. Complementing these efforts, infection control programs focus on preventing and managing the spread of infections within healthcare facilities [30–32].

Although this study provides valuable insights, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, it is a retrospective study focusing on the data from 2018, 2022, and 2023, which limits real-time variability assessment. However, completing this study with recent data strengthens the reliability of the trends observed. It is important to note that after 2018, there were comprehensive renovations and improvements in the hospital’s infrastructure, aligned with the goals of Saudi Vision 2030 in enhancing the healthcare system. These improvements encompassed various projects to reinforce the application of infection control policies, launch an antimicrobial stewardship program, and implement bundles to reduce HAI. However, our study faces limitations in explaining some unusual results, such as the high number of blood culture requests in 2018 compared to 2022 and 2023, and the underlying reasons for the elevated contamination rate. Moreover, we could not confirm the presence of resistant organisms using molecular techniques as such resources were not available at the time of the study in 2018, but data from 2022 to 2023 followed the national protocol of confirming resistance isolates by molecular testing. In addition, including clinical data comprising morbidity and mortality information would significantly enhance the depth and scope of this project. Despite these limitations, we hope that our study contributes valuable insights to the field and lays the foundation for further research and advancements in healthcare practices in the region.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study indicates the critical role of studying positive blood cultures in healthcare settings as a vital diagnostic tool for identifying BSI, guiding appropriate antibiotic therapy, and enhancing patient and overall healthcare outcomes. The decline of contaminated blood culture rates observed in our study between 2018, 2022, and 2023, reflects improvement related to infection control measures, aseptic sampling techniques, and antimicrobial stewardship programs. Though improved, persistent rates between 2022 and 2023 indicate the need for continuous monitoring and staff training. We found factors associated with higher contamination rates, such as patient age and department. Additionally, our findings on the predominance of Gram-negative bacteria and MDRO isolated from ICU patients emphasize the urgent need for targeted antimicrobial stewardship initiatives and infection control strategies to combat the rise of antimicrobial resistance and protect patient safety. Further, longitudinal monitoring of contamination rates and resistance trends is deemed essential. Additionally, future research is needed to explore the impact of specific risk factors on blood culture contamination and to develop innovative strategies to minimize false-positive results and enhance the accuracy of blood culture diagnoses.
