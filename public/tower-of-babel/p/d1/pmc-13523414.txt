Cardiac Myeloid Sarcoma Arising From a Mediastinal Mass Encasing the Aorta
Authors: Wassim Assaad, Patrick Sarkis, Michael B. Gotway, D. Eric Steidley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyeloid sarcoma is a rare extramedullary tumor most commonly associated with acute myeloid leukemia. Cardiac involvement is rare and can pose a diagnostic challenge.Case SummaryA 40-year-old male with a history of allogenic bone marrow transplant for acute myeloid leukemia presented with angina. Computed tomography angiography raised concern for acute aortic syndrome. Multimodality imaging demonstrated an infiltrative mass encasing the ascending aorta with atrial extension. Conventional biopsy approaches were unsafe due to the lack of a suitable percutaneous window and hemodynamic risks. Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) guided biopsy-enabled safe tissue sampling confirming myeloid sarcoma. The patient received chemotherapy followed by repeat transplant, achieving remission.DiscussionThis case highlights a diagnostic pitfall in which an infiltrative malignancy mimicked acute aortic syndrome and highlights the role of ICE-guided biopsy as a safe, alternative diagnostic strategy when standard routes are unsafe.Take-Home MessageMultimodality imaging and ICE-guided biopsy are effective and safe diagnostic strategies for complex intracardiac/mediastinal masses.

History of Present Illness

A 40-year-old male presented initially to his primary care physician with symptoms of sharp subxiphoid chest pain of 1 week duration. Subsequent focused bedside ultrasound of the ascending aorta raised concerns for abnormalities in patient's aorta, necessitating urgent referral to the emergency department. Computed tomography (CT) aortography demonstrated findings concerning for a possible intramural hematoma of the ascending aorta with a small pericardial effusion concerning for acute aortic syndrome. The patient was emergently transferred to our tertiary center for further evaluation and management. On arrival at our institution, the patient reported sharp subxiphoid chest pain of 1 week duration. He had slightly elevated blood pressure at 130/100 mm Hg and a heart rate of 75 beats per minute with otherwise normal vital signs. On physical examination, he did not appear to be in acute distress and had otherwise normal cardiovascular, pulmonary, and neurological examinations. Laboratory studies on admission showed normal complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel except for a mildly elevated alanine aminotransferase at 142 μ/L (Normal 7-55 U/L). Due to concerns for acute aortic syndrome, an esmolol infusion was started for blood-pressure and heart-rate control. Given the patient's clinical stability, resolution of pain, and recent apixaban use, a surgical intervention was deferred unless hemodynamic instability ensues. Repeat CT angiogram of the chest and abdomen demonstrated a large soft-tissue mass encasing the ascending aorta, separating it from the pulmonary arteries. The mass invaded the pericardium, inferior vena cava, and right atrium, extending to the inferior interatrial septum (Figure 1). Past Medical History

The patient had a past medical history significant for acute myeloid leukemia (AML) with a t (6; 11) status after bone marrow transplant (BMT), medullary thyroid cancer status after thyroidectomy and radioiodine therapy resulting in secondary hypothyroidism, left upper-extremity deep venous thrombosis on apixaban, and graft-versus-host disease with eye and oral mucosa involvement.

Differential Diagnosis

The primary differential diagnosis included post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) and extramedullary relapse of AML. In addition, less likely considerations included infectious and noninfectious aortitis, lymphoma, and acute aortic syndrome.

Investigations

Subsequent evaluation included transthoracic echocardiography, which showed a 5.5 × 5.8 cm, immobile right atrial mass adherent to the septal, lateral, and posterior walls (Figure 2, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3, Video 4). [18F]Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT) scan demonstrated intense tracer uptake within a 6.6 × 7.7 × 5.5-cm right atrial mass, with extension into the interatrial septum and narrowing of the superior and inferior vena cava. Additional tracer-avid mediastinal and upper abdominal lymphadenopathy was noted (Figure 3). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging redemonstrated infiltrative abnormal soft tissue encasing the ascending aorta with extension to the base of the heart, appearing as a mass-like structure at the base of the right atrium. A tissue diagnosis was required to establish a definitive cytologic and immunohistochemical diagnosis. Interventional radiology deemed percutaneous biopsy unsafe due to the lack of an acceptable access window. Endobronchial ultrasound-guided biopsy (EBUS) was also deemed unsafe due to concerns of hemodynamic compromise in the presence of a large atrial mass, the use of positive pressure ventilation, and general anesthesia. A decision was made to pursue intracardiac echocardiography (ICE)-guided biopsy of the right atrial mass under conscious sedation. Venous access was obtained via right femoral and right internal jugular veins. ICE imaging was used to localize the mass and guide the biopsy. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry demonstrated immature myeloid cells that showed positivity for CD34, CD117, CD68, CD15, and lysozyme consistent with myeloid lineage differentiation. Epstein barr virus-encoded RNA staining was absent, effectively ruling out PTLD and supporting myeloid sarcoma (MS) (Figure 5). Management

Following a histopathologic confirmation of cardiac MS, the patient was initiated on high-dose systemic cytarabine. A subsequent PET-CT done 60 days after the initiation of the therapy demonstrated marked reduction in radiotracer uptake, consistent with a significant treatment response (Figure 6). One month later, the patient underwent a second allogenic BMT. Outcomes and Follow-Up

One year following the patient's allogenic BMT, surveillance PET-CT scan showed tracer avid mediastinal lymphadenopathy and intracardiac mass involving the left-ventricle outflow tract, interatrial septum, and right atrium suggestive of disease recurrence. Bone marrow biopsy and EBUS of mediastinal lymphadenopathy confirmed recurrence of AML with extramedullary involvement. The patient is currently undergoing therapy with cladribine, low-dose cytarabine, and venetoclax.

Discussion

MS, also described in the literature as chloroma, granulocytic sarcoma, myeloblastoma, or extramedullary myeloid cell tumor, is an extramedullary proliferation of myeloid blast lineages that occur outside the bone marrow.1 MS may occur concurrently with AML, is reported in 2% to 8% of AML cases, or may arise as an isolated lesion in patients with normal appearing bone marrow, with AML developing later.1 MS may also serve as the first manifestation of AML relapse after BMT occurring in 5% to 12% of patients who receive a transplant.2 Data regarding extramedullary relapse following a second allogenic BMT is limited. In one cohort of 19 patients who underwent a second allogenic BMT, the incidence of extramedullary relapse relapse was around 30.9%.2 In patients with prior allogenic BMT, the differential diagnosis of a right atrial/mediastinal mass is broad and includes MS, PTLD, de novo sarcoma/lymphoma, and thrombotic or infectious etiologies.3 Commonly MS affects skin, soft tissues, lymph nodes, gastrointestinal tract, and bones. Cardiac involvement is uncommon and described in case reports rather than large studies.4 Among patients with cardiac infiltration, clinical manifestations occur in <1% of patients and are typically nonspecific, most often reflecting a pericarditis-like presentation.4,5 Early recognition and accurate diagnosis of MS is important to direct appropriate therapy, and a multimodality imaging approach assists in this effort,6,7 particularly in regards to obtaining a tissue biopsy.

This case is unique in that infiltrative cardiac MS initially was confused for an acute aortic syndrome on CT, highlighting an important diagnostic pitfall in post-transplant patients presenting with chest pain. The presence of soft-tissue encasement of the vascular structure, particularly the ascending aorta in this case, with evidence of pericardial effusion may be misinterpreted as intramural hematoma or inflammatory aortopathy on CT angiography, especially in the absence of classic features like intimal flap or contrast extravasation. Infiltrative malignancies, including MS and PTLD, may demonstrate homogenous or mildly heterogenous attenuation that can resemble hemorrhagic or inflammatory processes when viewed in isolation. In this case, repeat imaging and the use of different imaging techniques, combined with clinical stability and resolution of pain, were essential in narrowing the differential diagnosis. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in post-transplant patients, particularly with atypical presentations.

A definitive diagnosis of most mediastinal/cardiac masses requires histological confirmation by cytology and immunohistochemistry.8,9 This is important as an accurate diagnosis is essential to provide information on prognosis and to guide surgical and neoadjuvant treatment strategies.9 However, biopsy approaches can be limited by anatomical considerations and procedural risk.

Tissue sampling can be done surgically via open heart surgery, thoracotomy, or exploration of metastatic sites, but such approaches are invasive and associated with perioperative morbidity. EBUS may be considered when there is mediastinal or pulmonary lymph node involvement but typically requires general anesthesia and positive pressure ventilation. In patients with large intracardiac or mediastinal masses, especially those causing hemodynamic compromise, the risk associated with general anesthesia and application of positive pressure ventilation may be prohibitive. Percutaneous CT-guided biopsy represents another potential sampling strategy but may be limited by the absence of a safe window and carries risks of hemorrhage, injury to adjacent structures, tamponade, and pneumothorax.

ICE is an imaging modality commonly used in electrophysiology and interventional cardiology procedures. It provides high-resolution, real-time intracardiac visualization; is well-tolerated by patients; and can be performed under conscious sedation.5,8,9 Moreover, it can reduce fluoroscopy time.9 In this case, comprehensive imaging using cardiac CT, magnetic resonance imaging, and PET-CT identified potential anatomical challenges and hemodynamic risks that rendered conventional biopsy approaches unsafe. Specifically, both interventional radiology and pulmonary medicine teams believed that percutaneous CT-guided biopsy and EBUS posed significant risk to the patient given the lack of a safe biopsy window, the need for positive pressure ventilation, and general anesthesia. Consequently, ICE-guided biopsy under conscious sedation was pursued, enabling precise, real-time visualization and safe tissue acquisition. Although this approach has been described in the literature, it remains underutilized.

Management of cardiac MS, particularly after allogenic BMT, remains challenging and is not guided by standardized protocols. Systemic AML-directed chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of therapy, although anthracycline-based regimens may be limited by cardiac toxicity.4 Alternative regimens like etoposide, mitoxantrone, and cytarabine have also been described in post-transplant extramedullary relapse. Hypomethylating agents like azacytidine have been used in older patients with impaired cardiac function. Targeted therapies guided by molecular profiling have shown success in isolated reports, although data continues to be limited.1 Radiotherapy can provide local control, with modern image-guided radiation therapy/intensity-modulated radiation therapy techniques enabling targeted treatment of cardiac lesions.4 The role of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in MS has been evaluated in retrospective series showing the potential role for allogenic BMT in prolonging survival. In a retrospective study of 118 patients, the median survival was only 4 months, while among the 11 patients who underwent allogeneic BMT, the median survival was 19 months.10 There are no standardized surveillance protocols for patients with extramedullary AML following remission or during treatment. Current practice mostly involves a combination of standard AML surveillance strategies with targeted imaging and site-directed assessment. FDG-PET/CT is recommended by current guidelines for evaluating metabolically active extramedullary disease, with surveillance tailored to individualized risk assessment and clinical context.

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in patients undergoing BMT presenting with atypical symptoms and emphasizes the benefits of a multimodality imaging approach for guiding diagnostic reasoning and procedural planning. Moreover, ICE-guided biopsy represents a safe, minimally invasive, and effective alternative that can facilitate timely diagnosis and prompt initiation of appropriate therapy.Take-Home Messages•Cardiac myeloid sarcoma is an uncommon but important manifestation of extramedullary acute myeloid leukemia relapse after allogenic bone marrow transplant and may present with nonspecific symptoms.•Integration of multimodality imaging is essential for distinguishing an infiltrative mediastinal and cardiac mass and for defining the anatomic extent and safe biopsy windows.•Intracardiac echocardiography-guided biopsy offers a safe alternative for tissue sampling of complex mediastinal/cardiac masses when conventional biopsy approaches are limited.
