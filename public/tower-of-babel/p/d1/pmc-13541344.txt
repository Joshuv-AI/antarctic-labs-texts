Central Diabetes Insipidus as a Rare Cause of Polyuria
Authors: Stuti Bhandari, Samyama Sagare Venkatesh, L.U Chirag, Rajeev Gowda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Central diabetes insipidus (CDI) is a rare condition characterized by arginine vasopressin deficiency, leading to persistent polyuria and polydipsia. Although it can present at any age, idiopathic CDI in young adults is uncommon and diagnostically challenging. Inflammatory conditions such as infundibuloneurohypophysitis must be considered, particularly when magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reveals a thickened pituitary stalk and absence of the posterior pituitary bright spot. Early recognition and differentiation from diabetes mellitus, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, and primary polydipsia are crucial for effective management. We report the case of a 20-year-old male who presented with excessive urination and thirst for 6 weeks. Physical examination was unremarkable, and the patient had no history of trauma or systemic illness. Urine osmolality was markedly low, and MRI of the brain showed absence of the posterior pituitary bright spot and thickening of the infundibulum, suggestive of infundibuloneurohypophysitis. The patient responded well to desmopressin therapy with symptomatic improvement, thus supporting the diagnosis of CDI. This case highlights the importance of considering idiopathic CDI in young adults presenting with unexplained polyuria and polydipsia. Infundibuloneurohypophysitis should be suspected in patients with characteristic MRI findings even in the absence of overt systemic autoimmune disease. A methodical diagnostic approach, including hormone assays, imaging, and therapeutic trials, can enable early diagnosis and effective long-term management.

INTRODUCTION

Central diabetes insipidus (CDI) is a rare disorder characterized by a deficiency in arginine vasopressin (AVP), also known as antidiuretic hormone (ADH), resulting in impaired renal water reabsorption and leading to persistent polyuria and polydipsia. Unlike nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (NDI), which arises from renal resistance to AVP, CDI stems from dysfunction in the hypothalamic–neurohypophyseal axis.

The condition is rare, with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 25,000 individuals, and can occur at any age but is most frequently diagnosed in childhood or early adulthood. The etiology may be idiopathic, where no structural or systemic cause is identified, or secondary to a broad range of conditions.[1] Patients typically present with persistent polyuria, nocturia, and intense thirst. If unrecognized or untreated, patients may develop hypernatremia, dehydration, and fatigue.

Initial evaluation should include fasting blood glucose, hemoglobin A1c, and urinalysis to exclude common causes of polyuria and nocturia such as diabetes mellitus, urinary tract infections, and renal tubular acidosis. Once these are excluded, further diagnostic workup with the water deprivation test, the gold standard test, is performed, often followed by desmopressin administration for confirmation.[2] In confirmed cases, neuroimaging with brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the hypothalamopituitary region is recommended to assess for structural abnormalities, though normal findings do not exclude idiopathic disease.[34]

CASE REPORT

A 20-year-old male presented with excessive urination (10–15 times/day, including 5–6 nocturnal episodes) and intense thirst for 6 weeks. He had no polyphagia, fever, weight loss, headaches, trauma, or visual disturbances. Family and medical history were unremarkable.

Routine laboratory tests were normal, including fasting glucose and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Serum osmolality was 277 mOsm/kg, sodium 145 mmol/L [Table 1], and urine osmolality significantly reduced at 74 mOsm/kg. Urine sodium was 29 mEq/L [Table 2]. MRI of the brain revealed the absence of the posterior pituitary “bright spot” and a thickened infundibulum (3.9 mm near the optic chiasm), suggesting infundibuloneurohypophysitis [Figures 1-3].

DISCUSSION

Diabetes insipidus is broadly classified into two main types: CDI, resulting from deficient secretion of ADH, and NDI, which arises from renal insensitivity to ADH. In cases of CDI, serum osmolality and serum sodium levels are typically elevated, while urine osmolality is reduced. Urine sodium levels are variable, but are often found to be decreased. In this patient, serum osmolality was within normal limits, serum sodium was at the upper end of the normal range, and both urine osmolality and urine sodium levels were decreased. These results are consistent with those commonly observed in CDI.[1]

The particular MRI findings of absent pituitary bright spot in preenhanced T1-weighted imaging and thickened pituitary infundibulum also point us in the direction of CDI.[34] One possible mechanism underlying the absence of the posterior pituitary “bright spot” on T1-weighted MRI involves depleted vasopressin stores. In a study of water-deprived and subsequently rehydrated rabbits, dehydration was associated with increased plasma vasopressin levels and significant decrease in T1 signal intensity, whereas rehydration led to a decline in vasopressin levels with recovery of the T1 signal intensity. This demonstrated a strong inverse linear correlation between tissue vasopressin concentration and MRI brightness. These findings support the hypothesis that the hyperintense “bright spot” reflects the stored neurosecretory granules of vasopressin.[5]

The water deprivation test was advised for our patient, but could not be performed due to the patient’s refusal. Administration of desmopressin is the next appropriate step to support a definitive diagnosis. Upon administration, in CDI, there is a robust increase in urine osmolality (typically >50% from baseline), indicating intact renal responsiveness to AVP.[12] Additional tests include direct measurement of plasma ADH or measurement of copeptin, the C-terminal part of AVP precursor, which is considered a reliable marker of AVP secretion and a useful alternative to vasopressin measurement. However, these tests are not routinely performed.[6]

CDI may have either a genetic or an acquired etiology. Despite this wide range of possibilities, a large number of acquired cases are idiopathic. Idiopathic CDI is reported to be accountable for 10%–30% of the cases in spite of the advancements in radiology.

Hereditary CDI is a rare condition, and given the patient’s age and absence of a positive family history, this diagnosis is unlikely. Wolfram syndrome, a rare autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by CDI, juvenile-onset diabetes mellitus, optic nerve atrophy, and sensorineural hearing loss, has been reported in several cases, but is considered less likely in this patient. The absence of hyperglycemia, along with a normal HbA1c level and lack of supportive clinical evidence, argues against this diagnosis.[7]

Several differential diagnoses for acquired CDI, including Immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4)-related hypophysitis, were considered. However, the patient’s normal serum IgG4 levels and absent signs of panhypopituitarism make this diagnosis less probable. An alternative possibility is granulomatous hypophysitis, which includes causes such as sarcoidosis and tuberculosis. However, this patient’s normal ACE levels, normal sputum acid-fast bacilli, normal chest X-ray, and no known history of sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, or Wegener’s granulomatosis reduce the likelihood of this diagnosis. The patient has had no prior surgical interventions or history of trauma, and the MRI findings do not show evidence of any pituitary or hypothalamic masses. Other common causes include Langerhans cell histiocytosis. Nevertheless, a conclusive diagnosis requires a pituitary biopsy, but this carries significant risk and was not performed in this patient.

Infectious etiologies and recent reports implicating postviral inflammatory changes, like SARS-CoV-2 infection, and presence of anti-rabphilin-3A antibodies, a specific diagnostic marker of lymphocytic infundibuloneurohypophysitis (LINH), have expanded the recognized spectrum of potential triggers.

Several pathophysiological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the development of CDI secondary to the COVID-19. Notably, the expression of ACE2 receptors in the paraventricular nucleus renders it susceptible to SARS-CoV-2. Emerging evidence suggests that the virus may penetrate the brain parenchyma, potentially inducing reversible inflammation-mediated hypophysitis or directly infecting the hypothalamus, thereby leading to hypothalamic–pituitary axis dysfunction.[8]

An alternative hypothesis suggests that the high prevalence of anti-rabphilin-3A antibodies in patients with LINH supports its role as an underlying cause of idiopathic CDI. Rabphilin-3A is known to be expressed in the posterior pituitary as well as in AVP-producing neurons of the hypothalamus. It is proposed that the development of anti-rabphilin-3A antibodies occurs as a secondary autoimmune response triggered by the onset of LINH, which leads to inflammatory destruction of the posterior pituitary and subsequent CDI. As rabphilin-3A appears to be a key autoantigen in LINH, detection of these antibodies may offer a useful diagnostic marker to distinguish LINH-related CDI from other etiologies.[9]

Nevertheless, the cornerstone of treatment is desmopressin, which is a synthetic vasopressin analog. Treatment response is assessed based on clinical symptoms, and the dose is gradually titrated until adequate control is achieved. It was noted that in most people, sufficient antidiuresis was attained at a total daily maintenance dose of 200–600 μg divided into 2 or 3 doses.[12] One of the most common complications seen after initiation of desmopressin is hyponatremia since water that surpasses the physiological need is no longer being excreted. Other side effects seen are dizziness, headache, flushing, tachycardia, nausea, and vomiting.[10]

This particular patient was advised a combination regimen of 100 μg of desmopressin twice daily. The patient’s initial complaints of polyuria, in which he experienced approximately 10–15 episodes of urination during the day, accompanied by frequent and bothersome nocturnal awakenings due to the urge to void, significantly impacted his quality of life, particularly by disrupting his sleep. Following the initiation of treatment, within 1 month, there was a marked improvement in his urinary symptoms, with a reduction in urine output and frequency to 2–3 times per day and a complete resolution of nocturia, indicating a favorable therapeutic response.[10]

Respecting the patient’s informed preference for pituitary MRI over water deprivation testing exemplifies shared decision-making (SDM) in diagnostic evaluation, aligning the workup with individual values while confirming central diabetes insipidus.[1112] This approach upholds ethical autonomy and minimizes procedural burden, especially when high-yield imaging suffices amid uncertain incremental benefits of dynamic tests.[12] Endocrinology evidence demonstrates that integrating patient preferences enhances engagement and quality of care without safety trade-offs.

CONCLUSION

This case underscores the need for high clinical suspicion of CDI in patients with unexplained polyuria and polydipsia. Despite extensive workup, some cases remain idiopathic. Imaging findings and treatment response can guide diagnosis when invasive testing is not feasible. Early diagnosis and individualized therapy with desmopressin significantly improve patient outcomes and quality of life. Hence, the case emphasizes that respect for patient autonomy within shared decision-making is not a concession but a quality marker of endocrine practice, enabling protocol-adherent yet patient-tailored diagnostic pathways in central diabetes insipidus.
