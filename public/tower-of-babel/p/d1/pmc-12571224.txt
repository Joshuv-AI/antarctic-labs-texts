Combined endoscopic and laparoscopic surgery for staged repair of complex cloacal anomalies: A case series
Authors: Erica M. Weidler, Matthew Ralls, Gwen Grimsby, Marcus Jarboe, Casey Calkins, Kathleen van Leeuwen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The combined endoscopic and laparoscopic surgery (CELS) technique can be used during reconstruction for infants with complex congenital anomalies. In persistent cloacal malformations, this approach permits delineation of challenging anatomy and facilitates safer surgical separation of structures while minimizing tissue trauma for future procedures.Cases presentation:Three patients with persistent cloacal malformations and complex upper tract reproductive anatomy underwent the CELS technique during their series of reconstructive procedures. The average age at first stage reconstruction, that included anorectoplasty, was 14 months (range 9–22). The average follow-up length from the time of reconstruction was 6.5 years (range 4–9 years). All patients have since undergone colostomy takedown. Two patients have undergone delayed vaginal pull-through and/or introitoplasty, and one patient has not yet undergone vaginoplasty.Conclusion:The CELS technique may help reduce complications from open surgery, allows time for structural development, and minimizes tissue dissection and trauma at an early age. Staged repair for complex cloaca can be safely considered and allow for delay of vaginal reconstruction with minimal adverse short-term outcomes and impact on subsequent procedures.

Introduction

Persistent cloacal malformation occurs when the rectum, vagina, and urethra remain as one common channel that usually presents as a single perineal orifice at birth [1]. Traditionally, patients undergo fecal diversion and urgent drainage of any obstructions in the first few days of life. A single stage repair of the cloaca may be offered within the first year of life. This approach has a high rate of complications involving the genitourinary system (e.g., recurrent fistula, prolapse, orifice stricture/stenosis, wound dehiscence) reportedly ranging from 9 to 57 % [2]. Recently, there has been a call to improve the care of these complex patients through innovative approaches [3]. Here we present a case series of patients with persistent cloacal malformation, highlighting a staged approach facilitated by the combined endoscopic and laparoscopic surgery (CELS) technique.

CELS is a technique that serves as an adjunct to laparoscopic surgery alone. In addition to the laparoscope in the abdomen, a second scope within the urinary tract/vaginal tract/urogenital sinus is placed to help identify structures, their location, their relationships, and to help with tissue retraction intraoperatively. Additionally, instruments can be used in lieu of a second scope; for example, a sound or other dilator may be placed within the common channel to provide counter-traction while performing laparoscopic dissection to separate the rectum from the vagina. The staged approach to cloaca repair, where the reconstruction of the urinary, vaginal, and anorectal openings may not occur in the same procedure, is made feasible by the CELS technique. This repeated use of minimally invasive laparoscopy results in less tissue trauma and adhesive scarring, making each subsequent procedure safer and less technically challenging. Thus, the goals of using the CELS technique during staged cloaca repair are to: 1) perform diagnostic evaluation of the internal anatomy and structures, and 2) facilitate laparoscopic dissection and allow the use of minimally invasive techniques in cloaca repair.

The principles guiding this approach are described in this case series. This manuscript was prepared following the CARE guidelines (https://www.care-statement.org)

Case series

This was a single-institution retrospective case series of three patients with cloacal anomalies who underwent staged cloaca repair using the CELS technique during their reconstructive procedures. The Institutional Review Board granted an exemption for this study and informed consent was obtained from the three cases described in this manuscript. Charts were reviewed for common channel anatomy, Mullerian structure anatomy, operative procedures performed, age at operation, operative time, complications, and outcomes.

Preoperative preparation and operative details

The patients underwent the staged approach based on the principle that multiple operations would be required, leading to eventual full reconstruction with an anus, a vaginal opening, and an orifice to pass urine. First, immediate needs such as bowel, bladder, and/or vaginal outlet obstruction are addressed in the first days of life. Particularly in the case of urinary obstruction, the goal is to prevent upstream organ injury and maintain kidney health and function. Operations are then planned for diagnostic evaluation and to delineate anatomy; creation of anus/vaginal opening/urinary orifice are considered at each operation if technically safe and feasible. During these operations, the use of CELS allowed direct visualization/exploration of pelvic anatomy, while minimizing tissue trauma and disruption for future procedures. At our institution, this operative diagnostic evaluation is performed in addition to advanced imaging including magnetic resonance imaging and ultrasound, which may not be sensitive enough in infants to detect/identify all structures.

For all three patients, a multidisciplinary preoperative approach was undertaken, and families were involved in a shared decision-making process that included regular evaluation of their understanding of the conditions, treatment options, and treatment rationale through checklists and a dedicated patient care coordinator. Teams involved included pediatric surgery, pediatric urology, pediatric and adolescent gynecology (PAG), pediatric plastic surgery, and pediatric psychology.

Operatively, two teams of experienced surgeons were involved in each case, often working concurrently: Team 1 was the laparoscopic team performing the intra-abdominal exploration, dissection, and tissue separation. Team 2 was the reconstruction team performing cystoscopy/vaginoscopy where necessary/possible, and the perineal reconstruction. The specialties scrubbed on each team varied between cases, based on the skillsets required. Multiple methods were used to delineate the anatomy and facilitate the safe separation of structures including simultaneous cystoscopy and laparoscopy with instrumentation (using catheters or dilators) of the various fistulous connections (Figs. 1–3).

Individual case descriptions

Case 1:

Patient born at 36 weeks gestation underwent diverting colostomy through an open left lower quadrant incision. No Mullerian structures were visualized. At five months of age, examination under anesthesia (EUA) and cystoscopy were performed and a two-centimeter common channel was present to the confluence of the urethra and an intestinal fistula was documented, but no separate vaginal opening was noted. Patient was voiding from the common channel. A magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan was also performed at five months and demonstrated caudal regression and sacral agenesis. The ultrasound (US) showed two kidneys on the right side with crossed fused ectopia. No uterus was identified on MRI or US. At nine months, a laparoscopic anorectoplasty was performed via a CELS approach and no Mullerian structures were seen during the reconstruction. At 18 months, a colostomy takedown and a laparoscopic cecostomy tube was performed for antegrade enemas. At age 3.5 years, the patient was noted to have difficulty with passing urine and repeat cystoscopy was performed. A short vagina was identified with no cervix, consistent with the absent uterus. An introitoplasty with a split omega-shaped perineal flap was performed with mobilization of the posterior vagina. This allowed better egress of urine. At age 6.5 years, the patient underwent urinary dynamic testing which demonstrated a leak point at 100 cc. A bladder augmentation was performed with harvested small intestine and the appendix was used for the catheterizable channel. Future needs include vaginal dilation versus vaginal reconstruction. The patient is now 10 years old and is followed under a prospective outflow tract obstruction protocol monitoring for functional Mullerian structures, if present.

Case 2:

Patient born at 34 weeks gestation with known persistent cloaca and no patent openings on the perineum. Underwent urgent diverting colostomy, vesicostomy, and vaginostomy tube placement on first day of life via an open approach. At two months of age, an MRI was performed that demonstrated a duplicated vagina with two hemiuteri and an atrophic right kidney. At this time, the vaginostomy tube was no longer productive and was removed. At 13 months, an EUA and cystogram was performed through a 5 French feeding tube placed in the now patent opening on the perineum and the fistulous connection from the vagina to the bladder was demonstrated. At 22 months, the patient underwent laparoscopic anorectoplasty via a CELS approach; the distal intestine inserted into the posterior vagina at the level of the pelvic inlet. At 27 months, a colostomy takedown was performed. At age five years, cystoscopy via the vesicostomy was performed that demonstrated no bladder neck and the small fistulous connection to the vagina. At this time urodynamics were accomplished by placing a foley with balloon inflated into the vesicostomy and placing traction on the foley. Contrast was used and fluoroscopy during the testing revealed reflux into the left ureter at 100 cc and no reflux into the vagina. At 5.5 years of age, the patient underwent creation of an appendiceal vesicostomy with bladder augmentation. During this laparotomy, the vaginas were separated from the posterior bladder and the fistulous connection was oversewn. A feeding tube was placed as a stent into the left ureter through the open bladder to aid in the dissection and urogenital separation. A portion of the longitudinal vaginal septum was resected and a vaginal pull-through was performed by creating an omega-shaped skin flap on the perineum and mobilizing the distal vagina to reach the flap. Multiple dilations of the vaginal pull-through were required under anesthesia over the next six months to maintain patency. The patient is now nine years old and is followed under a prospective outflow tract obstruction protocol to evaluate uterine function and to address vaginal patency as she has vaginal stenosis.

Case 3:

Patient born at 38 weeks gestation with known cloacal exstrophy and omphalocele. On day of life one, she underwent omphalocele repair, pelvic osteotomies with closure of the bladder halves, and creation of a vesicostomy. The distal intestine was found to be inserted into the hindgut and was brought out as an end colostomy. At eight months, MRI showed a normal spine and sacrum with possible visualization of the left hemiuterus, but difficulty visualizing the right hemiuterus. At 10 months, an EUA with cystoscopy through the vesicostomy noted a small bladder. At 11 months, a laparoscopic anorectoplasty was performed with takedown of the end colostomy and creation of a loop diverting colostomy. The patient was noted to have two diminutive hemivaginas and hemiuteri inserting into the hindgut. At 13 months, the colostomy was taken down and a laparoscopic cecostomy tube was placed into the cecum in the right abdomen. At age four years, the vesicostomy was taken down and an appendicovesicostomy was created using the reconstructed bladder and hindgut. The hemivaginas were each separated from the hindgut and left in the abdomen, marked with prolene suture. The patient is now seven years old and will be followed for uterine function via the outflow tract obstruction pathway. Vaginal reconstruction will be considered in the future as part of a shared decision-making process with the patient and family.

Table 1 outlines the series of procedures performed to date for each patient. The average age at first CELS-related reconstruction, consisting of at least an anorectoplasty, was 14 months (range 9–22). The average operative time was 281 minutes (range 195–420) and mean length of follow up was 6.5 years (range 4–9 years). All patients have undergone colostomy takedown and are stooling well on either a medical bowel regimen or enema program.

Discussion

Persistent cloacal malformations often have complex anatomy and require challenging surgical solutions. Shortly after birth, certain aspects of cloaca must be addressed urgently to achieve fecal management as well as relieve urinary or vaginal obstruction. After early management of these urgent issues, patients with persistent cloacal malformation have then traditionally undergone definitive reconstructive surgery. This is typically a single stage open complex surgery that includes anorectoplasty, mobilization and in some cases, separation of urogenital structures (bladder from vagina), and creation of separate perineal openings. Due to the complex and intricate nature of the long procedure, it has a high risk of complications with urinary and fecal incontinence being commonly cited problems [4,5]. The procedure involves a very technically difficult dissection that could disrupt tissues due to its complexity and subsequently contribute to post-operative urinary and fecal incontinence. With the staged approach over several surgeries, facilitated by the CELS technique when feasible, the planning/diagnostic evaluation and reconstruction can be safely separated into steps based on the patient’s anatomy and what it will allow. Liem et al. reported employing a similar staged approach in two patients with persistent cloaca who underwent laparoscopic-assisted anorectal pull-through, followed by vaginoplasty/urethroplasty 1–2 years later [6].

Long term studies evaluating adult cloaca patients are rare, but one review has documented chronic renal failure in 50 % of patients and urinary incontinence in up to 72 % of patients [5]. Furthermore, the traditional one-stage open approach (posterior sagittal anorectal vaginal urethralplasty) may not adequately address all the different anatomical challenges that could accompany cloaca, such as an absent urethral opening or a severely abnormal bladder neck that is not amenable to intermittent catheterization. Patients with this type of anatomy may need a vesicostomy and then creation of a catheterizable channel at a later date.

When the vaginal tissue cannot be mobilized to the perineum or when the vagina is absent, bowel vaginoplasties have been performed in the newborn period during single stage reconstructions of cloaca. Long term studies evaluating the outcomes are based on limited long-term data, but results have been sub-optimal [7–9]. Complications include mucosal prolapse, fistula, obstruction, and stenosis requiring repeat procedures (dilation, introitoplasty). There are also numerous case reports of inflammatory diseases and neoplasms developing in bowel neovaginas [10–13]. A recent thorough review by Versteegh et al. stresses a personalized approach to each patient with a cloacal malformation, stating that “exploration of approaches that involve less dissection and less unlike-for-like tissue import are desirable”(3). They advocate for a shift in emphasis to best functional outcomes achievable for the patient’s anatomy, rather than restoration of normal anatomy. The debate on timing of vaginal reconstruction has not been settled and the authors note that while it is desirable to allow an older patient the chance to individualize her choices regarding tissue source for vaginoplasty, the scarring that is undoubtedly in place due to the newborn surgery presents a challenge. Vaginal stenosis has been reported to occur in 25 % of patients following the traditional single-stage posterior sagittal anorectal vaginal urethralplasty [13]. Early vaginal reconstruction in infancy is also associated with later stenosis that may impede menstruation in 36–41 % [14,15]. Within our case series, the initial urogenital reconstruction was performed at varying ages. The minimally invasive approach decreases the tissue disruption, and in our patients who underwent procedures involving the introitus after the laparoscopic anorectoplasty, there was no disruption of the pulled through rectum and neo-anus. One patient did require multiple dilations under anesthesia to maintain patency of the pulled-through vagina.

The staged approach with the CELS technique offers a chance at the personalized approach as suggested. Dougherty et al. recently described a case where this approach was used in a 5-month-old patient with cloaca who has undergone anorectal reconstruction, with plans for urogenital reconstruction in adolescence [16]. The minimally invasive nature of CELS spares tissue from extensive dissection which could in turn improve bowel and bladder function outcomes and result in less scarring for future reoperation. Theoretically, the risk of future bowel obstructions from adhesions is minimized by laparoscopic surgery, but this requires long-term follow up in this patient subset to confirm a benefit. As was done for the patients in this case series, we propose using concurrent endoscopy or instrumentation of the urogenital sinus or created openings (vesicostomy or vaginostomy) while performing the laparoscopic reconstruction to aid in the dissection. Fig. 1shows laparoscopic images of one of our patients during the first stage anorectoplasty. The cystoscope can be helpful in two ways. First, the light can be visible laparoscopically within the abdomen in whichever structure it is placed into, helping to illuminate the fistulous connections. The intestinal fistula can be intubated with the cystoscope if the lumen is large enough. Second, it can be used to distend the bladder or (hemi)vagina, which can be helpful in defining the extent and size of these structures and/or their connections to one another. Insertion of catheters and/or dilators through the perineal openings during the procedure also aids in better delineating tissue planes and anatomy. Clearly identifying each structure’s location and connection to other structures within the complex anatomy enables safer dissection and separation of the structures laparoscopically. In addition to having no major complications directly related to the endoscopic or laparoscopic procedures, the three patients in this series had a short postoperative length of stay at an average of 2.4 days after their laparoscopic-assisted anorectoplasty.

The CELS technique has enabled customization of the planning and timing of each patient’s procedure. Using multiple scopes and/or retracting devices, the internal anatomy/structures of each patient can be catalogued for operative planning and surveillance. In being able to objectively describe to families what we know and do not know, they are better informed in making decisions regarding operative intervention versus monitoring. The expertise of several multidisciplinary team members as well as the goals and desires of the family can be incorporated into discussions. Each patient’s anatomy associated anomalies such as cardiac lesions, social challenges, and family circumstances are different and can have a significant impact on the best timing of each surgical procedure. Dividing the reconstructions into separate topics and procedures for the family helps in their understanding of the function of the intestinal, urinary, reproductive, and neurological systems. Overall, the principles guiding this decision-making process are: 1) medical necessity to relieve obstruction, 2) patient and family readiness, and 3) permanency of the procedure proposed.

The two-team approach allows real-time intraoperative collaboration across surgical divisions, including pediatric surgery, urology, and PAG. The multidisciplinary team is involved in the preoperative planning, operative procedures, and post-operative care of these patients. Constant communication between the multidisciplinary team and the family allows the family to play an active part in the decision-making process, laying the groundwork for the patient to participate in these discussions as they become older.

We advocate waiting until adolescence to address a vaginal replacement if required, as was what occurred for case one. By involving the patient in the decision-making process, they are allowed time to develop the mental maturity necessary to handle catheterization and/or vaginal dilation [17,18]. Additionally, multiple options exist for vaginal reconstruction, each with unique benefits and drawbacks, which can be discussed with the patient when they are able to participate in the decision-making process [19]. It is the practice at our institution to use bowel to reconstruct the vagina only if the patient is at an age where they are able to accept one and understand its challenges and the need for long-term surveillance.

Acknowledging that this approach is new, we are following these patients closely in the multidisciplinary differences of sex development clinic as well as the cloaca clinic. These patients will also participate in the bowel management program, thus providing multiple opportunities to evaluate them over the coming years. In addition to clinical exams, ultrasounds are needed to surveille the urinary system and gynecologic system as the patients approach menarche and, in all patients, particular attention will be paid to signs of outlet obstruction. At this time, the timing for surveillance imaging is individualized for each patient. There are some drawbacks to the staged reconstructive approach. One significant concern with delayed urogenital reconstruction regards patients that present with hydrocolpos and hydronephrosis secondary to extrinsic ureteral compression. These patients must have successful urinary diversion (e. g., vesicostomy, vaginostomy) if a delay in urogenital reconstruction is considered. Surveillance of the function of the bladder and kidneys should be performed often to help drive decision-making.

There are several limitations to this approach. Although the CELS technique may be useful in multiple types of pelvic procedures, we have applied it here in only three patients with persistent cloacal malformation. Additionally, the patients described are young and it is early in their management plans. It is possible with more follow-up that different or new complications may arise. Long-term follow-up data is being gathered in our sample and is warranted in this population. This staged approach is best suited in patients who are cared for in a tertiary institution and in their own local community with multidisciplinary support to ensure and optimize long term follow-up.

Conclusion

The CELS technique may help reduce complications from open surgery, allows time for structural development, and minimizes tissue dissection and trauma at an early age. Staged repair for complex cloaca can be safely considered and allow for delay of vaginal reconstruction with minimal adverse short-term outcomes and impact on subsequent procedures.
