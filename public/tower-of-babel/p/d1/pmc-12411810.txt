Perfect image, depressed mood: Pathways from perfectionistic self‐presentation to depressive symptoms through online and in‐person connectedness among Canadian adolescents
Authors: Melissa Blackburn, Danielle S Molnar, Dawn Zinga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Perfectionism Social Disconnection Model (PSDM) contends that perfectionism predicts psychopathology via social disconnection and has received support in adult and adolescent samples. However, little is known about the role of online connection. Consequently, this work tested whether online social connection and in‐person social support uniquely mediate the relation between perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms among adolescents. A community sample of 354 adolescents (M age = 15.51, SD = 2.07; 68.93% female; 73.44% White) completed surveys assessing these constructs at three time points, each spaced approximately 2 months apart. Results supported the PSDM at the between‐persons level such that perfectionistic self‐presentation was related to depressive symptoms via online social connection and in‐person social support. Importantly, these findings both replicate previous work which has predominantly focused on in‐person connectedness and extend this body of work by demonstrating that online social connection is a significant mediator over and above the role of in‐person social support. However, there was no longitudinal evidence for the PSDM at the within‐persons level. Future directions and implications are discussed.Keywords: depressive symptoms, perfectionistic self‐presentation, social connection

INTRODUCTION

Perfectionism (e.g., requiring perfection of the self and/or others; Hewitt & Flett, 1991) is a widespread and growing public health issue among adolescents (Flett & Hewitt, 2022). Illustrating this trend, Curran and Hill (2019) demonstrated that recent generations report elevated levels of perfectionism relative to older cohorts, which is particularly alarming given that perfectionism has been linked with a range of detrimental outcomes among adolescents, including anxiety, depression, eating disorders, and suicide (e.g., Bills et al., 2023; Lunn et al., 2023; Roxborough et al., 2012). Consequently, perfectionism researchers have called for empirical tests of theoretical models that underscore explanatory mechanisms between perfectionism and maladjustment to inform prevention and supports in substantive ways (Smith et al., 2022). One such model is the Perfectionism Social Disconnection Model (PSDM; Hewitt et al., 2017), which posits that perfectionism confers risk for maladjustment through profound experiences of social disconnection. Although the PSDM has garnered support in adult (e.g., Smith et al., 2020) and adolescent samples (e.g., Blackburn, Zinga, et al., 2024; Goya Arce & Polo, 2017; Magson et al., 2019; Roxborough et al., 2012), a key gap in the extant literature lies within our understanding of online contexts that are critical to examine among adolescents, given the central role of social media during adolescence (e.g., Boak & Hamilton, 2024).

Indeed, research indicates that social media use is nearly ubiquitous among adolescents, with prevalence rates steadily increasing since 2012, following the rise of the smartphone (e.g., Twenge et al., 2018). For instance, Statistics Canada (2023) reported that 97% of Canadians between the ages of 15 and 24 years own a smartphone, and 91% are active social media users. Moreover, over 70% of Canadian youth report checking their phones at least once every half hour (Statistics Canada, 2023). Similarly, in a large‐scale study of Ontario students, Boak and Hamilton (2024) found that 94% of adolescents indicated they use social media daily, with over 20% spending at least 5 hours per day online. Altogether, digital cultures are an integral component of adolescents' experiences and are inherently interwoven with many aspects of the psychosocial functioning of young people (e.g., Hendry, 2024).

Further, research suggests social media use may be implicated in adolescent perfectionism, given high levels of comparison and competition in the pursuit of recognition on social media and the accompanying pressures to curate a “perfect” persona on these platforms (e.g., Blackburn, Zinga, et al., 2024; Ceilutka, 2023; Etherson, Curran, et al., 2022). Yet, little is known about the role of online social connection in the relation between perfectionism and well‐being. Specifically, it has yet to be examined whether online social disconnection represents an explanatory pathway from perfectionism to poorer well‐being, over and above the degree of connectedness experienced in person. As such, the aim of the current work was to provide a rigorous, longitudinal test of the PSDM that included both in‐person and online indicators of social connection as mediators from perfectionism to psychopathology among adolescents to elucidate our understanding of the unique roles of these two related, yet distinct, social contexts.

Perfectionistic self‐presentation

At the broadest level, perfectionism is defined as a multifaceted personality style that manifests at three key levels: trait, behavior, and cognition (Hewitt et al., 2017). The current work focused on the behavioral level (i.e., perfectionistic self‐presentation) as the interpersonal facets of perfectionism are thought to be central when examining the social costs of this personality style (Hewitt et al., 2017; Sherry et al., 2016). Furthermore, an examination of the behavioral level of perfectionism was warranted considering work suggesting that perfectionistic self‐presentational concerns may be amplified in online contexts (e.g., Blackburn, Zinga, et al., 2024; Casale et al., 2015; Flett & Hewitt, 2022; Milson & Madigan, 2024).

Perfectionistic self‐presentation reflects an individual's need and commitment to presenting a flawless public image, expressed through promotional and withholding impression management strategies (Hewitt et al., 2003; 2017). With respect to the former, perfectionistic self‐promotion is a promotional strategy whereby individuals engage in active efforts to portray the self as perfect to garner and maintain a flawless reputation. With respect to the latter, perfectionistic self‐presentation involves two withholding strategies: non‐display of imperfections and nondisclosure of imperfections. While both strategies focus on concealing shortcomings, non‐display of imperfections involves avoiding overt demonstrations of flaws, whereas nondisclosure of imperfections is related to the avoidance of verbal disclosures of less‐than‐perfect achievements, behaviors, and qualities (Hewitt et al., 2003; 2017).

A focus on perfectionistic self‐presentation during adolescence is particularly important given that this developmental period is characterized by a desire for social acceptance among peers as well as the avoidance of public failures or embarrassments (Berndt, 1979; Hewitt et al., 2011). Moreover, adolescents tend to believe that other people their age are faring better than they are, which may lead them to be less willing to disclose their own perceived failures or shortcomings. For adolescents, perfectionistic self‐presentation is likely driven by a need to fit in with their friends and peers, guided by perceived normative pressures and linked to the navigation of one's own sense of identity (Hewitt et al., 2011). Indeed, although most perfectionism research focuses on the trait level, perfectionistic self‐presentation has been linked with maladaptive correlates among adolescents, including depression, negative attitudes toward the self, and maladaptive coping styles (e.g., Hewitt et al., 2011; Stoeber & Rountree, 2021).

More recently, there has been growing interest in the role of perfectionistic self‐presentation in young people's social media use, with a handful of studies linking perfectionistic self‐presentation with problematic social media use (e.g., Casale et al., 2015; Fioravanti et al., 2024; Milson & Madigan, 2024). However, it is important to note that this body of work has focused on young adults and has not yet focused directly on how perfectionistic self‐presentation may be linked with one's sense of social connection online. Further work with adolescents is required to elucidate the relations between perfectionistic self‐presentation and adolescents' online social connection, particularly given the central role of social media in adolescents' social worlds, as highlighted above (e.g., Boak & Hamilton, 2024; Hendry, 2024; Twenge et al., 2018).

Perfectionism Social Disconnection Model

The gap in our understanding of the link between perfectionistic self‐presentation and online social connection among adolescents is critical given the relational roots of perfectionism as well as theory and empirical evidence highlighting the social costs of perfectionistic behavior (e.g., Flett & Hewitt, 2022; Hewitt et al., 2017; Sherry et al., 2016). Indeed, the PSDM details the self‐defeating nature of perfectionistic behavior such that it is often developed as a tactic to facilitate meaningful relationships, yet often incurs subjective feelings of disconnection and fractured relationships due to behaviors and attitudes that are seen as distant and inauthentic (Hewitt et al., 2017). In turn, these feelings and experiences of disconnection generate profound distress among perfectionistic individuals. In sum, the PSDM posits that social disconnection is a key mediator between perfectionistic behavior and maladjustment.

To date, the PSDM has received ample support among adult samples. For instance, meta‐analytic work has revealed that social disconnection significantly explains the prospective link between perfectionism and depression (Smith et al., 2020). However, it is worth noting that much of this research has focused on the trait level of perfectionism or has not solely focused on perfectionistic self‐presentation. This is a key gap in the extant literature given that perfectionistic self‐presentation has been shown to predict unique variance in both social disconnection and mental health outcomes (Hewitt et al., 2003).

Moreover, a growing body of work with adolescent samples provides empirical evidence for the tenability of the PSDM. For instance, Roxborough et al. (2012) found that bully victimization and social hopelessness mediated the relations between perfectionistic self‐presentation and suicide risk among adolescents enrolled in a psychiatric outpatient program. Further, Goya Arce and Polo (2017) provided evidence for sequential mediation from perfectionistic self‐presentation to depressive symptoms via social anxiety and loneliness among ethnic minority adolescents. Finally, Magson et al. (2019) demonstrated that the relations between both self‐oriented trait perfectionism (e.g., unreasonably high standards for oneself accompanied by punitive self‐criticism; Hewitt & Flett, 1991) and socially prescribed trait perfectionism (e.g., the belief that others set unattainable standards and will be unforgiving when these standards are unmet) and psychopathology were sequentially mediated by rejection sensitivity and social isolation among early adolescents. Although these studies provide important insight into the tenability of the PSDM in adolescent samples, particularly in relation to the role of perfectionistic self‐presentation, the extant research is limited in terms of generalizability due to the specialized nature of these samples as well as by the cross‐sectional design of their respective analyses. Consequently, longitudinal research with community adolescent samples is required to strengthen our understanding of the PSDM among adolescents.

Finally, to our knowledge, previous work testing the PSDM has not yet examined online contexts. As such, a significant gap remains in our understanding of the unique contributions of online versus in‐person disconnection to psychopathology. This is particularly concerning given evidence suggesting that in‐person and online connectedness are distinct constructs that uniquely predict wellbeing outcomes (Grieve et al., 2013). To address this empirical gap, Hewitt et al. (2017) proposed two competing hypotheses. In line with the PSDM, these authors suggested that online social connection will mirror face‐to‐face connection, such that perfectionism will predict psychopathology via lower levels of online social connection. However, their alternative hypothesis suggests that perfectionism may predict increased levels of online social connection, as individuals higher in perfectionism might seek out online communities to supplement the lack of connection they experience in person (Hewitt et al., 2017). From this perspective, perfectionism may predict increased levels of online social connection and, consequently, lower levels of psychopathology, countering the effects of face‐to‐face disconnection.

Although the full model has yet to be tested, a recent qualitative study provided preliminary support for the PSDM in online contexts (Blackburn, Zinga, et al., 2024). Specifically, interviews with adolescent self‐identified perfectionists revealed that, compared to their non‐perfectionistic peers, young perfectionists tend to feel deeply disconnected in online settings. Although they used social media to connect with friends, they described intense and pervasive concerns surrounding the quality of these interactions due to the dearth of social cues available online (Blackburn, Zinga, et al., 2024). In other words, although teenage self‐identified perfectionists use online platforms to connect with others, they continue to feel disconnected online due to their inability to confirm they have executed these interactions perfectly, leaving them unsure about the status of their friendships. However, whether this sense of online disconnection plays a unique predictive role in explaining psychopathology among adolescents remains unknown. Consequently, the current work sought to answer the following research questions: (1) Do in‐person and online social connectedness uniquely mediate the relation between perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms among community adolescents? (2) Does this pattern of relations operate at both the between‐persons and within‐persons levels?

CURRENT STUDY

The aim of this study was to provide a longitudinal test of the PSDM among adolescents, while assessing both in‐person and online social connection. Specifically, the present work examined whether in‐person social support and online social connection were unique, simultaneous mediators between perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms among a community sample of adolescents. Consequently, this study addressed several gaps in the literature. First, this was the first test of the PSDM to distinguish between social connection in face‐to‐face versus online contexts in either adolescent or adult samples. Second, all studies assessing the PSDM in adolescent samples to date have been cross‐sectional and, as such, cannot speak to the longitudinal relations among perfectionism, social connection, and psychopathology. The current work addressed this gap by using a random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel model design that allowed for the separation of stable, between‐persons differences from temporal within‐person changes (Hamaker et al., 2015). This offered a robust approach to assessing the prospective relations hypothesized by the PSDM given that traditional cross‐lagged designs have been critiqued for invoking longitudinal interpretations, despite combining within‐ and between‐persons sources of variance (Hamaker et al., 2015). As such, the present study both fills a necessary gap by assessing prospective relations proposed by the PSDM among adolescents, while employing an appropriate analytic design equipped to assess longitudinal change. Finally, the current work draws from a community sample of adolescents, whereas the extant literature examining the PSDM focused on specialized samples.

The present study focused on depressive symptoms, considering global estimates indicating that 34% of adolescents report elevated levels of depressive symptoms, underscoring its pervasive nature during adolescence (Shorey et al., 2022). Further, depressive symptoms are a key outcome to examine within the PSDM, given theory highlighting the interpersonal nature of depression in adolescence (e.g., Rudolph, 2009) alongside literature demonstrating consistent links between perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms (e.g., Goya Arce & Polo, 2017; Hewitt et al., 2011) as well as between social disconnection and depressive symptoms (e.g., Rueger et al., 2016) among adolescents. Age and gender were also assessed as covariates, given that older adolescents and adolescent girls tend to experience higher levels of depression (e.g., Salk et al., 2017) and social disconnection (e.g., Boak & Hamilton, 2024) compared to younger adolescents and boys, respectively. Finally, it is worth noting that the current study took place during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Considering trends suggesting that adolescents relied heavily on online interactions to supplement restricted in‐person interactions during this time (e.g., Cauberghe et al., 2021), it is critical to examine adolescents' sense of connection in both online and in‐person contexts during these unprecedented circumstances.

In line with the PSDM and based on the results of Blackburn, Zinga, et al. (2024), it was hypothesized that both in‐person and online social disconnection would represent explanatory pathways linking perfectionistic self‐presentation to depressive symptoms. Specifically, the hypotheses for the present study were: (1a) at the between‐persons level, perfectionistic self‐presentation would be related to lower levels of in‐person social support which, in turn, would help to explain higher levels of depressive symptoms; (1b) at the between‐persons level, perfectionistic self‐presentation would be related to lower levels of online social connection which, in turn, would help to explain higher levels of depressive symptoms; (2a) at the within‐persons level, perfectionistic self‐presentation would predict decreases in in‐person social support which, in turn, would predict increases in depressive symptoms across time; (2b) at the within‐persons level, perfectionistic self‐presentation would predict decreases in online social connection which, in turn, would predict increases in depressive symptoms across time; (3) all hypothesized relations would hold after accounting for the effects of gender and age.

METHODS

Participants

Three‐hundred and fifty‐six participants were recruited for the project from which the current study is drawn (see Procedure). Two participants were removed from the present analyses as they did not have valid data for any model variables. As such, the analytic sample included 354 community adolescents between the ages of 12 and 18 years old (M = 15.51, SD = 2.07). The sample was predominantly female (n = 244; 68.93%). The remainder of the sample identified as follows: male (n = 93; 26.27%), non‐binary (n = 10; 2.82%), female trans (n = 1; 0.28%), and other (n = 3; 0.85%). Three participants (0.85%) did not identify their gender. The sample was predominantly White (n = 260; 73.44%), with the remaining participants identifying as Asian (n = 44; 12.43%), Black (n = 9; 2.54%), Indigenous (n = 3; 0.85%), Latin (n = 3; 0.85%), or other (n = 28; 7.91%). Seven participants (1.98%) did not report their race. Finally, when asked how their family compares to the average Canadian, over half of the participants (n = 204; 57.63%) indicated that they thought their families were about as rich as the average family. In contrast, 52 participants (14.69%) rated their families as less rich or a lot less rich than the average Canadian family, whereas 93 participants (26.27%) rated their families as more rich or a lot more rich than the average Canadian family. Five (1.41%) participants did not respond.

Participants also provided information regarding their social media use. Specifically, participants reported using an average of 3.68 social media platforms (SD = 1.48; range: 0–6). Across these platforms, participants reported between 0 and 190,447 online followers and friends, with a median of 575. Participants reported spending between 1 and 18 h per day on social media (M = 4.28, SD = 2.66) and using social media for an average of 6.77 days per week (SD = 0.84; range: 1–7). Popular platforms among the participants included Tik Tok, Snapchat, and Instagram. Notably, 16 participants (4.52%) reported that they were not allowed to use social media and, as such, were not presented any survey items or measures related to social media use.

Procedure

Data for this study came from a larger project examining adolescent personality, well‐being, and school experiences during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The design of this project was preregistered before data collection began (see https://osf.io/fp34x/). Data collection began in June 2021 and ended in April 2022. Participants were recruited via a variety of methods, including social media posts, stories in the online university newspaper, physical posters in the community, and passive snowball sampling. Before participating in the study, informed consent was obtained for participants aged 18 years, and adolescent assent and parental consent were obtained for participants under 18 years. All consent procedures were completed via email. The study included three time points, spaced roughly 2 months apart, given the rapidly changing social climate of the COVID‐19 pandemic. More specifically, the mean lag between the first and second time points was 73.75 days (SD = 17.46) and the mean lag between the second and third time points was 67.71 days (SD = 13.68). At each time point, all participants had the opportunity to complete an online survey, and all measures described below were presented to participants at every time point. Additionally, at each time point, a subset of participants was invited to complete online interviews. For each survey, participants received a virtual $20 CAD Amazon gift card. For each interview, participants received a virtual $25 CAD Amazon gift card. The current work focused on the longitudinal survey responses. Participant confidentiality was maintained throughout the study using unique ID codes. At the end of each survey, participants were provided with a study debriefing form with information about the study, contact information, and mental health resources. All procedures were approved by the university's ethics board.

Measures

Demographics

Participants reported their age, gender, race, and socioeconomic status. Participants also reported on their social media use, which included questions about platforms used, number of friends and followers, as well as time spent on social media platforms. All demographic questions were created by the authors (see https://osf.io/nes86).

Perfectionistic self‐presentation

The Perfectionistic Self‐Presentation Scale—Junior Form (PSPS‐JR; Hewitt et al., 2011) was used to measure perfectionistic self‐presentation. This scale involves 18 items that assess tendencies toward perfectionistic self‐promotion (e.g., “It's important to act perfectly around other people”), non‐display of imperfections (e.g., “I do not want my friends to see even one of my bad points”), and nondisclosure of imperfections (e.g., “I should fix my own problems rather than telling them to other people”). Items were rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Disagree strongly) to 5 (Strongly agree), such that higher scores represented higher levels of perfectionistic self‐presentation. The PSPS‐Jr has demonstrated adequate reliability and validity among adolescent samples (Hewitt et al., 2011).

Online social connection

The Facebook Connectedness scale (adapted for social media use broadly; FCS; Grieve et al., 2013) was used to assess online social connection. This measure involves 10 positively worded items (e.g., “I am able to relate to my peers on social media”) and 10 negatively worded items (e.g., “I don't feel I participate with anyone or any group on social media”). Items were rated on a 6‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 6 (Strongly agree). Negatively worded items were reverse scored before creating the total score, such that higher scores represented a greater sense of connection online. The FCS has demonstrated good reliability and validity in previous work (Grieve et al., 2013).

In‐person social support

In‐person social support was assessed using the Social Support Questionnaire (adapted for in‐person contexts; SSQ; Sarason et al., 1983). This measure involved 6 items that inquire about the size of one's in‐person social support network (e.g., “Please select the number of people that you connect with in‐person that you can count on to console you when you are really upset”) and 6 items that assess level of satisfaction with one's social support network in in‐person settings (e.g., “How satisfied are you with this level of support?”). The current work focused on the satisfaction subscale. Each of these items was rated on a 6‐point Likert scale from 1 (Very dissatisfied) to 6 (Very satisfied). The SSQ has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in previous research (Sarason et al., 1983).

Depressive symptoms

The Center for Epidemiological Studies—Depression Scale Revised (CESD‐R; Radloff, 1977) was used to assess depressive symptoms. The CESD‐R includes 20 items, representing symptoms of depression (e.g., “I felt that everything I had to do was an effort”). Participants indicated how often they experienced each symptom over the past week using the following options: rarely or none of the time (less than 1 day), some or a little of the time (1–2 days), occasionally or a moderate amount of the time (3–4 days), and most or all of the time (5–7 days). Before calculating total scores, negatively worded items were reverse coded so that higher scores represented greater frequency of depressive symptoms. The CESD‐R has demonstrated good reliability and validity among adolescents (e.g., Essau et al., 2013).

Data analysis

The data and analytic code for the current work are publicly available; see https://osf.io/fp34x/files/osfstorage#/. Preliminary analyses assessed missing data, attrition, outliers, and normality. Of the participants included in the analytic sample, 57.63% (n = 204) had fully complete data. Notably, much of the missing data was attributed to attrition as well as 16 participants who were not shown items related to social media use as they were not permitted to use social media. Little's (1988) Missing Completely at Random test indicated the data was not missing completely at random, χ 2(340) = 398.82, p = .015. Follow‐up analyses indicated that participants who were missing data for at least one variable were significantly younger, more likely to be male, and had significantly higher levels of depressive symptoms at the second time point compared to those with fully complete data (see Table S1).

With respect to attrition, 79.94% of participants (n = 283) participated in a survey at all three time points. Compared to participants who were retained for the entire duration of the study, those who dropped out were significantly younger and had significantly higher levels of depressive symptoms at the first two time points (see Table S2). Univariate outliers were identified based on z‐scores of ±3.29 and were Winsorized, such that rank order was maintained to one decimal place (Field, 2018). Finally, all skewness and kurtosis values fell within ±2, indicating normal distributions (Field, 2018). Consequently, maximum likelihood (ML) was used to estimate the hypothesized model. Statistical significance for all models was evaluated at the α = .05 level.

Before testing the hypothesized model, two versions of the measurement model were compared: Model 1 included perfectionistic self‐presentation as a total score whereas Model 2 included a three‐factor model (e.g., subscales for each facet). Although the three‐factor model has typically been employed, these models were compared given concerns surrounding collinearity between the subscales. Results indicated that Model 1 (i.e., total score; χ 2(36) = 212.33, p < .001, RMSEA = .118 [.103, .133], p < .001, CFI = .913, SRMR = .216) fit the data significantly better than Model 2 (i.e., subscale scores; χ 2(90) = 530.20, p < .001, RMSEA = .118 [.108, .127], CFI = .874, SRMR = .252), χ 2Δ(54) = 307.87, p < .001. Additionally, the reliability coefficient for the total score at each time point (.93) improved compared to those for the non‐display of imperfections (.80 to .81) and nondisclosure of imperfections (.70 to .77) subscales. Finally, although less common, a one‐factor model of perfectionistic self‐presentation has been used in previous work (e.g., Casale et al., 2020; Mackinnon & Sherry, 2012). As such, a total score for perfectionistic self‐presentation was employed in the present analyses.

Additionally, before testing the hypothesized model, preliminary analyses were conducted to test for the possibility of reciprocal longitudinal relations between perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms at the within‐person level given evidence suggesting psychopathology may predict changes to perfectionism across time among adolescents (e.g., Asseraf & Vaillancourt, 2015; Spadafora et al., 2022). However, there was no evidence for reciprocal relations in the current sample and, as such, the hypothesized model as posited by the PSDM was tested (see Table S3 for full results).

The main hypotheses of the present study were tested via a random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel model with indirect effects using Mplus version 8.4 (Muthen & Muthen, 1998–2019). In this model, each construct was separated into between‐persons, trait‐like differences and within‐person, temporal changes (see Figure S1; Hamaker et al., 2015). At the within‐persons level, temporal changes in each construct were set to predict themselves across time (i.e., autoregressive paths). In line with the PSDM, cross‐lagged paths from perfectionistic self‐presentation at Time 1 to online social connection at Time 2, in‐person social support at Time 2, and depressive symptoms at Time 3, as well as from in‐person social support and online social connection at Time 2 to depressive symptoms at Time 3 were tested. Similarly, at the between‐persons level, trait‐like differences in depressive symptoms were regressed on trait‐like differences in perfectionistic self‐presentation, online social connection, and in‐person social support. Additionally, trait‐like differences in online social connection and in‐person social support were regressed on trait‐like differences in perfectionistic self‐presentation. Indirect effects were tested at both the within‐ and between‐persons levels. The following indices were used to determine acceptable model fit: a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) <.10 (Browne & Cudeck, 1992), a comparative fit index (CFI) ≥.95 (Hu & Bentler, 1998), and a standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) <.08 (Hu & Bentler, 1999). Chi‐square values with degrees of freedom and significance values were also reported.

RESULTS

Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations

Descriptive statistics, reliability coefficients, and bivariate correlations are presented in Table 1. Age was positively related to gender, such that female participants tended to be older. Age was also positively related to perfectionistic self‐presentation across all time points and depressive symptoms at T1 and T2, such that older participants reported higher levels of perfectionistic self‐presentation at each time point and higher levels of depressive symptoms at the first two time points. Finally, age was negatively related to online social connection and in‐person social support at T2, such that older participants reported lower levels of online social connection and less satisfaction with in‐person social support at the second time point. Gender was positively related to perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms across all time points, such that female participants reported higher levels of both perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms across time. Moreover, gender was negatively related to online social connection at the first two time points, such that female participants reported lower levels of online connection at T1 and T2.

All primary model variables were positively related to themselves across time. Perfectionistic self‐presentation was positively related to depressive symptoms and negatively related to online social connection and in‐person social support across the current study, such that higher levels of perfectionistic self‐presentation were correlated with higher levels of depressive symptoms as well as lower levels of online social connection and in‐person social support within and across all time points. Hypothesis 1a and 1b

The hypothesized model, testing for indirect effects at both the within‐ and between‐persons levels, demonstrated good fit (χ 2(22) = 19.872, p = .591; RMSEA = .000 [.000, .039], p = .990; CFI = 1.000; SRMR = .035). At the between‐persons level, all hypothesized paths were statistically significant (see Table 2; Figure 1 for results with standardized coefficients; see Table S4 for unstandardized coefficients). Specifically, higher average levels of perfectionistic self‐presentation were associated with higher average depressive symptoms as well as lower average levels of online social connection and in‐person social support. Further, higher average levels of online social connection and in‐person social support, respectively, were associated with lower average levels of depressive symptoms. In line with the PSDM, mediation analyses at the between‐persons level revealed a significant direct effect of perfectionistic self‐presentation on depressive symptoms as well as two simultaneous indirect effects via online social connection and in‐person social support (see Table 2; Figure 1). Online social connection uniquely and partially mediated the association between perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms, such that higher average levels of perfectionistic self‐presentation were associated with lower average levels of online social connection which, in turn, helped to explain higher average depressive symptoms. Similarly, in‐person social support uniquely and partially mediated the link between perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms at the between‐persons level, such that higher average levels of perfectionistic self‐presentation were associated with lower average levels of in‐person social support which, in turn, helped to explain higher average depressive symptoms.

DISCUSSION

The present study offers key insights into the unique roles of online and in‐person social connectedness within the PSDM among adolescents. Most notably, at the between‐persons level, results supported the PSDM for both online social connection and in‐person social support. Specifically, and consistent with previous cross‐sectional work (Goya Arce & Polo, 2017; Magson et al., 2019; Roxborough et al., 2012) as well as research with adult samples (Smith et al., 2020), adolescents reporting higher average levels of perfectionistic self‐presentation tended to experience less satisfaction with their levels of social support in face‐to‐face contexts, which then predicted higher average depressive symptoms. Notably, these findings extend previous cross‐sectional work with adolescents, given that most work to date has been done with relatively specialized samples (e.g., minority youth, early adolescents, psychiatric sample; Goya Arce & Polo, 2017; Magson et al., 2019; Roxborough et al., 2012). Although these studies offer an important foundation for this body of work, the community‐based nature of the present sample supports the generalizability of the PSDM among adolescents.

Importantly, the most notable finding from the current work was that online social connection uniquely and partially mediated the relation between perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms, such that higher average levels of perfectionistic self‐presentation predicted lower average levels of online social connection, in turn, predicting higher average depressive symptoms over and above the effects of in‐person social support. To our knowledge, this is the first study to empirically test Hewitt et al.'s (2017) competing hypotheses regarding the role of online connection within the PSDM using quantitative methods. Specifically, the current findings support Hewitt et al.'s (2017) main hypothesis that individuals higher in perfectionism will tend to feel more disconnected in online spaces, mirroring what is seen in face‐to‐face contexts, rather than their alternate hypothesis which suggests online spaces may offer individuals with perfectionistic tendencies a space in which they can garner a sense of social connection that is missing or lacking in their day‐to‐day lives. This work extends the findings of Blackburn, Zinga, et al. (2024), which indicated that most young self‐identified perfectionists tend to feel more disconnected on social media compared to their non‐perfectionistic peers, by demonstrating that this unique form of social disconnection represents an explanatory pathway between perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms. Interestingly, Blackburn, Zinga, et al. (2024) suggested that, among adolescent self‐identified perfectionists, online disconnection may be phenomenologically different from disconnection in face‐to‐face settings due to the limited ability to self‐monitor via social cues, leading to excessive concerns and feelings of uncertainty surrounding the quality of their connections with peers. This may help to explain the unique predictive utility of online social connection over and above in‐person social support within the PSDM demonstrated in the current work.

Contrary to Hypotheses 2a and 2b, the present findings did not support the PSDM for either in‐person or online social connection at the temporal, within‐person level. Indeed, beyond null indirect effects, none of the cross‐lagged paths were significant. One possibility may be that perfectionism is so deeply rooted within the self that levels of perfectionism and related patterns of emotion and behavior are engrained from a young age, rather than continuing to shift over time (Hewitt et al., 2017). In other words, it may be possible that relations between perfectionism and maladjustment occur predominantly at the between‐persons level. Another possibility may be that the time points in the present study were simply too close together to capture within‐person change, as surveys were completed only 2 months apart. When planning the current project, this choice was intentional due to the unpredictable nature of the COVID‐19 pandemic and accompanying restrictions. However, rather than representing a period marked by chaos and change, research emerging from the pandemic suggests that it may have had the opposite effect for adolescents such that they experienced a suspended reality with little change to their psychosocial experiences (e.g., Blackburn, Zinga, et al., 2024; Gittings et al., 2021). Notably, this sense of stagnancy among adolescents may have compounded the already brief lag between waves and may be reflected in the present data, given the stability of the constructs. For instance, the means and standard deviations for each construct reveal very little change across time (see Table 1). As we continue to transition to a post‐pandemic world, it is critical to undertake longitudinal tests of the PSDM to examine whether its hypothesized relations are truly driven by the between‐persons level, or whether the present results are unique to the COVID‐19 pandemic and/or the design of the current study. Future work should consider employing longer lags and greater than three time points to capture more subtle changes (e.g., McGrath et al., 2012).

Limitations and future directions

Despite the novel contributions of the current work, there are limitations worth noting. First, although this study was the first to test the PSDM among a community sample spanning from early to late adolescence, the final sample was relatively homogeneous with respect to gender and race. Specifically, participants included in this study were predominantly female and White. Although this trend is common in both perfectionism research (Blackburn, Molnar, et al., 2024) and psychological research more broadly (Neilsen et al., 2017), it is a notable limitation as the results may not generalize to adolescents with varying racial backgrounds and gender identities. Future research should employ purposeful sampling strategies to recruit more heterogeneous samples. Moreover, despite the range of ages included in the current sample, no age‐related differences emerged as the hypothesized model did not vary with the inclusion of age. Future work should continue to examine age‐related differences in perfectionistic self‐presentation, social connection, and depressive symptoms.

Another limitation of the present study is that it relied solely on self‐report measures. In the future, the use of varied data collection techniques (e.g., informant reports, observational research) is encouraged to test the PSDM among adolescents to strengthen the reliability of the findings and allow for researchers to tease apart the unique contributions of subjective and objective indicators of disconnection to psychopathology within the framework of the PSDM (Hewitt et al., 2017). Future work should consider the use of parents, friends, peers, and teachers as informants, as well as varied data collection strategies, such as observational techniques in social situations, to test the theoretical assertions implicated in the PSDM framework.

Finally, to expand our understanding of the PSDM among adolescents, future work should consider the use of at least four time points to capture sequential mediators within this model. For instance, the PSDM highlights that interpersonal hostility (e.g., anger or suspicion directed toward others) and interpersonal sensitivity (e.g., social anxiety, fear of evaluation) may precede objective and subjective disconnection, respectively, in the relation between perfectionism and psychological distress (Hewitt et al., 2017). To our knowledge, this assertion has yet to be tested among an adolescent sample. Moreover, future studies may consider examining the role of trait perfectionism in predicting depressive symptoms through sequential mediation via perfectionistic self‐presentation and social disconnection, considering evidence of such relations in adult samples. For example, Rnic et al. (2021) found that socially prescribed perfectionism predicted dimensions of perfectionistic self‐presentation which, in turn, predicted greater social disconnection followed by greater depressive symptoms. Taken together, testing sequential mediators within the PSDM is a key area for future research with adolescents.

Implications

The present study carries several implications. First, and consistent with clinical recommendations (e.g., Hewitt et al., 2015), the present findings suggest that perfectionistic adolescents may benefit from interventions that target perfectionistic self‐presentation as well as strategies for coping with feelings of disconnection from others in both face‐to‐face and online contexts. However, practitioners should consider that young people may need alternative coping strategies for addressing online disconnection given that the strategies they rely on in‐person (e.g., self‐monitoring) may not be as effective in the online world (Blackburn, Zinga, et al., 2024).

That said, one key challenge when designing supports for perfectionistic adolescents is that those with perfectionistic self‐presentational tendencies are often skilled at masking their struggles, making it difficult to identify a young person who requires support in addressing their perfectionistic tendencies and mental health needs (Flett & Hewitt, 2013). Moreover, perfectionistic youth for whom perfectionistic self‐presentation is critical may be unwilling to admit they need support, due to the belief that requiring help is a flaw in and of itself and would tarnish their public image (Flett & Hewitt, 2013, 2014). Collectively, the mask of perfection is a critical barrier in providing support for adolescents struggling with perfectionism and, in extreme cases, can have deadly consequences (Flett et al., 2014). Additionally, even when perfectionistic adolescents are willing to seek help, there may be other barriers to therapeutic intervention (i.e., cost, location, waitlists; Kourgiantakis et al., 2023). As such, we must consider alternative supports for perfectionistic adolescents beyond formal, individualized intervention.

One approach, suggested by Flett and Hewitt (2014), is universal programming through public school systems to promote resilience among all students via the provision of strategies for setting reasonable standards, lowering the importance of being perfect, fostering a growth mindset, managing stress, and developing self‐compassion. One strategy that may be particularly helpful in addressing relatively low levels of social connection among perfectionistic adolescents is mattering (e.g., feeling that you are noticed and important to others; Flett, 2022). Mattering is a powerful component of resilience and is modifiable, such that mattering can be fostered among adolescents through exercises that prompt reflection on the ways they matter to people in their lives (Flett, 2022). Given that mattering has demonstrated inverse links with perfectionism (Etherson, Smith, et al., 2022) and social disconnection (McComb et al., 2020), a focus on mattering within universal prevention frameworks has potential as both a promotive factor in developing resilience among all youth and a protective factor that may particularly benefit adolescents higher in perfectionism who are feeling disconnected from their peers (e.g., Masten, 2014).

Beyond school‐based programming, social media may represent another tool for disseminating information about the costs of perfectionism among adolescents as well as strategies for coping with perfectionism. For instance, the creation of easily digestible social media content to engage youth in learning more about and reflecting on the impacts of perfectionism is a low‐cost strategy that may help to reach young people who are not ready to engage in a structured program. Indeed, given research highlighting the utility of social media as a knowledge sharing tool (e.g., Del Castillo et al., 2020), resources that can be shared freely on social media may be a promising avenue for promoting resilience among perfectionistic adolescents who are unwilling or unable to seek support.

CONCLUSION

This study supported the PSDM among a community sample of adolescents by demonstrating that perfectionistic self‐presentation contributes to depressive symptoms at the between‐persons level via lower levels of social connection. Importantly, this study is the first to distinguish between social connection experienced face‐to‐face versus online. Specifically, results indicated that online social connection significantly mediated the relation between perfectionistic self‐presentation and depressive symptoms at the between‐persons level, over and above in‐person social support. Although the current work does not support these relations at the within‐persons level, future work should continue to test the PSDM across time to examine whether these patterns are truly captured by stable, trait‐like differences between people or whether these findings were a byproduct of the stagnant nature of the COVID‐19 pandemic for young people. Nonetheless, it is clear that adolescents with perfectionistic self‐presentational tendencies may require support in lessening the importance of appearing perfect as well as coping with feelings of social disconnection rooted in both in‐person and online experiences to mitigate the impact of perfectionism on adolescent mental health.
