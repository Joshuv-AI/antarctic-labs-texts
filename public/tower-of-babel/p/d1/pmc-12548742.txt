Federal Research Funding for Child Lung Health and Disease
Authors: Bria M. Coates, Hara Levy, Phyllis Dennery, Thomas J. Mariani
Published: 2025

Research focused on fetal and early life has lifelong benefits for children, adults, and society (1) and contributes to the broader field of medical knowledge, fostering innovations and discoveries that benefit people of all ages (2). Without adequate funding for pediatric research, opportunities for better health outcomes, reduced healthcare costs, and advances in medical knowledge for people of all ages may be lost.

Developmental Origins of Human Disease

The “Barker hypothesis,” initially posited by Dr. David Barker, suggests that there are developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) across the lifespan. Early studies revealed an association between early 20th century infant mortality and ischemic heart disease rates some 50 years later (3). This led to the “fetal origins” hypothesis of adult diseases, which proposed a relationship between early-life nutrition and the development of chronic disease as an adult. Through the later part of the 20th century, a larger group of scientists continued to study these relationships, resulting in the World Congress on Fetal Origins of Adult Disease in 2001 and 2003. Attendees of these meetings subsequently formed the International Society for Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (4). Through the lens of the DOHaD hypothesis, one can appreciate the impact of the fetal, perinatal, and pediatric “environment” on an individual’s health status across their lifespan. The DOHaD hypothesis emphasizes the importance of the prenatal environment, maternal health, and early childhood exposures for achieving optimal adult health. It suggests that factors such as poor maternal nutrition, exposure to toxins, or stress during pregnancy can influence the development of organs and metabolic systems in the fetus, which may affect the individual’s susceptibility to conditions such as obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and hypertension in later life, highlighting the significance of early-life conditions in shaping an individual’s health trajectory (5). Among many exposures that may increase risk for chronic disease are tobacco-related products (6).

A central tenet of DOHaD is the concept of “developmental plasticity,” which can be thought of as types of adaptations that occur commonly at the level of the individual. Defined examples include the thrifty phenotype (7) and environmental mismatch (8) hypotheses. Both essentially describe a response in which fetal deficiencies, such as malnutrition, potentiate developmental changes in physiology that lead to maladaptive responses when resources are no longer scarce. Although it is likely that many mechanisms can confer these maladaptive responses, a considerable focus has been on the role of epigenetics (9), including deoxyribonucleic acid and histone modifications, as well as noncoding ribonucleic acids.

Perhaps the developmental origins of respiratory disease have been most appreciated in the context of dysanaptic lung growth. Dysanapsis refers to the “atypical” growth of the lung during early life, resulting in disproportionate size of the parenchyma relative to the conducting airways (10). Dysanapsis has long been believed to be a contributing factor to lung function deficits in asthma, and contemporary data support abnormalities in lung growth as a risk factor (11). More recently, dysanapsis has been associated with irreversible airway obstruction in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (12).

Emerging evidence supports the developmental origins of adult lung diseases, including COPD, highlighting how early-life factors critically shape long-term respiratory health. Early-life lung infections and premature birth, in particular, have been identified as a significant risk factors for impaired lung development and subsequent COPD (13). Individuals born preterm exhibit persistent lung function deficits that extend into middle adulthood, increasing their susceptibility to chronic respiratory conditions. In addition, exposure to adverse intrauterine environments, such as maternal smoking and poor nutrition, further compounds risk by disrupting lung development and repair mechanisms. Understanding these developmental influences is crucial for identifying at-risk populations early and developing preventive interventions that aim to improve lifelong respiratory outcomes.

An understanding of DOHaD has broad implications for public health, emphasizing the importance of prenatal and early childhood care, nutrition, and overall well-being in preventing and managing chronic diseases into adulthood. Research in childhood health and disease can lead to advances in the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of pediatric illnesses and conditions relevant to adults (14). Early intervention and research can mitigate the long-term impact of diseases in adulthood. In addition, treating conditions in their early stages is often more cost effective than dealing with advanced or severe cases.

State of National Institutes of Health Funding for Pediatric Research

Despite the growing recognition of childhood origins of disease, only a fraction of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) research budget supports investigation into pediatric health. Overall, only approximately 12.9% of the total NIH budget, or $5.5 billion, was awarded for research coded as pediatric in 2021 (5). Although NIH spending for pediatric research has increased over time, when adjusted for inflation, funding for pediatric research declined by 15.9% from 2004 to 2015 (15).

The largest funder of pediatric research at the NIH is the Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD), but this institute funds only approximately 16% of research categorized as pediatric. This means that 84% of pediatric research is spread across the 25 other institutes and centers. To coordinate pediatric expertise and resources across the NIH, Dr. Diana Bianchi, former director of the NICHD, established the NIH Pediatric Research Consortium in 2018. In addition to tracking pediatric funding, this consortium’s objective is to explore gaps in the pediatric research portfolio across the NIH, enhance new areas of collaboration, and promote research training for the next generation of pediatricians. In the consortium’s rough estimate, about $500 million supported research coded as pediatric and lung related in 2022, with National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) and the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases being the largest contributors. However, this limited categorization likely includes projects without an exclusive, or even primary, focus on the lungs. In addition, research potentially relevant to health and disease in childhood, but not coded as pediatric, is not captured in these data.

The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine has assembled National Academies efforts to enhance pediatric health research funding through a program titled, “Strategies to Enhance Pediatric Health Research funded by NIH,” in response to a congressional mandate and request from NIH to examine pediatric research supported by NIH. The committee’s task is to conduct a comprehensive examination of the NIH pediatric research portfolio and structure. This review will assess the methods and rationale used to categorize projects as pediatric research through the Research, Condition, and Disease Categorization process, as well as how pediatric components are included or excluded from broader NIH initiatives. In addition, the committee will identify structural or procedural barriers that may impede pediatric research applicants and evaluate how research priorities for child health are established across NIH institutes and centers. On the basis of this assessment, the committee will develop recommendations to enhance the NIH’s support for pediatric research, with a focus on improving funding mechanisms, infrastructure, and policy while considering the administrative burdens on both researchers and NIH staff members. The committee may also explore opportunities to leverage the NIH Clinical Center to advance innovative pediatric research. The committee will convene throughout 2025, with a summary consensus report to be published in early 2026.

Restructuring of the NHLBI Division of Lung Disease

The NHLBI has initiated a reorganization of its Division of Lung Disease, adding branches to the division including one focusing on lung development/pediatric diseases. Dr. James Kiley, former director of the Lung Division at the NHLBI, described the proposed reorganization as intended “to go where the science is headed.” According to Dr. Kiley, research within the lung development/pediatric diseases branch will address lung development, stem cells, lung cell lineage, bronchopulmonary displasia, surfactants, cystic fibrosis, primary ciliary dyskinesia, and gene editing. With the addition of a lung development/pediatric diseases branch, we are hopeful that there will be renewed attention to and increased funding for pediatric research and enhanced alignment of the division with cutting edge science to further collaboration with the Division of Lung Disease to advance pediatric research funding and new grant opportunities.

Supporting Pediatric Research through Advocacy

Although pediatric funding has increased, the core problem remains: funding for research in children represents only a fraction of the funding for research in adults. Given proposed cuts to the overall NIH budget and to the NICHD, there is concern that pediatric research is at risk of falling behind even further. Effective, targeted advocacy in pursuit of decreasing this disparity is a critical strategy to pursue, as leading professional medical and research organizations recognize, such as the American Thoracic Society, the Pediatric Policy Council, and the Society for Pediatric Research. With changing healthcare needs, pediatric research funding is a priority for many advocacy groups and may require partnering with health organizations to increase effectiveness. Through meetings on Capitol Hill and with directors of NIH institutes and centers, these organizations have championed NIH-sponsored pediatric and perinatal research, with their advocacy strengthened by firsthand narratives demonstrating the impact of health policy funding decisions.

It is essential for medical professionals and researchers to educate elected officials about the importance of pediatric research funding by building productive relationships with members of Congress regardless of their political affiliations. To be effective and most meaningful, advocacy should involve telling compelling stories about research-related issues that directly affect patients. These personal narratives help decision makers understand how sustained pediatric research funding translates into real-world health improvements. Furthermore, effectively persuading target audiences and winning their agreement requires significant patience and time. Writing op-eds and doing media interviews in areas of one’s expertise can also help educate the public alongside key stakeholders. Although the long-term goal may be to secure substantial and permanent pediatric research funding, short-term objectives may be more incremental, such as introducing legislation to support increased research funding for a specific innovative treatment. Overall, successful advocacy requires an understanding of the political landscape, the ability to adapt to the partisan climate, and a pragmatic approach, which may require advocates to continue to return and engage key leaders and policymakers again and again to advance pediatric research funding priorities.
