The clinical value of lymph-node dissection in hepatitis B virus-associated intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Fu-Ping Zhong, Xue-Qin Tan, Xiao-Fang Wang, Hao Peng, Ye Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hepatitis B virus-associated intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (HBV-ICC) exhibits distinct clinicopathological characteristics, and the role of lymph-node dissection (LND) in its management remains contentious. This study aims to evaluate the clinical value of LND in HBV-ICC. Clinicopathological data of 76 HBV-ICC patients treated at Pingxiang People’s Hospital between 2015 and 2020 were analyzed. The cohort comprised 41 patients who underwent LND (LND[+] group) and 35 patients who did not (LND[−] group). Clinicopathological characteristics, perioperative outcomes, and survival were subjected to statistical analysis. The cohort had 1-, 2-, and 5- year overall survival (OS) rates of 85.5%, 27.6%, and 13.2%. The LND(+) group had significantly higher postoperative complications than the LND(−) group (P < .05). OS and postoperative recurrence rates did not significantly differ between the 2 groups (P = .71 and P = .44). Multivariate analysis showed that independent risk factors for poor prognosis after ICC resection included resection margin < 1cm (HR = 3.97 [2.11–7.48], P < .05), poor tumor differentiation (HR = 0.43 [0.19–0.95], P < .05), microvascular invasion (HR = 1.92 [0.98–3.78], P < .05), and nonanatomic hepatectomy (HR = 2.07 [1.13–3.81], P < .05). Resection margin, tumor differentiation, microvascular invasion, and anatomic hepatectomy were identified as independent prognostic factors in HBV-ICC. LND did not improve patient outcomes while potentially increasing complication risks, thus not recommended.

1. Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is currently widely regarded as a common malignant liver tumor originating from intrahepatic bile duct cells. It accounts for about 15% of all primary liver cancers.[1] ICC is known for its insidious onset, rapid progression, high recurrence rates after surgery, and generally poor prognosis.[2] The best chance for long-term survival for ICC patients currently is radical resection.[3] Most current expert guidelines and studies recommend radical tumor removal combined with standard regional lymph-node dissection (LND).[4–6] However, this approach is not without controversy. Several recent studies, including a meta-analysis by Zhou et al,[7,8] suggest that performing LND does not actually extend overall survival or lower recurrence rates for ICC patients. In fact, it may actually increase the risk of surgical complications.

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is recognized as a significant independent risk factor for ICC.[9,10] Current research suggested that HBV-ICC may originate from bipotent hepatic progenitor cells within the portal tracts.[11] HBV-ICC differs significantly from nonviral ICC in clinical, imaging, and pathological features, but shares similarities with HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinoma, including frequent cirrhosis, predominantly mass-forming tumors, and infrequent lymph-node metastasis.[12,13] The lymph-node metastasis rate in HBV-ICC may be lower than in nonviral-related ICC, which could contribute to the variable survival benefits of LND observed across different studies.

For patients with HBV-ICC, LND decision-making requires special consideration of the underlying liver status. Underlying cirrhosis with portal hypertension increases the technical difficulty of hilar dissection and the risk of hemorrhage. However, studies specifically evaluating the value of LND in HBV-ICC are currently scarce. Therefore, this study aims to conduct a retrospective case-control analysis to evaluate the utility of LND in HBV-ICC.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Patients and groups

This retrospective clinical study was conducted in accordance with established national and institutional ethical guidelines for research involving human subjects and the use of human tissues. We included surgical ICC patients with serologically confirmed positive HBsAg or anti-HBc treated between January 2015 and December 2020 (Table 1). Patients were divided into 2 groups: the LND(+) group (n = 41) and the LND(−) group (n = 35). Clinical and pathological characteristics were collected for all patients. Postoperative complications recorded included severe pneumonia, bile leakage, intra-abdominal hemorrhage, intra-abdominal infection, organ failure, lymph leakage, and stubborn ascites. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Pingxiang People’s Hospital (Approval Number: PK-2023Z043-HS02). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

2.2. Inclusion criteria

Liver function: Child-Pugh Class A, or Class B optimized to Class A with treatment. Hepatitis B status: HBsAg-positive or anti-HBc-positive by serological confirmation. Postoperative pathology showing ICC with clear margins. No prior cancer history. No chemotherapy or radiation before surgery. Patients aged 18 to 80 years. Complete available records.

2.3. Exclusion criteria

Patients who underwent lymph-node biopsy or extended LND. Recurrent ICC or cancer spread outside the liver. Patients with concomitant infections such as HCV, HDV, or EBV were excluded.

2.4. Surgical standards

For curative resection, ICC requires a microscopically negative margin confirmed by postoperative pathology. Regarding LND, the standard scope includes routine clearance of lymph-nodes around the hepatic artery and within the hepatoduodenal ligament. For right-sided intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: additional clearance of lymph-nodes posterior to the pancreas. For left-sided intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: additional clearance of lymph nodes around the gastric cardia and along the lesser curvature of the stomach. A minimum of 6 lymph nodes must be retrieved for pathological examination.

2.5. Follow-up

Follow-up was conducted every 3 months postoperatively for the first 2 years and twice a year thereafter via telephone or outpatient clinic. If recurrence was suspected, it was confirmed with abdominal computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. The study endpoint was 5 years after the operation or patient death, whichever occurred first.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 22.0, Chicago). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (range). Categorical variables are presented as counts. Comparisons between groups for continuous variables were made using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons between groups for categorical variables were made using the Chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Survival rates were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and survival curves were plotted. Survival analysis was performed using the Log-rank test. Univariate analysis of survival was conducted using the Log-rank test. Multivariate analysis of survival was performed using the Cox proportional hazards regression model. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Clinicopathological characteristics of the patients

No significant differences were observed between the 2 groups regarding age, comorbidities, tumor diameter, tumor location, CA19-9 levels, AFP levels, Child-Pugh grade or tumor stage (all P > .05). Similarly, no significant differences were found for microvascular invasion, resection margin status (>1 cm vs ≤ 1 cm), tumor differentiation (poor vs moderate/well), surgical approach (laparoscopic vs open), surgical method (anatomic vs nonanatomic hepatectomy), or gross tumor type (mass-forming vs non-mass-forming) (all P > .05). However, the LND(+) group had a significantly higher proportion of female patients (P < .05) (see Table 1).

3.2. Perioperative outcomes

There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of operative time or intraoperative blood loss (both P > .05). However, the rate of postoperative complications was significantly higher in the LND(+) group compared to the LND(−) group (P < .05). In the LND (+) group, 1 patient developed postoperative rupture of a pseudoaneurysm of the proper hepatic artery; successful hemostasis was achieved by a second interventional procedure. All 14 patients who experienced complications were graded Clavien–Dindo ≤ II.

3.3. Overall survival

The entire cohort (n = 76) had 1-, 2-, and 5-year overall survival (OS) rates of 85.5%, 27.6%, and 13.2%, respectively, with a median survival time of 23.8 months (follow-up range: 5–60 months). Among HBV-ICC patients, the LND(+) group (n = 41) had 1-, 2-, and 5-year OS rates of 85.3%, 24.4%, and 9.8%, respectively, with a median survival time of 22.51 months. The LND(-) group (n = 35) had 1-, 2-, and 5-year OS rates of 85.7%, 31.4%, and 17.1%, respectively, with a median survival time of 25.3 months. Comparison of OS between the 2 groups showed no statistically significant difference (P = .71). However, the LND(-) group demonstrated a trend towards better long-term survival compared to the LND(+) group (see Fig. 1).

3.4. Disease-free survival

In the LND(+) group, the 2-year recurrence rate was 90.2%, with a median time to recurrence of 9.7 months. In the LND(−) group, the 2-year recurrence rate was 82.8%, with a median time to recurrence of 12.2 months. There was no statistically significant difference in postoperative recurrence rates between the LND(−) and LND(+) groups (P = .44). However, the LND(−) group showed a trend towards lower long-term DFS compared to the LND(+) group (see Fig. 2).

3.5. Survival analysis

Univariate analysis identified the following factors as significantly associated with prognosis in patients undergoing radical resection for HBV-ICC (all P < .05): CA19-9 level, tumor differentiation grade, tumor stage, microvascular invasion, and type of hepatectomy (see Table 2).

4. Discussion

The incidence of ICC has been rising in recent years.[14] Hepatitis B virus infection is a known risk factor for ICC, particularly in China, HBV infection is present > 30% of ICC patients,[13] though the exact oncogenic mechanisms remain unclear. Emerging evidence suggests that HBV-ICC may arise from hepatocytes through a complex, multifactorial process involving multiple genes and steps. It primarily stems from both the virus’s direct carcinogenic effects and indirect mechanisms driven by chronic inflammatory microenvironments.[15–17] Given these findings, HBV - specific therapeutic strategies for ICC warrant further investigation and clinical emphasis. However, major cholangiocarcinoma guidelines, including those from the NCCN, BSG, and CSCO, do not provide separate treatment classifications or specific recommendations for HBV - ICC. Instead, they maintain consistent treatment recommendations for ICC regardless of etiology, including HBV.[18,19]

Skeletonized lymph node dissection (LND) has become a well-established technique and is routinely performed in many medical centers. Studies report LND rates of 50% to 80% in ICC patients.[20] While LND is no longer a technical barrier in radical ICC surgery, surgical risk remains a key concern. Our findings demonstrate that LND during radical resection for HBV-ICC increases postoperative complication rates. Therefore, given the uncertain clinical benefit of LND, routine lymphadenectomy is not recommended. Additionally, our study found no association between LND and long-term prognosis in HBV-ICC.

HBV-ICC frequently arises against a background of liver cirrhosis.This pathological microenvironment exhibits unique lymphatic vessel distribution characteristics. Studies have found that lymphatic vessel density is significantly lower in cirrhotic liver tissue compared to normal liver, with impaired lymphatic drainage function.[21] Furthermore, the chronic inflammatory microenvironment induced by HBV is enriched with inflammatory cytokines such as TGF-βand IL-6. While these cytokines promote tumorigenesis, they concurrently suppress the VEGF-C/VEGF-D-mediated lymphangiogenic signaling pathway.[22] HBV-ICC retains progenitor cell properties, resulting in an expression profile intermediate between hepatocytes and cholangiocytes. These tumors characteristically show: Low expression of molecules associated with lymphatic metastasis, such as: VEGF-C, The CCL21/CCR7 axis; High expression of hepatocyte-characteristic molecules, such as:

HepPar-1, ALB mRNA. Single-cell sequencing studies further reveal that the expression level of the chemokine receptor CXCR4 is significantly lower in HBV-ICC compared to nonviral ICC. Critically, CXCR4 is the key receptor mediating tumor cell homing to lymph nodes.[23] Collectively, these findings suggest that the incidence of lymph node metastasis in HBV-ICC is likely lower than in other ICC subtypes. This could be a key reason why routine LND fails to improve prognosis in HBV-ICC. Moreover,the overall poor prognosis may be attributed to the lack of standardized systemic therapy for HBV-ICC. Currently, gemcitabine plus platinum-based chemotherapy or S-1 (tegafur/gimeracil/oteracil) remains the first-line adjuvant treatment for ICC.[24,25] However, its efficacy in HBV-ICC remains unclear.[26] In our study, all patients received long-term oral entecavir postoperatively, with dosing titrated according to viral load. Emerging evidence suggests that antiviral therapy (pre- or postoperatively), transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), or combination therapy with lenvatinib may significantly improve OS and cancer-specific survival in HBV-ICC patients potentially outperforming traditional chemotherapy.[27–29]

In addition, our research showed anatomic hepatectomy is a significant prognostic factor. Recent retrospective studies suggest that anatomic hepatectomy improves 5-year OS and DFS compared to nonanatomic hepatectomy.[30] Given its similar clinical characteristics to HBV-HCC, anatomic hepatectomy when feasible based on future liver remnant volume and functional reserve should be the preferred surgical approach for HBV-ICC. Resection margin, tumor differentiation, and microvascular invasion are independent prognostic factors, consistent with prior research.[31,32]

Certainly, several limitations of this study should be taken into account. As a retrospective study, it lacked randomization; surgeons may have selectively performed LND in patients with better preoperative liver function and earlier tumor stages, and the extent of LND was not standardized, so the results could be skewed. Moreover, incomplete records for CEA, CA-125, and histological subtyping in the original dataset precluded comprehensive or stratified analyses of their clinical value in HBV- ICC. Consequently, the assessment of lymphadenectomy may not have adequately reflected inter-subgroup heterogeneity.This study is a single-center study, with a small sample size.

In summary, LND does not improve prognosis in HBV-ICC patients but increases perioperative complications. Routine LND should not be recommended. These findings should be validated by larger, multi - center, high - quality studies, especially randomized controlled trials.
