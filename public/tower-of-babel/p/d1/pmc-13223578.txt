Onchocerciasis elimination mapping in Malawi: mapping of vector Simulium breeding sites using an integrated approach of community knowledge and topographical maps
Authors: Kareen Atekem, Laston Sitima, Dylo Pemba, Adoum M Oumar, Ben Isake, Steven Gowelo, Chinsisi Chipatso, Kondwani Muheka, Franklin Ayisi, Lassane Koala, Olabanji Surakat, Arwa Elaagip, Dziedzom K de Souza, Anne Heggen, Charles D Mackenzie, Daniel A Boakye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge areas in Malawi have not been treated for onchocerciasis. To understand whether to provide treatment in these areas of unknown onchocerciasis endemicity, the Malawi Onchocerciasis Control Program conducted identification and mapping of breeding sites as a prerequisite for onchocerciasis elimination mapping (OEM).MethodsThe team employed a community knowledge approach using questionnaires and discussions, alongside the standard method of using topographical maps, to plan and implement river prospections for Simulium vector breeding sites.ResultsA total of 188 sites were prospected from 180 rivers, with 122 sites (67.8%) identified from community information and 58 (32.2%) from topographic maps. Of the 188 sites visited, 30 were positive for Simulium damnosum larvae/pupae and 91 were considered potential breeding sites and need to be visited in a different season for confirmation. A breeding site map for OEM areas was developed. Prospection using this approach lasted for 24 days (approximately eight rivers/day) compared with a previous prospection with the same team that used 12 days to prospect 25 rivers (approximately two rivers/day).ConclusionThis demonstrates the potential of using the health system structure and community knowledge for breeding site surveys, shortening the prospection time while effectively identifying fast-flowing rivers and breeding sites.Keywords: breeding site mapping, community knowledge, Malawi, onchocerciasis elimination mapping (OEM)

Introduction

xOnchocerciasis, one of the neglected tropical diseases (NTDs), has been earmarked for elimination by 2030.1 With the paradigm shift from control of the disease as a public health problem to elimination of transmission, the need to identify all sites endemic for the disease has become imperative. Elimination of transmission implies all endemic areas, including hypo-endemic areas previously not under any interventions, will require entomological and serological evaluations to confirm interruption of transmission.2,3 Thus, all the hypo-endemic areas need to be remapped to determine if treatment is needed. In Malawi, hypo-endemic areas that need remapping are about 3–4 times the size of the hyper- and meso-endemic areas and just like in many endemic African countries, the main vector of the disease in this area is the Simulium damnosum s. l. complex.4 Equally, evidence exists of the Simulium neavei group, specifically Simulium nyasalandicum and Simulium woodi, which were originally discovered in the country,5 but in recent surveys, there have not been any records of their presence.

For an effective entomological evaluation, there is a need to conduct breeding site surveys (BSSs; searches for unknown onchocerciasis vector breeding sites along a river) to identify areas of blackfly breeding. BSSs are also required for the selection of first-line communities for targeted interventions and epidemiological evaluations,6,7 because the risk of onchocerciasis is highest closer to the breeding sites, thus the name river blindness. They are essential for onchocerciasis elimination mapping and involve actively searching for potential or productive Simulium damnosum s. l. breeding sites in river stretches. Potential breeding sites are identified based on their physical characteristics, including fast running stretches (rapids) and vegetations, and these areas are searched for the presence or absence of vector larvae. Productive or active breeding sites are determined by the presence of at least one larva or pupa. Submerged substrates such as trailing vegetation, roots, branches, fallen leaves or stones found around these sites are examined for the presence of Simulium damnosum s. l. larvae. Once larvae are found on the substrates, they are examined morphologically and those belonging to the S. damnosum s. l. stored for cytotaxonomic identification.8

The first stage of every BSS is to conduct a desk review using topographic maps, satellite images and historical data such as the rapid epidemiological mapping of onchocerciasis, and any published documents on onchocerciasis prevalence in the country. Relying solely on topographical maps may lead to missing potential or active breeding sites and could take a longer time or both. A breeding site assessment—defined by the WHO onchocerciasis elimination mapping manual as a visit to preselected locations on rivers to look for evidence of and/or suitable conditions for blackfly breeding—conducted in Malawi’s hyper- and meso-endemic areas in 2015 relied solely on the use of topographical maps, leading to the prospection of only well-known major rivers and their tributaries and missing out on smaller rivers that may potentially harbor breeding (unpublished data).9 This 12-day activity averaged two rivers prospected per day. With BSSs being a crucial activity for any onchocerciasis evaluations, there is need for an innovative, effective and rapid approach to overcome the old approach of using only topographical maps (manuscript submitted). In this study, we highlight the importance of leveraging a decentralized health system structure and community knowledge to effectively plan and identify fast-flowing stretches and/or breeding sites during BSS activities in Malawi.

Methodology

Description and transmission of information through Malawi health system

The Malawi health system is organized at four levels linked through an established referral system: community, primary, secondary and tertiary. The community, primary and secondary care levels fall under the District Councils. The Director of Health and Social Services is the Head of the District Health Care System and reports to the District Commissioner (DC), who is the Controlling Officer of public institutions at district level. The functions of the district health offices (DHOs) include managing all public health facilities at district level and directing provision of both primary and secondary level health services at district level. The DHOs report to the DC and are under the Ministry of Local Government.

At the community level, health services are provided by Health Surveillance Assistants (HSAs) and staff at health posts, dispensaries and maternity clinics. Each HSA is responsible for a catchment area of about 1000 people, although most are responsible for a larger population. The HSAs are supported by Senior HSAs (SHSAs) (unpublished data).10

Health information is often transmitted in a vertical pattern, moving from the national level to the district level and then to the communities, before flowing back up from the communities to the national level. Study design and approach

This operational research was embedded within Malawi’s countrywide BSS, to assess whether incorporating health workers and community members in the identification of breeding sites could expedite BSS implementation while ensuring no breeding sites are missed.

Field implementation

This work was conducted in three phases from February to April 2024: (i) planning and development of the field questionnaire; (ii) administration of field questionnaires by HSAs and community members; and (iii) river prospection by entomology teams. One month before the scheduled field activities, SHSAs at the district level were provided with questionnaires to collect information on fast-flowing river stretches within their health catchment areas. With support from HSAs and community members, SHSAs collected and consolidated feedback on these water bodies, which was then shared with the entomology team to guide the river prospection. Conceptual framework for implementation.

To facilitate onchocerciasis elimination efforts, Malawi was previously divided into six operational zones, A–F, based on factors such as river basins, onchocerciasis-treated areas and natural boundaries and ecological factors. For this prospection activity, four districts within Zone E—Mulanje, Thyolo, Blantyre and Neno—were excluded, as extensive prospection had already been conducted in these areas.

Phase 1: Planning and development of field questionnaire

A series of meetings, led by the NTD Program Manager, were held to plan the implementation of the BSS. During these meetings, a questionnaire (see Supplementary file) was developed to obtain information on local rivers and fly bites. The questionnaire included questions on the presence of rivers with fast-flowing stretches, name and location of the rivers and whether the river was seasonal or perennial. Fly-related questions were also included in the questionnaire to assess whether community members experienced bites, the time of the day the bites occurred and the months of the year when biting was most prevalent.

Phase 2: Administration of field questionnaires

The SHSAs oversee multiple health facilities (HFs), which coordinate activities in smaller communities in their catchment area, so they were well positioned to distribute the questionnaires to the various facilities. The Program Manager sent the questionnaires to the SHSAs, which were then sent via the HSAs to the communities for information. Once the questionnaires were completed, they were returned to the SHSAs, who summarized the river information according to each HF, and sent this back to the District team. The District team, together with the Program Manager, collated the information and shared it with the entomology teams at the time of prospection.

Phase 3: River prospection

Upon the entomology team’s arrival at the district, a meeting was held with the health district team, including SHSAs and HSAs, to plan field activities. During this meeting, the summarized information from the SHSAs and an open satellite map (https://mapcarta.com/s/malawi) were reviewed to identify rivers to be prospected, the communities in which the rivers were located and the driving route to be used. Potential breeding sites on the map were identified by looking for sections of river flow over very steep surfaces. In satellite images this was achieved through terrain analysis focusing on the slope and elevation on the maps. Based on photographs and videos of previously prospected rivers, a majority of the river stretches indicated on the summarized sheets by the community were excluded because they were unsuitable for supporting the breeding of blackfly vectors. In cases where this summary information was unavailable, discussions with the HSAs and SHSAs were conducted with the communities to identify potential rivers within the communities.

Entomologists involved in previous breeding site assessments in Malawi were trained on the current protocol and partitioned into two teams for the BSS activity. During the river visit, either the HSAs or SHSAs (or both) led the teams to the preidentified rivers in communities within their health zones. In each community, an individual was selected by the local leadership to lead the BSS team to the preidentified rivers and any other tributaries. Additional conversations with community members also led to visiting rivers not originally listed. Global positioning system coordinates were collected for all sites visited irrespective of whether larvae were found or not. The river coordinates were uploaded into ArcGIS software (ArGIS Desktop 10.8 software by Esri) and used to develop the breeding site map of the untreated areas in Malawi.

The entomology teams prospected 188 sites on a total of 180 rivers. From the 180 rivers prospected, 122 (67.8%) were obtained from community information (46 [37.7%] from questionnaire sheets and 76 [62.3%] from onsite discussions with the SHSAs/HSAs and community members). The rest (58; 32.2%) were from topographic maps confirmed with information from the health facilities and communities. Out of 188 sites visited, larval samples were collected from 107 sites, and 30 were positive for Simulium damnosum larvae/pupae. A total of 91 sites were found to be potential breeding sites. Discussion

Community engagement has been a longstanding approach used in many health interventions and has shown beneficial outcomes. In 2020, Questa et al. reported its significance in reducing neonatal mortality through women participating in learning and action groups, reducing HIV and other sexually transmitted infections through empowering and mobilization interventions among marginalized groups, and reducing the incidence or prevalence in malaria, as well as infant diarrhea through community health worker interventions.11 Furthermore, its importance has been valuable in getting to hard-to-reach communities where indigenes from remote villages are trained and used to deliver health services,12 and in conflict-affected areas.13 Although previously community information has been used during field activities and implementation for BSSs, this study is the first to report on using a combination of community knowledge with topographical maps in planning river prospections for Simulium vector breeding sites.

The current study also demonstrates the feasibility of using the community and the health system structure in conducting BSSs, overcoming some of the major challenges often encountered in actively searching for breeding sites for entomological activities based on only topographical maps. Prospection under this approach was completed within 24 days (7–30 March), averaging approximately eight rivers a day. By contrast, a previous prospection in Malawi by the same team using only topographical maps covered 25 rivers over 12 days, averaging two rivers per day.9 Involving community members in preidentifying rivers before entomological visits reduced the time-consuming process of searching for suitable rivers.

The use of community knowledge and involvement not only impacted the prospection time, but also could have a positive impact on cost efficiency. Furthermore, this approach was incorporated within the health system. Although costing analysis was not performed, spending fewer days in the field and not having an additional budget for incentives would translate to lower personnel and overall operational costs. For example, in 1998, Amazigo et al. reported the benefits of cost reduction in using community involvement in mass drug treatment for onchocerciasis, where the community‐directed treatment approach was less costly compared with mass treatment delivered via mobile teams.14

Despite the benefits of leveraging community knowledge and health system structure to identify fast-flowing rivers and accelerate prospection, several challenges and limitations emerged during implementation. Initially, there was some confusion in understanding the questionnaire forms, which led to an excessive number of rivers being listed. However, incorporating photographs and videos into discussions improved comprehension. In addition, variations in river names across different communities created a risk of over-reporting the same river under multiple names.

Conclusion

To conclude, a breeding site map for the untreated areas in Malawi has been developed. BSSs using community knowledge and the health system were shown to shorten the prospection time while effectively identifying fast-flowing rivers and breeding sites. However, recommendations to improve the implementation of this approach in other settings or countries include providing enough time for the planning and implementation of this strategy, ensuring a proper understanding of the questionnaire and monitoring of the rollout process before field activities commence and attaching photographs of fast-flowing rivers to facilitate their identification.
