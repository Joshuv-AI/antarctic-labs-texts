Association between slow-wave activity from multi-night at-home wireless EEG records and cognitive performance in older adults
Authors: Shuo Qin, Ju Lynn Ong, Kian Foong Wong, Tian Liang, Eric Kwun Kai Ng, Woon-Puay Koh, Juan Helen Zhou, Michael W L Chee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesEvidence for the association between slow-wave sleep and cognitive function in older adults has been inconsistent. We measured the variability in-home electroencephalography (EEG) recordings over multiple nights to determine its impact on the association between sleep slow wave measures and cognition.MethodsParticipants were 49 (26 females) community-dwelling older adults (median age 75.3 years) who were functionally independent and without cognitive impairment or self-reported sleep disorders. Each contributed eight nights of at-home EEG headband sleep recordings (Dreem Headband, Beacon Biosignals, Boston, USA). Night-to-night variability in sleep stage percentage and spectral power in SWA (0.8–4.5 Hz), Theta (4.5–7.5 Hz), Alpha (8–12 Hz), and Sigma (11–15 Hz) was determined using the coefficient of variation (CV). CV of <20% was considered stable. Correlation between stable sleep metrics and multi-domain cognitive performance was assessed.ResultsMost older adults independently operated the Dreem band with good acceptability. Night-to-night variability in the current sample (n = 49; 302 nights) was high for N3% (CV = 47%), moderate for rapid eye movement (REM)% (CV = 22%), and low for N2% and relative power from all frequency bands (CV = 3%–17%). Only relative slow-wave activity (SWA) (0.8–4.5 Hz) was significantly associated with global cognition, when considering both averaged (r = 0.55, p < .001) and single-night (r = 0.2–0.5) assessments.ConclusionsWearable EEG can reliably collect at-home, multi-night sleep data in older adults. SWA but not N3 duration showed high night-to-night stability within each participant and was the only measure significantly and robustly associated with cognitive function.

Introduction

There are clear associations between several sleep measures and cognitive performance in older persons [1, 2]. Disrupted sleep in such individuals has been linked with cognitive impairment and risk of dementia [3–9]. The gold standard for objective assessment of sleep is in-laboratory polysomnography (PSG). However, as most studies are for a single or two nights only, it is unclear specific PSG sleep metrics are stable across multiple nights and maintain consistent correlations with older adults’ cognitive function [10, 11].

Although experimental work has highlighted the importance of deep sleep for the clearance of neurotoxic metabolites [12], the association between different slow-wave sleep measures and cognition in older adults is less consistent [10, 11]. Two comprehensive meta-analyses found that duration (or ratio) of N3 sleep was not correlated with overall cognitive function and memory performance [1, 5]. However, slow-wave activity (SWA), or relative power from the Delta band (0.8–4.5 Hz), appeared marginally associated with cognition in one meta-analysis [5]. This marginal effect was likely driven by two large-scale PSG samples that found significant but weak correlations between SWA and cognition in over 2000 older adults [13]. In contrast, studies with smaller samples have reported strong correlations between SWA and memory in older adults [6], as well as SWA and risk for Alzheimer’s dementia [14, 15].

The difference in association with cognition between N3 sleep and SWA may be attributed to older adults’ oscillations being smaller in amplitude and not reaching N3 criteria for slow-wave sleep [16, 17]. This inclusion of an arbitrary voltage criterion for scoring N3 sleep can also result in greater variability in N3 than SWA across nights.

Most sleep-electroencephalography (EEG) studies are conducted in controlled laboratory settings over 1–2 nights. However, it is unclear how sleep measures from one night in a sleep laboratory are representative of one’s habitual sleep. In contrast, multi-night PSG studies have shown that power spectra exhibit trait-like characteristics, akin to fingerprints, across sessions (at least in adolescents) even 1.5–3 years later [18–20]. Conversely, a recent study using wireless PSG devices showed relatively large fluctuations in N3 sleep duration in middle-aged men across five consecutive days [21]. In-laboratory PSG studies also reported high intra-individual stability in power spectra in older adults across 2–4 nights [22, 23]. However, the extent of within-subject variability for both stages and power spectra and how this is related to cognition in older adults (aged 65 and above) remains a gap in our knowledge.

Wireless, dry-electrode PSG devices have been used to assess sleep in community-dwelling adults at home [21, 24], but little is known about their usability and acceptability in older adults, particularly those with lower technological proficiency. In this population, it remains unclear whether individuals can independently operate these devices to successfully collect sleep data over multiple consecutive nights.

Using a well-validated wireless PSG device (Dreem, Beacon Biosignals, Boston, USA) [25–27], we examined sleep-cognition associations in 49 older adults, utilizing over 300 nights of high-quality sleep data. The aims of the current study were: (1) to quantify variability in both sleep stages and power spectra from SWA, theta, sigma, and alpha bands in adults over the age of 65, (2) to investigate associations between stable sleep metrics and cognition, and (3) to explore the usability and acceptability of older adults independently operating the Dreem headband and collecting high-quality sleep data in situ. We hypothesized that sleep metrics with low within-subject variability across nights, such as SWA, may be more consistently associated with cognition than N3 sleep percentage.

Materials and Methods

Participants characteristics

The participants were adults between the ages of 68 and 80, recruited from the Singapore Towards Healthy Longevity (SG70) study [28]. The SG70 study is a longitudinal aging study that aims to comprehensively assess multiple facets of aging and associated factors in Singaporean older adults.

SG70 participants were invited to enroll in a home-based sleep study. Interested participants were further screened for eligibility. Exclusion criteria for the current study included: (1) having a neurological or psychiatric disorder, (2) having a sleep disorder such as sleep apnea or insomnia, (3) taking medication that could influence sleep, and (4) having uncontrolled cardiovascular or cardiometabolic disease. In addition, the cognitive status of participants was screened by the 10-item Abbreviated Mental Test (AMT-10) [29], and only participants who got 8 out of 10 items correct on the AMT were included. The AMT-10 had been validated in Singapore as a quick screening tool to detect cognitive impairment.

Eligible participants were invited to a nap adaptation session in the sleep laboratory. During this session, participants were asked to nap for 1 h wearing the Dreem headband. Participants who reported discomfort with the headband were excluded from further participation. The overall cognitive status of the participants was assessed using the Singapore-modified Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [30]. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National University of Singapore.

Dreem headband

The Dreem headband (Beacon Biosignals, Boston, USA) is an FDA 501(k) certified dry-electrode, wireless EEG system whose accuracy of sleep staging and power spectra analysis has been compared against PSG [25–27] in older adults and those living with dementia [27]. A recent validation study conducted in older adults and Alzheimer’s patients found the device to have <20 min of bias in stages N2 and N3, compared to the gold-standard PSG. Moreover, relative power from Dreem strongly correlated with PSG power (r2 = 0.6 for NREM SWA, theta, and alpha), supporting the use of this device to examine power spectra in older adults [27].

The Dreem headband records at 250 Hz from 5 recording electrodes (F7, F8, O1, O2, and ground at Fp2). Data collected can be transferred directly to the Beacon servers and reviewed remotely. Dreem uses a proprietary deep-learning-based sleep staging algorithm.

Sleep monitoring

Following the nap session, a home visit session was scheduled for every participant. During the home visit, a research staff member demonstrated how to operate the Dreem headband. A smartphone with mobile data was provided to each participant for the purpose of uploading Dreem data. Participants were instructed to refrain from interacting with the Dreem app on the study smartphone. The start of the Dreem recording was through triple-pressing the “Start” button on the front of the Dreem band. The recording ended when the band was connected to its charging cable. Data uploading started automatically when the band was charging. Participants were instructed to wear the device for eight consecutive nights.

During the sleep monitoring period, Dreem data were checked daily by research staff. Check-in calls were made if prior night data was not detected or if data quality was below Dreem’s 80% quality threshold, to provide help in cases of equipment failure or improper use of the device. In such cases, participants were asked if they were willing to extend the sleep monitoring period by another day (to a maximum of 12 nights).

Quality control procedures

Dreem data was downloaded and processed locally. Sleep macro architecture variables were extracted from the report CSV, and sleep stages were extracted from the hypnogram text file.

A “quality index” is provided for each Dreem headband recording. The EEG data undergoes bandpass filtering between 0.4 and 35 Hz. Notch filters are then applied to remove electrical line noise. Subsequently, each of the four bipolar channels is down-sampled to 60 Hz. The signal amplitude is then normalized between −1 and 1. Next, every 2 s of data are processed by a convolutional neural network. The network returns the probability of the signal being of good or bad quality for each two seconds of EEG data. For each channel, the portal shows the percentage of two-second bins being estimated as being good quality.

Additionally, Dreem provides an “off-head” indicator to denote sub-optimal electrode contact with the scalp. The distributions of off-head percent and signal quality from Dreem data are presented in Supplementary Figures 1 and 2, showing the proportion of data that had to be removed before analyses. Censoring data for signal quality and off-head percent is particularly important for N3 sleep and SWA. Without such censoring, off-head percent was significantly associated with N3% and SWA, pointing to the classification of low-frequency noise from sub-optimal electrode contact as deep sleep (Supplementary Figure 3). After censoring, off-head percent was not associated with deep sleep measures (Supplementary Figure 4).

Nights with an overall quality index less than 80%, more than 20% “off-head” periods, and recordings that were not stopped after wake, were excluded from the analysis. Examples of Dreem recording from different stages can be found in Supplementary Figure 5.

Sleep stage percentage and power spectral analyses

Time spent in N2, N3, and rapid eye movement (REM) was obtained for each night for every participant. Sleep stages were defined by Dreem’s automated staging algorithm. Minutes from sleep stages were then divided by the total sleep time (TST) for the night to calculate the percentage of the night spent in each stage.

The ascertainment of stage N1 sleep is unreliable [31, 32]. More specifically, Dreem identification of N1 epochs is similarly problematic [33]. As a result, we chose not to analyze the N1 stage, although N1 sleep was present in most recordings.

Power spectra estimates were calculated [34] without Hjorth parameters for both F8-O1 and F7-O2. These are not dependent on the proprietary sleep staging pipeline and are based on the filtered EEG signals downloaded from Dreem’s servers. SWA (0.8–4.5 Hz), Theta (4.5–7.5 Hz), Alpha (8–12 Hz), and Sigma (11–15 Hz) bands were calculated for each Dreem recording using the pwelch function in EEG lab [35]. Power estimates were calculated in 6-s windows [34, 36–38], and windows that were larger than 3 SD in the 0.75–4.5 Hz range and 2 SD in the 20–30 Hz range in a 21-epoch window were excluded from analysis. The estimates for both channels were averaged for each night. The power of each band for each epoch was normalized to the total power (0.5–30 Hz) of the epoch to get an epoch-by-epoch relative power ratio. The average relative power of each band was calculated for NREM (N2 and N3) epochs.

In the current study, power spectra were only calculated from NREM epochs. This was because REM sleep showed the lowest agreement between Dreem and PSG in the recent validation study by Ravindran and colleagues [27]. In the same validation study, only power from NREM epochs was examined, and we followed this practice.

Outcome variables assessment

Variability analyses

Within-subject variability of each sleep metric was examined by calculating its coefficient of variation (CV) [21], first within each subject across nights, and then averaged across subjects. Levels of variability were defined based on the following thresholds: CV 0%–20% = low variability; CV 20%–40% = medium variability; and CV >40% = high variability.

Cognitive assessment

A comprehensive neuropsychological battery was administered to all SG70 participants as part of the project. Participants from the current study all provided a signed agreement to link their sleep data with their SG70 cognitive assessment scores. Cognitive assessments took place within one month of the sleep data collection.

A total of 10 cognitive tasks encompassing seven cognitive domains were administered (Supplementary Figure 6). Scores of each test were first standardized to T scores with a mean of 50 and an SD of 10. Response times from the Color Trail Tests were inverted prior to standardization, such that a larger value indicated better performance. Scores in cognitive domains covered by more than one test were the mean of all that domain’s test scores. A global cognition T-score was computed as the average of the seven domains. For a detailed description and calculation of T-scores, see the supplementary material.

Association with age and cognition

Partial correlation analyses were conducted between sleep metrics (averaged across nights) with CV less than 20% and chronological age, controlling for gender, years of education, BMI, and average TST. Another set of partial correlations examined the association between stable sleep metrics (averaged across nights) and global cognition, controlling for the same set of covariates and chronological age.

Results

Participant characteristics

During the recruitment period, a total of 169 SG70 participants were approached about their interest in participating in a sleep study. Sixty-four participants agreed to try the Dreem headband (Figure 1).

Consort diagram showing the flow of participants from recruitment to final sleep monitoring. QC: Quality control, Dreem quality >80%.

From the 64 participants who tried on the Dreem, one was excluded due to an invalid participant ID for data linking to SG70 cognitive assessments, one due to antidepressant use, and another due to high resting blood pressure (systolic blood pressure > 160 mmHg). Two participants withdrew due to time commitment problems, while six participants withdrew citing discomfort with Dreem. No participant reported having a sleep disorder. Participants all scored above 8 for the AMT-10 test. A total of 53 participants proceeded to the 8-day sleep monitoring protocol. Within this group, one participant dropped out due to discomfort with the headband, one was excluded because of erratic sleep patterns, and two were excluded as they were unable to operate the Dreem band independently.

The 49 contributing participants’ average age was 75.3 years; they received 11 years of education and slept an average of 6 h 22 min a night. Participants who wore the Dreem headband at home furnished 419 nights of sleep data. Sleep measurements had to pass a quality threshold to be eligible for analysis (i.e. ≥80% quality and <20% off-head duration). This resulted in 110 nights being discarded. An additional 7 nights were discarded due to uninterpretable sleep stages or long recording periods after wake (Figure 1). After all filtering steps were applied, a total of 302 (72% or recordings) nights were included in the final analyses. Participants in the current study were older (mean age = 75) compared to those in other studies on aging and sleep. Participants were cognitively intact on average (mean MMSE = 27).

Out of the 64 participants who attempted using Dreem, only 6 (9%) withdrew due to band-related discomfort. Of the 53 who participated in the home monitoring phase, only one reported discomfort from the band, and only two could not operate the band independently. The majority of participants could operate and sleep with the Dreem band, with an average TST of 6.4 h per night and other measurements in line with sleep recorded in adults of this age (Table 1). From 419 nights of data provided by 49 participants, 302 nights (72%) with good signal quality were retained for analysis. These retention results show an acceptable success rate in older adults providing good signal sleep PSG data independently. On average, each participant contributed 6.2 nights of Dreem data, with a range of 3–8 nights.

In addition, findings concerning within-subject variability (CV) for sleep duration (TST and TIB) and sleep continuity (sleep efficiency and WASO) from the current sample (Table 1) were consistent with those from a recent study examining sleep variability using Dreem in a younger sample [21]. Specifically, WASO had high variability (CV > 40%) in both the current sample and in middle-aged men from the previous study.

Association between stable sleep metrics and age

For sleep metrics with CV less than 20%, including N2% and power from all bands, partial correlation analyses between their averages (across nights) and chronological age were conducted. Effects of gender, education level, BMI, and averaged total sleep duration across days were controlled for in these correlation analyses.

Even within the current sample’s restricted age range, SWA decreased with age (r = −0.3, p = .05). N2% and power from all other bands were not correlated with age. CVs from all sleep metrics were not associated with age (Supplementary Table 1).

Association between stable sleep metrics and cognition

For sleep metrics with less than 20% CV, including N2% and power from all bands, partial correlation analyses between their averages (across nights) and global cognition were conducted. Effects of age, gender, education level, BMI, and averaged total sleep duration across days were controlled for in these correlation analyses.

Averaged SWA across multiple nights was strongly correlated with global cognition (r = 0.55, p < .001; Figure 3, E). Exploratory correlation analyses between SWA and the separate cognitive domains (Supplementary Table 2) showed that SWA correlated with verbal memory (r = 0.39, p = .01) and processing speed (r = 0.38, p = .01). Averages from N2% and other power bands were not correlated with cognition. CVs from all sleep metrics were not associated with cognition (Supplementary Table 3).

To further explore the night-to-night consistency of the SWA-cognition correlation, a series of correlation analyses was conducted between SWA from each night (up to eight nights) and global cognition. Correlations from all individual nights were significant (r = 0.27–0.47, ps < .05, Supplementary Table 4). Notably, the correlation coefficient from averaged SWA across the nights (r = 0.55) was larger than the coefficient from any of the nights.

Discussion

The current study examined the intra-individual stability of multi-night sleep PSG data collected at home in older adults using the Dreem headband. Older adults were able to use the Dreem headband independently. The multi-night sleep data show that stage N3 sleep varied significantly night-to-night while stage N2 sleep and NREM relative power from all power bands were stable across nights. Most importantly, SWA was strongly and robustly correlated with cognition.

To our knowledge, to date, this current study is the first to quantify multi-night variability in sleep stages and power spectrum in older adults over the age of 65 years. Although the PSG power spectrum has been termed an “electroencephalographic fingerprint” [18], most studies on EEG-power variability have been on younger adults (e.g. adolescents). It is an open question whether these signatures retain their stability through old age. Our finding is novel in that these sleep fingerprints are, in fact, well-preserved as people age, well into their 70s.

The current study highlights inadequate consideration of within-subject variability in stage N3 sleep in older adults in prior studies. Such between-night variation in N3% could underlie the inconsistent association between N3 sleep and cognition in older adults, both in the current sample and in previous single-night PSG studies [5, 11].

Of importance to studies engaging biomarkers to assess risk, measurement error inflates its observed variance and systematically attenuates the regression slope, biasing associations toward the null—a phenomenon known as regression dilution bias [39]. This bias cannot be mitigated by increasing sample size, as increasing sample size only reduces random sampling error and tightens confidence intervals. It does not remove systematic bias from measurement error. Using more stable metrics of deep sleep (e.g. SWA), is thus an important step in formulating clearer associations between sleep and cognitive function/risk of dementia.

Some researchers have proposed replacing N3 with SWA (0.8–4.5 Hz) to indicate the occurrence of deep sleep [4, 14]. SWA evaluated in this manner has been linked with cognitive performance and dementia biomarkers in single-night studies [6, 13, 40], while stage N3 sleep has not shown such a link.

In the current study, SWA was highly stable across nights and strongly correlated with cognitive function, especially verbal memory and processing speed, cognitive domains that also show significant age-related declines in longitudinal studies [41].

The correlation between a single night of SWA and cognition was significant and robust across independent individual nights of data, showing how a single night of SWA measurement may be enough to capture this sleep marker of age-related cognitive impairment. Our results, therefore, strengthen support for using SWA as a marker of both deep sleep and cognition in older adults.

In the current study, only SWA was found to correlate with cognition in these old adults. Of note, spindle-band power (i.e. Sigma power) has been associated with cognition in younger adults [42, 43]. This difference across age groups could be attributed to the age-related reduction in SWA. A significant age-related reduction in SWA was observed in our study population, with a narrow age range of 70 to 80 years. The continuous reduction of SWA in late life could reflect underlying neurodegeneration that negatively affects cognitive function. In younger adults without age-related neurodegeneration, cognition may be influenced by other power bands such as Sigma and Theta, although the neurophysiological basis for this is unclear.

Our study is also the first to show the feasibility of at-home, multi-night EEG data collection in older adults, persons who are often less familiar with operating electronic devices. The advantages of the Dreem band include a simple start and end of recording (no app involved), online monitoring of data quality, and a low level of non-acceptance (9% in the current sample). Future work could extend this evaluation to the collection of multi-night sleep PSG data from patients with mild cognitive impairment or dementia, who have altered sleep EEG characteristics [44].

An unsolved question remains the source of night-to-night variability in N3 sleep. Demographic variables, including age, gender, years of education, BMI, and sleep macro architecture (e.g. sleep duration and efficiency), appear unrelated to this variability either individually or in combination. Notably, N3 from a younger sample (mean age = 43) showed moderate variability (CV = 22%) [21]. It is possible that N3 variability increases with age. More participants over a broader age range are needed to clarify this.

Additionally, it is important to explain the selected metric for quantifying within-subject variability. Although the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) has been typically used to measure device agreement and measurement stability in validation studies, ICC was not used to measure within-subject variability in the current study. ICC depends on the ratio of between-subject to within-subject variance. If the sample is homogeneous (i.e. very low between-subject variance), ICC can be low even if the measurement is stable. CV, on the other hand, is unaffected by between-subject variance and is unit agnostic. For a detailed explanation and a demonstration of why ICC is not appropriate for the current study, see the simulated data in the Supplementary material (Supplementary Figure 7).

Although obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was screened for by questionnaire, several cases were likely undetected [45]. Notwithstanding, OSA patients have been shown to have high stability in SWA and theta power [46]. Additionally, in older adults at risk of OSA, SWA remained significantly associated with cognition [47]. In sum, the possible impact of undiagnosed OSA on the current results is likely minimal.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that older adults can independently use the Dreem headband to reliably collect multi-night in-home PSG sleep data. While night-to-night variability was observed in stage N3 sleep percentage, measures such as stage N2 sleep percentage and NREM relative power from various frequency bands were stable. There was a strong association between SWA and cognitive performance. Our findings demonstrate the usefulness of using wearable EEG headband to advance our understanding of sleep physiology in older persons and its relation to cognition.
