Optoacoustic brain imaging: A systematic review and field mapping☆
Authors: Yikai Wang, Amir Khansari, Qingxiang Ding, Jun Wang, Zhanshan Wang, Kamran Avanaki, Daniel Razansky, Zhenyue Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptoacoustic (OA) imaging has emerged as a powerful hybrid modality for investigating brain structure and function by combining optical absorption contrast with ultrasonic detection. This approach enables high-resolution imaging beyond the optical diffusion limit while preserving the strong molecular sensitivity of optical methods, thereby providing a unique platform for mapping cerebral vasculature, hemodynamics, oxygen metabolism, and disease-specific labels across spatial scales ranging from microvascular networks to whole-brain volumes. Here we present a systematic review on OA brain imaging, including both technological advances and biomedical applications while also providing a quantitative overview of the evolving research landscape. Specifically, we map the development of the field by analyzing worldwide research activity, including the geographical distribution of contributing institutions, yearly publication trends. This analysis provides a comprehensive perspective on the state of the art and emerging research directions. We then summarize recent advances in OA microscopy and tomography dedicated to brain imaging and discuss emerging multimodal platforms integrating fluorescence imaging, electrophysiology, ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging to enable multiparametric interrogation of brain dynamics. Applications in animal models of major neurological disorders are reviewed, highlighting functional and molecular biomarkers of disease progression and therapy. Finally, we discuss key challenges facing the field, including spectral quantification, transcranial imaging, system standardization, and clinical translation. Overall, this review serves as a comprehensive reference for capturing the multi-scale trajectory of OA neuroimaging.Keywords: Brain, Neuroimaging, Optoacoustic imaging, Photoacoustic imaging, Neuroscience, Brain disease

Introduction

Brain activity unfolds across spatiotemporal scales ranging from local microcircuits to distributed large-scale networks [1]. A central goal in neuroscience and clinically oriented brain research is to capture these dynamics and relate them to anatomical structure, physiological function, molecular or metabolic processes. Achieving this objective requires imaging approaches capable of resolving anatomical, functional, and biochemical context across meaningful spatial extents while maintaining temporal resolution appropriate to neural and vascular dynamics [2], [3]. In practice, however, established neuroimaging modalities face fundamental trade-offs among imaging depth, spatial resolution, temporal resolution, field of view (FOV), and contrast specificity [2]. As a result, modern brain research relies on complementary techniques, each optimized for particular image contrast and measurement scale while being constrained by practical considerations including safety, cost, and operational complexity (Table 1) [3], [4].

These trade-offs are especially pronounced when it comes to imaging the brain using optical and acoustic methods. The skull in particular restricts optical penetration and focusing and distorts acoustic propagation, thus imposing practical limitations on non-invasive transcranial imaging [1], [3]. Intravital optical microscopy techniques, such as confocal and two-photon imaging, provide cellular-scale resolution for in vivo studies but are generally limited to highly invasive cranial window preparations, sub-millimeter depths, and restricted spatial coverage in mammalian brains. Photon scattering rapidly degrades image quality with increasing depth, limiting applications that require large-scale coverage or quantitative measurements in deeper tissue [5]. Clinical neuroimaging modalities provide complementary capabilities but incur other types of limitations. MRI and CT offer whole-brain anatomical coverage yet have limited sensitivity to cellular and molecular events. PET and SPECT provide high detection sensitivity but operate at lower spatial resolution and rely on radioactive tracers with finite observation windows [5]. Additional constraints include ionizing radiation exposure in X-ray–based imaging, radioactive tracer requirements in nuclear imaging, and substantial infrastructure costs for MRI-based techniques [4]. Consequently, many neuroscience and clinical questions demand imaging approaches capable of combining deep tissue access, high spatiotemporal resolution, and molecular sensitivity in ways that conventional modalities are unable to achieve simultaneously [2], [3], [4].

Hybrid imaging strategies have therefore emerged as an important direction for overcoming these limitations. Among them, optoacoustic (OA) imaging, also known as photoacoustic imaging, has gained increasing attention as a modality that converts the absorbed optical energy into detectable ultrasound (US) waves - which exhibit substantially reduced scattering in biological tissues as compared to light, - thereby effectively combining the molecular sensitivity of optical methods with the high penetration and spatial resolution characteristic of US [3], [4], [5]. Hence, OA imaging occupies a unique niche within the neuroimaging landscape, bridging the gap between high-resolution optical microscopy and macroscopic observations with clinical imaging modalities.

OA brain imaging encompasses a range of spatial regimes defined by the trade-off between resolution and penetration depth. A useful way to conceptualize the emerging landscape of OA neuroimaging is through a scale continuum that spans the microscopic, mesoscopic, and macroscopic realms as well as potential clinical implementations (Fig. 1). At the microscopic end of this spectrum, optoacoustic/photoacoustic microscopy (OAM/PAM) enables high-resolution imaging of cortical microvasculature and oxygenation dynamics at the level of individual vessels [6], [10]. Spanning the gap between single vessels and whole-brain networks, mesoscopic OA imaging typically target millimeter penetration depths with tens-of-micrometers resolution. Depending on the custom detection configuration and imaging geometry, this mesoscopic regime can manifest either as wide field OAM or high-resolution, small-animal optoacoustic/photoacoustic tomography (OAT/PAT). At the macroscopic scale, full-scale OAT systems support imaging spanning from large-scale cortical network to the entire brain in rodents [8]. The emerging translational systems further aim to adapt OA imaging for human studies [9]. Viewed in this way, OA neuroimaging forms a continuous methodological bridge linking microscopic imaging at the capillary and cellular levels with macroscopic brain imaging, thus providing a scalable framework for studying neurovascular and metabolic processes across different biological scales (Table 2).

A major advantage of OA in brain research is its intrinsic sensitivity to hemoglobin absorption, enabling label-free visualization of vascular and oxygenation dynamics. Multispectral OA imaging exploits the distinct optical absorption spectra of oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) to separate these components and derive physiological parameters such as total hemoglobin (HbT), commonly used as a proxy for blood volume, as well as hemoglobin oxygen saturation (sO₂). Early feasibility studies demonstrated monitoring of cerebral oxygenation through extracerebral or superficial vascular signals [17], [18], [19]. Subsequent advances enabled transcranial imaging of HbT and sO₂ in small-animal models in vivo, including mapping of blood volume and oxygenation changes during controlled physiological perturbations such as hyperoxia and hypoxia [20], [21]. Volumetric multispectral OAT further enables cortex-wide mapping of HbO, HbR, and HbT dynamics during sensory stimulation in small-animal models [22], [23]. Ongoing technical developments focus on increasing acquisition speed and volumetric coverage, including high-speed three-dimensional (3D) OAT systems and compact illumination technologies based on high-repetition-rate laser diodes [24], [25], [26], [27].

Beyond endogenous contrast, OA also supports molecular imaging with exogenous agents [28], [29] with multimodal integration further expanding its capabilities. Taken together, OA brain imaging combines rich absorption-based optical contrast with acoustic detection, enabling scalable imaging from microvascular to whole-brain regimes while maintaining strong sensitivity to vascular and hemodynamic signals.

Motivation and significance of this review.

OA neuroimaging has progressed rapidly from early demonstrations of oxygenation-sensitive measurements to a diverse ecosystem of imaging platforms capable of structural, functional, and molecular brain imaging across multiple scales [1], [30], [31], [32]. However, rapid technological development has produced a heterogeneous literature encompassing diverse hardware architectures, imaging geometries, reconstruction algorithms, and analysis pipelines, highlighting the need for structured frameworks that improve comparability and reproducibility across studies [1], [30], [31].

One motivation of this review is to map out the technical landscape of OA neuroimaging across its multiple pathways, which, despite sharing a common physical foundation, address distinct spatial regimes. Microscopic systems enable high-resolution imaging of cortical microvasculature, whereas tomographic implementations support transcranial imaging of large brain volumes [20], [22], [24], [30], [32], [33]. A complementary trajectory involves multimodal integration, in which OA is combined with other imaging modalities to provide anatomical reference, validation, and multiparametric readouts [21], [23], [34], [35].

A second motivation arises from comprehensive presentation of the breadth of biological questions addressed with OA imaging. Multispectral measurements of hemoglobin components support functional paradigms related to neurovascular coupling, sensory stimulation, and pharmacological perturbations [17], [20], [22], [36], while other approaches monitor venous oxygenation signals derived from superficial vascular structures. Disease-focused applications extend these methods to tumor imaging, including spectroscopic characterization of glioblastoma, including molecular targeting and hypoxia-related functional assessments [37], [38], [39]. Emerging applications further expand the modality’s scope, including demonstrations of OA calcium imaging in controlled brain preparations [40], [41] as well as in zebrafish [16], [42] and mouse brains [43] in vivo. These developments position OA within a broader ecosystem of neuroscience tools aimed at linking vascular, metabolic, and molecular processes [1], [44].

A third motivation derives from brain-specific imaging constraints. Transcranial propagation introduces skull-related limitations on optical delivery and acoustic detection, degrading image quality and complicating quantitative interpretation. Early studies explored imaging through the infant skull as a model of transcranial imaging feasibility [45], whereas subsequent work has focused on mitigating skull-induced distortions and improving oxygenation quantification [9]. From a translational perspective, clinical adoption requires progress not only in imaging performance but also in system engineering, safety validation, and workflow integration, with compact light source technologies representing a promising pathway toward practical implementations in resource-limited clinical settings [26], [31], [46].

Against this backdrop, a systematic overview of the field is needed to clarify the technical landscape and identify key development priorities. This review therefore provides a structured synthesis of OA brain imaging across spatial scales and application domains. Specifically, we map the literature across time, geography, and disciplinary context; analyze the distribution of biological targets and experimental paradigms; examine the evolution of system architectures and contrast strategies from micro- to macro-scale imaging; and highlight recurring technical bottlenecks that shape future research and translational trajectories [1], [26], [31].

Methods

Literature retrieval and study screening

To construct a comprehensive and representative literature corpus, we performed systematic searches of the Web of Science Core Collection and PubMed databases (up to December, 2025). The search strategy was iteratively refined to ensure broad coverage of relevant literature while accounting for synonymous terminology. The final query was: ("optoacoustic" OR "photoacoustic") AND ("brain" OR "cerebral" OR "cortex" OR "cortical" OR "neural") AND ("functional" OR "hemodynamic" OR "oxygenation" OR "blood oxygen" OR "metabolic" OR "glucose" OR "neurovascular coupling" OR "structural imaging"). No restrictions were placed on publication year in order to capture the full developmental trajectory of the field. The search was limited to original research articles and yielded 546 records from Web of Science and 169 from PubMed. After removal of 134 duplicate entries, 581 unique records remained for subsequent abstract screening.

Prior to screening, predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria were established. Studies were included if they: (1) investigated animal or human brain systems; (2) employed OA imaging methodologies; and (3) reported technical development, experimental validation, or biological application. Studies were excluded if they focused on non-brain tissues, presented simulation-only results without experimental validation, or lacked sufficient methodological detail to confirm alignment with the review scope.

Screening was conducted in two sequential stages. In the first stage, two independent reviewers evaluated titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved through arbitration by a third senior reviewer. This process identified 319 potentially eligible studies for full-text evaluation. In the second stage, the same reviewers assessed the full texts to confirm compliance with all criteria, again resolving discrepancies through arbitration. After full-text review, 259 studies were retained for subsequent analysis and synthesis (Please refer to Supplementary Material 1). It should be noted that several review articles and recent publications in 2026 were cited to provide a comprehensive and up-to-date overview of the field. However, these publications were not included in the bibliometric analysis. The overall screening workflow is summarized in Fig. 2.

Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted to characterize the global development landscape of OA brain imaging research. Results

OA brain imaging methodologies

Following the scale continuum that spans microscopy, mesoscopy and macroscopy, this section reviews the methodological developments in OA brain imaging.

Optoacoustic microscopy (OAM)

OAM occupies a unique niche in brain imaging capable of resolving individual capillaries—the fundamental units of cerebral oxygen delivery—while maintaining penetration depths sufficient to interrogate cortical layers where neurovascular coupling and metabolic regulation occur [10], [47], [48], [49]. This capability is particularly important since many neurological disorders, including stroke, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and glioma, originate from microvascular dysfunction that precedes detectable macroscopic structural abnormalities [49], [50], [51].

Early development of OAM for neuroscience applications was constrained by several practical limitations, including insufficient imaging speed, restricted FOV, limited functional dimensionality, and physiological distortions introduced by anesthesia. Addressing these limitations has driven a series of technological advances that have collectively transformed OAM from a primarily structural microvascular imaging technique into a dynamic platform for investigating neurovascular function and metabolism.

Imaging speed represents a fundamental requirement for capturing neurovascular dynamics. Neurovascular coupling unfolds on millisecond timescales: the initial dip in oxygenation can occur within approximately 200 ms following stimulus onset, followed by hemodynamic responses evolving over the next hundreds of milliseconds to seconds [52]. Early OAM systems, however, were far too slow for such measurements, often requiring minutes to hours to acquire multi-wavelength or volumetric datasets [10]. To overcome this temporal mismatch, numerous engineering innovations have been introduced, including high-speed scanning mechanisms, resonant or voice-coil scanners, spiral scanning geometries, and high-repetition-rate laser sources [14], [53], [54], [55], [56], [57]. These developments increased A-line and B-scan acquisition rates by several orders of magnitude, enabling real-time imaging of stimulus-evoked hemodynamics, spreading depolarization, and pharmacologically induced vascular responses across cortical networks. Consequently, OAM evolved from a static structural imaging modality into dynamic readouts capable of resolving neurovascular events at capillary resolution. A more detailed review on fast OAM can be found in [58].

While improvements in imaging speed enabled dynamic measurements, limited spatial coverage remained a major constraint. Conventional OAM systems typically imaged only millimeter-scale regions, which was sufficient for localized microvascular studies but inadequate for investigating brain-wide pathological processes such as bilateral hemispheric interactions during stroke [59], [60]. Expanding the FOV therefore became essential for linking local microvascular alterations with global brain physiology. Innovations including arched scanning geometries, fiber-array illumination, and optical–acoustic combiners significantly expanded imaging coverage while preserving microvascular resolution [61], [62], [63]. These advances enabled centimeter-scale cortical imaging and longitudinal monitoring, facilitating direct comparisons between diseased and contralateral brain regions. Such internally controlled measurements are particularly valuable in neurological disease models, where subtle vascular alterations must be distinguished from normal physiological variability [49], [61].

Beyond spatial and temporal limitations, early OAM studies were largely restricted to structural vascular imaging, providing anatomical information but limited insight into brain metabolism or neuronal function [49], [64]. To address this limitation, multi-wavelength OA excitation was introduced, enabling quantitative measurement of physiological parameters such as sO₂ [48], [65], hemoglobin concentration [47], [51], cerebral blood flow [66], [67], oxygen extraction fraction [61], [68], [69], and cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen (CMRO₂) [61], [66], [68], [70]. This multiparametric capability substantially expanded the functional relevance of OAM. For example, OAM measurements demonstrated that anesthesia markedly suppresses cerebral metabolism [61], [68], resolved the long-debated initial dip in oxygenation at single-vessel resolution [48], [52], [71], and enabled absolute quantification of tumor metabolic heterogeneity that cannot be inferred from hemodynamic signals alone [70]. Additional advances—including improved fluence compensation [72], red-wavelength sensitivity [73], tissue-clearing strategies [73], and molecular probes [29], [74], [75]—have further extended OAM toward molecular and cellular investigations of brain physiology.

Another important challenge arises from the widespread use of anesthesia in small-animal imaging experiments. Although anesthesia simplifies experimental control, it profoundly alters cerebral blood flow, metabolism, and neurovascular coupling, potentially confounding interpretation of brain function [68]. Consequently, studying authentic brain physiology requires imaging in awake and behaving animals. This need has stimulated the development of miniaturized and wearable OAM systems [76]. Head-mounted probes weighing only a few grams, fiber-based heterodyne detection OA microscopes [77], [78] (Fig. 4a), and MEMS-scanner-based devices now enable single-vessel-resolution imaging in freely moving rodents [78], [79], [80], [81] (Fig. 4b), and head-mounted systems can integrate multiple imaging modalities to capture more detailed information [6], [82], [83], [84] (Fig. 4c). These systems have revealed significant physiological differences between anesthetized and awake states and allow simultaneous monitoring hemodynamics [77], [80], [85].

In parallel with the above-mentioned functional advances, improvements in image reconstruction and signal processing have further enhanced OAM performance. Early implementations suffered from limited axial resolution, restricted viewing angles, and poor spatial isotropy, which hindered accurate visualization of complex vascular networks. A range of optical, acoustic, and computational approaches have been developed to address these challenges, including axial resolution enhancement via Grueneisen relaxation effects [86] and virtual-point-based deconvolution [87], optimized detection geometries using aspheric acoustic mirrors to improve spatial isotropy [88], limited-view correction methods to recover vertical vascular structures [89], and deep-learning-based reconstruction algorithms for low-fluence denoising [90] and extended depth-of-focus [91]. These developments improve signal-to-noise ratio, spatial resolution, and depth-of-field, enabling more accurate visualization of penetrating vessels and cortical microvascular architecture.

Collectively, advances in imaging speed, spatial coverage, functional dimensionality, awake-animal imaging, and computational reconstruction have established OAM as a powerful platform for high-resolution structural, functional, and metabolic imaging of the brain. By enabling capillary-level investigation of neurovascular coupling and microvascular dysfunction, OAM provides an essential tool for understanding the vascular mechanisms underlying neurological disease.

Optoacoustic tomography (OAT)

OAT occupies a complementary position in brain imaging. While OAM resolves individual capillaries, it remains largely restricted to superficial cortical layers. In contrast, OAT trades spatial resolution for imaging depth and volumetric coverage. By combining ultrasonic detection with tomographic reconstruction, it enables imaging through the intact skull and across the entire brain volume in small animals, permitting visualization of deep structures, subcortical nuclei, and interactions between bilateral hemispheres [15], [25], [92], [93], [94], [95], [96], [97], [98]. OAT therefore forms an important methodological bridge between microscopic vascular imaging and whole-brain functional neuroimaging.

Early OAT systems relied on single-element detectors combined with mechanical scanning, resulting in volumetric acquisition times of hours and limiting their ability to capture dynamic physiology like seizure propagation. To boost the imaging speed, parallel data acquisition with array detection is a natural solution. The introduction of full-ring detector arrays eliminated mechanical rotation and reduced acquisition times to seconds [99], [100], [101]. Subsequent developments—including sparse spherical arrays [102] and panoramic detection architectures [15], [103]—further increased volumetric imaging rates to tens or even hundreds of frames per second, enabling real-time monitoring of cardiac-cycle–resolved hemodynamics and stimulus-driven brain activity [43], [104], [105], [106]. Together, these advances transformed OAT from a static volumetric technique into a dynamic platform capable of resolving rapid brain-wide physiological processes.

Beyond structural imaging, the spectroscopic capability of OAT provides a powerful route to functional and molecular neuroimaging. Because OA signals depend on wavelength-dependent optical absorption, multi-wavelength excitation enables label-free quantification of physiological parameters such as cerebral blood volume and sO₂ through the intact skull [20], [38], [97], [107], [108]. Multidimensional OAT—integrating spatial, temporal, and spectral dimensions—has further enabled real-time whole-brain mapping of resting-state functional connectivity [94], [109], stroke progression [110], [111], [112], [113], epileptic seizure propagation [93], [114], [115], and spreading depolarization waves [116], [117]. In parallel, OAT has been widely used to capture stimulus-evoked hemodynamic responses [22], [92], [116], [118], [119], [120], [121] and region-specific vascular responses [22], [122], [123], [124], [125].

At the same time, molecular imaging strategies are expanding the functional scope of OAT. Genetically encoded calcium indicators allow direct visualization of neural activity [41], [42], while targeted molecular agents permit simultaneous assessment of tumor hypoxia and receptor expression [38], [126], [127]. These multiparametric capabilities have been applied across diverse neurological models, including stroke [110], [112], traumatic brain injury [128], [129], brain tumors [38], [126], [130], neonatal brain injury [131], [132], [133], and epilepsy [93], [115].

High-quality transcranial imaging introduces additional challenges because the skull distorts acoustic wave propagation. Acoustic attenuation reduces signal strength, while phase aberrations degrade spatial resolution and quantitative accuracy. Early studies showed that imaging through the intact skull could reduce spatial resolution by approximately twofold, highlighting the need for improved hardware and reconstruction strategies [134]. Progress has since been made on both fronts. Advances in acoustic detector technologies have contributed to improved performance. Capacitive micromachined ultrasonic transducer (CMUT) arrays offer broader detection bandwidth [135], optical micro-ring resonators enable highly sensitive acoustic detection with resolutions approaching ∼100 μm [136], and fiber-based detection arrays support dual-band operation to balance penetration depth and spatial resolution [137]. In parallel, super-resolution OA approaches have been proposed to overcome the acoustic diffraction limit. Localization optoacoustic tomography (LOT), employing microdroplets or other contrast agents, has achieved capillary-scale resolution below 20 μm, enabling microangiographic visualization and quantitative oxygenation mapping beyond conventional limits [138], [139], [140], [141]. Model-based reconstruction methods incorporating detector geometry and acoustic propagation physics substantially improve image fidelity [142], [143], while signal processing approaches such as adaptive apodization and deep-learning-based reconstruction enhance visualization of deep structures and improve SNR [144], [145], [146].

More recently, wearable OAT implementations have extended functional neuroimaging capabilities to freely behaving animals, allowing hemodynamic dynamics to be measured without anesthesia-related confounds. A lightweight wearable OAT system (∼8 g) was developed for imaging cerebral hemodynamics in awake, freely moving rats, revealing significant differences in seizure-induced responses compared with anesthetized animals [125]. A following wearable OAT system with a three-layer transducer array achieved 6 Hz volumetric imaging and enabled the first visualization of visually evoked hemodynamic responses in behaving rats [147]. The subsequent scanning design further achieved ∼200 μm in-plane resolution and ∼5 mm imaging depth, allowing noninvasive functional brain imaging in freely moving rodents [148].

In brief, the evolution of OAT—from mechanically scanned single-element systems to array-based real-time volumetric imaging, from anatomical visualization to multi-wavelength spectroscopic mapping of hemodynamics and molecular targets, and from skull-limited acquisition to hardware- and reconstruction-enabled transcranial imaging with wearable extensions for behaving animals—has transformed it into a powerful modality for whole-brain investigation. From its origins as a relatively slow anatomical imaging technique, OAT has evolved into a real-time, volumetric, and multiparametric neuroimaging platform capable of probing hemodynamics, metabolism, and molecular processes across the entire brain, thereby strengthening its potential as a bridge between preclinical neuroscience and future clinical translation.

Multimodal imaging

No single imaging modality can fully capture the complexity of brain function. Optical microscopy resolves neuronal architecture with high spatial precision but remains limited to superficial depths [11], [149]. MRI provides whole-brain anatomical coverage yet lacks sufficient temporal resolution to resolve fast neurovascular dynamics [150]. US imaging quantifies cerebral blood flow but cannot directly measure oxygenation. OA imaging—despite its unique capability to map hemoglobin dynamics with high spatiotemporal resolution—does not directly record neuronal firing or provide the structural context required for comprehensive interpretation of functional signals. These complementary limitations have driven increasing interest in multimodal integration, in which OA imaging is combined with other modalities to simultaneously capture neural activity, hemodynamics, metabolism, and tissue structure (Table 4) [44], [151].

Hybrid platforms combining micro-electrocorticography arrays with functional OAM enable simultaneous recording of somatosensory evoked potentials and hemodynamic responses in models of transient ischemia, revealing rapid neuronal recovery despite incomplete restoration of vascular perfusion [60]. Simultaneous OAT and electroencephalography (EEG) demonstrated that hemodynamic alterations may precede seizure-related neural spikes, providing new insight into the temporal relationship between neuronal activity and vascular responses [115]. Wearable hybrid OA–EEG systems extend these measurements to freely moving animals and have shown that hemodynamic changes can precede seizure-related neural spikes, providing new insights into the temporal dynamics of epilepsy [80], [155].

US imaging provides high-resolution anatomical structure and quantitative blood flow thus completing the hemodynamic picture. Combining US with OAT therefore enables simultaneous mapping of cerebral blood flow, flow direction, and oxygenation through the intact skull. Various integrated systems have been developed to achieve this [113], [156], [159], [160], [161], [162]. Although each platform differs in its specific implementation (e.g., focusing strategy, number of wavelengths, or imaging mode), they all share the same core goal: co‑mapping of hemodynamics and oxygen saturation. More recently, a more highly integrated optoacoustic, US, and angiographic tomography platform has extended these capabilities to whole‑brain stroke imaging (Fig. 5d) [156]. Recently, US localization microscopy (ULM) has greatly advanced deep brain microvascular imaging, hence its combination with OAT and LOT may provide complementary strengths [163]. This may find valuable applications in cerebrovascular disease applications such as ischemic stroke research where both flow disruption and oxygen deficits reveal tissue viability.

Integration of OAT with MRI can provide important mechanistic insights. Functional MRI relies on the blood-oxygenation-level-dependent (BOLD) signal, which reflects a complex mixture of blood flow, blood volume, and oxygen metabolism that cannot be disentangled by MRI alone. Hybrid MRI–OAT systems address this limitation by enabling simultaneous acquisition of BOLD signals and quantitative maps of HbO and HbR [21], [34], [35]. These measurements have revealed, for example, that negative BOLD responses during transcranial US stimulation can arise from increases in HbT accompanied by preferential HbR elevation [120]. Another study revealed that BOLD signals correlate more strongly with HbT than with HbR, suggesting a revised interpretation of BOLD contrast mechanisms [35]. Furthermore, sensory stimulation experiments demonstrate that OA derived hemodynamic parameters can provide higher sensitivity and specificity than standalone BOLD measurements (Fig. 5c) [164].

Beyond instrumentation, multimodal strategies increasingly rely on tailored contrast agents capable of reporting multiple biological processes. Examples include genetically encoded near-infrared calcium indicators for deep OA calcium imaging [165], rare-earth-doped nanoparticles enabling dual-modality imaging [29], amyloid-targeting croconium dyes validated by positron emission tomography [166], and multifunctional theranostic nanoparticles designed for integrated imaging and therapy in models of traumatic brain injury, ischemic stroke, and nerve regeneration [129], [167], [168]. Additional efforts have focused on developing HaloTag-based probes with improved turn-on ratios for calcium sensing [129], bioreducible hypoxia-responsive agents for simultaneous mapping of intravascular and tissue oxygenation [74], and blood–brain barrier-penetrable probes for dynamic imaging of oxidative stress [169]. pH-responsive nanoplatforms by combining magnetic resonance and OA contrast further expanded the molecular toolkit for assessment of acute ischemic stroke [168]. Comprehensive reviews on multimodal OA imaging and contrast materials can be found in [44].

Building on these advances, an emerging frontier is the integration of multimodal OAT into closed-loop neuromodulation and real-time monitoring systems, where sensing and intervention are dynamically coupled [120], [121]. Here, blood oxygenation, flow, and calcium dynamics—are directly linked to neuronal activity. When integrated with neuromodulation modalities (e.g., US stimulation, optogenetics, or electrical stimulation), these platforms can enable feedback-controlled operation, in which stimulation parameters are continuously adjusted based on real-time OA signals. This approach has recently been employed in multimodal imaging studies looking into vascular and functional brain responses to transcranial pulse stimulation in AD models [119]. Such closed-loop paradigm is particularly promising for applications in epilepsy, stroke recovery, and neurodegenerative diseases, where adaptive, patient-specific modulation strategies are required. Overall, multimodal OAT-based feedback systems represent a critical step toward intelligent neuroimaging–intervention frameworks, in which diagnosis, monitoring, and therapy are unified within a single, dynamically responsive platform.

Collectively, these developments demonstrate that multimodal integration is not merely additive, but transformative. By combining complementary contrasts across optical, acoustic, and electromagnetic domains, multimodal OA platforms enable simultaneous interrogation of neuronal activity, vascular physiology, and molecular processes, bringing the field closer to a comprehensive understanding of brain function in health and disease. For a more in-depth discussion of multimodal optoacoustic methodologies and their expanding roles in neuroscience, readers are referred to Ref [44], which systematically reviews recent technical advances and representative applications.

3.2 Applications of OA brain imaging

OA-derived imaging features and biomarkers

The versatility of OA brain imaging lies in its ability to extract a broad range of structural, biochemical, and functional biomarkers across multiple spatial and physiological scales. By exploiting the strong optical absorption of endogenous chromophores—primarily hemoglobin—and the specificity of targeted exogenous probes, OA imaging provides a multiparametric view of brain physiology that bridges microscopic vascular architecture and large-scale network dynamics.

Vascular and structural features.

One of the most fundamental capabilities of OA imaging is the visualization of cerebral vasculature using intrinsic optical absorption contrast dominated by hemoglobin. In this regime, OA signal intensity primarily reflects local hemoglobin concentration, enabling label-free mapping of vascular anatomy throughout the brain. Early demonstrations established the feasibility of 3D transcranial imaging of the mouse brain, resolving major anatomical structures such as the cerebellum, hippocampus, and ventricles [97]. Compared with purely optical techniques, OA imaging maintains spatial resolution beyond the optical transport mean free path (∼1 mm), enabling high-resolution imaging in regimes where optical scattering limits conventional microscopy [10].

Recent technological advances have further expanded structural vascular imaging capabilities. High-speed 3D OAT systems provide volumetric imaging with scalable fields of view and near-isotropic spatial resolution [24]. At smaller spatial scales, ultrafast widefield functional OAM enables mapping of cortical microvascular networks across large regions while preserving high spatial resolution. These systems allow quantitative characterization of vascular morphology, including vessel diameter measurements and stimulus-induced vasodilation in cortical vessels within the ∼80–200 μm range [99]. Super-resolution approaches are beginning to extend these capabilities beyond conventional acoustic diffraction limits. Super-resolution imaging techniques such as ULM and LOT enable visualization of microvascular structures deep within the brain at resolutions below 40 μm [170], [171]. These approaches provide increasingly detailed maps of the cerebral vascular network, forming the structural foundation for subsequent physiological measurements.

Spectral and biochemical features.

Beyond vascular morphology, OA imaging enables quantitative assessment of biochemical parameters related to blood oxygen transport and metabolism. Because hemoglobin exhibits wavelength-dependent absorption spectra, multispectral OA imaging can separate HbO and HbR signals, allowing calculation of HbT concentration and sO₂ [17], [20]. These parameters serve as proxies for cerebral blood volume and oxygenation state, respectively.

Several studies have demonstrated accurate in vivo monitoring of vascular oxygenation using multispectral OA systems. For example, triple-wavelength configurations have been used to measure sO₂ in large vessels with strong agreement to invasive CO-oximeter measurements [172]. At the microscopic level, the development of OA flowoxigraphy enables quantification of sO₂ gradients and oxygen release rates from individual red blood cells in flow, providing unprecedented insight into microvascular oxygen transport [173]. OA imaging can also estimate metabolic parameters such as CMRO₂ by combining hemodynamic measurements with physiological modeling [174]. High-speed imaging platforms further allow dynamic mapping of oxygen delivery across complex vascular networks in real time [7].

The biochemical sensing capability of OA imaging can be further expanded through molecular probes. For example, croconium-dye–based contrast agents have been engineered to selectively bind cerebrovascular amyloid deposits, enabling high-resolution detection of amyloid pathology while remaining compatible with complementary PET validation [166]. Such probes illustrate how OA imaging can extend beyond hemodynamics to probe disease-specific molecular processes.

It should be noted, however, that the accuracy of these quantitative parameters—including sO₂, HbT, and CMRO₂—can be affected by fluence heterogeneity, skull-induced acoustic distortion, spectral coloring, and reconstruction assumptions. Addressing these limitations represents an important direction for future work.

Dynamic and functional features.

When measured dynamically, hemodynamic biomarkers such as HbT and sO₂ become time-resolved indicators of neurovascular activity and metabolic demand. Functional OA imaging exploits this capability to monitor spontaneous and stimulus-evoked hemodynamic fluctuations associated with neuronal activity.

One widely studied phenomenon is resting-state functional connectivity, in which spontaneous low-frequency hemodynamic oscillations exhibit correlated patterns across functionally related brain regions. OA imaging has revealed coherent activity across cortical areas including motor, somatosensory, and visual networks [94]. Hybrid imaging platforms combining OAT with MRI enable cross-validation of these functional maps by co-registering OA-derived vascular and oxygenation signals with structural and BOLD MRI datasets [21].

A major advance in functional OA imaging has been the ability to directly visualize neuronal activity using genetically encoded calcium indicators. Volumetric OA neuro-tomography systems can capture calcium dynamics at volumetric frame rates approaching 100 Hz, enabling real-time monitoring of stimulus-evoked neuronal activity across the zebrafish and ex vivo mouse brain [16], [41]. These systems have demonstrated whole-brain calcium imaging in scattering tissues, revealing spatiotemporal patterns of neural activation. However, OA calcium imaging remains challenged by strong hemoglobin background, depth-dependent fluence variations, and low probe sensitivity or SNR [43]. To mature as a reliable neuroscience tool, this emerging approach requires rigorous cross-validation against established gold standards like fluorescence imaging or electrophysiology.

The high temporal resolution of OA imaging also allows monitoring of rapid physiological perturbations, including carotid artery occlusion, pharmacological modulation, and hypoxic or hyperoxic challenges [49], [108], [128]. Sensory stimulation paradigms such as whisker activation in rodents further demonstrate the ability of OA imaging to link microvascular hemodynamics, oxygenation dynamics, and metabolic parameters such as CMRO₂ [7], [174].

Applications in brain diseases and neuroscience

The ability of OA imaging to simultaneously quantify oxygenation, blood volume, and vascular dynamics across multiple spatial scales makes it particularly valuable for studying neurological diseases. Unlike purely anatomical modalities, OA imaging provides functional measurements that directly reflect disease-related physiological alterations, including pathological angiogenesis, ischemic hypoxia, and disrupted neurovascular coupling.

Brain tumors.

Malignant gliomas are characterized by abnormal angiogenesis that produces structurally disorganized and functionally inefficient vascular networks. Conventional imaging methods provide limited information about these vascular abnormalities, particularly with respect to oxygenation and metabolic heterogeneity. OA imaging addresses this limitation by enabling simultaneous visualization of vascular architecture and oxygenation gradients. Early studies demonstrated transcranial imaging of gliomas in mice, revealing chaotic vascular organization and pronounced intratumoral hypoxia [10], [175]. Multispectral OA imaging further enabled longitudinal monitoring of tumor growth and oxygenation dynamics in orthotopic glioblastoma models [37], [176]. Molecular imaging approaches expanded these capabilities by targeting tumor-associated biomarkers. Integrin-targeted probes have linked receptor expression with hypoxic tumor niches [38], while glucose analogues allow simultaneous assessment of metabolic activity and vascular function [177]. Intrinsic melanin contrast has also been used to visualize melanoma brain metastases without exogenous labeling [175].

In addition to tumor detection, OA imaging has been applied to evaluate therapeutic responses. For example, vascular disrupting agents produce characteristic hemodynamic changes that can be monitored using OA imaging (Fig. 6a) [176], [178], and microvascular imaging has revealed tumor-induced disruption of neurovascular coupling in surrounding brain tissue at single-vessel resolution [179].

Cerebrovascular diseases.

Cerebrovascular disorders represent another major application area. In ischemic stroke, determining the viability of hypoperfused tissue—the ischemic penumbra—is critical for guiding therapeutic intervention. Because OA imaging can simultaneously map blood volume and sO₂, it provides a direct assessment of the balance between oxygen supply and metabolic demand.

Early demonstrations showed that OA imaging could visualize cerebral ischemia through the intact skull. Subsequent studies monitored vessel-specific oxygenation changes during arterial occlusion and collateral circulation recruitment (Fig. 6d) [180], [181]. 3D OA imaging further enabled spatiotemporal mapping of penumbral evolution and tissue viability [110], [182]. These measurements provide a quantitative framework for evaluating therapeutic interventions such as thrombolytic therapy and neuroprotective treatments [181], [183], [184]. OA imaging has also been used to investigate cerebrovascular dysfunction associated with comorbidities such as metabolic disease [185]. In mouse models, transfontanelle OA imaging allows monitoring of stroke and hemorrhage in the developing brain (Fig. 6b) [170], [186], [187].

Neurodegenerative disorders.

Growing evidence suggests that vascular dysfunction and protein aggregation contribute to neurodegenerative diseases such as AD and Parkinson’s disease (PD). In this context, OA brain imaging has been used to assess cortical hypoperfusion, oxygen-metabolic impairment and neurovascular coupling deficits in AD models [188], while multispectral and molecular OA/OAT strategies have further enabled visualization of amyloid-β deposits, tau-targeted probe uptake, and related disease-specific pathology [5], [158], [166], [189]. Beyond AD, multimodal OA-compatible imaging has also been applied to PD models to probe alpha-synuclein-associated pathology and iron deposition [190]. Recent TPS-related studies further illustrate how OA and complementary neuroimaging readouts can be used to monitor cerebrovascular, neuronal and network-level responses in neurodegenerative-disease-relevant intervention settings [119], [191].

Traumatic brain injury, epilepsy, and neuroinflammation.

OA imaging is also well suited for studying rapidly evolving secondary injury processes that follow traumatic brain injury (TBI), seizures, and inflammatory events. These processes involve complex interactions between vascular, metabolic, and neuronal dynamics that are difficult to capture using slower imaging modalities. In TBI models, OA imaging has revealed impaired cerebrovascular reactivity despite apparently normal baseline hemodynamics [192]. Early hypoxic events detected by OA imaging correlate with long-term neurological deficits [154], [193]. High-resolution imaging has also visualized spreading depolarization waves that propagate across cortical layers following injury [194].

In epilepsy research, combined OA imaging and EEG recordings have captured hemodynamic changes preceding seizure-related neural spikes, providing insight into seizure initiation mechanisms (Fig. 6c) [80], [85], [115], [155], [195], [196]. OA imaging has also been used to detect hippocampal neuronal activity transcranially [33] and visualize inflammatory processes using targeted contrast agents [182], [197].

Fundamental neuroscience.

Beyond disease studies, OA imaging has significantly contributed to fundamental neuroscience research. Its high spatial and temporal resolution allows investigation of neurovascular coupling across scales—from single vessels to distributed cortical networks [6], [12], [47], [65], [69], [82], [83], [196], [198]. Recent developments in miniaturized imaging systems enable studies in awake, freely moving animals, eliminating anesthesia-related confounds [77], [78], [79], [80], [81], [155], [199].

OA imaging has also been applied to investigate pharmacological modulation of cerebral microcirculation [199], [200], [201], exercise-induced vascular adaptations, and metabolic regulation [202], [203], [204]. These studies demonstrate how OA imaging can reveal physiological mechanisms underlying brain function and behavior.

Clinical translation of OA brain imaging

Despite the extensive progress in preclinical neuroscience described in this Review, translation of OA brain imaging to clinical settings remains at an early stage. The fundamental advantages of OA imaging—high optical absorption contrast, ultrasonic detection enabling deeper penetration, and multiparametric functional readouts—suggest strong potential for human neuroimaging. However, several technical and physiological challenges must be addressed before routine clinical deployment becomes feasible.

One of the most significant barriers is the human skull, which is substantially thicker and more heterogeneous than that of small animal models. The skull strongly attenuates both optical excitation and acoustic signals while introducing complex acoustic aberrations [205]. These factors reduce signal amplitude and distort image reconstruction, limiting the spatial resolution and quantitative accuracy of transcranial measurements [45]. While some feasibility studies have demonstrated that OA signals can be detected through the thinner portions of the temporal bone in an adult human skull [36], [206], clinical translation efforts have mostly focused on anatomical windows where the skull barrier is reduced. One promising application is neonatal brain imaging, where the fontanelle provides a natural acoustic and optical window. Through this window, OA imaging can visualize cerebral vasculature and measure oxygenation in the neonatal brain with high sensitivity [207], [208], [209], [210]. These capabilities may prove particularly valuable for monitoring neonatal stroke, hypoxic–ischemic injury, and intracranial hemorrhage, conditions in which rapid physiological assessment is essential.

Another translational pathway involves intraoperative imaging. During neurosurgical procedures, the skull is temporarily removed, eliminating the major barrier to OA signal propagation. In this context, OA imaging can provide real-time mapping of vascular structure and oxygenation during tumor resection or vascular surgery. Such information may help surgeons identify tumor margins, assess tissue viability, and avoid damage to critical vascular structures [9].

Hybrid imaging systems may further facilitate clinical translation. Integration with US imaging provides complementary structural and hemodynamic information while leveraging hardware already widely used in clinical practice. Similarly, hybrid OA–MRI systems allow cross-validation of physiological measurements and may accelerate adoption by linking OA-derived parameters with established clinical imaging biomarkers [35], [164].

Advances in contrast agent development will also play an important role in clinical translation. Although endogenous hemoglobin contrast enables label-free imaging, molecular probes targeting disease-specific biomarkers could significantly expand the diagnostic capabilities of OA imaging. However, clinical adoption requires probes with strong optical absorption in the near-infrared window, favorable safety profile, and regulatory approval for human use. Continued development of biocompatible probes—including nanoparticles and small-molecule dyes—may therefore be essential for expanding OA-based molecular neuroimaging [28], [29].

Finally, progress in laser technology and detector arrays is making clinical systems increasingly practical. Modern laser sources with higher repetition rate enable real-time imaging while remaining within safe exposure limits [26]. At the same time, improvements in US detector sensitivity and array geometry are enhancing signal detection through scattering tissue [211]. Together, these developments are gradually closing the gap between experimental systems and clinically deployable devices. Although significant challenges remain, these early studies demonstrate that the translation of OA brain imaging from animal models to human applications is both technically feasible and scientifically promising.

Discussion and conclusion

While OA imaging has rapidly evolved into a powerful neuroimaging modality, several key challenges must be addressed to fully realize its potential for neuroscience research and clinical translation. These challenges span instrumentation, signal quantification, multimodal integration, and biological interpretation.

Challenges and future directions

Quantitative accuracy and spectral unmixing.

One central challenge is achieving accurate quantitative measurements of chromophore concentrations. Multispectral OA imaging relies on spectral unmixing algorithms to separate signals from HbO, HbR, and other absorbers. However, optical fluence within biological tissue is highly heterogeneous due to scattering and absorption, leading to wavelength-dependent variations that can bias spectral measurements. These effects complicate quantitative estimation of physiological parameters such as sO₂. Several approaches have been proposed to address this issue, including model-based reconstruction incorporating light transport models, fluence correction algorithms, and machine-learning–based spectral unmixing methods [91], [142], [145]. Continued progress in quantitative reconstruction will be critical for ensuring that OA-derived physiological parameters are both reliable and comparable across experiments.

Imaging depth and transcranial signal distortion.

Another major limitation arises from acoustic and optical attenuation through the skull and brain tissue. While OAT has been shown to operate at depth of several centimeters within living tissues, the signals are rapidly attenuated with depth. In addition, the skull introduces acoustic scattering and phase distortions, both degrading the imaging resolution. Future improvements may emerge from several directions. Advanced detector technologies—such as CMUT arrays, optical micro-ring resonators, and fiber-based detectors—offer increased sensitivity and broader bandwidth [63], [135], [136], [211]. Adaptive reconstruction algorithms capable of compensating for skull-induced aberrations may further improve image fidelity. In parallel, optimized illumination strategies and wavelength selection could enhance signal generation in deeper brain structures.

Spatiotemporal resolution trade-offs.

OA imaging systems must balance spatial resolution, imaging depth, and temporal resolution, which are often constrained by fundamental physical trade-offs. High-resolution imaging typically requires high-frequency US detection, but these frequencies attenuate more strongly in tissue, limiting penetration depth. Conversely, deeper imaging often requires lower acoustic frequencies that reduce spatial resolution.

Recent developments aim to mitigate these trade-offs through hybrid detection schemes and multi-band acquisition strategies that simultaneously capture signals across different frequency ranges. High-speed volumetric imaging platforms are also enabling dynamic imaging of large brain volumes without sacrificing temporal resolution [7], [53], [102], [170]. Continued innovation in detector design and reconstruction algorithms will be essential for optimizing these performance parameters.

Multimodal integration.

Given the established clinical use of MRI for brain imaging, a promising direction for OA neuroimaging is the integration of multimodal approaches. For example, OA has demonstrated its ability to fill key gaps in MRI-based clinical assessments [35]. Other paths forward may link OA imaging with fluorescence microscopy, electrophysiology, or US, which provide richer datasets that link neuronal activity with vascular and metabolic responses [196], [212], [213].

Computational enhancement.

At the same time, machine- and deep-learning approaches are emerging as powerful tools for optimized system design, image reconstruction, noise reduction, spectral unmixing, feature extraction, and accelerated image analysis and interpretation [214], [215]. These methods facilitate the management of increasing complex OA datasets, particularly those involving volumetric, multispectral, and high-speed acquisition, which require advanced computational tools for data processing and interpretation. These techniques may ultimately facilitate real-time analysis of brain activity and disease-related biomarkers.

Expanding molecular imaging capabilities.

Another promising direction involves expanding the range of molecular contrast mechanisms accessible to OA imaging. While hemoglobin provides strong endogenous contrast, imaging additional biological processes requires specialized probes. Recent advances—including genetically encoded reporters, enzyme-activated probes, and targeted nanoparticles with favorable photostability and biocompatible profiles designed to report molecular events with high specificity—will enable OA imaging to probe cellular and molecular processes beyond hemodynamics [28], [115], [166], [169]. In neuroscience, such probes could enable imaging of neurotransmitter release, inflammatory signaling, or metabolic pathways, providing deeper insight into the biological mechanisms underlying brain function and disease.

Standardization and reproducibility.

As OA brain imaging continues to expand across laboratories and application domains, standardization and reproducibility have emerged as important priorities for the field. Differences in laser parameters, detector bandwidth, system geometry, wavelength selection, reconstruction algorithms, and spectral unmixing approaches can lead to substantial variability in measured OA signals and subsequently derived physiological parameters. Such variability complicates quantitative comparisons between studies and may hinder the translation of experimental findings into clinically meaningful biomarkers. Establishing standardized acquisition protocols, calibration procedures, and reconstruction pipelines will therefore be essential for ensuring reproducibility across platforms and institutions. Recent community-driven efforts have begun addressing these issues through the development of reference phantoms, benchmarking datasets, and consensus guidelines for multispectral imaging and quantitative parameter estimation [144], [145], [146]. In parallel, open-source software frameworks, shared data repositories, and the increasing availability of curated datasets accompanying journal publications are facilitating cross-validation of algorithms and promoting transparent reporting of experimental parameters. Continued progress in these directions will be critical for enabling reliable comparison of OA measurements across studies and supporting the broader adoption of OA imaging in neuroscience and the clinics.

Limitations of this review

While this review has sought to provide a comprehensive descriptive overview of OA neuroimaging and is based on a systematic search that identified and included 259 articles, the findings are synthesized and presented in a primarily narrative format. This structure required judgement in how specific themes and examples were organized. Moreover, the broad range of results did not enable performance of a formal quantitative meta-analysis. In addition, the review does not describe specific hardware designs or implementations in detail, leaving interested readers to consult the cited literature for technical specification.

Summary

OA imaging has emerged over the past two decades as a powerful modality for investigating brain structure, physiology, and function. By combining optical absorption contrast with ultrasonic detection, OA imaging bridges the gap in penetration and spatial resolution between optical microscopy and US imaging. This unique capability enables label-free visualization of vascular networks, quantitative measurement of blood oxygenation, and dynamic monitoring of neurovascular responses across multiple spatial and temporal scales.

Advances in laser technology, US detectors, high-speed data acquisition, and image reconstruction have transformed OA imaging into a versatile platform capable of capturing rapid physiological processes throughout the brain. Integration with complementary modalities—including fluorescence microscopy, electrophysiology, MRI, and US—has further expanded the scope of OA imaging, enabling simultaneous interrogation of neuronal activity, vascular dynamics, and metabolic processes. These capabilities have already yielded important insights into fundamental neuroscience and neurological diseases, including stroke, brain tumors, traumatic brain injury, epilepsy, and neurodegenerative disorders. At the same time, emerging molecular probes and multimodal imaging strategies continue to broaden the range of biological questions that can be addressed with OA imaging.

Despite outstanding challenges—particularly those related to quantification accuracy, transcranial imaging, and clinical translation—ongoing technological and methodological advances are rapidly expanding the capabilities of the field. As these developments continue, OA imaging is poised to play an increasingly important role in bridging preclinical neuroscience with clinical investigations, providing a comprehensive view of brain physiology that integrates structural, functional, and molecular information.
