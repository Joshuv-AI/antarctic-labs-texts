A multigenerational fabry disease case caused by a de novo GLA mutation: Diagnostic delay, phenotypic variability, and the impact of early detection
Authors: Senay Topsakal, Işık Tekin, Selma Tekin, Hüseyin Kaya, Vefa Çakmak, Hüseyin Onay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFabry disease (FD) is a rare X-linked lysosomal storage disorder characterized by α-galactosidase A deficiency, marked phenotypic heterogeneity, and frequent diagnostic delay. We report a multigenerational family with Fabry disease caused by a de novo GLA (α-galactosidase A gene) mutation in a female patient, affecting two adult sons and an infant granddaughter. The index patient, a 25-year-old male, experienced neuropathic pain, recurrent unexplained fever, gastrointestinal symptoms, anhidrosis, and cerebrovascular events for more than a decade prior to diagnosis. Cardiac hypertrophy, cornea verticillata, and ischemic cerebral lesions were detected. His mother showed neurological and cardiac involvement and was confirmed to carry a de novo GLA mutation. The younger brother presented a milder phenotype without cardiac or ocular involvement. Early cascade genetic screening enabled diagnosis of an affected infant at 6 months of age. Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) was initiated in all adult patients, leading to symptomatic improvement, although cerebrovascular events persisted in the index case. This case series highlights the pronounced intrafamilial phenotypic variability of Fabry disease and underscores the need to consider FD even in the absence of a positive family history. Early genetic screening, including prenatal and early postnatal testing, is critical to reduce diagnostic delay and optimize long-term clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Fabry disease (FD) is a progressive X-linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by α-galactosidase A deficiency, leading to the accumulation of globotriaosylceramide and related glycosphingolipids in multiple organs. Although classically described as an early-onset multisystem disorder in males, FD demonstrates marked phenotypic variability, particularly in heterozygous females, frequently resulting in delayed diagnosis and misclassification as rheumatologic, gastrointestinal, or inflammatory diseases.[12345678]

De novo GLA (α-galactosidase A gene) mutations constitute a minority of FD cases but pose a distinct diagnostic challenge due to the absence of family history. Here, we report a multigenerational family affected by FD caused by a de novo GLA mutation, illustrating diagnostic delay, pronounced intrafamilial phenotypic variability, and the critical value of early genetic screening.[12345678]

Such early recognition has the potential to prevent irreversible organ damage, improve long-term patient outcomes, and mitigate the profound consequences of diagnostic delay across generations.

Case Presentations

Index case

A 25-year-old male presented with an 11-year history of episodic burning pain and paresthesia in the hands and feet, accompanied by recurrent febrile episodes, fatigue, abdominal pain, and anhidrosis. He had multiple emergency department admissions and had previously been misdiagnosed with acute rheumatic fever, Raynaud’s phenomenon, and Crohn’s disease. Fabry disease was suspected based on neuropathic pain, autonomic dysfunction, and gastrointestinal involvement, and was confirmed by markedly reduced α-galactosidase A activity and genetic testing. Cardiac MRI revealed biatrial dilatation and concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, while ophthalmologic examination showed cornea verticillata. Cranial MRI demonstrated ischemic lesions consistent with cerebrovascular involvement, and anticoagulation therapy was initiated [Figure 2, upper row]. Despite enzyme replacement therapy, the patient experienced two cerebrovascular events; however, neuropathic pain and febrile episodes decreased substantially after 1 year of treatment.

Maternal case (de novo mutation carrier)

Following the diagnosis of the index case, his mother underwent clinical and genetic evaluation and was diagnosed with Fabry disease. A de novo GLA mutation was confirmed, as no pathogenic variants were detected in her parents. Her medical history was notable for long-standing anhidrosis, fatigue, neuropathic pain, and headaches. Cranial MRI revealed Chiari type I malformation previously treated surgically [Figure 2, middle row]. Cardiac MRI showed mild concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, and ophthalmologic examination demonstrated cataract formation [Figure 2, lower row].

Younger brother

Family screening identified Fabry disease in the index patient’s younger brother, who reported fatigue, episodic pain, and anhidrosis without evidence of cardiac or ocular involvement. His phenotype was notably milder than that of the index case.

Infant case

Owing to awareness of the family history, the younger brother’s daughter underwent early genetic testing and was diagnosed with Fabry disease at 6 months of age. She was born at term with prolonged neonatal jaundice and experienced recurrent infections during infancy requiring hospitalization; she remains under close multidisciplinary follow-up.

Therapeutic intervention and follow-up

Enzyme replacement therapy was initiated in all three adult patients, resulting in improvement in neuropathic pain and reduction in febrile episodes. The index patient continues anticoagulation due to cerebrovascular involvement. The pediatric patient remains under surveillance, with treatment guided by disease progression. Discussion

This case series highlights several major challenges in FD. Diagnostic delay remains substantial, particularly in patients presenting with nonspecific symptoms such as recurrent fever, abdominal pain, and neuropathic complaints. The presence of a de novo GLA mutation emphasizes that FD should be considered even in the absence of a positive family history. Marked intrafamilial phenotypic variability was observed, ranging from severe multisystem involvement in the index case to milder disease in his brother and variable manifestations in the female carrier. The early diagnosis of the infant case underscores the clinical value of proactive genetic screening in affected families.

The diagnosis of FD remains difficult due to its clinical heterogeneity and rarity, with delayed recognition leading to unnecessary interventions and preventable complications.[25101112131415] Neuropathic pain is the earliest and most frequent symptom, typically emerging in childhood, and manifests as episodic Fabry crises or chronic paresthesia. These attacks, often accompanied by fever and elevated inflammatory markers, are frequently misdiagnosed as rheumatologic disease, profoundly impairing quality of life, and potentially leading to irreversible complications despite enzyme replacement therapy.[123456789101112131415161718] In our series, prolonged painful crises and fatigue remained unrecognized for years; the index case was initially misdiagnosed with acute rheumatic fever and Raynaud’s phenomenon, and his brother with ankylosing spondylitis.

Fever represents a key but underrecognized feature of FD, contributing to diagnostic delay and triggering painful crises, particularly in patients with classic phenotypes. It is hypothesized to result from impaired thermoregulation due to Gb3 accumulation in sweat glands and cholinergic neurons.[1131415161718] Both male patients experienced recurrent high fevers from early adolescence, often requiring repeated hospitalizations and prolonged antibiotic use.

This study reinforces the importance of genetic counseling and systematic screening of at-risk relatives, including prenatal and neonatal evaluation, and emphasizes the need for heightened clinical awareness. Early initiation of disease-specific therapy combined with multidisciplinary follow-up is critical to limit organ damage and improve quality of life. Strengths include prospective follow-up of multiple affected family members and documentation of a de novo mutation, while limitations are the small sample size and limited long-term outcome data, particularly for the infant case.

Conclusion

This case series highlights the marked clinical heterogeneity and diagnostic challenges of Fabry disease, underscoring the importance of early recognition, particularly in familial settings. Recurrent neuropathic pain, unexplained fever, gastrointestinal symptoms, and subtle cardiac or neurologic findings should prompt consideration of FD, even in atypical presentations. Male patients tend to exhibit more severe disease, while de novo mutations in female carriers may remain unrecognized for decades. Early genetic screening and timely enzyme replacement therapy can alleviate symptoms, limit irreversible organ damage, and improve long-term outcomes. Heightened clinical awareness and multidisciplinary evaluation are essential to reduce diagnostic delay and optimize patient care.
