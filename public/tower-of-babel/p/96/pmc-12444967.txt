Bidirectional relationship between autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat” among Chinese youth
Authors: Huanhua Lu, Jinli Wang, Feng Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAmid China’s competitive social environment, young people increasingly perceive “involution” as undermining autonomy, with some asserting that “lying flat” serves as an autonomy-restoring strategy. Here, we attempted to explore whether unmet autonomy needs drive “lying flat” or if “lying flat” genuinely fulfils individuals’ autonomy need through empirical research.MethodWe combined a large-sample questionnaire survey (Study 1) and a longitudinal follow-up study (Study 2) to investigate the relationship between individuals’ autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat”.ResultsIn Study 1, we found that there was a significant negative correlation between individuals’ autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat”. More importantly, the cross-lagged analysis in Study 2 found that individuals’ autonomy need satisfaction negatively predicted “lying flat” 1 month later, and “lying flat” also negatively predicted individuals’ autonomy need satisfaction 1 month later.ConclusionsThese findings revealed a bidirectional negative prediction relationship between individuals’ autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat”, which challenge the folk viewpoint that “lying flat” satisfies autonomy needs.

Introduction

In 2021, a post titled “Lying flat is justice” made “lying flat” a buzzword in China. The author described himself as having not worked for 2 years, claiming that “only by lying flat, man is the measure of all things” (Ou, 2023; Xiong, 2022). Since then, “lying flat” has come to describe a passive, low-desire lifestyle characterized by disengagement from intense social competition (Y. Chen & Cao, 2021; Deng, 2022; Hu & Wang, 2023; Lin & Gao, 2021; Ling & Wang, 2023; C. Ma & Wang, 2022; Qin & Dai, 2022; Wei & Wang, 2023). For some young people, “lying flat” has not only become a way of expressing momentary resistance or flirtation with social norms but has even developed into a part of their life beliefs. Notably, similar low-desire or passive coping patterns are not unique to Chinese youth but can be found in other countries and cultural contexts as well. For example, in some European countries, there is a growing group of young people known as “NEETs” (Not in Education, Employment, or Training), who are not engaged in education, work, or vocational training and are often regarded as actively or passively withdrawing from mainstream social competition due to structural exclusion and employment insecurity (Eurofound, 2012). Likewise, in Japan, scholars have described the “Satori Generation” as young people who show little interest in consumerism or traditional upward mobility and instead pursue lifestyles marked by low desire and minimalism (Dai, 2022). These phenomena indicate that, in the context of globalization, facing economic pressures, social stratification, and the decline of traditional success narratives, young people in different societies may adopt various forms of disengagement from mainstream social expectations.

Some young people have declared on the Internet (e.g., Baidu Post Bar, Weibo, Douban, Douyin, Bilibili) that “lying flat reflects the subjectivity of human beings”, and this idea has attracted considerable attention and resonance among many youth groups. Many of these young people argue that the highly competitive social environment marked by intense “involution” limits their sense of autonomy and that “lying flat” represents a way to resist this pressure and regain personal freedom (Luo, 2021; Z. Ma, 2021; Sun et al., 2021; Yang, 2021). That is to say, they believe that the lack of autonomy brought about by the pressure of “involution” has led them to choose “lying flat”, and “lying flat” can enable them to satisfy their need for autonomy. However, whether “lying flat” actually contributes to the satisfaction of individuals’ autonomy needs remains theoretically and empirically unclear. Existing discussions often treat “lying flat” as an expression of agency and freedom, but self-determination theory suggests that passive withdrawal and avoidance may not truly foster a sustained sense of autonomy (Deci & Ryan, 2000). Given the increasing popularity of “lying flat” among young people, it is necessary to examine the actual relationship between “lying flat” and autonomy need satisfaction through empirical research. Doing so can provide a more nuanced understanding of whether “lying flat” genuinely helps young people meet their needs for autonomy or whether it might have unintended negative consequences in the long term.

The impact of youth’s autonomy need satisfaction on “lying flat”

Autonomy refers to an individual’s need for self-determination, self-control, and self-expression (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2008; Deci et al., 2001). As a higher-order need in Maslow’s hierarchy, autonomy becomes more prominent when basic material needs are met (Kenrick et al., 2010; Maslow, 1943). Post-materialist theory further suggests that as societies develop economically, people increasingly value freedom and self-expression over material gains (R. Inglehart, 1997; R. F. Inglehart, 2008; Ronald & Norris, 2016; Welzel & Inglehart, 2010). For contemporary youth growing up in relatively affluent conditions, autonomy has thus become a core psychological need.

However, the satisfaction of individual autonomy need is shaped by cultural and social contexts. Research shows that supportive cultural norms, flexible environments, and positive family support promote autonomy satisfaction (Assor et al., 2004; Chirkov et al., 2003; Grolnick et al., 1997; Grolnick & Ryan 1989; Ryan & Deci 2000; Sheldon & Niemiec 2006; Soenens & Vansteenkiste 2010). In China’s “tight” culture, strict social norms maintain order but often constrain individual expression (Gelfand et al., 2006, 2011). Externally, the widespread phenomenon of “involution” has narrowed the upward mobility path for some young people, making them unable to pursue careers aligned with their interests (Cai, 2014; Cao & Chen, 2023; Hua, 2011; N. Liu, 2018; T. Liu, 2024). Within families, high parental expectations and an emphasis on conformity may further limit young people’s choices (R. K. Chao, 1994; R. Chao & Tseng, 2002; X. Chen et al., 2015; Fong, 2004; Wu & Chao, 2005). Together, these cultural and structural constraints have made it difficult for many youth to satisfy their autonomy needs.

According to self-determination theory, unmet autonomy needs weaken intrinsic motivation and may lead to avoidance and withdrawal behaviours (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2008; Vansteenkiste & Ryan, 2013). Against intense “involution” pressure, “lying flat” has emerged as a passive coping strategy, reflecting some young people’s withdrawal from relentless competition as a way to reclaim a sense of control and autonomy. By opting out of excessive striving, they symbolically resist the pressures imposed by rigid social norms and limited mobility pathways. In this sense, it is reasonable to assume that the more unsatisfied the youth’s need for autonomy is, the more likely they are to choose “lying flat” as an alternative response to an overly pressurized reality.

The impact of “lying flat” on the satisfaction of youth’s autonomy need

Against the backdrop of the “involution”, some young people are trying to choose to “lie flat” in order to demonstrate their autonomy; however, can “lying flat” really satisfy young people’s need for autonomy? “Lying flat”, as a lifestyle strategy that deliberately abandons mainstream social competition and actively lowers material and achievement expectations, is often not based on a highly autonomous, value-consistent choice, but rather represents a helpless retreat in response to external pressures (Ou, 2023; Xiong, 2022; Yang, 2021). Previous studies have indicated that when individuals experience feelings of helplessness, lack of control, or frustration with structural barriers in society, they are more likely to adopt passive coping strategies to alleviate psychological burdens (Seligman, 1975). In this case, “lying flat” is more accurately viewed as a form of passive resistance against external discipline and excessive expectations, rather than a positive manifestation of self-determination and volitional choice. According to the self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000), the core of autonomous needs lies in individuals taking meaningful actions based on their own will, rather than simply passively evading. Existing research has also shown that remaining in a state of low motivation and avoidance-oriented behaviour over time can undermine individuals’ sense of internal control over their actions, which is significantly negatively related to autonomy need satisfaction (Vansteenkiste et al., 2005; Weinstein et al., 2012). For example, studies have found that passive behavioural choices tend to diminish individuals’ perceived agency over their own behaviour and are associated with lower levels of autonomy satisfaction (Weinstein et al., 2012). Therefore, in the context of a high-pressure society, while “lying flat” may superficially appear to be an active rejection of external discipline, its underlying passivity and avoidance are unlikely to truly support an enduring sense of autonomy and may instead hinder the sustained satisfaction of autonomy needs. Besides, from a Marxist perspective, human independence and freedom can only be realized through objective activity and practical engagement with the world (Bottomore & Rubel, 1957; Marx, 1845; Marx & Engels, 1975; Sayers, 2011). In other words, the need for autonomy can only be fulfilled through meaningful practice and active participation in social life. However, young people who choose to “lie flat” often have no clear goals, are unwilling to strive or exert effort, and deliberately detach themselves from social practice, which is far removed from the Marxist view on autonomy. Based on the reasons mentioned above, it is reasonable to assume that the more “lying flat” young people are, the more difficult it is for them to satisfy their need for autonomy.

Current study

To sum up, we believe that there is a bidirectional predictive relationship between the degree of satisfaction of contemporary youth’s autonomy need and “lying flat”. Under the pressure of “involution”, the more unsatisfied the youth’s autonomy need is, the easier it is for them to choose “lying flat”, but the more “lying flat” they are, the more difficult it is to satisfy their autonomy need. At present, most scholars mainly focus on discussing the influence of social and external environmental factors on the phenomenon of “lying flat”, and few studies have explored the reasons for the phenomenon of “lying flat” from the perspective of youth’s psychological needs; at the same time, current research mainly examines the influence of factors such as cultural background, external social environment, family support and social relationships on the satisfaction of individuals’ autonomy need, and no studies have explored the influence of individuals’ “lying flat” behaviour on the satisfaction of autonomy need. This study attempts to use empirical research to investigate the bidirectional relationship between the satisfaction of young people’s autonomy need and the degree of “lying flat”, in order to reveal the interactive relationship between individuals’ psychological need satisfaction and their behaviours. In Study 1, a large-sample questionnaire survey was used to investigate the association between the satisfaction of youth’s autonomous need and the degree of “lying flat”, and it was hypothesized that there was a significant negative correlation between these two variables. Study 2 combined longitudinal follow-up research and cross-lag analysis to explore the bidirectional predictive relationship between the satisfaction of youth’s autonomy need and the degree of “lying flat” over time, and we hypothesized that the satisfaction of youth’s autonomy need can negatively predict the degree of “lying flat”, and the degree of “lying flat” can also negatively predict youth’s autonomy need satisfaction. Different samples of participants were used in Study 1 and Study 2, respectively.

Study 1

The purpose of Study 1 was to investigate the association between the satisfaction of autonomy need and “lying flat” through a cross-sectional survey.

Method

Participants

A stratified random sampling method was used to select 1050 participants from both key universities (i.e., leading Chinese universities included in national initiatives such as “Double First-Class”, “Project 985”, or “Project 211”) and general colleges (i.e., ordinary higher education institutions not included in these projects) to participate in the questionnaire survey, excluding some data due to missing information, leaving a total of 1005 valid data, including 558 males and 447 females, 494 from the key university and 511 from the general college, with a mean age of 19.05 ± 0.84 (range 18–25). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of China University of Geosciences (Beijing) and was performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. All participants signed an informed consent form prior to the survey.

Measures

Lying flat: The “lying flat” was evaluated by the “lying flat” Tendency Scale (LFTS) (Lu et al., 2023), which was designed to measure the degree to which an individual “lies flat”. The scale comprised six items (e.g., I don’t make an effort at anything.) and was rated on a 4-point Likert scale. A higher total score means a higher degree of “lying flat”. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the LFTS in this study was 0.852.

Autonomy Need Satisfaction: The autonomy need satisfaction was assessed using the autonomy dimension of the General Need Satisfaction Scale (GNSS) (Gagné, 2003). It included five items (e.g., I feel that I am able to make autonomous decisions about how to live my life), three of which are reverse-worded. Each item was rated on a 7-point scale. Higher scores represent higher levels of autonomy need satisfaction for individuals. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the autonomy dimension in this study was 0.500. To further assess the reliability, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.415 to 0.611, with reverse-scored items showing relatively lower loadings. Based on these loadings, the composite reliability (CR) was 0.626, which is marginal but acceptable for a short scale with few items (Hair et al., 2019). The overall model fit indices indicated an acceptable fit to the data (e.g., χ2/df = 4.33, CFI = 0.944, TLI = 0.901, RMSEA = 0.087, SRMR = 0.016). Although Cronbach’s α values are relatively low, the acceptable CR and CFA fit indices indicate that the subscale demonstrates acceptable reliability and validity (Bentler, 2009; Raykov, 1997).

Beyond the assessment of “lying flat” and autonomy need satisfaction, the study also incorporated measures of demographic factors such as gender, age, subjective socioeconomic status (SSES), and educational background (key university/general college).

Statistical analysis

Utilizing SPSS 26.0, correlation analysis and hierarchical multiple regression were conducted to investigate the association between the satisfaction of autonomy need and “lying flat”.

Results

A Pearson correlation analysis indicated a significant negative association between the satisfaction of autonomy need and “lying flat” (r = −0.361, p < 0.001). The above results suggested that there may be a bidirectional predictive relationship between the satisfaction of autonomy need and “lying flat”. In Study 2, we used a longitudinal follow-up study to explore the relationship.

Study 2

Based on the initial exploration in Study 1, in Study 2, we used a longitudinal follow-up study to investigate the bidirectional predictive relationship between autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat”.

Participants

One hundred and twenty participants volunteered to participate in our longitudinal follow-up study. After excluding 11 participants who dropped out in the middle, we retained 109 valid responses, comprising 65 males and 44 females with a mean age of 19.09 ± 0.8 (range 18–22). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of China University of Geosciences (Beijing) and was performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. All participants signed an informed consent form prior to the study. The sample size in this study was determined using a priori power analysis with GLIMMPSE 3.0 (Kreidler et al., 2013). Given the characteristics of the cross-lagged panel model (CLPM), we adopted an effect size of f2 = 0.10 (Cohen, 1988) as a small-to-medium effect and set the autoregressive coefficients to 0.40 and 0.50 as conservative estimates (Adachi & Willoughby, 2015). The results indicated that, with α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.80, the required sample size was 92 participants for an autoregressive coefficient of 0.50 and 108 participants for an autoregressive coefficient of 0.40. In this study, we initially recruited 120 participants and obtained 109 valid cases, meeting the sample size requirement.

Procedures

A total of 120 participants were recruited from the university. We implemented a follow-up survey protocol, administering assessments at one-month intervals. Initially, a questionnaire was administered to 120 participants, and 1 month later, 109 of them participated in a second questionnaire measure. Both autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat” were measured in the questionnaire measurements at two time points.

Measures

“Lying flat”: the LFTS (Lu et al., 2023), the same as in Study 1, was used to assess the degree of “Lying flat”. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the LFTS in this study was 0.823 at T1, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.876 at T2.

Autonomy Need Satisfaction: the autonomy dimension of the GNSS (Gagné, 2003) the same as in Study 1 was used to assess autonomy need satisfaction (T1: Cronbach’s α = 0.551, CR = 0.648, the standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.425 to 0.620, χ2/df = 3.38, CFI = 0.971, TLI = 0.916, RMSEA = 0.08, SRMR = 0.017, the corrected item – total correlations above 0.5; T2: Cronbach’s α = 0.536, CR = 0.637, the standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.414 to 0.614, χ2/df = 3.55, CFI = 0.962, TLI = 0.903, RMSEA = 0.092, SRMR = 0.03, the corrected item – total correlations above 0.5). Although Cronbach’s α values are relatively low, the acceptable CR and CFA fit indices indicate that the subscale demonstrates acceptable reliability and validity (Bentler, 2009; Raykov, 1997).

In addition, this study also measured the participants’ basic information such as gender, age, and SSES.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS 26.0 for descriptive statistics, common method bias assessment, and correlation analysis. AMOS 22 was employed for the measurement equivalence test and cross-lagged analysis. A cross-lagged model (Figure 1) was constructed to test the bidirectional predictive relationship between autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat”, consisting of autoregressive paths (a and b) for each variable (autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat”), cross-lagged paths (c) from autonomy need satisfaction at the first time point (T1) to “lying flat” at the second time point (T2), and cross-lagged paths (d) from “lying flat” at T1 to autonomy need satisfaction at T2. Cross-lagged analysis of the autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat”

To examine the bidirectional predictive relationship between autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat”, a cross-lagged panel model (Figure 2) was tested using Amos 22. The model demonstrated an acceptable fit (χ2/df = 2.142, CFI = 0.989, TLI = 0.933, RMSEA = 0.103, SRMR = 0.0297). Autoregressive effects were significant for both autonomy need satisfaction (β = 0.261, p = 0.006, 95%CI [0.076, 0.446], Table 6) and “lying flat” (β = 0.599, p < .001, 95%CI [0.472, 0.726], Table 6), indicating the stability of the two variables over time. Cross-lagged paths revealed that autonomy need satisfaction at T1 significantly negatively predicted the degree of “lying flat” at T2 (β = −0.170, p = 0.026, 95%CI [−0.319, −0.021], Table 6), and the degree of “lying flat” at T1 also significantly negatively predicted autonomy need satisfaction at T2 (β = −0.193, p = 0.044, 95%CI [−0.380, −0.005], Table 6). After controlling for demographic variables (gender, age, and SSES), the bidirectional predictive relationship persisted, with autonomy need satisfaction at T1 remaining a significant predictor of the degree of “lying flat” at T2 (β = −0.165, p = 0.035, 95%CI [−0.319,-0.012]), and the degree of “lying flat” at T1 remaining a significant predictor of autonomy need satisfaction at T2 (β = −0.205, p = 0.035, 95%CI [−0.395, −0.014]). These findings suggested a bidirectional predictive relationship between autonomy need satisfaction and the degree of “lying flat”. Discussion

This study combined a cross-sectional study and a longitudinal follow-up study to investigate the bidirectional relationship between the satisfaction of youth’s autonomy need and the degree of “lying flat”. The cross-sectional study found that there is a significant negative correlation between the satisfaction of youth’s autonomy need and the degree of “lying flat”; the longitudinal follow-up study found that the satisfaction of youth’s autonomy need can negatively predict the degree of “lying flat” 1 month later; meanwhile, the degree of “lying flat” can also negatively predict the satisfaction of youth’s autonomy need 1 month later, i.e., the satisfaction of youth’s autonomy need and the degree of “lying flat” have a bidirectional temporal direction.

First, this study found that the satisfaction of youth’s autonomy need negatively predicted the degree of “lying flat” 1 month later, i.e., the more youth’s autonomy need was satisfied, the lower the degree of “lying flat”, and conversely, the less youth’s autonomy need was satisfied, the higher the degree of “lying flat”. Self-determination theory suggests that autonomy need is an important factor influencing individuals’ intrinsic motivation and that satisfaction of autonomy need enhances individuals’ intrinsic motivation, while frustration of autonomy need weakens individuals’ intrinsic motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2008; Deci et al., 2001; Ryan & Deci, 2000). A large number of previous studies have also shown that strategies such as autonomy support and providing choices can satisfy individuals’ need for autonomy and enhance intrinsic motivation (Banack et al., 2011; Bono & Judge, 2003; Chiniara & Bentein, 2016; Griffin, 2016; Meng & Ma, 2015), while when an individual perceives the work or task environment to be controlling, or perceives work pressure, the individual’s need for autonomy is thwarted and intrinsic motivation is weakened (Bartholomew et al., 2014; Gillet et al., 2015; Radel et al., 2013; Trépanier et al., 2016). As a form of avoidance behaviour, “lying flat” is mainly characterized by the weakening of intrinsic motivation, and the present study found that the satisfaction of youth’s autonomy need negatively predicted the degree of “lying flat”, which expanded previous research on the influence of autonomy need on intrinsic motivation. While most previous studies have analysed the causes of youth’s “lying flat” phenomenon from external factors such as social system and economic development, this study for the first time empirically proved that youth’s unsatisfied autonomy need was an important reason for “lying flat” phenomenon, which enriched previous studies and provided a clearer picture on the causes of youth’s “lying flat” phenomenon.

Our study also found that the degree of “lying flat” negatively predicted the satisfaction of youth’s autonomy need 1 month later. Previous studies have shown that having sufficient material resources (e.g., time, money, and information) enhanced individuals’ sense of autonomy (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2008; Dittmar et al., 2014; Kasser & Ahuvia, 2002; Kasser & Ryan, 1996); positive social identity helped to increase the satisfaction of individuals’ autonomy need (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Reeve et al., 2004; Ryan & Deci, 2000; Vallerand, 1997); and individuals with a high sense of self-efficacy are more confident in the face of challenges and are more likely to experience a sense of autonomy (Bandura, 1977; Deci & Ryan, 2000; Ryan & Deci, 2000; Vansteenkiste et al., 2004). As a kind of avoidance behaviour, “lying flat” is not conducive to the enhancement of youth self-efficacy, the acquisition of sufficient material resources and positive social identity, which in turn is not conducive to the satisfaction of youth autonomy need. Previous studies have mainly examined the effects of cultural background, external social environment, social support, and personal characteristics on the satisfaction of individuals’ autonomy needs (Assor et al., 2004; Chirkov et al., 2003; Grolnick & Ryan, 1989; Grolnick et al., 1997; Ryan & Deci, 2000; Sheldon & Niemiec, 2006; Soenens & Vansteenkiste, 2010), but this study first found that the degree to which individuals were “lying flat” could also affect the satisfaction of their autonomy needs, revealing that individuals’ behaviours constitute a significant predictor of their psychological needs’ satisfaction.

Implications

This study was the first to reveal a bidirectional predictive relationship between youth’s autonomy need satisfaction and the degree of “lying flat”, which has important theoretical and practical significance. Theoretically, this finding demonstrates that autonomy need satisfaction is not a static state but a dynamic, mutually reinforcing process with behaviour, which extends self-determination theory by showing how low-autonomy coping behaviours such as “lying flat” can further erode autonomy need satisfaction, creating a self-perpetuating loop. This highlights the importance of breaking this loop to prevent the persistence of “lying flat” behaviour.

Based on this theoretical insight, this study provides clear guidance for practical applications. For example, since higher autonomy need satisfaction predicts lower “lying flat” behaviour, interventions should aim to enhance youth’s experiences of choice, volition, and self-directed action. Schools and career counselling agencies can design learning tasks that give students meaningful decision-making power, so that daily participation directly builds autonomy satisfaction and reduces the tendency to “lying flat”. At the same time, because “lying flat” behaviour itself undermines autonomy need satisfaction, practical measures should also directly target passive withdrawal tendencies, helping youth replace avoidance with active goal setting and engagement. This can be achieved by mental health professionals who design programmes that strengthen self-efficacy and help young people experience mastery and control in small, incremental steps. What’s more, enterprise organizations should not only increase job autonomy in principle but actively create environments that interrupt the autonomy – “lying flat” cycle. For example, providing flexible work arrangements and employee voice mechanisms can prevent employees from resorting to withdrawal behaviours that undermine their own autonomy satisfaction. Finally, policymakers can promote labour policies that expand young people’s real options for meaningful, self-directed work, thereby addressing structural drivers that feed the negative loop between autonomy and “lying flat”.

Limitations and future research directions

First, the longitudinal design in this study used relatively short intervals and a modest sample size. Future studies should adopt longer-term longitudinal follow-ups with larger and more diverse samples and extend measurement intervals to further test the stability and robustness of the bidirectional relationship between autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat”. Second, the scale used to measure autonomy need satisfaction showed relatively low internal consistency in our sample, likely due to the small number of items, the use of reverse-worded items and cultural differences, as the scale was originally developed in Western contexts but used with Chinese participants. Future research may consider expanding the item pool or adapting the scale to the Chinese cultural context to further improve internal consistency. Third, because this study relied solely on self-report measures, future research could incorporate multiple assessment methods – such as behavioural observations or experimental manipulations – to reduce the potential bias inherent in self-report data and to provide stronger causal evidence. Finally, this study primarily focused on Chinese youth as participants. Since “lying flat” is a social mindset that also shares similarities with phenomena such as the “NEET” group in Europe and the “Satori Generation” in Japan, future studies should explicitly compare youth across different cultural contexts to examine whether the dynamic relationship between autonomy need satisfaction and “lying flat” is Chinese culturally specific or universal. Such cross-cultural comparisons can help clarify how social norms, cultural values, and institutional factors may shape young people’s attitudes towards autonomy and their responses to social pressure.

Conclusion

This study combined a cross-sectional study and a longitudinal follow-up study to find that youth’s autonomy need satisfaction can negatively predict the degree of “lying flat”, and the degree of “lying flat” can also negatively predict youth’s autonomy need satisfaction, revealing that there was a bidirectional negative predictive relationship between youth’s autonomy need satisfaction and the degree of “lying flat” over time. The results revealed the psychological motivation for the formation of “lying flat” and also suggested that there was an interaction between individuals’ psychological need satisfaction and behavioural choices. Meanwhile, it revealed that education, enterprise management, and social policy making should take targeted measures to enhance the satisfaction of youth’s autonomous need, thereby reducing “lying flat” phenomenon and promoting youth development and social progress.
