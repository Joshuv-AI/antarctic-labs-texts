Study on the anti-asthmatic effect of Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair and its mechanisms in regulating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway
Authors: Qianru Yang, Xiaoxue Chen, Jintong Cai, Zhengwen Xie, Yuhan Nie, Jinghui Sun, He Li, Chunmei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGinseng-Schisandra herb pair is a classic representative one of the “combination of tonification and astringency” in traditional Chinese medicine. Ginseng tonifies lung Qi and benefits heart Qi, while Schisandra restrains the lung, nourishes the kidney, calms the mind, and soothes the spirit. When they are used in combination, one can play a tonifying role and another can serve as an astringency, with tonification but without stagnation and with astringency but without depression, so the combination can not only enhance the efficacy of replenishing Qi and nourishing Yin but also strengthen the effect of astringing and consolidating. However, the underlying mechanism and action of its combined use in the prevention and treatment of asthma remain unclear.MethodsAn asthma model in mice was established by using ovalbumin (OVA) combined with aluminum hydroxide (Al(OH)3), so as to evaluate the anti-asthmatic effect of Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair and determine its optimal compatibility ratio. For further validation, an in vitro asthma model was developed by stimulating human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) with interleukin-4 (IL-4) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). The chemical profile of the herb pair extract was comprehensively analyzed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS). Potential bioactive components and anti-asthma mechanisms were predicted through network pharmacology analysis and subsequently verified by Western blot and immunofluorescence assays.ResultsGinseng-Schisandra herb pair could improve the behavior of asthmatic mice, alleviate the pathological damage of lung tissue, reduce the levels of inflammatory factors in the serum of asthmatic mice and the asthma model BEAS-2B cells, increase the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD), and decrease the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and reactive oxygen species (ROS). A total of 72 bioactive components were identified from the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract, and the herb pair could inhibit the expression of PI3K-AKT pathway-related proteins in the lung tissue of asthmatic mice and BEAS-2B cells.ConclusionGinseng-Schisandra herb pair can effectively alleviate the airway inflammatory response and oxidative stress injury by regulating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway in mice to exert an anti-asthmatic effect. Among different compatibility ratios and compared with single herbs, the 2:1 ratio of the herb pair exhibits the optimal therapeutic efficacy.Keywords: Ginseng, Schisandra, Herb pair, Asthma, PI3K-AKT signaling pathway

Introduction

Bronchial asthma (asthma) represents a chronic inflammatory disorder affecting the respiratory tract, characterized by the airway hyperresponsiveness, inflammation and cellular injury, overexpression of T helper 2 (Th2)-type cytokines, elevated immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels, and excessive mucus secretion [1]. According to statistics, more than 300 million individuals worldwide suffer from asthma, the morbidity in adults in China is 4.2%, and both the incidence and mortality rates are rising annually [2]. The development of asthma is linked to the excessive production of cytokines mediated by Th2 or Th1, including interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-5 (IL-5), among others. [3].At present, the medications employed for the prevention and management of asthma are primarily anti-inflammatory drugs, bronchodilators, and immunomodulators, etc. However, these available therapies fail to fully control asthma symptoms, so it is crucial to strive for the discovery of novel therapeutic agents. Asthma is called “Xiaobing” (asthmatic disease) in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). In TCM practice, it was Huangdi Neijing (Yellow Emperor's Internal Classic) that documented and recognize this disease first. In recent years, the potential of TCM in asthma treatment has become increasingly prominent, and its bioactive components, extracts, and complexes have been found to inhibit airway inflammation in asthma patients through multiple pathways, with multiple pharmacological effects [4].

Ginseng, the root of the Panax ginseng Meyer plant (Araliaceae family), is characterized by a sweet and slightly bitter taste, a warm nature, and affinity for the spleen, lung, and heart meridians. Renowned as the “King of Herbs”, modern studies have identified ginsenosides as its primary bioactive constituents, which exhibit multiple pharmacological effects including anti-inflammatory, anti-pulmonary fibrosis, and immunomodulatory activities, making it valuable in the management of respiratory diseases [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Schisandra, the dried ripe fruit of Schisandra chinensis (Turcz.) Baill. (Magnoliaceae family), is sour and sweet in taste, warm in nature, and acts on the lung, heart, and kidney meridians. Classified as a superior herb in Shennong's Classic of the Materia Medica, its main active constituents are lignans, which have shown to alleviate chronic airway inflammation, improve airway epithelial barrier function, and exert protective effects against chronic pulmonary diseases [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

The Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair is a classic compatibility in TCM clinical practice. Ginseng tonifies qi, strengthens the spleen, and replenishes lung fluid, while Schisandra astringes and consolidates qi, as well as replenishes fluid. Following the compatibility principle of “tonifying qi when deficient and consolidating qi when dispersed,” they jointly achieve the effects of fortifying the spleen, regulating qi, replenishing the lung, and astringing lung qi. The Ginseng-Schisandra Decoction, recorded in the Qing dynasty work “Youyoujicheng”, is indicated for chronic cough with spleen deficiency and weakened central qi. Clinical studies have demonstrated that this formula significantly improves symptoms and alleviates airway inflammation in pediatric patients with bronchial asthma of the lung-spleen qi deficiency pattern [13]. However, the material basis and molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-asthmatic action of this herb pair remain unclear. Systematic investigations are needed regarding the changes in active components after compatibility, as well as the major targets and signaling pathways involved. The present study was therefore designed to explore the material basis and mechanism of action of the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair in the treatment of asthma, so as to provide theoretical support for the development of novel preventive and therapeutic agents against asthma.

Materials and methods

Preparation of drugs

Ginseng and Schisandra were obtained from Jilin Boyang Pharmacy, and identified as authentic products by Professor Wang Wei (Department of Pharmacognosy, Beihua University), complying with the relevant provisions of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 Edition). An appropriate amount of Ginseng and Schisandra were weighed separately, crushed into powders, and sieved. Based on ancient herbal records and clinically commonly used doses, Ginseng and Schisandra was mixed in the ratios of 1:1 and 2:1. The mixture was immersed in 10 vol of distilled water for 1 h, followed by reflux extraction at 100°C for three consecutive cycles, with each extraction cycle lasting 1 h. The combined filtrates were concentrated in a round-bottom flask via rotary evaporation at 60 rpm and 60°C. The resulting concentrated solution was transferred to culture dishes, completely frozen at −20°C, subsequently lyophilized to obtain its powders (the yield were37.52% and 41.9%). The Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract was stored at 4°C for further use. The single-herb extracts of Ginseng and Schisandra were prepared in the same procedure, respectively (the yields were 27.56% and 39.89%). In this experiment, the ratio of the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair was based on ancient medical records and modern clinical medication standards: in classic prescriptions such as Treatise on Febrile Diseases Xiaoqinglong Decoction, Synopsis of the Golden Chamber Linggan Wuwei Jiangxin Decoction, Jingyue's Complete Works Renshen Wuweizi Decoction, and Annotated Effective Prescriptions for Women Tianwang Buxin Dan, Ginseng and Schisandra were mostly compatible at a ratio of 1:1 or 2:1 to achieve the effects of replenishing qi and astringing the lung; for modern medication, it refers to the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 Edition), where the commonly used clinical dose of ginseng is 3-9 g and that of Schisandra chinensis is 2-6 g, and the ratio of their commonly used doses is consistent with the experimental ratio.

Reagents

Dexamethasone Sodium Phosphate Injection (Dex, Quanyu Technology Animal Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China); Al(OH)3 (Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., 21645-51-2, Shanghai, China); Ovalbumin (OVA, A5503, Sigma Aldrich, USA); TNF-α and IL-4 Protein (rp152506, rp147593, Aladdin Scientific, Shanghai, China); Methylthiazolyldiphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) (Amresco0793, Amresco, USA); Penicillin-Streptomycin (Genview, Beijing, China); Fetal bovine serum (FBS, 11011-8611, Tianhang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China); Specific antibodies for PI3K, p-PI3K, p-AKT, mTOR, p-mTOR, and GAPDH (A4992, AP0854, AP1208,A2445, AP1413, A19056, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), AKT (A75065, Nature Biosciences, Wuhan, China).

Experimental animals

Female ICR mice aged 6-8 weeks, with a body weight of 20 ± 2 g [Changchun Yisi Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd, Animal License NO: SCXK (Ji) 2023-0005]. All mice were housed under standardized conditions for 1 week of acclimatization prior to experimentation, including a 12 h light-dark cycle, ad libitum access to chow and fresh water, ambient temperature controlled at23.0 ± 2°C, and humidity of 60.0 ± 10.0%. All animal procedures in this study were conducted in accordance with the approval of the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee at Beihua University. (Authorization number: No.203091303)

Establishment of asthma mouse model induced by OVA combined with Al(OH)3

The asthma mouse model was established as described in previous literature and our own published study [[14], [15], [16]]. In brief, mice were randomly divided into 9 groups (n = 12): control group (Control), asthma model group (Model), low-dose Ginseng-Schisandra extract (1:1) group [Gin-Sch (1:1)-L; 200 mg/kg], high-dose Ginseng-Schisandra extract (1:1) group [Gin-Sch (1:1)-H; 400 mg/kg], low-dose Ginseng-Schisandra extract (2:1) group [Gin-Sch (2:1)-L; 200 mg/kg], high-dose Ginseng-Schisandra extract (2:1) group [Gin-Sch (2:1)-H; 400 mg/kg], Ginseng extract group (Gin-H; 400 mg/kg), Schisandra extract group (Sch-H; 400 mg/kg), and positive drug group (Dexamethasone; 1 mg/kg). The choice of drug dosage was based on the clinical dosage of Ginseng and Schisandra in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 Edition), in combination with extract extraction rate, and determined according to the equivalent dosage conversion between mice and humans. On day 1 and 14 of the experiment, mice in the model group and each administration group received 0.2 mL sensitization solution intraperitoneally. [10 μg OVA + 2 mg Al(OH)3 dissolved in 0.2 mL normal saline] for sensitization, while those in the control group were given the same volume of normal saline intraperitoneally. From day 21 to 28, mice in the model group and each administration group were challenged with the inhalation of 2% OVA solution (200 mg OVA dissolved in 10 mL normal saline) for 40 min daily, and the control group received the same amount of normal saline. One hour before nebulization, mice in each administration group were intragastrically administered the corresponding drugs, while the control group and the model group received the same amount of normal saline. During the modeling period, body weights of mice were recorded every 2 days, and the behavioral status of mice was observed and documented during nebulization.

Collection of serum, BALF and cell counting

On day 29 of the experiment, mice were anesthetized with diethyl ether. Six mice from each group were subjected to blood collection via the inner canthus vein, followed by lung tissue harvesting. Transfer the collected blood into sterile EP tubes and centrifuged at 4500 r/min for 10 min at 4°C to separate the serum. The lung tissue was washed with pre-cooled normal saline to remove the blood on its surface, and the residual liquid on the surface was dried with a piece of absorbent paper. The wet weight was accurately weighed (to 0.001 g). The lung index was calculated as follows: lung index = Wet Lung Mass (g)/Mouse Body Weight (g) × 100%. Both the serum and lung tissue samples were aliquoted and stored at −80°C for subsequent experiments. The other mice in each group were immobilized by tying up their limbs on a mouse fixed wooden board. The skin and thoracic wall of mice were sequentially incised from the neck to the thoracic cavity to fully expose the trachea, and then carefully separated from the surrounding tissues. A gavage needle was inserted into the trachea, and the junction between the needle and the trachea was clamped with a hemostat to prevent leakage. A total of 1 mL of pre-cooled normal saline was perfused into the trachea, and then aspirated back, and this procedure was repeated 3 cycles to obtain bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). The BALF was centrifuged at 4°C for 10 min at 1500 r/min. The supernatant was collected and stored at −80°C for later use, and the precipitate was resuspended in 1 mL of normal saline. The precipitate suspension was gently pipetted to mix evenly for cell counting with a hemocytometer.

Histopathological analysis

The mouse lung tissues were fixed in formalin solution, dehydrated by gradient ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, the lung tissues were sectioned into thick slices, followed by dewaxing and hematoxylin-eosin (H&E, Sigma, USA) staining to evaluate the severity of lung tissue inflammation. A semi-quantitative scoring system (0-4) based on the extent and severity of inflammatory cell infiltration was adopted [17,18]. The mean score was calculated by evaluating three high-power fields per section. Additionally, the slices were subjected to periodic acid-Schiff (PAS, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for observing the mucogenesis in the lung tissues. The density of PAS-positive goblet cells was measured using Image-Pro Plus software with the area percentage method.

Cell cultures

BEAS-2B cells (Procell, Wuhan, China) were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. When the confluency of the cell monolayer reached approximately 90%, the cells were digested with trypsin for subsequent experimental procedures.

Cell viability assay

BEAS-2B cells were planted into 96-well plates at a concentration of 5 × 103 cells per well and placed in a CO2 incubator for routine culture. Following cell adhesion, different concentrations of Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract (ratios: 2:1, 1:0, 0:1) and IL-4/TNF-α were introduced into the assigned wells, followed by incubation for 24 or 48 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of MTT (5 mg/mL, protected from light) reagent was applied to every single well, and the cells were further incubated for 4 h. Then, 100 μL of DMSO was supplemented into every single well, and the plates were gently shaken to ensure complete dissolution of the formazan crystals. The optical density (OD value) at 490 nm was detected with a microplate reader. Cell viability was evaluated in accordance with the equation: Cell viability (%) = [(OD490 of experimental group - OD490 of blank group)/(OD490 of control group - OD490 of blank group)] × 100%.

The results showed that the cell viability remained above 80% at 24 h incubation and drug concentrations ≤80 μg/mL (indicating no significant cytotoxicity), so drug concentrations for subsequent experiments were determined as 20 μg/mL (low), 40 μg/mL (medium), and 80 μg/mL (high). Co-treatment with 20 ng/mL IL-4 and 10 ng/mL TNF-α reduced cell viability to approximately 50% (consistent with the degree of cell damage in the inflammatory model), and based on the literature report [19], this concentration combination was determined as the optimal modeling condition. Detailed experimental results are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Establishment of cellular inflammatory model and drug administration

Logarithmically proliferating cells were planted into both 96-well plates and 10 cm2 culture dishes, and then incubated under 37°C with 5% CO2 conditions until complete adherence. The cells were allocated into the following experimental groups: control group (Control, the cells were maintained in medium without any intervention), model group (Model, the cells were stimulated with 20 ng/mL IL-4 combined with 10 ng/mL TNF-α to induce an asthmatic inflammatory cell model), drug intervention groups [the cells were pretreated with Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract in a ratio of 2:1 at a low-, medium-, and high-doses (Gin-Sch-L, M, H: 20, 40, and 80 μg/mL, respectively), or with single extracts of Ginseng or Schisandra (Gin/Sch-L, M, H: 20, 40, and 80 μg/mL, respectively)]. Cells in all the administration groups were pretreated with the corresponding agents for 1 h before adding IL-4/TNF-α for inflammatory stimulation, and three replicate wells were designated per condition. After the 24-h incubation, the cell supernatants from 96-well plates and cells from culture dishes were collected for the detection of inflammatory factors and oxidation-related indicators.

Detection of inflammatory factors and oxidative stress-related indicators

The levels of inflammatory factors in BALF and cell culture supernatants were determined by ELISA, and the serum IgE and OVA-sIgE. Specific operations were carried out in strict accordance with ELISA kits (Langton Biotechnology Co., Shanghai, China). Furthermore, MDA and SOD were measured in mouse serum and BEAS-2B cells as directed by the respective test kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Cells were seeded on 6 well plates, and the methods of grouping and treatment were identical to those described in section 2.9. Intracellular levels of ROS were measured using a DCFH-DA fluorescence probe, and specific operations were carried out in strict compliance with the ROS detection kit (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

UPLC-MS analysis

Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract and individual herb extracts (Ginseng and Schisandra) were prepared as described in Section 2.1. After drying, equal amounts of each extract were weighed accurately, dissolved in an equal volume of distilled water, and filtered through a 0.45 μm micropore membrane to remove impurities. Chemical components in the test solutions were qualitatively analyzed by UPLC-MS. The chromatographic separation was conducted on an Ultimate 3000 UPLC system (Thermo, San Jose, CA, USA) equipped with a Supelco C18 column (3.0 × 50 mm, 2.7 μm; Sigma-Aldrich). The column temperature was maintained at 35°C, mobile phase A was acetonitrile, and mobile phase B was H2O containing 0.1% formic acid. The gradient elution protocol utilized for the chromatographic separation of experimental samples was set as detailed below: 60% B (0-5 min), 60-40% B (5-12 min), and 60% B (5 min). The sample injection volume was controlled at 5 μL, with a constant mobile phase flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The mass spectrometry detection was performed using a Q-Orbitrap-MS/MS spectrometer (Thermo, San Jose, CA, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, and the data were collected in positive ion mode. Key parameters of the ion source were configured as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 40 Arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 10 Arb; sweep gas flow rate, 1 Arb; capillary voltage, +4.0 kV; and capillary temperature, 350°C. Full-scan MS data were acquired over the range of m/z 150-2000 Da in centroid mode with a resolution of 70,000. The automatic gain control (AGC) target value was 1×10^6, and the maximum injection time (IT) was 100 ms. Data were collected in Full-MS/ddMS2 mode with a resolution of 17,500, the AGC target value was 1×10^5, the maximum IT was 50 ms, and the normalized collision energy (NCE) was set within the range of 25-45 eV. A database of chemical components in the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair was constructed by reviewing relevant literature and database information for further analyzing analyze the chemical constituents in the UPLC-MS.

Target prediction and mechanism exploration via UPLC-MS combined with network pharmacology

First, the names of chemical components in the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract identified by UPLC-MS were systematically collated. Subsequently, the component-related targets were screened in the Swiss Target Prediction database (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/) and PharmMapper database (http://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/index.html). Meanwhile, “asthma” was used as the search keyword to retrieve asthma-related targets from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM database (https://www.omim.org/), TTD database (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/), and DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com/). The collected herb pair component targets and asthma disease targets were imported into the Venny 2.1.0 online platform (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html), and a Venn diagram of intersecting genes was drawn through the platform computation, visually presenting the overlapping target information. The obtained intersecting targets were imported into the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/), with Homo sapiens set as the target species; a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was subsequently constructed using the default parameters. Cytoscape 3.9.1 software was applied to conduct visualization and topological analysis of the network diagram. Finally, Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were conducted on the intersecting targets, and a “drug-component-target” composite network diagram was constructed to illustrate the multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway regulatory mechanisms of the herb pair in asthma treatment.

Verification of the mechanism of Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract in preventing and treating asthma

The total proteins were extracted from the lung and BEAS-2B cells, and the BCA test kit (Dingguo Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Beijing, China) was used to quantify the protein concentration in order to ensure the standardization of sample loading. Total proteins were separated on 8% or 10% SDS-PAGE gels.The separated proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes, and then sealed at room temperature with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h to avoid nonspecific binding. Subsequently, the membranes were probed overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies, including PI3K (1:1000), p-PI3K (1:1000), AKT (1:1500), p-AKT (1:800), mTOR (1:2500), p-mTOR (1:800), and GAPDH (1:2000). After three washes, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000) at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, ECL chemiluminescent reagent was used to develop the target protein bands, and the band images were acquired using a chemiluminescence imaging system. The gray values of the bands were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software to calculate the relative expression levels of the target proteins. Meanwhile, immunofluorescence detection of cells was performed following the standard protocol provided in the kit instructions. The location and fluorescence intensity of the target proteins were observed by laser-scanning confocal microscopy.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation” (Mean ± SD), and the sample size of each group was indicated by “n". The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 software. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc tests was used for comparisons between groups, in which a P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair ameliorates behaviors in OVA-induced asthmatic mice

During the sensitization phase (days 1-14) of the OVA-induced asthmatic model mouse, no significant differences in behavior were detected among all groups. However, mice in the model group exhibited typical asthmatic symptoms shortly after OVA inhalation, such as tachypnea, ear and face scratching, forelimb retraction, coughing, and head nodding. In contrast, these abnormal behaviors were reduced to varying degrees in all drug-administered groups. No significant differences in body weight were found among groups throughout the experimental period, indicating that the drugs had no obvious impact on the basic physiological status of mice (Fig. 1B). Additionally, the lung index and total cell count in BALF were observed. The results showed that compared with those in the control group, the lung index and total leukocyte count in BALF of mice in the model group were significantly increased. After drug intervention, both the lung index and total cell count decreased to different extents. These findings suggest that the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract can alleviate symptoms and relieve inflammatory responses in OVA-induced asthmatic mice, with a superior effect to that of single-herb extracts at the same dose (Fig. 1C and D).

Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair attenuates the lung tissue pathological damage in OVA-induced asthmatic mice

Effects of the extracts on the lung tissue pathological morphology were observed by the H&E and PAS staining of lung tissue sections. As presented in Fig. 2, the lung tissue structure of mice in the control group remained intact, with no obvious inflammatory cell infiltration observed around the bronchi and blood vessels. In contrast, mice in the model group exhibited a significant airway damage, including thickened bronchial walls, extensive inflammatory cell infiltration around bronchi and blood vessels, a significant elevation in the number of goblet cells within the bronchial mucosa, and excessive mucus secretion. However, the inflammatory cell infiltration, goblet cell hyperplasia, and mucus secretion were allieviated in a dose-dependent manner in the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair-treated groups, especially a superior inhibitory effect to that of the other extract groups at the same dose was observed in the high-dose herb pair extract (ratio 2:1) group, indicating that the herb pair can alleviate the pathological damage in asthmatic mice.

Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair inhibits the inflammatory response and oxidative stress in OVA-induced asthmatic mice

To explore the anti-inflammatory effect of the herb pair, the levels of inflammatory factors (IL-4, IL-5) in BALF and immunoglobulins (IgE, OVA-sIgE) in serum were detected. The results demonstrated that, compared with the control group, the levels of IL-4 and IL-5 in BALF, as well as IgE and OVA-sIgE in the serum, were markedly elevated in the model group. Conversely, these indicators were significantly reduced in the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair treated groups, and a more prominent effect was observed in the high-dose herb pair extract (ratio 2:1) group (Fig. 3A–D). The antioxidant effect of the herb pair was evaluated by detecting oxidative stress-related indicators in serum. As shown in Fig. 3E–F, compared with that in the control group, the serum SOD level was notably reduced while the MDA level was markedly elevated in the model group. Nevertheless, relative to the model group, all treatment groups exhibited an increased SOD level and a decreased MDA level. Based on the in vivo experimental results, it could be seen that the therapeutic effect of the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract at the ratio of 2:1 was superior to that at 1:1, so Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract at 2:1 ratio was selected for subsequent experiments.

Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair attenuates inflammation and oxidative stress in IL-4/TNF-α-induced BEAS-2B cells

To further verify the effect of Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract (ratio 2:1) in preventing and treating asthma, an in vitro asthmatic model was established by inducing BEAS-2B cells with 20 ng/mL IL-4 with 10 ng/mL TNF-α. Firstly, the effects of the herb pair on the viability of the cell were investigated. Compared with the blank control group, cell viability in the model group was reduced by approximately 50%, whereas cell viability was elevated to different degrees in all treatment groups. These findings verified the successful establishment of the asthmatic cell model and indicating that the herb pair could alleviate IL-4/TNF-α-induced cellular damage (Fig. 4A–C). Then, the levels of inflammatory factors in the supernatants were measured. The results indicated that IL-4, IL-5, IL-1β in the supernatants of the model group were higher than that in the control group. In contrast, the levels of these inflammatory factors were decreased to varying degrees in all treatment groups relative to the model group (Fig. 4E–G), indicating that the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair extract could suppress the inflammatory response. To explore the antioxidant effect of the herb pair, the intracellular levels of MDA, SOD, and ROS were detected. As shown in Fig. 4D–H, and I, compared with the blank control group, the model group exhibited a significant increase in intracellular MDA content, a significant decrease in SOD activity, and a marked enhancement in ROS fluorescence intensity, indicating an increased ROS accumulation. After drug intervention, MDA content and SOD activity improved in a dose-dependent manner with increasing drug concentration, and the ROS fluorescence intensity was significantly weakened, further confirming that the herb pair can alleviate IL-4/TNF-α-induced cellular oxidative stress damage.

Identification of active components in Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair by UPLC-MS

To systematically clarify the material basis of the anti-asthmatic effect of Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair, UPLC-MS was used for the qualitative analysis of the herb pair extract (ratio 2:1) and extracts of its individual herbs (Ginseng and Schisandra). Based on the component information (retention time, characteristic ion peaks, etc) reported in published literature [[20], [21], [22]], types and main compositions of components in each sample were determined. The results showed (Fig. 5A–F and Supplementary Tables 1–3) that a total of 72 active components were identified in the herb pair. Different from the individual herbs, some new components were detected in the herb pair, including Phenylalanine, Chlorogenic acid, Ginsenoside F5, Ginsenoside Rb3, Ginsenoside Rs5, Gomisin L2, Gomisin M2, γ-tocopherol, Gomisin K1, etc., while some components present in the individual herbs, such as Tyrosine, Ginsenoside Rd2, Ginsenoside Re4, Ginsenoside Rb1, Ginsenoside R0, Gomisin H, and Schisantherin C were not detected in the herb pair, indicating that interactions between components occurred during the extraction of the two herbs.

Analysis on candidate antiasthmatic targets and pathways of Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair

Based on the active components identified by UPLC-MS, 1239 component-related targets and 2683 asthma-associated disease targets were predicted though the databases. A total of 399 potential candidate targets of the herb pair for asthma treatment were identified through the Venny 2.1.0 platform (Fig. 6A). A PPI network was constructed by importing the intersecting targets into STING database (Fig. 6B), and the top 10 core targets were identified by the topological analysis using Cytoscape 3.9.1 software (sorted by “degree value”), including AKT1, TNF, and IL-1β (Fig. 6F), indicating that these targets may play pivotal roles in anti-asthmatic effects of the herb pair. GO functional enrichment analysis (Fig. 6C) revealed that the targets were mainly enriched in biological processes (BP) such as inflammatory response, cell apoptosis, as well as molecular functions (MF) including ATP binding, enzyme binding, and protein binding. KEGG pathway enrichment results (Fig. 6D) showed the key pathways included the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, Th17 cell differentiation, and the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, of which the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway exhibited the highest enrichment, suggesting that the herb pair may exert an anti-asthmatic effect by regulating this pathway. A composite network was constructed using Cytoscape 3.9.1 (Fig. 6E). According to the degree value, “major active components” such as Gomisin G, Schisantherin B, Panaxynol, and Schisandrol A were screened out (Fig. 6G), and these components can act on multiple core targets simultaneously, reflecting the “multi-component and multi-target” characteristic of traditional Chinese medicines.

Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair regulates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway

Based on the results of UPLC-MS and network pharmacology, Western Blot (WB) and immunofluorescence (IF) were employed to validate the regulatory effect of the herb pair on the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. The results demonstrated that, compared with the control group, the protein expression ratios of p-PI3K/PI3K, p-AKT/AKT, and p-mTOR/mTOR were significantly upregulated in both lung tissues of asthmatic mice and BEAS-2B cells in the model group. In contrast, these phosphorylated protein ratios were markedly downregulated in all groups treated with the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair (Fig. 7A–D). The cellular immunofluorescence staining revealed that the fluorescence intensities of p-PI3K and p-AKT in the model group were significantly higher than those in the blank control group. After drug intervention, the fluorescence intensities were significantly weakened (Fig. 7E), consistent with the WB results. To further verify whether the herb pair could exert its effects through the PI3K-AKT pathway, a specific PI3K inhibitor (LY294002，10 μm) was used in the cell experiment. Compared with the model group, both LY294002 and the herb pair significantly reduced p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR protein levels, although the herb pair showed a weaker inhibitory effect than LY294002. Combined intervention with the herb pair and LY294002 did not significantly enhance the inhibitory effect beyond LY294002 alone (Fig. 8A and B). These findings further suggest that the anti-asthmatic effect of the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair may be associated with the suppression of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

Discussion

As a classic herb pair, Ginseng-Schisandra has a long application history. Existing studies have demonstrated its significant efficacy in the treatment of respiratory diseases such as pneumonia, cough, and lung cancer [[23], [24], [25]]. However, the mechanism of action and material basis of this herbal pair in the prevention and treatment of asthma remain unclear. In this study, a mouse asthma model was established for observing behavioral changes in mice, and detecting the total number of white blood cells in BALF, the levels of OVA-sIgE and IgE in serum, and lung histopathology. The results showed that Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair could alleviate asthma to a certain extent.

Asthma is characterized by the abnormal activation of Th2 cells, and the inflammatory response driven by Th2 cells can directly induce typical pathological changes of asthma, specifically including the infiltration of inflammatory cells such as eosinophils and macrophages in the airway wall, excessive airway mucus secretion, airway epithelial damage, and goblet cell hyperplasia [26,27]. These pathological changes ultimately lead to airway narrowing and airflow limitation, which in turn result in asthma symptoms such as wheezing, coughing, and chest tightness. In this study, key indicators of the pulmonary inflammatory response were measured in asthmatic mice. The results showed that treatment with the extract of the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair reduced lung inflammation, decreased goblet cell numbers in pathological sections, and lowered the levels of Th2-type cytokines (IL-4 and IL-5) in both BALF and cell supernatants. Collectively, these findings indicate that the extract of the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair effectively alleviates the pulmonary inflammatory response in asthma.

Besides inflammation, there is a close relationship between oxidative stress and the pathophysiology of asthma. The core association lies in the imbalance between oxidation and antioxidation in the airway of asthmatic patients, characterized by excessive production of ROS and decreased activity of antioxidant enzymes. This imbalance can damage airway epithelial cells, impair the integrity of the mucosal barrier, induce airway inflammatory responses, promote the release of inflammatory factors, and further aggravate airway spasm. Antioxidant therapy, however, can assist in alleviating asthma symptoms. Studies have shown that both Ginseng and Schisandra exhibit significant antioxidant effects. For example, Ginseng polysaccharides can regulate glycolysis and gluconeogenesis to reduce oxidative stress-induced damage to red blood cells, while Schisandra extract can improve Alzheimer's disease through antioxidative stress [28,29]. In this study, key indicators of oxidative stress in the serum of mice and BEAS-2B cells were detected. It was found that the extract of Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair could reverse the oxidative stress imbalance (elevated MDA and decreased SOD) in serum and cells, and reduce the accumulation of ROS in cells, suggesting that the extract of Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair can exert an anti-asthmatic effect by improving oxidative stress.

In order to further elucidate the major ingredients and mechanism of action of Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair in exerting its anti-asthmatic effect, UPLC-MS combined with network pharmacology technology were used for component analysis and mechanism prediction. UPLC-MS, leveraging its dual capabilities of “separation + detection”, can separate the complex components in the herb pair one by one, and then accurately identify the molecular structure and molecular weight of each component via mass spectrometry, and ultimately screen out the active components. Based on this technology, a total of 72 active components in the extract of the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair were identified in this study. Compared with single Ginseng or single Schisandra, some new components such as Ginsenoside F5, Ginsenoside Rb3, Ginsenoside Rs5, Gomisin L2, and Gomisin M2 were identified. However, components present in the single herbs, including Ginsenoside Rd2, Ginsenoside Re4, Ginsenoside Rb1, Ginsenoside R0, Gomisin H, and Schisantherin C, were not detected. These findings suggest that interactions between the components occurred during the extraction process of the two herbs. This transformation between these components may be related to the optimal anti-asthmatic efficacy observed when the ratio of the two herbs is 2:1.

Network pharmacology can accurately capture the interaction network among drugs, components, targets, and diseases, effectively predict the mechanism of drug action, and clearly clarify the association logic between drugs and diseases [30]. A total of 399 common targets between components and the disease were identified by network pharmacology analysis. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the core pathways were PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, Th17 cell differentiation, AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, etc., of which the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway showed the highest enrichment level. Whereas, an OVA-induced allergic asthma model was employed in this study, and its core pathological mechanism is airway inflammation dominated by Th2-type immune response. The PI3K-AKT pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating immune cell activation and inflammatory cytokine release, which aligns more closely with the pathological characteristics of this model. Although the Th17 cell differentiation pathway and AGE-RAGE pathway are also associated with inflammation, they are not the dominant pathways in the OVA-induced allergic asthma model. Therefore, it is hypothesized that the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair may alleviate asthma by regulating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Existing studies have shown that the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway plays a key role in the pathogenesis of asthma, since its excessive activation can enhance bronchial smooth muscle contraction, promote the secretion of inflammatory factors and oxidative stress responses, and aggravate airway inflammation and tissue damage [31,32]. After being activated by upstream inflammatory signals, PI3K phosphorylates and activates AKT. On the one hand, the activated AKT can promote the release of inflammatory factors such as IL-4 and IL-5 by regulating transcription factors, enhancing airway inflammatory responses such as eosinophil recruitment [33]. On the other hand, it activates downstream mTOR, enhances bronchial smooth muscle contractility, and accelerates airway remodeling, leading to luminal stenosis, and impairs the barrier function of airway epithelial cells while increasing airway sensitivity to stimuli [34]. Whereas, inhibitors targeting key nodes of this pathway can inhibit inflammatory responses and airway remodeling. To verify this mechanism, Western Blot and immunofluorescence assays were used. It was revealed that the protein expression ratios of p-PI3K/total PI3K, p-AKT/total AKT, and p-mTOR/total mTOR in the lung tissue of asthmatic model mice and BEAS-2B cells were significantly increased. However, Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair significantly reduced the above phosphorylated protein ratios. The combination of LY294002 and the herb pair did not further suppress the phosphorylation of these proteins beyond the level achieved by LY294002 alone. These findings suggest that Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair may exert anti-asthmatic effects through the suppression PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, thereby regulating inflammatory response and oxidative stress. This mechanism is consistent with the prediction results of network pharmacology and echoes the effects of “the herb pair alleviating inflammation and improving oxidative stress” in in vivo and in vitro experiments.

In this study, we demonstrated for the first time the anti-asthmatic efficacy of the Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair through in vivo and in vitro experiments, and preliminarily confirmed its putative regulatory effect on the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Although we analyzed the potential major active components of the herb pair using UPLC-MS combined with network pharmacology, quantitative analysis of key components and pharmacological validation of individual monomers have not yet been performed, and whether synergistic effects exist among these components remains unclear. These limitations also point to directions for future research.

Conclusions

Ginseng-Schisandra herb pair exhibits an anti-asthmatic effect. Its mechanism of action may be related to regulating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway to effectively alleviate airway inflammatory responses and oxidative stress-induced damage. Moreover, the therapeutic efficacy is significantly enhanced at a herb pair extract compatibility ratio of 2:1.
