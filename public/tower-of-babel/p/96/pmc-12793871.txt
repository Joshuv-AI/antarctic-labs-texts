Acute Myeloid Leukemia Associated With Catastrophic Arterial and Venous Thrombi

Background

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is characterized by the malignant proliferation of myeloid-derived hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in cytopenias and cytoses that clinically manifest as bleeding, infection, or constitutional symptoms, leading to diagnosis. Although venous vascular thrombosis is common in solid tumor malignancies, its incidence in leukemia is less frequent, and in AML it is rare and most commonly linked to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in the setting of stage M3 AML (acute promyelocytic myeloid leukemia).1, 2, 3 Arterial thrombi in AML are extremely rare, and when complicated by acute limb ischemia they carry high morbidity.4 Here we present a unique case of stage M1 AML associated with multifocal arterial and venous thromboemboli complicated by acute limb ischemia.

Case Presentation

A 64-year-old man with a medical history notable for hypertension and hyperlipidemia presented to an outside institution with subacute retro-orbital headache and fatigue; on presentation he was found to have leukocytosis, with a white blood cell count of 47.6 × 103/mL, with 97% of blasts concerning for acute leukemia. Computed tomography scans at presentation showed 2 distinct aortic thrombi (in the distal descending and suprarenal aorta), however anticoagulation was deferred owing to thrombocytopenia (platelet count in the 50 × 103/mL range). The patient was subsequently transferred to our institution for further management of presumed AML.

On arrival at our institution, the patient had a rapid decline in mental status and needed intubation, with magnetic resonance imaging showing multifocal strokes concerning for embolism and a likely chronic subdural hematoma. Given the concern for leukemic thrombi, he was started on cytoreductive therapy with hydroxyurea, cytarabine, and idarubicin pending confirmation of diagnosis. When initially evaluated, there was not concern for limb ischemia despite clot burden given pulses on Doppler, although limb criticality was not able to be fully assessed owing to the patient's mental status. DIC evaluation was notable for normal bilirubin (both total and direct), normal activated partial thromboplastin time, mildly elevated international normalized ratio of 1.3 (upper limit of normal: 1.1), D-dimer >20 μg/mL (above upper limit of normal), and fibrinogen of 324 mg/dL (within normal limits), with no schistocytes on peripheral blood smear. These findings remained stable over the course of his hospitalization.

Anticoagulation was initially limited owing to severe thrombocytopenia, and as thrombocytopenia improved, the patient was placed on a heparin drip targeting an activated partial thromboplastin time of 45 to 60 seconds. However, on hospital day 2 the patient rapidly progressed to Rutherford 3 status, with acutely decreasing bilateral lower extremity pulses that subsequently became non-Dopplerable, and a cold and unresponsive right lower extremity. Computed tomography of the aorta confirmed previously noted thrombi in the distal descending and suprarenal aorta (Figures 1A and 1B) as well as a new complete occlusive thrombus of the infrarenal aorta extending into the left and right common iliac arteries with distal reconstitution (Figure 1C). It was also notable for acute bilateral pulmonary emboli and a right atrial filling defect concerning for thrombus. The ankle-brachial index confirmed lack of detectable flow in the right lower extremity, and the patient was taken for emergent right lower extremity above-the-knee amputation and attempted thrombectomy of the femoral and iliac arteries. Retrograde angiogram confirmed the iliac thrombus filling defect; however no clot was recovered despite multiple retrieval balloon passes. Bilateral kissing iliac stents were placed, with final angiogram showing patent iliac arteries. Pathology revealed arterial leukemic thrombus of the popliteal and tibial arteries, with 100% vessel occlusion. Transesophageal echocardiography performed postoperatively confirmed finding of right atrial thrombi, without evidence of interatrial shunt on agitated contrast or other shunts on Doppler (Figure 2).

Transesophageal Echocardiography Demonstrating the Right Atrial Leukemic Thrombus

Transesophageal echocardiography was performed after emergent lower extremity amputation and surgical thrombectomy for extensive arterial embolization. Midesophageal views demonstrated a mobile, echogenic mass within the right atrium consistent with a thrombus (arrow). Agitated saline contrast injection showed no evidence of a right-to-left shunt, and color Doppler imaging did not reveal additional intracardiac shunting. These findings supported a diagnosis of leukemic thrombus as the embolic source of pulmonary emboli.

Hematopathology confirmed M1 myeloblastic AML, with 97% of blasts notable for pathogenic mutations in NPM1, IDH1, PHF6, and DNMT3A. However, the patient's postoperative course was complicated by persistent respiratory failure and acute kidney injury, necessitating initiation of renal replacement therapy. The patient's family elected to pursue comfort focused care, and the patient died on hospital day 7.

Discussion

This case is unique in that the patient presented with catastrophic thromboembolism manifested as multifocal venous and arterial thrombi without overt DIC, ultimately leading to amputation followed by death. This is an extremely rare complication of AML, and it highlights the challenges that providers face in managing this critically ill population of AML patients.

It is known that active malignancy confers an increased risk of thrombosis by altering the balance of the Virchow triad of endothelial injury, stasis, and hypercoagulability to favor coagulation activation. Increased thrombosis risk has typically been ascribed to solid malignancies, but there is now greater awareness of the increased risk of venous thrombosis in myeloproliferative disorders.2,3

In AML, the presence of abnormal coagulation is strongly associated with thromboembolism, with a prospective cohort study by Libourel et al1 showing that a DIC score of >5 was strongly associated with an increased risk and that of all the DIC parameters the D-dimer level was the most predictive. Arterial thrombi are uncommon but are also being increasingly recognized. The incidence of thromboembolism in the Libourel et al study was 4.7% venous/4.0% arterial for the 18- to 65-year-old cohort and 4.4% venous/5.9% arterial for the ≥65-year-old cohort. Another study by Mitrovic et al5 of 626 AML patients in Serbia found an arterial thromboembolism incidence of 2.9%, strongly correlated with body mass index, history of thromboembolism, comorbidities (such as hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, coronary heart disease, or arrhythmia), and cytogenetic risk. Our patient certainly had hypertension and hyperlipidemia as risk factors for the development of multifocal thromboembolism, and although he was mildly coagulopathic as evidenced by his mildly elevated international normalized ratio, he was not in overt DIC, making his presentation atypical. The normal bilirubin and absent schistocytes suggested against hemolysis; the normal, then increasing, fibrinogen suggested against coagulation factor consumption; and the anemia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated D-dimer were felt to be due to the patient's frank AML.

Critical/acute limb ischemia (CALI) is the rarest form of arterial thromboembolism in AML, representing just 1 in 18 patients and 3 in 17 patients with arterial thromboembolism in Mitrovic et al's5 and Libourel et al's1 cohorts of AML patients, respectively. It has also been reported in several case reports and case series.4,6 CALI in the cancer patient population is poorly understood overall, with Mouhayar et al7 reporting only 74 cases across a 10-year period at a tertiary-care dedicated cancer hospital, and Bryce et al8 reporting only 29 cases across a 6-year period at a similar institution.

CALI management consists of anticoagulation, thrombus removal, and resection of nonviable tissue if needed, however concomitant cytopenias and coagulopathy in the AML population can make management extremely challenging.9 Involvement of vascular surgery and hematology/oncology are critical to navigating each patient's case. The study by Mouhayar et al7 of CALI patients, which included 8 patients with hematologic malignancy, found that operative intervention was the most common management strategy (49%), followed by catheter-based intervention (28%), medical management alone (24%), and no therapy (8%). In the case series by Bontinis et al4 of 26 patients with CALI in the setting of AML, 42.3% of CALI events were managed with chemotherapy and thrombectomy, 15.4% with chemotherapy alone, and 15.4% with thrombectomy alone; 30.8% progressed to amputation. The investigators did not report anticoagulation or antiplatelet use.4 Chotai et al6 had previously reported on 20 cases of CALI in the setting of AML (included in the study by Bontinis et al); of those cases, 10 patients were treated with anticoagulation of some form and 1 patient received clopidogrel; aspirin use was not recorded.

Although aspirin is known to reduce arterial thrombi risk in patients with multiple myeloma or polycythemia vera, the use of antiplatelet agents in leukemia is guided by expert consensus based on platelet count (aspirin if platelets >10 × 103/mL, and dual-antiplatelet therapy if platelets >30 × 103/mL).3,9 Management of venous thrombi in leukemic patients is largely extrapolated from the studies of nonleukemic malignancies, as they were largely excluded from both the prophylactic and treatment trials given the risk of bleeding.3 Treatment is based on a combination of venous thrombi location, severity, and platelet count, with full-dose therapeutic anticoagulation being the general treatment of choice if platelet count is >50 × 103/mL.3,10

CALI is known to be a relatively morbid condition in the general population, however in the cancer patient population, CALI is associated with even worse mortality: 20% to 41% at 3 months and 52% at 1 year.7,8 In leukemic patients, this appears to be enhanced even further, as Bontinis et al4 reported that CALI conferred a 46.2% in-hospital mortality rate, although with significant heterogeneity (54% for the 14 AML patients but 0% for the 2 patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia). The patients who were managed with amputation had a lower mortality than those who were not (37.5% vs 50%); limb salvage therapies (thrombolysis, thrombectomy) had heterogenous outcomes.4 These findings should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size and retrospective nature of the study.

Clearly, further investigation is needed to determine the optimum treatment of AML complicated by CALI, however given its rarity we will likely continue to rely on case reports, case series, and expert opinion for its management. Ideally, the patient should be managed with a multidisciplinary care team, with the patient's comorbidities and clinical findings dictating the degree of intervention for CALI.

Conclusions

This case highlights the rare but catastrophic complication of arterial and venous thrombi in AML. When complicated by CALI, the condition caries a high mortality, and aggressive management with intervention, chemotherapy, anticoagulation, and antiplatelet therapy if able should be pursued.
