Strategies and sacrifices of family caregiving in the Somali-American community: a qualitative study
Authors: Kamal Suleiman, Elizabeth Lightfoot, Rajean Moone
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmong the many pressing concerns of the Somali-American community, and other similar immigrant and refugee communities as they grow in the United States, is the provision of care for older adults and adults with disabilities. The implications of effective caregiving range from the facilitation of community building and place-making to the delivery and management of healthcare on a systemic level. However, little is currently known about Somali-American family caregivers, including their duties and responsibilities, primary concerns, and the impacts of surrounding influences on their ability to fulfill their role.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with 10 Somali-American family caregivers in Somali language. The resulting transcripts were translated into English by a professional interpreter and analyzed using an inductive thematic analysis approach. A key informant from the community was additionally consulted for insights regarding cultural nuances and interpretations of idiomatic expressions and concepts.ResultsThematic analysis of the reports revealed the principal themes of visitation, patient accompaniment, and self-sacrifice through acceptance and God-consciousness as pervasive and salient concerns across participants. Furthermore, the heightened stresses of the COVID-19 pandemic revealed just how severe the consequences can be when access to culturally habituated navigational tools and coping mechanisms are restricted.ConclusionsIn light of our findings, the growing concern for discrimination and sociocultural discord in the Somali-American community presents a particularly prescient threat to the well-being and sustainability of family caregivers. Their experiences must be understood and used to promote education and partnership between the healthcare system and the community in order to build trust and ensure a healthy future for this indispensable population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11543-6.

Background

The Somali-American community is a predominantly refugee population that has settled across the United States following civil conflicts and ongoing violence in Somalia since 1991. Research on this population is sparse, but a significant body of evidence suggests that strenuous refugee experiences have left the community at elevated risk for poorer health outcomes. Post-resettlement acculturative stressors, including a thwarted sense of belonging and perceived discrimination, have been associated with heightened risk for PTSD, depression, and other negative mental health outcomes among Somali-Americans [1–4]. Moreover, experiences of war-related trauma are prevalent in the population, further increasing the risk for psychological distress and illness [5, 6]. These findings justify concern for the present and future well-being of this community and warrant further research into possible paths to remediation.

Some new literature, however, has explored strengths and sources of resilience in the community’s sociocultural composition as they settle into their new homes – i.e., “place making.” Reliance upon Islam, the majority religion for Somalis across the globe, for spiritual and moral foundation, as well as deeply rooted cultural concepts of community connectedness and generosity have emerged as common sources of strength and resilience [6–10]. Continued research into these salient sociocultural factors is necessary to understand the tools and strategies for place making employed by the Somali-American community, and to develop policy and practice interventions based on these strengths.

Among the most pressing concerns of the Somali-American community, and many similar immigrant and refugee communities as they grow in their new homes, is the provision of care for older adults and adults with disabilities. Somali communities are gerontocratic, meaning that elders occupy positions of esteem and authority in the social structure. Research on older Somalis in Canada and the US has revealed that even in their new North American communities, Somali elders have relied on their positions as counselors, decision-makers, and leaders to feel purpose and fulfillment in their old age [7, 11]. Given this cultural context, it follows that the facilitation of a safe and dignified aging process for those elders is a priority for the community at large. Unfortunately, a detailed literature search has revealed a dearth of available data on the topic of family caregiving among Somali-Americans. This is a striking gap, given that threats to social cohesion in the community, including discrimination, economic inequity, and acculturation stress, can potentially raise concern for its ability to effectively fulfill the goal of facilitating a dignified aging process. The limited existing data points to escalating intergenerational tensions, familial dysfunction, and thwarted belongingness, all of which can threaten the efficacy and sustainability of caregiving efforts [4, 11–13]. Furthermore, the prevalence of disabilities, including Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), has been cited as a significant concern for Somalis across the global diaspora, while the relevant education and caregiver support have remained underdeveloped and under researched [14, 15]. Consequently, it becomes imperative to develop a body of research that understands the experiences of Somali-American caregivers in order to prepare community members, leaders, advocates, and other researchers to face the challenges.

In the United States, the majority of care for older adults and adults with disabilities is provided by their own family members [16]. Extensive research into family caregiving in the general US population has found that family caregivers can face enormous stress, including loss of income, insufficient support, and lack of respite. Not surprisingly, family caregiving is often associated with negative health and mental health outcomes, though there are positive outcomes associated with caregiving as well [17–19]. To cope with caregiving stress, family caregivers must develop strategies for generating motivation and meaning and must be afforded access to adequate external support. To this end, existing research on family caregivers from various backgrounds has emphasized the roles of moral and cultural values, including religiosity and filial piety, in coping with stress, finding purpose, and maintaining commitment to care [20–23]. Additionally, external supports such as home healthcare services and assisted living facilities are often used to alleviate the burden and stress of caregiving [24].

To date there have been no studies examining the particular ways in which Somali-Americans navigate caregiving for older relatives and relatives with disabilities. Consequently, the present qualitative study sought to investigate the primary challenges faced by family caregivers in the Somali-American community and highlight the critical tools and approaches they employ to combat the challenges and facilitate care. Through this exploration of the sacrifices and strategies of the family caregivers in our study, we believe critical insight can be gleaned about the needs of the Somali-American community as they interact with the larger healthcare system. As mentioned above, caregivers not only provide services to the most vulnerable members of their community, but are vulnerable to a variety of harms themselves. Therefore, gaining insight into their first-hand experiences with navigating health systems, networks, and supports will help identify how they can be reinforced and sustained. Furthermore, their attitudes and perspectives can hold a mirror to the present state of health services, particularly as it relates to minority populations. Our inductive thematic analysis will help reveal how some Somali-American caregivers are affected by policies and practices in the healthcare system, which we hope will spark reflection and action among researchers, practitioners, and community members. Moreover, this study was conducted during the heightened stress of the COVID-19 pandemic, which exposed a number of systemic barriers facing the community while simultaneously highlighting the indispensability of their culturally habituated methods of care. For further information about the impacts of COVID-19 and the resulting public health regulations on the provision of care for aging relatives and relatives with disabilities, see the following studies [25, 26].

Methods

This study emerged out of a larger qualitative study aimed at exploring caregiving of older relatives and relatives with disabilities during the COVID-19 pandemic. That parent study collected data using semi-structured interviews in multiple languages with family caregivers of various backgrounds, namely European-American, Latino, and Korean, and is described in detail elsewhere [25, 26]. The present article represents data from a new sample of Somali-American caregivers that has not been previously reported. Ethical approval for human research was provided for this study by the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board (approval no. STUDY00009958), and informed consent was obtained prior to the beginning of each interview (Additional File 2).

Research participants

We recruited a sample of 10 Somali-American caregivers using convenience sampling in two metropolitan regions in the Midwest, including Minnesota where the largest Somali community in the US is located [27]. Due to the preliminary nature of this study and the lack of existing supporting research, we closed our recruitment at this number in order to proffer an initial foundation of data upon which future research may draw. As a result, we did not reach a point of data saturation within our sample. Professional and personal networks were drawn upon to reach participants through e-mail and telephone, in both English and Somali language, including through a Somali-run caregiver support agency. The demographics of the sample are displayed in Table 1. All participants were Somali-born and spoke Somali as their first language. We provided no incentives for participation, and no participants refused to participate or dropped out.

Characteristics of study participants

Our study’s recruitment methodology is notable given the sparse available guidance regarding best practices for conducting research in the Somali-American community. As elaborated in the forthcoming positionality statement, the lead author of this article is a member of the Somali-American community. Furthermore, a Somali-American key informant was recruited in order to conduct interviews in Somali language, translate them competently, and consult for insights into cultural nuances and sensitivities (see: Data Collection, and Analysis Strategy). The knowledge of the first author in collaboration with the key-informant was critical in identifying, contacting, and liaising with potential participants for the study. The benefits of partnering with an insider for community studies are well-founded in the qualitative research literature, and they include expediting recruitment, increasing comfort and trust on behalf of participants, and ensuring ethical and empathic practices throughout the study process [28]. Accordingly, our collaboration with our key informant allowed us to access community channels that were not well-advertised, including a Somali-run healthcare support agency, which became critical to our recruitment process. Without this access, our ability to gather a meaningful sample of Somali-American caregivers would have been hampered.

Data collection

This study used the same semi-structured interview guide that was created in English for the larger parent study, which allowed for some comparability in the study while allowing for additional probing for unique experiences and circumstances [29] (Additional File 2). This interview guide was translated into the Somali language using a professional interpreter who also served as a key informant for the project. The guide offered useful context regarding the goals and justifications of the project, explored demographics, main responsibilities of caregiving, concerns related to the role, changes to caregiving during the pandemic, and barriers and facilitators to caregiving resulting from COVID-19. The researchers pre-tested the interview guide with community members and the translated interview guide was approved by the IRB.

Data were collected via semi-structured interviews ranging from 28 to 55 min in length that were conducted from May to October of 2020 on Zoom using the dial-in feature and were recorded via Zoom. We used the dial-in feature in order to facilitate the interview process for our participants, many of whom expressed concern over their ability to navigate the zoom application, which was needed for its frictionless recording capabilities. Similarly, we anticipated that some participants may be uncomfortable with presenting their face, so limiting our interaction to audio appeared to be the most comfortable option. All interviews were conducted in Somali language by a bilingual interviewer, who is a professional translator and interpreter, licensed in the state of Minnesota, and was trained in the research protocol and approved by the IRB. He was accompanied by a Somali-American member of the research team, namely the first author, who has limited working proficiency in Somali but was familiar with the interview guide and holds CITI ethics certification for human research. No interviews were repeated. The video/audio files were stored in a secured Box account that was only accessible to researchers. The bilingual interviewer and the accompanying research team member translated and transcribed the recorded interviews into English, paying particular attention to idioms and cultural expressions to capture the nuances of colloquial Somali speech.

As discussed previously (see: Research Participants) our partnership with a community insider to bolster recruitment, data collection, and analysis was crucial to our methodology. Somali populations are often reported to be emotionally reticent and reluctant to discuss personal matters in detail [30]. In contrast, the relative vulnerability of our participants and their candid, authentic reports indicated that we managed to establish enough trust to elicit detailed responses. Without an insider to speak their language and match their sensitivities, our study may have attained a much shallower understanding of their experiences, potentially devoid of the expressive storytelling and idiomatic expressions of Somali culture. Future research within this population, particularly when conducted by outsider research teams, must focus on securing community partnerships and incorporating a native voice into every level of the research process. Recommendations for building and maintaining partnerships are outlined in community based participatory research literature and are vital for research teams who lack insider membership [31].

Analysis strategy

We used NVIVO 12 to analyze the transcripts using an inductive thematic analysis approach to identify and describe the cultural and religious ideas that Somali caregivers had about their caregiving role following the guidelines proposed by Braun and Clarke [32]. As this was an inductive study, we did not begin our analysis with any predetermined theoretical constructs, but rather focused on theme generation based on the Somali caregivers’ own words about caregiving. The first author of this study, who was present during all the interviews, reviewed each transcript and assigned initial codes in NVIVO. The first 5 transcripts were also independently coded by the second author, the primary investigator of the parent study, who examined them along the same guidelines. The first and second author then discussed the themes, making use of our varied lenses to dive deeper into each theme’s meaning, and achieved consensus. After establishing agreement, the first author completed the initial analysis of the remaining transcripts. The final themes met the initial analysis criteria while also cutting across participants to form a clearer picture of the Somali-American family caregiver role as distinct from the larger caregiver population. During this process, the first and second authors met numerous times to critically reflect and clarify the themes that emerged from this sample, drawing on our varied positionalities. The transcripts were then given a final review by the second author to verify the established themes. Furthermore, the findings were reviewed with our Somali key-informant for further verification and insight to bolster our final analysis and interpretation.

Author positionality

The first author of this article is of mixed ethnicity with one Somali immigrant parent and one European-American parent. He grew up frequenting a number of Somali-American community gathering locations including mosques, schools, restaurants, and more. His father is a prominent and well-connected community advocate, program organizer, and court translator, and his mother worked in a majority Somali-American charter school for over 10 years. The second author, who served as the principal investigator of the original parent study, is of European ancestry. She has collaborated on several long-term research projects with Somali-Americans, though has no personal insight into Somali-American caregiving. She has also partnered with researchers to conduct family caregiving research on several continents, so she does have insight into research regarding international and cultural variations in family caregiving. Finally, she is a family caregiver herself. The third author is of European ancestry and is a recognized mixed-methods researcher in the field of aging in minoritized communities. His work has included the translation of age-friendly models into diverse communities and the creation of organizations designed to elevate the voices of minoritized groups to support positive systems change. The three authors discussed and drew upon our multiple and varied positionalities, including our ethnic backgrounds and our personal and professional experiences with family caregiving, throughout all stages of this study.

Results

Our interviews revealed a number of insights into the concerns and experiences of Somali-American family caregivers during the heightened stress of the COVID-19 pandemic. Three primary themes emerged from our analysis that appeared to cut across participants and highlight salient sociocultural factors that affected their experiences: visitation, patient accompaniment, and self-sacrifice through acceptance and god-consciousness.

Theme 1. Visitation

The most ubiquitous concern expressed by participants in our study was the detrimental impact of restricting their ability to pay visits to or receive visits from friends and relatives. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, participants reported that spending time hosting visitors and traveling to see friends and relatives provided emotional support, social connection, and physical activity for both family caregivers and care recipients. Additionally, multiple participants reported that visitation was an important source of burden reduction, allowing caregivers to meet the socioemotional needs of their loved ones while distributing the effort across the family network. When the COVID-19 pandemic restricted this activity, participants reported that it both increased the caregivers’ personal stress and diminished the mental well-being of their loved ones. One participant caring for her 67-year-old mother described that her mother often became frustrated and resentful when compelled to stay home and not accept visits from relatives; this precipitated conflict in the home that added to her emotional burden:The relatives and friends that used to come see her, and the people she used to go out to visit, that used to relax her. It bothers her that she cannot talk and have conversations with those people… That really affected her behavior, and her personality became more difficult… She gets angry and says, ‘it’s you who’s not giving me things, it’s not even other people it’s just you.’

As participants described the importance of visitation to their caregiving process, emphasis was often placed on the “Somali culture” as a driving force behind the practice. It was stated that the desire to share news, spend time together, and even hug was a product of Somali cultural norms, and the choice to decline such activities due to safety concerns was reportedly distressful for both caregivers and care recipients. In particular, the participants who cared for aging relatives emphasized that the role of an elder in the Somali community was closely tied to their connection to the extended family network. One participant caring for his 90-year-old father described the near-constant stream of relatives, both immediate and distant, who paid visits to them prior to the pandemic, and the hours they spent discussing the affairs of friends and family across the world. This participant, and others, reported that these activities provided important emotional outlets for their aging parents, and solidified their positions as elders in the community. Attempting to restrict these activities that are culturally expected of a community elder resulted in caregivers feeling that they were denying their parents one of their most valuable duties. One participant caring for her 75-year-old mother related that her mother perceived the loss of connection to relatives as the utmost negative outcome of the pandemic:She [mother] is a community elder, and everyone used to visit her, but we had to stop them because of the disease… sometimes she just asks, “What happened to all the people who used to come to see me? What happened to my relatives and my other children?” I tell her, “People are worried because of the disease…” Then she says, “This disease created a big problem for us. Theme 2. Patient accompaniment

Several caregivers in our study reported that among their primary responsibilities was acting as a translator, guide, and advocate during hospital visits on behalf of their family members. Multiple participants described that their loved ones were unable to navigate visits to doctors’ offices, clinics, and hospitals on their own, and that the services provided by health systems to meet those needs were insufficient. As a result, prior to COVID-19, participants reported attending appointments and treatment sessions with their loved ones multiple times a week, refusing hospital translation services to undertake it themselves, and organizing overnight accompaniment shifts among relatives. Consequently, when COVID-19 began restricting caregivers’ abilities to accompany their relatives into the hospital it became a source of distress. One participant caring for her 80-year-old mother even reported:She had many doctor appointments that we canceled because the hospitals said that if someone has to go to the hospital they have to go alone, and she can’t manage that.

She had many doctor appointments that we canceled because the hospitals said that if someone has to go to the hospital they have to go alone, and she can’t manage that.

The choice to cancel appointments rather than risk sending an aging parent into the hospital without family accompaniment illustrated the severity of this concern. Moreover, multiple caregivers in our study related experiencing a noticeable reduction in quality of care when they were restricted from accompanying their relatives to treatment sessions. One participant caring for his 90-year-old father powerfully described the experience of admitting his father for dialysis on a regular basis, and the notable decrease in quality of care he witnessed as a result of his inability to accompany him:Before when we [caregivers] were with him [father] we were able to communicate with the doctors about his situation… But now, the situation is that when the person comes in alone, he becomes just like the other people the doctor sees on a daily basis… He [father] goes into the hospital by himself three days a week and we don’t even know what’s happening to him during those hours… he says ‘the staff don’t take care of me very well. Today I said something to them, and they didn’t give me attention, and I raised my hand, and I was ignored.’

This caregiver additionally reported that the mood and energy level of his father upon returning from unaccompanied dialysis sessions was extremely variable. The participant posited that his father’s inability to communicate with nursing staff likely meant he was not receiving water, not being made physically comfortable, and experiencing unaddressed fluctuations in blood pressure. Cases such as these in which caregivers were forced to admit their loved ones unaccompanied were reported to cause emotional distress for multiple participants. One participant caring for her son with ASD reported that one of her greatest fears resulting from the pandemic was the possibility of leaving her son alone in the hospital where he would be confused and afraid. In another particularly powerful report, a participant caring for her 81-year-old mother described the experience of being forced to leave her mother alone in the hospital for four consecutive days:We drove to the ER… a man came out and said no one except her [mother] could go inside… I said, “She doesn’t even know how to use the telephone.” They didn’t accept that… She was in the hospital for four days, and we didn’t hear a peep from them. We kept waiting and saying, ‘We’ll get a call’… That was the most heartbreaking experience for us. That kind of thing scares us, and I hope God doesn’t make it happen again.

These reports demonstrated that the Somali-American caregivers in our study used their family support system as a navigational tool to ensure that ill relatives were not subjected to poor care as a result of language barriers or any forms of discrimination. When this support was stripped away, stress, fear, and reduced quality of care became significant concerns.

Theme 3. Self-sacrifice

Participants in our study expressed their dedication to caregiving in numerous ways, and demonstrated a willingness to sacrifice time, comfort, and resources for the sake of fulfilling their role. One participant caring for his 78-year-old mother reported quitting his job at the airport to avoid transmitting COVID-19 to his mother, and several participants reported that the pandemic-related restrictions resulted in a nearly 24-hour daily commitment to care with little or no time for personal self-care. These challenging circumstances, and others reported in our study, seemed to exacerbate burnout and damage the moods of caregivers and care recipients. Such sacrifices may be part and parcel of the family caregiver role, but our study revealed compounded difficulties and particular methods of coping that seemed rooted in the Somali-American experience.

For some participants, a sense of personal and familial responsibility over caregiving duties created an aversion to certain forms of external support. In fact, one participant caring for her 75-year-old mother expressed that utilizing many forms of non-familial support would be interpreted as capitulating to the challenges of the role and would be unacceptable:We don’t take her to elder care centers, we don’t get money assistance, or any of that. Since we are here for her care, we are taking care of her, and we don’t want any of those things. She wouldn’t have accepted those things anyway; she doesn’t like them.

We don’t take her to elder care centers, we don’t get money assistance, or any of that. Since we are here for her care, we are taking care of her, and we don’t want any of those things. She wouldn’t have accepted those things anyway; she doesn’t like them.

While some participants did report accessing non-familial services including virtual exercise applications, medical resources, and payment programs, this report illustrated a sentiment that was echoed in multiple interviews, indicating a hesitancy to seek assistance from non-familial or non-culturally traditional sources. The resolve required to persevere through these expectations and sacrifices could be strenuous and the participants in our study provided valuable insight into the coping strategies that were utilized to sustain themselves; namely through acceptance and God-consciousness.

Theme 3a. Acceptance

The role of the family caregiver appeared to be closely tied to cultural and religious attitudes regarding filial piety, intergenerational responsibility, and firm faith in a divine plan. Most of our participants were caring for their aging parents or children with disabilities, and the weight of that relationship reportedly created the motivating force to cope with the sacrifices and stresses of the role. Acceptance of the responsibilities that accompanied each individual’s role within the family structure helped to inspire and maintain the caregivers in our study. In a particularly poignant report, a participant caring for her son with ASD described her personal paradigm regarding the difficulties she faced on a daily basis:When someone, especially a mother, is taking care of their own child, they don’t see it as difficult. Even if it is, they don’t see it that way.

When someone, especially a mother, is taking care of their own child, they don’t see it as difficult. Even if it is, they don’t see it that way.

Similar sentiments were echoed by participants caring for their parents, who cited the indispensability of the parental figure as a reason to maintain caution and vigilance in the face of the pandemic, despite frustrations and exhaustion. These reports emphasized that the assigned roles and responsibilities within the Somali family structure were internalized and deeply accepted by the caregivers, and they derived purpose and meaning from them.

Theme 3b. God-consciousness

Another salient avenue for strengthening resolve and bolstering against the pressures of caregiving was God-consciousness and faith in a divine plan. The participants in our study were all Muslim, and this was made clear by the frequent references to God and various Islamic beliefs and values. As is common among Muslims, our participants habitually praised God and made short prayers in their colloquial speech. One example of many came from a participant caring for her son with ASD:I can’t even get a few hours of rest, and if I want to do other things I can’t. But I still praise God.

I can’t even get a few hours of rest, and if I want to do other things I can’t. But I still praise God.

This report exemplified how remembrance of God and expressions of gratitude for one’s blessings served to keep participants grounded despite the struggles they were facing. Similarly, many participants referred to a divine plan or larger purpose when describing their interpretations of the past and their hopes for the future. Phrases like, “I hope God never makes us go through that again,” were common throughout our sample. On an individual level this appeared to assuage anxiety and facilitate acceptance of past difficulties. Interestingly, we also saw on the interpersonal level that caregivers appealed to spirituality as a tool for negotiating with their loved ones who struggled to grasp the necessity of observing caution during the pandemic. One report from a participant caring for her 75-year-old mother exemplified this coping tool:She [mother] said ‘Why are you trying to cut me off from my relatives?’ I said ‘No, I’m not. This disease has been decreed by God, but you are required by God to protect yourself and be careful…’ Then she understood what happened and accepted it as God’s decree. So, anything that is decreed by God, she doesn’t reject.

She [mother] said ‘Why are you trying to cut me off from my relatives?’ I said ‘No, I’m not. Accordingly, similar appeals to spirituality and a divine plan appeared to be important tools for both personal self-care and interpersonal reconciliation among the Somali-American caregivers in our study. While this was partially elicited by the particular demands of the pandemic, the prevalence and apparent positive impact of this as a coping tool indicate that it is a fundamental aspect of the Somali-American caregiving strategy.

Discussion

This study was designed to shed light upon the experiences, strengths, and challenges of family caregiving in the Somali-American community. Several salient themes emerged from our analysis, including the principal roles of visitation, patient accompaniment, and self-sacrifice in caregiving, as well as the supporting roles of acceptance and God-consciousness. The relative scarcity of research on this topic among Somali-Americans further emphasizes the importance of our results, as we offer novel understandings of how the challenges of family caregiving are experienced and navigated by this population. Contextualizing our findings within the existing literature about community composition, healthcare delivery, and place-making among Somali-Americans provides an opportunity to further advance our understanding of how to combat the social, cultural, and medical challenges facing caregivers in the community.

The first major theme of our analysis dealt with visitation. Social isolation was one of the foremost features of the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in markedly deleterious effects on the mental health of every age group across all communities [33–36]. The Somali-American participants in the present study emphasized that the strain of losing social connections was further compounded by culturally rooted expectations. Somali communities are gerontocratic, meaning that elders occupy positions of esteem and authority in the social structure. Research on Somali elders in Canada and the US has revealed that even in their new North American communities, Somali elders relied on their positions as counselors, decision-makers, and leaders to feel purpose and fulfillment in their old age. Additionally, they cited frequent close contact with their relatives and descendants as critical to feeling “useful” to their families and communities [7, 11]. Consequently, family caregivers in our study who were compelled to prohibit their older parents from the visitation that is so vital to their culturally ascribed identity felt guilt and shame. Moreover, they faced significant resistance and resentment from their parents and found it taxing to justify the need for their actions. This raises concerns that extend beyond the particular circumstances of COVID-19.

Research in Somali immigrant populations suggests that social cohesiveness in their new communities has been challenging to establish and maintain even prior to pandemic-related restrictions. Discrimination, economic inequity, and acculturation stress are among the foremost threats to the social health of the community, resulting in intergenerational tensions, familial dysfunction, and thwarted belongingness [4, 11–13]. Our findings demonstrated that family caregivers are particularly concerned with these harmful effects. Our participants reported that the restriction of their ability to facilitate socioemotional connection for their loved one, which they perceived as among their foremost responsibilities, sowed discord in their relationships and elicited feelings of guilt, shame, and resentment. While COVID-19 may have precipitated this situation for the caregivers in our study, the aforementioned evidence suggests that many more caregivers may face similar challenges irrespective of pandemic-related restrictions. Regardless of the source, threats to the social cohesion of the larger community may make caregivers more vulnerable to burnout and cause further harm to the well-being of care recipients. Efforts must be made to mitigate the root causes of social strife in the Somali-American community, namely discrimination, economic inequity, and acculturation stress, in order to ensure the sustainability and effectiveness of family caregiving.

The second major theme of our analysis was patient accompaniment. The caregivers in our study reported acting as translators, guides, and advocates for their loved ones during visits to the hospital. This is a well-documented strategy for navigating the medical system for immigrant and refugee communities, as well as other underserved and at-risk populations. A meta-analysis of studies about patient accompaniment during routine medical visits found that it promoted information exchange between patients and providers, fostered healthy rapport, and facilitated care management in the long term [37]. These benefits have been reported among both the underserved and general population [38]. However, immigrant and refugee communities further benefit from the additional function of cultural brokerage.

Cultural brokers during medical visits can consist of family members, friends and other supporters, or hired staff. These brokers provide language translation, communication assistance, supervisory companionship, and access to critical resources and information; unfortunately, this role is often undertaken by children or other people with limited training or assistance of their own [39–41]. Our study emphasized the indispensability of this function among Somali-American family caregivers. When pandemic-related restrictions stripped our participants of their ability to accompany their relatives, they were exposed to myriad deleterious effects. Poor communication, weak translation services, neglect, and noticeable harms to physical health were reported by multiple participants. The stress and fear this engendered in the caregivers was also a significant detriment to their mental and emotional well-being.

If the absence of this accompaniment strategy prompts such negative effects, then serious concern for discrimination in the healthcare system towards older Somali-Americans and those with disabilities is warranted. Recent data has corroborated this concern, indicating a trend towards increased experiences of medical discrimination among patients of marginalized backgrounds, including Somali-Americans [42–44]. Effective responses to those system-level concerns must include recognition and integration of patient accompaniment and cultural brokerage in the healthcare delivery process. Accompaniment should be valued and supported by hospital staff and administration as a community-driven strategy for improving healthcare and not seen as an optional privilege nor a burden or hindrance to be jettisoned in times of crisis.

The third and final major theme of our analysis was self-sacrifice, with the supplemental roles of acceptance and God-consciousness. Our participants described culturally rooted impetuses for their firm dedication to family caregiving and highlighted the many sacrifices they incurred in service of that role. The ramifications of these sacrifices were often managed through cultural and religious coping mechanisms. However, indications were made across participants that they were hesitant and sometimes averse to utilizing support services from non-familial and non-Somali sources. Our participants viewed their caregiving role as intrinsic to their position in the family structure and would not compromise that responsibility for their own ease or comfort. Evidence suggests this may be a common conundrum among immigrant and refugee populations. Numerous studies indicate that immigrant populations utilize caregiver support services at a disproportionately low rate compared to the general population [45–47]. Possible causes for this underutilization include culturally rooted stigma against help-seeking, as it may be seen as capitulating to the challenge of caregiving, as well as negative past experiences with healthcare providers and facilities.

Evidence about Somali-American caregivers in particular is scant, but one study suggested that home healthcare services that focus on supporting and empowering family caregivers are preferable to those that offload responsibility to facilities and staff [48]. Future efforts to improve healthcare delivery in this population must pay attention to these preferences and incorporate the underlying cultural and religious values that are foundational to the provision of care in Somali-American families. We recommend a two-pronged approach to mitigating this concern: (1) healthcare systems must continue developing culturally competent strategies for service delivery that respect and support Somali-American caregivers on their terms, and (2) caregivers in the community should combat the concept that accepting external support detracts from their value as caregivers. On both fronts, promoting education and partnership between the healthcare system and the community are paramount to building trust and ensuring a sustainable future for family caregivers.

Study limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, this study used a convenience sample that was recruited via professional networks. However, as one of the first studies of Somali-American caregivers and given the challenges of research recruitment within this population, particularly during the COVID-19 lockdown, this approach seemed appropriate for our goal of uncovering themes and shedding a first light on these important issues. Second, the interview guide for this study was developed in English and then translated into Somali, rather than formulated from scratch specifically for Somali caregivers. We may have asked different questions if this was not a sub-sample of a larger study. A future study, perhaps building on the findings of this study, could be developed specifically for Somali-American caregivers. Third, those analyzing the data were not fluent in Somali, and had to rely on a translator. While the reliability of the translation was not tested through reverse translation or external review, the translator was professionally licensed in the state of Minnesota and was an active member of the research team who was consulted as a cultural informant throughout the analysis process. Therefore, while this is a limitation in our rigor, we remain confident in the trustworthiness of our findings. Additionally, the first author of this article is Somali-American, and his positionality is relevant to the insights presented in this article, as articulated in the positionality statement.

Conclusions

This article represents the first in-depth qualitative investigation of the particular experiences and perspectives of Somali-American family caregivers. Existing research into the sociocultural composition of Somali-American communities has demonstrated that they are collectivist in nature and largely gerontocratic; however, despite this emphasis on familial connection and respect for elders, the specific priorities and challenges of family caregivers are poorly understood. As the community continues to grow, age, and develop in their new homes, the strategies and tools needed to effectively facilitate care will become increasingly complex. Consequently, a body of research that transparently reports the variety of sociocultural factors at play in their caregiving strategy is imperative. We believe that the primary themes that emerged from our analysis, namely visitation, patient accompaniment, and self-sacrifice through acceptance and God-consciousness, are a valuable initial step towards elucidating the experience of Somali-American caregivers. We hope that further research, both qualitative and quantitative, will build from our findings and continue supporting the development of both evidence-based understanding of this integral role in the community, as well as pragmatic solutions to the myriad challenges they face.

A number of questions remain unanswered regarding family caregiving in the Somali-American community. We recommend that researchers consider the following avenues for future investigation. Firstly, our study was both limited in scope and did not delineate between a variety of relevant socioeconomic factors; therefore, it would be valuable to collect larger samples of caregivers from a variety of backgrounds within the community to analyze the impacts of such variables as resource availability, education-level, social integration, and more. Secondly, there is a dearth of quantitative data elucidating the distribution of various caregiving roles and responsibilities on a community-wide level, as well as the prevalence of relying on various forms of external support (financial aid programs, social support groups, full- and part-time care facilities, etc…). Lastly, we reiterate the necessity of partnering with community insiders and integrating native voices in all steps of the research process. The publication of evidence-based guidelines for conducting ethical and impactful research within the Somali-American community in particular would support all forms of continued research and development.
