Impact of Radiation Therapy on Diastolic Function and Cardiac Troponin I Levels in Cancer Patients Undergoing Contemporary Radiotherapy
Authors: Fumika Haga, Masayoshi Oikawa, Tetsuya Tani, Tetsuro Yokokawa, Shunsuke Miura, Tomofumi Misaka, Takashi Kaneshiro, Akiomi Yoshihisa, Takafumi Ishida, Yasuchika Takeishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiation-associated cardiac disease (RACD) remains a clinically relevant late toxicity of thoracic radiotherapy. Although modern techniques have reduced cardiac exposure, myocardial injury may still occur.Methods and ResultsWe analyzed 355 patients with esophageal, lung, or left breast cancer who underwent baseline cardiac evaluation before cancer therapy. For the primary analysis, patients without radiotherapy (no RT; n=237) were compared with those who received radiotherapy involving the heart (cardiac RT; n=41). Patients who underwent thoracic radiotherapy without direct cardiac irradiation (n=77) were evaluated separately. Cardiac troponin I (cTnI) and echocardiographic parameters were assessed at 12 months. cTnI levels remained stable and showed no association with cardiac radiation exposure. Left ventricular ejection fraction was preserved, whereas the cardiac RT group exhibited lower septal e′ velocity and E/A ratio. Higher cardiac radiation dose was associated with lower E/A ratio (R=−0.448, P=0.009) and septal e′ velocity (R=−0.389, P=0.023). In multivariable logistic regression analysis, cardiac irradiation independently predicted a reduced E/A ratio (<0.8; odds ratio 2.46; 95% confidence interval 1.04–5.83; P=0.041).ConclusionsCardiac irradiation was associated with declines in diastolic indices despite stable cTnI. Diastolic impairment may represent an early subclinical manifestation of RACD, underscoring the importance of echocardiographic monitoring and minimizing cardiac radiation exposure.

Central Figure

Radiation-associated cardiac disease (RACD) remains an important late toxicity of thoracic radiotherapy. The risk of cardiac events increases proportionally with the mean heart dose, and recent 3-dimensional dosimetric analyses have demonstrated that radiation exposure to specific cardiac substructures can predict future cardiovascular complications.1,2 RACD encompasses a broad spectrum of manifestations, including coronary artery disease, valvular dysfunction, and diastolic impairment.3 Previous echocardiographic studies have shown early reductions in myocardial strain despite preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) after breast radiotherapy,4 suggesting that subtle myocardial dysfunction may precede overt systolic decline.

In recent years, technological advances such as intensity-modulated radiotherapy and stereotactic body radiotherapy have markedly reduced the mean heart dose.5,6 However, the impacts of modern-day radiotherapy on cardiovascular function remain incompletely understood.7 In parallel, the clinical importance of cardiovascular monitoring in contemporary cardio-oncology practice has been increasingly emphasized.8–11 This study aimed to elucidate the characteristics of early cardiac functional changes after thoracic or cardiac irradiation by analyzing the associations among cardiac troponin I (cTnI) levels and echocardiographic parameters in patients who underwent radiotherapy involving the heart.

Methods

Study Population and Protocol

We enrolled 375 patients with esophageal, lung, or left-sided breast cancer who underwent baseline cardiac evaluation before anthracycline or immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy at Fukushima Medical University Hospital between January 2016 and October 2024. Patients who had received radiotherapy at other hospitals (n=11) or underwent palliative irradiation (n=9) were excluded. Patients were initially classified into 3 categories according to their radiotherapy history: (1) those without a history of radiotherapy (no RT group); (2) those who received radiotherapy including the heart within the irradiation field (cardiac RT group); and (3) those with a history of thoracic irradiation that did not involve the heart (thoracic RT group). Finally, 237 patients without radiotherapy, 77 with thoracic irradiation, and 41 with cardiac irradiation were included in the analysis (Figure 1). For the primary analysis, comparisons were performed between the no RT group and the cardiac RT group. Patients in the thoracic RT group were excluded from the primary analysis and were evaluated separately as a supplementary analysis. Patient flow diagram showing enrollment, exclusions, and final analysis. ICI, immune checkpoint inhibitor.

Hypertension was defined as a history of antihypertensive drug use or systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg. Diabetes was defined as current use of insulin or hypoglycemic drugs, or hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%. Dyslipidemia was defined as a history of cholesterol-lowering drug use, or triglyceride ≥150 mg/dL, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥140 mg/dL, and/or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥40 mg/dL. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed 12 months after the initiation of radiotherapy in the cardiac and thoracic irradiation groups, whereas baseline data were used for the non-irradiated group. We obtained approval from the Ethics Committee of Fukushima Medical University (approval no. 2839) that the study protocol conformed to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Radiation Dose Evaluation

All patients who received radiotherapy underwent the treatment as part of their primary cancer management. Using the radiotherapy planning computed tomography images (Optima CT 580, GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA), patients whose irradiation field included the heart were classified as the cardiac irradiation group, whereas those whose field did not include the heart were classified as the thoracic irradiation group. The total prescribed radiation dose was used as a representative dose metric.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed by a trained sonographer, and the images were reviewed by another trained sonographer as well as an echo cardiologist. Cardiac function was assessed using EPIQ 7G (Philips Healthtech, Best, The Netherlands). LVEF was calculated using the modified Simpson’s method in B-mode, according to the guidelines of the American Society of Echocardiography and the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging.12 Diastolic function was assessed using transmitral and tissue Doppler imaging parameters. Early and late diastolic inflow velocities were measured to determine the E/A ratio, and early diastolic mitral annular velocities at the septal and lateral sites were used to calculate the average E/e′ ratio. Because atrial fibrillation causes beat-to-beat variability and makes E/A ratio interpretation unreliable, patients with atrial fibrillation were excluded from the E/A ratio analysis.

Blood Sampling

Of the 118 patients in the cardiac and thoracic RT groups, blood samples were collected at 3, 6, and 12 months after the start of radiotherapy. High-sensitivity cTnI was measured using an assay based on luminescent oxygen channeling immunoassay technology and run on a Dimension EXL integrated chemistry system (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Deerfield, IL, USA).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, LA, USA) and SPSS version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of variables. Normally distributed variables are shown as mean±standard deviation. Non-normally distributed variables are indicated as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. For comparisons between groups, the Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed variables, the Mann-Whitney U-test for non-normally distributed variables, and the χ-squared test for categorical variables. Correlations between radiation dose and echocardiographic or biomarker parameters were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to evaluate predictors of reduced E/A and e′ velocity. Variables with P<0.1 in the univariable analysis were also included in the multivariable analysis. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons.

Results

A total of 355 patients was included in the final analysis: 237 patients were assigned to the no RT group and 41 to the cardiac RT group for the primary analysis. In addition, 77 patients who received thoracic radiotherapy without direct cardiac irradiation were included in secondary exploratory analyses (Figure 1). Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Patient characteristics were comparable between the no RT and cardiac RT groups with respect to age, sex distribution, and major comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Cancer type distribution differed between the groups, with a higher proportion of esophageal cancer in the cardiac RT group and a higher proportion of lung and left-sided breast cancer in the no RT group. Prior anthracycline use tended to be less frequent in the cardiac RT group; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Among the laboratory findings, white blood cell count and lymphocyte percentage were lower in the cardiac RT group. Baseline cTnI levels did not differ significantly between the 2 groups. Next, logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with reduced E/A ratio (<0.8) and reduced septal e′ velocity (≤6.0 cm/s), both of which are generally considered abnormal values. The multivariable logistic regression model included age, sex, cardiac irradiation, and established risk factors for diastolic dysfunction, such as hypertension,13 diabetes,14 and prior use of anthracyclines.15 Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified age (odds ratio [OR] 1.096; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.061–1.133; P<0.001), diabetes (OR 3.018; 95% CI 1.334–6.827; P=0.008), and cardiac irradiation (OR 2.458; 95% CI 1.036–5.827; P=0.041) as independent risk factors for a reduced E/A ratio (<0.8; Table 4A). For reduced septal e′ velocity (≤6.0 cm/s), age was the only independent factor (OR 1.061; 95% CI 1.035–1.088; P<0.001; Table 4B). Discussion

In the present study, we evaluated the association between radiation exposure and early cardiac changes using serial cTnI measurements and echocardiographic assessment in patients undergoing contemporary radiotherapy. The principal findings were that cardiac irradiation was associated with dose-dependent deterioration of diastolic indices, whereas no significant changes in cTnI levels or LVEF were observed during the follow-up period. These findings indicate that subtle diastolic impairment occurs in patients exposed to cardiac irradiation even with contemporary techniques.

RACD is recognized as a late complication that becomes clinically apparent several years or even decades after radiotherapy.16 With the widespread use of advanced radiotherapy techniques such as intensity-modulated radiotherapy and volumetric modulated arc therapy, cardiac exposure has been substantially reduced in recent years.5,6,16 Nevertheless, our findings indicate that even with these modern modalities, subtle myocardial changes may still develop in patients who receive direct cardiac irradiation. These findings underscore that RACD risk should not be overlooked even in contemporary radiotherapy.

Several echocardiographic studies have reported early impairment of diastolic function after thoracic radiotherapy, even when systolic function remains preserved. Cao et al. demonstrated that among patients with left-sided breast cancer treated with adjuvant radiotherapy and trastuzumab, left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, as indicated by an increased E/e′, occurred in more than one-third of patients.17 Berlin et al. found that over a median follow up of 5.1 years after modern breast irradiation, changes in diastolic indices were more pronounced than the decline in systolic function, particularly in patients who received higher doses to the left anterior descending coronary artery.7 The present study demonstrated a radiation dose-dependent decline in the E/A ratio among patients in the cardiac RT group. The E/A ratio is widely used as a supportive echocardiographic parameter for evaluating left ventricular diastolic dysfunction.18 A reduced E/A ratio serves as an early indicator of diastolic abnormality and is known to be associated not only with aging19 but also with cardiac damage caused by anthracycline-based chemotherapy.15,20 Together with the modest reduction in e′ velocity, the decreased E/A ratio observed in this study suggests an initial phase of diastolic dysfunction associated with RACD.

In contrast, cardiac biomarkers such as troponin have shown inconsistent results in radiation-related myocardial injury.21,22 In the present cohort, cTnI levels remained within the normal range and did not correlate with the cardiac radiation dose. This finding is consistent with the absence of overt myocardial necrosis and suggests that early radiation-related myocardial changes may not be accompanied by detectable biomarker release. Similar variability in the clinical significance of troponin elevation has been highlighted in contemporary cardio-oncology settings, including immune checkpoint inhibitor–associated myocardial injury.23,24 Experimental and pathological studies have suggested that radiation-induced myocardial injury is characterized by endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signaling, leading to loss of microvasculature, interstitial fibrosis, and myocardial stiffening rather than acute cardiomyocyte necrosis.25–28 Such microvascular and fibrotic changes are known to impair ventricular relaxation and compliance before systolic dysfunction becomes evident, providing a plausible mechanistic explanation for the observed diastolic abnormalities in the absence of troponin elevation.

Importantly, in multivariable analyses adjusting for age, diabetes, and prior anthracycline exposure, cardiac irradiation remained independently associated with a reduced E/A ratio, supporting an independent contribution of cardiac radiation to early diastolic impairment.

From a clinical perspective, the magnitude of the observed diastolic changes was modest and did not translate into overt heart failure or systolic dysfunction during the relatively short follow-up period. However, diastolic dysfunction is widely regarded as an early manifestation of myocardial injury and may precede the development of clinically apparent cardiac disease. Therefore, longitudinal echocardiographic assessment of diastolic function may be useful for the early detection and monitoring of radiation-related cardiac effects, particularly in patients receiving cardiac irradiation. This approach is in line with recent efforts to strengthen cardio-oncology care delivery and survivorship management.20,29 Furthermore, these results underscore the importance of optimizing treatment planning to minimize cardiac radiation exposure as much as possible.

Study Limitations

There are several limitations to the present study. First, this study was a retrospective, single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may have limited statistical power. Second, detailed dosimetry (e.g., mean heart dose and cardiac substructure doses) was unavailable; therefore, the total prescribed dose was used as a surrogate exposure metric. Third, follow up was limited to 12 months, and delayed cardiac effects may have been underestimated. Fourth, advanced echocardiographic measures such as myocardial strain were not routinely assessed. Future studies incorporating strain analysis may better characterize early subclinical myocardial dysfunction. Fifth, plasma B-type natriuretic peptide was not routinely measured, and clinical heart failure events were not a prespecified outcome and were assessed only by review of medical records. Information on heart failure medications (including sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors) was not systematically collected; however, their use appeared limited in this cohort. Sixth, this study was designed as a cross-sectional analysis, and echocardiographic parameters were assessed at a single time point, 12 months after irradiation. Therefore, longitudinal changes in echocardiographic parameters were not evaluated in this study. Last, patients who received thoracic radiotherapy without direct cardiac irradiation were included in secondary exploratory analyses. Given the heterogeneous and generally limited cardiac exposure in this group, these findings were interpreted cautiously and are presented in the Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Further prospective studies in larger cohorts with detailed dosimetry and longer follow up are warranted.

Conclusions

Cardiac irradiation was associated with subtle, dose-dependent declines in diastolic indices despite preserved systolic function and stable serum troponin levels in patients undergoing contemporary radiotherapy. These findings suggest that diastolic impairment may represent an early subclinical manifestation of RACD. Echocardiographic monitoring and careful minimization of cardiac exposure remain important in the contemporary radiotherapy practice.
