Molecular characterization of cell dynamics during osteosarcoma progression
Authors: Xiangjie Li, Junjie Liu, Yu Wang, Jiangtao Hu, Qianqian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlight•This study aims to integrate high-resolution single-cell maps of large-scale clinical samples during the progression of osteosarcoma.•Targeting YY1 or blocking the Treg-TAM interaction pathway may be a new strategy for inhibiting osteosarcoma metastasis in the future.•We provide new targets and ideas for the immunotherapy of osteosarcoma.Keywords: Osteosarcoma, Spatial transcriptomics, Cell differentiation trajectories, UMAP, Tumor metastasis

Introduction

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents, with extremely high invasiveness[1]. According to epidemiological data, the incidence of osteosarcoma mainly concentrated in the rapid growth period of adolescents [2] and the elderly population over 60 years old (often secondary to Paget's disease)[3]. Although it accounts for a relatively low proportion of all cancers, it holds an important position among the causes of cancer-related deaths among teenagers[4]. Approximately 20% of patients have already developed metastasis, mainly lung metastasis[5], at the initial diagnosis, and 30% to 40% of localized patients will relapse after treatment. Once metastasis or recurrence occurs, the 5-year survival rate of patients drops sharply to below 20%[6]. This indicates that the current chemotherapy regimens have limited effects on clonal subpopulations with metastatic potential. We need to re-examine the mechanism of tumor progression at the molecular level.

Tumor is not a static mass of cells but an evolving ecosystem. During the process of the disease developing from the primary lesion to the metastatic lesion, tumor cells undergo intense changes in cell dynamics. The cellular heterogeneity and complex cellular dynamic characteristics of osteosarcoma are the key reasons for its high metastasis and treatment resistance[7]. As evidenced by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies, osteosarcoma comprises multiple malignant subclusters, including osteoblastic, chondroblastic, fibroblastic, and stem cell-like populations, each with distinct molecular profiles, proliferative potentials, and metastatic capacities[8]. These subpopulations continuously interconvert along differentiation trajectories, a phenomenon termed cellular dynamics, which enables osteosarcoma cells to adapt to microenvironmental stress, evade therapy, and acquire invasive phenotypes. Recent analyses have identified a highly invasive subpopulation characterized by elevated expression of COL6A1, COL6A3, and MIF, which is strongly associated with lung metastasis and poor clinical outcome[9]. Concurrently, a small subset of CXCL14+ stem cell-like cells exhibits mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) properties, driving tumor initiation, chemoresistance, and metastatic dissemination through CXCL14-integrin α11β1 axis [10]. The dynamic interplay between osteosarcoma cells and the tumor microenvironment (TME) is critical for metastatic progression. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), particularly the FABP4+ proinflammatory subset, accumulate in metastatic lesions and secrete cytokines that enhance tumor cell migration and immune suppression[8]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) remodel the extracellular matrix (ECM) and secrete TGF-β, which promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in osteosarcoma cells, thereby facilitating invasion and intravasation[11]. Osteoclasts, activated by RANKL/CSF-1 signaling, degrade bone matrix and release pro-tumorigenic factors, creating a permissive niche for metastatic seeding[12]. Although heterogeneity is known to exist, it is not entirely clear if specific cell subpopulation dominates the evolutionary trajectory from primary to metastasis.

Based on the above background, this study aims to address the key gaps in osteosarcoma research by utilizing high-throughput single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptome technology. This study aims to integrate high-resolution single-cell maps of large-scale clinical samples (primary, recurrent, and metastatic) during the progression of osteosarcoma, and precisely define the heterogeneity of tumor cells and microenvironment cells. Recurrent osteosarcoma samples are rarely included in single cell studies, our study fills this gap by enabling comparison of primary, recurrent, and metastatic stages.

Methods

Data acquisition and manipulation

The scRNA-seq datasets are derived from GSE152048, GSE162454, GSE198896, GSE234187, GSE250015 and GSE299023 in the GEO database, combined with the data in the PMID: 39,164,791[13]. We totally acquired the data of 39 osteosarcoma samples, including 30 primary, 5 metastasis and 4 recurrent samples. This study conducted bioinformatics analysis of high-throughput sequencing data using the R package (version 4.1.3). The core analysis process is built on the Seurat package. First, strict cell-level quality control is carried out on the original expression matrix: The scDblFinder algorithm is utilized to identify and eliminate double cells (doublets), while setting the mitochondrial gene proportion threshold below 20% and the red blood cell proportion below 3%, and filtering out abnormal cells with UMI counts not within the 200–30,000 range or detection gene numbers not within the 200–7000 range.

During the data preprocessing stage, the NormalizeData function in the Seurat package is called in sequence to normalize the data, the FindVariableFeatures function screens out 2000 highly variable genes, and the ScaleData function is used for data transformation and regression analysis. It is clearly stated that the cell cyclin-related scores (S.core and G2M.Score) are used as covariates for correction to eliminate technical noise. The correction of batch effects is implemented using the Harmony algorithm[14].

In terms of dimensionality reduction and cluster analysis, the Unified Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) is adopted for low-dimensional space embedding, and the Louvain algorithm is used for unsupervised clustering. The identification of differentially expressed genes was accomplished through the FindAllMarkers function. The screening criteria were set as follows: the significance p value was <0.05, the logarithmic multiple change (log2FC) was greater than 0.25, and the proportion of expressing cells in the target cluster was greater than 10%.

For spatial transcriptome data, this study obtained the original data from the GSE299025 dataset with 2 tumor samples, and the literature PMID: 39,164,791[13] with one tumor sample. The conditional autoregressive deconvolution (CARD) algorithm was adopted, combined with the fine cell annotation results obtained from the above single-cell sequencing, to predict and analyze the cell type composition in each spot of the spatial transcriptome, and the CARD software package was used to complete the visualization of the spatial distribution of cell types.

Cell annotation

Using the SingleR algorithm, the individual cells after dimensionality reduction and clustering are preliminarily annotated based on its built-in reference as the Human Primary Cell Atlas. This method assigns a preliminary biological identity to each cell cluster by comparing the similarity between the single-cell gene expression profile and the reference dataset. Subsequently, for the cell populations with low confidence or ambiguity in the automatic annotation results, manual annotation was adopted for correction. Specifically, by searching the PubMed database and related literature, specific marker genes of various types of cells, such as T cells, B cells, macrophages, endothelial cells, etc. were systematically collected, and a custom list of marker genes was constructed. Visualization methods such as Feature Plot, Dot Plot and Heatmap are utilized to display the expression and distribution patterns of key marker genes in each cell cluster. Based on the known expression characteristics of cell type-specific marker genes, the automatic annotation results are manually reviewed and corrected to eliminate low-quality or non-biologically significant clusters like double-cell or multi-cell complexes, and ultimately the precise cell types of each cell cluster are determined.

Cell subpopulation analysis

This study conducted a deep subpopulation analysis of the pre-defined key cell lineages, including osteoblast lines, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), T/NK cells, and myeloid cells. Firstly, precisely extract the above-mentioned target cell population from the initial Seurat object through the subset function and construct an independent analysis object. For the extracted cell subsets, re-execute the dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis process: Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is adopted for feature dimensionality reduction, and a K-nearest neighbor graph is constructed based on the first 15–20 principal components. Subsequently, the Louvain algorithm is used for unsupervised clustering (with the resolution parameter set at 0.5–1.0 to obtain a fine subgroup structure). Differential expression analysis was conducted using the FindAllMarkers function to identify the highly expressed genes specific to each subcluster with the screening criteria typically setting as: p value 〈 0.05, average log2FC 〉 0.25, and the expression ratio in this cluster was higher than that in other clusters. Top 10 differentially expressed genes (Top Markers) are visually displayed of each subcluster.

Gene enrichment analysis

For the list of significantly differentially expressed genes, enrichment analysis of the Over-Representation analysis (ORA) algorithm was conducted using the clusterProfiler software package. The background database of functional annotation mainly covers the biological processes (BP) and molecular functions of gene ontology (GO). There are three major levels: MF and cellular component (CC). Meanwhile, to explore the specific biological states related to tumorigenesis and development, the "Hallmark" gene set in the MSigDB (Molecular Signatures Database) was introduced as the prior knowledge base. This gene set contains 50 highly refined and iconic gene sets. It can effectively summarize complex biological phenomena and reduce the redundancy among gene sets.

First, we obtain the gene set definitions of the above-mentioned functional database through the msigdbr package and input them as parameters of the enricher function. For the differentially expressed gene sets, their enrichment significance is calculated in each functional item or pathway, conducting the Benjamini-Hochberg method to perform multiple test corrections on the original P-values to obtain the false detection rate (FDR). The screening criteria are usually set at FDR < 0.05 and the Enrichment Factor greater than 1 to ensure the reliability of the results. For the visualization of enrichment results, functions in the enrichplot package are used to multi-dimensionally display significantly enriched GO entries or Hallmark pathways, where the size of the dotplot represents the number of enriched genes. The depth of the color represents the significance level of enrichment (p value or FDR value), thereby intuitively revealing the aggregation characteristics of differentially expressed genes in specific biological functions.

Pseudotime analysis

In this study, the Monocle2 software package was used for Pseudotime analysis[15]. Firstly, based on the results of the previous analysis of cell subpopulations, fibroblast subpopulations were extracted using the subset function of the Seurat object, and their expression matrix (count matrix) and metadata (metadata) were converted into the CellDataSet (CDS) format required by Monocle2. After constructing the CDS object, data normalization processing is carried out, and Highly Variable Genes are screened as the input features for subsequent dimensionality reduction ranking to reduce noise and improve the accuracy of trajectory construction.

In the trajectory construction stage, the reduceDimension function is called for nonlinear Dimensionality Reduction. The dimensionality reduction algorithm is specified as DDRTree (Discriminative Dimensionality Reduction with Trees). This algorithm can effectively capture the branch structure of cell state transitions. Subsequently, the cells are sorted through the orderCells function to calculate the relative position of each cell in the assumed developmental path, thereby inferring the Pseudotime value.

Cell communication analysis

It evaluated potential intercellular communication using the CellChat package. CellChat function is performed to import the normalized gene expression matrix to create a CellChat object. Then we use the identifyOverExpressedGenes, identifyOverExpressedInteraction and ProjectData function using the default parameters for preprocessing of the data. The computeCommunProb, filterCommunication, and computeCommunProbPathway functions are used to determine any potential ligand-receptor interactions. Finally, the aggregateNet function is used to generate the cell communication network.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. At indicated time point (24 h), 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Sigma). One microgram (1 ug) of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System. Gene expression levels were normalized to the internal control ACTIN.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. Cells were then incubated with primary antibody against YY1 (dilution 1:200) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500) for 1 hour at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Western blot

Tumor tissue (10 mg) lysed in 300 µL of RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Beyotime) on ice. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein samples were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer (Epizyme) and denatured at 100 °C for 10–12 min. The following primary antibodies were used for immunoblotting: anti-YY1 (Protintech, 66,281-1-Ig, 1:10,000), anti- SLC40A1 (Protintech, 26,601-1-AP, 1:1000), and anti-ACTIN (ABclonal, #A11283, 1:20,000).

Statistical analysis

All data processing, statistical analysis and plotting were carried out in R 4.1.3 software. The correlation between two continuous variables is evaluated through the Pearson correlation coefficient. Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables, and Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare continuous variables. The survminer package was employed to determine the optimal cut-off value. Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier analyses were performed via the survival package.

Single-cell atlas of osteosarcoma

We presented the cell type atlas of the single-cell data, including 247,774 cells and 12 cell types (Fig. 1A). It contains chondroblasts, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, myeloid cells, myoblasts, osteoblasts, osteoclasts, pericytes, plasma cells, proliferating cells and T/NK cells, which is the basis for all subsequent analyses. Tumor samples from 7 datasets at different stages show different distribution patterns on the UMAP map (Fig. 1B), which intuitively indicates that there are significant differences in cell composition among primary, recurrent and metastatic tumors, providing a basis for studying the dynamic changes during the metastasis process. Subsequently, the expression of markers for each cell type was presented to verify the accuracy of the annotations (Fig. 1C): RUNX2 (chondrocytes), ACAN (chondrocytes), ACP5 (osteoclast), LYZ (myeloid cells), CD3D (T/NK cells), DCN (fibroblasts), RGS5 (pericytes), PECAM1 (epithelial cells), MYL1 (myoblasts), JCHAIN (plasma cells), MS4A2 (mast cells), and TOP2A (prolif cells). Figure D and E both demonstrate the proportion of different cell types in primary, recurrent and metastatic tumor samples. Compared with the primary tumor, the microenvironment of metastatic tumors has undergone remodeling. Specifically, it is manifested as the proportions of fibroblasts, mast cells, endothelial cells, pericyte cells and T/NK cells increased significantly, while the proportions of osteoclasts, myeloid, and chondroblast were significantly reduced. Subsequently, Ratio of Observed to Expected (Ro/e) analysis was conducted to demonstrate the preference of each cell type among three tissues (Fig. 1F). Among primary, recurrent and metastatic tumors, the proportions of chondroblasts in recurrent samples, fibroblasts and mast cells in metastatic samples significantly increased (red), while the proportions of endothelial cells in recurrent samples and chondroblasts in metastatic samples significantly decreased (blue). Fig. 1G verified the predictive results of single-cell sequencing and provided spatial information of the cell composition in each spot. It can be seen that osteoblasts have stronger signals in metastatic tumors, while chondroblasts and osteoclasts have weakened signals, in complete agreement with the previous results.

Analysis of malignant cell trajectories

We performed the monocle2 software to conduct trajectory analysis on the malignant cell population. The bifurcation pattern indicates a divergent evolutionary path from subtype 2 towards either sub1/sub3 or the sub4/sub5 branch. State analysis revealed that state 1 and state 3 primarily contained a mixture of subtype 1–4 cells, while state2 was dominated by subtype 5 cells (starting point) (Fig. 4A–E). Subtype 1 represents the initial or dry state of the tumor (l root), while Subtypes 3, 4, and 5 represent the terminal state of differentiation in different directions. More importantly, metastatic cells are mainly enriched at the end of the trajectory, suggesting that the cell state of this branch is closely related to tumor Metastasis. Subsequently, the calculation of differentially expressed genes at bifurcation point 1 was studied. From this analysis, the gene set of Cluster 3 was obtained, and most of these gene sets were highly expressed in the pre-branch (State 2 or Subtype 5), indicating that tumor progression is accompanied by large-scale transcriptional reprogramming (Fig. 4F). Meanwhile, the related functions of the gene set were studied in Fig. 4G–H. The genes in Cluster 3 are mainly enriched in pathways highly related to malignant tumor progression, including "proteoglycan in cancer", "PI3K-Akt signaling pathway", "ECM-receptor interaction", "focal adhesion", etc. This functionally confirms that this evolutionary branch has a high potential for invasion and metastasis. Expression dynamics of 12 representative genes were visualized along pseudotime, showing progressive upregulation during sub3 differentiation.

YY1 transcriptionally activates IER5L to promote osteosarcoma cell proliferation

We first extracted paired cancer and adjacent non‑cancerous tissues from osteosarcoma patients and performed protein quantification of SLC40A1 and YY1. Western blot analysis revealed that the protein expression levels of both YY1 and SLC40A1 were up-regulated in cancer tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues, with statistical significance (Figure S2A). Furthermore, we detected IER5L mRNA expression in patient tumor tissues by qPCR, which showed a marked increase (Figure S2B). To explore the regulatory relationship, we transfected osteosarcoma cell lines with a YY1 full‑length overexpression plasmid and confirmed successful overexpression by qPCR, demonstrating a high increase in YY1 mRNA levels (Figure S2C). Given that YY1 is a transcription factor, we performed immunofluorescence co‑localization experiments and observed that YY1 overexpression led to a pronounced increase in its nuclear localization (Figure S2D). Notably, upon YY1 overexpression, IER5L transcript levels also increased correspondingly (Figure S2E). To test functional significance, we designed siRNA targeting IER5L and transfected it into cell lines. Knockdown of IER5L significantly reduced cell proliferation capacity. Importantly, IER5L knockdown reversed the YY1 overexpression‑induced increase in cell proliferation, indicating that YY1 regulates malignant cellular behavior through transcriptional activation of IER5L (Fig. S2F).

Discussion

The fundamental reason for the poor clinical prognosis of osteosarcoma lies in its profound intratumoral heterogeneity and dynamic cellular remodeling during progression, which lead to chemotherapy resistance and metastatic dissemination[17]. Most existing studies focus on single-stage or single-center samples, while this study’s design enables systematic characterization of cell dynamics throughout osteosarcoma progression, improving the universality and reliability of the results. This study adopted a comprehensive and rigorous multi-omics analysis strategy, combining scRNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, bioinformatics, and statistical analysis, which is consistent with the latest technical trends in osteosarcoma research.

This study constructed a single-cell atlas of osteosarcoma containing 247,774 cells and several cell types, clarifying the dynamic changes of cell composition during OS progression. Compared with primary tumors, the TME of metastatic tumors underwent significant remodeling: the proportions of fibroblasts, mast cells, endothelial cells, pericytes, and T/NK cells increased significantly, while the proportions of osteoclasts, myeloid cells, and chondroblasts decreased. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that TME remodeling is closely related to osteosarcoma metastasis [18], and further supplemented the specific changes of various cell types in different stages.

In the analysis of osteoblast subsets, the study identified 21 heterogeneous osteoblast subgroups, among which Osteoblast_08, Osteoblast_15–17, Osteoblast_19, and Osteoblast_21 were significantly enriched in metastatic samples, and metastatic osteoblasts exhibited higher scores of angiogenesis, EMT, hypoxia, and invasion signatures[9]. Additionally, Osteoblast_06 had a significantly higher CNV burden, and malignant osteoblasts showed distinct hallmark pathway activities compared with benign ones, suggesting that malignant osteoblast subgroups may drive osteosarcoma metastasis through regulating tumor-related pathways such as URG4/Wnt signaling [19], which required dedicated experimental validation in future studies.

For malignant cells, NMF analysis identified 10 MPs and 5 evolutionarily connected malignant subtypes (Subtype 1–5). Trajectory analysis revealed a divergent evolutionary path from Subtype 2 to other subtypes during metastasis, and metastatic cells were mainly enriched at the end of the trajectory. The genes in Cluster 3 (differentially expressed at the trajectory bifurcation) were enriched in metastasis-related pathways such as "proteoglycan in cancer" and "PI3K-Akt signaling pathway", confirming the key role of this evolutionary branch in osteosarcoma metastasis [20,21]. A 2026 review identifies YY1 as an architectural regulator of chromatin organization and enhancer-promoter communication, functioning as a clock-controlled gene that bridges circadian transcription with spatial chromatin architecture. This positions YY1 as a regulator operating across both spatial and temporal dimensions[22].

SLC40A1 exhibits a complex relationship with TAMs, with its expression pattern distinguishing between pro-inflammatory M1 and immunosuppressive M2-like TAM phenotypes. M1 macrophages display low SLC40A1/ferroportin expression coupled with high ferritin levels, creating an iron-sequestering phenotype that supports antitumor responses, while M2-like TAMs show high SLC40A1 expression with an iron-release phenotype that provides iron to tumor cells and promotes cancer progression[23,24]. Recent evidence demonstrates that SLC40A1 can also mediate tumor suppression through a positive feedback loop with M1 macrophages-SLC40A1 regulates CXCL11 secretion, which activates JAK2-STAT1 signaling to promote macrophage TNF-a production, which in turn upregulates SLC40A1 expression, ultimately enhancing antitumor immunity and improving immunotherapy responses[25]. This dual role highlights that SLC40A1′s impact on TAMs depends on the macrophage polarization state and tumor microenvironment context, with distinct expression patterns serving as markers differentiating M1 from M2 macrophage populations in hepatocellular carcinoma and other malignancies. In myeloid cell analysis, 13 TAM subtypes were identified, and the differentiation trajectory (SLC40A1 → MT1G → CXCL10) revealed that TAMs undergo functional transformation during OS progression, with TAM_SLC40A1 enriched in primary tumors, TAM_MT1G in recurrent tumors, and TAM_CXCL10 in metastatic tumors. This functional transformation of TAMs may contribute to the immunosuppressive TME and promote osteosarcoma metastasis. Functional analysis showed significant differences in dysfunctional, cytotoxic, and exhausted scores among different T/NK subsets. Importantly, Treg_TNFRSF4 and CD4T_NIBAN1 had strong interactions with TAM_MT1Q, jointly constructing an immunosuppressive TME, which is a key link in osteosarcoma metastasis. Based on the comprehensive analysis of multi-center, multi-stage scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics data, this study drew the following key conclusions. Malignant cells of osteosarcoma exhibit significant intratumoral heterogeneity, with 5 evolutionarily connected subtypes, and Subtype 2 serves as a key intermediate in the evolutionary trajectory toward metastasis. The transcription factor YY1 in Subtype 2 acts as a spatial regulator of IER5L, which may be a core molecular node driving osteosarcoma metastasis. This finding fills the gap in the identification of key malignant subpopulations and their regulators during osteosarcoma progression. Targeting YY1 or blocking the Treg-TAM interaction pathway may be a novel strategy for inhibiting osteosarcoma metastasis. This provides a clear direction for the development of targeted therapies for osteosarcoma, which is urgently needed in clinical practice. TAMs undergo a functional transformation trajectory (SLC40A1 → MT1G → CXCL10) during osteosarcoma progression, and the crosstalk between Treg cells and specific TAM subsets constructs an immunosuppressive TME, which is a key link in osteosarcoma metastasis. This conclusion highlights the importance of immune cell remodeling in osteosarcoma progression and provides new targets for immunotherapy[26]. Despite the significant innovations and contributions of this study, it also has certain limitations. First, although the study integrated multiple datasets, the sample size of metastatic and recurrent osteosarcoma samples is still relatively small (5 metastatic, 4 recurrent), which may affect the statistical power of the analysis of these stages. Second, the study is a bioinformatics analysis based on public datasets, and the key findings (e.g., the regulatory role of YY1 in IER5L, the TAM differentiation trajectory) need to be verified by in vitro and in vivo experiments. Third, the study did not explore the correlation between the identified cell subsets/molecular targets and clinical parameters (e.g., patient age, treatment response), which limits the clinical translation of the findings.

In future research, more multi-center, large-sample osteosarcoma datasets, especially metastatic and recurrent samples, should be integrated to further verify the conclusions of this study. We acknowledge that the sample size imbalance across disease stages—with 30 primary samples but only 5 metastatic and 4 recurrent samples—represents a certain limitation of this study. This imbalance could potentially bias the observed cell proportion changes, particularly for cell types that are rare in metastatic tissues. Therefore, larger independent cohorts enriched for metastatic and recurrent osteosarcoma samples are necessary to validate and generalize our findings. In vitro experiments of migration, invasion assays and in vivo animal models should be used to validate the functional role of YY1, IER5L, and key cell subsets in osteosarcoma metastasis. In addition, clinical samples should be collected to explore the correlation between the identified biomarkers and clinical prognosis, providing a basis for clinical application.
