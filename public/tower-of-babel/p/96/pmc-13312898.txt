Opposing Activity at the Apical Surface: An Antagonistic Collaboration Between Crumbs and Myosin II Determines Organ Shape
Authors: Ji Hoon Kim, Deborah J Andrew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMorphogenesis, the sculpting of tissues into functional architectures, requires precise orchestration of cell shape changes and rearrangements. In the Drosophila salivary gland (SG), recent studies reveal that Crumbs (Crb) and non‐muscle Myosin II (MyoII) act as collaborative antagonists to drive SG internalization. Crb, a conserved type I transmembrane protein best known as a master regulator of apical‐basal polarity, stabilizes attachments by forming homophilic extracellular bridges between neighboring cells and restricting the distribution of junctional MyoII. Conversely, MyoII is a highly conserved motor protein that drives apical constriction and junctional shrinkage by generating contractile forces on apicomedial and cortical actin and promoting apical membrane removal. During SG invagination, these opposing activities are exquisitely tuned, with Crb‐mediated stabilization balancing MyoII‐mediated destabilization to facilitate apical constriction for internalization and neighbor exchange for tube elongation. The conserved interplay between Crb and MyoII goes beyond the shaping of organs, serving as a critical mechanism for cell extrusion and EMT‐like processes in animal development.Keywords: adherens junction (AJ), apical constriction, cell intercalation, Crumbs, Drosophila, E‐cadherin, epithelia, mechanobiology, non‐muscle myosin II, septate junction (SJ), subapical complex (SAC), tight junction (TJ)

Introduction

There is breathtakingly huge variation in organ form even within a single organism, with each organ achieving its correct size, shape and placement in the organism in such a way as to fit and optimize function. Organ complexity ranges from simple secretory organs such as the pineal gland, whose major function is the production of melatonin for sleep regulation [1], to the mammalian kidney with its millions of tiny functional units known as nephrons, each with multiple parts that together function to remove water and waste, balance electrolytes, regulate blood pressure, and control blood acidity, among other functions [2, 3].

A central question in developmental biology is how is the complexity in organ architecture achieved? Many organs are either derived from or incorporate epithelia, which are defined as polarized sheets of tightly adherent cells. The functional integrity of these sheets is manifested in the precise, localized distribution of proteins along the plasma membrane. Three distinct plasma membrane domains are found in all epithelial cells [4, 5]. The free or apical surface faces the outside world or a lumen. The lateral domains contact neighboring cells through a set of junctions, with the most critical for morphogenesis located close to the apical surface. The basal domain, which contacts underlying tissues often, at least in mature epithelia, through a basal lamina (a thin sheet‐like layer of extracellular matrix that is secreted by cells). Contact among epithelial cells is maintained by a set of junctions that feature at least one transmembrane protein that through its extracellular domain binds in trans to the extracellular domains of the same protein in neighboring epithelial cells. These proteins also make connections to the cytoskeleton through their intracellular domains, which bind to a set of adaptor proteins. Although functionally quite similar, different classes of apical junctions occupy slightly different positions along the lateral membrane in mammals and insects (Figure 1).

Specialized Multiprotein Complexes—The Apical Junctions

Among the junctions most critical to establishing initial polarity and defining the different membrane domains is the subapical complex (SAC) [6] (Figure 1). The SAC is found at the most apical position along the lateral domain in both mammals and insects and is composed of the core Crumbs (Crb) Complex [7, 8, 9], which comprises the single membrane spanning protein Crb and three adapter proteins: Stardust/Pals1 (Std), Drosophila PALS1‐associated tight junction (TJ) protein (Patj) and Lin‐7 (encoded by the veli gene) [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15], and the Bazooka complex, which is recruited by the Crb complex and is made up of Bazooka (Baz, aka Partitioning defective 3—Par3), Partitioning defective 6 (Par6) and atypical protein kinase‐C (aPKC) [7, 9, 14]. The SAC has multiple functions: it antagonizes basolateral proteins to ensure apical‐basal separation [12, 15], it links to the apical actin cytoskeleton through adaptor proteins—Moesin (Moe) and βH‐Spectrin (encoded by the karst [kst] gene)—to establish and maintain other apical complexes [16], it regulates growth through interactions with the Notch [17] and Hippo signaling pathways (Expanded, Kibra, and Merlin) [18, 19, 20], and, finally, as will be discussed in more detail, it coordinates MyoII activity to concentrate and focus MyoII‐driven cell shape change and cell rearrangement [21, 22, 23, 24].

Drosophila Crb has a long N‐terminal extracellular domain, containing 30 EGF‐like repeats and three Laminin A type‐G domain‐like repeats, a single transmembrane pass domain, which anchors the protein into the membrane, and a short C‐terminal (37‐residue) cytoplasmic domain, containing a FERM binding domain (FBD) and C‐terminal PDZ‐binding motif (PBM) (Figure 2) [25]. The large extracellular domain mediates homophilic binding both in cis and in trans to concentrate Crb within the membrane and to bridge neighboring epithelial cells [26]. The Crb extracellular domain has also been shown to negatively regulate Notch signaling, potentially through binding to the Notch extracellular domain to prevent ligand‐independent Notch endocytosis [17]. The juxtamembrane intracellular FERM (Four‐point‐one [Band4.1 protein], Ezrin, Radixin, Moesin)—binding domain (FBD) of Crb binds several proteins relevant to Crb function, including Yurt (a protein that antagonizes Crb's apical domain promoting function), Moesin, which links Crb (and the apical membrane) to the underlying apical actin network, and Expanded (Ex), an upstream regulator of Hippo signaling [16, 18, 27]. The C‐terminal PBM is required for Crb binding to Sdt (an integral component of the core Crb complex) [11, 28] and to Par6 [29], linking the core Crb complex to the Par polarity complex. Although the intracellular domain of Crb is quite short, binding of proteins to the FBD and to the PBM is not mutually exclusive, allowing for Crb protein to build greater complexity into its associations and functions.

The SAC/Crb complexes in mammals are similarly positioned and organized, but are considerably more complicated due to gene amplifications, where for example, there are three Crb orthologues (two with large extracellular domains: CRB1 and CRB2, and one with a much shorter extracellular domain: Crb3) (Figure 2) as well as variants of Sdt (PALS1 and PALS2 [Protein Associated with Lin Seven 1 and 2]) (Table 1) [6]. As in Drosophila epithelia, vertebrate Crb complexes interact with the Bazooka/Par3 complex, with ERM proteins (Ezrin, Radixin and Moesin) to link to molecules that bind to the cortical actin cytoskeleton, and other regulators of polarity and growth.

In most insect epithelia, adherens junctions (AJs) are found just below the SAC and typically feature DE‐cadherin (ECad; the Drosophila orthologue of the vertebrate Ecadherins) as the transmembrane protein that, through its extracellular domain, binds in trans to ECad molecules in neighboring epithelial cells and, through its intracellular domain, to linker proteins (Catenins) that bind to the actin cytoskeleton [30, 31] (Figure 1). AJs surround the entire apical‐lateral domain of epithelial cells and through their binding to actin form belt‐like structures that provide strong mechanical linkage between neighboring cells. Both SACs and AJs are crucial for tissue development and homeostasis and are necessary for tissues to respond to the forces generated by the actomyosin cytoskeleton. Importantly, AJs disassemble through endocytosis and actin depolymerization during morphogenetic processes such as apical constriction. Apical expansion, on the other hand, involves exocytosis, actin polymerization, and reassembly of cadherin‐catenin complexes. The structural organization of vertebrate AJs is quite similar to that of insects (Figure 1), with some variation in the length and complexity of the extracellular domains of the cadherins and the number and type of catenins (Table 1).

Although functionally similar, apical junctions occupy slightly different positions along the apical‐basal axis in mammals and insects (Figure 1). The major difference is in the type and position of barrier‐forming occluding junctions. Mammalian TJs, which function to selectively seal the paracellular space, are located apical to the AJs, whereas in insects, the functionally equivalent septate junctions (SJs) are found basal to the AJs. Mammalian TJs feature two flavors of tetraspan proteins—Claudins and Occludins—that form continuous strands surrounding and “stitching together” the apical domains of mammalian epithelia [32]. These strands function as selective permeability barriers limiting the free diffusion of water and solutes between epithelial cells. Additional TJ proteins, including junctional adhesion molecule A (JAM‐A), also contribute to barrier function. Three adaptor proteins—Zonula occludins 1–3 (ZO‐1, ZO‐2, and ZO‐3) serve to link claudins and occludins to the actin cytoskeleton and function in both the assembly and stabilization of TJs. Mammalian ZO proteins and JAM‐A have been shown to bind components of the core Crb complex (PATJ and PALS1) and the Baz/Par3 complex [32].

Insect SJs carry out the same sealing and selective paracellular permeability functions as the TJs of vertebrates but are found immediately basal to the AJs. Among the more than 30 proteins known to be required for SJ assembly and function in insects are three tetraspan Claudins: Pickel/Megatrachea, Sinuous, and Kune‐kune. Insect SJs have been implicated in several activities beyond providing barrier function—including roles in large embryonic movements such as head involution and dorsal closure, organogenesis of the trachea, salivary gland (SG), and dorsal vessel (heart), remodeling of epithelial tissues during metamorphosis, and oogenesis [33]. Interestingly, although the core SJ proteins are all present in lateral membranes shortly after organogenesis initiates and are required during these early stages, insect SJ do not form mature localized structures until the very end of embryogenesis when organs have formed and are ready to function.

Myosin II and the Forces of Tissue Morphogenesis

Among the forces relevant to tissue morphogenesis, the myosins play a major role. Myosins are a large family of motor proteins that convert chemical energy in the form of ATP into mechanical work and are the only known actin‐based motors. The myosin family is quite diverse and members have a vast array of functions including muscle contraction, intracellular transport, mitosis, as well as maintaining and modifying cell shape [34]. Myosin proteins have a conserved N‐terminal head (motor) domain that binds ATP and actin, a regulatory neck domain and a highly divergent C‐terminal tail domain that attaches to cargo or to other myosins. Non‐muscle myosin II (MyoII) is the primary myosin driving cell migration, cell shape change and cell rearrangement [31, 35, 36, 37]. To generate force, MyoII assembles into bipolar thick filaments aligned with the motor domains at both ends [34]. When these domains engage actin filaments of opposing polarity, they drive the sliding of actin filaments past each other, resulting in an ATP‐dependent contraction. Importantly, regulation and function of MyoII is conserved between Drosophila and mammals [38, 39].

The MyoII monomer is composed of two myosin heavy chains (MHCs), two essential light chains (ELCs), and two regulatory light chains (RLCs), which assemble into higher order bipolar thick filaments [40, 41]. Upon phosphorylation of conserved residues in the RLC, MyoII monomers first partition into dense assembly‐competent clusters that subsequently form the bipolar thick filaments (of ∼15–30 subunits) that can bind actin [42]. Phosphorylation and activation of the RLC is mediated by either Rho‐activated kinase (Rok) or Ca2+ calmodulin‐activated myosin light chain kinase (MLCK). Typically, the MyoII monomer is in an inactive state and is freely diffusible throughout the cell, whereas MyoII filaments are abundant in the apical‐lateral region of polarized epithelia juxtaposing the SAC and AJs. Activation of Rok and MLCK is both temporally and spatially restricted, thus driving dynamic localized MyoII assembly and contractility [36]. Importantly, MyoII can accumulate in areas of high mechanical stress, including the MyoII‐induced mechanical stresses that allow for positive feedback loops wherein mechanical tension generated by MyoII stabilizes and recruits additional MyoII [43, 44, 45, 46]. This allows for the active, directed movement and transport of myosin motors along actin filaments—so called “myosin flow”, a process essential for convergent extension and cell shape change [47], as well as for cell division and migration [48]. MyoII activity is also dynamically regulated by opposing inhibitory phosphorylation and dephosphorylation events [39, 49].

Crb Versus MyoII

In this review, we provide data supporting the model that in the Drosophila SG and in other examples of embryonic developmental processes, epithelial morphogenesis is fine‐tuned through cross‐inhibitory interactions between Crb (and the SAC) and MyoII, with MyoII providing the forces driving apical constriction and cell rearrangement, and Crb resisting these changes by stabilizing cell‐cell interactions and AJs. This fine tuning is necessary in the SG to achieve its correct final dimensions, ensuring that it is neither too long nor too short, allowing the mature organ to make appropriate contact with surrounding tissues. Crb‐MyoII fine tuning also functions during neuroblast (NB) ingression, which is critical for the timely separation of NBs from the surrounding ectoderm and for the precise temporal control of neuronal programming [50, 51]. Likewise, invagination of mouse mesodermal precursors, a process also dependent on Crb and MyoII, is necessary to ensure that these cells reach their correct anatomical positions in sufficient numbers to form the appropriate tissues [52].

The Drosophila Embryonic SG Reveals Key Roles for Crb‐MyoII Dynamics

The Drosophila embryonic/larval SG is an excellent system for uncovering the cellular and molecular mechanisms of epithelial morphogenesis [53, 54, 55]. The SG forms in the ventral region of the posterior head and is first visible as two plates of cells (placodes) flanking the ventral midline. During the next six hours, these two‐dimensional plates of cells on the embryo surface will morph into elongated, fully internalized three‐dimensional epithelial tubes. Each SG placode has approximately 140 columnar epithelial cells and, once specified, SG cells no longer divide nor do they die. Thus, morphogenesis is limited to only a few cellular processes. Indeed, transformation of the SG placodes into salivary tubes—a process that takes only six hours—appears to primarily involve only two: apical constriction and cell intercalation (Figure 3). Apical constriction begins with a small cluster of ∼eight cells in the posterior dorsal region of the placode [56, 57, 58]. As these initial cells constrict and bend inward to form an invagination pit, cells surrounding the pit converge circumferentially and extend radially, undergo apical constriction and internalize [57, 59]; this process continues until all SG cells are internalized. The apical surfaces of SG cells can be easily imaged up to the point of internalization, allowing visualization of apical area and shape as well as localization of key morphogenetic effectors, including importantly, MyoII, Crb and ECad. Moreover, the consequences of gene mutation or overexpression on final tube morphology can be easily ascertained.

It has long been known that SGs internalize by spatially regulated apical constriction under the control of two early expressed transcription factors (TFs): Fork head (Fkh) and Huckebein (Hkb) [56, 57, 58]; linking TF activity to the changes in the cytoskeletal machinery driving morphogenesis, however, took a couple of decades. Key insight was provided by the foundational discovery that apical constriction during Drosophila mesoderm invagination did not fit the widely accepted “purse‐string” model wherein a ring of actin and MyoII cinches up the apical domain by applying circumferential forces around the entire apical surface, much like drawstrings on a purse [60]. Instead, a pulsatile apical‐medial pool of actomyosin that contacts the cortex orthogonally pulls apical junctions toward the center of the apical region in a stepwise ratcheting‐type mechanism. Subsequent to this discovery, Katja Röper described three distinct pools of apical MyoII in the invaginating SG: an apical medial pool much like that described for the mesoderm, a junctional pool within each cell, and a supracellular MyoII cable that encircles the primordia and is under tension [21]. She demonstrated that the supracellular MyoII cable forms at the boundary where SG cells, which express high levels of Crb, contact the surrounding epithelial cells, which express only low levels. The Röper group later described a molecular pathway through which Crb recruits p21‐activated kinase −1 (Pak1)—indirectly through Par6 recruitment of Cdc42, which recruits and activates Pak1. In turn, activated Pak1 phosphorylates Rok (Drosophila Rho‐kinase) in such a way to reduce its plasma membrane association [61]. The reduced membrane‐associated Rok reduces MyoII activation at the membrane. Thus, where Crb is high—within the SG and on the internal edges of SG cells at the border, MyoII is low. Where Crb is low—on the external edges of the border cells, MyoII is high.

Subsequently, the Andrew lab linked apical medial and junctional MyoII activation to Fkh‐mediated SG internalization [62]. They found that the apical medial pools of MyoII in the SG are activated downstream of Folded gastrulation (Fog), the same secreted signal that drives apical medial MyoII accumulation in the mesoderm. They further showed that Fkh is required for SG expression of fog [62]. Fog acts through one or more G‐protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) to ultimately activate apicomedial MyoII (Fog → GPCR → Gα12,13 → RhoGEF2 → Rho → Rok → MyoII) [63, 64, 65], which is essential for apical constriction (Figure 4) [39]. The same study revealed that the high levels of Crb protein observed in the SG also require Fkh [62].

More recent work from the Andrew lab focusing on an additional Fkh target—known as arc—has provided additional insight into the roles of MyoII and Crb in apical constriction and SG internalization [24]. During this process, ECad is evenly distributed around the apical periphery of all SG cells. In contrast, at the onset of SG morphogenesis, Crb and MyoII exhibit reciprocal anisotropy at apical junctions within individual SG cells: where junctional Crb is high, MyoII is low and vice versa. Crb intensity tends to be higher where two cells abut along junctions radial to the invagination pit [24]. Junctional MyoII is higher where two cells abut along junctions circumferential to the pit, as well as at vertices where three or more cells contact [24, 59] (Figure 3). Importantly, they found that arc is required for sustaining high levels of Crb at apical junctions and that with reduced arc there are higher levels of both apical medial and junctional MyoII in cells several diameters away from the invagination pit [24]. The consequence of arc loss and the increases in MyoII is that more cells invaginate at a time, giving rise to SG tubes with more cells in cross‐section and fewer along the length of the tube. Correspondingly, overexpression of arc leads to decreases in both apical medial and junctional pools of MyoII, increases in apical area, and fewer cells invaginating at a given time—creating SG tubes that have fewer cells in circumference with some SG cells often failing to internalize altogether. Importantly, the effects of Arc overexpression on MyoII accumulation are nearly completely attenuated simply by the removal of a single copy of crb, supporting a model wherein Arc's effects on MyoII are entirely through Crb.

These discoveries lead to a model wherein Crb and MyoII are biochemically and physically at odds and that it is this conflict that coordinates early stages of tube morphogenesis to determine final organ architecture (Figure 4). We propose that localized high level Crb limits MyoII accumulation through the same mechanism as described by the Röper group for the supracellular MyoII cable [61]—Crb indirect recruitment of Pak1, Pak1 phosphorylation of Rok, which reduces dwell time for Rok, and consequently MyoII, at the apical plasma membrane. Correspondingly, high levels of MyoII limits Crb accumulation through endocytosis of Crb‐containing vesicles [23]. In addition to attenuating each other's cortical localization, the two proteins have opposing effects on cortical membrane dynamics. The binding of the extracellular domain of Crb in one cell to the extracellular domain of Crb in neighboring cells [21, 22] as well as intracellular binding of Crb, through its FBD, to the FERM protein Moesin, stabilizes cell–cell interactions, opposing the MyoII‐induced shrinking of apical junctions. MyoII‐regulated junction contraction is required both to stabilize apical constriction between pulses of apical medial MyoII activation and to drive cell rearrangement. Apical medial MyoII drives apical constriction by first pulling the apical cortex inward and subsequently by “flowing” into and along the cell cortex, where it generates contractile forces at the apical plasma membrane to shrink overall apical area, likely through membrane bending and subsequent endocytosis of apical components—specifically Crb and ECad.

Crb‐MyoII Interactions in Other Drosophila Epithelia

Support for a role for Crb in stabilizing apical epithelial junctions and counteracting the effects of MyoII comes from several sources. Bajur et al. showed using quantitative fluorescence techniques that mutations in the Crb FERM‐binding domain that disrupt binding to Moesin result in increased junction tension (likely through increased MyoII activity) and increased ECad turnover [66]. The FERM protein Moesin links the Crb complex to the apical actin cytoskeleton through the recruitment of βheavy‐Spectrin [16]. Flores‐Benitez and Knust showed a similar requirement for the Crb FERM‐binding domain in also preventing over‐contraction of the amnioserosa during dorsal closure, a phenotype that could be rescued by overexpression of a MyoII (inactivating) phosphatase or the heterozygous loss of Rho1 (an upstream positive regulator of Rok and MyoII activity) [67]. Likewise, Osswald et al. showed that loss of aPKC (a kinase found in the SAC) results in increased apical constriction of the follicular epithelia (the monolayer of somatic cells surrounding the developing egg chamber of Drosophila oocytes) [68]. This group further showed that the same increased apical constriction phenotypes in follicle cells could be created by light‐induced apical recruitment of a Rho‐GEF, which would presumably activate Rho, Rok, and MyoII. These examples in Drosophila support the role for Rok and MyoII in promoting apical constriction and of the Crb complex in countering this activity.

Crb‐MyoII Interactions in the Mouse Intestinal Epithelia

Examples of a role for Crb family members in stabilizing epithelial junctions extend beyond Drosophila. A role for CRB3 (the shorter of the three Crb family members in vertebrates) in antagonizing MyoII contractility has been demonstrated in the intestinal epithelium of mice. Using primary murine colonic epithelial cells derived from a conditional CRB3‐knockout, Fan et al. showed that CRB3 loss results in hyper‐contractility of the junctional actomyosin network and impaired formation of AJs [69]. These authors further showed increased RhoA activity and increased junctional tension associated with CRB3 loss. Importantly, they could reverse the effects of CRB3 loss by inhibition of either Rock (mammalian Rho‐kinase) or MyoII. They further demonstrated that loss of the mammalian FERM protein Merlin (aka NF2) gives similar phenotypes to loss of CRB3, suggesting that Crb interactions through FERM proteins are generally required for its role in countering MyoII‐driven contractility [69, 70].

Crb‐MyoII Control of NB Ingression in Flies

The above examples highlight a role for the antagonizing activities of Crb and MyoII in coordinating apical constriction and epithelia integrity in both flies and mice. A related phenomenon is at play during NB delamination in developing Drosophila embryos [23, 71, 72]; indeed, studies on NB ingression—an EMT‐like process where future neuronal cells exit the ectoderm—greatly informs the model for how Crb and MyoII function during SG invagination. Prior to exiting the epithelia, NBs go through several rounds of apical constriction and expansion using the same ratcheting‐type mechanism as invaginating mesoderm and SG. Not surprisingly, the process is dependent on apicomedial and junctional pools of MyoII [71, 72]. Indeed, although apical constrictions were more tightly correlated with pulses of apicomedial MyoII, MyoII flow into both anterior‐posterior (AP) and dorsal‐ventral (DV) junctions was also apparent. Importantly, every round of MyoII‐driven constriction‐expansion was accompanied by Crb endocytosis and exocytosis. At the junctions of NBs and neighboring non‐ingressing cells (the NICs), Crb was planar polarized and notably enriched at AP versus DV junctions; in contrast, ECad showed the same concentration per surface unit around the entire apical domain of NBs. Importantly, the high levels of Crb appeared to protect the AP junctions from shrinking since MyoII‐dependent contractions and junctional loss occurred primarily on DV boundaries. Only during the final rounds of apical ratcheting, when the Crb complex was degraded, did the AP junctions begin to shrink and eventually disappear, allowing NBs to completely exit the epithelium.

Crb‐MyoII and Mesodermal Ingression During Mouse Gastrulation

Another related example wherein Crb‐MyoII anisotropy functions in delamination of cells from an epithelium is observed during mouse gastrulation when individual epiblast cells exit the epithelium to give rise to mesoderm. Indeed, individual ingressing epiblast cells undergo the same ratchet‐like apical constriction observed during Drosophila mesodermal and SG invagination and during NB ingression. Although not quite as cleanly demarcated as in the Drosophila SG [24], ingressing mesodermal cells exhibit similar anisotropic reciprocal enrichment of the CRB2 complex and the actinomyosin network: where CRB2 was high, Myosin heavy chain IIB (MHCIIB) was low and where MHCIIB was high, CRB2 was low [73, 74]. Some junctions had similarly low levels of both proteins. Interestingly, loss of crb2 gave slightly unexpected phenotypes in that although mesodermal precursors did undergo extensive apical constriction, the apical junctions never fully resolved and mesodermal cells only succeeded in exiting the epithelium by tearing their cell bodies away from the apical domains [73, 74]. Indeed, this phenotype was accompanied by significant reductions in junctional MHCIIB, ppMRLC, and F‐actin, suggesting a requirement for Crb proteins not only in concentrating and organizing the actomyosin network, but also in recruiting these molecules to the apical domain in the first place. This fits with another described role for Crb in recruiting a RhoGEF, known as Cysts, in ingressing Drosophila NBs to activate MyoII [75]. Thus, the relationship between Crb and MyoII is complex and not fully understood—although high levels of Crb can clearly negatively regulate MyoII junctional localization and activity, Crb is also required to recruit MyoII activators to these junctions in the first place [23, 73, 76, 77, 78].

Summary and Future Directions

In this review, we highlight the contrasting roles of Crb and MyoII in driving the cell shape changes and cell rearrangements required to form SGs of correct final dimensions [24]. We show that the same antagonistic relationships hold in other examples of epithelial morphogenesis and in situations where cells are exiting the epidermis to give rise to alternative cell types. We suspect that the examples described herein are just the tip of the iceberg and that a role for Crb‐MyoII anisotropy will feature across systems as a mechanism to provide spatial and temporal control on morphogenetic processes. Indeed, it is interesting that the major TF controlling SG morphogenesis—Fkh—not only upregulates the machinery driving apical membrane disruption (Fog) but it also drives expression of components that attenuate these disruptions (Crb and its positive regulator Arc).

These initial studies bring up several exciting, unanswered questions. Key among them is understanding how the spatial orientation of Crb‐MyoII anisotropies is established, particularly in the SG where Crb largely aligns with radial junctions and MyoII with circumferential ones [24, 59]. Moving forward, we must identify and characterize the specific Crb binding proteins and MyoII regulators directly driving this process. Furthermore, we need to uncover how Crb acts to simultaneously recruit the actomyosin cytoskeleton while also restricting its activation. We should also uncover where and how additional signaling components that have been implicated in SG invagination fit into the Crb‐MyoII conflict, for example, the Toll‐like receptor 18‐wheeler, which promotes SG invagination by inhibiting SG‐expressed RhoGAPs [79]. Beyond the SG, determining if and how this mechanism translates to morphogenesis of complex tissues—such as the mammalian lungs and kidneys—is critical. Finally, it will be fascinating to explore how external forces from surrounding tissues contribute to the initial Crb‐MyoII anisotropies and shape the final, physical outcome.
