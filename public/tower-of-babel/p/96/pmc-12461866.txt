The Take 5 Campaign: Effects of an Intervention to Promote Five-Day Durations of Antibiotic Therapy for Common Infections in Urgent Care
Authors: Timothy C Jenkins, Axel A Vazquez Deida, Lindsey E Fish, Michael J Breyer, Amy Quinones, Melody Zwakenberg, Cory K Hussain, Allison L Sabel, Katherine C Shihadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe implemented an intervention to promote 5-day durations of antibiotic therapy for common infections in urgent care. The objectives of this study were to evaluate the effects of this intervention on prescribed durations and clinical outcomes.MethodsThis was a quasi-experimental study involving 2 urgent care centers in an integrated health care system. The Take 5 campaign was a multifaceted intervention to promote adherence to institutional guidance for 5-day durations of therapy for skin infections, urinary tract infections, sinusitis, otitis media, pneumonia, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbations. Patients ≥18 years of age with an urgent care visit between January 2017 and December 2023 for 1 of the target infections were included. The primary outcome was the proportion of antibiotic prescriptions for ≤5 days before and after the intervention, as assessed by interrupted time-series analysis (ITS).ResultsBefore the intervention, there was a significant increasing trend in the proportion of prescriptions for ≤5 days. The intervention was associated with an immediate 10.7% increase in the proportion of prescriptions for ≤5 days (P < .001), with a subsequent sustained upward trend of 0.18% per month (P < .001). The aggregate proportion of prescriptions for ≤5 days increased from 57.5% before the intervention to 82.9% after implementation (P < .001). Rates of new antibiotic prescriptions and hospitalizations within 14 days were similar between the periods.ConclusionsThe Take 5 campaign was associated with increased adoption of 5-day antibiotic durations. Shortening durations of therapy appears to be an effective strategy to reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure in the urgent care setting.Keywords: antibiotic stewardship, duration of therapy, urgent care

In 2016, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimated that 30% of outpatient antibiotic prescriptions in the United States were inappropriate [1]. Urgent care centers in particular have been shown to have a high rate of inappropriate antibiotic use [2–4]. Many published interventions to improve antibiotic use—including those in the urgent care setting—have centered on preventing prescribing for acute respiratory conditions in which antibiotics are not indicated [3–8]. However, antibiotics are indicated, or may be indicated, for a substantial proportion of infections treated in the ambulatory care setting such as skin and soft tissue infections, urinary tract infections, pneumonia, and acute bacterial sinusitis [1]. Therefore, a focus solely on preventing prescriptions for indications where antibiotics are not indicated is not sufficient; interventions must also address optimizing antibiotic prescribing when antibiotics are indicated.

For most outpatient infections where antibiotics may be warranted, evidence suggests that antibiotic durations of ≤5 days are effective [9]. However, in clinical practice, prescribed durations are much longer. For example, in recent large-scale studies, 10-day prescriptions for conditions such as acute sinusitis and acute otitis media were the norm rather than the exception [10–13]. As such, there is substantial opportunity to reduce antibiotic exposure across ambulatory care settings through prescribing the shortest effective duration of therapy when an antibiotic is indicated.

Urgent care centers are a key clinical area where there is opportunity to shorten the duration of antibiotic prescriptions. In 1 study, nearly 40% of urgent care prescriptions were for longer durations than recommended in institutional guidance [14]. Promoting use of the shortest effective durations of therapy in urgent care settings when antibiotics are indicated is therefore likely an important adjunct to preventing unnecessary prescriptions for conditions where antibiotics are not indicated. We developed a quality improvement intervention to promote use of institutional guideline–recommended 5-day antibiotic durations for common infections—the Take 5 campaign. The objectives of this study were to evaluate the effects of this intervention on prescribed durations of therapy and overall antibiotic exposure.

METHODS

Study Setting and Population

Denver Health is an urban, academic, integrated health care system with a 550-bed teaching hospital, emergency department, 3 urgent care centers, 11 community health centers, and 20 school-based health clinics in the Denver metropolitan area. The intervention under study was implemented in 2 urgent care centers: 1 located on the main hospital campus (Urgent Care A) and 1 associated with an outlying community health center (Urgent Care B). At the time the intervention was developed, a third urgent care center in Denver was just opening, so it was not included.

Denver Health has had a formal antibiotic stewardship program since July 2008 [15]. Standardized guidance for treatment of common outpatient infections has been available via an institutional smartphone application (hereafter referred to as the “antibiotic stewardship app”) since 2014. We have previously demonstrated that this app is widely utilized by clinicians [16, 17] and perceived to be a useful clinical resource [17]. The app contains recommendations for 5 (or fewer) days of antibiotics for skin and soft tissue infections, urinary tract infections, and select respiratory tract infections such as pneumonia and acute bacterial sinusitis. The guidance for 5-day durations for these conditions predated the period of study in this report.

Take 5 Campaign Intervention

To increase adherence to the aforementioned institutional guidance for 5-day durations of therapy, we engaged leadership of the 2 urgent care centers (L.F., M.B.) to develop and implement the Take 5 campaign. This was a pragmatic quality improvement initiative centered on promoting the recommended 5-day durations for the following conditions: (1) cellulitis or drained skin abscesses; (2) acute cystitis; (3) acute bacterial sinusitis; (4) community-acquired pneumonia; (5) acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); (6) acute otitis media; and (7) acute pyelonephritis when a fluoroquinolone is used, as shown in Table 1.

The intervention began in late December 2019. Peer champion clinicians (M.Z., A.Q.) were engaged to promote 5-day durations of therapy. An infectious diseases (ID) pharmacist (K.S.) and ID pharmacy resident (A.V.D.) provided an in-person education session at Urgent Care B that included sharing data on baseline durations of therapy and the rationale and recommendations for 5-day antibiotic durations. At Urgent Care A, this information was distributed to clinicians via email. An infographic describing the Take 5 campaign (Supplementary Figure 1) was posted in clinician work areas at both sites. The severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic began <3 months after the start of the intervention and disrupted further planned education.

Clinician-specific feedback on prescribed durations of therapy with comparison to peers was performed at both sites. Urgent Care A opted to provide data to its clinicians only for skin and soft tissue infections. Beginning in January 2020, the average duration of therapy prescribed for skin infections (with a target of 5 days) was added as a new metric to an existing quality scorecard that was disseminated monthly to clinicians via email and posted on a visual management board. The scorecard included the names of all clinicians at the site and their average prescribed duration, allowing for peer comparison. Urgent Care B utilized the metric of overall proportion of antibiotic prescriptions for ≤5 days for all target conditions combined. Beginning in October 2020, a quarterly report with the names and data for all clinicians at the site was posted on a visual management board and referenced at monthly clinician meetings. Both sites provided this individualized feedback with peer comparison for ∼2 years. In August of 2020, antibiotic orders in our electronic health record were updated to include help text with duration of therapy recommendations for common infections. In addition, the quick buttons for days dispensed in the antibiotic orders were revised to match these duration of therapy recommendations.

Study Design

To determine the effects of the intervention, we performed a retrospective, quasi-experimental study including patients age 18 years or older with an in-person or virtual visit to 1 of the 2 participating urgent care centers who were prescribed an oral antibiotic for 1 of the Take 5 campaign conditions. During the study period, the recommended empiric first-line antibiotic for acute pyelonephritis was a 10-day course of oral cephalosporin to spare fluoroquinolone use. For this reason, acute pyelonephritis was not included for analysis in this evaluation. For the purposes of this study, the pre-intervention period was January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2019, during which time 5-day durations were recommended in the antibiotic stewardship app, but no other active interventions were in place (hereafter referred to as the “app-only period”). The Take 5 campaign intervention period was January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2023.

Data Collection

Urgent care visits for patients age 18 years or older with an International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), diagnosis code (Supplementary Table 1) for 1 of the Take 5 campaign conditions (excluding pyelonephritis) associated with an antibiotic prescription were included. There were no exclusion criteria. A given patient could have had >1 visit over the study period; thus, the unit of analysis was visits and not patients. For qualifying visits, a limited standardized data set was extracted electronically from the electronic health record including the following variables: demographic characteristics, qualifying ICD-10 diagnosis code, antibiotic and duration of therapy prescribed, and new antibiotic prescriptions and clinical visits within 14 days of the initial urgent care visit. As this was a programmatic evaluation of an institutional quality improvement initiative, the Denver Health Quality Improvement Review Committee (QUIRC) reviewed the proposed work and deemed it to be quality assurance.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the overall proportion of antibiotic prescriptions for the prespecified infections for ≤5 days (ie, guideline-concordant duration). Secondary outcomes included the proportion of prescriptions for ≤5 days by type of infection, by antibiotic, and by urgent care site, aggregate antibiotic-days prescribed by year, and the following clinical outcomes within 14 days of the initial urgent care visit: (1) a new antibiotic prescription for any reason; (2) a new antibiotic prescription for the same infection treated at the initial visit; (3) hospitalization for any reason; and (4) positive tests for Clostridioides difficile (within 30 days).

Statistical Analysis

Given that this was a programmatic analysis, there was no prespecified sample size calculation. The outcomes of interest were aggregated into 84 monthly intervals by date of initial urgent care visit. An interrupted time-series analysis was performed using segmented regression to estimate changes in outcomes that occurred after the start of the Take 5 campaign, controlling for pre-intervention trends and other autocorrelation. The models included a constant, a baseline slope term to control for secular trends, and terms estimating changes in the level and slope of outcome rates. Serial autocorrelation was assessed by the Durbin Watson (DW) statistic. Analyses were conducted using SAS statistical software (PROC AUTOREG; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). This was reviewed by the Denver Health Quality Improvement Review Committee and determined not to be human subjects research.

RESULTS

During the 7-year study period, there were 32 352 urgent care visits for the prespecified infections where an antibiotic was prescribed: 14 698 during the app-only period and 17 654 during the intervention period (Table 2). The median age at the visits (interquartile range [IQR]) was 38 (28–52) years, and 65% were female; demographic characteristics were similar between the app-only and intervention periods. Cellulitis or skin abscess and acute cystitis accounted for 40.6% and 35.6% of the infection types, respectively, with the remainder being respiratory tract infections. The location of urgent care visits and frequency of the types of infections were similar between the 2 study periods. The 5 most prescribed antibiotics were doxycycline, nitrofurantoin, cephalexin, amoxicillin, and amoxicillin-clavulanate (Table 3). Doxycycline was more commonly prescribed during the intervention period (19.1% vs 28.1% of prescriptions), while trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole was less commonly prescribed (12.8% vs 5.1%).

By interrupted time-series analysis, there was a significant increase in prescriptions for ≤5 days of 0.57% per month during the app-only period (t = 7.52; P < .001) (Figure 1A). After controlling for this pre-intervention trend, there was an estimated 10.7% increase in prescriptions for ≤5 days immediately after the start of the intervention (t = 5.30; P < .001), followed by an estimated increase of 0.18% per month over the course of the intervention period (t = −4.17; P < .001). Therefore, the intervention had a strong immediate effect and was associated with sustained increases in 5-day durations of therapy over time. The findings were similar when stratified by care location (Urgent Care A vs B) (Figure 1B); however, the immediate increase in prescriptions for ≤5 days was larger in Urgent Care B. During the app-only period, the aggregate proportion of prescriptions for ≤5 days was 57.5%; this increased to 82.9% during the intervention period (P < .001) (Table 4). In subgroup analyses of the individual infection types, statistically significant increases in durations of ≤5 days were observed during the app-only period for acute bacterial sinusitis, cellulitis or skin abscess, acute cystitis, and pneumonia (Figure 2; Supplementary Table 2). There was an immediate effect of the intervention on increasing durations of ≤5 days for sinusitis, cellulitis or skin abscess, COPD exacerbation, and acute cystitis. For all infection types, the changes in prescribed durations were sustained over the intervention period.

With respect to clinical outcomes, the aggregate proportions of new antibiotics prescribed for any reason within 14 days of the initial visit, new antibiotics prescribed for the same infection within 14 days of the initial visit, and positive tests for C. difficile were similar between periods (Table 4). The frequency of hospitalization within 14 days of the initial visit was lower during the intervention period.

DISCUSSION

Urgent care centers are a key driver of antibiotic utilization in the ambulatory care system and are central to efforts to improve antibiotic use. To our knowledge, this is the first description of a multifaceted intervention to shorten prescribed durations of antibiotic therapy for a broad range of infections encountered in urgent care. During the pre-intervention period when an antibiotic stewardship app with guidance on durations of therapy was in place, we observed increasing adherence to 5-day durations of therapy. After implementation of the Take 5 campaign, there was a significant immediate increase in adherence to 5-day durations and a sustained increasing trend over 3 years.

Historically, many antibiotic stewardship interventions in the outpatient setting have focused on reducing unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions for acute respiratory infections [3–8]. Indeed, such antibiotic stewardship approaches have been successful in the urgent care setting. Stenehjem and colleagues implemented a multifaceted intervention consisting of education, electronic health record tools, and clinician-specific prescribing feedback to reduce unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions for respiratory conditions in a large urgent care network [3]. This was associated with a decline in antibiotic prescriptions from 48% to 33% of visits. Our data suggest that shortening durations of therapy is also an effective approach to reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure in the urgent care setting. Our findings are notable for several reasons. First, they indicate that point-of-care access to institution-specific guidance for durations of therapy—in this case via a smartphone app—can on its own shorten durations of therapy and increase adherence to guideline-concordant durations. This suggests that providing such guidance is a fundamental antibiotic stewardship intervention and should serve as a component of any multifaceted intervention. Second, although the availability of the antibiotic stewardship app itself led to reductions in durations of therapy over time, the addition of the Take 5 campaign, which focused on promoting 5-day durations for key target conditions, had an immediate incremental benefit with respect to adherence to shorter courses. This is perhaps not surprising as active interventions have been shown to be more effective than passive interventions [18].

It is noteworthy that the intervention appeared to be more effective for some conditions than others. For example, we observed a marked increase in 5-day durations for acute otitis media but little change in durations for acute cystitis. This is likely because 5-day prescriptions of nitrofurantoin for acute cystitis have been recommended at Denver Health for >15 years with high adherence rates to this guidance, and as such, there was less opportunity to improve practice for this condition. In contrast, 5-day durations for acute otitis media before the intervention were uncommon; thus, there may have been more opportunity for change with the Take 5 campaign. It is also of interest that for some conditions, the slope of the increase in the proportion of 5-day prescriptions during the intervention period was not as large as it was during the app-only period. We suspect that this is because the opportunity for improvement was diminishing due to higher adherence to the app guidance over time. One may therefore question whether the same results ultimately may have been achieved over a longer period without any specific intervention other than maintaining the app. Our study was not designed to answer this question; however, it seems apparent given the immediate increases in 5-day prescriptions that the Take 5 intervention at least shortened the time it would have taken to achieve such improvements.

Our intervention was unique in that it did not attempt to alter whether an antibiotic was prescribed or which antibiotic was prescribed, but simply the duration of therapy prescribed. In aggregate, our intervention resulted in >6000 fewer antibiotic-days dispensed per year during the Take 5 campaign—a >20% decline. This represents a substantial reduction in overall antibiotic exposure, and widespread implementation could make a substantial contribution to further reducing unnecessary outpatient antibiotic use. It may also lead to potential benefits such as fewer antibiotic-related adverse events, less inconvenience and expense for patients, and lower selective pressure for the development of antimicrobial resistance.

Our study has several limitations. First, it was performed within 2 urgent care centers of an academic, integrated health care system, which limits its generalizability. Second, given the pragmatic nature of this quality improvement initiative, the 2 sites implemented components of the intervention bundle differently. In addition, given that the intervention was a bundle, it is unknown which components were necessary or most effective at changing prescribing practices. Third, because of the quasi-experimental design, our results do not prove that the intervention itself changed prescribing practices; however, the consistency of the observed changes in durations between the 2 urgent care centers and the individual target infections are highly suggestive that the intervention had the intended effect. We are not aware of other interventions that were implemented during the intervention period that would have led to shorter durations of therapy; however, as stated previously, we cannot rule out that the improvements during the intervention period would not have ultimately been achieved with the ongoing availability of the antibiotic stewardship app alone. Our data at a minimum suggest that the Take 5 campaign accelerated the uptake of this guidance. Fourth, we used ICD-10 diagnosis codes to identify the target infections. It is therefore likely that some included visits were misclassified, although one would expect this to occur with similar frequency between periods. Some infections may also have been complicated or refractory, where a longer duration of therapy may have been appropriate. Thus, adherence to 5-day durations for uncomplicated infections may have been even higher than these data reflect. Finally, the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic began <3 months after the start of our intervention. We did not account for potential SARS-CoV-2 co-diagnoses during the intervention period. Although this was a potential confounder of our study, it is unlikely that the pandemic would have impacted durations of antibiotic therapy prescribed, particularly for conditions such as skin and soft tissue infections and urinary tract infections. Thus, we suspect that the pandemic had little impact on our primary outcome. It is notable that despite only having just introduced the intervention when the pandemic began, the Take 5 campaign still had a clinically meaningful effect on shortening durations of therapy. This suggests that providers may be particularly receptive to using shorter courses of antibiotic therapy, and interventions to promote shorter durations may be among the easier outpatient antibiotic stewardship interventions to obtain clinician buy-in.

In summary, a campaign to promote institutional guideline–recommended 5-day durations of therapy for common infections treated in urgent care centers was associated with an immediate and sustained increase in prescribing of shorter durations as compared with the availability of institutional prescribing guidance in a smartphone app alone. Promoting use of the shortest effective duration of therapy for infections where antibiotics are indicated or may be indicated appears to be an effective antibiotic stewardship approach in the urgent care setting and may complement efforts to prevent unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions for acute respiratory infections.
