Effect of remimazolam on postoperative delirium in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Suihan Xu, Qiuyan Wan, Tao Li, Yunfei Shu, Yinhao Guo, Jun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOur study aimed to compare remimazolam, midazolam, and no sedative on postoperative delirium (POD) incidence, cognitive function, inflammation, and recovery in elderly colorectal cancer (CRC) laparoscopic radical resection patients.MethodsIn this single-center, randomized, double-blind trial, 60 patients aged ≥ 60 years were assigned to the Control, Remimazolam, or Midazolam groups (n = 20 each). Anesthesia included remimazolam (6 mg/kg/h for 5 min), midazolam (0.04 mg/kg) before induction, or no sedative. Primary outcome was POD incidence (diagnosed using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) algorithm. Secondary outcomes included CAM/Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores, serum interleukin-6 (IL-6) and S-100 protein (S-100) levels, intraoperative metrics, and recovery times. Data were analyzed using chi-square, ANOVA, and Kruskal-Wallis tests (P < 0.05).ResultsRemimazolam significantly reduced POD incidence (5%) compared to the Control (30%) and Midazolam (40%) groups (P = 0.021). At postoperative day 7, compared to the Midazolam group, the Remimazolam group showed both significantly lower CAM scores (12.00 [11.00–13.00] vs. 13.50 [12.00–20.75]) and higher MMSE scores (29.00 [28.00–29.00] vs. 28.00 [21.25–28.00]) (both P = 0.029). Compared to the Control group, the Remimazolam group had numerically lower CAM scores and higher MMSE scores, but these differences were not statistically significant (P = 0.340 for both comparisons). Serum IL-6 levels in the Remimazolam group were consistently and significantly lower than those in the Midazolam group at all measured postoperative time points (Postoperative 1, 3, and 7; all P < 0.05). Compared to the Control group, the Remimazolam group also exhibited significantly lower IL-6 levels on postoperative 1 and postoperative 3 (both P = 0.005), but this difference was no longer significant by postoperative 7 (P = 1.000). Awakening (8.00 [6.00–10.00] min) and extubation times (2.50 [1.25–4.00] min) were significantly shorter with remimazolam (P < 0.001 and P = 0.019, respectively). No significant differences were observed in intraoperative metrics.ConclusionRemimazolam reduced POD incidence and demonstrated faster postoperative recovery in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic CRC surgery. While remimazolam showed cognitive and anti-inflammatory benefits relative to midazolam, its advantages over the control group were less pronounced. The most consistent clinical advantage was faster postoperative recovery, as evidenced by significantly shorter awakening and extubation times. These findings suggest that remimazolam is a promising alternative, particularly compared with midazolam, warranting further multicenter validation.Clinical trial numberChiCTR2000034677; Registration Date: 2020/07/14.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with approximately 152,810 new cases and 53,010 deaths estimated in the United States in 2024 [1]. The Estimated numbers of new invasive colorectal cancer cases and deaths ≥ 65 years is 85,260 in 2023 [2] with surgery being the primary treatment [3]. Postoperative delirium (POD) frequently occurs in older adults after major surgery, with incidence rates ranging from 10% to 50% [4–6]. POD is associated with prolonged hospital stays and cognitive decline, making its prevention a critical focus [4, 6]. Risk factors include advanced age, prolonged anesthesia, and neuroinflammatory responses, potentially exacerbated by anesthetic agents [5]. Furthermore, POD prevention is now recognized to involve a broader set of perioperative factors, including structured multicomponent interventions, depth of anesthesia monitoring, avoidance of benzodiazepines, and optimal pain management [7].

Benzodiazepines, such as midazolam, are widely used for sedation in surgical procedures but may increase POD risk due to their effects on gamma-aminobutyric acid-ergic (GABAergic) pathways [8, 9]. Remimazolam is an ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine, and has shown promise in reducing adverse events, including respiratory depression and hypotension, in procedures like gastrointestinal endoscopy and general anesthesia [10, 11]. Compared with midazolam, remimazolam offers faster onset of sedation, quicker recovery, and lower rescue medication requirements, with similarly low rates of hypoxia [12, 13]. However, its impact on POD in elderly patients undergoing colorectal surgery remains underexplored. Recent studies suggest that anesthetic choice influences postoperative cognitive outcomes, with dexmedetomidine and regional anesthesia showing variable effects on delirium incidence [3, 10]. Given the increasing burden of CRC and the vulnerability of elderly patients to POD, identifying anesthetics that minimize cognitive complications is essential.

Older patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery represent a distinct perioperative-risk population in whom recovery quality, complication prevention, and tailored perioperative strategies are of particular importance [14]. This single-center, double-blind, randomized controlled trial (RCT) aimed to compare the effects of remimazolam, midazolam, and no sedative (control) on POD incidence, cognitive function, inflammatory markers, and recovery metrics in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery for colorectal cancer. We hypothesized that remimazolam would reduce POD incidence and improve postoperative outcomes compared to midazolam and control groups.

Method

Study design and participants

This randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted at Mianyang Central Hospital from March 2021 to April 2022. A total of 60 patients aged 60 years or older, scheduled for elective laparoscopic radical colorectal cancer surgery were enrolled and equally allocated to the Control, Remimazolam, and Midazolam groups (n = 20 each) using computer-generated randomization. Inclusion criteria encompassed American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grades I–III, absence of significant organ impairment, and preoperative cognitive assessments with the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) algorithm and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores ≥ 27. All surgeries were laparoscopic radical resections for colorectal cancer without diverting stoma creation. Exclusion criteria comprised emergency surgery, severe comorbidities (e.g., heart, lung, liver, or kidney failure), history of mental illness or psychotropic drug use, and refusal to provide informed consent. Routine specialist mental health evaluation by a neurologist or psychologist was not performed. Instead, the MMSE was used as a validated screening tool to exclude patients with preoperative cognitive impairment, consistent with standard practice in this field [15, 16]. This RCT was conducted as an investigator-initiated ancillary study under the registered clinical trial ChiCTR2000034677. The original trial protocol encompassed anesthetic comparisons in abdominal surgery. The present study represents a pre-specified subgroup analysis of colorectal cancer patients drawn from this broader abdominal surgery trial; accordingly, the colorectal cancer cohort was ethically and methodologically covered within the original protocol, and no post hoc patient recruitment was undertaken. All procedures were approved by the ethics board of hospital (approval No. S20210321-01), and all participants provided written informed consent before joining the study.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the chi‑square test for comparing proportions among three independent groups. With a significance level of α = 0.05, a power of 0.80 (1‑β), and degrees of freedom v = 2 (where v = number of groups − 1), we assumed a maximum POD incidence of 50% (Midazolam group) and a minimum of 8% (Remimazolam group) based on prior literature [6, 17–20]. The corresponding non‑centrality parameter λ was 9.63. The calculated sample size was 18 patients per group. To accommodate a maximum dropout rate of 10%, the study established a sample size of 20 participants per group, yielding a total of 60 participants.

Randomization, blinding and quality control

Participants were allocated to one of three groups—Control, Remimazolam, or Midazolam (20 per group)—through a computer-generated randomization sequence. Randomization was performed prior to enrollment to ensure balanced allocation across groups. This trial utilized a double-blind approach, concealing group allocations and medication details from participants, surgeons, outcome evaluators, and statisticians. The study coordinator managed group allocations and prepared sealed envelopes containing randomization details. Before surgery, the attending anesthesiologist opened the sealed envelope and administered anesthesia according to the assigned protocol, overseeing induction, maintenance, and recovery documentation. Outcome assessors, blinded to group assignments, recorded preoperative baseline data, intraoperative vital signs, anesthesia drug doses, surgical metrics, and cognitive assessments (MMSE and CAM scores). The study coordinator separated results by group, and a blinded statistician performed data analysis to minimize bias and ensure data integrity.

Anesthesia protocol

All patients received total intravenous anesthesia combined with nerve block. Anesthesia induction was performed using sufentanil (0.3–0.5 µg/kg), propofol (1.5–2.0 mg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.15–0.2 mg/kg). The Remimazolam group received remimazolam tosilate (6 mg/kg/h for 5 min) based on a previous study [21] in elderly patients. The Midazolam group received midazolam (0.04 mg/kg) before induction, and the Control group received no sedative. Anesthesia was maintained with propofol (4–6 mg/kg/h) and remifentanil (0.1–0.2 µg/kg/min), titrated to achieve a Sedation Sensitivity Index (IOC1) of 40–60 and a Pain Sensitivity Index (IOC2) of 30–50. Postoperative pain relief was administered through patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) using sufentanil (2 µg/kg) and dexmedetomidine (4 µg/kg) in 100 mL saline, configured for a 0.5 mL/h continuous infusion, a 2 mL bolus, and a 15-minute lockout interval.

Intraoperative fluid management followed a goal-directed fluid therapy (GDFT) protocol, with Ringer’s lactate infusion titrated to maintain a mildly positive fluid balance. Allogeneic red blood cell transfusion was indicated when the hematocrit fell below 26% or the hemoglobin level fell below 8 g/dL in the presence of ongoing surgical bleeding or hemodynamic instability, and was additionally guided by intraoperative blood loss, urine output, arterial blood gas analysis (including lactate and hemoglobin levels), and hematocrit. No patient in this study required blood transfusion.

It should be emphasized that the present trial was designed to compare the effect of a brief pre-induction sedative strategy rather than a complete anesthetic technique, as all three groups subsequently received an identical core general anesthetic regimen (propofol/remifentanil) and the same postoperative PCIA background. Any observed differences in outcomes can therefore be attributable specifically to the presence or type of pre-induction sedative agent.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the incidence of postoperative delirium (POD), diagnosed using the CAM algorithm. The CAM is a standardized diagnostic tool that assesses four features: acute onset and fluctuating course, inattention, disorganized thinking, and altered level of consciousness; POD is diagnosed when features 1 and 2 are present along with either feature 3 or 4. POD was assessed daily at a standardized time (between 9:00 AM and 11:00 AM) during the first 7 postoperative days by trained personnel blinded to group assignment. Secondary endpoints encompassed CAM and MMSE scores (evaluated on preoperative day 1 and postoperative days 1, 3, and 7). The MMSE is a 30-point cognitive screening instrument assessing orientation, registration, attention, recall, and language, with higher scores indicating better cognitive function. Serum levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and S-100 protein (S-100) protein (quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay), intraoperative parameters (heart rate, mean arterial pressure, oxygen saturation), anesthesia depth indices (IOC1, IOC2), medication dosages (propofol, sufentanil), surgical parameters (blood loss, fluid administration, duration of surgery), and recovery metrics. (awakening time: from anesthesia cessation to eye opening; extubation time: from anesthesia cessation to tracheal tube removal). Postoperative pain intensity was assessed at rest using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS, 0–10), where 0 represents no pain and 10 represents the worst pain imaginable. Data were collected prospectively using standardized forms to ensure consistency.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical data, such as POD incidence, were evaluated with the chi-square test and presented as frequencies (percentages). The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data following a normal distribution were analyzed with one-way ANOVA and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Non-normally distributed data were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test and expressed as median [interquartile range (IQR), P25–P75]. For significant Kruskal-Wallis findings, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test with Bonferroni adjustment. A P-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Result

Baseline characteristics

As in Table 1, a total of 60 patients were randomly allocated in this RCT, equally allocated to the Control, Remimazolam, and Midazolam groups (n = 20 each). The mean ages were 72.00 ± 7.29 years for the Control group, 68.65 ± 5.88 years for the Remimazolam group, and 70.95 ± 7.77 years for the Midazolam group (P = 0.312). The ASA classifications were similarly distributed across the three groups (P = 0.803): ASA II was assigned to 14, 12, and 13 patients in the Control, Remimazolam, and Midazolam groups, respectively, and ASA III to 6, 8, and 7 patients. Education levels varied slightly, with most patients having primary or junior high education, but no significant differences were observed (P = 0.448). Postoperative pain was consistent across groups (mean score: 1.95 ± 0.76 in Control and Midazolam, 1.95 ± 0.69 in Remimazolam; P = 1.000). There was no significant difference among the three groups.

Surgical and anesthetic characteristics

POD incidence

As shown in Fig. 1, the Remimazolam group had a notably lower incidence of postoperative delirium (POD) at 5%, compared to 30% in the Control group and 40% in the Midazolam group (P < 0.05). Given the small number of absolute events, these results should be interpreted as a promising signal rather than definitive evidence of clinical superiority. They suggest that remimazolam may be associated with a reduced risk of POD in older patients undergoing major surgical procedures, warranting confirmation in larger trials.

Cognitive scores

Assessments of the CAM and MMSE were conducted preoperatively (day 1) and on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7. No significant differences were found in CAM scores preoperatively (P = 0.117), or on postoperative days 1 (P = 0.198) and 3 (P = 0.110). On postoperative day 7, the Remimazolam group had significantly lower CAM scores (median [IQR]: 12.00 [11.00–13.00]) compared to the Midazolam (13.50 [12.00-20.75], P < 0.05), but did not differ significantly from the Control group (13.00 [12.00-18.75], P > 0.05) (overall P = 0.021). (Fig. 2A) For MMSE scores, no differences were found preoperatively (P = 0.088) or on postoperative day 1 (P = 0.497). On postoperative day 3, the Remimazolam group showed significantly higher MMSE scores (29.00 [28.00–29.00]) compared to the Control group (27.50 [24.00–28.00], P = 0.024), but the difference compared to the Midazolam group (27.00 [15.00-28.75], P = 0.158) was not statistically significant. On postoperative day 7, the Remimazolam group had significantly higher MMSE scores (29.00 [28.00–29.00]) compared to the Midazolam group (28.00 [21.25-28.00], P = 0.029), but not compared to the Control group (28.00 [22.50–29.00], P = 0.340). Overall group differences reached statistical significance on postoperative days 3 (P = 0.008) and 7 (P = 0.009). (Fig. 2B) These findings indicate that, compared to midazolam, remimazolam was associated with improved cognitive recovery by postoperative day 7. However, no significant cognitive benefit was detected between the remimazolam and control groups at this time point.

Inflammatory markers

Measurements of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and S-100 protein (S-100) were conducted preoperatively (day 1) and on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7. No significant differences in IL-6 levels were found preoperatively (P = 0.328). On postoperative day 1, serum IL-6 levels were significantly lower in the Remimazolam group (median [IQR]: 12.70 [9.07–18.55] pg/mL) compared to both the Control group (25.72 [18.78–44.09] pg/mL; P = 0.005) and the Midazolam group (35.70 [16.98–72.48] pg/mL; P = 0.005); no significant difference was observed between the Control and Midazolam groups (P = 0.618). On postoperative day 3, IL-6 levels remained significantly lower in the Remimazolam group (23.75 [18.07–36.16] pg/mL) compared to the Control (45.83 [31.97–68.34] pg/mL; P = 0.005) and Midazolam groups (61.56 [28.61–129.20] pg/mL; P = 0.034), with no significant difference between the latter two groups (P = 1.000). On postoperative day 7, the Remimazolam group (39.13 [26.08–58.31] pg/mL) showed significantly lower IL-6 levels compared to the Midazolam group (81.47 [49.48–194.03] pg/mL; P = 0.005), but not compared to the Control group (46.66 [37.31–120.69] pg/mL; P = 1.000); the difference between the Control and Midazolam groups was also significant (P = 0.005). The overall group differences were significant on postoperative days 1 (P < 0.001), 3 (P < 0.001), and 7 (P = 0.008). (Fig. 3A) No significant differences were observed in serum S-100 protein concentrations among groups at any measured time point (P = 0.419–0.894). (Fig. 3B) These findings indicated that remimazolam was associated with a significantly attenuated early postoperative inflammatory response compared to both the control and midazolam groups, although this difference relative to the control group diminished by postoperative day 7. However, the absence of any between-group differences in S-100 protein levels at all time points suggests that these data support a possible anti-inflammatory association rather than a demonstrated neuroprotective mechanism.

Recovery and other metrics

The Remimazolam group demonstrated significantly faster recovery, with a median awakening time of 8.00 [6.00–10.00] min, which was significantly shorter than both the Control group (13.50 [9.50–17.50] min, P = 0.001) and the Midazolam group (14.00 [10.00–20.00] min, P = 0.004). Overall, both recovery metrics showed significant differences across groups (P < 0.05). In contrast, intraoperative vital signs—including heart rate, mean arterial pressure, and oxygen saturation—remained stable and comparable among the three groups at all measured time points (pre-anesthesia, intubation, and extubation; all P > 0.05). These results indicate that the accelerated recovery observed with remimazolam was not attributable to differences in intraoperative hemodynamic management. (Table 3) Taken together, the shorter awakening and extubation times represent a clinically meaningful practical benefit of remimazolam in this elderly surgical population.

Discussion

This RCT showed that remimazolam significantly reduced the incidence of POD (5%) compared to the control group (30%) and the midazolam group (40%) among elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery. Additionally, remimazolam improved cognitive recovery (higher MMSE scores, lower CAM scores), attenuated systemic inflammation (lower IL-6 levels), and facilitated faster postoperative awakening and extubation.

Our study’s strength lies in its focus on elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery, a population with distinct surgical and neurocognitive challenges due to advanced age and cancer-related stress. Unlike prior studies, which primarily explored remimazolam in orthopedic [17–19, 22], cardiovascular [23], or gastrointestinal procedures [24], our trial specifically addresses the colorectal cancer context, where POD incidence is notably high (10–50%) [4, 6]. This targeted investigation, which specifically isolates the effect of a brief pre-induction sedative strategy rather than a complete anesthetic technique, reveals remimazolam’s potential to address a critical gap in perioperative care for this understudied surgical cohort.

The observed reduction in POD incidence (5%) is notably lower than rates reported in other settings. For instance, Fujimoto et al. [18] reported a POD incidence of 14.8% with remimazolam in elderly hip fracture patients, compared to 29.5% with other agents, while Yang et al. [17] found comparable POD rates between remimazolam (15.6%) and propofol (12.4%) in orthopedic surgery. Aoki et al. [23] observed no significant difference in cardiovascular surgery (30.3% vs. 26.6%). These variations highlight the influence of surgical type and comparator agents on outcomes. In contrast, our study’s lower POD incidence may reflect the synergy of remimazolam’s ultra-short-acting pharmacokinetics and the less invasive nature of laparoscopic colorectal surgery, which may reduce neuroinflammatory triggers compared to open or more invasive procedures. Regarding the markedly lower POD incidence with remimazolam, several factors suggest a potential protective effect. First, its superior hemodynamic stability reduces hypotensive episodes, a known delirium risk factor [4, 8]. Second, rapid clearance promotes cleaner emergence, limiting sedative exposure. Emerging evidence also indicates anti-inflammatory properties may mitigate the neuroinflammatory response linked to POD [22, 25]. Thus, the lower POD incidence observed with remimazolam suggests it may offer a protective advantage over both midazolam and no sedation in this high-risk population.

The improved cognitive outcomes (higher MMSE scores on postoperative days 3 and 7) in our remimazolam group may suggest a potential cognitive benefit, although the current study was not designed to establish neuroprotective mechanisms and any such interpretation should be considered preliminary. The observed cognitive advantages may be linked to its rapid clearance and minimal GABAergic overinhibition [9], but larger studies with formal mechanistic endpoints are required to confirm this hypothesis. This aligns with Wu et al. [22], who reported enhanced frontal alpha-band power and reduced postoperative cognitive dysfunction with remimazolam in lower extremity fracture surgery. However, our study extends these findings by demonstrating cognitive benefits in a colorectal cancer cohort, where systemic inflammation and surgical stress are pronounced. The lower IL-6 levels in our remimazolam group further support a potential anti-inflammatory mechanism, consistent with Lee et al. [25], who noted reduced postoperative NLR/CRP levels with remimazolam-based anesthesia. Unlike previous studies, our trial measured IL-6 longitudinally (days 1, 3, and 7), providing a more comprehensive profile of remimazolam’s anti-inflammatory effects in the postoperative period. It should be noted, however, that S-100 protein levels did not differ among groups at any time point, which limits mechanistic claims of direct neuroprotection. The observed IL-6 pattern therefore supports a possible anti-inflammatory association, but the current study design does not permit causal inference regarding a specific pathway to delirium prevention.

Remimazolam’s faster recovery metrics (awakening time: 8.0 vs. 14.0 min; extubation time: 2.5 vs. 4.0 min compared to midazolam) underscore its clinical utility in enhancing perioperative efficiency. These findings are consistent with rapid emergence reported by Choi et al. [26] in thyroid surgery. Rather than invoking neuroprotective mechanisms, we interpret these recovery advantages primarily as a reflection of remimazolam’s favorable pharmacokinetic profile, namely its ultra-short-acting clearance and rapid elimination, which facilitates faster attainment of extubation criteria and may improve early postoperative recovery quality [27–29]. As observed across various minimally invasive surgical settings, small differences in perioperative management can translate into measurable improvements in patient-centered recovery quality, even when major surgical variables remain unchanged [30]. This patient-centered recovery benefit may represent the most clinically robust finding of the current trial. However, our study demonstrates these benefits in elderly colorectal cancer patients, a group prone to prolonged recovery due to age-related comorbidities. Notably, Zhang et al. [31] reported prolonged post-anesthesia care unit stays due to re-sedation with remimazolam, suggesting that our protocol’s dosing (6 mg/kg/h for 5 min) and avoidance of re-sedation complications may offer a practical advantage. This optimized dosing strategy warrants further exploration to standardize remimazolam use in high-risk populations.

The notable effects observed with only a brief pre-induction dose of remimazolam are of interest and may relate to its potent and rapid action on GABAA receptors during the early perioperative period [22]. Even short-term exposure may transiently modulate the early surgical stress response and systemic inflammation, and we observed associations with outcomes like delirium and IL-6 levels [18]. However, the study design does not permit causal inference, and these mechanistic interpretations remain speculative. Consistent with this possibility, remimazolam has been reported to exhibit anti-inflammatory properties independent of prolonged infusion [25]. One potential explanation is that brief GABAergic modulation during periods of heightened sympathetic and inflammatory activation may attenuate pro-inflammatory pathways through mechanisms that do not require sustained drug exposure [9, 22]. Further studies specifically designed to test these mechanistic hypotheses are needed.

In contrast to Lee et al. [19], who reported an increased POD risk with remimazolam compared to dexmedetomidine in spinal anesthesia (OR: 2.21), our study found a clear advantage over midazolam and no sedation. This discrepancy may stem from differences in anesthetic protocols (total intravenous anesthesia vs. spinal anesthesia) or surgical contexts, highlighting the need for tailored anesthetic strategies based on procedure type. Wang et al.’s [32] meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (OR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.23–1.68) supports remimazolam’s favorable safety profile but notes variability across studies, reinforcing our study’s contribution in clarifying its efficacy in colorectal surgery. More recently, Zhang et al. [33] conducted a large RCT (n = 370) comparing remimazolam-based versus propofol-based general anesthesia in elderly patients undergoing major abdominal surgery and found no significant difference in POD incidence (17.1% vs. 19.7%, P = 0.578). The apparent discrepancy may be explained by the choice of comparator: remimazolam’s advantage is pronounced against midazolam, but attenuated when compared against propofol. This further supports our interpretation that remimazolam’s primary clinical value in POD prevention lies in its superiority over midazolam rather than a universal protective effect.

Regarding the dosing regimen, the mg/kg induction dose of remimazolam was higher than that of midazolam. This is due to remimazolam’s ultra-short-acting metabolism (clearance approximately 3 times faster than midazolam) and rapid onset/recovery profile, which necessitate a higher mg/kg exposure to achieve reliable sedative depth and hemodynamic stability rather than strict mg/kg equivalence [11, 12]. The present dose was selected to optimize reliable induction while prioritizing hemodynamic protection in elderly patients.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center design, modest sample size, and 7-day follow-up period limit the generalizability of our findings and preclude long-term neurocognitive assessment. Second, the control group received no sedative pre-induction, which, while enabling isolation of the sedative effect, may not reflect routine clinical practice where premedication is commonly administered; this should be considered when extrapolating our results. Furthermore, the trial was designed to compare pre-induction sedative strategies rather than complete anesthetic techniques, as all groups shared the same maintenance and analgesia regimen; the findings should therefore be interpreted within this specific scope. Third, the dosing regimens for remimazolam and midazolam were selected based on equipotent sedative effects guided by pharmacokinetic profiles rather than equal mg/kg body weight. Although clinically relevant, this approach introduces a substantial confounder: the observed differences may be attributable not only to the drugs themselves but also, in part, to the specific doses chosen. Future studies employing formal dose-equivalence designs are needed to disentangle these effects. Fourth, while we observed differences in postoperative IL-6 levels, the study was not designed to establish a causal mechanistic link between inflammation and delirium. Finally, the absence of comparative arms involving other sedative agents, such as dexmedetomidine or propofol, limits further interpretation of remimazolam’s relative efficacy. Future multicenter trials with larger, more diverse cohorts are warranted to validate our findings, optimize dosing protocols, and evaluate long-term neurocognitive outcomes. In addition, mechanistic studies incorporating comprehensive biomarker panels and neuroimaging are needed to elucidate the pathways through which remimazolam may exert anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects. Comparative effectiveness research against other anesthetic agents in colorectal surgery will also help define remimazolam’s role in perioperative care for elderly high-risk populations.

Conclusion

In elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal surgery, remimazolam reduced POD incidence compared to both midazolam and no sedation. While remimazolam showed cognitive and anti-inflammatory benefits relative to midazolam, its advantages over the control group were less pronounced and did not consistently reach statistical significance across all time points. Its most consistent clinical advantage was faster postoperative recovery, as evidenced by significantly shorter awakening and extubation times. These preliminary findings suggest that remimazolam is a promising alternative, particularly compared with midazolam, warranting further multicenter validation with larger cohorts and extended neurocognitive follow-up.
