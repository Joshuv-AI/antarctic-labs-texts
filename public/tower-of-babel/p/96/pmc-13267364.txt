TRPC6 mediates goblet cell differentiation in COPD

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGoblet cell metaplasia is a pathological feature of airway remodelling in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Basal cell clones with goblet cell metaplasia demonstrate high transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily C member 6 (TRPC6) expression, but its biological function remains unclear.MethodsTRPC6 expression was analysed by immunostaining in lung tissues and airway basal cells from patients with COPD, and in the airways of mice exposed to cigarette smoke or interleukin-13. In vitro, the role of TRPC6 was assessed by TRPC6 overexpression and depletion in air–liquid interface cultures of human bronchial epithelial cells. In vivo, TRPC6 function was tested by airway TRPC6 depletion and TRPC6 inhibition in mice exposed to interleukin-13. NOTCH signalling was confirmed as a downstream pathway of TRPC6 by performing rescue experiments.ResultsTRPC6 expression was increased in the airway epithelium of patients with COPD and in interleukin-13-exposed mice. TRPC6 overexpression in basal cells from patients without COPD promoted their differentiation into goblet cells, while TRPC6 depletion in basal cells from patients with COPD suppressed goblet cell metaplasia in air–liquid interface cultures. TRPC6 function in suppressed goblet cell metaplasia was confirmed in TRPC6 depletion mice and TRPC6 inhibitor-treated mice. NOTCH signalling increased in interleukin-13-exposed control mice and human bronchial epithelial cell air–liquid interface cultures and decreased after TRPC6 inhibition.ConclusionsTRPC6 regulated basal cell differentiation, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target in COPD. The in vivo experiments used mice exposed to interleukin-13, so its clinical potential requires clarification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03645-y.Keywords: Airway basal cells, Cell differentiation, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Goblet cell metaplasia, Mucus hypersecretion, TRPC6

Introduction

Epithelial goblet cell metaplasia is the main pathological change seen during airway remodelling in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Mucus hypersecretion caused by goblet cell metaplasia is the main cause of persistent respiratory symptoms in patients with COPD [1, 2]. Goblet cell metaplasia not only signifies the occurrence of COPD, but it is also an important component of the pathophysiological process [3]. Mucus hypersecretion can cause airway obstruction, increase infection risk, and enhance airway inflammation and oxidative stress damage [4]. Critically, excessive mucus can lead to mucus plug formation, occluding the small airways. Recent clinical evidence indicates that mucus plugs, identifiable on computed tomography imaging, are strongly associated with increased mortality and a higher exacerbation risk in patients with COPD, representing a distinct and treatable clinical phenotype [5]. Currently, treatments for mucus hypersecretion are limited. Bronchodilators, the cornerstone drug class for COPD treatment, reduce mucus hypersecretion and improve mucociliary clearance, but bronchodilators do not reduce goblet cell hyperplasia. Mucolytics are another treatment option for COPD; however, they also do not reduce goblet cell hyperplasia or mucus hypersecretion [6, 7]. Therefore, it is necessary to identify new targets for the treatment of goblet cell metaplasia in COPD.

Airway basal cell dysfunction is considered the core event that triggers early abnormalities in COPD [8]. The airway basal cells of patients with COPD exhibit abnormal proliferation and loss of differentiation ability, eventually leading to goblet cell metaplasia and squamous metaplasia in the airway epithelium [9, 10]. Therefore, airway basal cells are a potential target for the treatment of abnormal airway remodelling in COPD [11]. Single-cell transcriptome sequencing of the airway epithelium of patients with COPD revealed huge heterogeneity in the basal cell transcriptome. Transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily C member 6 (TRPC6) has been identified as a marker of the basal cell subgroup with goblet cell metaplasia [12]. However, it is not clear whether TRPC6 plays a biological role in this basal cell subgroup.

TRPC6 is a member of the canonical TRP family of non-selective cation channels, characterised by its high permeability to calcium. Structurally, it functions as a receptor-operated channel, specifically activated by diacylglycerol in response to phospholipase C-coupled receptor stimulation. Beyond its chemical activation, TRPC6 exhibits mechanosensitive properties, allowing it to transduce physical stimuli, such as airway stretch or vascular pressure, into intracellular calcium signals [13]. Evidence suggests that TRPC6, which is abundantly expressed in the lungs, participates in the development of lung diseases via multiple pathways. For instance, TRPC6 activation leads to airway smooth muscle cell contraction and bronchial constriction [14], pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell contraction and vascular stenosis [15], airway inflammation, fibroblast proliferation and differentiation, and fibroblast migration in the lung tissues [16]. In this study, we use in vitro and in vivo models to explore whether TRPC6 participates in the abnormal differentiation of basal cells, providing a theoretical basis for TRPC6 as a new therapeutic target for mucus hypersecretion in COPD.

Material and methods

For further details of the materials and methods, please refer to the supplementary material.

Human subjects

Human lung tissues and primary human bronchial epithelial cells (HBECs) were obtained from patients who underwent resection or bronchoscopy for solitary pulmonary nodules. COPD was defined according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease criteria (https://goldcopd.org). The clinical characteristics of the human participants are shown in Table S1. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (approval number: 2022–138), and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Mouse experiments

C57BL/6Smoc-Krt5em1(2A−CreERT2)Smoc (Cat. No. NM-KI-190016), B6;129S-Trpc6tm1(flox)Smoc (Cat. No. NM-CKO-00104), and wild-type C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center Inc. (China). STOCK Gt(ROSA)26Sortm4(ACTB−tdTomato,−EGFP)Luo/J mice (Strain No. 007576) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (US). All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou Medical University (approval number: 20240508). Animal use was in accordance with the guidelines issued by the Chinese Council on Animal Care. For TRPC6 deletion from basal cells, Krt5-CreERT2;Trpc6f/f mice were used. Krt5-CreERT2;Trpc6+/+ mice were used as control mice. To lineage trace the cells derived from basal cells, Krt5-CreERT2;mT/mG mice were used.

To establish the mouse model of cigarette smoke exposure, 6–8-week old male wild-type mice were exposed to cigarette smoke in a whole-body exposure chamber for 24 weeks, as previously described [17, 18]. Female mice showed high mortality rates during exposure to cigarette smoke, while male mice showed higher tolerance. Therefore, only male mice were subjected to cigarette smoke exposure. The control groups were exposed to room air.

To establish a mouse model of mucus hypersecretion caused by abnormal airway basal cell differentiation, the mice first received naphthalene (Cat. No. 84679, Sigma-Aldrich) intraperitoneally to eliminate all epithelial cells except basal cells in the trachea [19, 20]. The mice were then treated with interleukin-13 by intratracheal aerosolisation to induce abnormal differentiation of regenerated airway basal cells, or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as the control [21]. The mice were sacrificed on day 10 for analysis.

Cell cultures

HBEC culture was performed according to the manuals for PneumaCult-Ex Plus Medium (Cat. No. 05040) and PneumaCult-ALI Medium (Cat. No. 05001) from STEMCELL Technologies (Canada). Air–liquid interface (ALI) cultures were established using transwell plates (Cat. No. 3460, Corning, USA). HBECs (passage 0) were cultured at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% carbon dioxide (CO2) until 80% confluence (basal cell marker identification is shown in Figure S3A), followed by dissociation with TrypLE Express Enzyme (Cat. No. 12604021, Gibco, USA) and seeding into transwell plates at a density of 2.3 × 105 cells/cm2. The transwell plates were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2, with medium changes performed every 2 days. Once the cells had reached 100% confluence, the apical medium was removed, and the basal medium was replaced with a differentiation medium, marking day 0 of differentiation. Medium changes were performed every 2 days thereafter. The formation of visible ciliary beating could be observed on day 14. ALI cultures were collected for analysis at various timepoints, as indicated in the text.

For doxycycline treatment (Cat. No. D3072, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), a 100 mg/mL stock solution was diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to 100 ng/mL. For Hyp9 treatment (Cat. No. H9791, Sigma-Aldrich), a 50-mM stock solution was diluted in PBS to 6 µM. For SAR 7334 treatment (Cat. No. 5831, Tocris, USA), a 100-mM stock solution was diluted in PBS to 1.25 µM. For DAPT treatment (Cat. No. 5831 Tocris), a 100-mM stock solution was diluted in PBS to 10 µM. Drug treatments were added as indicated in the text.

TRPC6 was overexpressed or deleted in HBECs using the lentivirus-mediated Tet-On system and the CRISPR/Cas9 system, respectively. The lentivirus was provided by Yunzhou Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). The tetracycline-inducible (Tet-On) expression of TRPC6 was constructed using pLV[Exp]-TRE > hTRPC6[NM_004621.6]-EF1α > Puro and pLV[Exp]-EF1α > {tTS/rtTA/Hygro}. The Tet-On control group was constructed using pLV[Exp]-TRE > EGFP-EF1α > Puro and pLV[Exp]-EF1α > {tTS/rtTA/Hygro}. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated depletion of TRPC6 was constructed using pLV[2CRISPR]-U6 > hTRPC6[gRNA#1AGACAGTTCTCCGTGAGAAG]-U6 > hTRPC6[gRNA#2GATTAATGCCTATAAAGGCC]-EF1a > hCas9(ns):P2A:Puro. The site of genomic RNA in the TRPC6 gene is shown in Figure S3D. The control group used pLV[2CRISPR]-U6 > Scramble1[gRNA#1GTGTAGTTCGACCATTCGTG]-U6 > Scramble2[gRNA#2GTTCAGGATCACGTTACCGC]-EF1α > hCas9(ns):P2A:Puro.

HBECs were infected with lentivirus in the second to third passages. HBECs were dissociated when they reached approximately 80% confluence, and 500,000 cells were transferred to a flask. The lentiviral solution was added at a multiplicity of infection of 10, and the cells were cultured in PneumaCult-Ex Plus Medium supplemented with 5 µM Y-27632 (Cat. No. 05001, STEMCELL Technologies) for 16 h. After incubation, the lentivirus-containing medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS, followed by continued culture in fresh PneumaCult-Ex Plus Medium with medium changes every 2 days. Once the cells had reached 60% confluence, 2 µg/mL puromycin (Cat. No. 73342, STEMCELL Technologies) was added for selection for the CRISPR/Cas9 system, or 2 µg/mL puromycin and 100 µg/mL hygromycin B (Cat. No. 03813, STEMCELL Technologies) were added for selection for the Tet-On system. After 4 days of selection, puromycin was maintained at 1 µg/mL and hygromycin B at 50 µg/mL. Once the cells had reached 80%–90% confluence, they were transferred to the ALI for differentiation, and maintenance concentrations of puromycin or hygromycin B were added to the differentiation medium.

Histology

ALI cultures were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C, followed by washing three times in PBS, before blocking for 1 h at room temperature in 4% goat serum/PBS/0.2% Triton X-100 solution. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by washing three times in PBS and incubating with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The cultures were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by three PBS washes. The culture was harvested from the transwell plate and transferred to a slide. Prolong Gold anti-fade mounting medium (Cat. No. 05001, P36930, Invitrogen, USA) was added to the centre of the cultures and covered with a coverslip.

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C and incubated in 10% sucrose for 24 h at 4 °C and 30% sucrose at 4 °C until the tissue sank, before being mounted in OCT embedding compound and sectioned to a thickness of 8 µm. For immunostaining, the sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, followed by three PBS washes. The sections were incubated in blocking solution (4% goat serum/PBS/0.2% Triton X-100) for 1 h at room temperature, incubated in primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed, incubated in secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, washed, stained with DAPI, and then mounted for imaging.

The following primary antibodies were used: mouse anti-acetylated α-tubulin (ACT; 1:1500, Cat. No. T7451, Sigma-Aldrich), rabbit anti-ACT Alexa Fluor 647 conjugate (1:100, Cat. No. 81502, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), mouse anti-mucin 5AC (MUC5AC; 1:100, Cat. No. MA5-12,178, Invitrogen, USA), rabbit anti-TRPC6 (1:100, Cat. No. ab62461, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-keratin 5 (KRT5) Alexa Fluor 647 conjugate (1:500, Cat. No. ab193895, Abcam), rabbit anti-cleaved NOTCH1 (1:100, Cat. No. 4147, Cell Signaling Technology), and mouse anti-transformation-related protein 63 (1:100, Cat. No. ab735, Abcam). Secondary antibodies included goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG; H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 488/568/647, and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody. Alexa Fluor 488/568/647 were purchased from Invitrogen. Cleaved NOTCH1 was detected by tyramide signal amplification according to the manuals for the Tyramide SuperBoost kit (Cat. No. B40922, Invitrogen). Images were captured using a Zeiss Axio Imager.A2 (Zeiss, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 16 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Comparisons between groups were performed using the Student’s t-test, paired t-test, or one-way analysis of variance with Bonferroni correction, as appropriate. All analyses were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

TRPC6 expression increased in COPD airway epithelium

We first evaluated TRPC6 expression in COPD (n = 3 individuals) and non-COPD (n = 3 individuals) lung tissues. TRPC6 expression was increased in COPD airway epithelium compared with non-COPD airway epithelium (ratio of TPRC6+ cells to total airway epithelial cells: COPD: 0.70 ± 0.07 vs. non-COPD: 0.31 ± 0.04, P = 0.001) (Fig. 1A–B). To clarify the airway epithelial cell types in which TRPC6 expression increased, we analysed TRPC6 expression in a single-cell sequencing dataset (Gene Expression Omnibus accession number: GSE136831) containing data from GOLD stage IV COPD (n = 17 individuals) and age-matched control donors (n = 15 individuals). TRPC6 expression was increased in basal cells and goblet cells (Fig. 1C). To explore whether basal cell differentiation was associated with TRPC6 expression, we first detected TRPC6 expression using HBEC ALI cultures on day 7. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated increased TRPC6 expression in COPD bronchial basal cell differentiation compared with non-COPD (ratio of TPRC6+ cells to total KRT5+ cells: COPD: 0.76 ± 0.07 vs. non-COPD: 0.35 ± 0.07, P = 0.002) (Fig. 1D–E).

TRPC6 expression increased in the airway epithelium of mice exposed to cigarette smoke or interleukin-13

Cigarette smoke exposure is an important risk factor for COPD [22]. Therefore, we used a cigarette smoke-exposed mouse model. Compared with the group exposed to room air, TRPC6 expression was higher in the tracheal epithelium, and there was a higher number of TRPC6+ basal cells in the cigarette smoke-exposed group at 24 weeks (ratio of TPRC6+ cells to total KRT5+ cells: cigarette smoke: 0.82 ± 0.06 vs. room air: 0.16 ± 0.03, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A–B). Mice exposed to cigarette smoke are commonly used in animal studies of COPD; however, goblet cell metaplasia is not demonstrated in the airway of cigarette smoke-exposed mice. Therefore, the airway epithelium of cigarette smoke-exposed mice cannot effectively simulate goblet cell metaplasia characterised by increased MUC5AC expression [23, 24]. Therefore, we used interleukin-13 exposure to simulate the pathological morphology of mucus hypersecretion, with an increase in goblet cells and a decrease in ciliated cells [21], for the subsequent mouse experiments (Figure S1A–S1B). Furthermore, the effects of interleukin-13 on the airway epithelium resemble a subset of patients with COPD who have type 2 airway inflammation as a main driver of disease [25]. To investigate the function of TRPC6 in basal cell differentiation, we used a high naphthalene dose in a mouse model to eliminate all epithelial cells except basal cells in the trachea [19, 20]. We then treated the mice with interleukin-13 to induce aberrant differentiation of regenerated tracheal basal cells (Fig. 2C). Using Krt5-CreERT2+;mT/mG mice for basal cell lineage tracing, we observed that tracheal epithelial regeneration was supplemented by basal cells, and goblet cell metaplasia occurred in the regenerated tracheal epithelium (Figure S1C). Consistent with cigarette smoke exposure, TRPC6 expression was increased in the tracheal epithelium, and there was a higher number of TRPC6+ basal cells in the interleukin-13 group (ratio of TPRC6+ cells to total KRT5+ cells: interleukin-13: 0.76 ± 0.04 vs. PBS: 0.20 ± 0.02, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2D–E).

TRPC6 overexpression promoted goblet cell differentiation

To clarify whether TRPC6 promoted basal cell differentiation into goblet cells, we performed TRPC6 overexpression during differentiation (days 0–28) of non-COPD HBECs using the lentivirus-mediated Tet-On system and treated the cells using TRPC6 agonists for channel activation. Lentiviral transfection efficiency was > 90% (Figure S3B). TRPC6 protein levels increased 4.08-times compared with the vector group (Figure S3C). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated an increase in numbers of goblet cells (fold change in the ratio of MUC5AC+ cells to total cells: days 0–28 TRPC6 overexpression vs. vector: 17.46 ± 6.07, P < 0.001) and a decrease in numbers of ciliated cells (fold change in the ratio of ATC+ cells to total cells: days 0–28 TRPC6 overexpression vs. vector: 0.25 ± 0.06, P < 0.001) in TRPC6 Tet-On non-COPD HBEC ALI cultures (Fig. 3B–C). This suggests that TRPC6 regulated the differentiation of basal cells. To verify whether TRPC6 promoted basal cell differentiation rather than increasing MUC5AC expression in goblet cells after differentiation, we activated the Tet-On system at the early (days 0–7) and advanced (days 21–28) differentiation stages of HBEC ALI cultures (Fig. 3A). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that increased TRPC6 expression promoted the differentiation of basal cells into goblet cells in the early stage of HBEC ALI differentiation (fold change in the ratio of MUC5AC+ cells to total cells: days 0–7 TRPC6 overexpression vs. vector, 12.03 ± 6.59, P = 0.007) but not in the advanced stage of differentiation (fold change in the ratio of MUC5AC+ cells to total cells: days 21–28 TRPC6 overexpression vs. vector, 1.84 ± 0.98, P = 0.326) (Fig. 3B–C).

Reduced TRPC6 expression suppressed goblet cell metaplasia

Next, we deleted TRPC6 using the lentivirus-mediated CRISPR/Cas9 system in COPD HBEC ALI cultures to determine whether TRPC6 loss of function improved goblet cell metaplasia. We measured TRPC6 protein expression in COPD HBEC ALI cultures, confirming that TRPC6 expression was reduced by 16.81-times compared with the vector group (Figure S3E). Staining demonstrated a decrease in numbers of goblet cells (fold change in the ratio of MUC5AC+ cells to total cells: TRPC6 depletion vs. vector, 0.27 ± 0.10, P < 0.001) and an increase in numbers of ciliated cells (fold change in the ratio of ACT+ cells to total cells: TRPC6 depletion vs. vector, 1.97 ± 0.19, P < 0.001) in TRPC6-depleted COPD HBEC ALI cultures (Fig. 4A–B). In vivo, we measured TRPC6 expression in the airway of Krt5-CreERT2;Trpc6f/f mice, confirming depletion of TRPC6 in the tracheal epithelium (Figure S4). Then, we treated the TRPC6-depleted mice (Krt5-CreERT2;Trpc6f/f) with interleukin-13. Staining demonstrated a decrease in the numbers of goblet cells (ratio of MUC5AC+ cells to total airway epithelial cells: IL-13-exposed TRPC6-depleted group: 0.24 ± 0.02 vs. interleukin-13-exposed control group: 0.04 ± 0.01, P < 0.001) and an increase in numbers of ciliated cells (ratio of ACT+ cells to total airway epithelial cells: interleukin-13-exposed TRPC6-depleted group: 0.45 ± 0.03 vs. interleukin-13-exposed control group: 0.22 ± 0.03, P < 0.001) in the tracheal epithelium of the TRPC6-depleted group (Krt5-CreERT2;Trpc6f/f) compared with the control group (Krt5-CreERT2;Trpc6+/+) under interleukin-13-exposed (Fig. 4D–4E). Furthermore, staining demonstrated a decrease in numbers of TPRC6+KRT5+ cells accompanied by a decrease in numbers of MUC5AC+ cells in the tracheal epithelium of the TRPC6-depleted group (Krt5-CreERT2;Trpc6f/f) compared with the control group (Krt5-CreERT2;Trpc6+/+) under interleukin-13-exposed (Figure S2A).

TRPC6 inhibitor treatment suppressed goblet cell metaplasia

As TRPC6 is a channel protein, we evaluated whether TRPC6 inhibitor treatment could also suppress goblet cell metaplasia in COPD HBEC ALI cultures. By adding a TRPC6 inhibitor (SAR 7334) during the differentiation of COPD HBEC ALI cultures (Fig. 5A), we observed a decrease in goblet cells (fold change in the ratio of MUC5AC+ cells to total cells: SAR 7334 vs. PBS: 0.57 ± 0.11, P = 0.003) and an increase in ciliated cells (fold change in the ratio of ACT+ cells to total cells: SAR 7334 vs. PBS: 1.70 ± 0.31, P = 0.018) in COPD HBEC ALI cultures (Fig. 5C–D). In vivo (Fig. 5B), immunofluorescence staining revealed a decrease in numbers of goblet cells (ratio of MUC5AC+ cells to total airway epithelial cells: SAR 7334 + interleukin-13-exposed group: 0.06 ± 0.03 vs. PBS + interleukin-13-exposed group: 0.27 ± 0.01, P < 0.001) and an increase in numbers of ciliated cells in the tracheal epithelium of the SAR 7334 + interleukin-13-exposed group (ratio of ACT+ cells to total airway epithelial cells: SAR 7334 + interleukin-13-exposed group: 0.42 ± 0.03 vs. PBS + interleukin-13-exposed group: 0.29 ± 0.04, P = 0.006) (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, staining demonstrated an unchanged in numbers of TPRC6+KRT5+ cells while a decrease in numbers of MUC5AC+ cells in the tracheal epithelium of SAR 7334 + interleukin-13-exposed group compared with the PBS + interleukin-13-exposed group (Figure S2B).

TRPC6 promoted goblet cell metaplasia partially via regulation of NOTCH signalling

According to previous studies, NOTCH signalling plays a key role in airway basal cell differentiation [9, 26]. We measured the expression of the NOTCH1 intracellular domain (N1ICD) in COPD lung tissues and confirmed that N1ICD was expressed in TRPC6+ basal cells (Fig. 6A). To explore whether TRPC6 functions through NOTCH signalling, we used a NOTCH signalling inhibitor (DAPT, γ-secretase inhibitor) to rescue goblet cell metaplasia in TRPC6 Tet-On non-COPD HBEC ALI cultures (Fig. 6B). Staining demonstrated a decrease in goblet cells (fold change in the ratio of MUC5AC+ cells to total cells: DAPT vs. PBS: 0.11 ± 0.04, P = 0.011) and an increase ciliated cells (fold change in the ratio of ACT+ cells to total cells: DAPT vs. PBS: 27.59 ± 16.13, P = 0.012) in DAPT-treated TRPC6 Tet-On non-COPD HBEC ALI cultures (Fig. 6C–D). In vivo, we measured N1ICD expression in the tracheal epithelium of interleukin-13-exposed TRPC6-depleted mice (Krt5-CreERT2;Trpc6f/f) and confirmed that N1ICD expression was decreased compared with the control group (Krt5-CreERT2;Trpc6+/+) (ratio of N1ICD+ cells to total KRT5+ cells: TRPC6 depletion: 0.66 ± 0.04 vs. control: 0.25 ± 0.11, P = 0.001) (Fig. 6E–F).

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that increased TRPC6 expression in airway basal cells promotes their differentiation into goblet cells, at least in part via NOTCH signalling activation. Previous studies have shown that a basal cell subpopulation with high TRPC6 expression in COPD will undergo airway goblet cell metaplasia during differentiation [12]. However, no studies have shown whether the TRPC6 gene plays a biological role in this process. Our study confirmed that TRPC6 is not only a marker of this basal cell subpopulation, but that it also plays a biological role in goblet cell metaplasia. By overexpressing TRPC6 in the basal cell ALI differentiation model in non-COPD, we determined that TRPC6 overexpression led to the pathological phenotype of goblet cell hyperplasia only during early differentiation (days 0–7), but not advanced differentiation (days 21–28). These results suggest that increased TRPC6 expression promoted goblet cell metaplasia by altering the differentiation of basal cells rather than increasing mucus secretion. This study provides a theoretical basis for TRPC6 as a therapeutic target for airway mucus hypersecretion.

The protein structure of the TRPC family contains several domains, including the ankyrin repeat domain (ARD) and coiled-coil domain in the N-terminal region, with the ARD playing an important role in protein interaction and signal transduction. TRPC family also contains six transmembrane regions that form channels on the cell membrane and are responsible for ion permeability. Moreover, the C-terminal region contains binding sites for calmodulin, protein kinase A, and protein kinase C, which play key roles in TRPC regulation and function [13]. The protein structure of the TRPC family suggests that the biological function of TRPC6 can be exerted by enhancing ion channel activity and increasing the binding of corresponding kinases. In the present study, TRPC6 activity in TRPC6 Tet-On non-COPD basal cells could be activated by endogenous factors; thus, it is difficult to determine whether the effects of TRPC6 occurred through increased ion channel activity or increased binding of corresponding kinases. In addition, the low expression of TRPC6 in non-COPD limited the effects of TRPC6 agonists. Based on these findings, when exploring TRPC6 function, we not only overexpressed the TRPC6 protein, but we also used agonists to activate the channel. Although our study could not clarify the mechanism by which TRPC6 regulated NOTCH signalling, we did confirm that TRPC6 regulated the differentiation potential of airway basal cells. In addition, by adding TRPC6 inhibitors to the COPD ALI model, the pathological phenotype of goblet cell metaplasia was alleviated, but the effect was less pronounced than with TRPC6 depletion. Inhibiting TRPC6 channel activity did not achieve the same effect as TRPC6 depletion in reducing the number of MUC5AC+ cells. Based on this evidence, we suggest that TRPC6 may regulate basal cell differentiation in multiple ways. However, further studies are needed to explore the exact mechanisms.

Recent studies have reported that the use of cryospray via bronchoscopy to ablate epithelial cells in lesions with mucus hypersecretion in the COPD airway allowed the airway epithelium to regenerate, improving the symptoms of mucus hypersecretion [27, 28]. However, these improvements did not reach statistical significance after 12 months. Previous studies have found that even when COPD airway basal cells were isolated in vitro and cultured for more than 30 passages, the differentiated airway epithelium still maintained the mucus hypersecretion phenotype [12], which is thought to be largely due to the epigenetic changes in COPD airway basal cells [29, 30]. This evidence suggests that ablation of the mucus-hypersecreting airway epithelium is not a highly effective treatment. Our study showed that TRPC6 depletion in HBEC ALI cultures from patients with COPD alleviated mucus hypersecretion. Moreover, TRPC6 inhibitor treatment reduced mucus hypersecretion in HBEC ALI cultures from patients with COPD. Therefore, in addition to ablating the mucus-hypersecreting airway epithelium, further inhibiting TRPC6 activity in basal cells may be a potential treatment strategy for mucus hypersecretion.

Airway mucus hypersecretion is a multifaceted pathological process driven by diverse stimuli [31, 32], including chronic cigarette smoke exposure, environmental pollutants, and various inflammatory cascades. Goblet cell metaplasia characterised by increased MUC5AC+ cells cannot be effectively reproduced in cigarette smoke-exposed mouse models [21, 22]. Therefore, in the present study, our in vivo functional validation primarily utilised an interleukin-13-induced mouse model, which is a well-established system for simulating goblet cell metaplasia. However, it is essential to acknowledge that this model predominantly reflects the T helper 2 (Th2) inflammatory endotype. While Th2 inflammation characterises a specific subset of patients with COPD, the broader disease population is often defined by a non-Th2 inflammatory profile, frequently associated with neutrophilic infiltration. It remains to be determined whether TRPC6 plays a consistent regulatory role in mucus hypersecretion triggered by non-Th2 factors, such as oxidative stress, viral infections, or neutrophilic inflammation [23, 30]. The clinical relevance of our in vivo data should be interpreted within this specific inflammatory context. Consequently, future studies are warranted to explore the involvement of TRPC6 across a wider range of experimental models. Elucidating whether the TRPC6-mediated pathway is a universal mechanism or context-specific will be crucial for the precise application of TRPC6-targeted therapies in the clinically heterogeneous COPD population.

In the present study, all participants in the COPD group were smokers, and all participants in the non-COPD group were non-smokers, meaning that smoking status constituted the main confounding factor. Although this distribution reflects the high tobacco dependence of COPD, the difference in TRPC6 may be partly attributed to the direct induction by smoke exposure. Future research should further clarify the independent role of TRPC6 in disease development by introducing a smoke-exposed non-COPD control group.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, this study lacked in vivo experiments to investigate whether increased TRPC6 expression regulated the differentiation of airway basal cells. Second, our results showed that NOTCH signalling was involved in the regulation of airway basal cell differentiation by TRPC6, but owing to the lack of unbiased sequencing data, NOTCH signalling may not be the most important or relevant downstream pathway. Third, our study only evaluated the expression of MUC5AC. Detecting total airway mucus protein and MUC5B would provide more information for predicting disease occurrence and progression [33, 34]. Therefore, future studies should investigate whether TRPC6 regulates other mucins to provide a more comprehensive understanding of mucus hypersecretion.

Conclusions

TRPC6 regulated basal cell differentiation, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target in COPD. However, the in vivo experiments were conducted using interleukin-13-exposed mice, so its clinical application needs to be further investigated.
