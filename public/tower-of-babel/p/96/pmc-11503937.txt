Imaging and Simulation of Ruthenium Derivative Coating Microbeads at the Opaque Electrode with Electrogenerated Chemiluminescence
Authors: Yanlong Feng, Wenshuai Zhou, Xiaofei Wang, Jian Zhang, Min Zou, Chengxiao Zhang, Honglan Qi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Electrogenerated chemiluminescence (ECL) imaging is gaining increasing attention in various fields because of its high sensitivity, low background, and good temporal and spatial resolution. However, ECL imaging of microsized objects at the opaque electrode via top-view configuration is challenged with the reactants’ diffusion and light propagation. Here, we imaged and numerically simulated ruthenium derivative coating polystyrene microbeads (Ru1-PS@MB) at the glassy carbon electrode (GCE) via top-view configuration by ECL imaging. The ruthenium derivative (bis(2,2′-bipyridine)-4′-methyl-4-carboxybipyridine-ruthenium N-succinimidyl ester-bis (hexafluorophosphate), Ru1), a typical ECL reagent, was covalently linked onto the surface of aminated PS@MBs via the amide reaction. “Strong emission in edge and weak emission in center” phenomena for fluorescence (FL) and ECL emissions were obtained from Ru1-PS@MB on GCE. Z-Stack imaging of the microsized Ru1-PS@MB luminescence was performed on GCE in the presence of tri-n-propylamine (TPA). It is found that the clear luminescence range of Ru1-PS@MB perpendicular to the electrode surface in ECL image is slightly smaller than that in the FL image. The bigger was the diameter of the microbeads (from 5 to 18 μm), the larger was the ECL luminescence range of Ru1-PS@MB perpendicular to the electrode surface (from 5 to 7 μm). Our findings, which are also supported by numerical simulation, provide insights into the ECL imaging of microsized objects at the electrode surface, which will raise promising ECL applications in bioassays and cell imaging at the microscale level.

Introduction

Electrogenerated chemiluminescence (electrochemiluminescence, ECL) is a kind of luminescence that occurs at or near the surface of the electrode generated by electron transfer reactions between electrochemically produced intermediates.1−3 The ECL imaging method, as a promising imaging method by recording ECL emissions on a charge coupled device paired with a microscope, is gaining increasing interest in recent years because of its high sensitivity, low background noise, and good temporal and spatial resolution. ECL imaging has been used in visualizing electrode,4−6 microbeads,7−10 nanomaterials,11,12 entities,13−15 latent fingerprint,16,17 as well as qualifying and imaging proteins on the cell surface,18−21 small molecules in the cell membrane,22,23 or biological molecules released from cells24,25 and cell morphology,26 as well as food safety inspection and environment surveillance.27 For example, our group developed an ECL imaging method for the determination of cell surface protein at the gold electrode21 and for visualizing cell morphology at the glassy carbon electrode (GCE).26 For ECL imaging of cells or microbeads on the electrode surface, objects labeled with the ECL probe need to be located at a limited distance from the electrode, and ECL signals need to be acquired using an optical detector. However, the microsized cells or beads can hinder the diffusion of reactants.

Nanomaterials have been widely used in the ECL assay or in ECL imaging as electrode modified materials, or as carriers of either ECL signal reagents or quenchers, or as an ECL emitter.2,3 One important example of nanomaterials in ECL applications is the employment of 2.8 μm immuno-magnetic beads (capture antibody immobilized on the surface of magnetic beads, MBs) in the commercialized ECL application, such as Elecsys immunoassays. Some research groups, such as Bard,28 Paolucci, Sojic, Valenti, et al., have studied the ECL mechanisms and mapped the ECL emissions of microsized objects labeled with the ECL probe, in which the ECL probe was modified onto the microsized objects surface via the biotin–streptavidin reaction, or the antigen–antibody reaction, or a covalent labeling strategy. For example, Dolci et al. developed a transparent electrochemical cell for ECL imaging of 8 μm Ru(bpy)32+ complex coated polystyrene (PS) beads on fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO).7 Valenti et al. reported a transparent carbon nanotube electrode as a new device for ECL imaging of cells and tissues.29 In 2020, Paolucci group’s mapped ECL generation from 2.8, 1, 0.5, and 0.3 μm streptavidin-coated polystyrene magnetic microbeads coupled with ruthenium-containing antibody functionalized with biotin at the platinum electrode. In this work, they found that the ECL emission can be increased through optimization of luminophore distribution by decreasing the bead size and addition of a branched amine to increase the efficiency of the coreactant mechanism.30 Additionally, Sojic group’s mapped the ECL reactivity of 12 μm Ru(bpy)32+-functionalized polystyrene microbeads in the presence of tri-n-propylamine (TPA) or 2-(dibutylamino)ethanol at the GCE. They found that only the ECL luminophores located in the 3 μm region next to the electrode contribute to the ECL signal.8 In following Sojic group’s work, they investigated the electrochemical and photophysical properties by mapping the ECL reactivity of 12 μm Ru(bpy)32+-functionalized PS microbeads10 and investigated the chemical lens effects to tune the ECL-emitting layer of 8, 12, and 14 μm Ru(bpy)32+-functionalized PS microbeads31 at the GCE. In view of the electrode-dependent property of the Ru(bpy)32+–TPA system32,33 and the dependence of ECL imaging behaviors on the distance between the luminophores and the electrode surface,8 we are curious about whether we can image the ECL emission from microsized objects at the opaque electrode by Z-stack imaging using a conventional imaging detector.

Here, we imaged and numerically simulated three ruthenium derivative coating polystyrene microbeads (Ru1-PS@MB) in the presence of TPA at the opaque GCE by ECL imaging via top-view configuration. The ruthenium derivative (bis(2,2′-bipyridine)-4′-methyl-4-carboxybipyridine-ruthenium N-succinimidyl ester-bis (hexafluorophosphate), Ru1) was used as the ECL reagent, and TPA was used as the coreactant. To obtain good resolution on a conventional microscope and to mimic a single cell in ECL imaging, three amino polystyrene microbeads (NH2-PS@MBs), with dimensions of 5, 10, and 18 μm, were employed and covalently linked with Ru1 via the amide reaction to form Ru1 coated polystyrene microbeads (Ru1-PS@MB, as shown in Scheme 1A). Here, the fluorescence (FL) and ECL emissions of three Ru1-PS@MB modified on GCE (Scheme 1B) were imaged in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4) containing 50 mM TPA via top-view configuration using a homemade microscope (Scheme 1C). Here, Z is the perpendicular distance between Ru1-PS@MB and the electrode surface, in which Z = 0 μm is on the electrode surface. Z-Stack FL and ECL imaging of the microsized Ru1-PS@MB was studied. The regions of microsized Ru1-PS@MB enlightened upon FL and ECL were discussed and compared.

Experimental Section

Materials and Apparatus

The aminated polystyrene microbead (NH2-PS@MB) was purchased from Tianjin Saiqun Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Bis(2,2′-bipyridine)-4′-methyl-4-carboxybipyridine-ruthenium N-succinimidyl ester-bis (hexafluorophosphate) (abbreviated as Ru1) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (U.S.). Tri-n-propylamine (TPA) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (China). All aqueous solutions were prepared using Milli-Q water (18.2 MΩ cm).

A MPI-E ECL detector (Xi’an Remax Analysis Instruments Co. Ltd., China) and CHI 660E electrochemical workstation (Shanghai Chenhua Instrument Co., Ltd., China), a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, SU8220, HITACHI, Japan), a Cary Eclipse fluorescence spectrophotometer (Varian Co., Ltd., U.S.), an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (Aglient 5110, U.S.), a dynamic light scattering particle size analyzer (DLS, NanoBrook 90 PlusPALS, Brookhaven, U.S.), an Olympus IX 73 inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan) coupled with Prime 95B complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS, resolution: 1200 × 1200, 11 μm/pixel, Photometrics, U.S.), and an Olympus IX 81 inverted microscope with confocal system (FV1200, Olympus, Japan) were used in this work.

Preparation of Ru1-PS@MB and Ru1-PS@MB Modified GCE

Ru1-PS@MB was obtained via the following procedure. 40 μL of 0.01 mol/L Ru1 suspensions in DMSO and 10 μL of 0.1 mol/L borate buffer (pH 8.5) were added to 20 μL of 1.0 mg/mL NH2-PS@MB suspension in water. The mixed solution was then gently shaken in the dark at room temperature for 12 h and then centrifuged for 30 min at 12 000 r/min. The precipitate was washed with 70% ethanol twice, and then suspended in 200 μL of 10 mM PB (0.0082 M Na2HPO4, 0.0018 M NaH2PO4, pH 7.4) and stored at 4 °C in a refrigerator before use.

Ten microliters of Ru1-PS@MB suspension (25 μg/mL 18 μm, 10 μg/mL 10 μm, or 5 μg/mL 5 μm Ru1-PS@MB) was dropped onto GCE (2.0 mm in diameter) and dried to obtain Ru1-PS@MB modified GCE.

FL and ECL Imaging of Ru1-PS@MB

Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) images of Ru1-PS@MB on a culture dish on an Olympus IX 81 inverted microscope with a confocal system were obtained over the collection windows of 550–650 nm with the excitation at 488 nm. ECL images and FL images of Ru1-PS@MB on GCE were recorded on an Olympus IX 73 inverted microscope coupled with Prime 95B CMOS. ECL and FL imaging were performed in 0.1 M PBS (0.082 M Na2HPO4, 0.018 M NaH2PO4, 0.1 M KCl, pH 7.4) containing 50 mM TPA. A three-electrode system (Ru1-PS@MB modified GCE as the working electrode, an Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) as the reference electrode, and a platinum sheet as the counter electrode) was employed in this work. For ECL imaging, the multipotential steps technique (initial potential, 0 V (pulse time, 0.01 s), high potential, +1.2 V (pulse time, 0.5 s), exposure time, 10 s) was employed. FL images of Ru1-PS@MB on GCE using an Olympus IX 73 inverted microscope coupled with Prime 95B CMOS were obtained over the collection windows of green excitation (530–550 nm) with the excitation by a mercury lamp light source (20 W cm–2) and an exposure time of 2 s. The obtained CLSM, FL images, and ECL images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Numerical Simulations

COMSOL software was used for the numerical simulations. The parameters of the numerical simulation are presented in the Supporting Information.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of NH2-PS@MB and Ru1-PS@MB

The morphology of the three NH2-PS@MBs was first characterized by FE-SEM and DLS. The three NH2-PS@MBs are almost perfectly spherical in shape (Figure 1A–C), and the average diameter is 5.0 ± 0.4, 9.9 ± 0.9, and 18.4 ± 0.9 μm for 5, 10, and 18 μm PS@MB, respectively, verified by DLS (Figure S1A–C). After Ru1 was labeled onto NH2-PS@MB, obvious FL emission can be seen from the FL spectra of three Ru1-PS@MBs with a maximum wavelength at 625 nm, which is consistent with that of Ru1 in solution (Figure 1D). In contrast, NH2-PS@MB had no FL emission. To get the loading amount of Ru1 on the Ru1-PS@MB surface, the concentration of Ru element in the Ru1-PS@MB dispersion (CRu, Table S3) was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES), while the number of Ru1-PS@MB particles (PRu1-PS@MB, Table S3) was counted by an automated cell counter. The loading amount of Ru1 on a single Ru1-PS@MB (SRu1, Table S3) then was calculated to be 7 pg/particle for 5 μm Ru1-PS@MB, 14 pg/particle for 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB, and 151 pg/particle for 18 μm Ru1-PS@MB, respectively.

To visualize the spatial distribution of Ru1 on the surface of NH2-PS@MB, here, a single-particle CLSM with 3D model was used to visualize the FL emission from 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB as an example. The schematic diagram of CLSM of Ru1-PS@MB was shown in Figure 1E, similar to that in our previous work.34 The CLSM images of one single 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB at different Z sections were shown in Figure 1F. Bright FL emission can be seen from CLSM images of 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB. At Z = 0 μm, a bright orange-red emission with dot shape is observed. With the increase of the height at Z, the shape of the CLSM images gradually changes from a dot to a ring, and then to a dot again. When Z = 2, 4, 6, and 8 μm, the ring diameters are 8.4, 9.7, 9.8, and 8.6 μm, respectively, which is consistent with the theoretical diameter of a 10 μm microbead (8.0, 9.8, 9.8, and 8.0 μm at Z = 2, 4, 6, and 8 μm, Table S4). A bright sphere with a diameter of 10 μm was obtained for the 3D-reconstructed Ru1-PS@MB (Figure 1G). These observations suggest that Ru1 is decorated on the whole outer surface of NH2-PS@MB, which is promising for FL and ECL imaging of Ru1-PS@MB in situ by recording its emission at the single-particle level.

Tracking the Emission from Ru1-PS@MB by FL and ECL Imaging

To track the emission from Ru1-PS@MB on GCE by FL and ECL imaging, a homemade microscope device was employed by placing the electrochemical system on the microscope operating platform (Scheme 1C). The ECL behaviors of 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB modified on different electrodes were first examined. The highest ECL intensity of 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB was observed at GCE as compared to that at the gold electrode, platinum electrode, indium tin oxide (ITO) electrode, and FTO electrode (Figure S2A,B). Additionally, strong and stable ECL emissions were obtained using multipotential steps at a potential of +1.2 V (Figure S2C–F). Therefore, GCE as a base electrode and multipotential steps at +1.2 V were employed in the following experiment.

Figure 2A–C shows the FL and ECL images of 5, 10, and 18 μm Ru1-PS@MB modified GCE in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4) containing 50 mM TPA, respectively. To get well-separated Ru1-PS@MBs on the GCE, a diluted Ru1-PS@MBs suspension was dropped on the GCE to get a Ru1-PS@MBs modified GCE. Obvious FL and ECL emissions can be seen from all Ru1-PS@MBs on the GCE. By analysis of the FL and ECL emission intensity profiles across one representative Ru1-PS@MB from one enlarged FL image (Figure 2A1–C1) and one enlarged ECL image (Figure 2A3–C3), “strong emission in edge and weak emission in center” phenomenon was clearly obtained (Figure 2A4–C4). The statistical analysis of 20 different Ru1-PS@MBs shows that the average diameters of Ru1-PS@MBs were 5.0 ± 0.2, 10.0 ± 1.3, and 18.1 ± 0.6 μm for 5, 10, and 18 μm PS@MBs from FL images and 4.9 ± 0.1, 10.0 ± 1.2, and 17.8 ± 0.6 μm for 5, 10, and 18 μm PS@MBs from ECL images (Figures 2D0 and S3), respectively. Of note, the diameter of Ru1-PS@MB measured in the FL and ECL images is the FL and ECL emission diameter of Ru1-PS@MB. This finding suggests that the FL and ECL imaging method can provide information regarding the bead diameter and the distribution of the emission intensity.

To further illustrate the phenomenon of bright edges and weak centers, the following experiment and simulation were conducted. First, the FL and ECL intensity should be related to the Ru1 content on the projection of Ru1-PS@MB. Therefore, the distribution of the emission intensity can be considered as the distribution of Ru1 content on the projection of Ru1-PS@MB. From the 3D relationship between nRuxy (Ru1 content at any tiny region around any point of 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB, Figure S4A) and the projection of the three-dimensional (detailed numerical simulation in the Supporting Information), it can be seen that the content of Ru1 on the edge of the projection is higher than that of in center (Figure S4B) and the content of Ru1 is the same on the same y-axis. The change trend of the experimental gray values is consistent with that of the theoretical Ru1 content values (Figure S4C). Second, in the top-view configurations, the emission of Ru1-PS@MB can only be detected if it passed through or around the Ru1-PS@MB. In the images recorded in the top-view configuration, the detected emission has to pass through or around Ru1-PS@MB. Ru1-PS@MB is the object. Thus, the emissions from the whole Ru1-PS@MB under the 40× objective lens are further simulated by using the geometric optics module according to the patent (Figure S5).35 The 40× objective lens is composed of four different lenses, and the parameters of each lens are shown in Table S5. The simulated emission images from 5, 10, and 18 μm Ru1-PS@MB were shown in Figure 2D1–D3. “Strong emission in edge and weak emission in center” phenomenon was also obtained from the simulated emission (Figure 2D4), which is basically consistent with the shapes and diameters of the experimental FL and ECL images, except that the edge intensity is relatively uniform. Simulated emission images were overlapped with the experimental FL images with an overlap coefficient according to Manders (R) of 0.97, 0.98, and 0.98 for 5, 10, and 18 μm Ru1-PS@MBs (Figure S6), respectively, further confirming the “strong emission in edge and weak emission in center” phenomenon.

Additionally, it was reported that the transmittance of the edge is greater than that of the center in the projection of microbeads.36 Although the transmittance of Ru1-PS@MB is nearly 100% (Figure S7, 99.67%), the Ru1 emission intensity on the edge through or around the Ru1-PS@MB is higher than that in the center. Therefore, the distribution of the Ru1 content, the imaging configuration, and the transmittance on Ru1-PS@MB affect the emission of the projection generated from Ru1 on the Ru1-PS@MB, results in the “strong emission in edge and weak emission in center” phenomenon.

Z-Stack Imaging of Ru1-PS@MB on the Electrode Surface

To track Z-stack imaging of Ru1-PS@MB luminescence on GCE surface via the top-view configuration, ECL and FL images of Ru1-PS@MB were recorded at different Z heights.

FL Image

Figure 3A shows the Z-stacks FL imaging of 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB as a model. It can be seen that the FL images keep clear in the Z range of 0–8 μm on the surface of GCE. The diameter of the FL images remained constant, ∼10.7 μm, as the Z increased from Z = 0 to Z = 8 μm, which is the same as that recorded using CLSM and DLS. With a further increase of Z height, the FL image becomes blurred and the diameter of the FL images increases (Figure S8). The diameter of the FL images increased from 12.3 to 29.2 μm as the Z increases from 8 to 30 μm (Table S6). These phenomena should be ascribed to the effect of the depth of the field on FL imaging.37 During the process of imaging of Ru1-PS@MB, Ru1-PS@MB is the object. The Ru1-PS@MB can be clearly recorded only if Ru1-PS@MB is located in the depth of field of the system (ds). ds includes the geometric depth of field (dg) and the physical depth of field (dp).38 By consideration of Ru1-PS@MB as the focal plane, the theoretical ds is calculated to be 3.6 μm (detailed calculation process in the Supporting Information). Actually, Ru1-PS@MB is a sphere. With the change of Z from a to b, the luminescence on Ru1-PS@MB remains consistent. As shown in Figure 3B, the actual luminous range (dtot) for the clear and stable imaging of Ru1-PS@MB should be the result of the joint influence of ds and uniform luminous range (df). That is to say, dtot = ds + df. In our work, df is about 4.4 μm. As a clear FL image can also be obtained in the Z range of 0–8 μm both for 5 μm Ru1-PS@MB (Figure S9) and for 18 μm Ru1-PS@MB (Figure S10), both ds and dtot are independent of the diameter of Ru1-PS@MB for the fixed detector.

(A) Representative pseudocolor FL images of one 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB at different Z values (0–10 μm) at 40× objective. (B) Schematic diagram of the relationship among actual luminous range dtot, ds, and df for FL imaging of Ru1-PS@MB. (C) The relationship between the experimental diameter of Ru1-PS@MBs (dot) and the theoretical diameter of the dispersion circle (line) with Z values of 5, 10, and 18 μm Ru1-PS@MBs at 40× objective.

With a further increase of Z upon dtot, the captured blurred and larger image may be the result of the convolution of the dispersion circle and the clear image.39 According to eq S14 (Supporting Information) and the theoretical model (Figure S11), the theoretical diameter (δ) of the dispersion circle can be stimulated. It can be seen that δ increases with the increase of Z and the fitted profiles match well with the experimental profiles for 5, 10, and 18 μm Ru1-PS@MBs (Figure 3C). Therefore, Ru1-PS@MBs can be clearly imaged within the actual range dtot, and the diameter of 5, 10, and 18 μm Ru1-PS@MBs can be directly obtained from the FL emission. The distance for clear FL imaging is independent of the diameter of the microbeads and dependent on the parameter of the detector at this top-view configuration.

ECL Image

For Z-stack ECL imaging of 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB, clear and stable ECL emissions can be obtained in the Z range of 0–6 μm (Figure 4A). When Z is larger than 6 μm, the ECL image is blurred. In the clear range of 0–6 μm, the diameter and gray value of the imaging remains relatively stable, with an average diameter of 10.0 μm and an average gray scale of 15.6 au (Table S7). Clear and stable ECL emissions can be obtained in the Z ranges of 0–5 μm for 5 μm Ru1-PS@MB (Figure 4B) and 0–7 μm for 18 μm Ru1-PS@MB (Figure 4C), respectively. From comparison of the ECL images of 5, 10, and 18 μm Ru1-PS@MBs, the clear luminescence range for the ECL images increases with the increase of the Ru1-PS@MB diameter. As compared to FL imaging, the clear luminescence range of Ru1-PS@MB perpendicular to the electrode surface in ECL image is slightly smaller than that in the FL image. This may be due to the difference mechanisms of ECL and FL emissions from Ru1-PS@MB on the GCE surface.

Numerical Simulation

To illustrate the different clear luminescence range of three Ru1-PS@MBs, the ECL imaging behavior of three Ru1-PS@MBs was further numerically simulated. At GCE, there are two ECL waves at +0.9 and +1.2 V (vs Ag/AgCl) for 1 nM Ru(bpy)32+-50 mM TPA (Figure S12), following typical coreactant mechanisms.28,40 At the Ru1-PS@MB modified GCE in the presence of TPA, one broad and strong ECL wave appeared at about +1.2 V (Figure S2A). Additionally, it was reported that direct oxidation of Ru1 on the beads would only occur for those within electron tunneling distance from the electrode surface, ≤3 nm,40 and the electrochemical oxidation of TPA was proposed to be dominant in the microsized beads–TPA system.8,15 To simplify, we also simulated the ECL imaging behaviors of Ru1-PS@MB on GCE by taking the electrochemical oxidation of TPA as the dominant pathway (Table S8). In the ECL pathway, TPA is oxidized to form TPA•+ and then deprotonated to form the TPA cation radical TPA• (eq R3). TPA• reduces Ru12+ to Ru1+ (eq R5), while pristine TPA•+ oxidizes Ru1+ to generate the excited state Ru12+* (eq R6). The relaxation process of Ru12+* produces light. The light is captured by CMOS to obtain the ECL image. The subdomain setting model (Figure S13) and simulation parameters (Tables S9–S12) are listed in the Supporting Information. Three physics fields, including the “transport of diluted species” physics field for simulating the concentration changes of the transport of TPA and its derivatives between the electrode and electrolyte or between the electrode and the surface of Ru1-PS@MB, the “coefficient form boundary partial differential equation” physics field for simulating the concentration changes of Ru12+ and its derivatives on the surface of Ru1-PS@MB, and the “radiation in absorbing-scattering media” physics field for simulation of light scattering and absorption on the Ru1-PS@MB surface, are employed.

First, the simulations of the concentration distributions of TPA•+ and TPA• were first studied and compared to those of the experimental results of ECL images. A brief summary is as follows. (1) In electrolyte solution, a characteristic planar diffusion mechanism was found for the concentration distribution of TPA•+ (decrease with the increase of Z height) and TPA• (increase with the increase of Z height) (Figure S14A). (2) On the surface of Ru1-PS@MB, the TPA• concentration increased with the increase of Z, while the TPA•+ concentration first increased and then decreased (Figure S14B). The maximum concentration of TPA• is 0.55 mM, which is smaller than that in electrolyte solution (0.62 mM, Figure S14A). At Z = 0.39 μm, the amount of TPA•+ and TPA• is the same. At Z = 0.43 μm, the amount of TPA•+ reached the maximum of 23.2 μM. The maximum distance (ZMax), defined as the distance that the concentration of TPA•+ decreased to 0.1% of the maximum concentration (CMax), is 3.4 μm. The minimum concentration (CMin) of TPA•+, defined as the concentration at ZMax, is 23.2 nM. At the same Z height, the TPA•+ concentration on the Ru1-PS@MB surface is lower than that in electrolyte solution (Figure S15). In other words, at the same TPA•+ concentration, Z on the Ru1-PS@MB surface is shorter than that in electrolyte solution. This should be ascribed to the presence of Ru1 on the surface of Ru1-PS@MB and the confinement of the solution between the MB and the electrode.8 (3) In eqs R1–R7 (in Table S8), the dependence of the concentration distributions of TPA•+ on the standard rate constant of eq R2 (ks2), or forward rate constant of eq R3 (kf), or rate constant of eq R6 (k6), was checked. The values of these parameters were adopted from the literature8,28 or assigned to reasonable values. It was found that similar trends of the relationship of the concentration distributions of TPA•+ with Z (first increased and then decreased) were obtained when ks2 values were in the range of 0.2–(1 × 104) cm/s (Figure S16A). With the increase of ks2 from 0.2 to 0.5, to 1, to 10, and to 1 × 104 cm/s, CMax decreased from 56 to 23 μM, but ZMax slightly increased from 3.0 to 3.2, to 3.3, to 3.4, and to 3.4 μm. kf has a great effect on the concentration distributions of TPA•+. When kf is 540, 1750, 3500, and 7000 s–1, ZMax values related to TPA•+ are 5.7, 4.2, 3.4, and 2.6 μm, respectively. The smaller was the decomposition rate kf, the larger was the expansion of TPA•+ (Figure S16B). With the increase of k6 from 3 × 105, to 5 × 105, and to 1 × 106 M–1 s–1, CMax decreased from 23 to 16 μM, but ZMax slightly decreased from 3.4, to 3.3, and to 3.1 μm (Figure S16C). As expected, the standard rate constant of eq R4 (ks4) and the rate constant of eq R5 (k5) had no effect on the CMax and ZMax related to TPA•+ (Figure S16D,E). Thus, it can be deduced that the concentration distribution of TPA•+ greatly depends on kf and slightly depends on ks2 and k6 and all, but is independent of ks4 and k5.

The half-life of the TPA radical cation has been reported by several groups.8,28,41 Assuming that eq R3 (in Table S8) is a first-order process, the half-life (τ1/2) of TPA•+ is τ1/2 = ln 2/kf according to ref (28). When kf is 540, 1750, 3500, and 7000 s–1, the τ1/2 value is 1.28, 0.24, 0.2, and 0.10 ms (Figure S16B). The smaller is the kf, the longer is the lifetime of TPA•+. Thus, the ZMax according to TPA•+ depends on τ1/2.

For a fixed kf = 3500 s–1,28ks2 = 10 cm/s, ks4 = 10 cm/s, k5 = 3 × 105 M–1 s–1, and k6 = 3 × 105 M–1 s–1,8 the effect of the diameter of Ru1-PS@MB on the concentration distributions of TPA•+ was studied. Similar trends of the concentration distributions of TPA•+ on Z (first increased and then decreased) were obtained for three Ru1-PS@MB values (Figure S16F). The ZMax values related to TPA•+ are 2.7, 3.4, and 4.0 μm on 5, 10, and 18 μm Ru1-PS@MB, respectively (Table S13). The smaller is the diameter, the shorter is the Z height. That is to say, the TPA•+ expansion range increases with the increase of the Ru1-PS@MB diameter, similar to the trend of the clear luminescence range with the Ru1-PS@MB diameter. However, the obtained simulated ZMax related to TPA•+ is smaller than that of the experimental clear ECL range.

Second, the concentration distributions of Ru12+* were further simulated in this work. Figure 5A,b shows the concentration distributions of Ru12+* on 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB. It can be seen that the concentration of Ru12+* increases first and then decreases with the increase of Z. The surface concentration of Ru12+* reaches the maximum (0.49 pmol/m2) at Z = 0.58 μm. The maximum distance (ZMax), defined as the distance that the concentration of Ru12+* decreases to 0.1% of the maximum concentration (CMax), is 3.5 μm. The minimum concentration (CMin) of Ru12+* at ZMax is 0.49 fmol/m2. In this work, Ru1 is covalently immobilized on the surface of the polystyrene microbeads, and the Ru1-PS@MB is confined between the GCE and the glass bottom of the electrochemical cell. The dependence of the concentration distribution of Ru12+* on the height of the geometric model (H) and the parameters of the Ru1 species (including the apparent surface diffusion coefficient of the ruthenium species over the bead surface (Dapp), the rate constant of eq R5 (k5), the rate constant of eq R6 (k6), and the rate constant of eq R7 (kECL)) were checked. At different H values (10, 30, 100, 250, and 500 μm), similar change trends of the concentration distributions of Ru12+* were obtained, and ZMax related to Ru12+* remained constant to 3.5 μm, although a different maximum concentration of Ru12+* was obtained (Figure S17). A different D value of Ru(bpy)32+ was reported in different conditions, such as 5 × 10–10 m2/s in aqueous solution,28 5 × 10–11 m2/s within the Ru(bpy)32+-doped silica nanoparticle,42 5 × 10–12 m2/s within the Ru(bpy)32+-doped silica nanoparticle,15 5 × 10–14 m2/s within the Nafion film,43 5 × 10–18 m2/s within the Ru(bpy)32+ embedded MOF complex,44 and 0 m2/s within the tris(2,2′-bipyridine)-ruthenium(II)-doped silica nanoparticles.45 Here, the effect of Dapp on the concentration distribution of Ru12+* was studied. With the decrease of Dapp from 5 × 10–11 to 1 × 10–12 m2/s, CMax decreased slightly, while ZMax remained basically unchanged. With the decrease of Dapp to 1 × 10–15 m2/s and further to 0 m2/s, both CMax and ZMax remained constant (Figure S18A), in which CMax is 0.60 pmol/m2 and ZMax is 3.5 μm. For k5 varied from 3 × 105 to 1 × 1010 M–1 s–1 (Figure S18B), CMax increased from 0.49 to 0.60 pmol/m2, but ZMax decreased from 3.5 to 3.4 μm. With the increase of k6 from 3 × 105 to 1 × 106 M–1 s–1 (Figure S18C), CMax increased from 0.49 to 0.69 pmol/m2, but ZMax decreased from 3.5 to 3.4 μm. With the increase of kECL from 1.6 × 107 to 3.2 × 107 (Figure S18D), and to 1 × 108 s–1, CMax decreased from 3.12 to 0.49 pmol/m2, but ZMax remained constant at 3.5 μm. Thus, ZMax is independent of H, Dapp, and kECL and slightly depends on k5 and k6. The dependence of the concentration distribution of Ru12+* on the diameter of Ru1-PS@MBs was further simulated by setting Dapp = 0 m2/s, k5 = 3 × 105 M–1 s–1, k6 = 3 × 105 M–1 s–1,8 and kECL = 1 × 108 s–1.45 It was found that when the diameter of Ru1-PS@MB increases from 5 to 10, and to 18 μm, the CMax of Ru12+* decreases from 1.26 to 0.49 and to 0.14 pmol/m2, and ZMax related to Ru12+* changes from 3.0 to 3.5, to 4.1 μm (Figure 5A and Table S14), with increase trends similar to those of TPA•+. The obtained simulated ZMax related to Ru12+* is also smaller than that of the experimental clear ECL range.

Figure 5B clearly shows different 3D concentration distributions of Ru12+* on three Ru1-PS@MBs. For 5 μm Ru1-PS@MB, the ZMax is 3.0 μm, which exceeds the radius of Ru1-PS@MB. That is to say, the light generated at 5 μm Ru1-PS@MB can cover about 2/3 of the area of the Ru1-PS@MB surface (Figure 5B,a). For 10 and 18 μm Ru1-PS@MBs, Ru12+* was distributed at the upper part of Ru1-PS@MB. The ECL emission from 10 and 18 μm Ru1-PS@MBs then can only be detected if it passed through or around the Ru1-PS@MB in top-view configurations by CMOS.

Third, the distribution of the light intensity of ECL (IECL) was further simulated, in which IECL is a parameter related to the ECL_hv concentration (IECL [W/m2] = 1 [W/mol] × ECL_hv [mol/m2]). Here, ECL_hv is regarded as matter related to the light. It was reported that the light propagation can be caused by the scattering and absorption of light on the surface of a human neck46 or a realistic human head model.47 In this work, light scattering and absorption should indeed affect the path of light and will prolong the luminous distance over Ru1-PS@MB. The “radiation in absorbing-scattering media” physics field then was employed for simulation of light scattering and absorption over Ru1-PS@MB. The dependence of the distribution of IECL on the parameters, including kECL, scattering coefficient (Sigma), and absorption coefficient on the microbead (kappa), was checked using 10 μm Ru1-PS@MBs as the model. With the increase of kECL from 1 × 105 to 1 × 108 s–1, both IECL-Max and ZMax related to IECL remained constant (Figure S19A). When Sigma increases from 0.01 to 15 μm–1 (Figure S19B), IECL-Max gradually increases and ZMax decreases from 10 to 3.6 μm. When kappa increases from 0.001 to 1 μm–1 (Figure S19C), IECL-Max gradually decreases and ZMax decreases from 10 to 3.6 μm. Thus, ZMax related to IECL strongly depends on Sigma and kappa and is independent of kECL. In this work, the experimental ECL emission only was detected in the range of 6.0 μm for 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB. According to 6.0 μm of experimental ZMax, Sigma and kappa are estimated to be 1.5 and 0.1 μm–1, respectively, for 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB. The dependence of the distribution of IECL on the diameter of Ru1-PS@MBs then was further examined by setting Sigma = 1.5 μm–1 and kappa = 0.1 μm–1. Figure 5C and D shows the distribution of IECL at different Z values and 3D intensity distributions on 5, 10, and 18 μm Ru1-PS@MB. The distribution curve of IECL is similar to that of Ru12+*, showing a first increasing and then decreasing trend over Z. With the increase of the Ru1-PS@MB diameter from 5 to 10, to 18 μm, IECL-Max decreased from 13.2 to 5.5 to 1.6 W/m2, but ZMax increased from 5 to 6, to 7 μm. These results indicate that ZMax depends not only on Sigma and kappa, but also on the diameter of the microsized beads. It can be deduced that the simulated ZMax related to IECL is more conducive to the experimental results of ECL imaging.

To further analyze the dependence of the emission intensity on the microbeads diameter, we used the top-view configuration to record the ECL images of Ru1-PS@MB and extracted the gray values using ImageJ software. Figure 5E is the ratio of the emission intensity normalized by 5 μm Ru1-PS@MB. As the diameter of the Ru1-PS@MB increases, the FL and ECL intensities increase. The ECL integral intensity [I (au)] of Ru1-PS@MB increases with the increase of diameter. Yet the integral intensity per unit area [I (au)/μm2] and volume intensity [I (au)/μm3] decrease significantly with the increase of the microbeads diameter (Table S15), similar to that of the trend of IECL-Max. Additionally, the increased ratios of the ECL intensity are much lower relative to those of their superficial areas and FL intensity. Although the light propagation will prolong the luminous distance over the microbead surface, it will also lead to light loss when the light diffuses around, which will lead to the decrease of light intensity. These results suggest that larger beads can be used for sensitive ECL analysis, and appropriately increasing the diameter of microbeads can effectively increase the ECL range and total ECL intensity.

Conclusion

We report the imaging and numerically simulated ECL emissions from Ru1-PS@MB in the presence of TPA at CGE surface via the top-view configuration. “Strong emission in edge and weak emission in center” phenomena for FL and ECL emissions were obtained from Ru1-PS@MB using a conventional CMOS detector. It was found that (1) FL emission can be clearly imaged in the actual range dtot within 8 μm, which is independent of the diameter of the microbeads and dependent on the imaging detector. (2) A clear imaging range for ECL imaging is dependent on the diameter of the microbeads and the imaging detector, which is slightly smaller than that of FL imaging. For example, ECL emission can be clearly imaged within 5 μm for 5 μm Ru1-PS@MB, 6 μm for 10 μm Ru1-PS@MB, and 7 μm for 18 μm Ru1-PS@MB. The bigger is the diameter of the microbeads (from 5 to 18 μm), the larger is the luminescence range of Ru1-PS@MB perpendicular to the electrode surface. (3) The concentration distributions of TPA•+, Ru12+*, and intensity distribution of IECL were numerically simulated by the consideration of the electrochemical oxidation of TPA. Similar trends (first increased and then decreased) were obtained for the concentration distributions of TPA•+, Ru12+*, and intensity distribution of IECL. The simulated luminescence range strongly depends on kf, Sigma, and kappa, slightly depends on ks2, k5, and k6, while it is independent of H, ks4, and kECL. (4) With the increase of the microbead diameter from 5 to 10 to 18 μm, the FL and ECL intensities and the integral intensities increase, while the integral intensity per area unit and the integral intensity per volume decrease. Additionally, the electrochemical instrument coupled with the confocal laser scanning microscope and objectives may provide more information on ECL imaging of microsized objects. This work provides insights into the ECL imaging of microsized objects on the electrode surface, which is promising for bioassay and cell imaging at the microscale level.
