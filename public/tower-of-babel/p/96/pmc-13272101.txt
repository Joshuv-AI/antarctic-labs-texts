Accelerometer‐derived daily steps, cognition, and cognitive impairment in U.S. community‐dwelling older adults: The Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study
Authors: Pablo Martinez‐Amezcua, Wuyang Zhang, Erin E Dooley, Bjoern Hornikel, Lisa Pompeii, Kelly R Evenson, Brady Rippon, B Gwen Windham, Thomas H Mosley, Jennifer A Schrack, Kelley Pettee Gabriel, Priya Palta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe relationship between daily steps and brain health is relatively underexplored, with inconsistent findings regarding the number of steps that confer cognitive benefits.METHODSWe conducted cross‐sectional and longitudinal analyses using data from the Physical Activity and Falls Ancillary (PAFA) study of the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study (ARIC) cohort. Participants wore an ActiGraph GT3X on the hip for ≥10 h on ≥3 days in 2016–2017. Daily step counts were analyzed continuously and categorically. Cognitive functioning and its change were assessed using global and domain‐specific factor scores from a neuropsychological battery. Cognitive impairment was adjudicated (mild cognitive impairment [MCI] or dementia). Regression models—adjusted for demographics, lifestyle, and chronic conditions—evaluated differences in cognitive scores, their 4‐year change, and odds of cognitive impairment by step counts. Spline terms assessed nonlinearity.RESULTSAmong 430 participants (mean age, 78.4 years; 59.5% female; 19% Black), the baseline mean daily steps were 3492 (SD = 1934). In adjusted cross‐sectional models, higher daily steps were associated with better global cognition and executive function/processing speed. Participants in the high‐stepping group (≥4025 steps) had a 0.37‐point higher executive function/processing speed score (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.17–0.55) versus the low‐step group (<2449). Those in the high‐step group had 54% lower odds of prevalent cognitive impairment (odds ratio [OR] = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.24–0.89) compared with the lowest group. Spline models showed better cognition with higher steps, plateauing after ≈4000. Step counts were not associated with 4‐year cognitive change.DISCUSSIONHigher daily step counts are associated with better cognitive function in older adults. Modest increases in activity, even among those who moved less (<4000 steps/day), may yield cognitive benefits, although reverse causation cannot be ruled out. Promoting achievable physical activity goals, such as step counts, could benefit older adults.Keywords: accelerometry, aging, cognitive function, daily steps, physical activity

BACKGROUND

Identifying modifiable lifestyle factors linked to mild cognitive impairment (MCI), dementia, and early or preclinical brain changes—when preventive interventions may be more effective—is a public health priority. Among these factors, higher levels of physical activity during midlife, particularly voluntary activity that extends into later life, have been associated with slower cognitive decline, a reduced risk of dementia, and more favorable brain structure. 1 , 2 Increasingly, research is turning to device‐based measures of physical activity, such as step counts, which provide a detailed assessment and address measurement errors that can result from recall or social desirability biases in questionnaires. 3 Daily step count is an intuitive and relatable metric commonly assessed with wearable devices. Walking is the most common form of physical activity for ambulatory adults, and many consumer‐grade devices summarize activity in daily steps. 4 Higher daily step counts are associated with lower risk of cardiovascular disease, mortality, and dementia. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Specifically, studies suggest that the most substantial mortality differences are observed when comparing individuals with very low levels to those with 3000–4000 steps per day, highlighting the importance of even modestly higher physical activity levels. 7 , 11 It remains unclear whether these step counts are associated with improvements in cognitive health, and whether a threshold exists beyond which additional steps confer diminishing or no cognitive benefits. For example, a study suggested that the optimal dose of daily steps to prevent dementia was 9800, 9 which is likely unattainable for most middle‐aged and older adults in the United States. According to normative data, the median daily steps among U.S. men decline from 6209 at age 60 to 2371 at age 80; among U.S. women, from 4,616 at age 60 to 1,718 at age 80. 12 We tested our hypothesis that higher daily step counts are associated with better cognitive function, slower rate of change, and lower odds of cognitive impairment among community‐dwelling middle‐aged and older adult participants of a cohort study in the United States. We also explored if this relationship had a nonlinear shape.

METHODS

Study population

Our study population was derived from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study(ARIC), a prospective cohort of community‐dwelling individuals from four U.S. communities: Forsyth County, North Carolina; Jackson, Mississippi; suburban Minneapolis, Minnesota; and Washington County, Maryland. 13 ARIC originally recruited 15,792 adults 45 to 64 years of age and completed baseline study visits between 1987 and 1989. These participants have been actively followed for over 30 years via in‐person visits, telephone interviews, and community surveillance. The institutional review boards approved ARIC at each participating center and coordinating center. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

At Visit 6 (2016–2017), step‐count data were collected from 512 ARIC participants in the Physical Activity and Falls Ancillary (PAFA) study, who had valid accelerometer readings (Figure S1). Our analytic sample included all 512 participants, of which 82 had missing covariate data, leaving a complete‐case population of 430 that we used for descriptive purposes (Table 1). For regression analyses, we employed multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE).

Measurements

Daily step counts

We collected step data using ActiGraph wGT3X‐BT (ActiGraph, LLC, Pensacola, FL, USA). Participants wore the device on their right hip, secured with an elastic belt, for seven consecutive days throughout all waking hours. 14 Raw (gt3x) accelerometry files were downloaded and we classified wear and non‐wear time using the Choi algorithm. 15 A day was considered valid if it included ≥600 min of wear time. Participants with fewer than 4 days of valid wear were excluded. Inclusion was determined solely by the accumulation of at least four valid days, without requiring representation of weekends or weekdays. Step counts per minute were derived using the manufacturer's proprietary step detection algorithm.

Daily (the sum of) minute‐level step counts were averaged across valid days for step variables to obtain a final “steps/day” variable.

Although participants were instructed to remove the device before bed, some wore it during sleep, potentially inflating step counts and wear time. Therefore, after visual inspection, we excluded data between midnight (12:00 a.m.) and 6:00 a.m.

Daily step counts were modeled continuously (per 1000 steps) and categorically based on the sample tertiles (low: 394–2449 steps/day, middle: 2454–4021 steps/day, high: 4025–20048 steps/day) to facilitate comparisons and interpretation.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were global and domain‐specific (memory, executive functioning/ processing speed, and language) cognitive factor scores derived from a factor analysis of the ARIC Neurocognitive Study (ARIC‐NCS) comprehensive neuropsychological battery administered at Visits 6, 7, and 9. Details on the creation and validation of cognitive factor scores in ARIC have been published elsewhere. 16 , 17 Briefly, The battery included tests across three domains: memory (Delayed Word Recall, Logical Memory, Incidental Learning), executive function/processing speed (Time to complete Trail Making Tests Parts A and B, separately; Digit Symbol Substitution Test), and language (semantic and phonemic fluency; Boston Naming Test). Scores from all 10 tests were combined using factor analysis to derive a global cognition factor score for each participant. This score was standardized to the Visit 5 baseline (mean = 0, SD = 1). Domain‐specific factor scores (memory, language, executive functioning/ processing speed) were similarly computed based on their respective tests and standardized to the same baseline. These factor scores were used to evaluate changes in cognitive performance over time, consistent with prior ARIC analyses (more details in the Table S1). 16

As secondary outcomes, we also included ARIC cognitive diagnoses from clinic exams and incident dementia status incorporating information from outside clinic exams as described previously for the whole cohort. 18 Briefly, based on information from neuropsychological assessments, Clinical Dementia Rating, Functional Activities Questionnaire, and Neuropsychiatric Inventory, ARIC participants were classified as having unimpaired cognition, MCI, or dementia at each study visit since Visit 5 (when ARIC‐NCS was launched, 2011–2013), which was produced by an established algorithm and confirmed by a team of expert clinician reviewers; briefly, for adjudication of dementia the algorithm requires substantial functional impairment (Functional Activities Questionnaire [FAQ] >5 or Clinical Dementia Rating [CDR] Sum of Boxes >2.5) 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 ; low neuropsychological test performance alone was not sufficient, and differentiation of dementia from MCI depended on impairment in daily functioning. Incident dementia after Visit 5 was identified through repeat in‐person evaluations, telephone interviews for nonattenders, and passive surveillance via medical records and death certificates. When dementia was identified through informant interview, hospitalization record, or death certificate, the diagnosis date was set 180 days before the documented incident or interview. Methods for classification of MCI and dementia were described previously. 18 In ARIC, incident dementia cases were captured routinely and ascertained using three hospitalization discharge codes and death records. 23 For this study, participants who remained dementia‐free were censored after 4 years of follow‐up.

Study covariates

Covariates were all fixed at Visit 6 (2016–2017), and included age, sex, study center (NC, MS, MN, and MD), education (≤high school vs college+), Area Deprivation Index (ADI: 1–100, higher = more deprived), hypertension (≥140/90 mmHg or medication/diagnosis), diabetes (glucose ≥126 fasting/≥200 non‐fasting or medication/diagnosis), coronary heart disease, heart failure, stroke, body mass index (BMI), and smoking (ever vs never). We also used data on race (White, Black, other [only two participants reported other]) for subgroup analysis.

Statistical analysis

We first conducted a descriptive analysis by examining participants’ characteristics and the distribution of cognitive factor scores across step‐count groups. Then, using multivariable linear regression models, we examined the cross‐sectional (Visit 6) associations between step count groups and cognitive factor scores (global and domain‐specific). All analyses were adjusted for a set of key covariates (age, sex, study center, educational attainment, ADI national ranking, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, smoking status, and the average daily time of wearing the accelerometer).

To investigate longitudinal associations of step counts (at Visit 6) with change in cognitive function, we set the time metric to days since Visit 6 and fit multilevel mixed effects models with an independent covariance structure for random effects (intercepts and slopes). Time was modeled continuously, and an interaction term between time and step‐count groups was used to test whether the rate of change in global and domain‐specific cognition differed by step‐count groups. The global and domain‐specific cognitive scores were rescaled to their mean and SD at Visit 6 (2016–2017) to facilitate comparisons.

In a secondary analysis, we examined the cross‐sectional (Visit 6) associations between step‐counts (continuous and categorically) and cognitive classification (unimpaired vs MCI/dementia) using multivariable logistic regression models. We displayed the patterns of incident dementia among individuals without dementia at or before Visit 6 (n = 11) across step‐count levels using Kaplan–Meier plots and a life table.

In sensitivity analyses, to explore potential nonlinear associations between step counts and study outcomes, we modeled step counts as categorical variable similar to prior research on daily step counts. 8 , 9 We created indicator (dummy) variables for the following step count categories: 0–2000 (reference); 2001–3000; 3001–3999; and ≥4,000 steps. Because the number of participants exceeding 5000 steps was relatively small (n = 101), we combined higher step counts into a single category of ≥4000 steps. After exploring these models, we used linear combinations of regression coefficients to perform pairwise comparisons of the step‐count categories defined in alternative ways, allowing comparisons between adjacent groups rather than only with the 0–2000 reference.

We also used restricted cubic splines (RCSs) with four knots, as they allow for flexible modeling of potential nonlinear associations. Coefficients from the RCS models, adjusted for the same covariates described previously, were used to estimate marginal global and domain‐specific cognitive scores. These estimates were plotted to visualize the shape of the associations, since the spline coefficients themselves are not directly interpretable. 24

To explore potential heterogeneity of associations, we repeated our primary analyses stratified by age (dichotomized using the median age in our sample: ≤78 and >78), sex (male and female), and race (White and Black, with two participants who reported race other than White or Black excluded from this sensitivity analysis, but included in all other models).

We examined patterns of missing data (Tables S2 and S3) and adopted the MICE approach to impute missing outcome and covariate data (MICE details in the supplemental material text and Table S4). A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was used in determining statistical significance. Analyses were conducted using Stata Version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX), and data visualization was conducted in R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, 2021).

RESULTS

Characteristics of the study sample

Table 1 presents the characteristics of our study sample. Of all study participants with complete covariate information (N = 430), the mean age was 78.4 (SD, 4.6) years, with a female majority (59.5%) and most having completed advanced education (55.8%). Hypertension, diabetes, and heart failure were the top three common cardiovascular health conditions observed, with prevalences of 74.4%, 28.6%, and 12.6%, respectively. The average BMI of the participants was 28.2 (SD, 5.2) kg/m2, and 40.9% of participants were never smokers. Overall, the average step count was 3492 (SD = 1,934), and the median was 3133 (25th and 75th percentiles = 2089 and 4473, respectively).

Differences between ARIC participants who attended Visit 6 and those in the PAFA study are presented in Table S5.

High step‐count participants were younger (77.2 vs 79.7 years), more likely male (45.5% vs 36.9%), had better socioeconomic status (ADI: 44.1 vs 55.9), lower cardiovascular disease prevalence (hypertension: 64.8% vs 80.9%; diabetes: 20.0% vs 31.9%; heart failure: 6.2% vs 17.7%), lower BMI (26.3 vs 29.9), and were more likely never‐smokers (42.8% vs 36.2%) compared to low step‐count participants.

At Visit 6, mean global cognition scores were 0.4 (SD 0.7) overall and 0.2, 0.4, and 0.6 for low, middle, and high step‐count groups respectively. Most participants were cognitively unimpaired (81.2%), with fewer having MCI (17.0%) or dementia (1.9%). The high step‐count group had more cognitively unimparied participants (86.9%) versus middle (80.6%) and low (75.9%) groups. More details on cognitive scores distributions across visits on the Figures S2–S4, and Tables S6–S8).

Cross‐sectional associations between step counts and cognitive function

Figure 1 presents the cross‐sectional associations between step counts and cognitive function from fully adjusted linear regression models using MICE for missing data. When modeled continuously, each additional 1000 daily step increment was associated with a 0.03‐unit (95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.01 to 0.07) higher score in global cognition and a 0.06‐unit (95% CI: 0.03–0.10) higher score in executive functioning/ processing speed. When modeled categorically, participants from the middle and high step count groups had 0.12 (95% CI: −0.06 to 0.29) and 0.17 (95% CI: −0.02 to 0.37) units higher global cognition scores and 0.25 (95% CI: 0.08 to 0.43) and 0.37 (95% CI: 0.17 to 0.56) units higher in executive functioning/processing speed scores relative to participants in the low step‐count group. No statistically significant associations were observed between step counts and memory and language cognitive factor scores; however, the coefficients were in the positive direction, indicating that more steps were associated with better cognitive performance in those domains.

In secondary analysis evaluating the cross‐sectional association between step‐count groups and cognitive diagnoses at Visit 6 (Table S9), we found that for each 1000 higher steps count, the odds of having MCI or dementia were lower by 16% (odds ratio [OR] = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.72–0.98). Moreover, participants in the middle (OR = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.34– 1.05) and high (OR = 0.46; 95% CI: 0.24–0.89) step count groups had an even lower odds of having MCI or dementia compared with the low step‐count group, although the finding was not statistically significant for the middle step‐count group.

Longitudinal associations between step counts and cognitive function and Dementia‐free survival

Consistent with the results from cross‐sectional analyses, mixed‐effects models (Table S10) showed a baseline difference across step‐count groups for executive functioning/processing speed scores comparing participants from the middle (β = 0.30; 95% CI: 0.05–0.55) and high (β = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.14–0.70) step‐count groups to the low. We did not find a statistically significant difference in the change in cognitive scores over time (overall or across step‐count groups: no interactions between step‐count group and time).

After a 4‐year follow‐up (median follow‐up time was 3.90 years, 25th and 75th percentiles 3.72 and 4.00 years, respectively; Figure 2), we observed 35 incident dementia cases in our study population, with 14 cases occurring among participants from the low step‐count group, 12 from the middle group, and 9 from the high group. Overall, >90% of participants remained dementia‐free after 4 years. Moreover, visual inspection of the Kaplan–Meier plot suggests that participants from the middle and high step‐count groups had similar survival throughout the study. Those in the low step‐count group had a lower dementia‐free survival starting around 1.5 years after the time origin. Still, the log‐rank test suggested no statistically significant difference (p = 0.385) in the dementia‐free survival distribution across the step‐count groups. We set up our data for a Cox proportional hazard regression model. However, due to the low number of events and relatively small sample size, considering the number of covariates, the model did not run.

Using alternative step categorizations (Figure 3) showed similar results. Positive associations persisted for global cognition and executive functioning/processing speed, with the largest benefits comparing 0–2000 versus 2000–3000 steps/day. Higher step levels showed additional but smaller benefits. No consistent patterns emerged for memory or language. In pairwise comparisons (Table S11), we did not observe significant differences in cognitive scores between adjacent step categories (e.g., those who walked 3000–4000 steps compared with 2000–3000 had a 0.04 higher global cognitive score [95% CI: −0.14 to 0.21]).

RCS models (Figure 4) showed nonlinear associations with steep cognitive improvements from 0 to 4000 steps/day, then plateauing. Narrow 95% CIs (especially 4000–8000 steps) supported robustness. Discussion

Higher daily step counts were associated with better global cognitive scores and executive functioning/processing speed and lower odds of MCI/dementia in cross‐sectional analysis. In longitudinal analyses, we did not observe significant changes in cognition over 4 years of follow‐up, and the rate of change did not differ by the number of daily steps at baseline. To provide context for the magnitude of the associations, previous studies have shown a typical age‐related decline in global cognitive scores of 0.04–0.05 SD units per year. 25 , 26 Our observed 0.20 SD difference in global cognitive function between high versus low step‐count groups represents ≈4–5 years of typical cognitive decline.

Our findings indicate a nonlinear relationship where most cognitive benefits occur at the lower end of the step‐count range (≈0 to 4000 steps), with little to no improvement in cognitive scores beyond that. This pattern suggests a possible graded association, implying that even moderate daily activity may offer cognitive advantages in this population. For context, dose–response evidence for other health outcomes indicates that ≈2600–2800 steps/day are associated with significant reductions in all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular disease risk, with progressive risk reductions up to about 8800 and 7200 steps/day, respectively. 27 Few participants in our sample reached high daily step counts; more data on individuals with higher step counts are needed to confirm whether higher levels provide additional benefit. Nonetheless, these results support using step counts as a practical measure of physical activity and may help guide future efforts to include step‐based recommendations for ambulatory adults. 28

Among the mechanisms explaining our findings, daily steps serve as a marker of physical activity, which is associated with better brain and cognitive health 1 , 2 via improvement in cardiovascular risk factors, cerebrovascular health, increased cerebral blood flow, neuroplasticity, reduced inflammation, better sleep, and beneficial myokines. 29 , 30 , 31

We found no statistically significant cognitive decline, likely reflecting the short follow‐up (≈4 years) and a relatively healthy sample, which limited variability and our ability to detect differences by step‐count groups. Selection and attrition biases may also have contributed: participants who stayed in the study were probably healthier and more cognitively resilient, thereby reducing generalizability and attenuating associations as less healthy individuals were more likely to drop out. This survivor bias narrows variability in cognitive trajectories. In addition, those who joined the PAFA ARIC sub‐study may have been more health conscious or active than the general population, masking true links between physical activity and cognitive decline. Similar selective attrition has been documented in studies of smoking and cognitive decline. 32

Previous studies have investigated the association between daily step counts and cognitive function. A study that used data from the Swedish SNAC‐K study found an association between daily step counts (continuously scaled per each 1000 steps) and processing speed, but no statistically significant findings with global cognition or executive functioning/processing speed. 33 But this study did not explore associations modeling step counts in a more flexible way as we did. Another study from the Women's Health Initiative explored daily steps and dementia risk and found that higher step counts reduced the risk of dementia. 34 This study did model step counts flexibly and found patterns similar to what we observed, with great benefit going from ≈0 to 2500 steps, followed by a plateau. But the study was limited to women, and most were non‐Hispanic White, thereby limiting its generalizability. Finally, a recent meta‐analysis investigating the health outcomes of step counts found higher daily step counts to be linked to lower risks of dementia and other major health outcomes, with most benefits seen at around 7000 steps per day. 11

Our study has some limitations. First, although we adjusted for several potential confounders, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Second, reverse causation could explain our cross‐sectional findings. Individuals with higher cognitive scores may participate in more activities that also promote or require increased physical movement. Still, recent research suggests that cognitive function has little impact on physical activity. 35 Third, our study had a short follow‐up time, which affects the number of events observed in survival analysis, and a relatively small sample size, with both of these factors impacting the statistical power. Finally, the impact of selective attrition we described may limit our ability to see longitudinal associations.

Conclusion

We tested the hypothesis that higher daily step counts are associated with better cognitive function and lower odds of cognitive impairment in older adults. Participants with ≥4025 daily steps had 0.36 points higher executive functioning/processing speed scores and 54% lower odds of MCI/dementia. The nonlinear dose–response relationship showed cognitive benefits even at low step counts, indicating modest activity increases may provide meaningful protection. This achievable 4025‐step threshold, if replicated in other studies, could inform public health recommendations, as step counts provide a more concrete and easily communicated target than traditional guidelines of 150 min/week of moderate‐to‐vigorous activity for health promotion. Future research should examine longer follow‐up periods and diverse populations to establish causality and underlying mechanisms.
