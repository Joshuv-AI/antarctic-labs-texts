Dog bite–associated pathogens: advances in pathogenic mechanisms and systemic clinical consequences in humans
Authors: Li Du, Xudong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDog bites are a common cause of injury worldwide and constitute a major public health challenge. In addition to mechanical trauma, they inoculate wounds with complex polymicrobial communities derived from the canine oral microbiota and, in rabies-endemic regions, may transmit rabies virus (RABV), leading to clinical outcomes ranging from localized wound infection to fulminant systemic disease and fatal encephalitis. This review summarizes recent advances in the microbiological profiles, pathogenic mechanisms, and systemic consequences of dog bite–associated infections in humans, integrating evidence from human and veterinary medicine within a One Health framework. Key bacterial pathogens include Pasteurella multocida (P. multocida), Capnocytophaga canimorsus (C. canimorsus), staphylococci, streptococci, and anaerobes, which can cause cellulitis, abscesses, necrotizing soft tissue infection, sepsis, meningitis, and endocarditis through tissue invasion, toxin production, and immune evasion. RABV remains the most devastating consequence of dog bites, with an almost universally fatal outcome after symptom onset, driven by glycoprotein-mediated neuronal entry, retrograde axonal transport, and profound evasion of host immune responses. Children, older adults, and immunocompromised individuals bear a disproportionate burden of severe disease. Reducing dog bite–associated morbidity and mortality requires coordinated progress in wound management, rapid molecular diagnostics, rational antimicrobial use, canine vaccination, and timely rabies post-exposure prophylaxis, underscoring the critical value of a One Health strategy.Keywords: Dog bites, Bacterial infections, Rabies virus, Systemic clinical consequences, One health

Introduction

Dog bites are a common cause of trauma worldwide and pose a multifaceted threat to public health. In most injury surveillance studies, dog bites account for the majority of animal-related injuries, with a disproportionate burden in children and adolescents [1]. Rabies remains the most serious fatal consequence of dog bites and contributes a major disease burden, particularly in Asia and Africa. In India alone, an estimated 20,000 people die each year from dog-mediated rabies, representing about one-third of global deaths [2]. Studies from China, Iran, Bulgaria, Brazil, and other countries consistently identify dog bites as the leading route of exposure for human rabies [1], [3], [4]. A large proportion of these bites are inflicted by free-roaming dogs, and vaccination coverage among owned dogs is generally low.

The impact of dog bites extends beyond immediate tissue damage to include complex infectious complications. Although rabies is the most feared outcome, the more frequent and clinically urgent problem in routine practice is bacterial infection. These infections are typically characterized by acute onset and rapid progression, and they represent a major cause of revisits, hospital admissions, and long-term sequelae. They occur when disruption of the skin barrier allows polymicrobial commensal flora from the canine oral cavity to be inoculated into host tissues. Established primary pathogens include Pasteurella spp., Staphylococcus spp., and Streptococcus spp., as well as various anaerobic bacteria (Table 1) [5], [6]. Using diverse virulence mechanisms, these organisms can cause disease ranging from local suppuration to severe systemic infection. A systematic understanding of their biological characteristics and pathogenic pathways is therefore essential to guide early, targeted antimicrobial therapy.

Pathogens associated with dog bite injuries

Key pathogens

Pasteurella multocida

P. multocida is a small, facultatively anaerobic, Gram-negative coccobacillus that commonly colonizes the oral cavity and upper respiratory tract of dogs and cats [7]. Multiple studies identify P. multocida as the predominant pathogen in acute dog bite infections. Infections following dog bites commonly present as rapidly developing local soft-tissue infections, characterized by pain, erythema, swelling, and cellulitis, sometimes progressing to abscess formation [7].

Upon inoculation into human tissue, P. multocida exhibits rapid proliferation facilitated by its polysaccharide capsule, which mediates resistance to phagocytosis [8]. The bacterium secretes various virulence factors, including dermonecrotic toxin and lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which disrupt local cellular integrity and promote inflammation, leading to the characteristic rapidly progressing cellulitis, pain, and edema [9].

While infections are typically localized, the organism can invade the bloodstream, particularly in immunocompromised hosts or those with hepatic comorbidities. Systemic dissemination may result in severe metastatic infections, including meningitis, osteomyelitis, infective endocarditis, and sepsis [10]. Notably, the absence of skin barrier disruption does not preclude infection; transmucosal transmission via licking has been documented to cause fatal septicemia and meningitis in infants, underscoring the need for stringent hygiene advisories for households with young children [11]. In patients with cardiac valvular disease and a history of animal contact, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for invasive P. multocida infection. Management requires prompt antimicrobial therapy combined with timely surgical intervention when indicated.

Staphylococcus species

Staphylococci represent the second most frequently isolated pathogens in dog bite wound infections after P. multocida. This genus encompasses zoonotic Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) as well as canine-adapted species, primarily Staphylococcus pseudintermedius (formerly S. intermedius) and Staphylococcus schleiferi, which constitute a significant portion of the normal canine oral and skin flora [12].

The pathogenicity of staphylococci in dog bite infections stems from a combination of virulence factors and increasing antimicrobial resistance. S. aureus strains isolated from canine sources frequently harbor virulence determinants, including Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL) and enterotoxin genes, contributing to necrotizing pneumonia and toxic shock syndrome [13]. A significant clinical challenge is the high prevalence of antibiotic resistance, particularly methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), which severely limits empirical therapeutic options [14].

Similarly, S. intermedius possesses potent hemolysins and leukocidins that facilitate deep tissue invasion. Although often misidentified as S. aureus by automated systems, S. intermedius is increasingly recognized as a causative agent of rapidly progressive cellulitis and, in rare instances, fulminant endocarditis following minor bites [15]. Clinically, S. aureus infections most often present as local cellulitis or lymphangitis but may progress to severe systemic disease. Notably, bidirectional transmission between dogs and humans occurs, and infections can manifest even after minor exposure; fulminant endocarditis following seemingly insignificant dog bites has been reported [16].

Beyond typical staphylococci, Neisseria weaveri, a component of the canine oropharyngeal flora, deserves attention. Infections following dog bites have been reported in patients with underlying conditions, presenting with cellulitis, abscess formation, bacteremia, and, in severe cases, sepsis [17].

Anaerobic bacteria and polymicrobial synergy

Beyond aerobic flora, obligate anaerobes from the canine oral cavity play a critical role in severe, necrotizing dog bite wound infections. Commonly implicated genera include Bacteroides, Porphyromonas, Prevotella, and Fusobacterium [6].

Anaerobic bacteria thrive in the hypoxic, necrotic environments created by tissue trauma [18]. Metagenomic analyses confirm that these organisms, particularly within the phylum Bacteroidetes, are abundant in the canine oral microbiome and synergistically disrupt host barriers. Their pathogenicity stems from the production of tissue-degrading enzymes (e.g., collagenase, hyaluronidase) and metabolic byproducts such as short-chain fatty acids and ammonia, which induce liquefactive necrosis, abscess formation, foul-smelling discharge, and subcutaneous emphysema. The clinical significance of these organisms lies in their propensity for hematogenous dissemination, leading to invasive diseases including sepsis, meningitis, infective endocarditis, and necrotizing fasciitis [18].

Capnocytophaga canimorsus

Among these, C. canimorsus warrants particular attention due to its high mortality rate despite low incidence. This fastidious, capnophilic, facultative anaerobe possesses a unique LPS structure that triggers excessive cytokine release and profound immunosuppression, leading to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), purpura fulminans, and Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome [19]. Meningitis represents the second most frequent clinical presentation, occurring even in previously healthy individuals [20]. Unlike typical bite infections, C. canimorsus can cause fulminant sepsis following trivial non-bite exposures (e.g., licking) in asplenic or immunocompromised individuals [21]. It is also a recognized cause of infective endocarditis, which often presents with nonspecific symptoms. Early recognition, prompt wound debridement, and initial treatment with penicillin combined with a β-lactamase inhibitor are critical for improving outcomes, although infection may rapidly progress to septic shock and death in high-risk patients [21].

Clostridium tetani

Clostridium tetani represents another fatal complication arising from dog bites. This strictly anaerobic, spore-forming bacillus germinates within devitalized tissue, producing tetanospasmin—a potent neurotoxin that blocks inhibitory neurotransmitters in the central nervous system [22]. This results in unopposed muscle contraction, manifesting as trismus (“lockjaw”), generalized rigidity, and painful spasms [23]. Although tetanus following dog bites is rare, the high case-fatality rate emphasizes the necessity of identifying high-risk wounds and administering appropriate tetanus prophylaxis [22].

Rabies virus (RABV)

While bacterial pathogens account for most infectious complications following dog bites, RABV represents a far more lethal and essentially untreatable threat. RABV is the leading cause of dog bite–related mortality, with a case fatality rate approaching 100% once clinical symptoms develop, and remains a major public health problem, particularly in resource-limited regions [24]. RABV, a member of the genus Lyssavirus within the family Rhabdoviridae, causes disease through selective neuroinvasion and progressive damage to the central nervous system. Beyond classical RABV, the genus Lyssavirus encompasses several other zoonotic species capable of causing fatal encephalitis. Notably, human cases and fatalities have been documented in Europe (European bat lyssaviruses 1 and 2; EBLV-1/2), Australia (Australian bat lyssavirus; ABLV), and South Africa (Duvenhage lyssavirus; DUVV), underscoring the global public health relevance of non-RABV lyssaviruses [25], [26]. Following entry, the virus spreads via peripheral nerves to the brain, leading to severe neurological dysfunction and death. Recent research has focused on viral receptor recognition, neural transport mechanisms, regulation of viral replication, and host immune responses, providing important insights for the development of improved vaccines and antiviral strategies.

Structure of RABV

RABV is an enveloped, bullet-shaped virus, approximately 180 nm in length and 75 nm in diameter, with a single-stranded, negative-sense RNA genome. The genome encodes five structural proteins: nucleoprotein (N), phosphoprotein (P), matrix protein (M), glycoprotein (G), and the large polymerase protein (L) (Fig. 1) [27]. The N protein encapsidates viral RNA to form the ribonucleoprotein complex, which serves as the template for viral transcription and replication. The P protein functions as an essential polymerase cofactor and also contributes to immune evasion by interfering with host innate immune signaling pathways, including STAT-mediated responses [28]. The M protein links the ribonucleoprotein complex to the viral envelope and plays a central role in virion assembly, budding, and virus-induced cellular dysfunction [29]. The G protein is the only surface protein of RABV and is the principal determinant of receptor binding, membrane fusion, neurotropism, and induction of virus-neutralizing antibodies [30]. The L protein possesses RNA-dependent RNA polymerase activity and mediates viral mRNA transcription and genome replication [27], [31]. After entry at the bite site, RABV infects peripheral nerve endings and is transported retrogradely along axonal microtubules to the spinal cord and brain, mainly through dynein-dependent transport. This neural spread underlies the long incubation period and subsequent fatal encephalomyelitis once central nervous system infection is established [31].

RABV receptors

Early studies identified several key receptors mediating RABV entry. The nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR), located at neuromuscular junctions, is involved in the initial binding and concentration of the virus at infection sites. Subsequent studies established neural cell adhesion molecule (NCAM) and the p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR) as major functional receptors in neural tissues [32]. NCAM, widely expressed on neurons, facilitates viral attachment through interactions between its immunoglobulin-like domains and the G protein, while p75NTR contributes via direct binding to key G protein residues, including K330 and R333 [33].

Recent genome-wide screening approaches have further expanded the repertoire of host entry factors, highlighting the complexity of RABV tropism. Integrin αvβ3 has been identified as a co-receptor, particularly in glial cells. In addition, metabotropic glutamate receptor 2 (mGluR2), a class C G protein–coupled receptor, forms a complex with transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) to mediate viral endocytosis [34]. Neuropilin-2 (NRP2) represents another important entry factor, interacting with transforming growth factor-β receptor I (TGFBR1) to activate Cdc42 signaling, promote actin polymerization, and facilitate viral internalization [35]. These discoveries provide novel opportunities for antiviral intervention; for example, soluble mGluR2-based receptors exhibit significant neutralizing activity [34]. Future studies integrating single-cell analyses, structural biology, and gene-editing technologies are needed to clarify receptor dynamics under physiological conditions.

Replication regulation and immune evasion of RABV

The trimeric structure of the RABV G protein mediates membrane fusion in acidic endosomal environments, enabling viral genome release into the cytoplasm [30]. Sequence variations within the receptor-binding domain influence affinity for host receptors such as NCAM, p75NTR, and mGluR2, thereby contributing to neurotropism and invasiveness [36]. Notably, single amino acid substitutions in the G protein, particularly at position 333, can markedly alter viral virulence [37]. G protein expression levels are also closely associated with pathogenicity: higher expression can induce neuronal apoptosis, enhance antiviral immune responses, and promote viral attenuation. Reverse genetics–mediated mutations, such as K83R, can upregulate G protein expression, increase blood–brain barrier permeability through matrix metalloproteinase induction, facilitate immune-cell infiltration, and reduce neurovirulence. The G protein also interacts with the intracellular domain of the M protein, regulating its localization, aggregation, and viral budding [38].

Viral transcription and replication are mediated by the L protein in complex with the P protein, which acts as both a chaperone and essential cofactor. Phosphorylation of P protein by host kinases, such as casein kinase II, functions as a molecular switch that activates polymerase activity and enhances RNA synthesis fidelity and processivity [39]. Host heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70) further stabilizes the viral ribonucleoprotein complex through interaction with the N protein, thereby promoting replication efficiency [40]. Blood–brain barrier integrity is also a critical determinant of pathogenicity, infection with attenuated strains induces chemokines such as IP-10, increases barrier permeability, and facilitates immune cell infiltration into the central nervous system, ultimately enhancing viral clearance and reducing tissue damage [41]. Collectively, RABV replication and pathogenicity are governed by a tightly regulated network involving viral proteins—centered on the G protein—and host factors including kinases, Hsp70, autophagy components, and immune signaling molecules. Dissecting this network provides essential targets for antiviral development and rational vaccine attenuation.

New genes involved in RABV infection

RABV infection is governed by complex interactions between viral components and host cellular factors. Beyond its classical neurotropism, RABV has evolved strategies to exploit host dependency factors while counteracting antiviral restriction mechanisms. Advances in high-throughput genetic screening, transcriptomics, and proteomics have markedly expanded the repertoire of host genes involved in viral entry, replication, immune evasion, and cell fate regulation, providing new insights into rabies pathogenesis and potential therapeutic targets (Table 2).

Host Determinants and Underlying Mechanisms Controlling Rabies Virus Replication.

Host dependency factors facilitating viral entry and internalization

Viral entry represents the first critical step in RABV infection and relies on multiple host receptors and accessory proteins. Neuropilin-2 (NRP2) and metabotropic glutamate receptor 2 (mGluR2) have been identified as key entry receptors. NRP2 cooperates with transforming growth factor-β receptor I (TGFBR1) to activate actin polymerization, thereby promoting viral internalization [35]. Consistently, mGluR2-deficient mice exhibit markedly improved survival following RABV infection compared with wild-type controls [34]. Integrin β1 (ITGB1) directly interacts with the RABV-G and co-internalizes with virions, facilitating early infection and peripheral nervous system dissemination [42]. In addition, the serine/threonine kinase AAK1 is required for clathrin-mediated endocytosis by phosphorylating the μ subunit of adaptor protein complex 2 (AP2M1), and pharmacological inhibition of AAK1, such as with sunitinib, effectively suppresses viral entry [43].

Host factors regulating viral replication, immune evasion, and inflammatory responses

After entry, RABV replication, assembly, release, and immune evasion are tightly regulated by host factors. Synaptosome-associated protein 25 (SNAP25) contributes to viral particle release, and its knockdown reduces nucleoprotein expression and viral replication [44]. Activation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) promotes RABV replication, whereas the natural compound pentagalloylglucose inhibits STAT3 signaling via the miR-455-5p axis, reducing IL-6 production and viral replication [45]. RABV also manipulates host ubiquitin pathways to suppress antiviral immunity. The E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 promotes viral replication by targeting interferon regulatory factor 7 (IRF7) for degradation, thereby inhibiting type I interferon responses [46]. Conversely, FBXL18 mediates atypical K11-linked ubiquitination of BST2, stabilizing this restriction factor and driving persistent NF-κB activation, excessive inflammation, and aggravated neuropathology. Genetic depletion of FBXL18 reduces IL-6 production, viral replication, and disease severity in vivo [47]. In addition, the RABV M protein enhances replication through interaction with host HDAC6 and activation of the MEK/ERK signaling pathway [29].

Antiviral defense network of host restriction factors

Host cells deploy multilayered restriction mechanisms that target different stages of the viral life cycle. G protein–coupled receptor 17 (GPR17) restricts RABV replication by activating NF-κB via the cAMP–PKA pathway, inducing BAK-mediated apoptosis in infected neurons [48]. Protein disulfide isomerase PDIA3 interacts with the viral G protein and, in an AP3B1-dependent manner, directs it toward autophagy–lysosome degradation, thereby limiting infection [33]. At later stages, TRIM72 targets the viral M protein, promoting its ubiquitination at K195 and impairing virion assembly and release [49].

Additional immune regulators contribute to antiviral defense. The interferon-stimulated genes Ifit2 and Ifit3 act synergistically to inhibit RABV replication [50], while TRIM25 suppresses infection through activation of the RIG-I–interferon axis [51]. The transmembrane E3 ubiquitin ligase MARCH8 further restricts RABV by ubiquitinating and downregulating viral envelope glycoproteins, exhibiting broad-spectrum antiviral activity [52].

The complex role of cell death programs in mediating viral infection

RABV infection triggers multiple programmed cell death pathways, including apoptosis, autophagy, and pyroptosis, which contribute both to antiviral defense and neuropathological damage. The G and M proteins serve as central regulators of these processes.

Overexpression of the G protein is sufficient to activate intrinsic apoptosis, characterized by caspase-3 activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cytoskeletal disruption, with apoptosis severity correlating with G protein levels [53]. G protein accumulation may also influence autophagy progression, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. The P protein likely modulates apoptosis indirectly by regulating viral gene expression, including G protein levels [31]. The M protein functions as a multifunctional regulatory hub. It directly targets mitochondria, inducing membrane potential dissipation and mitochondrial apoptosis at later infection stages. Simultaneously, the M protein recruits the host E3 ubiquitin ligase NEDD4 via its PPxY motif to induce autophagosome formation and interacts with the mitochondrial transporter Slc25a4, disrupting cellular energy metabolism and calcium homeostasis [54]. Host autophagy is further modulated during infection: overexpression of the neuron-specific autophagy regulator Bif-1 alleviates virus-induced autophagic flux blockade and suppresses replication [55]. Certain RABV strains activate a CASP2–AMPK–MAPK–AKT–MTOR signaling cascade via BECN1, leading to autophagosome accumulation [56]. The functional outcome of autophagy appears context dependent, varying with viral strain, cell type, and infection stage. In addition to apoptosis and autophagy, virulent RABV strains can induce neuronal pyroptosis, characterized by activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, Gasdermin D cleavage, membrane pore formation, and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby exacerbating neuronal injury [57]. Collectively, RABV manipulates host cell death networks through its G and M proteins to balance viral replication, immune evasion, and tissue damage. The interplay among apoptosis, autophagy, and pyroptosis ultimately determines infection outcome and disease severity (Fig. 2).

RABV infection thus involves a broad spectrum of host genes regulating viral entry, replication, immune evasion, and neuropathology. Studies on receptors and signaling molecules such as mGluR2, GPR17, ITGB1, and AAK1, together with analyses of interferon-stimulated genes and TRIM family proteins, have substantially advanced our understanding of host–virus interactions. Ongoing transcriptomic and proteomic profiling continues to reveal novel host factors, offering promising targets for improved diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of rabies.

Hazards of dog bites

Whether addressing bacterial or viral pathogenesis, the ultimate consequence is direct injury to specific host organs and systems. Clarifying the multisystem clinical harm of dog bites provides a key link between mechanistic research and clinical diagnosis and management. Dog-bite injury spans a continuum from local to systemic and from physiological to psychological outcomes, with severity determined by bite location and depth, inoculated pathogens, and host factors (Fig. 3).

Nervous system

Neurological manifestations

Dog bites pose the most serious risk to the nervous system. If the dog carries RABV, the virus can ascend via peripheral nerves to the central nervous system; once in the spinal cord and brain, it replicates extensively and causes widespread neuronal damage, heralding clinical disease [31]. Rabies is classically divided into furious and paralytic forms. Furious rabies accounts for ∼80% of cases and is characterized by marked agitation, restlessness, hydrophobia, aerophobia, photophobia, dysphagia, dyspnea, and sometimes hallucinations or aggressive behavior [58]. Paralytic rabies comprises ∼20% of cases and presents with early flaccid paralysis, typically beginning in the bitten limb and progressing to generalized paralysis with respiratory muscle involvement; hydrophobia and agitation are minimal or absent, which can delay recognition. In addition, deep, contaminated bite wounds may provide an anaerobic niche for Clostridium tetani [22]. Its neurotoxin, tetanospasmin, travels retrogradely in motor neurons and ascends to the spinal cord and brainstem, producing manifestations such as trismus, opisthotonus, and generalized muscle spasms [22]. Although tetanus risk after dog bites is not inherently higher than with other wounds, its lethality makes prevention and early recognition essential; therefore, documenting tetanus immunization status is a core element of initial dog-bite management.

Motor system

Dog bites can cause motor-system injury through both direct mechanical trauma and secondary infection. The bite force may result in fractures, joint dislocations, tendon or ligament tears, neurovascular injury, and extensive soft-tissue damage, with severity depending on the bite site, depth, and dog size [59]. Secondary infection further increases morbidity, as polymicrobial canine oral flora, including Pasteurella, Staphylococcus, and anaerobes, can spread into deep tissues and occasionally involve bones or joints, causing osteomyelitis or septic arthritis [8]. The hand is particularly vulnerable because of its tendon sheaths, joints, and enclosed fascial compartments, where even small puncture wounds may rapidly progress to deep-space infection and functional impairment [6]. Therefore, management should include careful neurovascular and tendon assessment, timely imaging when deep injury is suspected, thorough irrigation and debridement, empiric antibiotics covering common aerobic and anaerobic oral flora, and early rehabilitation to preserve long-term function.

Circulatory system

The primary hazard of dog bites to the circulatory system arises from systemic infection following bacterial entry into the bloodstream, which can be life-threatening. Local wound infection may rapidly progress to bacteremia, systemic inflammatory response, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome.

Among implicated pathogens, C. canimorsus, a conditional commensal of canine and feline oral cavities, is a major cause of severe circulatory infections, including sepsis and septic shock [20]. Infection risk is markedly elevated in individuals with asplenia, immunosuppression, or alcohol abuse. Once established, C. canimorsus infection often progresses rapidly to fulminant sepsis, with complications such as disseminated intravascular coagulation, acute renal failure, purpura fulminans, and multiple organ failure, ultimately resulting in hypotension, tissue hypoperfusion, and septic shock [60]. Fatal cases have been reported, including patients with hyposplenism or alcohol abuse who developed rapid circulatory collapse following dog bites [60]. In addition, hematogenous dissemination may lead to lymph node necrosis and metastatic abscess formation [15]. These findings underscore the need for heightened vigilance for circulatory system infection in dog-bite patients, particularly those at high risk.

Respiratory system

Dog bites can result in severe and potentially fatal respiratory compromise, depending on bite location, depth, and secondary infection. Complications range from localized subcutaneous emphysema to airway obstruction, mediastinitis, and tension pneumothorax [61]. Anatomical factors play a critical role, rendering children particularly vulnerable because of shorter necks and smaller airway diameters. Pediatric neck bites have been reported to cause respiratory failure, extensive emphysema, and laryngeal instability requiring intensive airway management, while adult cases may present with acute dyspnea, neck swelling, stridor, and the need for emergency tracheostomy [62]. Although direct tracheal or laryngeal injury is uncommon, especially in children, its occurrence demands rapid diagnosis and intervention because pediatric airway management is inherently difficult.

Beyond mechanical trauma, secondary bacterial infection from canine oral flora represents another major cause of respiratory complications. Pathogens may spread locally or hematogenously, and severe infections such as pneumonia may occur, particularly in immunocompromised individuals following dog exposure [63]. Accordingly, clinical evaluation of dog-bite victims—especially those with head, neck, or chest involvement—should extend beyond local wound assessment. Close monitoring for respiratory symptoms, timely imaging, early antibiotics covering typical canine oral flora, and prompt surgical management of deep infections are essential.

Reproductive system

Although rare, dog bites involving the reproductive system can cause distinctive anatomical and functional injuries with potentially overlooked long-term consequences [64]. Reported cases include scrotal bites leading to acute abdominal symptoms and intra-abdominal organ perforation, complete unilateral testicular loss, and traumatic resection of the vas deferens in pediatric patients [65]. Current clinical reports primarily emphasize acute management—such as wound debridement, rabies prophylaxis, and antibiotic therapy—while systematic investigation and long-term follow-up remain limited.

Dog bites may impair male reproductive function through direct mechanical damage, secondary infection and inflammation, and stress-induced neuroendocrine dysregulation [66]. Potential effects include injury to seminiferous tubules and the vas deferens, orchitis or epididymitis, and disruption of gonadotropin and testosterone secretion via the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis. However, evidence regarding long-term effects on spermatogenesis and endocrine function remains sparse, highlighting the need for longitudinal studies to clarify reproductive outcomes.

Systemic hazards

The most common hazards of dog bites are local infection and soft tissue injury. The canine oral cavity harbors diverse pathogens, including Pasteurella, C. canimorsus, Eikenella, Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, and anaerobes, which can cause cellulitis, abscesses, and invasive wound infections. Children represent a high-risk group, particularly those under five years of age, as facial exposure is more common due to shorter stature. Facial bites are associated not only with infection risk but also with cosmetic injury and psychological distress [67]. Similarly, bites to the hand are prone to deep infection because of complex compartmental anatomy, and delayed treatment may result in significant functional impairment. In rare cases, subcutaneous gas formation manifested as crepitus may indicate severe infection [68].

Beyond physical injury, dog bites—especially in children—can result in profound psychological sequelae, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, fear, depression, and behavioral changes [69]. Reported outcomes include persistent fear of dogs, avoidance behavior, selective mutism, night terrors, enuresis, aggression, social withdrawal, and learning difficulties. These effects may persist for years, emphasizing that the psychosocial burden of dog bites is often underestimated despite relatively low mortality [70].

Overall, the harm caused by dog bites spans a continuum from local to systemic injury and from physiological to psychological impact. Immediate risks primarily involve deep tissue infection caused by complex microbial flora, with particularly severe consequences for facial and hand injuries. In advanced cases, uncontrolled infection may progress to life-threatening multi-organ failure. In parallel, psychological trauma represents a substantial but often overlooked component of dog-bite morbidity. Comprehensive assessment and timely intervention are therefore essential to mitigate the full spectrum of health risks following dog-bite injury.

Summary and perspectives

Dog bite injuries constitute a global public health challenge characterized by complex microbial exposure and multisystem damage. This review highlights that the threat arises from the diverse canine oral microbiota—exemplified by P. multocida, antibiotic-resistant staphylococci, and anaerobes—together with the almost invariably fatal RABV Through distinct virulence mechanisms, these pathogens can cause outcomes ranging from severe local infections, such as necrotizing fasciitis, to disseminated systemic diseases including sepsis and meningitis. Children, older adults, and immunocompromised individuals are disproportionately affected. In particular, RABV induces irreversible central nervous system damage through glycoprotein-mediated neuronal entry and effective immune evasion.

Despite progress in understanding pathogenic mechanisms and preventive measures, including improved wound management and intradermal immunization strategies, substantial knowledge gaps remain. Current research largely focuses on individual dominant pathogens, with limited insight into the canine oral microbiome as a functional ecosystem and its interactions with the human host. In addition, key aspects of RABV dissemination and immune evasion within the central nervous system remain incompletely defined. Most available evidence is derived from animal models, and translating these findings into effective clinical diagnostics and therapies continues to be challenging.

Achieving the public health goal of eliminating dog-mediated rabies and reducing bite-related morbidity requires a fundamental paradigm shift in prevention and management. Scientifically, research should move beyond isolated pathogen analysis toward integrated investigation of canine oral microbial ecology and host–pathogen dynamics. From a public health perspective, rigorous implementation of the One Health approach is essential, with priority given to cost-effective source interventions such as large-scale dog vaccination to shift from reactive control to proactive prevention. Clinically, adoption of rapid molecular diagnostics, individualized risk stratification, and emerging biologics, including monoclonal antibodies, will be critical for advancing precision medicine. This integrated transformation—combining scientific innovation, public health strategy, and global collaboration—offers the most viable path to overcoming current control bottlenecks and achieving sustainable long-term solutions.
