Correlated variability and its attentional modulation depend on anatomical connectivity
Authors: Shraddha Shah, Jacqueline Hembrook-Short, Vanessa Mock, Farran Briggs
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe term “functional connectivity” has been increasingly used to describe correlated variability across neurons and brain regions. Whether correlated variability between neurons is dependent upon their anatomical connections has not been explicitly tested. We examined correlated spiking variability, and its attentional modulation, among visual cortical neurons sharing different types of anatomical connections. We found that both correlated variability and its attention modulation depend on the specific types of anatomical connections shared by neurons. These results highlight the importance of considering anatomical connections when inferring functional interactions in neuronal circuits.

Paying attention to a particular element in a visual scene enhances our perception of the stimulus attributes of the attended element. Although behavioral and psychophysical effects of attention are clear (1, 2), the neuronal mechanisms that give rise to these robust behavioral effects remain elusive. One attractive candidate mechanism is based on a large body of work showing that attention reduces the inherent variability in visual neuronal responses (3). Neurons throughout the visual system respond to repeated presentations of an identical stimulus with measurable variability across trials. Some of this variability is shared across pairings of simultaneously sampled neurons and many have suggested that such shared variability within neuronal populations is detrimental to information encoding and communication (4, 5, but see ref. 6). Accordingly, results demonstrating that attention reduces shared spiking variability among pairings of visual cortical neurons (7, 8) provided a candidate mechanism by which attention might improve encoding and transmission of visual signals. Subsequent observations, however, indicated that attentional modulation of shared variability depends on a number of factors including the visual area or areas, and even the specific layers, in which neurons are sampled, as well as the types of neurons sampled (9–13). Another factor that could be critical in dictating shared spiking variability across neurons is whether and how sampled neurons are anatomically connected. Whether anatomical connectivity contributes to shared variability, or whether attention differentially regulates shared spiking variability in neuronal circuits compared to randomly sampled, unconnected pairings of neurons has not been tested in alert animals. Studies showing larger shared spiking variability among neurons located closer to one another (14, 15) suggest that anatomical connectivity influences these measures. Furthermore, understanding how anatomical connectivity contributes to shared spiking variability could explain some of the inconsistencies across studies of shared variability. We addressed this knowledge gap by measuring shared spiking variability among visual cortical neurons for which anatomical connectivity was inferred from short timescale spiking cross-correlations and assessing how attention modulates shared variability in defined neuronal circuits.

In addition to modulating shared variability among sampled neurons, shifting attention toward or away from the receptive fields of visual cortical neurons produces a number of effects including modulations of neuronal firing rate and synaptic communication (16–20). Interestingly, although attention only weakly modulates V1 neuronal activity on average (18, 20, 21), stronger attention effects on single- and multiunit firing rates, as well as synaptic efficacy, were observed among V1 neurons tuned for stimulus features that were relevant for the specific feature detection required in the task (17, 22, 23). Similar relationships between task-relevant feature tuning and attention modulation have also been observed in MT and V4 (24, 25). Given possible relationships between neuronal tuning for task-relevant stimulus features and attentional modulation, we also tested whether shared spiking variability, and attentional modulation of shared variability, depend on whether connected neurons were similarly tuned for task-relevant stimulus features.

To examine whether and how anatomical connectivity influences shared variability, we conducted analyses of V1 datasets previously collected and published (17, 22, 26, 27). V1 neurons were recorded in monkeys performing contrast change detection and discrimination tasks. Connectivity between pairs of V1 neurons was assessed by computing spiking cross-correlations and pairs sharing putative monosynaptic excitatory and inhibitory connections, as well as pairs receiving common excitatory presynaptic input, were identified. Shared spiking variability among connected pairs and random pairings of unconnected V1 neurons was estimated by calculating pairwise spike count correlations (rSC) across trials in which the stimulus and task conditions were identical. Intriguingly, rSC values differed significantly across different types of neuronal circuits. Pairs connected via inhibitory synapses (negative cross-correlation peak pairs) and pairs receiving common excitatory presynaptic input (common-input pairs) produced remarkably high rSC values, while pairs connected via excitatory synapses (positive cross-correlation peak pairs) produced near-zero rSC values. Notably, attention-modulated rSC for pairs of neurons connected via excitatory synapses, while rSC values for randomly sampled, unconnected V1 neurons were not significantly modulated by attention, consistent with prior reports (28, 29). Furthermore, the effect of attention on rSC for positive peak pairs was larger than attention effects on rSC reported previously for randomly sampled neuronal pairings in the extrastriate cortex (7–9, 12, 30). Attention also reduced rSC values for pairs receiving common input and this effect size was also large relative to prior reports. These results indicate that anatomical connectivity strongly influences shared spiking variability, with different types of neuronal circuits producing markedly different levels of shared variability. Additionally, attention suppresses shared variability in excitatory neuronal circuits to a greater extent than it reduces shared variability among randomly sampled cortical neurons. Finally, similar task-relevant tuning among connected neurons predicts rSC for excitatory connected pairs. Thus, our findings demonstrate how attention selectively regulates activity in distinct neuronal circuits by differential modulation of shared variability. Furthermore, our results highlight the importance of identifying anatomical connectivity when estimating state-dependent functional modulations among neuronal populations.

Methods and Materials

This study involves analyses of data collected for a previous investigation of attentional modulation of communication among putative monosynaptically connected V1 neurons (17), with additional neurons included from datasets collected for other attention studies (22, 26, 27). Data used in this study were collected from three adult female rhesus macaque monkeys (Macaca mulatta). Procedures for behavioral measurements and neurophysiological data collection followed the guidelines set forth by the NIH and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees at the Geisel School of Medicine, Dartmouth College, and the University of California, Davis. Monkeys were group housed whenever possible, with additional enrichment provided in the form of a regular supply of treats, fresh produce, and toys. Light/dark cycling in the housing room was set at a normal cycle of 12 h each of light/dark. Details about surgical implantation of head-stabilization systems and cranial access chambers, daily chamber maintenance and insertion of recording electrode arrays, electrophysiological recording setup, visual stimulation, eye-tracking, attention task design, and behavioral training have been described previously (16, 17, 22, 23, 26, 27). To briefly summarize, in an aseptic procedure and using full surgical anesthesia, all monkeys were implanted with headposts and received small craniotomies over the opercular surface of V1 at parafoveal eccentricities that were encircled by chambers to allow for daily recording access. All monkeys learned to perform visual fixation and attention tasks using positive reinforcement through fluid rewards. Daily insertions of multielectrode arrays (Eckhorn array from Thomas Recording, Giessen Germany; V-probes from Plexon Inc., Dallas, TX, RRID:SCR_018784) were made in order to record simultaneously from multiple V1 neurons per session while monkeys performed the fixation and attention tasks.

Experimental Setup and Behavioral Tasks.

Monkeys were trained to perform two different tasks: 1) passive fixation during which visual stimuli were placed in the receptive field of recorded neurons and monkeys could ignore these stimuli; and 2) a covert visual spatial attention task in which monkeys detected a change in contrast of one of two displayed drifting sinusoidal gratings. Fixation tasks enabled measurements of neuronal feature tuning independent of the attention task. In this task, monkeys were required to maintain fixation within a 0.5-degree radius of a small central spot (diameter = 0.3 to 0.5 degrees) for 2 s. A drifting sinusoidal grating (diameter = 1 to 3 degrees) varying in contrast, orientation, spatial frequency, or temporal frequency was positioned on the monitor at a location overlapping the receptive fields of recorded neurons, which were determined using a hand-mapping procedure.

In the same sessions, monkeys performed an attention-demanding contrast change detection or discrimination task using a Posner cueing paradigm (31). Monkeys had to again maintain fixation on a central spot while two identical drifting gratings were presented within the same visual hemifield and at the same eccentricity relative to fixation. The color of the fixation spot cued monkeys to attend to the upper or lower grating, one of which was positioned in the receptive fields of recorded neurons. Trials were run in a block format of ~30 to 60 trials per block. Thus, in a given block, monkeys were cued to attend either to the stimulus overlapping the receptive fields of recorded neurons (attend-toward condition) or the other stimulus (attend-away condition). For all blocks, the cue was valid on 90 to 95% of trials. Correct detection of an unattended stimulus change on invalidly cued trials was rewarded; and invalid trial data were utilized for behavioral assessment of spatial attention allocation (see ref. 23 and Fig. 1B). Importantly, the duration of the grating display prior to a contrast change (between 1.2 and 3 s in duration) was randomized per trial so that monkeys could not predict when the grating contrast would change on any given trial. Monkeys reported a contrast change or discriminated the direction of that change by releasing a joystick or pressing a button (detection task) or making a saccade to one of two answer spots to indicate an increase or decrease in grating contrast (discrimination task). Further details of task parameters, visual stimulus presentation, and eye tracking for fixation and attention tasks have been described previously (16, 17, 22, 23, 26, 27). Neuronal data analyses were conducted for sessions in which the monkeys completed at least 30 trials per attention condition.

Neuronal Data Processing.

Single-unit spike sorting was performed on wideband recordings from electrode arrays using principal components analysis-based clustering methods available through Plexon Offline Sorter, as described previously (17, 22). Some session data were also spike-sorted using the automated spike sorting algorithm Kilosort 2.0 (32). Further criteria were applied to separate manually sorted single units from multiunits or noise: 1) spikes from single units had short (<1.2 ms) interspike interval (ISI) violations for <0.1% of spikes; 2) single-unit waveforms had signal-to-noise (SNR) >2.75; and 3) only single units with firing rates between 1 and 100 Hz were included as higher firing rates could indicate multiunit activity. Automatically sorted units were curated using open-source Phy software (github.com/cortex-lab/phy) and considered single units if they had biophysically realistic waveform shapes, stable amplitudes throughout the recording session, and the number of short ISI violations (number of spikes within the central 1 ms bin) was less than the mean spike count of the unit. Automatically sorted units on the same and nearby channels were merged into a single unit if their waveform shapes, amplitude, and principal components values in the Phy feature space were closely overlapping and their spiking cross-correlograms had near-zero spikes in the central 1 ms bin. Importantly, all manually sorted single units were also identified using the automated sorting method.

Anatomically connected pairs of neurons were identified as described previously; in fact, most of the connected pairs of V1 neurons analyzed here were the same pairs identified by Hembrook-Short et al. (17). To identify anatomically connected pairs of V1 neurons, spike trains of single units recorded during the same session were cross-correlated. Spike trains were obtained in 1-s periods per trial, corresponding to the presentation of the last four cycles of the drifting grating prior to the contrast change. This meant that the analysis windows for computing cross-correlations did not include onset transient responses to stimulus presentation because the window always excluded at least the first 200 msec of each trial. Spiking cross-correlations were computed using 1 ms bins ranging ±50 ms around zero (corresponding to coincident spikes). Cross-correlations were performed separately on attend-toward and attend-away trials. Per-trial cross-correlations were summed and normalized by the total number of spikes within the 1-s analysis period of the presynaptic neuron, determined based on the sign of the cross-correlation peak latency. A shuffle correction was performed as follows: 1) per-trial shuffled cross-correlations were computed by correlating the spike train of neuron A with that of neuron B shifted by one grating cycle (33, 34); 2) shuffled per-trial cross-correlations were summed and normalized as above; 3) resultant shuffled cross-correlations were subtracted from the time-aligned cross-correlations to generate shuffle-corrected cross-correlations for attend-toward and attend-away trials separately. Importantly, the shuffle-correction also removed a potential confound of circularity in using data from the same trial periods to estimate connectivity (via short time-scale spiking cross-correlations) and to measure spike count correlations (correlated changes in firing rate across trials). To be considered a connected pair, neurons had to meet the following criteria: 1) both neurons per pair had firing rates >1 Hz on at least 50% of attention trials; 2) a cross-correlation peak at or near zero (within ±14 ms) that rose >2 STD above or below the mean firing rate was present; 3) peaks at the same latency were present in cross-correlations from both attend-toward and attend-away trials; 4) peak height was >=1% of total spikes in at least one attention condition. Toward the first criterion, firing rates for neurons included were on average 26 ± 3.3 Hz (range 2 to 83 Hz), i.e., neurons had firing rates substantially above the criterion.

As described previously (17), pairings that satisfied all of these criteria were classified as sharing a common input or having a putative monosynaptic connection. A cross-correlation peak at zero latency indicated that the neurons in the pairing had a shared common presynaptic input. Pairings with cross-correlation peaks at zero latency were designated as “common-input pairs” only if the neurons in the pairing were separated by >100 μm i.e., were not recorded on the same or immediately neighboring channels on the multielectrode array (to eliminate the possibility of correlations due to spike sorting errors). A cross-correlation peak with a nonzero, small defined latency (<14 ms) suggested a monosynaptic connection between neurons. Pairs with a cross-correlation peak at nonzero latencies were included only if the latencies were shifted away from zero and were asymmetrically shaped (35). Pairs with nonzero latencies could have positive or negative peak amplitudes, referred to as “positive peak” and “negative peak” pairs respectively. A nonzero cross-correlation peak with a positive amplitude indicated an excitatory synapse as a positive signed peak implied the two neurons in the pair had a higher probability of firing in quick succession than would be expected by chance. Conversely, a negative amplitude cross-correlation peak implied a lower probability of firing in quick succession than expected by chance, indicative of an inhibitory synapse. Overall, 20 positive peak pairs, 5 negative peak pairs, and 38 common-input pairs were identified that satisfied the inclusion criteria across all three monkeys. Additionally, 352 “control” pairings of unconnected, but simultaneously recorded V1 neurons from the same recording sessions in Monkey E were analyzed in order to directly compare results to prior studies (an analysis of 42 control pairings from Monkey B [26 pairings from 3 sessions] and Monkey O [16 pairings from 2 sessions] revealed similar nonsignificant effects of attention on spike count correlations). Data from all three monkeys were pooled because raw spike count correlations as well as the change in spike count correlations with attention for identified pairs were comparable across all three monkeys. See ref. 17 for a detailed discussion of connected pairs per monkey.

To measure interneuronal spiking variability, spike count correlations (rSC) were calculated for all identified neuronal pairings (4, 36, 37). Spikes recorded during the last four complete grating cycles (4 Hz stimulus, 1 s analysis window) of the stimulus display period before the contrast change were used for rSC calculations. rSC was computed as the Pearson’s correlation of the trial-to-trial spike counts for all neuronal pairings. Separate rSC values were computed for attend-toward and attend-away trials and only correctly completed attention trials were used. For each pairing, attentional modulation of rSC (ΔrSC) was computed as the difference between the rSC values from the attend-toward and attend-away conditions: ΔrSC = rSC (attend-toward) – rSC (attend-away).

In addition to calculating rSC values per attention condition per neuronal pairing, neuronal feature tuning was also analyzed based on neuronal responses to gratings varying in stimulus parameters displayed during the passive fixation task (i.e., independent of attention). In this case, the most relevant tuning dimension for the attention task was neuronal tuning for grating contrast. Contrast tuning curves were computed by fitting a Naka Rushton function (38) to average spiking responses to gratings varying in contrast (contrast values: 1, 1.7, 2.8, 4.6, 7.7, 12.9, 21.5, 35.9, 60, 100%). A c50 value, corresponding to the contrast to evoke a half-maximal response, was computed from each curve fit. Only pairs/pairings in which both neurons had significant tuning for contrast (determined by ANOVA) with goodness-of-curve-fit R2 > 0.2 were included in subsequent comparisons of c50 and rSC (average R2 = 0.50 ± 0.03, range = 0.2 to 0.96). Application of this criterion excluded four positive peak pairs and 16 common-input pairs, and all but 73 control pairings from tuning-rSC comparisons. The difference in c50 values between the neurons comprising a connected pair (or control pairing) was used as a measure of tuning similarity per pair (pairing). In addition, signal correlation values per neuronal pair were also computed from contrast tuning data as a secondary measure of tuning similarity. Signal correlations were calculated as the Pearson’s correlation between the average responses to the grating across contrasts (10 levels listed above, 2 to 3 repeats per contrast level per neuron).

For all connected pairs and control neuronal pairings, the distance between recording contacts containing each unit was computed. Additionally, for all connected pairs, the relative laminar position was estimated as described previously (22). Briefly, position was determined relative to orthodromically stimulated neurons in layer 4C and/or a current-source density analysis of local field potentials recorded across the array was utilized to estimate the boundary between layers 4C and 5 and then relative laminar position estimated per contact from that boundary position.

Statistical Data Analysis.

For comparisons of pre- and postsynaptic firing rates across positive peak and negative peak pairs of connected neurons, unpaired nonparametric tests, Wilcoxon rank tests, were employed to account for limited sample sizes and to ensure robustness to outliers. Two comparisons were made per data type, so a Bonferroni correction using an alpha = 0.05 was applied (Table 1). To compare the distributions of raw rSC values, independent of attention, across pairs and pairings, a nonparametric one-way ANOVA (Kruskal–Wallis test) was applied with subsequent pairwise comparisons Bonferroni-corrected for multiple comparisons using alpha = 0.05 (Table 2). To assess attentional modulation of rSC for each pair/pairing type, Wilcoxon signed rank tests (two-sided, paired) were used with alpha = 0.05 (Table 3). Similar to the test of raw rSC across pairs and pairings, a Kruskal–Wallis test was also applied to compare ΔrSC across pairs and pairings (Table 4).

Results

Visual attention often modulates shared spiking variability among pairings of simultaneously recorded neurons. Whether anatomical connectivity influences shared variability, or attentional modulation of it, is not known. We addressed this gap by comparing attentional modulation of spike count correlations across pairs of V1 neurons with inferred anatomical connectivity as well as pairings of unconnected, but simultaneously recorded V1 neurons.

Data were gathered from prior studies in which multiple V1 neurons were recorded while monkeys performed a contrast-change detection or discrimination task that required covert visual spatial attention (Fig. 1A). In alternating blocks of trials, monkeys covertly attended to one of two drifting sinusoidal gratings, cued by the color of the fixation spot. Both gratings were equal-distant from the central fixation spot and displayed in the same hemifield such that one grating overlapped the receptive fields of recorded neurons. All trials followed the same timeline (Fig. 1 A, Right): The cue preceded grating display and gratings were visible for a variable amount of time per trial prior to a contrast change, which occurred ~90% of the time at the cued spatial location. Behavioral results showed that all three monkeys were significantly more accurate at detecting or discriminating contrast changes at validly cued locations (see figure 1B of 23), indicating correct allocation of spatial attention according to the cue. Neuronal spiking data were analyzed from correctly completed trials of the attention tasks, separating data on trials in which monkeys attended toward or away from the grating overlapping recorded neuronal receptive fields (Fig. 1 A, Left). Neuronal spiking data were also analyzed from trials in which monkeys passively fixated while gratings varying in contrast, orientation, spatial, or temporal frequency were presented at the location of recorded neuronal receptive fields, enabling measurements of neuronal feature tuning independent of the attention task.

Neurophysiological signals were spike-sorted offline using manual and automated procedures that ensured single-unit isolation (Methods and Materials). After defining well-isolated single units that were recorded simultaneously, anatomically connected pairs of neurons were identified using spiking cross-correlations, as described previously (17). Neuronal pairings whose shuffle-corrected cross-correlation produced a sharp, narrow, positive, and nonzero latency peak, indicating an excitatory connection, were termed “positive peak pairs” (Fig. 1B). Similarly, pairings whose sharp, narrow, nonzero latency peak was negative, indicating an inhibitory connection, were termed “negative peak pairs” (Fig. 1C). Pairings whose sharp, narrow, positive peak was centered at time zero, indicating common presynaptic input to both neurons, were termed “common-input pairs” (Fig. 1D). Importantly, all connected pairs of neurons demonstrated spiking cross-correlation peaks at the same latency on both attend-toward and attend-away trials (Fig. 1 B–D, red and blue curves respectively). We identified 20 positive peak pairs, five negative peak pairs, and 38 common-input pairs across three monkeys. Our hit rates for identifying connected pairs were 1.7 to 11%, dependent upon the type of arrays used (Monkey E, linear arrays: hit rate for connected pairs = 1.7% [21 pairs identified from 1201 possible pairings], hit rate for common-input pairs = 3%; Monkeys B and O, concentric arrays so data combined: hit rate for connected pairs = 11% [4 pairs from 37 possible pairings], hit rate for common-input pairs = 5%) and are well within the ranges of hit rates reported previously and predicted based on estimates of local connectivity (39, 40). Electrodes on the concentric arrays were independently positioned to optimize detection of connected pairs between layer four and the more superficial or deep layers, yielding higher hit rates with this array. All other possible pairings of well-isolated single units simultaneously recorded and not anatomically connected based on spiking cross-correlations were classified as unconnected or control pairings (n = 352 control pairings).

Monosynaptic latencies, inferred from the time of the cross-correlation peak, were small for both positive peak pairs (mean latency = 4.15 ± 0.82 msec) and negative peak pairs (1.03 ± 0.05 msec); all but two pairs had latencies less than 8 msec (Fig. 2A). The very short latency, negative cross-correlation peaks defining negative peak pairs suggested inhibitory monosynaptic connections. Two additional findings lent further support for the notion that negative peak pairs were connected via inhibitory synapses. First, presynaptic neurons in negative peak pairs, presumably inhibitory neurons, had significantly higher visually evoked firing rates than presynaptic neurons in positive peak pairs (presumably excitatory neurons) as well as postsynaptic neurons in negative peak pairs (Fig. 2B; see Table 1 for statistics). Second, the spike waveforms for presynaptic neurons in negative peak pairs were significantly narrower than the spike waveforms of their postsynaptic partners (Fig. 2C; P = 0.008, Wilcoxon rank test; presynaptic mean waveform width = 0.15 ± 0.02 msec, postsynaptic mean waveform width = 0.37 ± 0.07 msec). Finally, negative peak pair neurons were within two contacts of each other on the linear array or ~50 μm apart on concentric arrays (mean contact distance = 80 ± 34 μm), consistent with inhibitory neurons connecting onto proximal postsynaptic partners (41). In contrast, neurons in positive peak pairs and common-input pairs were located further apart (positive peak pair mean contact distance = 428 ± 109 μm; common-input pair mean contact distance = 384 ± 42 μm). Accordingly, positive cross-correlation peaks (defining positive peak pairs and common-input pairs) were not due to spike sorting errors because neurons were physically separated. Additionally, neurons per pair had distinct contrast tuning (Fig. 2D) and differently shaped spike waveforms (Fig. 2E).

Having identified a subset of V1 neurons sharing anatomical connections, we next asked whether attention modulates correlated spiking variability differently depending upon the type of connectivity forming each neuronal circuit. Spike count correlations (rSC) were computed as the Pearson’s correlation between trial-by-trial spike counts of neurons per connected pair, or pairing of unconnected neurons (4, 36, 37). Separate rSC values were computed for attend-toward and attend-away trials for each pair/pairing (see SI Appendix, Fig. S1 for spike count correlations across attention conditions for example pairs of each type). The range of rSC values across V1 pairs was wide (Fig. 3A; range of rSC across all connected pairs = −0.7 to +0.93). Furthermore, V1 pairs with different types of connectivity demonstrated significantly different distributions of rSC values (Fig. 3A and Table 2). Positive peak pairs had significantly lower, near-zero rSC values compared to negative peak pairs and common-input pairs, while both negative peak pairs and common-input pairs had significantly higher rSC values relative to control pairings (P = 1.66 × 10−5; Table 2). Although our sample of negative peak pairs was small, it is interesting that all had high positive rSC values, perhaps in part related to the fact that presynaptic inhibitory neurons had higher visually evoked firing rates (Fig. 2B). The distribution of rSC values for control pairings was also broad (Fig. 3A; range of rSC across all control pairings = −0.6 to +0.94), similar to prior reports (e.g., ref. 42), but positively shifted (P < 0.0001, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) with a median of +0.05. Across all neuronal pairs and pairings, there was a small but significant correlation between mean rSC and mean firing rate (correlation coefficient = 0.16, P = 0.0008). In contrast, the wide range in rSC values across neuronal pairs and pairings could not be attributed to different distances between neurons on the linear arrays (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B, linear regression P = 0.14). In general, rSC varied quite a bit across different types of V1 neuronal circuits, supporting the notion that shared spiking variability is in part dependent upon anatomical connectivity.

Given this dependence of rSC on anatomical connectivity, we next asked whether attentional modulation of rSC also varied across different types of neuronal circuits. We computed attentional modulation of rSC (ΔrSC) for V1 connected pairs and unconnected pairings. For V1 neuronal pairs connected via excitatory synapses, i.e., positive peak pairs and common-input pairs, attention significantly reduced rSC (Fig. 3 A, Left and Fig. 3B and Table 3). Moreover, ΔrSC for these excitatory V1 circuits was large (−0.09 for positive peak pairs and −0.07 for common-input pairs) compared to smaller attentional modulation of rSC (ΔrSC~ = −0.01 to −0.04) reported previously for randomly sampled pairings of neurons in the extrastriate visual cortex (7–9, 12, 30). Additionally, attentional modulation of rSC in excitatory V1 circuits was consistent: Most positive peak pairs and common-input pairs demonstrated a reduction in rSC with attention (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). For positive peak pairs, there was a nonsignificant but emerging relationship between the magnitude of rSC and ΔrSC (Pearson’s correlation coefficient r = 0.4, P = 0.06), such that more correlated/anticorrelated pairs (i.e., higher magnitude of rSC regardless of sign) had smaller, often positive ΔrSC, while pairs with near-zero rSC displayed larger effects of attention in reducing rSC. The relationship between the magnitude of rSC and ΔrSC was not significant for common-input pairs (Pearson’s correlation coefficient r = 0.25, P = 0.1). Finally, ΔrSC distributions were significantly negative for both positive peak pairs and common-input pairs (SI Appendix, Fig. S4, P < 0.01, one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests for each distribution; median ΔrSC for positive peak pairs = −0.1, median ΔrSC for common-input pairs = −0.03), and the ΔrSC distribution was significantly positive for control pairings (SI Appendix, Fig. S4, median = 0.01, P < 0.01, one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests), further highlighting differences in attentional modulation across connectivity types.

Robust attentional modulation of excitatory circuits contrasted with effects among unconnected/control pairings of V1 neurons, which did not show a significant effect of attention on rSC (Fig. 3 A, Right and Fig. 3B and Table 3). This result was consistent with prior findings from an overlapping dataset (22) and with V1 observations by other groups (28, 29). Although the sample of negative peak pairs was small (n = 5), most of these pairs had higher rSC values in the attend-toward condition (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3), suggesting that attention may differentially regulate correlated variability in local inhibitory circuits. Finally, positive peak pairs demonstrated significantly larger magnitude and negative ΔrSC relative to control pairings (P = 0.001; Table 4). There were no differences in ΔrSC across connected pairs of neurons located in different laminar compartments (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Together, these findings clearly demonstrate that attention significantly modulated rSC for V1 pairs sharing excitatory synapses, even though attention did not modulate rSC among randomly sampled, unconnected V1 neurons generally.

Thus, both raw rSC values and attentional modulation of rSC varied depending on whether and how V1 neurons were anatomically connected. But even within a single connectivity type, e.g., positive peak pairs, rSC values were distributed. We hypothesized that similarities/differences in neuronal tuning for task-relevant stimulus features could explain this distribution. Monkeys were performing a contrast-change detection or discrimination task, so we assessed neuronal tuning for grating contrast during the same recording sessions, but on a separate set of trials such that contrast tuning was measured independent of attention. For each pair/pairing in which both neurons demonstrated tuning for contrast (Fig. 2D; see Methods and Materials), we computed the contrast to evoke a half-maximal response (c50) per neuron. In support of our hypothesis, contrast tuning was predictive of rSC for positive peak pairs (Fig. 4). Specifically, when neurons in positive peak pairs had similarly low c50s, i.e., both were similarly tuned for small changes in grating contrast in the low-contrast regime, rSC values were near zero. Conversely, positive peak pair neurons with dissimilar c50s had larger magnitude rSC values that could be positive (correlated) or negative (anticorrelated; Fig. 4, note pair with values indicated). Indeed, relationships between similarity in contrast tuning and rSC were observed broadly across our sample of pairs: There was a significant positive relationship between mean rSC and signal correlation computed from contrast tuning curves across pairs/pairings (Pearson’s correlation r = 0.3, P = 0.0005), as well as a significant relationship between mean rSC and signal correlation for positive peak pairs alone (Pearson’s correlation r = 0.27, P = 0.032; SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

This relationship between contrast tuning and correlated variability was further indicated for positive peak pairs through application of a linear regression model in which mean rSC (across attention conditions) was the dependent variable and c50s per neuron were independent variables. There was a significant linear dependence of mean rSC on neuronal c50s for positive peak pairs (Fig. 4, colored hyperplane; see Table 5 for regression model statistics). Neither common-input nor negative peak pairs showed a linear dependence of rSC on neuronal contrast tuning (Fig. 4 and Table 5), suggesting that the distributions of rSC values for these groups were due to unmeasured factors. Finally, we examined whether attentional modulation of rSC depended upon similarity in neuronal contrast tuning. Although relationships between ΔrSC and neuronal c50s were not significant (Table 5), larger samples might support a positive relationship between contrast tuning similarity and attentional modulation of rSC for positive peak pairs (Pearson’s correlation coefficient r = 0.17, P = 0.5; SI Appendix, Fig. S7) and a negative relationship between contrast tuning similarity and ΔrSC for common-input pairs (Pearson’s correlation coefficient r = −0.4, P = 0.07; SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Attentional modulation of rSC among control pairings was also not correlated with contrast tuning similarity (Pearson’s correlation coefficient r = −0.03, P = 0.8, SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Thus, rSC values for positive peak pairs demonstrated a significant dependence upon neuronal tuning for task-relevant stimulus features, in this case grating contrast. These neuronal circuits also showed a significant reduction in shared spiking variability with attention and possibly an emerging trend whereby attention reduced shared variability more for pairs with similar task-relevant feature selectivity. While the distribution of rSC values for pairs receiving common presynaptic input was not explained by contrast tuning, attention also reduced shared spiking variability in these circuits and there was an emerging trend whereby attention reduced rSC more for common-input pairs with less similar contrast tuning. Together these results indicate that shared variability and its attentional modulation are dependent upon neuronal circuit connectivity as well as task-relevant feature tuning among connected neurons, pointing to nuanced relationships between anatomical connectivity, functional similarity, and correlated variability.

Discussion

The notion that attention reduces shared variability among visual cortical neurons is appealing as a possible mechanism to boost communication of perceptually relevant signals while dampening noise. A number of factors have been shown to influence the extent to which attention modulates shared spiking variability among visual cortical neurons including the area or areas of origin of the sampled neurons, the laminar locations of sampled neurons, their proximity to one another, and their cell type (8–13, 28). One notable factor, that has never been tested in behaving animals, is anatomical connectivity. Given that encoding and communication of perceptually relevant visual signals likely depend heavily on anatomical connectivity, this is a critical gap to fill. Here, we address this gap by analyzing shared spiking variability, and its attentional modulation, among neuronal circuits in V1 with known patterns of anatomical connectivity. We identified striking differences in shared variability across different types of neuronal circuits in V1. Neurons connected via excitatory synapses displayed low, often near-zero shared variability, while neurons receiving common excitatory input or connected via inhibitory synapses displayed high shared variability. In addition, attention significantly reduced shared spiking variability for excitatory neuronal circuits, while shared variability among randomly sampled, unconnected V1 neurons was not modulated by attention (Fig. 3). Finally, we observed a dependence of overall spike count correlations on task-relevant feature tuning for excitatory connected pairs as well as possible emerging trends whereby attentional modulation of shared variability could also depend on similarity in neuronal tuning for task-relevant stimulus features. In sum, these findings indicate that anatomical connectivity strongly influences shared spiking variability and its attentional modulation, highlighting the importance of understanding the precise connectivity within neuronal networks in order to accurately assess state-dependent and functional modulations in neuronal populations.

Establishing anatomical/structural connectivity is challenging, as the only ground truth for anatomical connectivity is electron microscopy data in which synapses are directly observed (43). To infer anatomical connectivity of neurons spanning cortical layers in behaving monkeys, electrophysiology is the only tool currently available. Numerous seminal studies have utilized short time-scale spiking cross-correlations to infer monosynaptic connectivity in anatomically well-defined retinogeniculate and geniculocortical circuits (44–53). Importantly, monosynaptic excitatory and inhibitory connectivity between cortical neurons as well as common inputs to cortical neurons can also be inferred from spiking cross-correlations (17, 35, 39, 54–56). Here, we rely on this method to infer monosynaptic connectivity and common inputs in V1 neuronal circuits, acknowledging its limitations. For example, spiking cross-correlation is more likely to reveal connections made by strong synapses, while weaker synaptic connections may be missed (57). This is especially true when strict criteria are applied to define cross-correlation peaks, as done here. Accordingly, our pairs were biased toward those with strong connections, i.e., presynaptic neurons in our sample contributed to between 1% and 13% of total spikes fired by postsynaptic neurons (average contribution = 4 ± 0.5% of total spikes in our sample). Given that a cortical neuron receives between 3,000 and 12,000 synapses from 100s to 1,000s of presynaptic neurons, including strongly and weakly contributing neurons (58, 59), cross-correlation peaks that meet strict criteria are heavily biased toward strong “driving” connections and cannot reliably detect weak inputs (60). Consequently, hit rates will be low when cross-correlation is used to identify connected neurons.

This synaptic input “numbers game” could also explain an apparent contradiction between our prior work and results reported here. We demonstrated previously that attention increases the efficacy of synaptic communication in V1 neuronal pairs connected by excitatory synapses (17). Here, we demonstrate that attention reduces shared spiking variability in these same pairs. If attention increases feedforward synaptic drive, why doesn’t shared spiking variability also increase? The likely explanation for this paradox is that 1) attention differentially regulates different types of local circuits including their shared variability, and 2) even the relatively strong synapses measured here only contribute a small percentage of the total synaptic input to any given postsynaptic neuron. It is therefore likely that while efficacy of some synapses increases with attention, aggregate synaptic inputs are modulated in a manner that causes correlated spiking to decrease. Thus, our results illuminate the complexity of various synaptic interactions that characterize attentional modulation at the circuit level. They also demonstrate that inferring functional connectivity from shared spiking variability is not straightforward, as it depends on anatomical connectivity. Additional experiments that interrogate synaptic interactions in subjects performing attention tasks will hopefully provide more insight into how attention alters aggregate synaptic inputs that underlie interneuronal relationships.

The amount of shared spiking variability in neuronal pairings has been a source of some debate, as numerous factors including brain state and spike sorting criteria may contribute to the range in rSC values reported across studies (e.g., refs. 61 and 62). The range and median rSC values we report here for randomly sampled, unconnected pairings of V1 neurons overlap with V1 rSC ranges and medians reported previously (11, 14, 15, 28, 29, 62–68). The median rSC we observed (0.05) was on the lower end of values reported previously in V1 and more in line with the observations of Ecker et al. (66). Also consistent with Ecker et al. (66), rSC did not depend on distance between neurons per pair or pairing in our sample (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Similarities between the findings of Ecker et al. (66) and ours are likely related to three factors: 1) we also recorded in awake-behaving monkeys; 2) we used strict spike sorting criteria to define single units; and 3) we recorded using linear arrays. In general, recordings in anesthetized animals and/or using single metal electrodes or Utah arrays seem to produce larger rSC values (e.g., refs. 14, 62, 63, and 65). Also, linear array recordings are likely to sample neurons within a single cortical column, while Utah array recordings enable sampling of neurons across cortical columns. Given that neurons within a column share orientation tuning (69, 70) and rSC values scale with orientation tuning similarity (14, 15, 66), it is perhaps surprising that reported rSC values tend to be lower for pairings of V1 neurons recorded on linear arrays relative to those from Utah arrays. The results we report here could shed some light on this apparent puzzle. Circuit connectivity within a cortical column is likely different than connectivity linking neighboring columns, the latter being biased toward columns with similar orientation preferences (71, 72). Here, we report that rSC differs significantly depending on the type of synapse connecting neurons within a V1 column. Following this logic, the majority of neuronal circuits within a column utilize excitatory connections, which we demonstrate have near-zero rSC. Whereas long-range, iso-orientated excitatory inputs are coupled with local inhibition (73). We show that local inhibitory circuits often have high rSC. Putting these pieces together, the mixture of neuronal circuits within a V1 column, dominated by low-rSC local excitatory circuits, may explain lower rSC values in linear array recordings. While more homogeneous long-range orientation-tuned excitatory inputs coupled with local inhibition could produce higher rSC values observed using Utah arrays. Thus, in addition to experimental factors such as brain state, electrode material/geometry, and spike sorting, different types of anatomical connectivity strongly influence rSC measurements.

Some studies have also reported differences in rSC when comparing pairings of V1 neurons located within the supragranular (superficial), granular, or infragranular (deep) cortical layers (67, 68). Specifically, rSC values are lower in the granular compared to the supra- and infragranular layers of V1. Interestingly, this pattern is flipped in extrastriate visual cortical area V4 (9). Among unconnected V1 pairings sampled from each laminar compartment in our dataset, mean rSC among granular pairings was smaller than rSC values from supra- and infragranular layers (rSC granular = 0.02; supragranular = 0.08; infragranular = 0.11); however, this difference was not significant (P = 0.16, Kruskal–Wallis test), perhaps due to the limited sample size (n = 53 pairings per laminar compartment). We did not observe laminar differences in rSC, or attentional modulation of rSC, for connected pairs of V1 neurons, but again, the sample size was low and many pairs spanned cortical layers. Some of the trends we observed for connected pairs could nonetheless provide insight into more global laminar differences in shared variability. In alignment with prior results showing larger rSC values for neuronal pairings in the deep layers of V1 (67, 68), we observed a wider range of attentional modulation of rSC among V1 connected pairs located in the deep layers (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). In addition, attention reduced rSC for the vast majority of neuronal circuits involving layer 4 neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), similar to larger attention-mediated reductions in rSC for V4 neurons in the granular layers (9). Thus, even though granular layers in V1 are dominated by divergent inputs from the visual thalamus, while granular layers in V4 integrate a variety of corticocortical feedforward signals, attention reduces rSC in the granular layers in both visual cortical areas. Further support for the notion that attention preferentially decorrelates spiking activity among input-recipient layer neurons includes our finding that attention significantly reduced rSC for common-input pairs, many of which were located within the granular layers (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). A prior study of attentional modulation of the efficacy of feedforward signals communicated from the visual thalamus to the granular layers of V1 also showed that attention reduces the probability of synchronous postsynaptic spiking among pairs of layer 4C neurons receiving common thalamic input (16). Taken together, these findings suggest that attention may be an important mechanism by which granular layers of the visual cortex in particular reduce redundant signals due to anatomical wiring constraints (e.g., divergent connections) by desynchronizing and/or decorrelating neuronal spiking activity. How attention reduces shared spiking variability more in granular layers compared to other cortical layers remains an open question that will require identifying and testing discrete neuronal circuits.

Just as other attention effects like firing rate modulation and individual neuronal variability modulation depend on cell type and area/laminar location (8, 9, 13, 22, 74), attentional modulation of shared spiking variability is more nuanced than previously thought. The apparent dichotomy in both rSC and attentional modulation of rSC among excitatory and inhibitory circuits in V1 is a striking example of such nuance. While positive peak pairs had low rSC and showed a large reduction in rSC with attention, negative peak pairs had high rSC and most showed an increase in rSC with attention (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). It is difficult to draw conclusions from a small sample of inhibitory circuits. But the contrast between excitatory and inhibitory circuits in V1 is noteworthy, especially given that attention effects on firing rate and neuronal variability are larger among putative inhibitory neurons compared to putative excitatory neurons in V4 (8, 12). There are a number of possible explanations for these apparently divergent observations, including different methods for identifying inhibitory neurons, different recording array geometries, and different cortical areas (e.g., attention effects are generally larger in V4 relative to V1 as in refs. 18 and 20). Also, inhibitory circuits within a column may be anatomically and functionally distinct compared to inhibitory/excitatory couplings over longer distances (40, 73, 75). It is clear that more data from identified inhibitory circuits are required in order to determine whether and how attention differentially regulates inhibitory circuitry across the visual cortex.

Our findings indicate another important factor contributing to nuanced attentional modulation of shared variability: whether sampled neurons are similarly tuned for attention task-relevant stimulus features. Because monkeys performed contrast detection and discrimination tasks in this study, we focused on relationships between shared variability and similarities in contrast tuning among neurons per connected pair. Significant interactions with task-relevant tuning emerged for excitatory circuits in V1. Specifically, for positive peak pairs, rSC scaled with tuning similarity: Pairs with lower tuning similarity had larger-magnitude rSC (were more correlated/anticorrelated), while pairs with similar contrast tuning had rSC closer to zero (Fig. 4). There was also a relationship between rSC and ΔrSC for these circuits such that pairs with lower rSC (i.e., more similarly tuned) also showed greater attentional modulation. Though not significant, a complementary trend was observed relating higher tuning similarity with larger attention-mediated reductions in rSC for positive peak pairs (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Interestingly, an opposite trend was also emerging for common-input pairs such that neurons with less similar contrast tuning showed larger attention-mediated reductions in rSC and vice versa. Together, these findings are consistent with positive relationships between tuning similarity and attentional modulation of communication efficacy for excitatory circuits in V1 (17) as well as a reduction in synchronous spiking among layer 4C neurons receiving common thalamic input with attention (16). In other words, attention may fine-tune signal transmission in different types of neuronal circuits including decorrelating and synchronizing activity in feedforward circuits conveying task-relevant information and desynchronizing redundant signals in divergent circuits. Such finely tuned attention effects on specific neuronal circuits suggest that attention acts dynamically and selectively in concert with the intricate functional architecture within the visual cortex. Understanding the dynamics and selectivities of these interactions is at the core of elucidating the neuronal mechanisms underlying visual attention.

In summary, we found that shared variability and its attentional modulation depend on the anatomical connections that make up local circuits within a V1 functional column. Attention reduced shared variability more in local excitatory circuits than for randomly sampled, unconnected visual cortical neurons. Furthermore, shared variability, and possibly also attentional modulation of shared variability, were also related to task-relevant feature tuning for excitatory neuronal circuits. These relationships were nuanced, suggesting that attention can dynamically and selectively fine-tune signal transmission in local cortical circuits.
