Second allogeneic stem cell transplantation for XMEN disease
Authors: Baldeep Wirk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryX-linked immunodeficiency with magnesium defect, Epstein-Barr virus infection and neoplasia (XMEN) disease is due to an inherited defect in immunity from loss-of-function mutations in the magnesium transporter 1 gene (MAGT1). Patients can present as adults with XMEN disease from a delayed diagnosis or lack of genetic diagnosis. Allogeneic stem-cell transplantation is curative in XMEN disease, but the mortality is high, especially in adults. Defective N-glycosylation of platelet glycoproteins impairs platelet aggregation and risks fatal mucosal haemorrhage (such as posterior epistaxis with airway obstruction and haemorrhagic shock requiring intubation) early during post-transplant aplasia. Maintaining a platelet level of at least 30×109/L until engraftment could avoid life-threatening haemorrhage. This is the first report of a successful second allogeneic stem-cell transplant in XMEN disease. Allogeneic stem-cell transplant in adults with XMEN disease should be considered as a curative option in patients with suitable donors.

Background

A syndrome of X-linked immunodeficiency with magnesium defect, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection and neoplasia (XMEN) results from an inherited loss-of-function mutation in the magnesium transporter 1 gene (MAGT1) on chromosome Xq21.1.1 The loss of MAGT1 function disrupts the transient T-cell receptor-induced magnesium flux necessary for optimal T-cell activation. MAGT1 is also a non-catalytic subunit of the oligosaccharyltransferase complex, which promotes N-linked glycosylation of substrates. The activating receptor, natural killer (NK) group 2, member D (NKG2D), mediates cytotoxicity against EBV.1 2 Impaired T-cell activation and reduced N-linked glycosylation of the NKG2D receptor of CD8+ T lymphocytes and NK cells lead to combined immunodeficiency, recurrent ear and sinopulmonary infections, EBV infection and EBV+ lymphoma.1 2

Allogeneic stem-cell transplant is a potentially curative therapy in XMEN disease, but the mortality is high, especially in adults. Most publications on allogeneic stem-cell transplant for XMEN disease focus on paediatric patients, as in other primary immunodeficiencies.3 However, patients can present as adults with primary immunodeficiency syndromes due to a delayed diagnosis or lack of genetic diagnosis. Adults with primary immunodeficiencies often have a high Haematopoietic Cell Transplant–Comorbidity Index score from organ damage, which compounds the mortality risk of an allogeneic stem-cell transplant.4 So far, only two adults with XMEN disease have had successful allogeneic stem-cell transplants in the published literature (table 1).2 5 6 Many patients succumb to catastrophic haemorrhage (such as intracranial haemorrhage or posterior epistaxis with haemorrhagic shock requiring intubation) early post transplant.2 5 6 Glycoprotein receptors, such as GP1b-IX-V, GPVI and integrin ⍺IIbβ3, control platelet haemostatic function.7 In two patients with XMEN disease, GP1b, GPVI and integrin ⍺IIb had lower molecular weights from diminished N-glycosylation of these receptors, resulting in impaired platelet aggregation and integrin ⍺IIbβ3 activation.7 Impaired N-glycosylation of platelet glycoproteins contributes to haemorrhage in XMEN disease.7 8 Prophylactic transfusion of normal platelets to maintain a level of at least 30×109/L could minimise the potential for life-threatening haemorrhage during early post-transplant aplasia before engraftment. This is the first report of a successful second allogeneic peripheral blood stem-cell transplant in XMEN disease.

Case presentation

A young male was diagnosed with XMEN disease in his early 20s. He had a hemizygous c.486+1G>A splice site variant in MAGT1 (chrX:77 126 304 (GRCh37) NM_032121) and a pathogenic heterozygous c.1998G>T (p.Lys666Asn) missense variant in RET (chr10:43 610 046 (GRCh37) NM_020975) associated with asymptomatic multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A. Since childhood, he had recurrent sinopulmonary infections, otitis media, epistaxis, splenomegaly, an inverted CD4:CD8 ratio and hypogammaglobulinaemia necessitating frequent immunoglobulin infusions. In his early teenage years, he had stage IV polyomavirus-positive Merkel cell carcinoma of the right arm metastatic to the bone and retropharynx, requiring tracheostomy due to impending airway obstruction (table 2). He began cytoreductive chemotherapy with topotecan, cyclophosphamide, cisplatin, etoposide, doxorubicin and vincristine. After reducing the tumour bulk, he underwent consolidation with tandem autologous stem-cell transplantation with thiotepa-cyclophosphamide, followed by modified carboplatin, etoposide and melphalan. He then had radiation therapy, 35 Gy to the arm, attaining complete remission (CR).

Clinical characteristics of the patient

CR, complete remission; DLI, donor lymphocyte infusion; FISH, fluorescence in situ hybridisation; GVHD, graft-versus-host disease; MDS, myelodysplastic syndrome; MUD, matched unrelated donor; NGS, next-generation sequencing; PBSC, peripheral blood stem cell; PTCy, post-transplant cyclophosphamide; VAF, variant allele frequency.

In his late 20s, he developed myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) post-cytotoxic therapy with 7q deletion and 10% myeloid blasts. After receiving azacitidine and venetoclax, he underwent myeloablative fludarabine, busulfan and alemtuzumab matched unrelated donor allogeneic stem-cell transplantation with post-transplant cyclophosphamide and sirolimus graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis. The platelet count was maintained above 30×109/L until stem cell engraftment. Post-transplant complications included haemorrhagic BK polyomavirus cystitis and recurrent epistaxis requiring packing. He achieved 100% donor chimerism with molecular CR. The CD4:CD8 ratio normalised, and the hypogammaglobulinaemia resolved.

At day 601 post transplant, the therapy-related MDS relapsed with 7% myeloid blasts and a 7q deletion. Bone marrow chimerism was 75% donor. Despite hypomethylating agents and venetoclax, the MDS persisted with 3% myeloid blasts and a 7q deletion. He became transfusion dependent. The severe thrombocytopenia of less than 10×109/L precluded meaningful platelet aggregation studies. Coagulation studies were normal. There was hypogammaglobulinaemia, epistaxis and inversion of the CD4:CD8 ratio. The Haematopoietic Cell Transplant–Comorbidity Index score was high at 7.

In his early 30s, he underwent reduced intensity fludarabine and melphalan 140 mg/m2 allogeneic stem-cell transplantation with post-transplant cyclophosphamide, sirolimus and mycophenolate mofetil using a different matched unrelated donor. The platelet count was maintained above 30×109/L until stem cell engraftment. He had haemorrhagic BK cystitis from days 52 to 71 after transplant. Grade 2 acute GVHD of the skin resolved with prednisone. Sirolimus was tapered off by day 180 post transplant. There was no EBV viremia or other opportunistic infections. At day 100 post transplant, there was complete donor chimerism in the myeloid and T-cell fractions and molecular CR. By day 196, the bone marrow biopsy showed relapse of MDS with 0.88% abnormal myeloid blasts with 7q deletion in 3% of cells. On day 219, he received a donor lymphocyte infusion of 5.36×106 CD3+ cells per kg. He then began filgrastim and mini-dose decitabine for six cycles for relapsed MDS and to expand cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, NK cells and regulatory T cells.9 He developed grade 2 skin acute GVHD that resolved with desoximetasone cream. On day 259, the bone marrow biopsy showed molecular CR with clearance of the 7q deletion, sustained at a year post transplant. Immune reconstitution allowed withdrawal of the monthly immunoglobulin infusions. He had no further epistaxis.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient is doing well in remission 17 years after the diagnosis of stage IV Merkel cell carcinoma and 5 years after the diagnosis of therapy-related MDS with 7q deletion at the time of this report.

Discussion

There is an increased risk of malignancy in XMEN disease, most commonly Hodgkin’s lymphoma, EBV+ lymphoproliferative disease, Burkitt’s lymphoma and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.1 MAGT1 deficiency in XMEN disease increases Merkel cell polyomavirus susceptibility in the skin, which raises the risk of developing early-onset polyomavirus-positive Merkel cell carcinoma, an aggressive cutaneous neuroendocrine tumour, as in our patient.10 Merkel cell carcinoma typically occurs at a median age of 75 years and is rare in individuals under 40 years.10 In XMEN disease, there are three reported cases of early-onset Merkel cell carcinoma, all which were under age 25 and were polyomavirus positive.10 Indeed, MAGT1 is a heritable risk factor for early-onset Merkel cell carcinoma, as are other germline disease variants in DNA repair genes, such as ATM, BRCA1, BRCA2 and TP53.11 The survival in our patient with XMEN of 17 years is better than the median survival of 14.5 months in the general population with stage IV Merkel cell carcinoma.12 Notably, polyomavirus-negative Merkel cell carcinomas have a higher mutational burden with a damaging ultraviolet signature pattern, characterised by more aggressive growth than polyomavirus-positive cases.13

There is no reported predisposition to MDS in XMEN disease. Our patient had therapy-related MDS from Merkel cell carcinoma chemotherapy given 13 years prior. Despite relapse 20 months after the initial allogeneic stem-cell transplant for therapy-related MDS and then 6 months after the second allogeneic stem-cell transplant, a donor lymphocyte infusion and filgrastim and mini-dose decitabine for six cycles to stimulate the expansion of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, NK cells and regulatory T cells and promote the graft-versus-malignancy effect resulted in complete molecular remission 5 years after the initial diagnosis of MDS.9 In contrast, the reported median survival of therapy-related MDS after allogeneic stem-cell transplant in the literature is 17 months.14

This is the first report of a successful second allogeneic stem-cell transplant for XMEN disease in an adult. Importantly, we maintained the platelet counts above 30×109/L prophylactically until platelet engraftment to lessen the risk of life-threatening haemorrhage, a major cause of mortality. Keeping platelet counts above 30×109/L during transplant requires validation in additional patients with XMEN disease. Indeed, allogeneic stem-cell transplantation from a healthy donor reverses the immune deficiency as well as the impaired platelet aggregation and integrin ⍺IIbβ3 activation from defective N-glycosylation in XMEN disease.6 The patient remains in remission 17 years after the diagnosis of stage IV Merkel cell carcinoma and 5 years after the diagnosis of therapy-related MDS with 7q deletion at the time of this report. Allogeneic stem-cell transplant in adults with XMEN disease should be considered as a curative option if the patient has a suitable donor.
