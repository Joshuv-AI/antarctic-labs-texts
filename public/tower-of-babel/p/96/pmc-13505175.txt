Assessment of bone density around impacted maxillary canine teeth by gray value analysis on cone-beam computed tomography images
Authors: Azita Ehsani, Pejman Hadadi Madiseh, Marzieh Ehsani, Seyed Amir Mansour Mohammadi, Naeimeh Nikfarjam Nouri, Shiva Esmaeili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimThis study evaluated the relationship between local alveolar bone density and maxillary canine impaction using gray values (GVs) obtained from cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans.Materials and methodsIn this retrospective cross-sectional study, CBCT images of 74 patients (58 females and 16 males; mean age: 25 years) retrieved from a single private radiology center were evaluated under standardized conditions. Impacted and erupted maxillary canines were selected based on the defined clinical and radiographic criteria. GVs were measured in the cervical and apical regions of the alveolar bone adjacent to maxillary lateral incisors. Intra- and interobserver agreements were evaluated.ResultsThe intra- and interobserver agreements were excellent (correlation coefficients ≥ 0.94). GVs in the apical region were significantly higher around impacted canines compared with erupted teeth (P = 0.003). Conversely, in the cervical region, erupted canines demonstrated higher GVs than impacted canines. Most impactions had a mesio-angular and predominantly palatal position. The prevalence of impaction was higher in females; however, no statistically significant correlation was found between the GVs and sex or type of impaction (P > 0.05).ConclusionThe results showed a significant relationship between the GVs and maxillary canine impaction status, such that the GV was lower in the cervical region and higher in the apical region around impacted canines compared with erupted canines. This pattern may reflect altered bone remodeling or increased osteoblastic activity in the area and may indicate a possible effect of bone density on tooth eruption. Further studies are recommended with a larger sample size using other devices.

Introduction

Tooth eruption is a continuous but complex process in which numerous local and systemic factors play a role. Dental anomalies include a wide range of disorders in the number, position, size, structure, and eruption of teeth and are of great importance in all fields of dentistry, especially orthodontics, because they can cause problems in the treatment process and negatively affect the outcome. Early identification of these anomalies can prevent the occurrence of adverse complications during orthodontic treatment [1, 2].

Impaction is among the common dental anomalies. Impacted teeth are those that remain unerupted or are partially erupted based on clinical and radiographic findings. Failure of eruption of permanent teeth is usually a dental anomaly [3]. Impaction is referred to as impaired tooth eruption into the dental arch by the expected time which can cause many problems such as affecting tooth movement, and esthetic and functional consequences [4, 5].

In tooth impaction, the tooth bud is present in the alveolar bone but normal tooth eruption does not occur. Maxillary permanent canine teeth are most commonly affected due to misalignment of tooth bud. Scientifically, impaction is confirmed when tooth eruption has been delayed by more than 18 months based on the individual’s dental age and radiographic evidence [6].

Maxillary canine teeth play a major role in facial esthetics and occlusion. The prevalence of maxillary impaction varies from 1% to 5% [7, 8]. Following their impaction, complications such as resorption of adjacent teeth, discrepancy in the length and width of dental arch, development of cystic lesions and tumors, infection and ankylosis may occur [9].

Over the years, several local, systemic and genetic factors have been proposed as the possible etiologies for tooth impaction. However, its exact cause remains unknown [10]. Two principal hypotheses have been proposed to explain maxillary canine impaction: the eruption guidance theory and the genetic theory. The eruption guidance theory suggests that the maxillary canine follows the root of the lateral incisor, which serves as a pathway for its eruption. Consequently, developmental absence or morphological abnormalities of the lateral incisor root may disrupt this guidance mechanism, leading to failure of eruption and subsequent impaction of the canine. In contrast, the genetic theory attributes canine impaction and palatal displacement primarily to hereditary factors. Clinically, impacted canines are often asymptomatic, which limits the reliability of clinical examination alone for their detection. As a result, radiographic evaluation plays a crucial role in the diagnosis of canine impaction. Various imaging techniques, including periapical, occlusal, panoramic radiographs, lateral cephalometric analysis, computed tomography and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), are commonly employed for this purpose [11]. With the development of three-dimensional imaging, CBCT is increasingly used for the diagnosis and treatment planning of impacted teeth because it has several advantages including high accuracy, low cost, and lower radiation dose than computed tomography [12, 13].

Furthermore, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is widely utilized for diagnostic purposes. This imaging modality offers high spatial resolution and image quality, producing distortion-free three-dimensional (3D) images that allow accurate assessment of bone quality in axial, sagittal, coronal and cross-sectional planes [14].

Recent evidence has highlighted the role of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) as a reference imaging modality for evaluating maxillofacial bone conditions. An integrative review has described CBCT as a gold standard for the diagnosis and follow-up of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaws (MRONJ), due to its ability to provide high-resolution three-dimensional images and to accurately detect trabecular bone alterations, osteosclerosis, cortical changes, and bone sequestration. These features support the potential of CBCT for assessing subtle changes in bone structure and density [15].

GV is a standard index used in CBCT to indicate radiation intensity based on numerical measurements [16, 17].

To date, bone density assessment by the gray value (GV) of CBCT has been studied for examining the bone quality around dental implants, detection of tooth ankylosis, and diagnosis and differentiation of pathological lesions [18, 19]. However, CBCT gray values are device- and protocol-dependent and are not directly equivalent to Hounsfield units; therefore, they should be interpreted as relative indicators of density rather than absolute values [15, 20].

Assessing the density of the adjacent alveolar bone may facilitate the early detection of canine impaction. To date, and to the best of the authors’ knowledge, only one study has investigated this issue, in which bone density was evaluated using Hounsfield units. The findings suggested that increased bone density could act as a local etiological factor in maxillary canine impaction [21]. Therefore, considering that the GV is affected by the type of scanner, the aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between local bone density and canine impaction using the GVs obtained from a Carestream CS9300 CBCT scanner.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted on CBCT scans of patients retrieved from the archives of a single private dentomaxillofacial radiology center in Karaj, Iran. The CBCT images had been acquired between 2020 and 2024 and belonged to patients referred for imaging to this radiology clinic for orthodontic treatment. Of a total of 342 CBCT scans taken between 2020 and 2024, 98 images visualizing impacted and erupted maxillary canine teeth were initially identified, and after applying the eligibility criteria, 74 images were included in the final sample. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: CBCT scans visualizing unilateral maxillary canine impaction, patient’s age older than 13 and up to 35 years, no prior orthodontic treatment, no syndrome or systemic disease affecting bone health, and CBCT scans without pathology or congenitally missing teeth, supernumerary teeth, dentigerous cyst, or an enlarged cystic follicle. The CBCT scans of patients with a history of dental trauma or surgery in the anterior maxilla and images of patients with motion or any significant artifact were excluded [10].

A maxillary canine was considered impacted when 3/4 of its root had been completely formed, or the patient’s age was older than 13 years (up to 35 years) while the contralateral maxillary canine had been completely erupted. Similarly, complete eruption was defined as a tooth fully erupted into its expected position in the dental arch and being in occlusion.

All CBCT images had been acquired with a Carestream CS9300 CBCT scanner (Kodak, France) with the following parameters: 90 kVp tube potential, 8 mA tube current, 150 mm voxel size, and 100 × 50 mm field of view (FOV). All images had been acquired by one technician. The images were analyzed on a medical monitor (Asus, China) using OnDemand software version 2.2.1.6 (Morita, Kyoto, Japan) with Slice thickness of 0.5 mm, under standardized viewing conditions. Brightness and contrast were interactively adjusted to optimize visualization of the trabecular bone, while image sharpness and other enhancement filters were kept unchanged. All evaluations were performed using the same monitor and software environment to ensure consistency and reproducibility. The images were reviewed under standardized viewing conditions on the same monitor and with the same software version. Using On Demand software, the region of interest measuring 6 mm x 4 mm was placed in the trabecular bone on axial CBCT images [10, 22].

The distal surface of bilateral maxillary lateral incisors was chosen as the reference because it lies in the expected canine eruption path. GVs were evaluated at two points on the distal surface of the lateral incisors in the axial plane, one in the cervical region and the other in the apical region of bone adjacent to the maxillary lateral incisors. The cervical and apical regions were assessed separately to capture possible site-specific differences in local bone density along the eruption path. (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4) The measures of central dispersion for the GVs were automatically calculated by the software. Information regarding age, sex, type of impaction, and GV were recorded in a checklist.

Intra- and interobserver agreements

All images were assessed by two dentomaxillofacial radiologists with at least 15 years of experience. A total of 35 images were randomly selected from the sample, and assessed for the second time for calculation of intraobserver agreement. There was an interval of 40 days between the assessments. Both observers had been previously calibrated regarding the placement of region of interest. The intra- and interobserver agreements were assessed by calculation of intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics for the categorical variables were reported as frequency and percentage (%). Descriptive statistics for the continuous variables were reported as median (minimum-maximum) and mean ± standard deviation depending on the data normality distribution. The normality distribution of the data was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Since the parametric test assumptions were met, comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the t-test, and comparisons among three groups were performed using ANOVA. The relationship between the categorical variables was analyzed using the Pearson’s correlation test. The threshold level of statistical significance was p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 74 patients, including 58 (78.4%) females and 16 (21.6%) males, were included in the study. Of the impacted maxillary canine teeth, 31 were in the right side, and 43 were in the left side. Of a total of 74 impacted teeth, 61 (82.4%) had a mesio-angular, 9 (12.2%) had a vertical and 4 (5.4%) had a horizontal position (Table 1). The mean age of the patients was 25 years (range 13 to 35 years). The intra- and interobserver agreements were excellent (ICC values ≥ 0.95 and 0.94, respectively).

Discussion

The prevalence of canine impaction is 1%-4%. Maxillary canine impaction affects approximately 2% of the population, is twice as common in females as in males, and is bilateral in 8% of cases [23]. Although many studies have been conducted on the local and systemic factors affecting canine impaction, its main cause is still unclear. Previous studies have pointed to genetic factors and the presence of anomalies or missing lateral incisors as the main etiological causes of canine impaction [24, 25]. investigated the relationship between unilateral and bilateral canine impaction and the microstructure of the maxillary alveolar bone. According to their results, the bone surface area was higher at the impaction side than on the contralateral side. Also, the bone density was lower around the impacted canines compared with erupted canines. These results indicate that canine impaction can be associated with localized changes in bone density, as in the present study, the GVs were higher around impacted canines.

Bone density can be assessed according to the CBCT GVs. Some studies have shown that CBCT can detect subtle changes in trabecular architecture and local bone density in various maxillofacial conditions. In this regard, gray value (GV) analysis may provide useful relative information about local bone conditions associated with impacted teeth; however, these measurements should be interpreted cautiously and within standardized imaging conditions [15].

In line with previous studies, radiographic evaluation of impacted teeth should be performed using a systematic and comprehensive approach, which assessing not only quantitative parameters but also the surrounding anatomical structures, including the alveolar bone, adjacent teeth, and the three-dimensional position of the impacted tooth [26]. The characteristics of the surrounding bone may influence the eruption pathway and impaction of maxillary canines; however, this relationship is complex and multifactorial. Therefore, relying solely on CBCT-derived gray values may oversimplify the underlying biological processes. Gray value differences observed in the present study should be interpreted with caution and within the broader anatomical context, rather than being considered as definitive indicators of bone density or cellular activity third molar).

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, only one study has examined the GV of alveolar bone around impacted canines in different FOVs, and according to the results, the GV around the impacted canines was significantly higher than the GV around the erupted canines in a 40 × 40 mm FOV [10]. The present study was the first to examine CBCT images of impacted canine teeth with a specific FOV and similar exposure values, obtained by the same operator. Because GV values are device-dependent, the present findings should be interpreted as relative comparisons under standardized acquisition conditions rather than universal numeric cutoffs.

One disadvantage of CBCT scanners is their lack of standardization. Different CBCT scanners have differences with each other in their hardware and image reconstruction algorithms, which can lead to generation of different GVs from the same area [20].

Therefore, in this study, we used images taken by one CBCT scanner to increase accuracy and comparability of the findings. Also, various studies have shown that the tube voltage and size of FOV are effective in the measured GV. Therefore, a 100 × 50 mm FOV with the same exposure settings was used in the present study. A significant relationship was found between occlusion and GV in this FOV. Seçgin et al. [10].used three FOVs with different sizes of 40 × 40 mm, 60 × 60 mm and 100 × 50 mm, and observed a significant relationship only in the 40 × 40 mm FOV, pointing to the effect of FOV on GV [20].

A previous study [27]. showed that the greatest difference in density was evident in a smaller FOV, while a smaller difference between different densities was seen in a larger FOV. The present study also used images obtained with the smallest FOV due to higher accuracy in determining the GVs. Also, in the present study, points were selected in the distal surface of lateral incisors because of the importance of lateral incisors in determining the canine eruption path. Previous studies have also indicated that missing lateral incisors and their malformations (such as peg shape) are usually associated with canine impaction [25]. The two selected points for examining the GVs in the present study were in the cervical and apical regions of bone distal to lateral incisors. According to the obtained results, there was a significant difference in GV in the cervical and apical regions between the impacted and erupted canines, and it was higher in the cervical region, which may be associated with canine impaction. In a study by Seçgin et al. [10], only one point at the center of bone between the canine and first premolar teeth was selected to examine the GV, but in the present study, the accuracy of GV measurement was increased by considering two separate points.

The predominance of female participants in the present study (78.4%) may be explained by the higher reported prevalence of canine impaction in females [23, 25]. In addition, female patients are generally more likely to seek orthodontic evaluation and treatment, which may have contributed to the observed distribution. Furthermore, the retrospective design of this study and recruitment from a single referral center may have introduced selection bias.

In the current study, there was no significant relationship between the GV calculated at these two points and the age or gender of patients, which was similar to a previous study [10].

The present study was the first to analyze the relationship between the type of canine impaction (buccal/palatal) and GV, but no significant relationship was found, which was probably due to limited sample size and unavailability of the same number of different impaction types.

One limitation of this study was the small sample size due to the strict inclusion criteria, including absence of known local and systemic factors associated with canine impaction, as well as assessment of images with similar FOV and exposure settings. Because this was a retrospective cross-sectional study based on scans from a single private center, selection bias cannot be completely excluded. In addition, unmeasured confounders such as genetic factors, lateral incisor morphology and other local or systemic influences could not be fully controlled.

Future studies are recommended with a larger sample size and different CBCT scanners to investigate the effect of bone density on canine impaction and the relationship between GV and impaction type.

Conclusion

This study showed a significant relationship between the GV and impaction status, such that this value in the cervical region of bone around impacted canines was lower than that around erupted canine teeth. Also, the GV in the apical region of bone around the impacted canines was higher than that around the erupted canines, which may reflect localized bone remodeling and could be related to increased osteoblastic activity in the area. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence of causation. Moreover, the findings of this study showed that the majority of maxillary canine impactions in the studied sample were oblique type and their crown and apex were mainly located palatally. No significant relationship was observed between gender and GV in the cervical or apical region.

Lack of dependence of these changes on type of impaction or demographic characteristics of patients could also be due to limited sample size, and therefore, prospective studies with a larger sample size and equal number of each type of impaction could help to identify the mechanisms involved in this phenomenon.
