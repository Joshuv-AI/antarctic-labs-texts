Protein-losing enteropathy after the Fontan procedure – A cardiologist’s and gastroenterologist’s perspective
Authors: Natalia Nawara-Węgrzyn, Łukasz Cichy, Kinga Kowalska-Duplaga, Aleksandra Morka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProtein-losing enteropathy (PLE) is a severe, multifactorial complication of Fontan circulation that affects approximately 12% of patients with single-ventricle physiology. Because no universal standard therapy exists, management is individualized and guided by the dominant hemodynamic and lymphatic drivers, clinical severity, and local expertise. Chronically elevated central venous pressure and impaired lymphatic drainage promote lymph congestion and leakage into the intestinal lumen, leading to hypoalbuminemia, edema, diarrhea, malnutrition, and immune dysfunction. Treatment is multimodal and includes optimization of Fontan hemodynamics, symptomatic and anti-inflammatory pharmacotherapy, and targeted nutritional strategies (high-protein diet, medium-chain triglycerides, and supplementation). Advances in lymphatic imaging have enabled phenotype-based, lymphatic-directed interventions such as lymphatic embolization and thoracic duct decompression, which can improve outcomes in selected patients. When conservative and interventional strategies fail, heart transplantation remains the definitive option. Emerging evidence also highlights the potential contribution of the gut–liver axis, including intestinal barrier dysfunction and alterations in the microbiome, which may influence inflammation and disease persistence. This review summarizes current concepts in PLE pathophysiology and therapeutic approaches, with emphasis on lymphatic dysfunction and evolving adjunctive targets.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with functionally single ventricles after palliative Fontan treatment constitute a growing population due to ever-improving results of cardiac surgery and the quality of cardiological care. Despite the continuous increase in life expectancy and quality of life in this group of patients, they still struggle with serious complications of Fontan circulation. One of the most serious is protein-losing enteropathy (PLE). In this paper, the latest pathophysiological concepts of this complication, possible treatments, and new hypotheses requiring further research are presented.

Patients with functionally single ventricles represent a heterogeneous group. This cohort includes, among others, individuals with hypoplastic left heart syndrome, tricuspid valve atresia, or extreme forms of Ebstein’s anomaly. Due to this varied anatomy, patients may have a functionally single right or left ventricle, which translates into different prognoses and complication rates.[1]

EPIDEMIOLOGY AND DEFINITION

PLE is one of the complications of Fontan circulation, affecting nearly 12% of patients.[2] It significantly influences prognosis and reduces quality of life. According to the latest studies, the 5-year survival rate from the time of the first episode is 88%.[3] For many years, various definitions of enteropathy were used, and only recently, an international team of experts introduced a unified version, according to which:

“PLE is a state of increased enteric protein loss (as measured by fecal α1-antitrypsin [spot >54 mg/dL, α1-antitrypsin clearance >27 mL/24 h without diarrhea and >56 mL/24 h with diarrhea] or by nuclear scintigraphy using technetium-99m-labelled albumin). It can be subclinical or associated with[1] hypoalbuminemia <3.5 g/dL and total protein <6 g/dL and[2] any of the following clinical symptoms: edema, abdominal distension or discomfort, diarrhea or effusions (ascites, pleural or pericardial effusions).”[4]

PATHO-MECHANISMS

Lymphatic dysfunction

Current scientific reports emphasize that lymphatic disturbances are the main pathomechanism of PLE. The significant role of this system in the development of enteropathy had long been suspected, but only in recent years – with substantial advances in lymphatic imaging and interventional treatment – has extensive evidence emerged demonstrating the major impact of lymphatic system disorders on enteropathy and offering new therapeutic options.[56]

Patients after the Fontan procedure function under conditions of chronically elevated central venous pressure and thus systemic venous congestion. Moreover, this phenomenon increases with elevated pulmonary resistance, pulmonary artery stenosis, or narrowing of the Fontan tunnel.

Hepatic venous congestion contributes to increased lymph production.[7] Simultaneously, lymph flows through the thoracic duct into the venous system – most frequently at the left venous angle (junction of the subclavian and internal jugular veins).[89] This leads to excessive lymph production with impaired outflow due to elevated venous pressure [Figure 1]. It causes significant disturbances of the lymphatic system, stimulating the formation of additional abnormal lymphatic connections to structures with low pressure; in enteropathy, that structure is the intestinal lumen.

This mechanism has been demonstrated endoscopically by showing lymph leakage into the duodenum during contrast injection into hepatic lymph nodes,[5] as well as by imaging studies such as T2-weighted magnetic resonance (MR) lymphangiography, and dynamic contrast-enhanced MR lymphangiography.[6]

With lymph leakage into the intestinal lumen, a large amount of proteins, including immunoglobulins, is lost into the gastrointestinal tract. This leads to the typical clinical symptoms of enteropathy, such as hypoalbuminemia, chronic diarrhea, peripheral edema, and immune dysfunction.

HEMODYNAMIC FACTORS

However, not every patient after the Fontan procedure with abnormal central venous pressure will develop PLE, which underscores the complex pathomechanism of this condition.

Among other causes of PLE, reduced cardiac output and its consequences are significant. Typically, Fontan patients have a cardiac output decreased by about 20%–50%.[10] By definition, cardiac output comprises myocardial contractility, preload, afterload, and heart rate. In Fontan patients, cardiac output may decline through any of these mechanisms. Many patients experience reduced myocardial contractility, pathological increases in preload due to significant valvular regurgitation, increased afterload from stenoses in the aortic arch and isthmus, and rhythm disturbances such as bradyarrhythmias (e.g., sinus node dysfunction) or tachyarrhythmias.[11]

GUT–IMMUNE AXIS

Fontan patients have also been shown to have increased vascular resistance in the mesenteric circulation.[12] Combined with reduced cardiac output, this leads to changes in intestinal perfusion characterized by chronic ischemia and resultant inflammation with elevated proinflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor [TNF]-α, C-reactive protein [CRP]), which may alter enterocyte permeability and predispose to intestinal protein loss.[13] In some patients, particularly during the acute PLE phase, decreased heparan sulfate expression on the basolateral surface of enterocytes has been observed, exacerbating intestinal protein loss and amplifying the effects of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α.[1314] Another proposed mechanism is intestinal inflammation with T-lymphocyte infiltration and increased interferon-γ in the mucosa, which worsens enteropathy symptoms and explains the favorable response to glucocorticoid treatment in some patients.[15]

All these mechanisms (elevated venous pressure, interferon-γ, TNF-α), especially when occurring together, lead to increased enterocyte permeability and lymph leakage into the intestinal lumen, resulting in clinical manifestations of enteropathy.[14]

Another factor potentially contributing to impaired intestinal barrier function and protein loss is gut microbiota dysbiosis, such as pathogenic bacterial overgrowth or reduced microbial diversity. The gut microbiota plays a key role in gastrointestinal physiology, affecting digestion, absorption, and immunity. Altered hemodynamics in Fontan patients may disrupt gut perfusion, leading to dysbiosis, which fosters inflammation and barrier dysfunction. It is hypothesized that targeted probiotic therapy could improve mucosal integrity and reduce the risk of PLE. Early reports on dysbiosis in PLE patients are promising and warrant further investigation.[1617]

DIAGNOSTIC APPROACH

Symptoms of PLE in Fontan patients often first appear after viral infections, which act as a trigger,[18] but may also arise without an evident cause.

The earliest symptoms typically include periorbital and facial edema in the morning, followed by abdominal distension and weight gain – manifestations that should raise urgent awareness in any Fontan patient. A full clinical presentation may include chronic diarrhea, ascites, and pleural effusions. In later stages, decreased bone mineral density, coagulation disorders, poor wound healing, and severe infections due to immunodeficiency may develop.

Generally accepted pharmacological methods include albumin and immunoglobulin infusions, diuretics (loop diuretics, aldosterone antagonists), steroids, heparin, and a diet high in medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs).[19] In questionnaire studies, these methods were used in most centers.[33]

Some authors emphasize the use of high-dose spironolactone, which, due to its effects on the mineralocorticoid system, may help reduce PLE symptoms.[2022]

Many patients exhibit elevated pulmonary vascular resistance. In such cases, phosphodiesterase inhibitors (sildenafil) or endothelin receptor antagonists (bosentan) are administered.[1920]

Some patients with severe PLE require oral glucocorticoid therapy due to the proposed inflammatory mechanism increasing intestinal permeability and protein loss.[34] This treatment carries significant systemic side effects and should be used for the shortest possible duration and at the lowest effective dose. Authors note that younger patients with recent PLE onset respond better to steroids, whereas older patients with chronic PLE may derive less benefit and experience adverse effects such as osteoporosis, hypertension, and immunosuppression, which significantly affect quality of life and survival.[20]

Standard heart failure medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, are also used despite controversy regarding their efficacy in this patient group.[35] In PLE exacerbations, positive inotropic agents such as milrinone or dopamine are employed.[34]

Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) may support PLE remission due to previously described heparan sulfate deficiency mechanisms. LMWH may reduce inflammation by inhibiting intestinal mast cells and improving enterocyte membrane integrity.[25] Another potentially effective agent is midodrine, owing to its action in increasing lymphatic vessel tone.[2734]

Nutritional management is an important component of PLE treatment. A high-protein diet (approximately 2 g/kg/day) and low-fat intake (<25% of caloric needs), with MCT supplementation, are recommended, along with a normo-/hypercaloric diet enriched with fat-soluble vitamins[1934] and calcium.

A diet restricting long-chain fatty acids reduces intestinal lymph production and loss, while MCTs can be absorbed directly into the bloodstream, bypassing the lymphatic system. In addition, diet may influence the microbiome; high-protein diets reduce Candida abundance and increase Saccharomyces.[36] Various Saccharomyces species substantially reduce mucosal levels of interleukin-1 beta (IL-1b), IL-6, and TNF-a,[37] and Saccharomyces boulardii was found to be potentially beneficial as adjunctive therapy in Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis.[383940] Studies analyzing the microbiome in Fontan patients and evaluating the potential benefits of probiotic supplementation are needed.

Nutritional management requires an experienced clinical dietitian to minimize malnutrition risk and ensure long-term adherence.[41]

INTERVENTIONAL TREATMENT

A patient experiencing a first PLE episode requires cardiac catheterization to assess Fontan circuit hemodynamics[31] – measuring pressures, identifying obstructions, and treating them through balloon angioplasty, stent implantation, or closure of collateral vessels that may exacerbate heart failure. Under certain hemodynamic conditions, creating or enlarging a fenestration between the Fontan tunnel and the atrium to decompress the Fontan circuit and increase cardiac output may be indicated.[30]

If conventional pharmacological and invasive therapies are unsuccessful, according to the latest recommendations,[31] lymphatic imaging should be performed using noncontrast heavily T2-weighted MR lymphangiography and, for functional assessment, dynamic contrast MR lymphangiography. One should also assess thoracic duct patency, and after this diagnostic protocol, lymphatic system–directed interventions such as cyanoacrylate glue closure of abnormal lymphatic connections or percutaneous thoracic duct decompression can be proposed.[642]

These interventions have demonstrated clinical benefit, but they remain unavailable in many centers. In the most recent European report,[33] only 10 centers (32% of those that returned the questionnaire) performed lymphatic interventions for PLE. Even among centers offering these procedures, the annual volume is low, with a median of 3 interventions per year. This suggests that despite the rapid development of lymphatic imaging and interventional techniques, access to these therapies remains limited. Given the low procedural volumes in individual institutions, the question of whether such interventions should be centralized remains open. Furthermore, the performance of lymphatic procedures is associated with considerable logistical demands – ideally requiring a catheterization laboratory integrated with MRI, as well as the availability of an endoscopist and radiologist.

HEART TRANSPLANT

If the patient is unresponsive to medical therapy, heart transplantation (HTx) should be considered. Current recommendations suggest evaluation for HTx in patients who do not respond to medical management, experience more than one PLE-related hospitalization per year, or require recurrent albumin infusions.[32]

Some authors emphasize the importance of not delaying referral, as progressive malnutrition, immunologic dysfunction, and frailty may develop and complicate the perioperative course.[32] On the other hand, recent studies have not demonstrated that the duration or severity of PLE independently worsens post-transplant outcomes.[43]

In a recent 20-center cohort study from the United States and Canada, 1-year post-transplant survival reached 92.1%, and no association was found between PLE and transplant outcomes.[44] However, other cohorts reported substantially higher perioperative mortality rates – up to 40% in patients with PLE.[4546]

In nearly all patients, PLE resolves relatively soon after transplantation, with a median time to resolution of approximately 1 month post-HTx.[43]

Pre-transplant evaluation should also include a comprehensive hepatic assessment. In patients with advanced liver disease – particularly those with esophageal varices or cirrhosis – a combined heart–liver transplantation should be considered, as some studies show superior outcomes compared with isolated HTx.[47] Conversely, other reports indicate that hepatic fibrosis may regress after restoration of biventricular circulation[48] and that Fontan-associated liver disease or variceal disease were not independent risk factors for mortality,[44] suggesting that isolated HTx remains a reasonable strategy.

CONCLUSIONS

PLE remains a significant and multifactorial complication of Fontan circulation, occurring in approximately 12% of patients with single-ventricle physiology. Its pathogenesis involves the convergence of chronic venous congestion, lymphatic dysfunction, reduced cardiac output, and low-grade intestinal inflammation. Management is multimodal and includes pharmacological therapy, nutritional support, targeted lymphatic procedures, and – when conservative measures fail – HTx as the definitive intervention.

Despite therapeutic advances, the current evidence base remains limited, as most data come from case studies and other observational reports rather than prospective or controlled trials. Future priorities include the refinement and broader validation of lymphatic-directed interventions; evaluation of emerging heart-failure therapies in the Fontan population; and deeper investigation of the gut–liver axis, including the roles of dysbiosis, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and chronic inflammation.

Improved understanding of these mechanisms, together with structured collaboration across cardiology, gastroenterology, radiology, and transplant teams, will be essential to advancing outcomes for patients with PLE.
