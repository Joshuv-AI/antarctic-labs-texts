To Drink or Not to Drink? Collective Alcohol Consumption Among Young University Students: Mapping a Path Towards Moderation. A Qualitative Study
Authors: Luz Stella Algarra‐López, Sandra Milena Hernández‐Zambrano, Mayra Solanye Galindo‐Huertas, Rafael Fernández‐Castillo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe aim of this study was to develop a theory based on the understanding of collective alcohol use as a cultural phenomenon by exploring the perceptions and meanings attributed to this behaviour by health science university students.DesignThis research is a grounded theory study.MethodThis study was conducted between 2021 and 2022. Data were collected through 10 focus groups, 15 in‐depth interviews, and 44 field journals documenting observations in alcohol consumption settings. A total of 72 health science students participated, including 51 nuring students, 14 medical students, and 7 psychology students from two cities. The data were analysed using Charmaz's constructivist grounded theory approach and coded through constant comparative analysis with Atlas.ti software.ResultsThe analysis revealed five categories that support the development of the theory: (1) ‘Understanding the Practice of Drinking,’ (2) ‘Replicating Learned Patterns: Cultural Influence,’ (3) ‘Adopting and Taking Risks,’ (4) ‘Confronting Prefabricated Imaginaries with Reality,’ and (5) ‘Reflecting on Mitigating Actions and Alternatives to Problematic Consumption.’ The findings demonstrate that culture significantly influences young adults' behaviours related to alcohol consumption, with a tendency to follow established patterns while accepting the risks involved. Studying health sciences, encourages deeper reflection on these behaviours, potentially guiding moderation in alcohol use. These insights contribute to multicultural nursing practice by examining the dynamics surrounding alcohol consumption and the factors that motivate moderation.ConclusionUnderstanding alcohol use among young university students enrolled in healthcare courses as a cultural phenomenon helps identify underlying factors and attitudes that shape these behaviours. This model was used as the foundation for developing a preventive care strategy, with young people and their experiences serving as key elements in its design and implementation.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.Keywords: alcohol consumption, constructivist grounded theory, culture, nursing, qualitative research, undergraduate students

Introduction and Background

According to the latest national consumption study conducted in Colombia in 2019, the highest prevalence of alcohol use is found among the 25–34 age group (39.7%), followed by young people aged 18–24 with 38.3%. It also shows that 23% of those who consumed alcohol in the last month in the age groups of 18–24 years and 25–34 years can be considered to have harmful alcohol use, which means practically one in every four people. This same study shows an average starting drinking age of 18 years for both men and women (Ministerio de Justicia y del Derecho—Observatorio de Drogas de Colombia 2019). Another specific study with young people shows an age of onset of 14 years, and even younger in some cases. This is relevant because of the relationship between early age of onset and risk of future dependence (Castaño Pérez et al. 2014; Dorey et al. 2021).

A study on the consumption of alcoholic beverages among university students showed that 97.5% (n=808) had consumed alcohol. It also showed the frequency, where 219 students (25.8%) consumed alcohol every month, 160 (18.8%) every two weeks and 147 (17.3%) between one and three times a year (Betancourth Zambrano et al. 2017).

The present study was conducted in two of Colombia's top five provinces in terms of alcohol consumption rates, according to the most recent national survey. In these regions, the following consumption patterns were observed: beer is the most commonly consumed alcoholic beverage due to its affordability and financial accessibility, followed by aguardiente and rum. According to the study, among those who reported drinking alcohol in the past month, 87.7% consumed beer, 38.2% aguardiente, and 28.1% rum (Ministerio de Justicia y del Derecho 2019).

In Colombia, socioeconomic strata range from 1 to 6, classifying areas and households according to their income and quality of life. Strata 1–3 represent the lowest socioeconomics levels, while strata 4–6 the highest, reflecting Colombia's socioeconomic hierarchy (Departamento Administrativo Nacional de Estadística DANE 2025).

At the two universities included in the study, it was observed that 97% of the 27,354 enrolled students at one institution belonged to socioeconomic strata 1, 2, or 3 (Universidad Pedagógica y Tecnológica de Colombia 2021) while at the other university, 76% of the 3448 students fell within the same categories (Fundación Universitaria de Ciencias de la Salud 2022).

The discussion about alcohol use among young people should not be limited to studies that focus on abstinence and reduction of consumption, but it is necessary to understand why alcohol is part of their socialisation strategies in a generalised manner, and make evident there is a culture of alcohol based on the need to build and maintain relationships (Galindo Huertas et al. 2022; Herold and Kolind 2022). Such patterns, should be interpreted within each specific context.

Regarding consumption as a cultural phenomenon, studies indicate that friends are the primary influencers in initiating alcohol use, with social groups significantly shaping patterns of harmful drinking (Buakat et al. 2022; Merrill et al. 2021). Additionally, gender can influence both drinking behaviours and perceptions of alcohol use (Álvarez Bernardo et al. 2022).

Although there are numerous publications on alcohol consumption among university students, most of them focus on consumption patterns and their consequences on health from an individual rather than a collective perspective (Castaño Castrillón and Páez Cala 2020; Cortaza‐Ramírez et al. 2022; Restrepo Escobar and Sepúlveda Cardona 2021). Another study by Mora and Herrán (2019) showed that men were at a greater risk of alcohol abuse than women. They also identified a taste for alcohol, daytime enrollment, and a higher educational attainment as risk factors. Additionally, self‐criticism and feelings of guilt following excessive alcohol consumption were common characteristics observed in this population.

Some studies have acknowledged a cultural trend in consumption and related behaviours in health‐care contexts. However, it is necessary to approach the issue from a social and cultural dimension, considering the meanings and social representations of young people who experience engage in such behaviors, based on their values and beliefs, because this approach is regarded a determining factor in health care planning (Hernández Zambrano 2016; Vargas et al. 2021).

Therefore, based on the understanding that alcohol use is strongly influenced by cultural contexts, it is essential to interpret this phenomenon in a way that recognises the elements that condition or influence immersion in consumption. This idea is consistent with Leininger's Transcultural Nursing Theory, given that recognition of cultural diversity and particularities can provide a basis for care that is in line with the needs of individuals and social groups.

A thorough analysis of alcohol use phenomenon may lead to the identification of the various contexts in which it occurs, as well as its evolution and drivers, eventually allowing for the determination of the aspects that can serve as a foundation for work on preservation or maintenance, adaptation or negotiation, and reorganisation or restructuring of cultural care. The aim is to help promote prevention strategies for young adults, in order to adopt new health practices that are more in line with their cultural beliefs and practices (McFarland and Wehbe‐Alamah 2019). Key aspects of Leininger's transcultural theory, such as cultural diversity, cultural universality, and cultural specificity, must be considered as part of care scenarios, always taking into account the values, beliefs, norms and lifestyles that have been learned, shared and transmitted within a particular culture, as they govern the thinking, decisions, and actions that can affect the health and well‐being of individuals and groups (Da Silva et al. 2021; McFarland and Wehbe‐Alamah 2019).

Methods

Study Desing

A qualitative constructivist grounded theory study was carried out based on Charmaz's epistemological foundations, data were codified and classified as they were collected, Grounded theory was the method of choice because it allowed researchers to engage the data to respond actively to alcohol consumption phenomenon, ultimately generating the theory that offers an action map (Broom 2021; Charmaz 2014; Sobel and Metzler Sawin 2016).

This study represents a preliminary step to approaching the problem of alcohol consumption, conducted as part of a broader project aimed at developing a strategy to reduce alcohol use through the methodology of Participatory Action Research (PAR) (Cornish et al. 2023). The findings represented here correspond the diagnostic phase of the PAR methodology.

Participants

Health science students were considered participants with the aim of comprehending their significance in the alcohol consumption phenomenon in connection to their disciplinary awareness as future health professionals.

The study was conducted in two universities located in different Colombian cities. Participants were recruited via email, social networks, and snowball sampling. Emails and information about the study were distributed through the institutional mass communication channels of each faculty. Videos, created by external researchers, were shared on Facebook and TikTok. The content focused on explaining the study's objectives and participation criteria. The individuals responsible for this process had no direct or prior relationship with the students. An enrollment link was included at the end of both the emails and the videos. Participants were approached via mail. Young undergraduate students of the nursing, medicine, and psychology programs, between 18 and 24 years of age, of both sexes, who had some experience with alcohol consumption, were included; no participant self‐identified as non‐binary. Students without consumption experience or who had cognitive impairment were excluded. Overall, 72 participants were included in the study: 24 men and 48 women (51 from nursing, 14 from medicine, and 7 from psychology). Of these, 66 participated only in focus groups, 6 participated only in interviews, and 9 participated in both data collection methods.

Data Collection

The researchers involved in data collection and analysis are mostly women, nurses with doctoral degrees and university professors experienced and trained in qualitative research. One of them was a doctoral student. The registration form included a question about each student's home university to ensure that the researchers moderating the focus groups and conducting the interviews were affiliated with institutions other than those of the participants. This measure ensured that the students did not know or have direct ties to the moderator of the session.

Data were collected between June 2021 and August 2022 through 10 focus groups, 15 in‐depth interviews, and 44 observations in consumer spaces, as explained below.

Using mapping techniques, students from both universities identified the most frequented consumption sites by them, where observations were subsequently made. In order to carry out these observations, it was necessary to wait until the restrictions applied by the pandemic were lifted. Two trained observers conducted observations, who used a structured guide to identify relevant aspects and take written notes (field diaries). A total of 44 field diaries were collected from the observations conducted in consumption places. The observers wrote a personal reflection with each field diary. Reflection diaries were records in which observers responded to questions related to feelings, emotions, experiences, and criticisms generated after the observation exercise; these diaries were also included in the analysis.

Focus groups and interviews were conducted via an online platform due to restrictions imposed by the COVID‐19 pandemic, which made in‐person meetings impossible. Both were digitally video and audio recorded with participants’ consent and lasted 60–90 minutes. Notes were taken during each session. There was an IT specialist at the session, but they did not intervene in any way. Participants received a gift card equivalent to $15 in local currency as a token of appreciation for their time and contribution to the study. A semi‐structured interview guide was used, for both the focus groups and the interviews, which was piloted preliminarily, and subsequent data collection was adjusted in line with the developing theory.

The sessions began with an open‐ended question, such as ‘Tell me about What do you usually do in your free time.’ This was followed by inquiries about drinking experiences, their first time drinking, motivations, and where they usually consumed alcohol. In addition, the researchers encouraged the participants without interrupting them, allowing them to express themselves comfortably; none of them refrained from participating.

The trustworthiness of data analysis was determined by conducting consistency checks, in which researchers analysed selected interview transcripts to determine whether focused coding and emerging theoretical coding were congruent.

To elaborate and refine the categories of the emerging theory, theoretical sampling was conducted to develop the properties of the categories until no new properties emerged, i.e., when saturation was reached.

Data Analysis

Verbatim transcripts of focus groups and interviews, along with field diaries of observations in consumption spaces and reflective journals, were organised using Atlas.ti 9 software (Atlas.ti 2021). The analysis followed a constant comparative method. Charmaz's (2014) process of initial, open, focused, axial, and theoretical coding was applied, with each step repeated cyclically (Belgrave and Seide 2019). A native English speaker handled both the direct and reverse translation of participant´ quotes.

Labels were assigned to data segments at the beginning of the coding process, which was done line‐by‐line and incident‐by‐incident, resulting in the development of initial codes (Belgrave and Seide 2019). Constant dialogue and comparison of the initial codes led to the next level of abstraction, which was related to the formulation and construction of focused coding.

As data collection progressed, the analysis became more robust, generating new questions and significant reflections on the emerging data. This process led to the creation and conceptual linkage of the final categories within a theoretical model, identifying their properties and dimensions (Charmaz 2014). Analytical memos played a crucial role throughout, enhancing the relationship between coding and understanding the phenomenon (Charmaz 2014; Saldaña 2021), although they were not included in the formal data analysis.

The initial analysis was conducted by one of the researchers. This was followed by a collective review of the results by all members of the research team, who assessed the relevance of the codes and categories. All data—including transcripts from focus groups and interviews, field diaries, reflective journals, and the analysis carried out in Atlas.ti, which included analytical memos—were accessible to all researchers facilitating collaborative analysis. Finally, the findings were presented to the participants in meetings, allowing them to express whether the emergence of the initial and focused codes, as well as the categories, reflected their personal experiences related to alcohol consumption—thus supporting the process of triangulation.

Rigour

Criteria for maximum variation sampling or representativeness, member verification, and study originality were applied to ensure rigour, dependability, and validity (Charmaz 2014). To ensure data credibility, multiple information sources were combined, including recordings and transcripts from focus groups and interviews, as well as field observation records. Throughout the research, a triangulation process (Noble and Heale 2019) was conducted, which included periodic meetings with both the research team and participants, separate from the focus groups and interviews. The researchers' meetings focused on discussing the thematic emergence and the direction of the research, while meetings with participants were held to confirm the findings and ensure they resonated with their experiences. The research ensured data sufficiency through saturation (Fofana et al. 2020). Theoretical saturation enhanced the fittingness and consistency (Cho et al. 2023).

Ethics Approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Universidad Pedagógica y Tecnológica de Colombia under Minutes No. 04 date May 20, 2019, It was classified it as a minimal risk study for the participants, according to Resolution 8430 of 1993 of the Ministry of Health, to be presented in call No. 844: Call for the generation of new knowledge through scientific research projects in medical and health sciences. The collected data were not used for any purpose other than the research, and the transcribed data were stored with password protection.

Results

The theoretical explanation is made up of five final concepts that emerged from the analytical process and are interconnected with one another. The meaning of each concept is supported by its own focused codes, which in turn contain the grouping of the initial codes. The following description indicates the inclusion of some codes focused on more than one concept, which is justified in each case. Each of the final emerging concepts is detailed below:

Understanding the Practice of Drinking

This concept (Figure 1) illustrates how young adults experience alcohol use, including their main motivations for drinking and the practices associated with drinking, such as planning, spaces, and the people with whom they share alcohol, among others. It also describes the differences they experience in terms of the role of being a woman or a man and their behaviour in the scenarios in which consumption takes place.

Representation of the concept Understanding the practice of drinking (ATLAS‐Ti).

Spotting Opportunities to Drink: Motivations

Motivations were analysed from two perspectives. First, intrinsic motivations were identified as follows: the desire to experiment and search for a feeling of euphoria in order to have fun, which is consistent with the feeling of disinhibition after drinking alcohol, alcohol as the perfect companion during difficult or stressful times that provides a mechanism of escape, being unconscious or unaware of what is happening around:

When we drink, we can feel that we are cooler, we enjoy ourselves more, we are more fun, more uninhibited, and we have more fun. FG6P5FB

Let's say I'm heartbroken or something like that, so obviously I'm going to be upset and all I will want to do is drink and forget about that situation, so the only way to forget is to get drunk and lose consciousness. FG9P1FB

Extrinsic motivations, by contrast, are closely related to the need to socialise. Peer pressure and the desire to belong or be accepted in the group combine to create tension, which is exacerbated by the fact that alcohol is traditionally part of parties and celebrations. As the group establishes the criteria for acceptance, a discrepancy may be perceived negatively, leading to rejection, exclusion, and the label of ‘boring.’ Pressure comes from a variety of sources that promote the idea that alcohol and entertainment are intimately related, such as the alcohol industry through advertising, retail businesses, or friends or family, either directly or through social networks; another relevant situation is the transition from school to university as a way to form new relationships, which is combined with the idea of being older, of acquiring a certain ‘freedom’ and independence, as illustrated below:

It's more about social pressure… The group of friends tells you to drink, and sometimes they grab you by the face and pour it in your mouth… You want to be accepted in a social group or have fun. FG1P1FB

There are videos on social networks, where you see what people do when they go to a party, and everything looks great. You feel like doing it, you are very influenced. I6FT

I watched movies, soap operas, YouTube videos, that made it look like they make you feel good if you are heartbroken. Other people may feel bad, but I think that's what made me go for it. ENT12HBs

Recalling the drinking experience This concept describes the experiences of young users, encompassing the different environments in which drinking takes place, starting with the planning, which in most cases arises by common agreement through electronic means in which the day, place and budget are defined. It is essential for the young person to know with whom they are drinking, and it is best to do so with people they trust, who can offer some security and ‘take care’ of them, in the event of drunkenness or the inherent insecurity of the environment. There is a tendency to consume alcohol on weekends, especially at night. The amount is determined depending on the resources available, the group, and the ‘mood’.

The types of beverages chosen depend on the place where they are being shared, the amount of money available, the group's mood, and the individual tastes of its members. Young adults are aware, as students, their financial resources are limited, which is why they need to be well managed.

Everything is planned, and the people who are going to drink get together to buy the alcohol, and their choice of beverage is determined by the amount of money they have. FG3P3FB

Beer is one of the most popular beverages due to its low cost. Mixed drinks are also a good option when the objective is to lighten drunkenness, that is, when the desire is to get drunk quickly, mixtures of different drinks provide this effect.

Consumption places can include a friend's house, nightclubs, bars, restaurant‐bars, neighbourhood stores, and places close to each university, all of which are well known for their low prices and the fact that these places are very frequented by them. In public universities in particular, it is possible to find areas where alcohol and other substances such as cigarettes, marijuana, and other drugs can be consumed.

There were times when people would drink alcohol on the university campus, so it was common to see people using tobacco, alcohol, and marijuana. I3MT

Along the path to excess, there are behaviours that show that consumption can be ‐ or can become ‐ problematic, and these young adults describe it as progressive. They acknowledge that they know when they overstep their boundaries, and they perceive when others are doing so as well. Negative perceptions of risk are downplayed, and responsibilities are either ignored or combined with consumption, as expressed by the participants.

Differentiating between drinking as a man or a woman: gender also affects how consumption is perceived and experienced. Men's consumption tends to carry more positive connotations due to their perceived masculinity and the ability to ‘flirt’ with women.

Many boys believe that, because they are men, they have to comply with certain stereotypes. FG4P4FT

Machismo. If boys drink, it makes them more masculin

In contrast, women’s drinking is more often met with discrimination and negativity, as they are seen to face greater and more frequent risk, which unconsciously puts pressure on them to exercise more restraint, take better care of themselves, or feel the need to be taken care of, this speaks not only about the youth culture, but a larger culture where women are always (although particularly when they drink) at risk.

I feel and have experienced that as a woman one feels vulnerable… as if she is blocked and is not able to say no… or I don't like it, or on the contrary, she feels guilty, or she feels threatened…. INT14FT

Women have always been able to drink large amounts of alcohol, but in the past, people believed that they couldn't… and they face the same risks as men do or maybe more because they are unprotected. I4MT

Observing risks in use scenarios: the most visible ones. Disinhibition as a bridge to sexuality, which frequently leads to increased risks of abuse or exposure to unprotected sex. While drinking, and especially as the level of alcohol increases, many men [whether from the group or strangers] attempt to ‘take advantage’ of women. In such situations, an intoxicated woman may temporarily lose control of herself, and while she may try to resist, this may not be enough to stop the harassment, ultimately making her a victim.

We see many demonstrations of affection between couples and groups of three people, who kiss and caress each other in a very explicit manner. FJB08

Many people prefer to dance alone because it has been noticed as a trend among men that they use the opportunity to dance with women to touch them without their consent. FG5P4FB

There was another man who thought I was drunk, so I got mad and told him ‘stay away from me, don't talk to me, stay away because I don't want to, and I am not drunk’. ENT9MT

Replicating Learned Patterns: Cultural Influence

Culture plays a key role in alcohol consumption (Figure 2) since it is embedded in patterns that are passed down from generation to generation. Institutions, media, age group, family, and friends are all directly involved in the learning of these traditions from an early age. Many of the behaviours young adults develop, as well as their perceptions of gender roles and opportunities for drinking, come from these contexts, resulting in the frequent presence of alcohol across a variety of scenarios.

Representation of the concept Replicating learned patterns: cultural influence. (ATLAS‐Ti).

Having said that, many of these motivations can be understood by grouping them within this concept, because alcohol has been attributed the role of “social lubricant” by being included in many celebrations and festivities, associating it with moments of both pleasure and discomfort as a good companion, in other words, assuming it to be a positive reinforcer in any occasion.

Recognising a drinking culture. Most young adults remembered their first encounters with alcohol occurring in family settings, which means that it was their close relatives—parents, uncles, aunts, grandparents, or cousins—generally decided, often at an early age, that they should be initiated into drinking, a behaviour that was more noticeable in men. Young adults have witnessed, learned and normalised since childhood the presence of alcohol in everyday celebrations such as baptisms, first communions, marriages, Christmas, New Year's celebrations, birthdays, etc.,

When you see your dad getting drunk, your young mind assumes that if he does it, you can too. I4MT

It's true that drinking alcohol at social events is very common and completely normal. FG3P5MB

Obviously, people won't stop drinking because society tells them to. If there's alcohol, there's a party, and if there's a party, there's a good mood. FG2P2FT

Social behaviours related to alcohol consumption are more permissive for men, as will be discussed further below. In fact, friends may use derogatory names to put pressure on their peers to consume more, implying that not consuming or consuming less is synonymous with being unmasculine. In this way, there is a desire to demonstrate the opposite, though this does not always occur, given that other young adults have embraced consumption as something positive, so it could be a coalition of these tendencies. These manifestations highlight the presence of a generalised hegemonic masculinity.

Adopting and Taking Risks

Participants were largely aware of the risks associated with drinking. They referred to both short‐ and long‐term risks to physical and mental health, as well as the risks posed by many drinking environments in cities with high rates of crime and violence.

Relationships built around alcohol can be seriously affected if alcohol is absent, and can become challenging or even exclusionary, or worthy of criticism if the non‐drinker is perceived as a dull person or a “party pooper”. However, their desire to be part of a group forces them to adopt and take implicitly associated risks, such as drinking large quantities of alcohol or binge drinking, alcoholic coma, open invitations to experiment with other drugs, altered behaviour that can turn into violence or aggression, driving while intoxicated, exposure to environmental risks such as walking late at night in places considered dangerous, robbery, and sexual abuse the latter poses a higher level of exposure in the case of women.

If you have a hangover, you won't want to eat or do things you used to enjoy. This is linked to depression and anxiety. GF4P5MT

In my social circle, it's easy to mix different alcoholic drinks and even other things. Someone or a friend will often give you a ‘substance’ for free when you are drunk, making it easy for you to try it. This is pretty normal. I4MT

Driving under the influence. FG1P1FB

My friend heard a guy say, ‘Look at that woman, she's so drunk I could take her to my apartment’ and when my friend turned to look at the woman, he realised that the drunk woman was me. FG4P5FT

These risks, whether experienced, observed or overheard, are viewed negatively and can lead to frustrating or embarrassing experiences that can influence future consumption (Figure 3).

Representation of the concept Adopting and taking risks (ATLAS‐ti).

Confronting Prefabricated Imaginaries With Reality

This concept is based on a comparison of pre‐established expectations with actual consumption experiences, as shown in Figure 4, Although these focused codes have already been discussed, they are pertinent to this concept due to the participants' reflective description of how a positive association with alcohol consumption can be altered after being exposed to the risks.

Representation of the concept Confronting prefabricated imaginaries with reality (ATLAS‐ti).

The following are some reflections that support this concept:

At about six o'clock in the morning they couldn't even stand up. They were lying on the floor, and some of them sat down and cried, others started to laugh, others started to dance. What a change! FG4P4FT

My situation deteriorated long before, which was a trigger, and I almost ended my life. FG4P5FT

If these limits are broken, we see what happens when drinking is out of control, that is, you no longer control what you do or what you drink. You drink whatever you can get your hands on. I1MT

This is significant because it creates a duality of positions regarding alcohol use and, consequently, lead to a modification of their initial meanings, in which case it may influence the modification of their behaviour or the adoption of risk‐mitigation measures.

Reflecting on Mitigating Actions and Alternatives to Problematic Consumption

Experiences with alcohol cause the emergence of critical reflections about drinking, as well as key elements about mitigating actions during drinking moments and some alternatives to problematic consumption, which are prompted by the variation of meanings (Figure 5). From this perspective, participants become involved as active actors in decision‐making developing skills inself‐recognition, self‐control, and boundary‐setting‐. An additional advantage was the knowledge they have as health science students, which provided them a greater understanding of the health risks associated with excessive alcohol consumption.

Representation of the concept Reflecting on mitigating actions and alternatives to problematic consumption (ATLAS‐ti).

Managing drinking: the tension between autonomy based on self‐knowledge and social pressure. Participants who maintained or had adopted moderate drinking behaviours recognised that autonomy allows them to keep a firm attitude about their experience of alcohol. From a critical stance, many are aware that abstinence is not an option for them, or that it may be somewhat utopian, so they considered that self‐awareness of their drinking patterns was essential for knowing when to take a break or stop altogether.

Every person knows their limits… I don't see anything wrong with it if it doesn't exceed that limit. FG3P1FB

Saying I don't want to, is also a way of reaching a limit. FG3P4F

It's the responsibility, of what I want to do with myself, with my body… you know how far you can go. FG9P6FB

Hence, they recognised that self‐ awareness of individual boundaries was a key element in enhancing self‐control, which goes in the same line of having fun without going overboard.

Reflecting on mitigating actions and alternatives: The mitigating actions captured were those that are implemented at the time and place of consumption, combining both boundary management and control over the amount of alcohol consumed or the reduction of associate risk. One of these strategies is to explain, even impose, why they want to limit their alcohol consumption; however, some people prefered not to take this step and instead pretend to drink or dispose of the drink in a discreet manner, such as spilling it on the floor or giving it to another person in the group, to avoid tense arguments prompted by the influence of alcohol.

When I feel very bad, I eat something. GF8P5MT

When I go out, I drink very little, dance, and drink water all the time. GF10P1MB

I've always been told that it's important to know the kind of people I'm going to be drinking with. FG6P1FB

Some others were more concerned with mitigating risks than with moderating consumption, so they also make sure they have a group that is trustworthy enough to look out for each other. Although these strategies were generally expressed by women, they are supported by their male peers, especially if they have been exposed to some negative event, such as theft, fights, falls, etc.

There are leisure alternatives not associated with alcohol and shared with friends and family in different scenarios or groups where drinking was not common, these alternatives were viewed as helpful in keeping them away from drinking and included, activities such as traveling, walking, cycling, visiting a new place, going to the movies, playing sports.

When duty calls: impact of being a health caregiver: Their role as future healthcare professionals demonstrated their desire to promote both self‐care and care for others. Complementing a greater awareness and commitment to influence and transmit to others the knowledge acquired in the academic environment about the negative physical and mental effects of alcohol.

I'm going to be responsible of other people's lives, so if I drink alcoholic beverages, I can probably hurt those people. I8FB

When you study this program, we talk about risk factors. The main ones are tobacco and alcohol, so that is very scary. Seeing myself sick in the future and sharing with sick people… When you know what happens in the body or the toxic effect these substances have, you understand what is happening in the body… Women who are close to me, listen to me and understand what I am talking about. For example, I have a boyfriend and I used to tell him ‘This happens to us or that happens to us' and he also stopped drinking for that reason’. I10FT

I believe it is something that can help me to strengthen many professional skills and to develop some communication competencies. RJB01

Some participants expressed concern about the responsibility inherent in professional practice and the possibility of making mistakes while under the influence of alcohol, which could potentially endanger the health or lives of others.

Theoretical Model of Alcohol Consumption Among Young Adults

The theoretical framework presented here provides an explanatory model of collective drinking behaviour among young people and the interrelationships that take place around this activity. It includes the initial encounter with alcohol, immersion in drinking practices, and the convergence of both aspects invariation of meaning, which ultimately leads to a decision that manifests in one of two directions: moderate consumption or problematic consumption. The figure begins and ends with alcohol consumption among young adults (Figure 6), implying that all of the foundations that support the model generate a cyclical motion shaped by elements ranging from the individual to the social, which are interconnected to give meaning to the possible actions.

Representation of the Theoretical Model of Alcohol Consumption among Young Adults (Author's own work).

The first encounter with drinking is strongly associated with the replication of patterns learned through cultural influence. These patterns arise from close relationships established with family and friends, the perception of such behaviour as an example to be followed, the expectations surrounding the role of being young, which is based on the expectations or normative values of the group, and interaction among peers. Therefore, these types of relationships, some of which are stronger than others, result in an assumed position towards consumption. For young adults who begin drinking, two main starting points can be identified. The first being the encouragement they receive from these pre‐established close relationships, which often turn into pressure exerted partly due to feelings of affection or obligation towards certain individuals, and the second being the motivations and meanings that assign drinking a positive and EN role in many moments of a young person's life, thus legitimising its incorporation into these moments to enhance the acquired meaning of pleasure, release, and support for mourning, which in some way strengthen and deepen their relationships.

Immersion is a phenomenon typically reinforced through close relationships. It may arise immediately after the first contact with alcohol or develop over time, sometimes coinciding with a significant event in the young person's life, such as the transition from school to university, which may represent a literal or figurative emancipation that increases their capacity for decision‐making. Within immersion, two scenarios can be distinguished: the adoption and assumption of risks and the confrontation of prefabricated imaginaries with reality.

Adopting and assuming risks are based on their evolving meanings, as it is evident that individual action determines social action and social action determines individual action. Moreover, due to the incorporation process into new scenarios and the beginning of adult life, and as a symbolic gesture of cohesion and bonding, young adults give great importance to basing their actions on the actions of others, which does not imply that the young adult is irremediably forced to adopt the pattern, but does imply a prevalent and generalised phenomenon, at least in the subject matter of this paper.

When experimenting with drinking and taking risks, prefabricated imaginaries are confronted with reality, shaped by previously established perceptions about alcohol, either from in individual or group experiences. Whether these experiences are their own or those of others, they become a significant determinant of future perceptions of drinking. This experimentation allows people to get to know themselves in relation to drinking, allowing them to assess excesses, post‐consumption behaviours, and the risks involved, resulting in a kind of self‐knowledge that leads to a reflective process.

Both scenarios create an obvious tension between positive and negative modulators, resulting in a variation in the initial meanings assigned to alcohol consumption. Which may deviate towards a negative or positive perception, prompting young adults to determine which may have more weight in a sort of “scale” that converges in two ways: The first direction is the perpetuation of the phenomenon, reinforced by maintaining adopted patterns, group pressure, and social behaviours, as well as the re‐potentiation of associated positive reinforcers. The second is the generation of reflections and mitigating actions: possible solutions, that emerge not as merely anecdotal process, but as one forged through the activation of critical capacity based on self‐awareness, will‐power, and introspection as essential elements of self‐management during drinking. At this stage, the young adult's capacity for agency is consolidated, not towards abstinence because it has been demonstrated that it is unrealistic, but rather towards moderation by establishing limits based on self‐control and autonomy.

Finally, the process returns to its starting point after a “choice” is made about how to experience consumption alcohol, as the factors described earlier continue regulate this experience.

Discussion

The results of the present study gave rise to a theoretical model that aims to understand alcohol consumption among university students of health sciences programs as a collective and cultural phenomenon, exploring the perceptions and meanings given to such consumption.

It was found that the starting point is their relationship with close circles of friends and family and that the purpose of consumption is socialisation. However, a very relevant fact is the transition to the beginning of university life, which implies a more introspective decision on consumption due to the detachment in many cases from the family environment.

There is also a tendency to follow pre‐established patterns in which alcohol is viewed as synonymous with fun or as a trusted companion for dealing with difficult situations. These assumptions encourage young adults to experiment with alcohol, and once immersed, they accept the risks associated with drinking, despite aware of them. Throughout this process, they confront preconceived imaginaries with real life experiences, leading to a shift of previous meanings and, consequently, strengthening or distorting the imaginaries that give alcohol the role of a positive reinforcer.

Regarding the first assumption, there is a predisposition to the permanence and perpetuation of consumption, which may change at some point, as illustrated in the theoretical explanatory model. The second assumption, in contrast, emphasises reflective behaviour through the activation of critical capacity, thus stimulating will and power, both of which can be considered decisive elements for self‐management of alcohol use and risk minimisation. This process is grounded in the consolidation of the young adult's capacity for agency, prompting moderation rather than abstinence through the establishment of boundaries based on self‐knowledge.

One of the most relevant findings of the present study is the confirmation that culture influences the drinking behaviour of young people, which is consistent with other studies in different contexts but with similar results or with other substances such as tobacco (Saetta et al. 2023). Alcohol use is seen as a highly social event, mostly linked to the desire for social interaction, group acceptance, and entertainment; excessive consumption would also be motivated by these same reasons (Carey and MacGregor 2019; García‐Carpintero‐Muñoz et al. 2020; Greene and Maggs 2020; Sibley et al. 2020).

In turn, gender is revealed as an attribute that shapes different positions on the perception and experience of risks and consumption since, in the case of men, it is associated with masculinity and, therefore, the risks to which they are exposed are underestimated, while in women it is associated with a high degree of vulnerability and the inherent need to take care of themselves and to be cared for by others (Álvarez Bernardo et al. 2022; Herrero‐Montes et al. 2022; Wright et al. 2022). The downplay manifestations observed here reveal a culture in which hegemonic masculinity prevails, associatting manhood with machismo, strength, power, and domination (Mejia et al. 2021; Mendieta‐Izquierdo et al. 2021). This cultural framework partly explains why the young men in this study tend to downplay they risks to which they expose themselves and to assume others because they have been taught that the fact of being men grants them a certain “immunity”.

The development of the drinking habit is clearly shaping by signals received over the years from relatives, the media, peers and friends environment, all of which reinforce alcohol as a regular companion and normalise and accept its use in multiple situations. This finding is consistent results of studies conducted in others contexts (Matjila et al. 2021). Along these lines, a direct association has been idenfified between of certain places and alcohol consumption, including bars, nightclubs, spaces near the university specifically designed for the use of alcoholic beverages, which are well known and popular among students. There is also a tendency to associate university years with a period of experimentation and enjoyment of alcohol, as well as with excesses (Gambles et al. 2022; Terry‐McElrath et al., Terry‐McElrath et al. 2023). Social networks play a key role in drinking behavior, as they serve as a channel through which young people receive or share moments related to the beverage or its advertising, and are also the main means of communication for planning (Hutton et al. 2020).

The willingness of undergraduate health science students to take on the role of caregiver is consistent with other findings showing that heavy drinkers adjust their behaviour at a drinking event if a friend is considered to be in a risky situation or if they are in the company of non‐drinking individuals or groups (Merrill et al. 2021). Furthermore, knowledge about the effects of alcohol, further developed in health science programs, seems to increase the degree of understanding and awareness of the risks and the tendency to reduce alcohol use in some cases, even using it to raise awareness in other drinkers who are close to them, such as friends or relatives.

The actions undertaken by young adults to mitigate risks and moderate consumption tend to operate in two ways. The first is an indirect way involving certain strategies, often unnoticed by the group, while the second is direct and identifies rejection (Banister et al. 2019; Greene and Maggs 2020) as a synonym for autonomy and control over consumption. In either case, the tension generated will be released by highlighting the young person's capacity for agency in terms of actions and recognition of the potential for action (Priestley 2020).

The capacity for agency of a young adult in relation to alcohol use is an issue that must be considered in terms of the factors that motivate action, so it is not enough to have knowledge; there must be a trigger to modify the way of thinking about alcohol, and this is where critical awareness, introspection, and volition become promoters of future agency in young adults in relatioll to alcohol use (Galindo Huertas et al. 2022). However, in order to generate transformation, it is necessary that this capacity for agency outweighs the motivations for consumption since this gives rise to a change in meanings, making the young person find sufficient reasons to maintain moderate consumption and influence others. In other words, there should be a powerful meaning that allows them to see themselves as active subjects and builders of their own reality and environment, as well as allowing the actors to interweave the act of drinking.

Conclusions

Understanding collective alcohol use among young adults as a cultural phenomenon provides a comprehensive view of their behaviors, as well as the elements involved in the process, such as initiation, immersion, and future attitudes towards drinking, whether directed to permanence or moderation. On the other hand, the present study can be used as a reference model for developing public policies and preventive strategies, since it reveals key points of the different moments that young adults may be going through in terms of alcohol consumption. These findings can be used to guide care, resulting in the empowerment of young people's agency.
