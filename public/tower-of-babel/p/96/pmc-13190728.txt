Directionally factorized light field reconstruction with cross-epipolar and spatial modeling
Authors: Ahmed Salem, Ebrahem Elkady, Hatem Ibrahem, Jae-Won Suh, Hyun-Soo Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-angular-resolution light fields (LFs) are essential for advanced imaging tasks such as post-capture refocusing, depth estimation, and virtual reality. However, acquiring densely sampled LFs remains challenging due to hardware limitations and high capture costs. We propose DFCNet, an end-to-end framework for LF angular super-resolution that reconstructs high-resolution LFs from sparse inputs. The network leverages a hierarchical Residual-in-Residual architecture with Directionally Factorized Convolutional Blocks to disentangle spatial, epipolar, and cross-epipolar features while preserving angular consistency. Channel recalibration via Squeeze-and-Excitation blocks adaptively emphasizes informative features, and a hybrid interpolation–reconstruction strategy ensures accurate and stable angular upsampling. Extensive experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that DFCNet consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods in average PSNR and SSIM, accurately reconstructing fine textures, occluded regions, and angular structures. The method also generalizes effectively to extrapolation tasks, synthesizing previously unseen views with high fidelity. These results establish DFCNet as a robust and efficient solution for high-quality light field reconstruction.Keywords: Light field reconstruction, View synthesis, Angular super-resolution, Convolution neural networkSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Light field (LF) and conventional imaging are rooted in fundamentally different theoretical frameworks for visual data representation. Conventional imaging follows a projection model in which light rays are integrated over all directions at each sensor pixel, yielding a two-dimensional intensity image corresponding to a single scene plane. While efficient, this approach discards angular information, limiting post-capture analysis to the spatial domain. In contrast, LF imaging is grounded in the plenoptic function, which describes light intensity as a function of both spatial and directional variables. By jointly sampling spatial and angular dimensions, LF imaging constructs a four-dimensional representation that preserves ray geometry. This structure naturally encodes parallax and depth cues and can be analyzed through epipolar-plane images, where line slopes directly correspond to scene depth 1,2.

LF cameras offer post-capture flexibility beyond conventional systems by recording the complete LF of a scene3. This enables operations such as digital refocusing, viewpoint synthesis, and 3D reconstruction4, opening possibilities for immersive applications in virtual and augmented reality, computational photography, and advanced image editing5. Moreover, LF imaging supports broader computer vision tasks, including salient object detection6, reflectance estimation7, occlusion removal8, and depth recovery9.

Microlens-based LF cameras (e.g., RayTrix10) enable single-shot capture of both intensity and angular information by inserting a microlens array (MLA) between the main lens and sensor. However, their limited sensor resolution imposes an inherent trade-off between spatial and angular resolution, which constrains downstream vision applications11. To mitigate this issue, LF images can be enhanced either spatially, through LF spatial super-resolution12,13, or angularly, via LF angular super-resolution or reconstruction14–20. In this work, we address this limitation by proposing a novel LF reconstruction framework that generates densely sampled LF images from sparse observations.

Recent advances in LF reconstruction have explored two main directions. One class of methods19,20 exploits local correlations across neighboring views for novel view synthesis. While effective, such methods are sensitive to misalignments between perspectives, limiting their ability to exploit long-range dependencies. Another class15,17,21 leverages depth as guidance for reconstruction. Although promising, these approaches require accurate depth estimation-often difficult to obtain-and incur high computational costs due to the joint processing of depth and angular cues, reducing their suitability for real-time applications.

This paper introduces a convolutional neural network (CNN) designed to effectively model LF data. Specifically, we employ the macro-pixel image representation to preserve angular consistency and spatial locality. Directional feature extractors are introduced to capture horizontal and vertical epipolar dependencies, cross-epipolar interactions, and spatial context, enabling effective integration of information across all views while retaining fine intra-view details. A channel recalibration and fusion mechanism balances contributions from multiple feature branches, and a hybrid interpolation–reconstruction strategy performs angular upsampling to generate dense LFs. Compared to state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods, our approach models long-range angular and spatial correlations more efficiently, achieving robust and high-fidelity LF reconstruction.

Related work

Many studies have investigated the problem of LF super-resolution in both the spatial and angular domains. In this work, we specifically address the challenge of enhancing angular resolution. Existing methods can broadly be divided into two groups: (1) depth-independent approaches that reconstruct LFs directly, and (2) depth-assisted approaches that synthesize novel perspective views by leveraging estimated depth information.

Depth-assisted approaches

LF reconstruction using depth information has been extensively explored through both conventional and CNN-based methods. In conventional approaches, Wanner and Goldluecke22 proposed estimating disparity maps from EPI cross-sections of 4D LFs, followed by optimization to synthesize high-quality novel views. This method, however, assumes ideal imaging conditions and does not account for noise or optical aberrations. Pearson et al.23 introduced a layer-based model that incorporates depth layers to more accurately represent scene geometry, enabling novel view synthesis from arbitrary angular positions using a limited set of input views. Zhang et al.24 developed disparity-assisted phase-based synthesis using micro-baseline stereo pairs to refine disparity maps and reduce phase inconsistencies. Zhang et al.25 further proposed decomposing the central view into editable depth layers, performing patch-based image synthesis on these layers, and propagating the edits across all views.

Recent CNN-based methods typically follow a two-stage framework: a depth or disparity estimation network followed by a view synthesis stage. To handle errors arising from transformations, especially in occluded regions, an additional refinement network is often incorporated. For instance, Kalantari et al.15 used sequential networks for disparity estimation and color prediction, but their approach struggled with large disparities. Salem et al.26 improved efficiency by introducing a predefined module in the initial stage and leveraging dual disparity vectors to reduce interpolation errors. Jin et al.17 employed a depth estimation network with a wide receptive field for large-disparity scenes and proposed a blending strategy using alternating spatial and angular convolutions. Wu et al.18 exploited the sheared LF structure and trained a CNN to evaluate shearing scores, followed by back-shearing and fusion. Shi et al.27 combined pixel-wise and feature-based reconstruction in a learning-based framework. Jin et al.16 designed a flexible LF reconstruction network with customizable angular resolution using disparity and confidence maps. Meng et al.28 generated target views via aperture disparity and warping confidence maps, while Ko et al.29 integrated channel-wise pooling with adaptive feature remixing to exploit view-specific differences. Finally, Chen et al.30 introduced CSTNet, which employs a multiplane-based cross-view interaction framework combining multiplane feature fusion and a cross-shaped transformer for effective cross-view information exchange and improved robustness to scene geometry variations.

Depth-independent approaches

A considerable body of work has focused on LF reconstruction without relying on depth information, spanning both traditional methods and more recent CNN-based techniques. In conventional approaches, Mitra et al.31 introduced a patch-based reconstruction framework using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), while Shi et al.32 and Vagharshakyan et al. 33 exploited the inherent sparsity of LFs for reconstruction.

With the advent of deep learning, a variety of CNN-based strategies have been explored. Yoon et al.34 employed a two-step network to jointly enhance spatial and angular resolutions. However, their method neglected the angular correlations between neighboring views during angular super-resolution. To improve reconstruction, Meng et al.35 adopted residual restoration and refinement modules with high-dimensional convolutions. Liu et al.20 extracted features along horizontal, vertical, and angular directions from EPIs, but the independent processing of each branch led to performance degradation. Wang et al.36 integrated both angular and spatial information to achieve more faithful reconstructions, further handling occlusions and disocclusions by explicitly modeling pixel visibility. Their multi-scale design also expanded the receptive field, improving fine-detail recovery.

Other notable contributions include the work of Liu et al.14, who reconstructed macro-pixel features from sub-aperture images using a 3D U-Net-like architecture, and Wang et al.37, who proposed a general extraction framework combining spatial, angular, and EPI features, later extending it to multiple LF tasks including angular and spatial SR as well as depth estimation. Elkady et al. 38 introduced a three-stage network that sequentially processes epipolar, spatial, and angular cues. By leveraging quadrilateral epipolar feature extraction with weight sharing, their design achieved efficient yet high-quality reconstruction.

Several approaches have explored the potential benefits of integrating both depth-based and non-depth-based methods. A notable example is the work by Chen et al.39 which proposes a disparity-guided combination strategy. Their method leverages the strengths of non-depth-based techniques in regions with small disparity while employing depth-based approaches for areas with larger disparity. This hybrid approach improves reconstruction quality by adapting to varying disparity levels, effectively leveraging the strengths of each method to enhance the performance.

Recently, several methods have been proposed for joint spatial-angular LF super-resolution. Mao et al. 40 propose a domain-specialized network with stage-tailored strategies for angular and spatial SR. Mao et al. 41 introduce sparse-to-dense inbetweening for multi-view LFs with adaptive feature alignment. Liu et al. 42 develop an implicit detail-enhanced network (IDNet) using 3D convolution and multi-representation enhancement. These works highlight the ongoing evolution toward unified LFSASR frameworks.

Methodology

Light field representation

Based on the two-plane parameterization43, the LF is modeled as a 4D tensor: where (X, Y) and (U, V) denote the spatial and angular dimensions, respectively. For efficient processing, the LF is commonly expressed in three complementary forms: sub-aperture images (SAIs), epipolar plane images (EPIs), and macro-pixel images (MacPIs), as showing in Fig. 1 and detailed in37.

Different representations of the LF. (a) Sub-aperture image (SAI). (b) Epipolar-plane image (EPI), where horizontal and vertical EPIs are obtained by fixing one angular and one spatial coordinate. (c) Macro-pixel image (MacPI).

The MacPI representation is particularly advantageous for angular super-resolution: unlike SAIs, which treat each view independently, or EPIs, which sacrifice spatial locality, MacPI preserves both spatial and angular dimensions in the feature map, enabling convolutions to operate along angular axes without spatial mixing. Building on this, our directional convolutions are geometrically aligned with light-field structure-a kernel along the angular dimension directly captures epipolar correlations (disparity lines), while dilation enlarges the angular receptive field to handle larger disparities. Factorizing into separate epipolar () and cross-epipolar () branches enforces a clean geometric separation: the former models correlations along the same epipolar line, the latter interactions across different epipolar planes (e.g., occlusions). This design embeds light-field-specific priors directly into the convolution kernels, offering lower computational cost and stronger angular consistency than generic long-range modeling strategies (e.g., transformers or non-local blocks).

Angular upsampling

For angular resolution enhancement, we adopt a hybrid interpolation–reconstruction strategy. Bicubic interpolation first provides a coarse initialization in the angular domain, which stabilizes learning and reduces complexity compared to purely learned upsampling. To handle arbitrary (including non-integer) upsampling factors, we further adopt the downsample–upsample scheme from DistgASR 37. Specifically, learned features are projected into a higher-dimensional angular space using a convolution with stride equal to the input angular resolution, followed by a convolution for channel expansion. A 2D pixel-shuffling operation then reconstructs the dense LF with resolution . Finally, the interpolated LF is added to the reconstructed features, yielding the final output with improved angular fidelity.

(2) DFCBlock without cross-EPI

Excluding the cross-epipolar kernels reduces PSNR by 0.28 dB on Reflective, showing their role in enlarging the angular receptive field.

(3) DFCBlock without factorization

Replacing the factorized design with standard convolutions results in a 0.43 dB reduction on Reflective, demonstrating the efficiency of the factorized formulation.

(4) DFCBlock without SE

Discarding the SE block causes a 0.34 dB PSNR drop on Reflective, highlighting the benefit of channel recalibration.

(5) DFCBlock without spatial filtering

Removing the spatial convolutions decreases PSNR by 0.18 dB on Occlusions and 0.11 dB on Reflective, indicating their contribution to spatial refinement.

(6) Summary

Overall, each component contributes positively, with the EPI and cross-epipolar branches being the most critical. The full DFCBlock consistently outperforms its ablated variants, validating its effectiveness for high-quality LF reconstruction.

Depth estimation for angular consistency validation

Densely sampled, high-resolution LF images are critical for accurate depth estimation. However, pixel-wise metrics like PSNR and SSIM do not directly measure structural coherence across views. To explicitly validate the angular consistency and epipolar continuity claimed for our method, we adopt a task-driven evaluation: estimating depth maps from the reconstructed LFs using the Spinning Parallelogram Operator (SPO) 49. SPO is well-suited for this purpose as it directly measures EPI line-structure continuity, and higher depth accuracy implies better preservation of disparity-consistent angular coherence.

We evaluate on the reconstructed Buddha scene from the HCIold dataset 47, using the depth map estimated from the ground truth LF as a reference. Table 6 reports the Mean Squared Error (MSE) of the estimated depth maps. Our method achieves an MSE of 0.0049, outperforming Jin 17, FS-GAF 16, EASR 14, and ELFR 39, while tying with DistgASR 37 as the best-performing method. Figure 8 provides a visual comparison. Our depth map more faithfully preserves fine structures (e.g., the Buddha’s facial contours and the background railings) and produces sharper occlusion boundaries compared to other methods. These quantitative and qualitative results demonstrate that our directional factorized design successfully preserves EPI line structures and yields geometrically consistent angular super-resolution.

Limitations

While our method demonstrates strong performance overall, it encounters challenges on the HCInew dataset. As shown in Table 2, our approach achieves a PSNR of 38.03 dB and SSIM of 0.985 on HCInew, which is slightly lower than the top-performing methods. The HCInew scenes contain wider disparity ranges and lower texture contrast, making accurate reconstruction more difficult, as shown in Fig. 9 . In contrast, the HCIold dataset, with higher texture contrast and narrower disparities, allows our method to achieve the highest PSNR (43.80 dB) and SSIM (0.992). These results suggest that further improvements are needed to better handle low-texture, wide-disparity scenarios like those in HCInew.

Illustration of a challenging failure case on the HCInew (dishes) scene. The green boxes highlight regions where our method struggles. Specifically, the black text on the wall behind the dishes is clearly reconstructed by ELFR but appears blurry and less legible in our reconstruction. This failure is attributed to the wide disparity range and low texture contrast in the background region, which makes accurate angular correlation modeling difficult.

On the 30scenes, Occlusions, and Reflective test sets, our method consistently produces strong results, with PSNR/SSIM of 43.90/0.995, 40.28/0.993, and 39.90/0.982, respectively. While not always the absolute best on 30scenes, our approach reliably reconstructs high-frequency details and preserves angular consistency across diverse real-world and challenging synthetic scenes.

Additionally, while the proposed DFCB is highly effective for angular super-resolution, its applicability to other LF tasks is not universal. We conducted preliminary experiments applying DFCB to spatial super-resolution, where the goal is to increase the spatial resolution of each sub-aperture image independently. In this setting, the directional factorization-which explicitly separates horizontal and vertical epipolar lines-did not yield significant improvements over standard 2D convolution-based methods. We attribute this to the fundamental difference in data structure: angular SR benefits from the regular, structured grid of the angular domain where adjacent views are related via disparity, making directional kernels a natural fit. Spatial SR, in contrast, requires modeling isotropic correlations within a single view, where directional factorization offers less advantage. This observation highlights that specialized architectural designs are required for different LF enhancement tasks, and we leave the development of a unified framework for both angular and spatial SR as an interesting direction for future work.

Conclusion

We have presented DFCNet, a novel end-to-end framework for high-quality light field angular super-resolution. By leveraging the MacPI representation and hierarchical Residual-in-Residual Directionally Factorized Convolutional Blocks, the network effectively disentangles spatial, epipolar, and cross-epipolar correlations while maintaining parameter efficiency. The inclusion of channel recalibration via Squeeze-and-Excitation blocks and a hybrid interpolation–reconstruction strategy further enhances feature fusion and angular upsampling fidelity. Extensive experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets, including HCIold, HCInew, 30scenes, and STFlytro, demonstrate that DFCNet consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods in terms of average PSNR and SSIM, accurately reconstructing fine textures and occluded regions. While the model achieves strong performance across a wide range of scenarios, challenges remain in scenes with extremely low texture contrast or large disparity variations, such as in HCInew. Future work will explore adaptive dilation and multi-scale feature aggregation to further improve reconstruction accuracy in such challenging conditions.
