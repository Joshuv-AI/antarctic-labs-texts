Effects of physical exercise on the developmental trajectory of social anxiety in middle school students: a mixed growth model
Authors: Lingxia Zhang, Changliang Hang, Shipeng Ding, Jingtao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveBased on longitudinal data collected from Chinese middle school students across four time points, this study employed a mixed growth model to examine the impact of physical exercise on the developmental trajectory of social anxiety, explore trajectory heterogeneity, and identify the longitudinal protective mechanisms of physical exercise.MethodsThe Physical Exercise Rating Scale and the Social Anxiety Scale were administered in four longitudinal surveys conducted from September 2024 in Chengdu and Leshan (Sichuan Province) as well as Hangzhou and Lishui (Zhejiang Province), with a three-month interval between each wave. Single-growth and mixed growth models were constructed to examine class membership probabilities and predictive factors.Results(1) Significant trajectory heterogeneity in social anxiety was identified, with two distinct classes: a “high–slow decline” group and a “low–fast decline” group. The intercept and slope were negatively correlated. (2) Social anxiety and physical exercise showed a cross-sectional negative correlation (r ≈ − 0.30) and stability across time (PE r ≈ 0.32–0.46; SA r ≈ 0.29–0.33). (3) After controlling for gender, age, place of origin, and grade, physical exercise still significantly and negatively predicted the probability of following an adverse trajectory (β = − 0.396, P < 0.001).ConclusionSocial anxiety among middle school students shows a steady decreasing trend over time. Sustained participation in physical exercise was longitudinally associated with lower social anxiety and a lower likelihood of following an adverse social anxiety trajectory. It is recommended that schools implement tiered, contextualized, process-oriented, cooperative, and enjoyable sports programs to enhance accessibility and continuity of exercise, thereby improving unfavorable social anxiety trajectories.Keywords: Physical exercise, Social anxiety, Middle school students, Trajectory, Heterogeneity

Introduction

In recent years, China has successively introduced national strategies such as “Healthy China 2030” [1], “Myopia and Obesity Prevention and Control among Adolescents” [2], “Integration of Sports and Education” [3], and “Double Reduction” [4], aiming to improve the physical fitness and mental health of primary and secondary school students through policy interventions.

During the transition from home to middle school, adolescents’ social anxiety (SA) is not merely a collection of single emotional symptoms [5], but rather reflects interactions among multiple psychological processes [6], including threat perception bias, negative affect activation, emotion regulation imbalance, and deteriorated peer relationships. Developmental psychologists have suggested that in early-to-mid adolescence, asynchrony between neural and cognitive maturation and delayed executive control may contribute to stage-wise elevations in SA [7]. Moreover, the “rise-then-fall” pattern observed in emotional development may mask clear trajectory heterogeneity, with some individuals showing rumination, avoidance, and insecure behaviors [8, 9].

Physical exercise has been proposed as a potentially relevant factor for SA. Prior work suggests that exercise may relate to SA through several pathways, including (a) enhancing self-efficacy and competence-related experiences, thereby weakening catastrophic expectations of negative social evaluation [10]; (b) promoting social security and belonging through cooperative and supportive activities that facilitate peer interaction [11]; and (c) supporting behavioral rhythms (e.g., sleep and daily routines) that benefit executive function and emotion regulation [12]. Importantly, these benefits may be context-dependent: competitive or evaluative physical education climates may exacerbate self-threat and SA, whereas supportive climates may strengthen psychological benefits [13].

Scholars have noted that important gaps remain. Many studies lack in-depth longitudinal designs that capture changes in psychological processes, and evidence on dose characteristics (intensity, frequency, duration) remains mixed, with limited discussion of subgroup differences and situational design [14].

Accordingly, this study focuses on the core question of how physical exercise affects the developmental trajectory and heterogeneity of social anxiety among middle school students. Using a multi-wave follow-up design and mixed/growth mixture modeling, we identify latent trajectory classes of social anxiety and test whether baseline physical exercise predicts membership in an adverse trajectory after controlling for key covariates, with the aim of providing evidence relevant to school-based physical activity programs. The key innovations include the use of four-wave longitudinal data, explicit modeling of trajectory heterogeneity, and testing the predictive effect of physical exercise on adverse social anxiety trajectories.

Developmental characteristics and trends of social anxiety in middle school students

With developmental psychopathology and social ecology as the overarching framework, social anxiety is a product of the coupling of multiple ecological systems, including the individual level, microsystem level, and macro-institutional level. At the individual level, emotion regulation, temperamental characteristics, self-consciousness, and metacognitive concepts are key; at the microsystem level, peer relationships, class culture, teacher-student interactions, and classroom teaching modes are emphasized; at the macro-institutional level, school pressure, assessment systems, and cultural value orientations collectively affect individual emotional changes. Adolescents’ physical development and immature prefrontal lobe functions, combined with worsened peer relationships and intensified competition, lead to stronger threat perception and excessive focus on situations [15]. Under Chinese practices such as class streaming, school choice, and social comparison, social anxiety and avoidance behaviors may be triggered, and alertness and attention may be heightened [16]—this is a notable characteristic and overt behavior of middle school students during adolescence.

Longitudinal studies have found that physical exercise in early, middle, and late adolescence shows an average “rise-then-fall” trend over tim e [17]: a short-term increase during the seventh-grade entrance and social integration phase, followed by a slow decline due to academic burden and resource accumulation [18]. However, the average trend masks individual differences and characteristics. Latent growth curve models, latent class growth models, and mixed growth models all reveal more or less heterogeneous features, typically classifying adolescents into 2–4 classes [19].

Different intercepts represent different initial levels, while slopes and curvatures indicate subsequent developmental trends, reminding practitioners to attend to individual developmental trajectories. Some studies have found that students with high initial levels but negative slopes differ from those with moderate initial levels and positive slopes in terms of educational interventions and environmental support, showing distinct developmental pathways and expression mechanisms [20].

Demographic variables also differ, with gender and grade showing overall stability. Girls are more sensitive than boys to negative evaluations, internalizing behaviors, and rumination, and are more likely to engage in extreme behaviors. The seventh grade is a period of social adaptation and group integration upon school entry, whereas the ninth grade is a period of adaptation and peak academic burden, with higher likelihood of emotional disturbance [21]. In addition, pubertal timing, body image, and self-concept are closely related to physical exercise.

Studies have found that contextual and institutional factors can predict slope and curvature development [22]. Class culture, peer support, teacher-student interaction, classroom norms, and school physical education climate provide sufficient protection for individuals’ social efficacy and emotion regulation, buffering external stress [23]; conversely, a dull class atmosphere, excessively high evaluation standards, and excessive external pressure can reduce social efficacy and self-efficacy [24]. Moreover, digital media environments and school discipline are also closely related to social anxiety.

In terms of theoretical integration, developmental task and social evaluation theory reveal the mechanisms underlying emotional fluctuations in middle school students, from leaving home to entering a new environment, to social integration and cultural interaction, and subsequent social exchange—an ongoing process of integration and interaction. Among these, individuals with deficits in emotion regulation strategies and excessive attentional bias are more prone to persistent social anxiety and sustained avoidance [25], keeping their condition at a high level for an extended period. In other words, self-processing theories of emotion further explain the reinforcing role of rumination and negative self-schemas. Social ecological systems theory emphasizes the coupling of resources and risks; changes in school and family lifestyles can reduce risk exposure [26] and the accessibility of resources and environments, thereby affecting the potential probability of social anxiety risk [27].

Based on the above theories and scholarly reviews, this study proposes that middle school students’ social anxiety generally shows a slow declining trend, with clear class-level heterogeneity. Different classes exhibit different relationships with covariates, closely related to the school ecology and family resources. This suggests that mixture modeling is a primary method for investigating different latent classes, avoiding estimation bias from focusing solely on the mean. Accordingly, research hypothesis H1 is proposed:

H1: The developmental trajectory of social anxiety among middle school students is heterogeneous and can be characterized by at least two latent trajectory classes (i.e., distinct intercept–slope patterns) across the four measurement waves.

H1: The developmental trajectory of social anxiety among middle school students is heterogeneous and can be characterized by at least two latent trajectory classes (i.e., distinct intercept–slope patterns) across the four measurement waves.

Dose effects of physical exercise on social anxiety

Scholars argue that the improvement of social anxiety through physical exercise is essentially a long-term shaping and reshaping of psychological processes, and its effectiveness is highly dependent on dose, intensity, and context. Based on developmental psychology perspectives, self-determination theory, and related theoretical frameworks, the literature evidence is summarized as follows.

Arousal regulation and cognitive reappraisal are not simply “relaxation” effects but systematic down-regulation of the threat system. Longitudinal studies have found that regular moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical exercise significantly reduces baseline physiological arousal and situational tension, lowers catastrophic expectations regarding classroom speaking and peer observation, and reduces the linkage between state anxiety and somatic symptoms [27]. Scholars believe that this “de-catastrophizing” effect is more easily consolidated in safe, controllable contexts [28], and the resulting exercise benefits are then translated into stable cognitive reappraisal habits.

Self-efficacy and mastery experiences exhibit “dose sensitivity”. Studies indicate that segmented, progressively challenging exercise tasks continuously produce benefits, mainly attributable to the accumulation of daily micro-successes, gradually repairing negative emotions [29]. Compared with sporadic exercise, stable frequency and sustained duration yield greater transfer effects, such as self-belief and confidence, which can alleviate anxiety and avoidance in social situations [30]. At the same time, mastery experiences need to be controllable rather than confrontational.

Sense of security and belonging depend on the quality of social interaction. Cooperative and interactive role-based activities can improve individual organizational patterns, translating physical activity into stable social positive feedback and reducing contexts and expectations of comparison and judgment [31].

Conversely, humiliating error correction and one-way evaluative narratives can reinforce self-preoccupation, strengthening the anxiety maintenance chain (anticipatory anxiety – avoidance – short-term relief – long-term consolidation) in high-sensitivity individuals [32]. Therefore, “context dependence” is no longer merely an ancillary condition but a threshold variable determining effect and efficacy.

Attentional and regulatory optimization is an important bridge linking short-term “state improvement” and long-term trajectory change. Regular physical exercise and better sleep quality significantly enhance attentional flexibility and executive control, increase the efficacy of cognitive reappraisal, control ruminative expression, and help individuals acquire learned safety from controllable exposure situations [33].

Cross-wave time-series studies have also found that within 1–3 days after exercise, the frequency of cognitive reappraisal increases, rumination decreases, and subjective social stress is reduced, indicating short-term lagged benefits [34].

Dose-response and contextual thresholds are closely related. Most studies point to an optimal range of “moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) + sufficient weekly duration”, which is not linear. When intensity is too high or competitive support insufficient, marginal benefits diminish or even reverse. Moreover, teacher autonomy support, peer support, and growth-oriented evaluation can “shift the threshold rightward”, maximizing psychological benefits within the dose range. This suggests that subsequent research should focus not only on “doing more exercise” but also on “how to organize exercise”.

In summary, the psychological benefits of physical exercise on social anxiety are not linearly cumulative but exhibit threshold and amplification effects as dose and context vary, presenting an overall contextualized and curvilinear pattern. The key pathways involve moderate intensity and stable frequency of exercise, coupled with external autonomous support and cooperative interaction, continuously shaping the four psychological processes of “threat appraisal - self-efficacy -social security - regulation strategies”, thereby improving the overall developmental trajectory of social anxiety.

Based on this, research hypothesis H2 is proposed:

H2: Higher levels of physical exercise (baseline) are associated with a lower likelihood of membership in a high social anxiety or otherwise adverse developmental trajectory class (e.g., a high–slow decline trajectory), after controlling for demographic covariates.

H2: Higher levels of physical exercise (baseline) are associated with a lower likelihood of membership in a high social anxiety or otherwise adverse developmental trajectory class (e.g., a high–slow decline trajectory), after controlling for demographic covariates.

Research design

Data source and participant description

This study adopted a stratified cluster sampling method. From September 2024 onward, surveys were conducted in Leshan and Chengdu (Sichuan Province) as well as Hangzhou and Lishui (Zhejiang Province). In each city, two middle schools (one urban and one township) were selected, and two administrative classes from each grade level were chosen. To ensure data utility and account for potential attrition, 30 students per class were randomly sampled, yielding an initial sample of 960 students.

The three subsequent follow up surveys were conducted three months after each previous wave. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before each of the four survey waves, written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of the participating students.

Before questionnaire administration, the study purpose, procedures, voluntariness of participation, and confidentiality protections were explained to the students, and assent to participate was obtained from the students themselves. Participants were informed that they could withdraw at any time without any penalty. All data were collected anonymously to protect privacy and confidentiality. The inclusion criteria were: participants were middle school students aged 12–15 years, without mental disorders or maladaptive behaviors, physically able to participate in regular school physical activities. Exclusion criteria were: presence of severe mental disorders or emotional distress, and medical conditions affecting physical activity within the past six months. Attrition due to transfer, absence, or other reasons resulted in 46 and 63 students missing from the follow-ups, respectively. Specifically, the sample size at each wave was: T1 = 960, T2 = 914, T3 = 851, and T4 = 851. After data screening, the final valid analytic sample consisted of 851 participants with complete identification information and valid questionnaire responses at T4. Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1, and the actual sample far exceeded the predetermined requirement. To assess attrition bias, baseline differences between retained participants (those who completed T4) and attrited participants were tested on key variables (physical exercise and social anxiety) and demographic characteristics (e.g., gender, age, grade, and place of origin), and no significant differences were found (all P > 0.05), suggesting that attrition was unlikely to substantially bias the overall data structure.

Measures

Physical activity rating scale

The Physical Activity Rating Scale was used to assess middle school students’ physical activity participation. Participants recalled their physical activity over the past seven days [35]. A 5-point scale was used, covering exercise frequency, duration, and intensity, with 5 levels for each item. The total score was calculated as (exercise intensity) × (exercise frequency) × (exercise duration – 1). Internal consistency reliability of the Physical Activity Rating Scale was acceptable across the four waves (Cronbach’s α = 0.762, 0.753, 0.782, and 0.718, respectively), which were calculated separately at each wave.

Social anxiety scale

The Chinese version of the Social Anxiety Scale for Adolescents (SAS-A) was used [36, 37]. The scale contains 18 scored items rated on a 5-point scale (1 = never, 5 = always), all positively worded. It comprises three subscales: Fear of Negative Evaluation (FNE, 8 items), Social Avoidance and Distress-New Situations (SAD-New, 6 items), and Social Avoidance and Distress-General (SAD-Gen, 4 items).

Measurement invariance across the four waves was tested, and the fit indices (CFI = 0.901, TLI = 0.927, RMSEA, SRMR, ΔCFI, etc.) all met criteria. Cronbach’s α coefficients for the four time points were 0.942, 0.905, 0.953, and 0.962, respectively, indicating good reliability.

Control variables

Considering time-varying covariation between social anxiety and physical activity, time-invariant covariates including gender, age, only-child status, boarding mode, and family economic environment were included.

Data analysis

Data were integrated and analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.3. SPSS 26.0 was used for descriptive analysis, homogeneity of variance tests, Pearson correlation tests, and common method variance tests. Mplus 8.3 was used for measurement equivalence testing, latent growth model analysis, class indicators, class probabilities, and trajectory development analyses.

Discussion

Trends and characteristics of physical exercise and social anxiety in middle school students

Based on longitudinal data from four time points and a mixed growth model, this study found that social anxiety among middle school students generally showed a stable declining trend. Both physical exercise and social anxiety exhibited cross-time stability, with a significant negative correlation between them. Social anxiety followed two distinct developmental trajectories: a “high-slow decline” group and a “low-fast decline” group. Physical exercise significantly predicted social anxiety, which is generally consistent with findings from international scholars [38] and provides a new methodological perspective for domestic researchers in terms of trajectory heterogeneity and longitudinal research.

Regarding the overall developmental trends of physical exercise and social anxiety, the results showed that social anxiety decreased steadily over time (negative slope in the single-growth model), a finding consistent with longitudinal studies on adolescent development, where increased academic burden is accompanied by emotional instability and social anxiety symptoms in some adolescent s [39]. Domestic researchers have also reported that internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors in early adolescence are accompanied by social anxiety symptoms [40]. The results revealed that physical exercise and social anxiety across different time points showed moderate cross-time stability (r ≈ 0.29–0.33), indicating overall stability, which aligns with the view proposed by psychopathology researchers that “symptom stability and plasticity coexist” [41].

Furthermore, the cross-time stability between variables is consistent with conclusions from international studies [42]. Relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses have confirmed that physical activity among children and adolescents exhibits small-to-moderate negative correlations with anxiety, depression, and stress, and that regular physical exercise is particularly conducive to early physiological arousal, improved sleep, and executive function, all of which are crucial for ameliorating social anxiety [43]. Based on four-wave data collected over one year, our results suggest a pattern of associations in which higher physical exercise is linked to lower social anxiety over time; however, the observational design does not allow causal conclusions about delayed or cumulative effects. Integrating self-determination theory and social cognitive theory, long-term participation in regular physical activity can enhance individuals’ mastery experiences and self-efficacy [44]. In social situations such as classroom speaking, peer interaction, and other social contexts, individuals can achieve effective self-regulation, reduce avoidance and rumination, and develop positive psychological effects, consistent with the chain of “arousal regulation – cognitive reappraisal – mastery experience – emotion regulation optimization” documented in the literature [45].

From a developmental trajectory perspective, latent growth modeling provides important methodological insights for understanding individual heterogeneity. The results showed that the “high-slow decline” and “low-fast decline” groups of social anxiety had different proportions, and the intercept and slope were negatively correlated, indicating that individuals with higher initial social anxiety showed faster subsequent declines. This is similar to the “high-plateau-fall” pattern reported by other researchers [46], reflecting the combined effects of contextual adaptation, social support, and friendship quality. The “slow decline” in the high-anxiety group suggests that although individuals possess self-repair functions, the pace is relatively slow and may be “interrupted” during periods of high pressure such as examinations or evaluations; that is, stress can temporarily halt the declining trend. Research indicates that individuals with high social anxiety rely more on avoidance behaviors, reducing exposure and correction opportunities, which delays symptom improvement [47], consistent with our findings. This suggests that educators should adopt structured and strongly supportive environments, comprehensively evaluate the benefits of exercise contexts, and avoid the accumulation of negative effects from unilateral competition or humiliating feedback in physical education classes.

The results also revealed that baseline physical exercise significantly and negatively predicted the development of social anxiety, exhibiting a “starting-point buffering” effect. Compared with cross-sectional studies, this finding provides evidence for a protective antecedent effect [48], confirming that higher levels of physical exercise at an early stage are associated not only with lower concurrent social anxiety but also with subsequent favorable trajectory class membership.

International research has also shown that physical exercise in early adolescence predicts internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors in mid-to-late adolescence, remaining significant and independent even after controlling for demographic variables [49]. Within the Chinese middle school educational context, higher levels of physical exercise can exert two major pathway effects. First, exercise improves sleep and energy levels, enhances students’ real-time performance in the classroom context, and reduces the impact of peer micro-aggressions [50]. Second, cooperative and mastery-oriented sports activities help individuals rebuild self-confidence and a sense of belonging, mitigating excessive interference from negative emotions [51]. In short, this accumulation of “antecedent” high-quality psychological resources can help students adapt positively during the school entry phase, stabilize their internal emotions, and achieve moderate stability in subsequent life situations.

Demographic variables also differentially influenced the development of social anxiety and physical exercise behavior, with trajectory patterns showing variation. Gender, grade, and place of origin were significant predictors. Compared with previous studies, girls showed more discomfort and resistance in social situations and discussions involving sensitive topics, and their sports participation was more constrained by contextual factors and self-efficacy [52]. Additionally, urban-rural differences in the accessibility and convenience of sports resources, extracurricular participation, and classroom organizational climate all played indirect roles [53].

These results indicate that while physical exercise significantly and negatively predicts social anxiety, dose effects and contextual dependencies across different subgroups persist. Practical interventions and psychological treatment should emphasize context-stratified designs and tailored exercise prescriptions rather than a uniform “one-size-fits-all” approach.

It should be noted that although the results demonstrate the developmental trajectory and effect size of physical exercise on social anxiety, and observe a negative correlation and “starting-point buffering” effect, fluctuations exist across different time points. This is consistent with international longitudinal studies, where physical activity exhibits a non-linear negative association with early adolescent internalizing problem behaviors, moderated by contextual factors such as classroom climate, teacher style, and peer support. Moreover, this study did not include more complex time-varying mediators or interaction tests, precluding causal inference, which opens avenues for future research.

Latent growth trends of sustained physical exercise on social anxiety in middle school students

Using a mixed growth model, this study explored the developmental trends of sustained physical exercise participation on social anxiety at the latent variable level.

The results showed that at the overall level, social anxiety exhibited a stable declining trend, with two latent trajectory classes: a “high-slow decline” group and a “low-fast decline” group. High levels of physical exercise significantly reduced the worsening of social anxiety, exerting a longitudinal protective effect of “starting-point buffering.”

From the perspective of latent growth structure, the single-growth model revealed the overall developmental trend of social anxiety, with significant variance in intercept and slope indicating that the “average decline” masked individual differences. The mixed growth model identified two relatively stable latent trajectories: the “high-slow decline” group and the “low-fast decline” group. This is consistent with findings that adolescent social anxiety is an accumulation of internalizing problems, where most individuals gradually adapt to their environment through physical activity and interpersonal relationships, but a small subgroup shows only a slow decline [54]. Notably, the negative correlation between intercept and slope in the two classes means that individuals with higher initial symptoms decline faster subsequently, consistent with regression toward the mean [55], which may partly account for this pattern; therefore, the intercept–slope association should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence that higher initial social anxiety necessarily causes faster improvement.

Regarding cross-time stability and negative correlations revealed by longitudinal results, physical exercise and social anxiety showed cross-time stability across the four waves (PE r ≈ 0.32–0.46; SA r ≈ 0.29–0.33) and negative correlations (concurrent about − 0.30; most cross-lagged correlations between − 0.14 and − 0.29).

Sustained physical exercise participation significantly reduced social anxiety, confirming that physical exercise is not only associated with concurrent reductions in social anxiety but also has delayed effects; that is, physical exercise at an earlier time point reserves benefits for subsequent reductions in anxiety levels. These results are consistent with evidence from domestic and international studies on the effects of exercise on internalizing problem behaviors [56], showing that regular physical exercise improves sleep quality, reduces physiological arousal, enhances executive function, maintains emotional stability, and increases self-control and self-efficacy, with psychological benefits exhibiting a positive mechanism. In short, the negative slope in latent growth may be a direct manifestation of the effect of exercise on social anxiety.

Compared with existing findings and theories, the latent variable results of this study are externally consistent in direction and magnitude. International meta-analyses show that early adolescent physical activity is closely associated with internalizing problem behaviors such as anxiety, stress, and depression [57]; longitudinal studies have also confirmed that sustained physical activity participation is closely related to subsequent reductions in internalizing problems [58]. Using Chinese sample data and longitudinally examining four waves, this study provides new evidence from a longitudinal perspective. Theoretically, integrating self-determination theory and social cognitive theory, the study also validates that physical exercise participation can improve satisfaction with mastery, basic psychological needs, and self-efficacy for success, increase cognitive reappraisal, reduce emotional rumination, and improve emotion processing styles. These mechanisms together point to the educational benefit of physical exercise—namely, the “psychological shaping effect” of sustained exercise—which is a key driver of improvement in the latent variable slope (anxiety reduction) and dialectically explains why high baseline exercise reduces the risk of entering the unfavorable “high-slow decline” trajectory.

From methodological and outcome perspectives, the mixed growth model used in this study provides stronger evidence for the relationship between “sustained exercise” and “trajectory class membership.” Compared with previous baseline studies, latent growth models can distinguish different latent class trajectories [59] and precisely identify the psychological benefits of physical exercise. The results showed high classification accuracy and fit indices for the two classes, increasing confidence in the “starting-point buffering” conclusion regarding physical exercise. At the same time, considering residual marginal effects, future studies should incorporate time-varying covariates and mediators to address potential confounding factors and elucidate the roles of self-efficacy, cognitive reappraisal, rumination, and other variables.

In summary, from the perspective of school mental health management, the protective benefit of sustained physical exercise on mental health is increasingly important. Efforts should shift from occasional physical activities in physical education classes to regular and sustained physical activity participation. Specifically, first, the accessibility and continuity of physical exercise should be emphasized, avoiding disruptions caused by increased academic burden or scheduling conflicts to ensure overall benefits. Second, for adolescents in the “high-slow decline” group, while increasing appropriate exercise volume, careful attention should be paid to the suitability of exercise contexts, for example by prioritizing cooperative sports projects, low-competition or non-ranking activities, structured peer-support group activities, and consistent positive teacher feedback to reduce self-threat and promote engagement. Third, attention should be given to different student subgroups; for those with limited resources, low-threshold, enjoyable, and cooperative sports activities can be provided to gradually enhance the continuity of participation. Overall, we need to focus on contextualized, stratified, and process-oriented school-based physical activity interventions to continuously shift the slope of social anxiety in a more favorable direction.

Conclusions and recommendations

Conclusions

Based on longitudinal tracking across four time points, this study employed a mixed growth model to identify the developmental patterns and trends of social anxiety among middle school students. The results showed that: (1) There was significant trajectory heterogeneity between groups, mainly characterized by a “high-slow decline” group and a “low-fast decline” group. (2) Sustained participation in physical exercise was significantly negatively correlated with social anxiety and exerted a stabilizing effect over time, which remained robust across different demographic variables. (3) Higher levels of physical exercise significantly and negatively predicted the probability of belonging to the unfavorable trajectory (high-slow decline), demonstrating a “starting-point buffering” effect and cumulative lagged effects.

Both practice and research point to a pathway of “stratification-context-process,” suggesting that school-based physical activity should shift from being an “activity event” to a “developmental process.” Attention should be paid to adjusting and improving the environment, including academic burden and competitive pressure, and cooperative sports programs should be provided for the “high-slow decline” group to facilitate the translation of psychological benefits into trajectory improvement.

Recommendations

This study proposes the following recommendations: (1) At the school level, schools should arrange moderately controlled, enjoyable, cooperative, and sustained sports activities within the scope of students’ academic burden and ability, and consider tiered participation options (e.g., optional low-pressure sessions, beginner groups, and cooperative modules) to support students at higher risk, especially those in the high–slow decline trajectory. (2) At the class/PE lesson level, teachers and peers should foster a supportive sports climate by reducing hyper-competitive ranking, using cooperative sports projects (e.g., small-group skill tasks and team-based challenges), providing consistent positive teacher feedback, and organizing peer support/buddy groups to lower self-focus and improve perceived belonging. (3) At the family and community level, families and communities should establish “accessible-affordable-sustainable” sports participation systems (e.g., safe venues, affordable programs, and regular time slots), providing nurturing contexts to jointly safeguard adolescents’ physical and mental health.
