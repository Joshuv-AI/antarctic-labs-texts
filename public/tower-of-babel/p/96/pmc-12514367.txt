Effectiveness of perampanel as the only adjunctive anti‐seizure medication in adults: Final results from the observational PERPRISE study in Germany
Authors: Bernhard J. Steinhoff, Tobias Goldmann, Edgar Kockelmann, Yaroslav Winter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe PERPRISE study (Study 509; NCT04202159) was a prospective, observational, non‐interventional study in a real‐world setting in Germany. This study was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of perampanel as the only adjunctive treatment for 12 months in patients aged ≥18 years with focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (FBTCS) or generalized tonic–clonic seizures (GTCS) in clinical practice.MethodsAdult patients with FBTCS or GTCS received perampanel as an adjunctive therapy to anti‐seizure medication (ASM) monotherapy (add‐on therapy) or as a substitute for one ASM in dual therapy (substitution therapy) per the approved indication. The primary endpoint was the 12‐month retention rate of perampanel; the secondary endpoints were the 6‐month retention rate, seizure freedom for FBTCS or GTCS at 12 months, and safety and tolerability. Exploratory endpoints included efficacy assessments by seizure type at 6 and 12 months and patient‐reported outcomes.ResultsOf the 185 patients enrolled in the study, 183 patients were included in the full analysis set (add‐on, 86 patients; substitution, 96 patients; missing, 1 patient). The 12‐month retention rate was 66.7% (add‐on, 67.4%; substitution, 66.7%); the 6‐month retention rate was 80.3% (add‐on, 82.6%; substitution, 78.1%). At 12 months, the seizure‐freedom rate for FBTCS or GTCS was 42.3% (add‐on, 51.7%; substitution, 35.1%). Treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) occurred in 44.0% of patients; 6.0% of patients reported serious TEAEs, and 16.5% of patients withdrew from the study due to TEAEs. Treatment with perampanel did not adversely affect cognitive function in patients with FBTCS or GTCS, and improvements in quality of life were reported by patients at both 6 and 12 months following perampanel initiation.SignificanceFindings from the PERPRISE study suggest that perampanel as an only adjunctive therapy is associated with favorable retention rates and good tolerability in patients with FBTCS or GTCS in a real‐world clinical setting in Germany.Plain Language SummaryPatients with epilepsy often take multiple treatments to control their seizures. It is important to look at how well they work in everyday life. This study looked at adult patients in Germany taking perampanel added to one other epilepsy treatment. After 1 year, 58 of 137 patients suffering from the most severe type of seizure were free of them. Side effects occurred in 80 patients (most commonly dizziness, fatigue, and nausea) and caused 30 of them to withdraw from treatment. Perampanel was effective and did not seem to negatively affect patients' thinking ability or quality of life.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is one of the most common neurological disorders and is estimated to affect 50 million people globally. Many patients with epilepsy could live seizure‐free if they were properly diagnosed and treated. 1 Patients with epilepsy often experience cognitive impairment due to morphological and functional changes in the brain, 2 and an increase in the number of anti‐seizure medications (ASMs) in these patients is associated with higher degrees of cognitive impairment. 2 , 3 Additionally, ASM polytherapy has been shown to be associated with worse quality of life (QoL) in patients with epilepsy, regardless of seizure control. 4 This is likely due to a higher incidence of adverse effects with increased ASM drug load. 5 , 6

Perampanel, a first‐in‐class α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazole‐propionic acid (AMPA) receptor antagonist, is a once‐daily oral ASM for focal‐onset seizures (FOS), with or without focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (FBTCS), and for generalized tonic–clonic seizures (GTCS) with idiopathic generalized epilepsy (IGE). 7 , 8 , 9 It is approved in >70 countries and territories including the United States, Japan, and various countries in Europe and Asia. 7 , 8 , 10 , 11 In the European Union (EU), perampanel is approved for adjunctive treatment of FOS, with or without FBTCS, in patients aged ≥4 years and for GTCS in patients aged ≥7 years with IGE. 8 Data on perampanel use in clinical practice in Germany are limited due to a temporary absence of perampanel from the commercial market between 2013 and 2017. 12 Prior to 2013, perampanel use was mainly as a late‐line adjunctive therapy for patients with refractory focal epilepsy. 13 , 14 , 15

The PERPRISE study (PERampanel in patients with PRImary or SEcondarily generalized seizures; Study 509; NCT04202159) was a prospective, observational, non‐interventional study in a real‐world setting in Germany. This 12‐month study evaluated the effectiveness of perampanel as the only adjunctive treatment (add‐on therapy) in patients aged ≥18 years with FBTCS or GTCS in clinical practice, including patients who were switching to perampanel from another ASM (substitution therapy) as part of a dual ASM regimen. Both add‐on and substitution therapies are common prescribing practices in clinic and are suitable for different sets of patients. In the 6‐month interim analysis of the PERPRISE study, patients with FBTCS or GTCS showed overall favorable retention (>70%) and seizure‐freedom rates (>50%), with results varying by treatment population (add‐on or substitution therapy groups), timing of perampanel use (early vs. late use), and seizure type. In addition, tolerability was consistent with the known safety profile of perampanel. 16 Here, we report the 12‐month effectiveness data from the PERPRISE study to build on the findings from the interim analysis. In addition, we present data on the effect of adjunctive perampanel on cognitive function and QoL in this patient population.

METHODS

Study design and patients

The design of PERPRISE has been previously published. 16 Briefly, PERPRISE (Study 509; NCT04202159) was a prospective, observational, non‐interventional study of perampanel effectiveness in a real‐world setting in Germany. The planned Observation Period for this study was 12 months, with data collected at baseline and at approximately 6 and 12 months after the baseline visit, corresponding to the previous 3 months of each time point. In case of early discontinuation of treatment, the final visit was documented at the time of withdrawal from the study or perampanel discontinuation whenever possible. Adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of FBTCS or GTCS based on focal epilepsy or IGE who had ≥1 FBTCS or GTCS ≤3 months prior to enrollment and provided informed consent were included in this study. The decision to prescribe perampanel was made by the physician before and irrespective of their decision to include the patient in the study and in line with the current perampanel Summary of Product Characteristics. 8

Patients received perampanel as a newly administered adjunctive therapy to baseline ASM monotherapy (add‐on therapy) or as a substitute for one ASM of a baseline dual therapy (substitution therapy). The study was registered at the Bundesinstitut für Arzneimittel und Medizinprodukte (BfArM; NIS study number: 7363), Kassenärztlichen Bundesvereinigung (A70190), Spitzenverband Bund der Krankenkassen (10823), and Verband der Privaten Krankenversicherung e.V. PERPRISE was approved by the ethics committee of the Albert‐Ludwigs‐Universität Freiburg, Germany (approval number: 472/18).

Outcome measurements

The primary endpoint was retention rate at 12 months, with perampanel as the only add‐on treatment in adult patients with FBTCS or GTCS. 16 Secondary endpoints were retention rate at 6 months, seizure freedom for FBTCS or GTCS, and safety and tolerability during the study's observational period as defined above (i.e., at 12 months); seizure freedom for GTCS was assessed over the entire 12‐month observational period. Patients were considered seizure‐free for GTCS if they experienced no GTCS in any documented month from the start of perampanel treatment to their last visit. Conversely, patients were not considered seizure‐free if they experienced any GTCS at any point during the observational period. Seizure freedom and frequency data were collected over the 12‐month observational period following the initiation of perampanel treatment. Although the intended observation period was 365 days, data entry for some patients occurred after this timeframe, resulting in extended observation periods for retention rate calculations. Exploratory endpoints included median percent change in monthly seizure frequency, 50% responder rate, and the proportion of seizure‐free patients with FBTCS or GTCS combined, focal impaired awareness seizures (FIAS), FBTCS and GTCS separately, and all severe FOS (FBTCS and FIAS combined) at 6 and 12 months. Additional exploratory endpoints included evaluating the impact of perampanel on cognition at 6 months compared with baseline and change in QoL at 6 and 12 months.

Cognition was assessed using the age‐corrected score of the neuropsychological screening tool EpiTrack® 17 at 6 months. EpiTrack® is a short and repeatable paper‐pencil screening test of neurocognitive function to evaluate possible cognitive effects of epilepsy and ASM treatment in adult patients. 18 , 19 It includes five sub‐tests that are completed sequentially to assess the following cognitive domains: response inhibition, psychomotor speed and cognitive flexibility, anticipation, phonematic verbal fluency, and working memory. Higher individual EpiTrack® total scores indicate better cognitive performance; a score of ≥39 indicates an excellent, 32–38 an average, 29–31 a mildly impaired, and ≤28 a significantly impaired cognitive function. Related to individual performance changes as compared with baseline, a change of ≥4 points indicates an improved cognitive function whereas a change of −2 to 3 points indicates an unchanged function, and a change of ≤−3 points indicates a worsened cognitive function.

The impact of perampanel on patient‐reported QoL outcomes was assessed using the patient‐weighted QoL in epilepsy inventory‐10‐P (QOLIE‐10‐P) questionnaire at 6 and 12 months and referring to the previous 4 weeks. The QOLIE‐10‐P is an abbreviated form of the QOLIE‐31 questionnaire that screens different aspects of patients' health and daily activities. 20 , 21 QOLIE‐10‐P assesses the following components: energy, emotions, daily activities, mental function, medication effects, worry about seizures, and overall QoL. The final QOLIE‐10‐P questionnaire score ranges between 0 and 100, with higher scores representing higher QoL.

All study endpoints were assessed using the Full Analysis Set (FAS), which included all patients who provided informed consent and received ≥1 dose of perampanel. Seizure frequency data were collected at baseline, 6, and 12 months for the preceding 3 months, and seizure freedom was recorded for each month. Safety and tolerability were monitored throughout the study and all adverse events (AEs), including serious AEs, were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Only descriptive analyses were performed. All data were analyzed for the overall population and stratified by treatment group (add‐on or substitution therapy group). In accordance with the study protocol, percentages for categorical variables were calculated using the number of patients with valid (non‐missing) data per parameter, and the number of missing values was reported. Analyses of conditional endpoints were based on the number of patients who fulfilled the respective condition. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS® software (version 9.4 or higher [SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA]). PERPRISE was a non‐interventional study; thus, no formal sample size calculations were performed and no controls for confounding effects were conducted. Endpoints were additionally evaluated by perampanel use (early [one or two prior ASMs], intermediate [three or four prior ASMs], or late use [at least five prior ASMs]). For subgroup analyses, only side‐by‐side comparisons with no statistical analysis were performed.

RESULTS

Patients

In this 12‐month study, 185 patients were enrolled at different sites across Germany between January 21, 2020, and February 24, 2023 (Figure S1). The FAS included 183 patients (add‐on, 86 patients; substitution, 96 patients; with group assignment missing for one patient). Of the 185 patients enrolled, two were excluded from the FAS due to invalid consent. Overall, 66.1% (n = 121/183) of patients completed the study (add‐on, 65.1% [n = 56/86] of patients; substitution, 67.7% [n = 65/96] of patients; FAS). Among the 33.9% (n = 62/183) of patients who discontinued from the study, the most common reasons were AEs (36.7% [n = 22/60]), lost to follow‐up and patient wish (each 16.7% [n = 10/60]), and lack of efficacy (15% [n = 9/60]); data from two patients were missing.

The median (range) duration of the Observation Period in this study was 365.0 (1–540) days; the longer range of this period was a result of the lower‐than‐expected enrollment rate of the study due to the COVID‐19 pandemic leading to an extended study enrollment period. Furthermore, as a non‐interventional study, visit and documentation periods exhibited wide variability, potentially falling outside expected ranges. The median (range) patient age was 36.0 (18–103) years and 56.0% of patients were female (Table 1). The mean (standard deviation [SD]) duration of epilepsy was 16.7 (14.2) years (add‐on, 12.5 [11.9] years; substitution, 20.4 [15.1] years). The etiology of epilepsy was symptomatic in 46.2% (n = 84/182) of patients, idiopathic in 26.9% (n = 49/182) of patients, and unknown in 26.9% (n = 49/182) of patients. The mean (SD) body mass index for patients in the overall population was 25.7 (5.5) kg/m2. Overall, 21.4% (n = 39/182) of patients had psychiatric comorbidities (add‐on, 24.4% [n = 21/86]; substitution, 18.8% [n = 18/96]).

In the 3 months prior to perampanel treatment, 67.2% (n = 123/183) of patients experienced ≥1 FBTCS, 31.1% (n = 57/183) experienced ≥1 GTCS, and another two patients were enrolled despite no FBTCS or GTCS being recorded during the 3 months preceding the initiation of treatment with perampanel. Following standard practice for observational studies, and acknowledging the study's non‐interventional design, the two patients who did not fully meet the inclusion criteria were retained in the FAS to better reflect real‐world clinical practice. The most common concomitant ASMs were lamotrigine (41.0% [n = 75/183]) and levetiracetam (33.3% [n = 61/183]). Most patients (40.7% [n = 74/182]) received perampanel as an early treatment according to the definition given above, while 31.3% (n = 57/182) received it as an intermediate treatment, and 28.0% (n = 51/182) as a late treatment. Further, most patients who received perampanel as an early treatment were in the add‐on group (70.3% [n = 52/74]) compared with the substitution group (29.7% [n = 22/74]). In contrast, most patients who received perampanel as a late treatment were in the substitution group (74.5% [n = 38/51]) compared with the add‐on group (25.5% [n = 13/51]).

Treatment initiation, dosage, and duration

The most common reason for prescribing perampanel was lack of efficacy with prior ASMs (overall, 91.8% [n = 167/182]; add‐on, 86.0% [n = 74/86]; substitution, 96.9% [n = 93/96]). The mean (SD) duration of observed perampanel treatment was 370.6 (39.6) days.

Physicians were asked at baseline to state a planned target dose for perampanel, which was 4 mg/day (overall, 45.8% [n = 77/168]; add‐on, 51.3% [n = 41/80]; substitution, 40.9% [n = 36/88]) or 6 mg (overall, 36.3% [n = 61/168]; add‐on, 32.5% [n = 26/80]; substitution, 39.8% [n = 35/88]) in the majority of cases; consequently, the mean (SD) planned target dose of perampanel was 5.4 (1.9) mg/day (add‐on, 5.0 [1.6] mg/day; substitution, 5.7 [2.1] mg/day). The mean (SD) observed end dose of perampanel in the overall population was 5.9 (2.6) mg/day (add‐on, 5.4 [2.2] mg/day; substitution, 6.4 [2.9] mg/day).

Figure S2 shows the planned and actual number of titration steps for the overall population. Overall, 42.5% (n = 76/179) of patients had the planned number of titration steps, 5.6% of patients (n = 10/179) required fewer steps, and 52.0% of patients (n = 93/179) required more steps than planned. The mean (SD) duration of the Titration Phase was 17.7 (16.1) weeks. Titration took longer than planned in 64.9% (n = 96/148) of patients, took less time than planned in 11.5% (n = 17/148), and proceeded as planned in 23.6% (n = 35/148) of the overall population.

Effectiveness

Overall, perampanel retention rate at 12 months was 66.7% in patients with FBTCS or GTCS (Figure 1A; time to discontinuation of perampanel treatment in the add‐on and substitution groups is presented in Figure S3), with 84.4% of patients remaining on dual therapy. At 6 months, the retention rate was 80.3% in patients with FBTCS or GTCS in the overall population and was slightly higher for the add‐on therapy group compared with the substitution group (82.6% vs. 78.1%). However, there were no substantial differences in retention rates among therapy groups.

A subgroup analysis stratified by early, intermediate, and late use indicated that retention rates at 12 months were higher in patients who received perampanel as an early or intermediate therapy compared with late therapy (Figure 1B). The retention rate at 6 months was lower in patients who received perampanel as late therapy (64.7%) than in those who received it as early or intermediate therapy (87.8% and 84.2%, respectively).

Seizure outcomes

Perampanel was associated with improvements in seizure control at 12 months in patients with FBTCS or GTCS. The effect of perampanel on seizure control by different seizure types was also investigated. For the overall population, seizure‐freedom rates at 12 months for FBTCS or GTCS, FBTCS only, or GTCS only were 42.2%–43.8%, which were higher than the rates for FOS or FIAS (27.2% and 34.7%, respectively [Figure 2A]). In addition, patients in the add‐on group showed higher seizure‐freedom rates than those in the substitution group, regardless of seizure type (add‐on: 35.4–52.4% vs. substitution: 19.2–38.5% [Figure 2A]).

When stratified by timing of perampanel use, seizure‐freedom rates at 12 months were numerically higher for patients with early perampanel use (30.0%–64.3%) compared with seizure‐freedom rates for patients with late perampanel use (13.8%–50.0%) across all seizure types except GTCS, where the rates were 33.3% for patients with early perampanel use vs. 50.0% for those with late perampanel use (Figure 3A). For the overall population, seizure‐freedom rates at 6 months for patients with FBTCS or GTCS in the FAS ranged between 39.4% and 54.8% depending on seizure type, and the 50% responder rate at 6 months ranged between 74.5% and 79.6%. Positive changes in cognitive function were more pronounced in the substitution group, with a mean (SD) change in score at 6 months from baseline of 5.7 (4.5) points. Patients in the add‐on group had a mean (SD) change in score at 6 months from baseline of 1.6 (5.0) points.

At 12 months, an event‐based analysis of TEAEs showed that the majority of TEAEs were mild (n = 94 events, 56.0%) or moderate (n = 55, 32.7%) in severity. In total, 104 (61.9%) TEAEs were considered related to perampanel. The most common TEAEs (≥2% of patients) that led to perampanel withdrawal in the overall population were dizziness (4.4% [n = 8/182]), nausea (3.8% [n = 7/182]), fatigue (2.7% [n = 5/182]), gait disturbance (2.7% [n = 5/182]), and aggression (2.2% [n = 4/182]). Serious TEAEs were reported in 6.0% (n = 11/182) of patients in the overall population. The most common serious TEAEs (occuring in ≥1% of patients overall) were aggression (1.1% [n = 2/182]) and Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19; 1.1% [n = 2/182]). Two deaths as a result of COVID‐19 infection were reported in the add‐on patient group and were considered not related to study treatment (FAS). Psychiatric TEAEs occurred in 13.2% (n = 24/182) of patients in the overall population; of these patients, 12.5% (n = 3/24) had a prior history of psychiatric conditions. The most common psychiatric TEAEs (occurring in ≥2% patients overall) reported in this study were irritability (4.4% [n = 8/182]) and aggression (2.7% [n = 5/182]).

DISCUSSION

The final results from the PERPRISE study showed that perampanel, as an only adjunctive therapy, was effective in reducing seizure frequency and was well tolerated in adult patients with FBTCS or GTCS. Overall, 66.7% of patients remained on perampanel at 12 months, with 84.4% of them using one additional concomitant ASM. It is possible that due to the pharmacokinetics of perampanel and the slow titration schedule, the 12‐month study duration was too short to consider further discontinuation of concomitant ASMs. The effectiveness of adjunctive perampanel observed in the PERPRISE study is in line with that observed in other real‐world studies of perampanel, which found 12‐month retention rates ranging from 48.3% to 90.5%, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 including studies that investigated the use of perampanel as an only add‐on treatment. 22 , 23 , 25 , 26 Additionally, patients in the early and intermediate use groups had higher retention rates at 12 months than patients in the late use group. Higher retention rates have been previously observed with early versus later use of perampanel. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Similar findings have been reported in other real‐world studies using other ASMs such as brivaracetam 28 and eslicarbazepine acetate. 29 , 30

Changes in seizure outcomes at 12 months in PERPRISE varied by patient therapy type and by timing of perampanel use (early vs. late use). Patients in the add‐on group tended to have higher seizure freedom and 50% responder rates at 12 months across different seizure types compared with patients in the substitution group. However, both add‐on and substitution therapy groups showed similar improvements; thus, the use of perampanel as adjunctive treatment in both clinical settings could be beneficial. The mean (SD) end dose of perampanel in the add‐on group was lower than in the substitution group (5.4 [2.2] mg/day vs. 6.4 [2.9] mg/day, respectively). Patients in the substitution group discontinued more prior ASMs and had a longer duration of epilepsy than patients in the add‐on group; both of these factors suggest that patients in the substitution group may have had a higher degree of drug resistance. 16 This proposition is supported by the findings of the subgroup analysis stratified by early, intermediate, and late use of perampanel. This subgroup analysis showed higher retention and better seizure outcomes for patients with early and intermediate use of perampanel compared with patients with late use, and there were fewer patients who had late use of perampanel in the add‐on group than in the substitution group.

The tolerability of perampanel treatment in PERPRISE was consistent with the known tolerability profile of perampanel 8 and that reported in other real‐world studies. 25 , 27 , 31 , 32 , 33 Overall, dizziness was the most common TEAE. Fatigue was the second most common TEAE in the add‐on group, whereas in the substitution group, it was irritability. Incidences of TEAEs were higher in patients in the add‐on group compared with the substitution group (50.0% vs. 38.5%), despite lower perampanel doses. This observation could possibly be related to the increased drug load in the add‐on group; however, the incidence of withdrawal due to TEAEs was similar between the two groups (16.3% and 16.7%, respectively). Moreover, despite experiencing more TEAEs, patients in the add‐on group may have been more likely to continue the treatment due to experiencing observable improvements in terms of seizure control.

EpiTrack® results demonstrated that adjunctive perampanel did not negatively affect cognitive function in patients with FBTCS or GTCS. These results support the findings from previous studies of the cognitive effects of perampanel in adult patients 9 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 ; most studies showed that perampanel appeared to be neutral with regard to cognitive function. Interestingly, the positive change versus baseline on a group level appeared more pronounced in the substitution group, possibly due to the replacement of ASMs or ASM combinations that had a less favorable cognitive tolerability. Patient‐reported changes in QoL from baseline with adjunctive perampanel treatment during PERPRISE were positive, regardless of the therapy group. These findings were consistent with other real‐world studies of the use of perampanel as adjunctive therapy in adult patients, which showed that perampanel did not result in deterioration of patient QoL 9 , 35 , 39 , 40 or led to improvement. 41 , 42 , 43 It is also possible that the improvements in patient QoL in the add‐on group could have contributed to the high retention rate for this group despite experiencing a higher incidence of TEAEs than patients in the substitution group.

There were some observed differences in the retention rates of perampanel at 6 months and those at 12 months. The retention rates for the add‐on and substitution groups at 12 months were similar, whereas at 6 months, a higher retention rate in the add‐on group was noted. It is likely that the differences in retention rates at 6 months were due to AEs in the add‐on group and due to less efficacy in the substitution group; however, at 12 months, tolerability issues were not as common anymore. Interestingly, a similar trend of a higher retention rate in the add‐on group was observed during the interim analysis conducted 6 months after study initiation 16 suggesting that the interim analysis data set was robust. Seizure‐freedom and 50% responder rates for patients in the overall population with FBTCS or GTCS at 6 months were numerically lower than those observed in the interim analysis (52.6% and 77.9% vs. 58.8% and 82.6%, respectively) whereas median percent reduction in seizure frequency for these patients was the same. The incidences of TEAEs and serious TEAEs for the overall patient population and those in the add‐on and substitution groups were comparable between the interim 16 and final analysis, with the most common TEAEs being dizziness and fatigue.

LIMITATIONS AND CLINICAL RELEVANCE

Limitations include a lack of a control group and blinding due to the real‐world nature of this study. However, the real‐world observational design of the PERPRISE study may better reflect routine clinical care. Another limitation is that the current study did not include formal sample size calculations and only descriptive analyses. While this approach is common in observational studies, it might limit the generalizability of the findings. 44 , 45 With regard to patient‐reported outcomes using the EpiTrack® and the QOLIE‐10‐P, there is a possibility that response biases, such as recall bias or extreme response style, may have impacted patient responses, as well as the possibility that the used measures may not have captured changes in all relevant cognitive domains 18 , 46 ; yet, cognitive screening tools and self‐reported questionnaires are easy to integrate into routine clinical practice, provide valuable input, and may allow for patients' information to be collected with less bias by clinicians on what symptoms they consider more clinically relevant. 47 Additionally, cognitive and QoL parameters were evaluated in patients who remained on perampanel treatment for at least 6 months in this observational study; consequently, these results may be subject to retention bias as they do not represent the effects in patients who discontinued due to lack of efficacy or due to tolerability issues. Due to small patient numbers, further analysis to investigate favorable combinations of perampanel and concomitant ASMs in terms of improved efficacy and tolerability was not conducted.

To the best of our knowledge, PERPRISE is the first study to look specifically at patients who are replacing one ASM with perampanel in an existing dual therapy and will help inform decision‐making in this patient cohort in clinical practice.

CONCLUSION

Overall, results from this study showed that perampanel, as an only adjunctive therapy (add‐on or substitution ASM), had a favorable retention rate (with 2 out of 3 patients remaining on perampanel) at 12 months and was well tolerated in adult patients with epilepsy during routine clinical care in Germany. Moreover, treatment with adjunctive perampanel did not negatively affect patients' cognitive function and QoL. These findings demonstrate the benefits of using perampanel as an add‐on ASM in patients with insufficiently controlled FBTCS or GTCS on current monotherapy or as a substitute for one ASM in a dual therapy.
