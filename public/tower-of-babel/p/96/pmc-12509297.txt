Temporal trends and machine learning prediction for chronic kidney disease due to hypertension in young adults from 1990 to 2021: an analysis of the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Yiqun Miao, Yuan Luo, Yuhan Zhao, Huiying Wang, Ying Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYoung adults aged 25–49, at the peak of work and family responsibilities, face serious health and productivity challenges when affected by chronic kidney disease (CKD) due to hypertension.MethodsUsing Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 1990-2021 data, we assessed global, regional, and national CKD burden due to hypertension in young adults, focusing on mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). Trends analyzed by Joinpoint regression and forecasted to 2040 using eight machine learning models.ResultsIn 2021, global mortality from CKD due to hypertension reached 18,675.24 cases, a 157.55% increase since 1990. The age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) rose from 0.31 to 0.45 per 100,000 persons, with an average annual percentage change (AAPC) of 1.24% (p < 0.05). DALYs increased by 145.84% to 1,122,316.38 person-years. The age-standardized disability rate (ASDR) grew from 19.39 to 27.37 per 100,000 persons, with an AAPC of 1.12% (p < 0.05). The greatest increase occurred in the 25-29 age group, while those aged 45-49 had the highest burden. Most of the CKD burden due to hypertension was in the three lowest SDI quintiles, while high SDI regions saw the greatest increase. The Prophet model was optimal in predicting the ASMR index, while the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model performed best for the ASDR, with projections indicating an increase in both indices by 2040.ConclusionsCKD due to hypertension is a growing global challenge in young adults. Substantial heterogeneity exists in the disease burden and its temporal trends across countries and regions, highlighting the strong distributive inequities of CKD worldwide.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has become a critical global public health challenge, impacting about 10% of adults and causing 1.2 million deaths along with 28 million years of life lost annually [1]. Hypertension is a well-recognized leading cause of CKD [2,3]. Uncontrolled hypertension can progress to CKD through sustained hemodynamic changes, resulting in renal vascular damage and progressive decline in kidney function [4–6]. However, this process often spans a long period and is typically asymptomatic in its early stages, making early detection and intervention challenging [7]. Therefore, understanding the burden of CKD due to hypertension, particularly its temporal trends, is essential for evaluating its long-term impact and formulating effective prevention and management strategies.

While CKD due to hypertension predominantly affects older adults, its detrimental effects often start accumulating much earlier in life [8]. Hypertension is common among young people, affecting one in eight adults aged between 25 and 45 years, with its prevalence steadily increasing in recent years [9]. This trend indicates that a growing number of young people are at risk of experiencing kidney damage at an earlier stage [10]. Therefore, focusing on young adults is crucial for understanding the early dynamics of disease progression and the cumulative risks over time. Furthermore, this age group represents individuals in their peak years of economic and social productivity, and CKD progression during this period can lead to substantial consequences [11]. As key drivers of economic growth and development, any reduction in their workforce participation strains healthcare systems and hampers national productivity. Thus, CKD due to hypertension in young adults represents not only an urgent public health concern but also a significant challenge to socioeconomic sustainability.

However, global evidence on the burden of CKD due to hypertension among young people is limited. Therefore, we analyzed the temporal trends of CKD due to hypertension among young adults aged 25–49 years across 204 countries/territories from 1990 to 2021, categorized by age, sex, region, country, and socio-demographic index (SDI), using data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study. This study emphasizes identifying key turning points in changes over the past 30 years and the public health challenges expected by 2040, providing a timeline to support policymakers in optimizing global health resource allocation. Understanding the epidemiological patterns and the burden of CKD due to hypertension in young adults is crucial for informing targeted prevention strategies, early detection programs, and effective resource allocation.

Methods

Data acquisition

This research used data from the GBD 2021, accessed via the GBD Results Tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). The GBD database provides an extensive and current evaluation of the impacts of 371 diseases and injuries at global, regional, and national levels. For 204 countries and regions, the GBD 2021 provided estimates, grouped into 21 regions [12]. CKD due to hypertension was categorized using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD-10), specifically under codes I12–I13.9. The data on CKD due to hypertension are collected through multiple high-quality sources, including End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) registry data, which track the distribution of causes such as hypertension, and electronic health records, which identify CKD cases attributed to hypertension using ICD-10 codes. For detailed data collection methods, refer to the GBD technical appendix (https://www.healthdata.org/gbd/methods-appendices-2021/chronic-kidney-disease-0). We gathered data on annual mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for CKD due to hypertension from 1990 to 2021. The GBD database integrates comprehensive statistical approaches to enhance data quality and ensure precise and consistent estimations (Supplementary Material 1). The bias of non-reference data is corrected using Bayesian priors, regularization, and pruning with meta-regression (MR-BRT), which applies a logarithmic transformation method. Disease epidemiology analysis is conducted using the Bayesian model-based meta-regression tool DisMod-MR [13].

In addition, GBD 2021 calculated the SDI for each nation, reflecting the social and economic factors that influence health outcomes in particular regions. The SDI is calculated using the geometric mean of three scaled factors: fertility rate among those under 25, average education level for individuals aged 15 and above, and income per capita adjusted for time lag. A value of 0 represents the lowest level of socio-economic development, characterized by high fertility, low income, and limited education. The SDI is classified into five levels: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high [14]. Additionally, the University of Washington’s Institutional Review Board granted a waiver for informed consent to utilize the GBD dataset [15]. The study followed the GATHER guidelines to ensure transparent and precise reporting of health estimates [16].

Statistics analysis

The burden of CKD due to hypertension was evaluated using two measures: the age-standardized rates and the absolute numbers of DALYs and deaths. The age-standardized DALYs rate (ASDR) is expressed as person-years per 100,000 persons, while the total number of DALYs reflects the overall disease burden in person-years. Age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) is expressed as deaths per 100,000 persons, with the total number of deaths reflecting the absolute disease burden in terms of cases. Both metrics come with associated 95% uncertainty intervals (UI). The standardized indicators enable comparisons across various age categories and help reduce the influence of age distribution differences. Specifically, young adults were divided into five distinct age groups: 25–29 years, 30–34 years, 35–39 years, 40–44 years, and 45–49 years. The direct standardization approach was employed to age-standardize the rates, utilizing the ageadjust.direct function from the epitools package [17].

To analyze the key turning points in the trends of CKD due to hypertension from 1990 to 2021, we utilized Joinpoint regression analysis to identify shifts in the disease burden [18]. The final model calculated the annual percentage change (APC), average annual percentage change (AAPC), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for both global and SDI regions, quantifying the trend shifts from 1990 to 2021. The Age–Period–Cohort model was employed to examine trends by age groups, time periods, and birth cohorts. Unlike traditional methods that fail to address collinearity among variables, this approach utilizes the Intrinsic Estimator (IE) to evaluate the effects of age, period, and cohort independently [19,20]. To assess the link between the burden of CKD and sociodemographic progress, we used frontier analysis as a quantitative approach to determine the minimum achievable age-standardized DALY and mortality rate based on development status, measured by the SDI. All analyses and visualizations were performed with R Software (version 4.3.2).

A variety of machine learning (ML) algorithms are applicable to time series prediction tasks. By incorporating historical data (lagged) as input, the model can capture temporal patterns in the data, thereby enhancing the accuracy of predictions. To forecast the future trends from 2022 to 2040 of ASDR and ASMR for CKD due to hypertension, we trained eight time-series ML models using data from 1990 to 2021. The dataset was initially split into an 80% training set and a 20% test set. The eight machine learning algorithms included the following: Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA), Prophet, Random Forest, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), AdaBoost, Decision Tree, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Bayesian Ridge Regression.

Results

Global level

Our study reveals the trends in the burden of CKD due to hypertension among young adults. To investigate these trends, we analyzed the status of the disease burden from 1990 to 2021. As shown in Table 1, in 2021, the global burden of CKD due to hypertension among young adults remained high, with a total of 18,675.24 death cases (95% UI: 8491.54–32134.06), representing a staggering 157.55% increase from 1990. The ASMR for CKD due to hypertension rose slightly from 0.31 (95% UI: 0.14–0.54) per 100,000 persons in 1990 to 0.45 (95% UI: 0.21–0.78) in 2021, with an AAPC of 1.24% (95% CI: 1.16–1.31, p < 0.05), indicating an overall increase. The global DALYs of CKD due to hypertension reached 1,122,316.38 person-years (95% UI: 543,261.00–1,815,048.20) in 2021, marking a 145.84% increase from 1990. The ASDR for CKD due to hypertension increased from 19.39 per 100,000 persons (95% UI: 9.26–31.72) in 1990 to 27.37 (95% UI: 13.25–44.27) in 2021, with an AAPC of 1.12% (95% CI: 1.06–1.18, p < 0.05), indicating a steady increase in young adults.

The ASDR for CKD due to hypertension showed an upward trend across the age groups from 25 to 49 years (Table 2; SFigures 1 and 2). The 45–49 age group had the highest ASDR, increasing from 63.22 per 100,000 persons (95% UI: 30.62–102.09) in 1990 to 84.73 per 100,000 persons (95% UI: 41.69–135.77) in 2021. Notably, although the 25–29 age group had the lowest burden, it experienced the fastest growth in disease burden over the past 32 years, with an AAPC of 1.42 (95% CI: 1.30–1.53, p < 0.05). A similar trend was observed in the ASMR, where the AAPC for young adults aged 25–29 was 1.63 (95% CI: 1.52–1.78, p < 0.05).

Regional level

The global burden of CKD due to hypertension in young adults shows considerable differences across regions, strongly associated with SDI values. Low SDI regions exhibited the highest ASMR at 0.65 per 100,000 persons (95% UI: 0.29–1.15), while high-middle SDI regions had the lowest at 0.18 per 100,000 persons (95% UI: 0.08–0.33) (Table 1; SFigures 3 and 4). As shown in Figure 2, high SDI regions showed the largest ASMR increase, with an AAPC of 2.77 (95% CI: 1.87–3.68, p < 0.05) The ASDR further underscored these regional disparities. Specifically, the ASDR was lowest in high-middle SDI regions (12.51 per 100,000 persons, 95% UI: 6.37–20.86) and highest in low-middle SDI regions (38.37 per 100,000 persons, 95% UI: 18.26–62.54). High SDI regions showed the largest increase (AAPC: 1.72, 95% CI: 1.09–2.34, p < 0.05), while low SDI regions had the smallest increase (AAPC: 0.36, 95% CI: 0.23–0.49, p < 0.05). Our analysis reveals that among the 21 GBD regions, the mortality of CKD due to hypertension in African regions warrants significant attention. Geographically, Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, the Caribbean and Western Sub-Saharan Africa are among the regions with the highest ASMR levels globally (SFigures 6 and 7). By contrast, Australasia and Western Europe demonstrated significantly lower ASMRs than these regions. Additionally, the trends over time from 1990 to 2021 show varying patterns across different regions. The findings show that the ASMR increased in 12 of the 21 geographical regions, with the highest increase observed in high-income North America (AAPC: 4.42, 95% CI: 2.86–6.01, p < 0.05), followed by Central Latin America (AAPC: 2.18, 95% CI: 1.54–2.82, p < 0.05) (Table 1; SFigures 8 and 9). By contrast, the ASMR in the high-income Asia Pacific, Central Europe, and Western Europe showed a downward trend.

In parallel, Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, Western Sub-Saharan Africa, and the Caribbean have some of the highest ASDR levels globally, while Australasia and High-income Asia Pacific report the lowest. Among the 21 regions, ASDR rose in 13, with the most significant increase observed in high-income North America (AAPC: 3.32, 95% CI: 2.20–4.46, p < 0.05), followed by Oceania (AAPC: 2.08, 95% CI: 1.93–2.22, p < 0.05). By contrast, the ASDR in High-income Asia Pacific, Central Europe, and Tropical Latin America showed a declining trend. We analyzed the link between SDI and ASR across 21 regions, finding all ASR values significantly and negatively correlated with SDI (correlation coefficient ASDR = –0.53, ASMR = –0.55, all p values <0.001) (SFigure 10).

Temporal trend

Joinpoint regression was utilized to identify specific time periods where significant changes in trends occurred. Figure 4A illustrates the Joinpoint regression analysis of ASDR for CKD due to hypertension among young adults globally and across SDI regions from 1990 to 2021. Despite fluctuations in growth rates, the global ASDR showed a significant and persistent upward trend from 1990 to 2021 (AAPC =1.12, 95% CI: 1.06–1.18, p < 0.05). There was a moderate increase from 1990 to 1996 (APC = 0.58), the fastest increase from 1996 to 2004 (APC = 1.83), and another rapid increase from 2011 to 2021 (APC = 1.11). The overall trend depicted can be characterized as a "stepwise growth" pattern. All SDI regions demonstrated an increasing trend except for the high-middle SDI regions. The ASDR in high-middle and high SDI regions was below the global average, while that in middle, low-middle, and low SDI regions exceeded it. However, significant differences in growth rates were observed during specific time periods. Specifically, the ASDR for CKD due to hypertension showed the fastest increase in high SDI regions (AAPC = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.09–2.36, p < 0.05). This could be attributed to the rapid growth from 2016 to 2021 (APC = 6.36, p < 0.05). After 2016, the growth rate slowed (APC = 1.46, p < 0.05). The ASDR for high-middle SDI regions exhibited a stable trend (AAPC = 0.13, 95%CI: −0.16–0.42, p = 0.365) from 1990 to 2021, and a decreasing trend during 2005 to 2021 (APC = −0.43, p < 0.05). Low-middle SDI regions showed a growing disease burden, with the most significant increase occurring from 1994 to 2003 (APC = 2.20, p < 0.05).

The age, period, and cohort effects

Age–period–cohort analyses were conducted to disentangle the contributions of different age groups, time periods, and birth cohorts to these trends. As shown in Figure 5A and 5B, after adjusting for period and cohort effects, the age effect on CKD due to hypertension showed a continuous increase in both DALYs and mortality risk from 25 to 49 years. These results underscore the progressive nature of the disease burden with age. The risk of DALYs increased by 2.32 times, and the risk of mortality by 2.94 times from the 35–39 to the 45–49 age group. Although the trends were similar for both sexes, males showed slightly higher relative risks than females in all age groups. The period effect demonstrated a moderate upward trend in DALYs and mortality risks from 1992 to 2012, with DALYs and mortality risks increasing by 1.51 and 1.62 times, respectively. The cohort effect showed a continuous decrease in DALYs and mortality from earlier to later birth cohorts. From the 1947–1951 to the 1992–1996 birth cohorts, the DALYs and mortality risks for CKD due to hypertension significantly decreased by 18.60% and 23.90%, respectively. The trends in period and cohort effects were similar for both males and females (STables 2–4).

Future forecasts

In the results of the time-series machine learning models, the optimal performance of the eight algorithms varied across different evaluation metrics, namely ASDR and ASMR. Based on comparative evaluation metrics, the ARIMA model performed best in predicting the global ASDR of CKD due to hypertension. The ARIMA model predicted that the ASDR would continue to rise from 2022 to 2040, indicating a likely sustained increase over the next 20 years (Figure 6). Specifically, the forecasted ASDR for 2040 is 45.50 per 100,000 persons in the total population (95% CI: 41.25–49.75). When stratified by sex, the projected ASDR is 58.20 per 100,000 among males (95% CI: 53.80–62.60) and 32.76 per 100,000 among females (95% CI: 28.30–37.23).

For ASDR, the effective difference decreases with increasing SDI. These countries have disproportionately higher DALY rates for CKD due to hypertension. Their rates are much higher than those of nations with similar sociodemographic characteristics. In contrast, the 10 countries and territories with the smallest gaps between observed and optimal ASDR (range: 0.32–2.78) include Australia, Finland, France, Iceland, Japan, Republic of Korea, Singapore, Slovenia, Sweden, and Switzerland (Figure 8A; STable 7).

Discussion

The globally increasing burden of CKD due to hypertension is progressively affecting younger populations. Given that young adults constitute the primary workforce in society, understanding their disease burden is crucial to reducing the overall global burden of CKD. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to comprehensively analyze the global, regional, and national burden and trends of CKD due to hypertension from 1990 to 2021, uncovering several important insights. First, it confirms a continuous increase in the ASMR and ASDR of CKD due to hypertension globally among young adults, with consistently higher rates observed in males compared to females. The highest burden was found in the 45–49 age group, while the 25–29 age group showed the fastest growth rate. Second, the burden of CKD was disproportionately higher in underdeveloped economies, whereas more developed regions exhibited the highest growth rates. Importantly, this study highlights pivotal years when significant changes in disease metrics took place for the first time. Third, the age effect exhibited an increasing trend with advancing age, while an unfavorable period effect and a favorable cohort effect were observed globally. Fourth, the 8 time-series machine learning algorithms indicated that mortality and DALYs for CKD due to hypertension are projected to continue rising by 2040, imposing a greater social burden globally. Fifth, our frontier analysis showed that some countries, despite being at the lower end of the development scale, demonstrated exceptional success in reducing DALYs and mortality rates.

This analysis of the global burden of CKD due to hypertension in young adults from 1990 to 2021 highlights a complex pattern of disparities and trends across different regions and socio-demographic factors. Globally, the burden of CKD due to hypertension has been increasing, which is likely associated with inadequate hypertension management driven by multiple contributing factors [21,22]. Specifically, sub-Saharan Africa and the Caribbean are regions with the highest ASDR and ASMR compared to other global regions. This is partly attributable to limited healthcare resources, which hinder effective hypertension screening and early intervention, resulting in poorly controlled blood pressure and an increased risk of progression to CKD [23,24]. In the Caribbean, recurring natural disasters, such as hurricanes, disrupt healthcare infrastructure and services, further complicating the management of hypertension and prevention of CKD [25].

In contrast, high-SDI regions have witnessed a more complex trend. Our study reveals an overall upward trend in ASDR and ASMR globally. Notably, high SDI regions, particularly high-income North America, have experienced the fastest growth in the burden of CKD due to hypertension. Possible reasons include lifestyle changes in high-SDI regions, such as shifts in dietary patterns and reduced physical activity, which further complicate hypertension management [26]. Additionally, we observed a rapid surge in high-SDI regions in 2010, with an APC of 6.36%. This increase may be partly explained by the peak of the obesity epidemic in high-income regions during this period. According to the CDC, the obesity rate among U.S. adults reached 36.0% in 2010 [27]. These factors likely contributed to earlier onset and poorer control of hypertension, accelerating CKD progression. The growth rate began to slow down by 2016, possibly due to increased efforts by the United Nations and the WHO after 2015 to control non-communicable diseases [28]. For instance, Japan’s ‘Reduce Sodium, Increase Potassium’ campaign and the American Sodium Reduction Initiative implemented public education programs and food industry reforms to address a key dietary factor influencing hypertension control [29].

While high-SDI regions show rapid increases, the burden appears to stabilize or even decline during specific periods in high-middle SDI regions, highlighting a contrasting pattern. Notably, these regions experienced a downward trend in ASDR and ASMR between 1994 and 1998, as well as from 2005 to 2021. This fluctuation suggests that some high-middle SDI countries may have implemented effective public health interventions. Examples include the Hypertension Management Program launched in Portugal in 2005, the National Cardiovascular Disease Prevention Program initiated in Spain in 2004 [30,31], and the Regional Consultation on Hypertension Prevention and Control convened by the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region in December 2003. These initiatives likely improved hypertension management and helped slow the progression of CKD due to hypertension.

In low-SDI regions, ASMR and ASDR showed the lowest growth rates, but this does not indicate a lighter disease burden, as India still accounts for the highest numbers of DALYs and mortality. Instead, it reflects extremely weak management capacity for hypertension and CKD. Fewer than 10% of CKD patients in developing countries are aware of their condition [32]. Additionally, research indicates that while access to pharmacotherapy and dialysis is nearly universal in high-SDI countries, many individuals in low and middle-SDI nations lack access to these essential treatments [33]. In India, the cost of SGLT2 inhibitors or renal replacement therapy is more likely to push individuals into poverty than the expenses related to communicable diseases [34]. As a result, numerous patients fail to receive timely diagnosis and treatment, which may contribute to an underreporting of true mortality and DALYs. Furthermore, Ukraine has seen the most rapid increase in the global burden, largely due to political instability and armed conflicts that have severely disrupted its healthcare infrastructure [35].

The analysis of age, period, and cohort effects highlights age as a critical factor in the progression of CKD due to hypertension, aligning with the pathophysiological mechanism by which hypertensive damage to the kidneys gradually intensifies over time [36]. This finding aligns with our descriptive analysis, which identified the 45–49 age group as bearing the heaviest burden of CKD due to hypertension among young adults. This burden is likely driven by their peak professional and familial responsibilities, compounded by healthcare challenges such as limited time for medical check-ups, work-related stress, unhealthy lifestyles, and various social and economic pressures [37]. These factors lead to the neglect of hypertension management, while early intervention is critical for controlling hypertension and preventing its progression to CKD [38]. Meanwhile, the 25–29 age group, despite bearing a lower absolute burden, exhibited the fastest increase over the past 32 years. This trend underscores the significance of our study, highlighting the growing impact of CKD due to hypertension on young adults.

Globally, unfavorable period effects and favorable cohort effects were observed. Period risks have continued to rise in recent years, potentially linked to cumulative hypertension risk factors, particularly global lifestyle and dietary changes such as increasing obesity, sedentary behavior, and salt consumption, which complicate hypertension control [39]. In terms of cohort effects, individuals born later generally exhibited a lower risk compared to those born earlier. This could be due to earlier-born individuals having accumulated more risk factors throughout their lives, while later-born individuals may benefit from improved management of early-life exposures. National and international policies for hypertension and CKD prevention and management during the latter half of the twentieth century have positively influenced health outcomes among younger generations [40].

Frontier analysis revealed that as SDI increased, the differences in DALYs and mortality rates diminished, showing less variability. However, particular attention should be paid to low-SDI frontier countries, which demonstrated remarkable outcomes despite their limited resources. This analysis highlights that low-SDI countries such as Somalia, Tajikistan, the Solomon Islands, and Papua New Guinea have managed the burden of these diseases exceptionally well. In spite of limited resources, these nations serve as notable examples by effectively implementing strategies such as community-based health interventions and strengthening primary healthcare systems, offering valuable insights for other nations facing similar challenges [41,42]. In contrast, several high-SDI regions, such as the United States and Mauritius, have shown suboptimal performance, suggesting that these nations often struggle to implement universal and equitable health strategies despite having more resources [43]. Therefore, future research should prioritize identifying the key factors driving success in these exemplary countries and addressing the barriers that hinder progress in nations with poorer outcomes.

A notable observation in our study is the consistently higher relative risk among males compared to females. This sex-based disparity is evident not only in absolute levels of mortality and DALYs but also in the temporal trends. In fact, behavioral and metabolic risk factors play a significant role in explaining the gender gap between men and women [44]. Differences in health-seeking behavior, such as lower rates of medical check-ups among men, may contribute to these disparities [45]. Moreover, estrogen has been shown to reduce renal fibrosis and inflammation and promote vasodilation, and is therefore believed to exert a protective effect against the progression of kidney diseases in premenopausal women [26,46]. While our study identifies persistent sex disparities in the burden of CKD, the underlying biological, behavioral, and social mechanisms remain unclear, underscoring the need for further investigation [47]. At the same time, despite a growing emphasis on personalized care, sex-specific strategies remain underutilized in CKD prevention and management [48]. Therefore, bridging the sex gap in CKD burden demands integration of sex-specific insights into clinical and public health frameworks to promote truly equitable kidney care [45].

Monitoring disease epidemics and predicting trends are critical components of effective disease prevention and control [49]. In this study, eight machine learning methods were used to predict that the burden of CKD due to hypertension will continue to rise, imposing an even greater societal burden globally. This projection highlights the urgent need for effective interventions and policy measures to manage CKD due to hypertension. However, it is important to acknowledge that the optimal algorithm for predicting the burden of CKD varies depending on the specific metric used. While ARIMA demonstrated the best performance in the ASDR model, its reliance on historical data patterns may cause a lag in reflecting recent changes, thus delaying its response to more immediate trends [50]. The Prophet model, which performed best for predicting the ASMR index, highlights the differing strengths of models across various metrics, emphasizing the need to select the most appropriate model based on the specific aspects and objectives of the disease burden analysis. Future research could further explore the reasons behind these variations and potentially combine models to achieve more comprehensive and accurate predictions.

Given the rapid increase in the disease burden among young adults, addressing modifiable risk factors through targeted programs for early screening, public awareness, and proactive hypertension management is essential. However, these efforts must be supported by ensuring equitable access to hypertension management and CKD prevention, particularly in low and middle-SDI countries, where the financial burden of treatments like SGLT2 inhibitors and dialysis often limits accessibility. Universal health coverage offers a practical solution to mitigate these challenges by reducing out-of-pocket expenses and improving access to essential healthcare services. In addition, learning from the innovative strategies of low-SDI frontier countries, which have achieved notable outcomes despite resource constraints, can help develop adaptable approaches for other resource-limited settings. For high-income nations, where lifestyle-related risk factors contribute significantly to the growing disease burden, targeted interventions must address these specific challenges while integrating lessons learned globally. Strengthened international collaboration, including resource sharing, disease monitoring, predictive analysis, and public health education campaigns, is essential to align efforts across regions, drive behavioral changes, and ultimately reduce the global societal and economic impact of CKD due to hypertension.

Limitation

As with any study of this magnitude, there are inherent limitations. First, the analysis is conducted at a broad level, examining global, regional, and national CKD due to hypertension trends over the past three decades. As a result, finer, localized trends may not be included, which is especially relevant in countries like China, India, and the United States, where regional variations in CKD due to hypertension could be overlooked. Secondly, the definitions of disability applied in this study rely on general disease categories and do not consider comorbidities or the intricacies of various health conditions, suggesting that more detailed and refined analysis is necessary. Although we recognize these limitations, we anticipate that ongoing annual updates to the GBD estimates will improve future data collection and methodology, addressing some of the current gaps, such as offering more detailed subnational data. A key strength of this research lies in its use of GBD data spanning from 1990 to 2021, which offers the most comprehensive global health dataset currently accessible.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the burden of CKD due to hypertension remains substantial, and this burden is expected to continue increasing over the next 20 years. Low SDI countries need to remain vigilant in addressing the burden associated with CKD due to hypertension. At the same time, high-SDI countries should pay attention to the growing CKD burden in their regions, adopting more preventive and interventional measures to cope with the rising burden of this disease. These results are crucial for improving our understanding of the disease’s epidemiology, trends, resource distribution, and the development of relevant health policies.
