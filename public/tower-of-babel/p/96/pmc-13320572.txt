Stability or Latent Risk? Interpreting MRA Withdrawal in Recovered Heart Failure
Authors: Abdülmelik Birgün, Mehmet Mustafa Yılmaz, Macit Kalçık
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the study by Hyun et al.1) investigating the feasibility and safety of mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) withdrawal in patients with heart failure and improved ejection fraction (HFiEF). The authors should be commended for addressing a clinically relevant and understudied question and for conducting the first randomized controlled trial focusing on the withdrawal of a single guideline directed medical therapy component in this population.

Nevertheless, several methodological aspects warrant careful interpretation. First, the small sample size and pilot design substantially limit statistical power, making it difficult to draw firm conclusions regarding non-inferiority or long-term safety. Although the low event rate is reassuring, the absence of hard clinical endpoints over a relatively short follow up period may underestimate late relapse, which has been shown to occur beyond 6 months in patients with recovered cardiomyopathy.2)

Second, the open-label nature of the trial introduces the potential for performance and detection bias, particularly in subjective outcomes and treatment adherence. Even though echocardiographic analyses were blinded, clinical decision-making and follow-up intensity may have differed between groups. Prior experience from withdrawal studies suggests that subtle hemodynamic or neurohormonal changes may precede overt declines in left ventricular ejection fraction.3)

Third, patient selection deserves particular attention. The cohort consisted predominantly of asymptomatic patients with non-ischemic etiologies and a high proportion receiving angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors. This well treated, low risk profile limits generalizability to broader HFiEF populations encountered in daily practice, including those with ischemic heart disease, residual congestion, or incomplete reverse remodeling. Current guidelines emphasize continued disease-modifying therapy in such patients due to relapse risk and pathophysiological memory of heart failure.4)

Finally, while the study focuses on safety, it does not fully address potential prognostic trade-offs. MRAs exert pleiotropic effects beyond left ventricular systolic function, including antifibrotic and antiarrhythmic properties. Withdrawal may therefore have implications not captured by short term echocardiographic stability alone. Larger, longer term randomized trials incorporating biomarkers, arrhythmic outcomes, and patient-centered endpoints are needed before routine MRA discontinuation can be recommended.5)
