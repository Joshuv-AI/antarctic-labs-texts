Sustained circulation of Aedes albopictus-derived novel almendraviruses in the urban parks
Authors: Xi Zhang, Chengyu Liu, Yang Han, Junying She, Wanqi Wu, Lili Wang, Jingdong Song, George F Gao, Ziqian Xu, Hao Liang, Jun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Scientific question: Aedes albopictus (Ae. albopictus) serves as a significant viral vector, and characterizing the diversity of viruses is essential for deciphering vector-viral community composition and refining viral surveillance strategies.•Evidence before this study: Almendraviruses (family Rhabdoviridae) have been exclusively isolated from mosquitoes or detected in dipteran insect pools. Most known strains originate from tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas and Asia. Although these viruses can replicate in mosquito cells in vitro, current evidences suggest they cannot infect vertebrates, indicating a potential mosquito-restricted host range.•New findings: We identified two novel rhabdoviruses from Ae. albopictus mosquitoes collected in the urban parks over two consecutive years. Genetic divergence analysis supported that these viruses represent two distinct new species within the Rhabdoviridae family. This discovery marks only the rarely reported occurrence of almendraviruses in the temperate zone. Furthermore, both viruses were successfully isolated using the mosquito-derived C6/36 cell line.•Significance of the study: This work not only significantly expands the known diversity of almendraviruses but also enhances our understanding of the virome harbored by Ae. albopictus in urban environments, which is critical for arbovirus surveillance in metropolitan ecosystems.Keywords: Almendravirus, Aedes albopictus, Virus isolation, Novel virus, Aedes albopictus almendravirus GCCDC15 (Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15), Aedes albopictus almendravirus GCCDC16 (Aealb-AlmV GCCDC16)

Introduction

Rhabdoviruses constitute a large and ecologically diverse family of viruses that infect a wide range of hosts, including vertebrates, invertebrates, and plants [1,2]. This broad host tropism is reflected in their significant impact on public health and agriculture, with various members causing devastating diseases in humans (e.g., rabies virus), livestock (e.g., bovine ephemeral fever virus), fish (e.g., infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus), and crops (e.g., lettuce necrotic yellows virus) [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. A large number of these viruses are transmitted by arthropod vectors, such as mosquitoes, sandflies, and leafhoppers, to their vertebrate or plant hosts [1]. Rhabdoviruses possess non-segmented, negative-sense, single-stranded ribonucleic acid (RNA) genomes ranging from approximately 10 to 16 kilobases (kb) in length. The virions are typically enveloped and exhibit a characteristic bullet-shaped or bacilliform morphology [7,8]. Almost all rhabdovirus genomes encode five canonical structural proteins in the order 3′-N-P-M-G-L-5′: nucleoprotein (N), phosphoprotein (P), matrix protein (M), glycoprotein (G), and the large RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (L) [9].

The family Rhabdoviridae is taxonomically complex and has expanded rapidly with the advent of metagenomics. It currently comprises four subfamilies (Alpharhabdovirinae, Betarhabdovirinae, Gammarhabdovirinae, Deltarhabdovirinae), 56 genera, and 434 species [10]. Among these, viruses assigned to the genus Almendravirus (subfamily Alpharhabdovirinae) comprises eight known members. These viruses can replicate efficiently in mosquito cell lines in vitro but demonstrate no ability to replicate in vertebrate cells, suggesting a host range restricted to invertebrates [11]. Almendraviruses have been isolated from a variety of mosquito genera, including Culex, Aedes, Anopheles, Armigeres, and Psorophora, indicating a broad potential host range within mosquitoes. The known distribution of almendraviruses includes regions in Peru, Panama, Colombia, Florida [The United States of America (U.S.A.)], and China [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. In China, three almendraviruses, including Menghai rhabdovirus (MRV), Shanxi arboretum virus, and Xiangshan rhabdo-like virus 1, have been identified [[13], [14], [15]].

As global warming accelerates and the geographic ranges of disease vectors expands, the distribution of vector-borne diseases is likely to undergo significant shifts [[16], [17], [18]]. To effectively mitigate disease transmission, conducting surveillance on the spatiotemporal distribution of mosquito-borne viruses will provide critical insights for controlling and preventing potential outbreaks of vector-borne diseases [[19], [20], [21]]. During a continuous mosquito-borne virus surveillance from 2023 to 2024, two novel rhabdoviruses were discovered from Aedes albopictus (Ae. albopictus) collected in two urban parks. We investigated the genomic organization and evolutionary relationship of the two newly discovered rhabdoviruses which are closely related to almendraviruses. Furthermore, the viruses were successfully isolated and bullet-shaped morphology were observed in mosquito cell line. The study indicated that divergent almendraviruses are continuously circulating among mosquitoes in urban areas.

Materials and methods

Mosquito collections

Mosquitoes were captured at two sites in Chaoyang District, Beijing, using manual traps (Shanghai Daolan Industry Co.). The mosquitoes were transported in liquid nitrogen to the laboratory, where they were identified by species and gender based on the morphological characteristics.

Next generation sequencing (NGS)

According to the locations and species, the mosquito samples were pooled into different groups. As previously described [22,23], each sample pool was washed with 1 × phosphate buffered saline (PBS) buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and 1.5 mL of Dulbecco's modified eagle's medium (DMEM) basal medium containing 1 % penicillin–streptomycin (GIBCO, Grand Island, NY, U.S.A.) was added. Subsequently, the samples were homogenized at 60 Hz for 5 min using a pre-cooled grinder (Shanghai Jingxin, Shanghai, China), followed by centrifugation at 12,000 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 10 min at 4 °C. Total RNA was extracted from 200 μL of the supernatant of each sample using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, Austin, TX, U.S.A.) and quantified with a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, U.S.A.) and the Qubit RNA High Sensitivity (HS) Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, U.S.A.). Sequencing complementary deoxyribonucleic acid (cDNA) libraries were constructed using the UltraClean RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The libraries were neither poly (A)-selected nor strand-specific. After quality control and purification, paired-end sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, U.S.A.).

Virus discovery

The sequencing reads were filtered to remove low-quality sequences using Trimmomatic with default parameters [24]. Megahit (v1.2.9) was then utilized for de novo assembly [25,26]. The assembled contigs were compared against a collection of viral RNA dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) sequences that represent the diversity of RNA viruses [27]. Potential viral contigs were further confirmed through Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST)-x analyses against the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) non-redundant protein database (NR) to ensure their viral origin and to identify homologous sequences. The confirmed viral scaffolds were assembled using SeqMan program (DNAstar v7.1), with the Minimum Match Percentage parameter set to 75 %. And scaffolds assembled into the same contiguous sequence were classified into the same group for subsequent analysis.

Rapid amplification of cDNA ends

Total RNA was extracted from 200 µL of homogenized virus isolates sample using TRIzol Reagent (Ambion, U.S.A.). Since the 3′-untranslated region (UTR) lacked a poly (A) tail, the Escherichia coli (E. coli) Poly (A) Polymerase Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was employed to enzymatically add a poly (A) tail to the RNA. The polyadenylated RNA was subsequently purified using VAHTS RNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Gene-specific primers were designed based on the genomic sequences of the rhabdovirus to perform rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE). The 5′- and 3′-UTR sequences were amplified using the RACE 5′/3′ Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China).

Amplified products were purified by 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis (TaKaRa MiniBEST v4.0, Japan) and cloned into the pCE2 TA/Blunt-Zero vector using the 5-min TA/Blunt-Zero Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The ligation product was transformed into Fast-T1 competent E. coli (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), plated onto Luria-Bertani agar plates supplemented with ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Single colonies were cultured for plasmid extraction, and inserted fragments were verified by Sanger sequencing (Beijing Tianyi Huiyuan Biotech Co., Ltd., China).

Amino acid sequence alignments and phylogenetic analysis

Predicting potential open reading frames (ORFs) of the viral genomes were predicted using the NCBI ORF Finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/). BLAST-protein (BLASTp) in NCBI was used to compare the identity of the predicted amino acid sequences of viruses. The viral L protein sequences were aligned with sequences from related viruses using MUSCLE (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/muscle), and ambiguous alignment regions were removed with trimAl software [28]. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed in PhyML (version 3.1) using the maximum-likelihood (ML) method, the LG amino acid substitution model, and the SPR structure optimization algorithm [29]. All reference viral sequences were downloaded from GenBank.

Virus isolation and electron microscopy

The C6/36 (Ae. albopictus) cell line was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, U.S.A.) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin solution at 28 °C with 5 % carbon dioxide (CO2) [30]. To isolate the virus, 100 µL of sample supernatant was inoculated into 80 % monolayer C6/36 cells in a 24-well plate, using RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 2 % FBS and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were observed daily for cytopathic effects (CPE). A virus isolate was considered positive when CPE was observed in three sequential passages [31]. After isolation, the virus was further propagated in Baby hamster kidney (BHK-21) and African green monkey kidney (Vero) cell lines. These two cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, U.S.A.) containing 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin solution at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator [30,31]. When CPE reached approximately 75 %, virus-infected C6/36 cells were harvested. The cell culture supernatant was centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The supernatant and pellet were then applied for negative staining and ultrathin sectioning for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) detection [32].

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and sanger sequencing

Nested primers for the amplification of the target gene were designed using Primer Premier 5 software. The first-round PCR products were obtained using the HiScript II one-step RT-PCR kit (Dye Plus) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) under the following cycling conditions: 50 °C for 30 min, 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 62 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 8 min. The second-round PCR products were amplified using GoTaq® Green Master Mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) under the following conditions: 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 62 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm the presence and size of the amplified fragments. Sanger sequencing was performed by Tianyi Huiyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Data sharing

The genome sequences of these novel viruses have been deposited in the China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB) under accession numbers C_AA110799.1 to C_AA110814.1, which correspond to the NCBI accession numbers PX373701 to PX373716. The associated raw sequencing data have been submitted to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the BioProject accession number PRJNA1320997.

Results

Virome in five Ae. albopictus libraries

From 2023 to 2024, a total of 146 adult female Ae. albopictus mosquitoes were collected from two sites in Chaoyang District, Beijing. Among these, 55 individuals were selected for high-throughput sequencing. Based on the locations and collection times, these samples were pooled into five libraries, which generated 66,840,876 to 166,866,390 clean reads for each library (Table S1). Through de novo assembly, 119 viral contigs spanning 18 viral families/orders were identified (Fig. S1). The viral sequences were assembled using the SeqMan program (DNAstar v7.1), resulting in a total of 62 sequences representing 33 distinct viral species, as detailed in Table 1. Novel viruses were tentatively defined based on sequence identity thresholds (<90 % amino acid identity to known viruses in the NCBI NR database), with only the longest representative sequence retained for each viral species sharing > 90 % identity. Among these, 16 sequences were identified as putative novel viruses, showing closest homology to Aedes flavivirus, Sopowof virus, Culex tritaeniorhynchus partitivirus, Coot Bay virus (CBV), MRV, Bafoussam mosquito solemovirus, Hubei toti-like virus 10, Yeltsovka tombus-like virus, Vaasa totivirus, Yongsan bunyavirus 1, Buriti virga-like virus, most of which were known insect-specific viruses, mycoviruses, or plant-associated viruses (Fig. S2).

Discovery of Aedes albopictus almendravirus GCCDC15 and GCCDC16

Among them, two novel almendraviruses were identified. From the samples collected from Beijing Wenyuhe Park in 2023, a novel rhabdovirus with genome sequence of approximately 10 kb was discovered in the Library L1 (Table S1). This virus, which was provisionally named Aedes albopictus almendravirus GCCDC15 (Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15), showed 59.81 % identity to the closest virus CBV over 55 % coverage. CBV was originally isolated from Anopheles quadrimaculatus in the U.S.A. From the samples collected from Beijing Olympic Park in 2024, two rhabdovirus sequences were identified in Library L5, in which the first one exhibited 98.98 % nucleotide identity to Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 (Data not shown). And the second one, which was provisionally named Aedes albopictus almendravirus GCCDC16 (Aealb-AlmV GCCDC16), showed 87.65 % nucleotide identity to the most similar virus MRV over 56 % coverage. MRV was originally isolated from Ae. albopictus in Yunnan Province, China.

Genomic organization

The complete genomic sequences of both viruses were confirmed through high-throughput sequencing of the virus isolates from C6/36 cell cultures. The genome of Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 consists of 10,933 nucleotides (nt), while that of Aealb-AlmV GCCDC16 comprises 10,808 nt. Their genomic organization is similar to those of other almendraviruses, consisting of the five canonical structural protein genes (N, P, M, G, and L), as well as an additional gene encoding a small hydrophobic protein (U1) located between the G and L genes (Fig. 1) [10]. The ORFs that encode the N proteins of Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 and GCCDC16 consist of 1,311 nt and 1,278 nt, respectively. The predicted translation products are most closely related to the corresponding proteins of CBV (38.67 % identity) and MRV (84.00 %). The ORFs encoding the M protein are 561 and 501 nt long, and their predicted translation products are most closely related to those of CBV and MRV, showing 45.39 % and 74.70 % identities, respectively. The ORFs for the G protein consist of 1,350 and 1,356 nt, with the predicted translation products exhibiting 36.80 % and 66.30 % identities to the corresponding protein of CBV and MRV. The largest ORFs in the genomes of Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 and GCCDC16 span 6,108 and 6,102 nt, and encode the L proteins, with the predicted translation products being most closely related to that of CBV and MRV, at 59.85 % and 87.20 % identity. The remaining two ORFs of Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 encode proteins that do not show significant similarity to any other sequences in GenBank. These two ORFs are presumed to encode the P and U1 proteins based on their positions within the genome. When aligned with the P and U1 proteins of CBV, they showed 14.30 % and 16.90 % identity, respectively. The remaining two ORFs of GCCDC16 encode P and U1 proteins consist of 720 nt and 210 nt, and their predicted translation products are most closely related to the corresponding proteins of MRV (66.53 % and 72.97 % identity).

Phylogenetic analysis and evolutionary relationships

To investigate the evolutionary relationships of Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 and GCCDC16, a ML phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the deduced amino acid sequences of the L proteins, including representative rhabdovirus species from the Alpharhabdovirinae subfamily (Fig. 2). The ML tree indicated that Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 and GCCDC16 clustered together with CBV, MRV and other almendraviruses, which is consistent with the amino acid sequence alignments. Analyses of different genome regions showed that the clustering of Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 and GCCDC16 with almendraviruses remained consistent (Fig. S3).

Virus isolation and morphology

Following inoculation of C6/36 cell line flask cultures with homogenate supernatants of L1 and L5 samples, infected C6/36 cells exhibited marked cellular aggregation while maintaining structural integrity within 2–3 days post-infection, without cell detachment or exfoliation (Fig. 3B). To morphologically characterize the viral agents, TEM was performed on fourth-passage C6/36 cells displaying CPE. Ultrastructural analysis revealed bacilliform virions in both samples, measuring approximately 300 nm in length and 70 nm in diameter (Fig. 3C).

Following three successive passages in C6/36 cells, the rhabdovirus isolates were subsequently inoculated into cultures of Vero and BHK-21 cells, which were maintained at 37 °C for an additional 6 days, followed by three further passages. No CPE or viral genome amplification was detected by RT-PCR in the mammalian cells. This species-specific tropism is consistent with previous studies on other arthropod-borne rhabdoviruses [11,12], which often exhibit limited replication in mammalian cell lines.

Prevalence of Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 and GCCDC16

Using specific primers (as detailed in Table S2) targeting the Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 and GCCDC16 strains, the 146 Ae. albopictus samples were screened for viral prevalence. Based on collection date and location, the samples were divided into 14 pools (including 5 pools of L1–L5), each containing 7–15 adult mosquitoes. PCR detection results revealed that 8 pools (57.1 % of the total pools) tested positive for Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 (including all pools from L1 to L5). In contrast, Aealb-AlmV GCCDC16 was detected only in the L5 pool (7.1 %, 1/14) that was collected in Beijing Olympic Park, indicating a significantly lower prevalence compared to Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15. The overall minimum infection rates (MIRs; calculated as the number of positive mosquito pools per 1,000 mosquitoes tested) of GCCDC15 and GCCDC16 were 54.8 and 6.8 (Table S3).

Discussion

In a two-year surveillance of viruses carried by Ae. albopictus in urban parks, two new mosquito-borne rhabdoviruses, Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 and GCCDC16, were discovered. Based on the species demarcation criteria [10], these viruses are considered two new species within the Almendravirus genus (Rhabdoviridae).

Until recently, eight species of almendraviruses had been identified [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]], with most found in tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas and Asia. In addition to Shanxi arboretum virus and Xiangshan rhabdo-like virus 1 [13,15], this marks the third time that almendraviruses have been identified in the temperate zone. This suggests a wide geographical range for almendraviruses and indicates that more diverse strains are yet to be discovered.

Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 was initially identified through metatranscriptomic sequencing from Ae. albopictus samples collected in 2023. Then, this strain was successfully isolated in C6/36 cells and subsequently re-detected in 2024 at both the original site and another park located 10 km away. And another almendravirus, Aealb-AlmV GCCDC16, was found in the urban park following Aealb-AlmV GCCDC15 in 2024. These data indicate that these novel divergent almendraviruses were constantly circulating among urban Ae. albopictus populations in Beijing. Among the recognized almendraviruses, except MRV which was discovered in Ae. albopictus, only these two almendraviruses were isolated from Ae. albopictus samples.

In summary, the discovery of two distinct novel viruses belonging to the same genus at different times and locations in Ae. albopictus samples has significant biological implications for understanding the transmission dynamics of these viruses. Further investigation into the circulating status of almendraviruses among mosquitoes is crucial for understanding their host range and potential impact on One Health.
