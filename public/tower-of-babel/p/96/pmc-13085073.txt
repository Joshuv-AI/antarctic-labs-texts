Robust detection in faeces and in vivo quantification of bacteriophages infecting enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli by quantitative PCR
Authors: Norbert Ács, Martin Rydal, Mattia Pirolo, Jens Peter Nielsen, Katrine Kiib Rørby, Laura Ladrón de Guevara Jeppesen, Hanne Bjørn Nedergaard, Jan Lorenzen, Lone Brøndsted, Luca Guardabassi, Michela Gambino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•developed a quantitative PCR (qPCR) method specific for the previously isolated ETEC phage ETEP21B and validated in vitro and in vivo.•the qPCR assay exhibited excellent specificity even with non-target phages and in faecal matrices.•in the in vivo PWD model, ETEP21B was detected in faecal samples from all pigs receiving the phage-supplemented diet, confirming successful passage through the gastrointestinal tract.•phage administration did not significantly reduce diarrhoea or shedding compared with challenged controls, the qPCR assay highlighted delivery challenges that ensure further optimisation.Keywords: Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli, Post-weaning diarrhoea, Bacteriophages, Quantitative PCR

Introduction

Post-weaning diarrhoea (PWD) is a widespread disease that affects pigs in the first two weeks after weaning, a particularly vulnerable period marked by abrupt changes in environment and feed that cause substantial stress on the immature immune system (Campbell et al., 2013). PWD leads to weight loss and occasionally to mortality, compromising animal welfare and causing considerable economic losses to the swine industry worldwide. PWD is a multifactorial disease, but enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC) strains are considered the main etiological agent (Dubreuil et al., 2016). ETEC are characterised by the expression of fimbriae to adhere to specific receptors on intestinal enterocytes, and toxins that cause the secretion of water and electrolytes into the intestinal gut lumen, resulting in watery diarrhoea (Fleckenstein et al., 2010). To control ETEC-induced PWD, antimicrobials and zinc oxide have been largely employed in pig production, but their use is now increasingly restricted, due to concerns regarding spread of antimicrobial resistance and its potential transmission from animals to humans (Vahjen et al., 2015; Ciesinski et al., 2018).

Bacteriophages (phages) are viruses that specifically infect bacteria, playing an essential role in shaping soil, marine, and animal ecosystems. Since their discovery in 1915, phages have been a fundamental tool to explain molecular and biological principles (Gordillo Altamirano & Barr, 2019). The use of phages to treat bacterial infections was initially considered controversial due to inconsistent results, until it was largely neglected in the Western world after the discovery of antibiotics (Summers, 2012). While in the post-Soviet states and a few other countries, the use of phages for medical purposes continued, it was only with the emergence of antimicrobial resistance that phage therapy was reconsidered as a valuable resource for treatment of infections caused by resistant bacteria, as well as for biocontrol applications (Wittebole et al., 2014).

Contrary to conventional antimicrobials, the narrow host range of phages allows specific depletion of the target pathogen, leaving other bacteria unaffected (Holtappels et al., 2023). This targeted contribution is a key player in highly complex environments, such as the animal gut, where phages represent more than 90 % of the viruses present (Gregory et al., 2020; Lathakumari et al., 2025). Counteracting the predation pressure, bacteria have developed several anti-phage defence mechanisms, and likewise, phages have also developed counteractive measures to evade these systems, in a continuous arms race (Hampton et al., 2020). The presence of numerous phages and bacteria in the gut environment, along with faecal material, can be a significant confounding factor in clinical trials for phage products against enteric pathogens, such as ETEC. Fast and precise detection and enumeration of the administered phages in this environment is therefore essential to optimise dosage and administration, and to demonstrate safety and efficacy, as required by authorities for market approval (Gambino & Brøndsted, 2021).

Detection and enumeration of phages are traditionally achieved by determining infective viral particles with the double-layer agar assay, as it is the only method that can discriminate between infective and defective viral particles. Nevertheless, amplification of specific DNA fragments by quantitative PCR (qPCR) is remarkably faster and practical, allowing phage detection in most laboratories and industrial settings (Ács et al., 2020).

In this study, we use the previously isolated lytic phage ETEP21B, a promising candidate for a phage product against ETEC-induced PWD. This phage alone was able to infect 38 % of a diverse collection of 79 ETEC strains, isolated from faecal or gut samples of pigs clinically affected by PWD in several European pig farms between 2014 and 2020 (Gambino et al., 2024). The primary objective of this manuscript was to develop a reliable tool to quantify and monitor the fate of phage ETEP21B in vivo, by tracking it during faecal excretion in pigs that were experimentally challenged with ETEC F4. To transition from the laboratory to in vivo testing, we established a molecular detection method based on qPCR, specific for phage ETEP21B, and compared its performance with enumeration by double-layer agar assay. The detection method was first evaluated in simplified model systems using pig faeces spiked with known quantities of phage ETEP21B to assess its sensitivity and accuracy in complex matrices. Finally, a formulation with phage ETEP21B was prepared at pilot scale and tested in an in vivo pig trial to validate the detection method and monitor phage dynamics under physiological conditions.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, phages stocks and media

E. coli ECOR04 (Ochman & Selander, 1984) and NCTC 12,900 were used as strains for plate lysis propagation, while the ETEC strains ET54 from the PHAGEBio ETEC collection (BioProject ID PRJNA770188 on GenBank database) were used to test infectivity towards ETEC strains and in the in vivo experiment. Both ETEC strains displayed haemolysis on blood agar and carried genes for F4 fimbriae along and enterotoxins ST2 and LT, and were used for liquid propagation of phage ETEP21B. Bacterial stocks were stored at −80 °C and streaked onto lysogeny broth agar (LB, Oxoid CM0996B) or blood agar (BA, Oxoid CM0055) plates, supplemented with 5 % bovine blood. Base agar was prepared using LB supplemented with 1.5 % agar, and soft agar for lawns was prepared using LB with 0.6 % agar.

Phage ETEP21B, a member of the Berlinvirus genus within the Autographiviridae family, was previously isolated and characterized to infect a large fraction of a diverse collection of porcine ETEC strains, as described (Gambino et al., 2024). ETEP21B was propagated on ECOR04, and the complete genome sequence can be found with the accession number OR979722. Phage CBA120, used as a specificity control as it does not infect ETEC strains, was kindly provided by Petr Leiman (NCBI Reference Sequence: NC_016570.1).

Bacteria and phages DNA isolation

Pure genomic DNA from phage stocks, their mixtures, and viral fractions was isolated as described (Jakočiūnė & Moodley, 2018). Briefly, enzymatic removal of DNA and RNA was followed by capsid lysis using proteinase K, then further lysis and DNA clean-up with the Qiagen Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The concentration of the resulting genomic DNA was determined using the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). Similarly, the Qiagen Blood and Tissue kit was used for extracting genomic DNA from bacterial cultures.

Titre determination using double-layer agar assay

The double-layer agar assay was used as a reference method to determine the number of infectious viral particles. Briefly, 100 µL of the bacterial strain grown in LB (Lysogeny Broth, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) for 5 h at 37 °C was mixed with 3.3 mL of molten overlay agar (LBov; LB broth with 0.6 % Agar bacteriological no.1, Oxoid) and poured onto LB (LA) plates with a 9 cm diameter. After drying, the undiluted samples and tenfold serial dilutions in SM buffer (0.1 M NaCl, 8 mM MgSO4 × 7H2O, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5) were spotted onto bacterial lawns. After incubation, plaques were counted, and plaque-forming units per mL (pfu/mL) were calculated in at least two independent replicates. Phage stocks were prepared by propagation using the plate lysis method (Hyman & Abedon, 2009).

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Primer3 (version 0.4.0) software was used to design oligonucleotide primers for qPCR targeting a unique region in the genome of ETEP21B. The PCR product is a 116 bp long fragment from a non-coding region (RT1-F: CAGTTCGGGAAGGCACTAAAG; RT1-R: GTGAGCTATAGCCCCTCAG). In silico verification of the designed primers was conducted using CLC main workbench.

The Roche LightCycler96 machine along with reagents and plastic consumables from Roche (Denmark) was used to perform qPCR reactions. FastStart Essential DNA Green Master Mix was employed to prepare the reaction mix according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The 25 µL qPCR reaction included 12.5 µL of the master mix, 2 µL of each primer (5 µM), and 4.5 µL of ultrapure H2O. Additionally, 1 µL of template DNA was added to each well, except for the non-template controls. The amplification of target nucleotide sequences involved one cycle of 95 °C for 600 s, followed by 40 cycles of: 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 15 s. The annealing temperature was optimised through several trial runs using a thermal gradient (from 53 °C to 62 °C) for the annealing step, resulting in the selection of 60 °C as the optimal temperature for the RT1 primer pair, obtaining consistent Ct values. This was followed by a melting point (Tm) analysis with one additional cycle. When possible, each sample was run in duplicates, and the cycle threshold (Ct) was calculated as the average of the two readings. Raw data were analysed using Roche LightCycler96 software (version 1.1.0.1320). A qPCR run was considered successful if the slope, intercept and coefficient of determination were within acceptable range. These were between −3.1 and −3.6 for the slope (representing 89.5 – 110.2 % PCR efficiency); between 39 and 43 for the intercept; and above 0.98 R2 value for the coefficient of determination.

ETEP21B detection and quantification in spiked faecal samples

Fresh pig faecal material from healthy 3-week-old piglets, with no diarrhoea, was collected, pooled into a sterile container, and stored at −80 °C. Before processing, samples were allowed to thaw at room temperature (RT). Tubes were weighed, and an equal amount of saline solution was added to the faecal material before mixing vigorously.

Aliquots of 500 µL from the manually homogenized mixture were spiked with known amounts of ETEP21B phage, ranging from 9 × 107 to 9 × 103, and SM buffer was added to reach a final volume of 5 mL. Samples were centrifuged for 20 min at 5000 × g to remove solid particles and filtered through 0.45 µm pore size filters (Minisart, Sartorius). Liquid samples were concentrated either by using Amicon Ultra-4 Centrifugal Filter Units (filtered samples) with a 50 kDa cut-off or by overnight (ON) precipitation with polyethylene glycol (PEG 8000 plus 2 M NaCl) at 4 °C (precipitated samples). Filters were centrifuged according to the manufacturer’s recommendations at 7500 × g for 30 min to achieve the desired volume of less than 500 µL. Concentrated samples were transferred to a centrifuge tube and supplemented with SM buffer to a final volume of 500 µL. Precipitated samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was allowed to dry before resuspension in 500 µL SM buffer. Finally, 450 µL was used for DNA extraction, while the remaining 50 µL was serially diluted to determine the recovered viral titre (pfu/mL).

In vivo phage delivery and tracking study: phage formulation

Phage ETEP21B was propagated for microencapsulation in 500 mL batches and then pooled into 3.5 L as follows: 5 mL of E. coli ECOR04 culture was incubated in LB for 1 h at 37 °C, after which 50 mL of a 106 pfu/mL ETEP21B stock was added and further incubated for 1.5 h at 37 °C and 200 rpm. The suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 × g, 4 °C for 10 min and filtered with 0.2 µm pore size filters (Minisart, Sartorius). The propagated phages were microencapsulated with maltodextrin, sucrose, arabic gum, Eurdragit L30 d-55, and Eudragit FS 30 D (Evonik, Denmark), Tween 80 (Sigma-Aldrich, Denmark) and Plasacryl T20, with the encapsulation being explicitly designed for gut delivery. The mixture was spray dried using a 4M8-Trix spray drier from Procept at 180 °C, at an inlet flow rate of 0.3 m2/h. The solution was pumped at a flow rate of 4.5 mL/min and atomized using a two-fluid nozzle with a diameter of 0.4 mm. The microencapsulated ETEP21B was mixed in the base diet for the ET54+ ETEP21B group to make up 1 % of the total diet content. The phage content in the diet was 4.5 × 108 pfu/kg feed, determined by double-layer agar assay on ECOR4.

In vivo phage delivery and tracking study: animals, housing and experimental design

The experiment was ethically assessed and approved by the natinal Animal Experiments Inspectorate, license number: 2020–15–0201–00,555.

A total of 30 Landrace x Yorkshire x Duroc (LY-D) pigs were included in the study. Pigs originated from a single production herd with specific pathogen free (SPF) status (Rydal et al., 2022). Pigs were kept with the mother sow until weaning and had no history of medical treatment in the preweaning period. No gilt litters were included. Pigs were genotyped at post-natal day (PND) 4 using the CHCF1 allele marker as previously described (Rydal et al., 2023). All pigs included in the study were tested ETEC F4ab/ac susceptible according to the DNA marker. The pigs were weaned at PND 22 and transported to the experimental facility, where they were housed in isolated rooms with one pen per room, assigned according to experimental group, and maintained under strict biosecurity conditions. These included complete changes of clothing and boots between rooms, replacement of gloves combined with hand disinfection, prohibition of shared materials between rooms, and maintenance of negative-pressure ventilation. Room temperature was set at 26 °C with access to a heated resting area (30 to 32 °C). Pigs had ad libitum access to feed and water and were housed on concrete floors with wood shavings as bedding material. The base feed was a standard weaner diet (19.3 % crude protein/pr. kg feed) without added antimicrobials and with the main ingredients ( % of diet): wheat (52.6 %), soy protein concentrate (12 %), soybean meal (10.1 %), milk powder (10 %), barley (8 %) and soy oil (2.7 %).

The thirty CHCF1 heterozygous susceptible (RS) pigs were randomized into three groups stratified by weaning weight: 1) a group of 10 pigs that received the base feed and was given a capsule with saline (Saline+basefeed), 2) a group of 10 pigs that received the base feed and was inoculated with a gelatine capsule containing 5 × 109 colony forming units (cfu) of ET54 (ET54+basefeed), 3) a group of 10 pigs that received the base feed mixed with microencapsulated ETEP21B (4.5 × 108 pfu/kg feed, to make up 1 % of the total diet content) and was inoculated with a gelatine capsule containing 5 × 109 ET54 (ET54+ETEP21B). Gelatines capsules with either sterile saline or ET54 were prepared as previously described (Rydal et al., 2022).

Pigs were weaned on the respective diets at the experimental facility from PND 22 and were acclimatized for 5 days before being challenged with ET54/given saline solution at PND 26. In these 5 days, the ET54+ETEP21B group received already the base feed mixed with microencapsulated ETEP21B. The pigs were followed for 6 days after inoculation to assess differences in ETEC-mediated diarrhoea occurrence, haemolytic shedding, ADG and faecal shedding of ETEP21B. At the end of the study pigs were sedated and euthanized using pentobarbital via intracardial injection.

In vivo phage delivery and tracking study: clinical and microbiological assessments

Pigs had faecal consistency scored visually (from 1 to 4) once daily according to Pedersen et al. (Pedersen & Toft, 2011) for diarrhoea assessment. Pigs were weighted once daily and clinically assessed twice daily from the day of inoculation. After inoculation pigs were monitored once every 3 h day and night. Clinical assessment included signs of dehydration (tested by pinch skin-fold test, cyanosis and looking at sunken eyes and flanks), and signs of affected mental status (e.g. unwillingness to move). The clinical condition was scored (from 1 to 3) according to appendix 1 as described (Rydal et al., 2023). If an animal reached a score of 3, it would be monitored intensively, and if it did not improve within 3 h, it would be euthanized for humane reasons. No score 3 was given in the study; however, five animals that were approaching a score 3 were euthanized for humane reasons to avoid unnecessary distress.

For the microbiological assessment, rectal swabs were collected at PND 21 from the herd before transporting the animals to assess haemolytic E. coli shedding. Swabs were cultured on blood agar (BA; Oxoid CM0055, supplemented with 5 % bovine blood, ThermoFischer, CM0055). Shedding was evaluated semi-quantitatively as the percentage of haemolytic E. coli colonies out of the total bacterial growth on the blood agar plate. Daily faecal samples from each pig were collected at the experimental facility for microbiological assessment from the day of inoculation (PND 26). Faeces were cultured on BA to assess shedding of haemolytic E. coli according to Rydal et al. (Rydal et al., 2023). In case of haemolytic E. coli shedding, one colony per sample was tested using multiplex PCR for the virulence genes F4, F18, Sta, STb, and LT as described (Zhang et al., 2007). Multiple loci VNTR analysis (MLVA) was used to confirm recovery of the challenge strain Løbersli et al. (2012). Furthermore, F4 and F18 qPCR was performed on faeces using a previously described method.

In vivo phage delivery and tracking study: ETEP21B detection and quantification

Faecal samples were collected daily and processed to count phages both by plating on ET54 lawns and by running the qPCR protocol, specific for ETEP21B detection. Plaque count was performed on 1:10 dilution of faeces in saline and converted to plaque forming units per mL (pfu/mL). Sample concentration or filtering was not possible due to the low volume. For quantification of ETEP21B by qPCR, total DNA was extracted from intact faeces using the QIAamp UCP Pathogen MiniKit (QIAGEN, Copenhagen, Denmark), following the manufacturer’s instructions, with a bead-beating step using the Pathogen Lysis Tube S (QIAGEN, Copenhagen, Denmark). Data from at least three replicates were used to calculate average and standard deviation.

Statistical analysis

Data was visualized using GraphPad Prism (v. 10.4.1) and analysed using R statistical software version 4.4.3 (https://www.r-project.org/). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and < 0.10 a tendency.

For the in vivo experiment, the primary outcome was ETEP21B shedding in faeces evaluated by qPCR. Secondary outcomes were ETEC F4 diarrhoea (defined as faecal consistency score 3 or 4 and simultaneous recovery of ETEC F4 from faeces) occurrence, F4 gene copies/g faeces between inoculated groups and average daily gain (ADG) after inoculation. Analysis for differences in F4 gene copies/g faeces (log10(+1) transformed) between inoculated groups (ET54+basefeed and ET54+ ETEP21B) was analysed using Mann-Whitney U test. Group differences in ADG following inoculation was analysed using ANOVA. Tukey’s HSD test was used post hoc.

Results

Optimisation, sensitivity and specificity of the qPCR assay

A qPCR protocol was developed to specifically quantify phage ETEP21B in faecal samples. Tenfold serial dilutions of phage ETEP21B DNA, ranging from 0.85 ng/µL to 8.5 fg/µL and corresponding respectively to 109 to 104 gene copies, were used to assess the sensitivity of the qPCR protocol. As summarized in Table 1, a positive signal was detected even at the lowest concentration of pure DNA tested (8.5 fg/µL, corresponding to 104 pfu/mL), with a Ct value of 25.3. Of the ten non-template controls, four produced signals before the reaction finished, with an average Ct value of 32.93 ± 0.75. This signal was due to primer dimer formation that was verified with the help of the melting curve analysis. Given the occasional late background amplification in non-template controls, and mixed faecal DNA carrying no target phage an operational limit of detection (LOD) threshold was defined to distinguish true positives from background. Samples with Ct values < 31 were classified as positive for ETEP21B, whereas samples with Ct values ≥ 31 were considered background amplification. For quantification, the limit of quantification (LOQ) was conservatively set to the lowest concentration of mixed infective phage particles that still yielded reproducible amplification (with low Ct variability), namely 102 pfu/mL (discussed more in-depth in the following paragraphs). The intra- and inter-run variability was calculated using the raw Ct values after amplification. These were between 0.03–3.26 % (median 0.71 %) and 0.2–2.7 % (median 0.8 %) for the intra- and the inter-run variability respectively.

The specificity of the designed primers was tested against genomic DNA isolated from the enterotoxigenic E. coli strain ET10. Results showed that the developed qPCR assay was specific for the targeted phage, since no off-target amplification was noticed even when ETEP21B DNA (85 fg/µL phage DNA) was mixed with abundant ET10 genomic DNA (0.17 ng/µL bacterial DNA).

Because the presence of other phages and the low copy number of phage ETEP21B may hinder accurate quantification, we evaluated the efficiency of the DNA isolation method by mixing phage ETEP21B with coliphage CBA120 at ratios ranging from 1:1 (50 % ETEP21B) to 1:107 (0.000001 % ETEP21B). Plaque forming units (pfu/mL) from ETEP21B in the mixes were calculated (“Expected ETEP21B”) and counted (“Measured ETEP21B”) by double-layer agar spot assays using ECOR04 as host, which CBA120 does not infect. The qPCR run on the DNA extracted from the nine phage mixes demonstrated good reproducibility, with similar Ct values between technical replicates, and gene copies that were very close to the expected and measured counts of ETEP21B (Table 2). In addition, the ΔCt value between each mix and the following one is in agreement with the expected value of 3.32 since a tenfold dilution was made, i.e. as for mixes 3 to 9. These results also confirmed that the DNA isolation protocol was sufficient to detect ETEP21B DNA, even at low concentrations and in the presence of an extensive viral background.

Phage ETEP21B detection and quantification in spiked faecal samples

To evaluate the developed qPCR protocol on a complex sample matrix, fresh faecal material from 3-week-old healthy piglets was collected, pooled, and spiked with infective ETEP21B phage. Two sets of faecal samples were spiked with known amounts of ETEP21B and then processed using either filtration (1F to 6F) or precipitation (1P to 6P), followed by DNA extraction. The flow-through of the filtered samples showed no lysis against the propagation host in the double-layer agar assay (assay’s limit of detection: 102 pfu/mL), indicating that filtration was sufficient to remove spiked phage particles from the viral-enriched fraction. The supernatant of the precipitated samples with the highest amount of spiked ETEP21B (9 × 107, 9 × 106, 9 × 103 pfu/mL) showed phage lysis (103 to 102 pfu/mL), implying a lower recovery rate compared to the filtered samples (Fig. 1A). Ct values were used to generate a calibration curve for both the filtered (R2 = 0.9957) and precipitated samples (R2 = 0.9916) (Fig. 1B). Both methods demonstrated a strong linear relationship between the spiked DNA concentration and the average Ct value within the spiked pfu range.

Results from faecal spiking after filtration or precipitation. A) Phages recovered and lost after filtration or precipitation compared to the initial stock. B) Calibration curves on spiked samples. The unspiked samples (1F, 1P) are omitted from the graphs since they contain no target DNA. However, the Ct value of these samples was significantly higher (31.01 ± 0.37 and 34.36 ± 2.32 for filtered and precipitated controls, respectively) than the Ct values of the sample spiked with the lowest amount of phage (6F and 6P).

In vivo phage delivery and tracking study

Given the reliability and specificity demonstrated in laboratory settings, we proceeded to use the qPCR protocol for ETEP21B detection and quantification in an in vivo experiment, involving 30 pigs. The animals were randomized into three groups of 10 pigs each and received: 1) base feed and a capsule with saline (Saline+base feed), 2) base feed and a capsule with the ETEC strain ET54 (ET54+basefeed), 3) base feed with microencapsulated ETEP21B and a capsule with ET54 (ET54+ETEP21B). Before transport to the experimental facility (PND 21), all pigs were free of haemolytic E. coli shedding and were clinically healthy. Immediately before inoculation (PND 26), five pigs across groups showed symptoms of diarrhoea, but with no other remarks on their clinical condition and no haemolytic E. coli in the faeces (Fig. 2). Within 24 h after inoculation with ET54, three pigs from the ET54+basefeed group and four pigs from the ET54+ETEP21B group developed ETEC F4 diarrhoea (Fig. 2). Within 4 days post-inoculation, all pigs in ET54+basefeed and ET54+ETEP21B had ongoing or previous ETEC F4-mediated diarrhoea. Three pigs in ET54+basefeed and two pigs in ET54+ETEP21B were euthanized for humane reasons due to severe diarrhoea and signs of dehydration. Pigs in the saline control did not develop ETEC diarrhoea, remained clinically healthy throughout the study and did not shed any haemolytic E. coli at any timepoint (Fig. 2). An additional ETEC F18 strain was recovered only in the ET54+basefeed group, which was first detected in culture at 1-day post-inoculation (Fig. 2). The ETEC F18 strain was confirmed to be a herd specific strain by comparison with ETEC F18 strains previously isolated at the nursery of the herd.

A) Diarrhoea and ETEC diarrhoea development in individual pigs, and B) shedding of haemolytic E. coli alongside results from multiplex PCR on recovery of fimbriae genes from each pig.

Results from MLVA confirmed that all ETEC F4 strains isolated from the inoculated groups were identical to the challenge strain ET54 (data not shown). All recovered strains remained sensitive to ETEP21B. Comparable levels of F4 copies were detected by qPCR in the faeces of the groups ET54+basefeed and ET54+ETEP21B after inoculation, with no significant difference between the two groups at any day (Fig. 3). After inoculation, the average daily gain (ADG) in the Saline+basefeed group (mean ± SD: 189.3 ± 89.5 g) was significantly different from the two inoculated groups (ET54+basefeed: −6.8 ± 77.8 g, p = 0.0001, and ET54+ETEP21B: 27.34 ± 101.5 g, p = 0.001). There was no significant difference in ADG after inoculation between ET54+ETEP21B and ET54+basefeed.

Mean log10+1 transformed F4 gene copies/g faeces between the three experimental groups from baseline sample before inoculation with ETEC F4 until 6 days post inoculation.

Tracking of the phage product with direct spotting was not successful, as direct spotting of faecal samples on ET54 lawns did not show any lysis plaque, possibly because of interference from other bacteria and inhibitors present in the faecal samples. The qPCR protocol, instead, was sensitive enough to detect ETEP21B. Only the feed with added ETEP21B tested positive for the presence of ETEP21B, with a pre-trial quantification of 4.5 × 108 pfu/kg feed. By testing the faecal samples during the trial, we observed that within 6 days of inoculation with the ETEC strain, the pigs in the phage-treated group shed the phage product (Table 3). Similarly to the laboratory conditions, the titre of the phage product showed a 1-log reduction over a month during the trial.

Discussion

Phages are increasingly recognized as promising tools to face the antibiotic crisis and the emergence of multidrug resistant bacteria. In animal production, phages may serve as disinfectants, biocontrol agents or direct therapeutic agents, thus being administered directly to the animals suffering from the bacterial infection (Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023). Despite encouraging results obtained in vitro against a range of animal pathogens, successful translation to field-applicable products remains difficult (Gambino & Brøndsted, 2021). Our work contributes to this process by focusing on the detection, quantification and in vivo behaviour of the ETEC phage ETEP21B, a promising candidate for porcine PWD control (Gambino et al., 2024).

Specifically, the primary aim of this research work was to develop and validate a qPCR assay for ETEP21B both in vitro and in vivo, in order to use it for phage therapy development. The qPCR assay developed for phage ETEP21B was highly sensitive, specific, and robust in complex matrices, and despite successful gastrointestinal passage of encapsulated phages, no therapeutic effect was observed in pigs challenged with ETEC F4. Together, these results underline the value of precise molecular tools for phage monitoring and the substantial challenges of achieving in vivo efficacy in phage-based interventions for PWD.

Reliable quantification is essential for evaluating phage delivery, persistence, and efficacy. While the double layer agar assays remain the gold standard for counting infectious particles, they are time-consuming and dependent on clear plaque formation (Ács et al., 2020; Paranos et al., 2024). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) offers rapid, high-throughput detection of viral genomes and is increasingly used for quality control of phage preparations and monitoring during clinical trials (e.g., Duyvejonck et al., 2019; Luo et al., 2022; Spiegelhauer et al., 2025). Our qPCR assay demonstrated excellent specificity, even in the presence of bacterial DNA, the complex of a faecal matrix, and high background levels of other phages.

Primer design is critical for a reliable qPCR assay (Refardt, 2012). False positives can arise from similarities with bacterial DNA, such as prophages encoded in enterotoxigenic E. coli genomic DNA, but also from similar phages already present where the phage product will be used. Therefore, we further confirmed specificity by testing the qPCR assay in the presence of ETEC ET10 DNA, the unrelated coliphage CBA120, and phage-rich pig faecal samples, which carry a substantial viral background, including other phages infecting E. coli (Lin et al., 2021). We also verified that qPCR performance was not hindered by common PCR such as complex polysaccharides (Monteiro et al., 1997; Hedman, 2011), as demonstrated by consistent detection of spiked phages in faecal samples.

As expected for molecular assays, qPCR quantification reflects total genome copies and may overestimate viable phages due to free DNA or defective particles. Although applying a correction coefficient is possible in specific contexts, its usefulness is limited because the relationship between infective particles (as measured by the double-layer agar assay) and genome copies (as measured by qPCR) varies among phages, taxonomic groups, and sample types (Kralik & Ricchi, 2017; Duyvejonck et al., 2019). This limitation underscores the value of combining molecular detection with infectivity-based assays whenever feasible.

In vivo, all pigs receiving the phage-supplemented diet shed detectable ETEP21B, confirming that the encapsulated formulation successfully delivered phage through pig gastrointestinal tract. However, no increase of phage titres was observed and no reduction in the proportion of pigs with ETEC F4 diarrhoea or in the amount of shed F4 copies per gram of faeces was observed, suggesting that the phage might have not replicated in vivo or exerted a therapeutic effect. Multiple reasons may explain this lack of efficacy (Gambino & Brøndsted, 2021). Effective phage therapy requires phages to remain stable, reach the target site (small intestine for ETEC) and encounter their bacterial target at sufficient densities to initiate productive replication (Smart et al., 2014). Several encapsulation and delivery methods have been developed to improve phage stability and resistance (Yang et al., 2023) and here we administered encapsulated phages orally with the feed that passed through the pigs' gastrointestinal tract, as shown by qPCR. The administered dose or release kinetics of the encapsulated formulation may therefore have been insufficient for productive phage replication. In this regards, a limit of the qPCR assay is the impossibility of verify if the phage is still encapsulated or no longer infectious. In addition, due of the structural complexity of the gut and the presence of mucosa protecting pathogens (Hansson, 2012; Von Mentzer et al., 2014), phages may have fewer opportunities to encounter ETEC once they bind to the intestinal epithelium (Kasman, 2005). Another potential explanation is the emergence of resistance, a common limitation of phage therapy. Bacteria can often evade infection by modifying or masking phage receptors. Although a cocktail of multiple phages can mitigate the risk (Oechslin, 2018), they also increase production complexity and cost (Abedon, 2025) and they may exhibit competitive interference. In addition, previous in vivo studies where phages were used against PWD reported better performance of single phages with high titre than combinations of two or three phages with low titre, suggesting that phage quantity might be more relevant than diversity for a therapeutic effect (Jamalludeen et al., 2009). In fact, in our study, only one phage was included in the product, and yet the re-isolated ETEC remained sensitive to ETEP21B, ruling out phage resistance as an explanation for the lack efficacy. Beyond physical barriers, previous research works found that phage performance may be further influenced by interactions with the host immune system, resident virome, and bacteriome with their prophages (Zuppi et al., 2022; Godsil et al., 2025). As in this research project, no data to test these potential explanatory mechanisms is available, further research is needed to verify if the immune system, virome or bacteriome could have restricted phage persistence and activity. Phages adapted to mucosa or capable of binding mucin with capsid decoration proteins might help overcome this constraints in future formulations (Coelho et al., 2025). The interpretation of our results is also limited by not being able to calculate the actual intake of phages per pig, as that would have required individual housing and measuring weight of feed before and after each feeding. Research studies aiming to optimise phage dosage and formulations can greatly benefit from monitoring the actual intake per pig to detect under-dosing.

In conclusion, we developed and validated a sensitive and specific qPCR assay capable of accurately detecting ETEC phage ETEP21B in complex matrices and demonstrated its utility for monitoring phage survival through the pig intestinal tract under in vivo conditions. Although no therapeutic effect was observed, the molecular data generated provide important insight into phage fate, informing how future formulations may be optimised for effective delivery and replication. Integrating precise phage-tracking tools, such as the qPCR assay presented here, with improved formulation and delivery strategies will be essential for advancing phage-based interventions against PWD.
