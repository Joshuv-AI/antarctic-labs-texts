Working hours influence preferences of residents for micro features of small urban green spaces
Authors: Lei Wang, Lina Shen, Huajun Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates whether duration of working hours affect residents’ preferences for the micro-features of Small Urban Green Spaces (SUGS), and explores differences across genders and visitation purposes. Little is known about this subject, yet it is crucial for creating more equitable green spaces. In phase 1, participants (n = 209; 30.9 ± 6.73 years, 59.3%male) were categorized by average daily working hours (0–8 h, 8–10 h, 10 h+), and rated 41 micro-feature images on their importance for visiting SUGS. Phase 2 included the top ten features ranked from phase 1, with participants (n = 256; 30.8 ± 5.12 years, 35.2% female) completing the Kano model survey to identify and explain the importance of features and different types of needs. Findings reveal a clear trend: extended work hours pivot resident preferences towards green space attributes promoting physical activity. For residents of 0–8 h valued aesthetic elements like sculptures and vegetation, while lighting became paramount for those with 8–10 h, and for those working over 10 h prioritized facilities for active engagement, such as playground equipment and slow runway. These distinctions provide valuable guidance for designing SUGS with diverse feature combinations that cater to the needs of residential areas with varying socio-economic backgrounds and occupational lifestyles, thereby enhancing urban livability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74607-x.Keywords: Small urban green spaces, Microscopic feature, Working hours, Equity, HealthSubject terms: Human behaviour, Psychology and behaviour, Sustainability

Introduction

Working hours can impact the physical and mental health of workers1–3. Overemployment (working hours exceeding expectations) exacerbates the emergence of health problems, as increased working hours lead to reduced leisure time for workers and an increase in mental stress, thereby deteriorating their overall well-being4–7. Underemployment (working hours less than expected) also inflicts harm on the health and well-being of workers8,9. Regarding working hours, Chinese labor law stipulates that the daily working hours for workers should not exceed 8 h, and the average weekly working hours should not exceed 44 h. However, the phenomenon of overtime work (overtime refers to working beyond the stipulated working hours) is quite serious in China, resulting in excessively long working hours for workers10. The reasons for this include industry demands, work pressure, or personal choice. As China’s urbanization process continues to advance, by the end of 2022, 65.22% of the population lived in urban areas, with urban employees accounting for about 62% of the total employed population, indicating that the majority of Chinese workers are concentrated in cities. Since the phenomenon of overtime work predominantly occurs in urban areas, and urban residents experience more harmful environmental exposures, attentional demands, and sources of stress compared to suburban/rural residents11, the need for urban health protection for residents might be greater than in other areas. Cities need to address the physical and mental health issues caused by working hours for residents, fully considering the different needs created by varying working hours.

One of the responsibilities of urban planning is to mitigate the negative impacts of “urbanization” on human well-being, with urban green spaces playing a pivotal role in achieving this objective (hereinafter referred to as UGS). UGS refers to green open spaces in urban landscapes, including public parks, forests, grasslands, and more. Research has demonstrated that UGS offer multiple benefits, including improving residents’ well-being, providing recreational opportunities, promoting physical and mental health12–14, and enhancing the quality of urban life15. Specific benefits include reducing negative emotions, aiding in fatigue recovery, and decreasing anxiety and depression16. Additionally, urbanization is accompanied by increased inequality17,18, and cities mitigate this through the provision of key social infrastructure, with UGS being a crucial component. UGS can offset some aspects of inequality by improving place attachment, neighborhood satisfaction, and social cohesion19, thereby mediating social conflicts. However, UGS themselves often embody inequalities in quality and quantity due to factors like historical land use, sociopolitical factors, social welfare, and the real estate market economy20. Unequal distribution of UGS can lead to unfair allocation of resources and environmental benefits, including restricted access to UGS for certain communities21, where some communities may enjoy more green space resources while others face limitations. Some communities exhibit variations in residents’ socioeconomic backgrounds and working conditions, necessitating diversity in the characteristics of green spaces to meet their needs, although spatial limitations restrict the fulfillment of these diverse requirements. Conversely, some communities are characterized by homogeneity in socioeconomic backgrounds and employment conditions. For instance, residential areas near labor-intensive factories or specialized industrial parks typically house a concentration of ordinary workers, while managerial staff may opt for more desirable living locations. Additionally, factors such as local housing prices set specific thresholds for most residential communities, influencing the social composition of these communities and leading to more homogeneous resident profiles. High housing prices often make it difficult for low-income families to reside in these areas, thus homogenizing the community in terms of income, occupation, and educational levels. This phenomenon occurs not only within cities but also between urban and suburban areas22. Providing equitable green spaces is essential for just cities, and while most research and solutions to green space inequality focus on spatial elements, some studies address historical and contemporary sociopolitical processes23,24. However, research from the perspective of UGS design features is relatively scarce.

The demand for UGS is multifunctional, with residents’ choices regarding UGS linked to personal needs, the quality of green spaces, and accessibility25. Constrained by urban spaces, the creation and maintenance of UGS depend on urban residents’ preferences for its features. Understanding the preferences of urban residents for UGS features can encourage their entrance into green spaces and assist in the planning and decision-making of urban landscapes26, which is crucial for achieving equity in green spaces. While the spatial distribution and density of UGS, as macro elements, have been widely studied27, research on the micro-features is relatively scarce, and few studies originate from Asia28. Micro-features of green spaces refer to the specific element attributes of green spaces, including vegetative landscapes (e.g., fountains, trees, flower beds) and amenities (e.g., restrooms, trash bins). Compared to modifying the macro features of green spaces, adjustments to micro-features may have a more significant and cost-effective impact29.

UGS regardless of their type and size, play a crucial role in enhancing the quality of life for city residents and serve complementary functions30. However, large urban green spaces (LUGS) and small urban green spaces (SUGS) differ in their functional structure, service radius, and resident preferences31, and their access methods also vary32. LUGS, due to their size, typically provide a broader range of ecological services and recreational opportunities and attract a wider audience. However, due to the limited availability of space and the trend towards urban densification, resources for large urban green spaces are restricted33, and their accessibility involves various modes of transportation such as driving, cycling, and public transit. SUGS, being smaller, are better integrated into the urban fabric and located near residential areas34, with access primarily by walking or cycling32. This difference in access methods may lead to different usage patterns and benefits; LUGS provide more ecological and recreational services but require more effort and resources to reach, especially for those living farther away35. Thus, in addition to physical distance and modes of transportation, socio-economic factors also significantly influence different groups’ access to green spaces; for instance, economically weaker communities may lack the means to reach distant large green spaces, while higher-income groups may more frequently use private vehicles to access these areas. In contrast, SUGS play a significant role in enhancing residents’ opportunities to access green spaces, which is crucial for the sustainability of daily urban life and resident well-being. Moreover, LUGS and SUGS have different connections to visitors’ psychological health, and the types of activities, frequency of use, and motivations also vary33. People tend to focus more on the visual features of LUGS, while for SUGS, user characteristics are a more important consideration34.

In summary, this study investigates whether changes in working hours affect residents’ feature preferences for SUGS and explores the relative importance of SUGS micro-features. This is crucial for building a more equitable city, increasing residents’ opportunities to access green spaces, and maintaining their physical and mental health. The study employed multiple methods, including rating photographic images of SUGS micro-features and the Kano model. Originating from the field of quality management, the Kano model is a tool for analyzing customer needs and satisfaction. It provides a multi-dimensional and dynamic analysis, emphasizes emotional experiences, scientifically reveals the impact of features on satisfaction, and offers clear evidence for decision-making36. It represents a novel and effective method for assessing the relative importance of green space features for urban residents with different work hour backgrounds.

Materials and methods

The study was divided into two main phases. In phase 1 (June 2023), an online survey was conducted, targeting residents with at least one year of urban work experience. Respondents were categorized into three groups based on their average daily working hours: 0–8 h, 8–10 h, and 10 h+. They rated a series of digital landscape photographs according to their preferences for SUGS micro-features. Additionally, respondents were asked to choose their primary purpose for visiting green spaces among social interaction, sports activities, and green space visits. Phase 2 (November 2023) involved an online survey incorporating the highest-rated features from each group in phase 1 into Kano model tasks. This provided us with a more detailed perspective to understand the relative importance of micro-features under different working hour regimes, highlighting participants’ functional preferences and emotional needs. Surveys for each phase were conducted both online and offline. All participants signed informed consent before the start of this investigation. All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Northwestern University (240103001).

Phase 1

Participant recruitment

Data were collected through the Credamo online survey platform (Creator of Data and Model, www.credamo.com), where participants were compensated to complete an online survey on the micro-features of SUGS. A study description and informed consent form were attached at the beginning of the survey. The Credamo platform is a reliable Chinese data collection platform that has been used in research experiments across multiple fields37. To ensure participants had sufficient work experience, thereby enhancing the reliability and applicability of the experiment results, participant ages were controlled between 20 and 55 years (according to the Labor Law of the People’s Republic of China, the retirement age for women is 55 years, and for men, it is 60 years). Participants worked in the secondary or tertiary sectors and had been engaged in paid employment activities for at least 2 continuous years. A total of 272 participants were recruited, with 209 completing the survey and providing valid results (invalid questionnaires were excluded based on 63 instances. The criteria for excluding invalid questionnaires were: using pre-survey data to determine the range of times for completing the questionnaire, then excluding responses in the official survey that were below the minimum completion time; and excluding questionnaires with obvious patterns of responses).

Online survey approach

Digital images of micro-features were collected using the Baidu Maps open platform (http://lbsyun.baidu.com) combined with offline real-scene photography. The Natural Environment Scoring Tool (NEST)38, which includes 8 domains and 47 items, was utilized to filter the digital images to ensure each image prominently displays a specific micro-feature. As a newly proposed tool, it is based on a comprehensive review of existing tools, emphasizing human preferences and perceptions of nature and considering how the use of urban parks may be affected by functionality, facilities, environmental maintenance, and natural quality33. After screening, a total of 41 digital images were obtained and imported into Adobe Photoshop for standardized processing (feature classification and images are presented in “File S1”).

When assigning ratings, participants were asked to rate each micro-feature on a 10-point scale (1–10, where 1 indicates dislike of the feature and 10 indicates liking), and were categorized into three groups (0–8 h, 8–10 h, and 10 h+). In addition, participants were to complete items on statistics including their primary purpose for visiting SUGS, which encompasses green space visits (for leisure and relaxation), social interaction, and sports activities. For descriptive purposes, participants were asked to complete a demographic survey, including: age; gender; nationality; highest level of education; residential address; average number of working days per week; average daily working hours; frequency of visits to nearby green spaces per week.

Data analysis

Stata/SE 15.0 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA) and Microsoft Excel 2019 were used to determine feature ratings, perform descriptive analyses, and explore the relationship between working hours and visitation purposes. The average scores and standard deviations for the SUGS features were calculated separately for the three groups and further differentiated by overall, male, and female categories. The features were ranked from 1 to 41 (1 representing the highest average score).

Phase 2

Participant recruitment

The Kano model was utilized to further explore the popular features of SUGS. The study recruited new participants using the same data collection platform as in phase 1. Participant criteria were controlled to be the same as in phase 1, with a study description and informed consent form attached at the beginning of the questionnaire and screening thereafter. Ultimately, 303 participants were recruited, with 256 completing the survey and producing valid results (after excluding 47 invalid questionnaires).

Online survey approach

Participants completed a survey designed based on Kano analysis research to further determine the importance of needs regarding SUGS micro-features. Kano et al. (1984) demonstrated that any product or service exhibits multiple attributes of quality. This led to the development of the Kano model for validation, utilizing standardized questionnaires for data collection39. The model introduces the theory of attractive quality, explaining how different attributes affect customers and classifying and understanding different quality attributes36. Based on user satisfaction with a feature’s presence and the degree to which a feature is provided, the Kano model categorizes user needs into five specific types of attributes: Attractive Quality (A), One-dimensional Quality (O), Must-be Quality (M), Indifferent Quality (I), and Reverse Quality (R).

In the Kano questionnaire, the assessment of each green space feature consists of two key dimensions: user satisfaction (US) when the feature is present and US when the feature is absent. US is further divided into five levels: like (high US) indicating the feature exceeds expectations; expected (higher US) meaning the feature is seen as a standard requirement; neutral indicating participants have a neutral attitude towards the presence or absence of the feature, tolerable (lower US) suggesting that the absence of the feature would not lead to extreme dissatisfaction, and dislike (low SD) indicating that the absence of the feature would cause significant dissatisfaction. For features included in phase 2, they were visually processed through Adobe Photoshop to control and only change a single feature in the image, thus accurately highlighting the visual and perceptual differences when the feature is present and absent. The visualization strategy aims to eliminate other potential interfering factors, allowing participants to focus on assessing the impact of a single feature, thereby providing more accurate and intuitive feedback on US. For instance, features such as “barbecue area” and “fitness equipment” set two contrasting conditions: “with this feature” and “without this feature,” the latter implying the feature is either completely absent or almost lacks this feature. Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were completed using Stata/SE 15.0 (Stata Corp LP, College Station, TX, USA) and Microsoft Excel 2019, while Kano model statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 27 (Statistical Product and Service Solutions 27). The traditional Kano model, when categorizing product features, usually aggregates various statistical indicators and corresponds them to a single evaluation scale. However, when statistical values from different categories are close to each other, there can be ambiguity in the impact of features on user satisfaction (US). This approach may lead to errors in the classification of product attribute properties, thereby affecting the decision-making process for product development and improvement.

Based on the Better-Worse coefficient proposed by Berger et al. (1993)40, different types of needs and their impact on US can be further identified and explained. Better measures the degree to which US is increased by providing a certain product feature, with a value closer to 1 indicating a greater positive effect. Worse represents the degree to which US is decreased by not providing a certain feature, with a value closer to -1 indicating a greater negative impact. This method reduces the classification disadvantages of the original Kano model, making the division of requirement attributes more precise and rigorous41. 0–8 H

Overall, for residents with an average daily work time of 0–8 h, the primary purpose of visiting SUGS is “Social interaction”. The top five micro-features encouraging their visit to SUGS are: trees and shrubs; shade; grassy open space; wildlife; paths and trails (Table 4). Further observations by grouping the top ten features reveal significant gender preferences differences for shelter/shade − man-made (ranked third by men, twenty-fourth by women), teahouse/cafe/shop (twenty-third by men, seventh by women), sculptures and art installations (twenty-eighth by men, sixth by women), and structures (eighteenth by men, third by women). The three lowest-rated features are open plazas, pet facilities, and trash bins.

8–10 H

For residents with 8–10 h, the main purpose of visiting SUGS is “Green spaces visitation”. The top five features they rated highest include: wildlife; picnic table; grassy open space; public restroom; shade (Table 4). When observing the top ten features by gender grouping, there are differences in preferences for shade (ranked first by men, twenty-fourth by women), lighting design (thirteenth by men, second by women), and landscape maintenance (seventh by men, sixteenth by women), although the gaps in preferences between men and women for visiting green spaces are smaller compared to those for other purposes. The three lowest-rated features are open plazas, safety facilities, and car parking areas.

10 h+

For residents of 10 h+, the primary purpose of visiting SUGS is “Physical activity”. The top five features that encourage their visit are: slow runway; playground equipment; wildlife; skateboarding area; water area (Table 4). When analyzing the top ten features by gender grouping, there are significant differences in preferences for some features, such as playground equipment (ranked first by men, twenty-ninth by women), water dispenser (second by men, thirty-third by women), skateboarding area (fourth by men, thirtieth by women), and water sports equipment (twenty-second by men, second by women). Overall, the top ten features match well with physical activity (the main purpose of visit). The three least appealing features are safety facilities, trash bins, and entrance points.

In classifying the importance of features, the following hierarchy is adhered to: M > O > A > I.

For both men and women (Fig. 2), wildlife represents an indifferent characteristic, whereas grassy open spaces are perceived as attractive by both genders. Other features reveal gender-specific differences in perceived importance. Men find structures to be attractive, while women are generally indifferent to them. Man-made shelter/shade is viewed as an expected characteristic by men but is indifferent to women. Women place greater importance on trees and shrubs, teahouses/cafes/shops, paths and trails, as well as sculptures and art installations. 10 h+

On the whole, for residents working of 10 h+, the most crucial features are structures, water area, playground equipment, and slow runway (as presented in Table 7). Regarding both genders (Fig. 4), only structures and playground equipment are considered one-dimensional quality. However, the significance of other features varies between genders. Trees and shrubs and outdoor fitness equipment are deemed must-be quality for men; whereas for women, they exhibit indifference towards them. The importance of water sports equipment, skateboarding area, and wildlife is higher among women compared to men; conversely, slow runway, water area, and water dispenser hold more significance for men than women.

Discussion

This study investigated the relative significance of micro-features within green spaces in encouraging residents with varying average daily work hours (0–8 h, 8–10 h, 10 h+) to visit SUGS and explored the correlation between residents’ average daily work hours and their primary motives for visiting SUGS, which include social interaction, green space visitation, and physical activity. To our knowledge, this is the first time the Kano model has been applied to study the relationship between green space features and residents’ work schedule regimes. Investigating green space features to promote residents’ visits to the green spaces around their homes is novel. The findings can assist stakeholders understand which micro-level features should be present in the green spaces around homes, thereby further ensuring the equitable distribution of urban green spaces.

We found that the primary reason for residents of 0–8 h to visit nearby green spaces is social interaction; for 8–10 h, it is the visitation of green spaces; and, surprisingly, for residents of 10 h+, it is physical activity. The most important features for each of these groups correspond to their respective visiting purposes.

Natural feature elements are crucial in encouraging residents of 0–8 h and 8–10 h to visit nearby green spaces, often manifesting as attractive and must-be characteristics. Green spaces should emphasize the presence of these features, which significantly aids in increasing residents’ visits to green areas, such as grassy open space and shade. Large green open spaces are vital functions of green areas because they can accommodate various activities. Studies have also shown their importance for youth and elderly visits to green spaces42,43, especially critical in the urban environments of Asian cities29. In highly urbanized environments, green spaces become appealing destinations by providing opportunities for contact with nature44,45. The importance of natural feature elements also explains why landscape maintenance (one-dimensional quality) is one of the most crucial features for 8–10 h residents visiting green spaces. Proper maintenance not only enhances the landscape’s aesthetic appeal but also increases the safety and comfort of public spaces.

For residents of 0–8 h, it is interesting to note that the most crucial features, trees and shrubs, and shade, are corresponding and both considered must-be quality. Shade, along with the equally effective non-natural element Shelter/shade-man-made, holds paramount importance for individuals. Wildlife appears as a desired feature across all three groups but is an indifferent characteristic for 0–8 h and 8–10 h residents, while it emerges as an attractive characteristic for residents working more than 10 h, especially for women. The demand for Trees and shrubs by residents may indirectly increase the demand for wildlife, both contributing to a more serene and relaxing environment. A survey targeting the elderly visiting parks showed that birds are a significant feature for park-related physical activities42.

In non-natural elements, lighting design is most crucial for residents of 8–10 h when visiting nearby green spaces, classified as an indispensable element. This importance can be attributed to extended working hours, with visits to green spaces concentrated in the evenings or at night. Research indicates that nighttime lighting can improve spatial cognition and utilization, reducing the perceptual differences between day and night, thereby enhancing the efficiency of use and satisfaction with urban green spaces46. Additionally, lighting can reduce potential safety risks in the dark, lowering the crime rate and making people feel more secure when using public green spaces at night47. Furthermore, considering that the primary purpose for residents of 8–10 h visiting green spaces is to view these areas, lighting design can also enhance the aesthetic appeal of SUGS to some extent. In cases where lighting design has an impact, future research could further explore other attributes of lighting, such as color and brightness. For residents of 0–8 h, non-natural elements such as sculptures and art installations are among the most important features, meaningful for social interaction. Artworks can serve as focal points for community gathering and interaction, where participatory art can increase social engagement and participation. A study indicated that residents’ satisfaction with urban green spaces is associated with physical perception, aesthetic cognition, and psychological cognition48. Additionally, features such as paths and trails, bicycle lanes, and slow runways explain their importance for residents who access SUGS located near residential areas mainly by walking and cycling49. These features are particularly significant for residents active for 0–8 h, 8–10 h, and over 10 h+, respectively, facilitating easy access and promoting physical activity in these green spaces.

For residents of 10 h+, the demand for non-natural features exceeds that for natural features, with water area being an exception. This heightened demand for non-natural features may stem from their primary purpose of visiting green spaces being physical activity. The expectation and importance of features such as slow runways and playground equipment underline this focus, further evidenced by the need for water dispensers. Surprisingly, as work hours increase, residents show a growing demand for engaging in physical activities in green spaces. For residents of 0–8 h, leisure and scenic features dominate their needs, with paths and trails being the most significant activity feature. Residents of 8–10 h still prioritize aesthetically pleasing and leisure-oriented features but show an increased amount of physical activity, such as barbecue areas and picnic tables, with bicycle lanes being the most significant activity feature. For residents of 10 h+, the features related to physical activities, including outdoor fitness equipment, slow runways, and skateboarding areas, see a significant increase. The interest in physical activities might be due to their potential positive effects on reducing fatigue50, with findings supporting the effectiveness of physical activities in improving mental health conditions51. Water areas, often providing beautiful natural scenery, may attract people to engage in various activities outdoors, like walking, jogging, and cycling. Additionally, water environments are considered beneficial for psychological health, potentially increasing the motivation to participate in outdoor activities52–55. Long hours of work can lead to negative emotions such as fatigue and anxiety2, making it important and necessary to consider the inclusion of Water areas and the construction of sports facilities like Slow runways in green space design.

Interestingly, across all three work-time intervals, men tend to have a greater demand for non-natural features compared to women, who overall have lower expectations for such features. However, the non-natural feature of public restrooms shows a significant opposite trend, likely due to physiological and socio-cultural factors affecting women. A YouGov UK survey56 notes that women require more toilets than men, due to time-consuming factors related to attire, menstruation, and anatomical differences. Furthermore, features with leisure attributes and aesthetic appeal, such as teahouses/cafes/shops and sculptures and art installations in the 0–8 h interval, and lighting design in the 8–10 h interval, show more attraction towards women. For individuals working more than 10 h, significant gender disparities are observed in preferences for green space features. Men demonstrate a preference for features such as outdoor fitness equipment and slow runways, whereas women show a greater inclination towards water sports equipment and skateboarding areas. This divergence may also stem from the primary purpose of visiting being physical activity, where physiological differences between genders lead to varying preferences in sports activities. Interestingly, playground equipment is an expected feature for both men and women, and some studies have similarly identified it as one of the key features encouraging park visitation43,57,58. Therefore, for landscape architects and urban planners, it is essential to consider the quantity and combination of various features judiciously. Subgroup analysis is crucial for understanding the needs of different groups, aiding in the design and planning process to cater effectively to diverse requirements.

In addition, considering the importance of feature combinations is crucial for communities with diverse resident compositions. For neighborhoods where there are significant differences in residents’ socioeconomic backgrounds and working conditions, the selection of essential features for each subgroup should be carefully considered. Even with limited space, SUGS surrounding these areas must strategically integrate must-be features from each work time interval to adequately serve the needs of all residents. Conversely, in communities where residents share similar socioeconomic and working conditions, it is important to prioritize must-be and one-dimensional features relevant to their specific work time intervals, along with a select few must-be features from other intervals, to ensure that green spaces meet the community’s overall needs effectively.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the relationship between work hours and preferences for micro-features in SUGS within China. The research encompasses a diverse sample of urban residents across various work durations, providing a comprehensive reflection of how the length of work hours influences preferences for specific features. However, future research should aim for a more detailed segmentation of work hours, distinguishing overtime lengths, as excessive work (prolonged work hours) can lead to acute physiological responses, potentially causing fatigue, psychosomatic stress, and other health issues. Moreover, it can decrease work performance and lead to productivity losses due to illness and occupational injuries6,59,60. The intensity of work should also be taken into consideration, with a detailed classification according to job categories.

In phase 1, the selection of micro-features for SUGS adhered to NEST, while also considering the importance of additional features, which were presented through digital graphics. Phase 2 further utilized digitally processed images to demonstrate changes in scenery. A limitation of using digital images is that participants’ reactions to these images may differ from their experiences in actual environments. Additionally, adhering to the NEST for pre-selecting images might overlook some of the users’ actual preferences. Despite these limitations, using digital imagery represents an effective method to assess residents’ recognition of green space features61, and landscape space photographs have been proven to effectively replace on-site environmental surveys62. Similar methods have been used in studies like Veitch et al.‘s research on park features that attract the elderly42 and Rivera et al.‘s research on important park features that encourage youth to visit parks for sports and social activities43. This approach eliminates the need for participants to physically visit green spaces, thereby facilitating a controlled comparison free from the influence of external conditions such as weather and air quality63,64. Although digital imagery is an effective method, exploring the combination of Virtual Reality (VR), Electroencephalography (EEG), and eye-tracking technologies in further research could be a viable approach to delve deeper into users’ actual micro-feature preferences and enhance the validity of the study. Current research indicates that VR systems are increasingly sophisticated in delivering immersive natural experiences that can evoke psychological responses similar to those experienced in real green environments, potentially offering health benefits akin to actual nature exposure65,66. Additionally, EEG has been used to decode cognitive states by analyzing brainwave patterns, offering insights into subjects’ underlying mental states without the need for verbal interaction67.

Phase 2 incorporates the Kano model to further understand participants’ satisfaction, distinguishing several types of needs. This approach enables a deeper comprehension of residents’ true expectations and the identification of key needs. Such differentiation is crucial for understanding the psychological shifts in green space preferences due to variations in work hours36. The presentation of features through a combination of text and images ensures participants can understand the depicted concepts. Participants’ evaluation of preselected feature functionalities is limited to two levels, presence or absence, acknowledging that other aspects, such as the quality of Grassy open spaces, may also be important. Future research should further explore the impact of other attributes of micro-features. Moreover, the survey primarily focuses on positive green space features, with less attention given to features that deter residents from visiting green spaces. Participants were predominantly from a Chinese socio-cultural background, where social factors significantly influence green space usage57,68. Consequently, whether the survey findings apply to residents from different social backgrounds remains to be explored. Relatedly, our study did not include stratified analyses based on socio-economic factors such as household income and ethnicity, nor did it further segment the working age groups. Incorporating these detailed analyses would enhance our understanding of how age, work intensity, and working hours collectively influence preferences for green space micro-features. Combining these detailed analyses and further considering participants’ psychological health and quality of life will enhance future research into how factors such as age, work intensity, and working hours collectively influence preferences for micro-features in green spaces.

Overall, this study provides insights into the importance of specific micro-features for the use of urban residential green spaces from different perspectives, highlighting the principle of equity in access to green spaces. This is crucial because features supporting residents with shorter work hours may not be important or as appealing to those with longer work hours. The research offers practical suggestions and references for the future planning and design of SUGS. Further studies should consider assessing participants’ psychological states and quality of life to more specifically investigate the impact of variations in work hours on residents’ preferences for green space features. Additionally, the study findings indicate that the micro-feature combinations considered relatively important are mainly applicable to the working-age group of 20–55 years old. Further exploration is needed to determine whether these micro-features are equally suitable for other types of users, such as children and the elderly. This would contribute to the creation of more equitable and just green spaces.

Conclusions

This study provides stakeholders with valuable insights, identifying the relative importance of green space features for residents with average daily work hours of 0–8 h, 8–10 h, and 10 h+, corresponding to the main purposes of visiting green spaces: social interaction, green spaces visitation, and physical activity. The survey results reveal that as work hours increase, residents’ preferences for green space features shift, with an increased demand for engaging in physical activities in green spaces. Thoughtfully considering the combination of features in green spaces, such as sculptures and art installations, shade, trees, lighting design, grassy open space, structures, playground equipment, and slow runways, to meet the needs and pr1eferences of residents with different lengths of working hours, may be a valuable strategy for the equitable and sensible allocation of features in Small Urban Green Spaces.
