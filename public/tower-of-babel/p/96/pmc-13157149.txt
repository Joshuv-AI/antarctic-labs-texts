Efficacy and Descriptive Safety Assessment of Programmed Cell Death Protein-(Ligand) 1 Inhibitors Plus Chemotherapy as First-Line Treatment for Gastric and Gastroesophageal Junction Cancer in Asian and Non-Asian Patients: A Meta-Analysis
Authors: Florian Lordick, Efrat Dotan, Lin Zhan, Xiaowei Zhang, Afsaneh Barzi, Nadeem Ghali, Keun-Wook Lee, Samuel J Klempner, Matthew R Strickland, Sara Lonardi, Taroh Satoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsProgrammed cell death protein-(ligand) 1 (PD-[L]1) inhibitors have improved survival outcomes for patients with advanced gastric cancer (GC) or gastroesophageal junction cancer (GEJC). Given the higher incidence of GC mortality in Asian vs non-Asian populations coupled with some differences in etiology and treatment, this meta-analysis compared survival outcomes associated with first-line (1L) PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy in Asian and non-Asian patients with advanced GC/GEJC.MethodsA systematic literature review was undertaken to identify randomized, phase III studies examining efficacy and safety of 1L PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus chemotherapy treatment in patients with advanced GC/GEJC. Hazard ratios (HRs) for overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were pooled using fixed and random effects models. Safety data were summarized descriptively.ResultsSeven studies (3669 Asian and 3104 non-Asian patients) were included; 76% of patients had GC, ∼40% were aged ≥65 years, and 71% were male. PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy improved OS (HR, 0.80; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.75–0.84) and PFS (HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.71–0.80) in the overall population. For the Asian and non-Asian regional breakdown, similar benefits were observed for OS (HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.73–0.85 and HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.74–0.89, respectively), with a comparable effect size for PFS (HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.64–0.76 and HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.70–0.89, respectively). Treatment-related adverse event rates were consistent across PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus chemotherapy (94%–98%) and placebo plus chemotherapy (92%–97%) groups.Conclusion1L PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy improved survival outcomes in patients with advanced GC/GEJC, regardless of Asian/non-Asian regional breakdown. These findings suggest that survival benefit is generalizable and independent of recruitment region.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Stomach Cancer, Ethnic Difference

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) and gastroesophageal junction cancer (GEJC) are the fifth most common malignancies worldwide, accounting for 968,784 new cases and 660,175 deaths in 2022, with disproportionate mortality burden in the Asian region (70%; n = 462,606).1 The most common histological type of GC/GEJC is adenocarcinoma, which accounts for ∼95% of all cases.2 GC/GEJC has a poor prognosis as it is often diagnosed at an advanced stage. In the United States, for example, around 36% of patients are found to have metastatic disease at presentation, with a 5-year survival of only 7% for patients with stage IV disease.3

In patients with advanced (unresectable or metastatic) human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)–negative GC/GEJC, the use of platinum and fluoropyrimidine doublet chemotherapy in combination with programmed cell death protein-(ligand) 1 (PD-[L]1) inhibitors is accepted as a standard of care worldwide. Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated improved survival in patients with GC/GEJC treated with PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy in the first-line (1L) setting.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 However, current regulatory guidance limits their use in advanced GC/GEJC to patients whose tumors express PD-L1 with a combined positive score (CPS) of ≥1.12 In the European Society for Medical Oncology guidelines, PD-(L)1 immune checkpoint inhibition plus chemotherapy is recommended for tumors expressing a PD-L1 CPS ≥1, and in the pan-Asian adapted European Society for Medical Oncology guidelines, PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus chemotherapy is recommended for tumors expressing PD-L1 CPS ≥5.13, 14, 15

These agents are now widely approved in many countries: pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy is approved in the United States, China, and Korea for all comers and in Europe for patients with tumor CPS ≥1,16, 17, 18, 19 and nivolumab plus chemotherapy is approved in the United States, Japan, Korea, China, and Taiwan for all comers and in Europe for patients with tumor CPS ≥5.20,21 Tislelizumab plus platinum- and fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy is approved for the 1L treatment of patients with locally advanced, unresectable or metastatic, HER2-negative GC/GEJC whose tumors express PD-L1 ≥1% (United States) or a Tumor Area Positivity score of ≥5% (Europe), and it is approved for all comers in China.22,23 Sugemalimab plus platinum- and fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy is approved for the 1L treatment of patients with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic GC/GEJC whose tumors express CPS ≥5 in China.24

However, it is important to assess whether PD-(L)1 inhibitors are consistently beneficial across different cohorts, for example, Asian and non-Asian populations. One meta-analysis showed a better survival benefit in Asian than the overall population,25 which may have been impacted by heterogeneity resulting from combining 1L and subsequent line (2L/2L+) studies. Another meta-analysis compared PD-(L)1 inhibitor efficacy among a broad spectrum of cancers. Based on 11,020 patients (19 randomized studies), this analysis demonstrated improved survival benefits in both Asian and non-Asian populations treated with PD-(L)1 inhibitors, but it only included 4 studies of 2 PD-(L)1 inhibitors for the treatment of GC.26 Other meta-analyses of PD-(L)1 inhibitors for GC have shown improved survival outcomes with the 1L combination of a larger number of different PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy, particularly in patients with higher PD-L1 scores.25,27,28 These meta-analyses were conducted using pooled studies that were designed for different patient populations. Given the heterogenous geographical distribution of GC/GEJC, as well as differences in the underlying etiology, diagnosis, and management between the Asian and non-Asian regions,29, 30, 31 it is important to explore any differences in outcomes between Asian vs non-Asian populations in contemporary studies dedicated to treatment-naïve patients with advanced GC/GEJC. Exploring outcomes in Asian vs non-Asian populations could also encourage regulatory agencies to consider data that originate from countries other than their own. This is also relevant in light of ongoing disparities in treatment access and testing.32

Although clinical evidence in the 1L treatment of GC/GEJC is well established, no meta-analysis has examined regional survival or safety differences within homogeneous chemoimmunotherapy cohorts based on race/ethnicity.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 To address this, we conducted a systematic literature review (SLR) and meta-analysis to assess the efficacy of PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy as 1L treatment for GC/GEJC by Asian/non-Asian regional breakdown. Safety data were extracted where available, but no statistical synthesis was performed for safety outcomes.

Methods

Search Strategy

An SLR was undertaken to identify studies examining the efficacy and safety of 1L PD-(L)1 inhibitor treatment plus chemotherapy in patients with GC/GEJC. The work was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, Centre for Reviews and Dissemination guidance and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Literature Reviews and Meta-analyses guidance.33, 34, 35 Database searches were performed from their inception to February 16, 2024, using the Ovid SP platform, which contains the following databases: MEDLINE (1946–2024), Embase (1974–2024), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (2024), and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (2005–2024). Additional manual searches were conducted to maximize the inclusion of all relevant studies; these included relevant conference proceedings (2022–2024), registry databases (including ClinicalTrials.gov), and health technology assessment agencies. The RATIONALE-30511 study was accepted for publication by February 16, 2024, and was included in the analysis.

The search strategy was developed through a peer-reviewed iterative process by combining free-text words, indexing terms (eg, Medical Subject Headings for MEDLINE), and study designs with Boolean operators (eg, “and,” “or,” and “not”) relevant to the disease area, interventions, and study design. The search was undertaken using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies checklist.36 This meta-analysis is based on previously published studies that were conducted in accordance with country-specific guidance and included patient informed consent. All data were anonymized.

Selection Criteria and Process

The Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study design framework was used to develop the eligibility criteria (Supplementary Table 1). The SLR included adult patients (≥18 years) with unresectable, locally advanced or metastatic HER2-negative GC/GEJC treated with 1L PD-(L)1 inhibitors in randomized, phase III studies. Records identified from the database searches were imported into EndNote X9 (Clarivate, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania), and duplicates were removed prior to exporting to the systematic review software (DistillerSR Evidence Partners, Ottawa, Canada) for study selection.37 Study selection was conducted by 2 independent reviewers who assessed eligibility based on the predefined Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study criteria. Reviewers documented their reasons for exclusion and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus or were referred to a third independent reviewer for resolution.

Data Extraction (End Points) and Quality Assessment

Data extraction from eligible studies was conducted using a standardized template in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Seattle, WA). If data were presented graphically, then manual calculations were performed by digitalizing the relevant curves using WebPlotDigitizer. Quality assessment of the included studies was performed using the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence single technology appraisal evidence submission checklist for assessment of risk of bias in randomized controlled studies.38 This checklist addresses 8 specific domains: randomization scheme, allocation concealment, balance of prognostic factors, blinding of patients/care providers/outcome assessors, imbalances in dropouts between groups, selective outcome reporting, intention-to-treat analysis, and handling of missing data. Data extraction and quality assessments were each performed by an independent reviewer and checked by a second reviewer. The presence of publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test.39,40

Statistical Analysis

Efficacy was measured using the hazard ratios (HRs) calculated in the models for overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) based on available study data. For studies where non-Asian regional breakdown data were not directly reported, HRs were recalculated through meta-analysis. Specifically, for CheckMate 649, KEYNOTE-062, and KEYNOTE-859, the HR of the non-Asian subgroup was recalculated by meta-analysis, while for RATIONALE-305, the HR was obtained from was obtained the longer follow-up analysis of the study.11 Only studies that included PD-(L)1 inhibitor treatment in combination with chemotherapy were included in analyses. Control (comparator) was placebo plus chemotherapy or chemotherapy alone; when studies had more than 1 treatment arm, they were compared against the same control arm separately. The Asian and non-Asian breakdown was based on geographical region and patient numbers (where available), as shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Pooled estimates of survival HRs in all patients and by Asian/non-Asian regional breakdown were calculated using R package meta (version 4.15.1).41 Between-study heterogeneity in the overall population and differences by Asian/non-Asian regional breakdown were estimated using the chi-square–based Q statistic. Heterogeneity was considered statistically significant when P < .05 or I2 > 50%. In the absence of heterogeneity, a fixed-effects model was used. If heterogeneity existed, data were analyzed using a random effects model. The difference in Asian and non-Asian regional breakdown was considered statistically significant when P < .05. A linear mixed-effects (LME) model was also applied that incorporated both fixed and random effects. In this model, region was treated as a fixed effect and used to describe the average treatment effect across Asian/non-Asian regional breakdown, and study was treated as a random effect to account for variations within different studies. Restricted maximum likelihood was used to estimate the parameters in the LME model.

Safety data were extracted (if available) for any treatment-related adverse events (TRAE), including whether they were grade ≥3 or resulted in treatment discontinuation, and immune-related AEs (irAEs), including grade ≥3 irAEs. No statistical synthesis was performed for safety data.

A sensitivity analysis was performed that excluded any studies in which PD-(L)1 inhibitor treatment was not superior to the comparator for the primary outcome. This was performed to identify and mitigate any confounding factors. For this analysis, studies that failed to achieve their statistical end points were considered a confounding factor, and they were excluded to determine if their removal would impact the results. A second sensitivity analysis was performed excluding the GEMSTONE-303 study, which enrolled only patients with PD-L1 CPS ≥5%, to assess whether the inclusion of a study with different patient selection criteria affected the overall results.

Results

Literature Search and Study Selection

Following screening of 5410 records, 83 were found to report on PD-(L)1 treatment, and 7 phase III, randomized studies that evaluated 1L PD-(L)1 inhibitor treatment plus chemotherapy vs a comparator (chemotherapy ± placebo) in 3669 Asian and 3104 non-Asian patients with GC/GEJC were identified (Supplementary Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 2).4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Three studies included patients from Asian regions only (ATTRACTION-4 [part 2], ORIENT-16, GEMSTONE-303) and 4 studies were global (RATIONALE-305, CheckMate 649, KEYNOTE-062, KEYNOTE-859) (Supplementary Table 2).4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 The baseline and clinical characteristics of patients enrolled in these studies are shown in Supplementary Table 3, and median survival outcomes are reported in Supplementary Table 4.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Overall, 76% of patients had GC, ∼40% were aged ≥65 years, and 71% were male. There was no significant evidence of overall publication bias, as shown by lack of funnel plot asymmetry (Supplementary Figure 2) and nonsignificant Egger’s test scores for OS (P = .511) and PFS (P = .165) analyses, respectively.

Overall Survival Benefits With First-Line Programmed Cell Death Protein-(Ligand) 1 Inhibitor Therapy

OS was estimated using data from 7 studies of 5 PD-(L)1 inhibitors (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, sintilimab, sugemalimab, tislelizumab) plus chemotherapy as 1L treatment in patients with GC/GEJC.

In the overall population, a PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus chemotherapy treatment effect was observed on OS (HR, 0.80; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.75–0.84), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = .83) (Figure 1A).4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Similar findings were observed in the Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.73–0.85, 7 studies) (Figure 1B) and the non-Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.74–0.89, 4 studies) (Figure 1C).4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Based on the LME model, the P value for region was .577, indicating that treatment effect on OS was not significantly different by Asian and non-Asian regional breakdown.

Progression-Free Survival Benefits With First-Line Programmed Cell Death Protein-(Ligand) 1 Inhibitor Therapy

In the overall population, a PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus chemotherapy treatment effect was observed on PFS (HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.70–0.80; 7 studies), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I2 = 16%, P = .31) (Figure 2A).4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Comparable effect sizes were observed in the Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.64–0.76, 7 studies) (Figure 2B) and in the non-Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.70–0.89, 4 studies) (Figure 2C).4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10

While both Asian and non-Asian patients had significant improvement in PFS, the PFS benefit was more pronounced in the Asian regional breakdown. With the rationale that PD-(L)1 inhibitors may be advantageous in patients with lower volume of disease,42 and the assumption that patients with recurrent metastatic disease have lower burden of disease, we explored the distribution of recurrent and de novo metastatic disease in RATIONALE-305.11 The finding was significant for a difference in the proportion of patients with recurrent metastatic disease in the Asian vs non-Asian population (36% vs 26%, respectively) (Table 1).11 These data are not reported (NR) in other studies, however, given the differences in screening and disease management, it is likely that this imbalance is also present in other studies and is a potential reason for a more favorable HR for PFS in Asian populations.

Sensitivity Analysis

A sensitivity analysis was undertaken to exclude 2 studies (ATTRACTION-4 [part 2] and KEYNOTE-062) in which PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy were nonsuperior to the comparator.4,7 In the sensitivity analysis, PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy also had a treatment effect on OS in the overall population (HR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.73–0.83, 5 studies) (Figure 3A), in the Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.70–0.83, 5 studies) (Figure 3B), and in the non-Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.71–0.87, 3 studies) (Figure 3C).5,6,8, 9, 10 PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy also had a treatment effect on PFS in the overall population (HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.69–0.80, 5 studies) (Figure 4A), the Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.64–0.77, 5 studies) (Figure 4B), and the non-Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.70–0.89, 3 studies) (Figure 4C), respectively.

An additional sensitivity analysis was performed excluding GEMSTONE-303, which enrolled only patients with PD-L1 CPS ≥5% expression. After excluding this study, PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy continued to demonstrate a significant treatment effect on OS in the overall population (HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.75–0.85), in the Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.73–0.86), and in the non-Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.74–0.89). Similarly, for PFS, the treatment effect remained significant in the overall population (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.72–0.81), in the Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.64–0.78), and in the non-Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.70–0.89). These results confirm that the exclusion of GEMSTONE-303 did not significantly alter the conclusions of our analysis.

Safety Data

Safety data were obtained from 7 studies (Table 2).4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 The percentage of patients experiencing TRAEs was consistent across the PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus chemotherapy (94%–98%) and comparator (92%–97%) treatment groups. Similar findings were observed for patients reporting grade ≥3 TRAEs; there were, however, fewer grade ≥3 TRAEs in the GEMSTONE-303 study for both treatment and comparator possibly because this is the only study that did not allow continuation of chemotherapy after the initial induction period. There was a wide range of withdrawals due to TRAEs across studies, but this may be related to differences in the reporting period, population, or AE definitions. There were few data specific to irAEs, but the incidence of grade ≥3 irAEs was comparable across studies that reported these outcomes.

Discussion

This meta-analysis evaluated the survival outcomes associated with 1L PD-(L)1 inhibitor treatment plus chemotherapy in 6773 patients with metastatic GC/GEJC enrolled in 7 randomized, phase III studies, who were stratified by Asian and non-Asian regional breakdown. 1L PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy improved OS in the overall population (HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.75–0.84). Similar benefits were observed by Asian (HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.73–0.85) and non-Asian (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.74–0.89) regional breakdown, with no significant differences between these groups. 1L PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy also improved PFS in the overall population (HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.70–0.80) and non-Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.70–0.89), with a numerically greater effect on PFS by Asian regional breakdown (HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.64–0.76), respectively. The findings indicate that patients with metastatic GC/GEJC experience survival benefits regardless of their geographical region.

The observed numerical differences in PFS benefit in Asian vs non-Asian patients in our study are intriguing. This may stem from variations in the disease status of the patients at study entry, specifically, differences in the ratio of recurrent metastatic disease vs de novo metastatic disease, which has been shown previously.43,44 While there are no data to verify these differences in this meta-analysis, we looked at the data from RATIONALE-305 to see if the percentage of patients with prior surgery was different in Asian vs non-Asian regions.11 It is possible that the greater numerical improvement in PFS may be related to the higher representation of patients with recurrent metastatic disease with lower burden of disease at the time of therapy initiation. However, it must be noted that this is hypothesis-generating, and represents a small subgroup analysis.

Unfortunately, there was insufficient data to examine outcomes by Asian and non-Asian regional breakdown based on PD-L1 status. Furthermore, GEMSTONE-303 only enrolled patients with PD-L1 >5%; this patient selection may explain why the efficacy outcomes observed in this study are better than that observed with other therapies. GEMSTONE-303 is not on par with KEYNOTE-859, CheckMate 649, or RATIONALE-305. Of note, the US Food and Drug Administration Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee recently stated that the risk-benefit assessment is not favorable for the use of PD-(L)1 inhibitors for 1L GC/GEJC in patients with tumor PD-L1 expression <1.45 This determination was based on the consistency of data between CheckMate 649, KEYNOTE-859, and RATIONALE-305 data, indicating that this is a class effect.

Our findings are also consistent with another meta-analysis of 8 eligible studies (5723 patients) that showed PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy prolonged PFS in advanced GC; however, this effect was more pronounced in patients receiving oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy.46 We did not examine the effect of different chemotherapy strategies in our analysis. In a large meta-analysis that covered 11,020 patients with metastatic solid cancers from 19 randomized, controlled studies, the overall HRs for OS were 0.69 (Asian) and 0.82 (non-Asian), and the overall HRs for PFS were 0.54 (Asian) and 0.69 (non-Asian), respectively.26 These authors reported a significantly improved survival benefit in Asian patients receiving PD-(L)1 inhibitors and speculated that genetic and baseline characteristics may account for these differences. In patients with lung cancer, differences in the distribution of EGFR and STK11 mutations are implicated as a factor for differences in survival outcomes associated with PD-(L)1 inhibitor therapy in Asian and non-Asian patients.29, 30, 31 While such data do not exist for GC, it is possible that there are differences in genomic or epigenetic alterations between GC in Asian and non-Asian patients that may impact outcomes. It should be noted that the published meta-analysis of 11,020 patients included phase II studies and did not exclude studies with nonsignificant primary outcomes. However, when we excluded negative studies (ATTRACTION-4 [part 2] and KEYNOTE-062), this did not significantly affect the survival outcomes in Asian and non-Asian patients. Similarly, our sensitivity analysis excluding GEMSTONE-303 (PD-L1 CPS ≥5% population only) confirmed consistent treatment effects across both regional breakdowns.

Global Implications

Our meta-analysis and safety data assessment indicate that the benefit of PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy in patients with advanced GC/GEJC is applicable to all patients, regardless of enrollment region, and should reduce skepticism about generalizability of data based on the enrolled population. From a regulatory perspective, while the Food and Drug Administration highlights the importance of multiregional studies to support applicability to local patients and medical practice,47 our findings may have wider implications for health technology assessments of PD-(L)1 inhibitor use in GC/GEJC. The results of this study could encourage regulatory agencies to adopt data from countries other than their own given the consistent evidence shown across regional subgroups, supporting biology-driven (not geography-driven) drug approvals.48 This could enable greater patient access to anti–PD-(L)1 therapy in this setting; thus, reducing disparities in access to new therapies.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths included strict inclusion criteria and pooling of data from randomized, controlled studies only. However, the study has several limitations inherent with meta-analysis design on published data; assumptions were made during the regional breakdown of Asian and non-Asian subgroups, including pooling of the control arm. We were limited to published data, and unable to extract missing data. Also, it was not possible to adjust for baseline characteristics, such as age or Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, or patient-level confounders. Unmeasured confounders (eg, PD-L1 status, peritoneal involvement) may contribute to regional trends. Also, study-level data cannot establish causality between surgery history and PFS. These confounders could preclude definitive conclusions about biological drivers of regional outcomes. Future individual patient data meta-analyses may resolve these gaps.

Some subgroup analyses were not feasible due to lack of data, including outcomes based on biomarkers (such as PD-L1 expression or microsatellite instability-high), tumor origin (gastric vs GEJ), which was NR separately, and number and distribution of metastatic sites. It was not possible to compare the type of most frequently reported AEs. Regional safety trends (eg, irAE incidence) were not analyzed despite clinical relevance; these AEs were not consistently reported, and small samples limited data analysis. Another potential bias was the use of different criteria for measuring PD-L1 positivity. Also, it was not possible to address deficient DNA mismatch repair/microsatellite instability status in the patient population receiving immunotherapy as these data were NR.

Furthermore, we did not compare combination strategies vs PD-(L)1 inhibitor monotherapy or examine the impact of different chemotherapy backbone treatments. A previous meta-analysis has indicated that the combination of PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus chemotherapy is additive.49 Previous studies have also shown that a combination approach has a comparable toxicity profile vs chemotherapy alone.49 We observed similar findings in our safety summary. Finally, survival outcomes may have been impacted by variation in follow-up time available from the individual studies, and we did not include other markers of treatment, such as objective response rates.

Conclusion

In conclusion, 1L PD-(L)1 inhibitors in combination with chemotherapy improved survival outcomes in patients with HER2-negative metastatic GC/GEJC by Asian and non-Asian regional breakdown. These findings were based on a meta-analysis of phase III randomized controlled studies and support the current guideline recommendations on the use of these agents in the advanced GC/GEJC setting. The absence of regional differences in outcomes observed in our study may help to facilitate regulatory approval and drug access to patients worldwide. This may also help reduce global disparities in access to new therapies.
