Implant fracture of the Trochanteric Fixation Nail Advanced (TFNA) lag screw through a cement fenestration: a case report
Authors: Timothy McAleese, Gareth Murray, Declan Reidy, James A. Harty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Cephalomedullary nails (CMNs) are commonly used to treat proximal femur fractures. The Trochanteric Fixation Nail Advanced (TFNA, DePuy Synthes) is a CMN system, introduced in 2015, featuring significant design modifications compared to its predecessors. These include a new Ti-15Mo alloy and a fenestrated lag screw that permits cement augmentation of the femoral head. Nevertheless, device-specific failures have emerged. We report a unique mode of failure: breakage of the lag screw through a cement fenestration.Case presentation:An 81-year-old woman with an AO/OTA 31-A2.1 pertrochanteric fracture underwent long TFNA fixation (130°, 10 × 400 mm) with a 90-mm lag screw positioned center–center in dynamic mode. At 7.5 months, she developed severe atraumatic hip pain and imaging demonstrated non-union, autodynamization, and lag screw breakage. Retrieval of the broken implant demonstrated a fracture through the first cement fenestration of the lag screw. She subsequently underwent conversion to total hip arthroplasty.Discussion:There have been several reports of increased implant breakage associated with the TFNA in a distinctive “stepped propagation” pattern of metal cracking. These typically occur at the lag screw aperture. However, fenestrations in the lag screw also appear to act as stress risers when cement is not used. In non-union, stress is concentrated at the first fenestration, initiating crack propagation and implant failure.Conclusion:TFNA lag screws are vulnerable to fatigue fracture in the setting of non-union. Fenestrated head elements should be reserved for cases in which cement augmentation is planned. Patients treated with TFNA implants should be monitored closely for delayed union to mitigate the risk of fatigue failure.

Introduction

The global incidence of hip fractures is estimated to be around 14.2 million[1]. Furthermore, the age-standardized rate has increased by 0.4% each year to 182.5 per 100 000 individuals in the last 30 years[1]. These injuries are increasingly managed with surgical fixation using cephalomedullary nails (CMNs) because of their ability to provide load-sharing and stable fixation while minimizing soft tissue damage[2,3].

One of the concerns with older CMN designs was the risk of mechanical complications compared to extramedullary devices[2]. The Trochanteric Fixation Nail Advanced (TFNA) system (DePuy Synthes, Raynham, MA, USA) was introduced in 2015 with several design enhancements aimed at reducing mechanical failure[4]. Notable changes were made to increase the nail’s strength and durability compared to its predecessors such as the formulation of a new titanium-molybdenum (Ti-15Mo) alloy. The nail geometry was also revised to better match femoral anatomy, adopting a reduced radius of curvature and incorporating a lateral relief cut and bump cuts at the proximal aperture. There has also been the addition of an option to use cement augmentation for osteoporotic bone through fenestrations in the lag screw[4].

However, despite these advancements, there are emerging reports of unique failure modes associated with the TFNA system[5–8]. The most common point of failure is at the lag screw aperture where the implant cracks in a specific “stepped propagation” pattern, and it has been hypothesized that the metal microstructure may predispose the nail to cracking[6]. There have also been recent reports of set-screw breakage, which has not been described in any other CMN system[5]. Our case adds to reports of a new mode of failure: fracture of the lag screw at a cement fenestration. We reported this case in accordance with the revised SCARE criteria 2025[9].

Case report

Our patient is an 81-year-old female who attended the emergency department after tripping and falling onto her right hip. Her past medical history included hypertension, hypercholesterolemia and gout. Her past surgical history included breast carcinoma, which was successfully managed in 1998 by wide local excision followed by adjuvant chemoradiotherapy and bilateral total knee replacements. She had no known drug allergies. At her baseline, she was mobilizing independently and living with her daughter. She was a non-smoker and non-drinker.

On initial examination, our patient had marked tenderness over the right hip and was unable to bear weight. Her right lower limb was shortened and externally rotated. Distal neurovascular status remained intact. Radiographs revealed she had sustained a pertrochanteric fracture (AO/OTA 31-A2.1) of her right hip (Fig. 1 A,B). She subsequently underwent long cephalomedullary nailing using the TFNA system (DePuy Synthes, Raynham, MA, USA). Internal fixation was achieved using a 130 degree × 10 mm × 400 mm nail with a 90 mm lag screw (DePuy Synthes; catalogue number 04.037.060S; lot number H505082). A fenestrated lag screw was used because this was the routinely used option at our institution, Cork University Hospital. Cement augmentation was not planned or performed. The lag screw corresponded to the TFNA system’s original fenestration geometry with fenestration diameters of approximately 2.25 mm. The operation was uneventful, and the lag screw was placed in the center–center position in the femoral head in a dynamic mode. She was permitted to bear weight as tolerated. Her post-operative recovery was also progressing well until she developed a deep venous thrombosis and was started on oral anticoagulation therapy. At 6 weeks post-operatively, she attended her routine outpatient appointment. At this time, she had mild right hip pain, although her mobility was improving gradually. She was mobilizing independently with two crutches. Radiographs of her right hip and femur revealed that the implant was in a good position and that there had been progression of bone healing at the fracture site (Fig. 2A,B). At 7.5 months post-operatively, our patient presented to the emergency department with severe right hip pain. She explained the pain has been getting progressively worse over the preceding 3 weeks and that it had reached a severity that was limiting her activities of daily living. She had not suffered any trauma to her right hip. On examination, she had an antalgic gait and pain over her right hip, which radiated into her groin. The range of motion in her right hip was globally restricted due to pain. Her leg lengths were equal, and distally, her neurovascular status was intact. Her blood investigations did not demonstrate any sign of infection: Haemoglobin 12.2, White Cell Count (WCC) 9.7, Neutrophils 6.69, Platelets 386, and C-reactive protein 8. Her radiographs at this time demonstrated fracture of the lag screw, non-union at the fracture site, and autodynamization of one of the distal locking screws (Fig. 3A–D). A subsequent CT scan of the pelvis and right hip was performed, which demonstrated non-union of the fracture site. Additionally, there was a breakage of the cephalic lag screw, which appeared to be through one of the cement fenestrations (Fig. 4A–D). Based on the acuity of her symptoms and the radiological findings, it was determined that the patient should undergo conversion to total hip arthroplasty using the RECLAIM Modular Revision Hip System (DePuy Synthes, Warsaw, IN, USA). This was performed through a posterior approach, and all components of the TFNA implant were removed. Examination of the explanted lag screw confirmed that it had fractured through the first cement fenestration (Fig. 5A–D). There were no other signs of implant cracking or wear. Thorough washout and tissue debridement were then performed. Intra-operative tissue specimens obtained from the deep tissue and bone–implant interface were sent for aerobic and anaerobic microbiological culture with a 7-day incubation period; all cultures returned negative. Histopathological examination demonstrated chronic inflammatory changes without evidence of acute infection or malignancy (Fig. 6A,B). At 3.5 years post-operatively, in May 2025, our patient was residing independently in her own home with a structured home support package comprising three visits per week, supplemented by family assistance for selected activities of daily living. She is independently mobile with the aid of a four-wheeled walker. Although she continued to experience some residual pain and stiffness in the affected limb, she reported satisfaction with her overall functional outcome and was content with her level of independence. Her patient-reported outcome scores were as follows: Oxford Hip Score = 20/48, Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Score (HOOS) = 38.1, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Arthritis Index (WOMAC) = 57/96, EQ-5D-5L Index = 0.26, and EQ-VAS = 50. Discussion

The introduction of the TFNA nailing system saw several design changes over its predecessors including a new Ti-15Mo alloy, a more anatomical nail geometry, and a fenestrated lag screw that permits cement augmentation in osteoporotic bone[4]. However, concerns have been raised regarding increased rates of mechanical failure associated with the TFNA nailing system[6,10,11]. This typically occurs at the lag-screw aperture of the nail and is associated with non-union[12,13]. However, there are recent reports of novel modes of failure, including set-screw and lag-screw breakage[5,7,8]. Our case adds to reports of atypical implant breakage patterns linked to the TFNA system.

Our patient suffered implant failure at 7.5 months post-operatively secondary to non-union. The reported rate of CMN implant breakage is approximately 0.6% and typically occurs around 9–12 months post-operatively, with delayed or non-union identified as a critical contributing factor[12,14,15]. In cases of non-union, the implant is subjected to continuous mechanical loading without the support of bone healing, ultimately exceeding its fatigue limit and leading to failure[16]. Other risk factors for implant breakage include unstable fracture patterns, pathologic fractures, lower American Society of Anaesthesiologist classes, and younger age[16]. In a laboratory analysis of 16 broken TFNA nails, Lambers et al reported increased risk of implant breakage in reverse-oblique (AO/OTA 31-A3) fractures and in revision intramedullary nailing[6]. They found no evidence of iatrogenic damage from reaming or lag-screw insertion and described a distinctive “stepped propagation” cracking pattern in the Ti–Mo alloy at the lag screw aperture. This seminal study was the first to recommend close monitoring of the TFNA nailing system.

The distinguishing feature of the TFNA is its fenestrated cephalic lag screw and helical blade to allow cement augmentation[4]. The lag screw has 8 radial fenestrations and the helical blade has 12, allowing cement extrusion into an osteoporotic femoral head to mitigate cut-out risk. While the role of cement augmentation remains contentious, fenestration holes create focal points of increased stress during loading[17,18]. Cyclic stresses generated by weight-bearing and micro-motion at non-united fracture sites trigger the initiation of micro-cracks at the lag screw fenestration with subsequent propagation through the metal[15]. If cement is injected, polymethylmethacrylate may slightly distribute the stress and increase the effective cross-section; however, its low stiffness and susceptibility to cracking and debonding limit this benefit. There are now three recent cases that describe this mode of failure, using the lag screw in dynamic or static mode but in different age groups[7,8]. Furthermore, all cases involve the use of a lag screw rather than a helical blade, with no cement augmentation, and failure occurred in the identical location: the first cement fenestration. Recently, the manufacturer has re-designed the TFNA lag screw. This involved reducing the diameter of fenestrations from 2.25 to 1.80 mm and shifting the holes laterally to lie fully within the thread profile, which lowered local stresses at the fenestration sites on finite-element analysis. Therefore, we recommend that fenestrated implants are only used when cement augmentation is planned and that hospitals stock non-fenestrated screws, given that the cost is equivalent. Patients treated with TFNA implants, particularly those at risk of delayed union, should undergo close clinical and radiographic surveillance.

This report describes a single case and cannot establish causation or incidence. Formal metallurgical fracture-surface analysis was not performed. In addition, detailed documentation regarding implant availability and implant-selection rationale at the time of index surgery was limited.

Mechanical basis of fenestration-related fatigue failure

Fenestrations represent geometric discontinuities within the nail’s metallic structure. Fracture-mechanics analyses demonstrate that the presence of a hole introduces a stress concentration factor, whereby stresses at the edge of the discontinuity substantially exceed nominal stresses within the bulk material, predisposing to fatigue crack initiation under cyclic loading conditions[19,20].

In the setting of fracture non-union, the intended load-sharing function of the bone–implant construct is lost. As a result, physiological loads are transmitted predominantly through the implant rather than progressively being off-loaded to healing bone. This leads to sustained cyclic bending of the cephalic lag screw, with maximal bending moments occurring proximally near the junction of the lag screw and nail body, in close proximity to the first cement fenestration.

Metallurgical analyses of failed TFNA implants have demonstrated a characteristic stepped fatigue-crack propagation pattern, reflecting progressive crack growth under cyclic stress rather than acute overload. Lambers et al described this phenomenon in fractured TFNA components and suggested that the fatigue-crack-propagation behavior of the Ti-15Mo β-stabilized alloy differs from that of traditional α + β titanium alloys, such as Ti-6Al-7Nb[6].

Limitations

This report describes a single case and therefore cannot establish causation or incidence. Formal metallurgical or microscopic fracture-surface analysis was not performed, limiting definitive characterization of the failure mechanism. In addition, detailed pre-operative documentation regarding implant availability and selection was limited. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this case and previously reported failures of fenestrated TFNA head elements, the following practical recommendations may be considered: Fenestrated cephalic lag screws should be reserved for cases in which cement augmentation is planned.Patients treated with fenestrated TFNA implants should be monitored closely for delayed union, with a low threshold for earlier radiographic or cross-sectional imaging in the presence of persistent pain or risk factors for non-union.Consideration should be given to metallurgical review of failed fenestrated implants and to ensuring the availability of non-fenestrated head elements for centers that do not routinely perform cement augmentation.

Conclusion

Our case adds to reports of atypical implant breakage patterns linked to the TFNA system. Fenestrated lag screws should only be used when cement augmentation is planned. Close follow-up is recommended for patients who have undergone hip fracture fixation using the TFNA nailing system, especially those with risk factors for non-union.
