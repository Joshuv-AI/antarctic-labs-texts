Infectious outcomes in heart-after-liver transplantation with or without domino (HALT+/−D): A case series with comparison to isolated heart and isolated liver transplantation
Authors: Alice Lee, Eleanor Oken, Nina Sharma, Evan Clemens, Catherine E. Kling, Jay D. Pal, Ioannis Dimarakis, Mark L. Sturdevant, Richard K. Cheng, Shin Lin, Robert M. Rakita, Ralph Tayyar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart-after-liver transplantation with or without domino (HALT+/−D) has emerged as a strategy to expand access for highly sensitized patients who face limited transplant options due to donor-specific antibodies (DSAs). While the liver’s immunoprotective effect may mitigate antibody-mediated rejection, the infectious risks of this approach remain poorly defined. We performed a retrospective single-center case series of all HALT+/−D recipients (n = 4) at the University of Washington, with detailed characterization of infectious complications. For context, we compared outcomes to highly sensitized (cPRA ≥ 89%) isolated heart (n = 6) and liver (n = 5) transplant recipients.All 4 HALT+/−D recipients were female with extreme allosensitization (median cPRA 99%) and prolonged pre-transplant hospitalization (mean 64 days). Median time for the first post-transplant infection was 29 days (range 6-52). Three of four (75%) developed Clostridioides difficile infection, associated with broad-spectrum antibiotic use. HALT+/−D recipients had median infection-related hospital days of 37, vs 7 in heart and 0 in liver transplant recipients. All 4 patients survived to one year with functioning grafts.HALT+/−D recipients experience a substantial infectious burden, particularly CDI. This case series provides clinical information to guide infection management. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to optimize protocols.

Background

Heart transplantation is a life-saving intervention for patients with end-stage heart failure, yet infectious complications remain a leading cause of post-transplant morbidity and mortality.1, 2 Among transplant recipients, highly sensitized patients—those with high levels of preformed donor-specific antibodies (DSAs)—incur long-wait times and face increased risk of antibody-mediated rejection, limiting their access to suitable organs and complicating post-transplant management.3 Desensitization strategies, such as plasmapheresis and aggressive immunosuppression, are often insufficient and difficult to implement for critically ill patients, particularly in urgent cases.4

Heart-after-liver transplantation with or without Domino (HALT+/−D) if the recipient liver is normal has recently emerged as a novel strategy to expand donor access and mitigate rejection in sensitized candidates. This approach leverages the liver’s intrinsic immunomodulatory properties, which may attenuate alloreactivity and reduce DSA-mediated injury to the heart allograft.5, 6, 7

Recent data suggest that HALT+/−D may provide a viable option for highly sensitized patients who would otherwise have limited transplant opportunities. A study examining HALT+/−D demonstrated that the liver transplant component confers immunoprotection to the heart, facilitating successful transplantation in patients with high calculated panel-reactive antibody (cPRA) levels.7 The liver is thought to act as an “immunologic sink,” reducing allosensitization and promoting graft survival, an effect mediated by the liver’s ability to absorb DSAs and foster a tolerogenic immune environment.8 However, the impact of this approach on infection risk remains unclear.9

The use of intensified immunosuppressive regimens to prevent rejection in HALT+/−D recipients as well as HALT+/−D’s increased operative complexity may increase susceptibility to bacterial, viral, and fungal infections. However, whether the liver’s immunomodulatory properties also influence infection risk compared to heart-alone or liver-alone transplant recipients has yet to be thoroughly evaluated.

We present a detailed case series of all 4 HALT+/−D recipients at our institution from 2023 to 2024, with comprehensive characterization of infectious complications during the first-year post-transplant. We also compared outcomes with that of highly sensitized isolated heart or isolated liver transplant recipients.

Methods

This was a retrospective single-center case series and comparative cohort study of adult patients who underwent HALT+/−D, isolated heart transplantation, or isolated liver transplantation at the University of Washington. All 4 HALT+/−D recipients transplanted between January 2023 and January 2024 were included. For comparison, highly sensitized heart transplant recipients (peak cPRA ≥89% with positive crossmatch, n = 6) and liver transplant recipients (peak cPRA ≥99% with positive crossmatch, n = 5) were selected based on recency of transplant and continued follow-up at the time of data collection. Data were extracted via manual chart review of the electronic medical record. The study was deemed exempt by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board.

The primary outcomes were post-transplant infection-related hospital days and total number of infections within 1 year of transplant, with donor-derived infections excluded from both. Secondary outcomes included total antibiotic, antiviral, antifungal, and Clostridium difficile treatment days, as well as rejection incidence; prophylactic antimicrobial days were excluded from drug exposure counts. All outcomes were censored at 1 year. Across-group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc pairwise comparisons where applicable (significance threshold p < 0.05), using R statistical software.

Study definitions

“Post-transplant infection-related hospital days” was defined as all hospital days post-transplant during which the patient received treatment for non-donor derived infection. Days with simultaneous infections were counted as 1 day. “Total number of post-transplant infections” was defined as the number of infections diagnosed within 1-year post-transplant, excluding donor-derived infections. An infection was defined as a clinical syndrome (eg, pneumonia, UTI, and bacteremia) supported by microbiologic, radiologic, or histopathologic evidence that prompted therapeutic antimicrobial intervention. Positive cultures without corresponding clinical symptoms or signs were considered colonization and not counted as infections.

“Antibiotic days,” “antiviral days,” and “antifungal days” were defined as the total number of days patients received antibiotics, antivirals, and antifungals, respectively. Prophylactic antibiotic, antiviral, and antifungal therapy durations were not included. “C difficile treatment days” was defined as the total number of days for which a patient was treated for C difficile infections. Additionally, we defined patients as having an incidence of rejection if patients had biopsy-proven rejection and/or were treated for presumed rejection.

Results

Case 1

A 30-year-old Hispanic woman with end-stage heart failure was admitted to the hospital 38 days prior to transplantation, requiring Veno-Arterial Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation as a bridge to transplant. She had a cPRA of 99%, reflecting broadly sensitized status. Her pretransplant course was complicated by prolonged hospitalization and exposure to multiple antimicrobial agents. She underwent HALT+/−D and was exposed to 2 donor-related infections, hepatitis C and syphilis, for which she received treatment. Induction immunosuppression consisted of anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG), plasmapheresis, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and eculizumab. She received standard antimicrobial prophylaxis, including amoxicillin, following transplantation. Her early post-transplant course was complicated by antibody-mediated rejection without histologic features (pAMR1), and DSAs cleared at day 86 post-transplant.

The patient's post-transplant course was marked by 3 sequential infections beginning as early as postoperative day 6. On day 6, she developed a ventilator-associated pneumonia and was treated with piperacillin-tazobactam for 10 days. Seven weeks after transplantation (day 52), she developed C difficile infection (CDI) and was treated with oral vancomycin for 14 days. Eight months post-transplant, she was diagnosed with COVID-19, treated with remdesivir for 3 days.

In total, the patient accumulated 36 hospital days post-transplant, 100% of which were infection-related. Despite this complex course, she remained alive at 1 year with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 70% and normal hepatic graft function.

Case 2

A 47-year-old Black woman with end-stage heart failure and a cPRA of 99% was hospitalized for 52 days prior to transplantation. She did not require mechanical circulatory support and had no documented pre-transplant antibiotic exposure. She underwent HALT+/−D and received the same induction immunosuppression protocol: ATG, plasmapheresis, IVIG, and eculizumab. She received standard amoxicillin prophylaxis following transplantation. Her post-transplant rejection surveillance demonstrated rapid clearance of DSAs, but she developed a T-cell mediated liver transplant rejection.

The patient's first infection occurred on day 52 post-transplant, she had candidal vaginitis treated with a course of oral fluconazole, which recurred a month later. Her second and final infectious event within a year post transplant was Cytomegalovirus viremia and hepatitis, requiring prolonged antiviral therapy. Notably, she did not develop CDI.

The patient accumulated 39 total hospital days post-transplant, of which 18 (46%) were infection-related. She was alive at 1 year with LVEF of 64% and normal hepatic graft function.

Case 3

A 62-year-old White woman with end-stage heart failure and a cPRA of 100% was hospitalized for 86 days prior to transplantation. She had no pre-transplant mechanical circulatory support and no documented pre-transplant antibiotic exposure. She received pre-transplant IVIG and plasmapheresis. She underwent HALT+/−D and received induction immunosuppression with ATG, plasmapheresis, IVIG, and eculizumab. She did not develop rejection during the early post-transplant period but had persistently low-level DSAs throughout the year after transplant.

Her surgical course was complicated by delayed chest and abdomen closure for 5 days, requiring broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage. The patient developed CDI on day 14 post-transplant, which was treated with oral vancomycin. On day 19 post-transplant, she was found to have an anterior mediastinal fluid collection with no organisms identified on tissue from washout, so antibiotics were stopped. On day 59, she was diagnosed with a superficial chest surgical site infection (SSI) caused by coagulase-negative Staphylococcus and treated with doxycycline.

The patient accumulated 47 total post-transplant hospital days, 28 of which (60%) were infection-related. She was alive at 1 year with LVEF of 60%-65%, normal hepatic function, decreased DSA titers, and complete wound healing.

Case 4

A 47-year-old Black woman with end-stage heart failure requiring left ventricular assist device (LVAD) had a cPRA of 99% and was hospitalized for 79 days prior to transplantation, during which she received suppressive levofloxacin for LVAD driveline infection. She underwent HALT+/−D, and her surgical course was complicated by delayed chest and abdominal closure on postoperative day 5, necessitating broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage. Her post-transplant immunologic course was complicated by persistently high levels of DSAs.

Her first clinically significant infection was identified on day 44. The patient's post-transplant course was dominated by a cascade of vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus infections of escalating severity. On day 44, she developed a vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus intra-abdominal collection and sternal wound dehiscence with hardware-associated osteomyelitis, requiring several surgical debridements. She was treated with daptomycin then linezolid for 12 weeks. On day 78, deep abdominal wound infection caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa was identified, requiring surgical debridement and another prolonged course of antibiotics. On day 157, after an estimated cumulative antibiotic exposure of 206 days, CDI developed and was treated with oral vancomycin. On day 290, she developed an Escherichia coli urinary tract infection, managed with ceftriaxone.

The patient accumulated 138 total post-transplant hospital days, 79 of which (57%) were infection-related. She remained alive at 1 year with persistently elevated DSAs requiring repeated IVIG infusions, LVEF 73%, and normal hepatic graft function.

Comparative outcomes across transplant groups

The study included 15 patients: 4 HALT+/−D, 6 heart transplant, and 5 liver transplant recipients. The median age was 45 years (IQR 41-49), and all patients were female (Supplementary Table 1). All patients completed 1-year follow-up with no deaths. Pre-transplant acuity was highest among HALT+/−D recipients, who were all hospitalized at the time of transplant with a median of 64 days of pre-transplant hospitalization (range 38-86 days), compared to a subset of heart (3/6, median 42 days) and liver (3/5, median 23 days) transplant recipients.

Infection-related outcomes were significantly worse among HALT+/−D recipients. The median number of infection-related hospital days within the first year was 40 (IQR 23-58) for HALT+/−D patients, compared to 7 (IQR 0-23) for heart transplant patients and 0 (IQR 0-8) for liver transplant patients (p = 0.03), with pairwise comparisons confirming significant differences between HALT+/−D and each of the other groups (p = 0.02 and p = 0.01, respectively) (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Clostridium difficile infection drove a substantial portion of this burden: median C difficile treatment days were 17 (IQR 0-33) among HALT+/−D recipients vs zero in both heart and liver transplant groups (p = 0.03). While the median number of infections per patient trended higher in HALT+/−D recipients (median 3, range 3-8) compared to heart and liver recipients (median 1 each), this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.06) (Supplementary tables 2).

Rejection occurred in 7 of 15 patients (47%) and was comparably distributed across groups (50% of HALT+/−D, 50% of heart, and 40% of liver recipients). Five of the seven patients with rejection received treatment; the 2 untreated cases were both HALT+/−D patients with mild rejection that resolved without additional immunosuppression, 1 with spontaneous resolution of pAMR1 and 1 with T-cell-mediated liver rejection that improved with antiviral therapy for concurrent surgical site infections hepatitis alone. Supplementary Table 4 includes details on immunosuppression regimens for all 15 patients. SSIs were uncommon overall (2 of 34 total infections, 6%), with 1 HALT+/−D patient developing both chest and abdominal SSIs and 1 liver transplant patient developing an intra-abdominal SSI, all requiring operative washout.

Discussion

This study represents the first evaluation of infectious outcomes following HALT+/−D in highly sensitized transplant recipients. Our findings suggest that HALT+/−D may be associated with a higher burden of post-transplant infection-related hospital days and C difficile treatment days compared to highly sensitized heart transplant patients and highly sensitized liver transplant patients.

All 15 patients in our cohort were female, which limits generalizability but is consistent with the overrepresentation of women among highly sensitized candidates. Women are more likely than men to develop anti-HLA antibodies because of prior pregnancies, which are a potent sensitizing event and can lead to broad class I and II HLA antibody formation that may persist for decades.10 Additional sensitizing exposures such as blood transfusions, prior transplantation, and durable mechanical circulatory support further increase HLA antibody burden and contribute to higher cPRA and reduced access to compatible donors for women.10, 11 These factors may have contributed to the exclusive selection of female candidates for HALT+/−D at our center rather than reflecting an intentional sex-based selection bias.

Although the absolute number of infections per patient was not significantly higher, HALT+/−D patients experienced more infection-related hospital days. One HALT+/−D patient developed SSIs involving both chest and abdominal sites, while one of the 5 liver transplant recipients developed an abdominal SSI; none of the heart-alone recipients required repeat surgical intervention for SSIs. The occurrence of SSIs in the HALT+/−D group may reflect the increased complexity of dual-organ transplantation, including longer operative times, multiple anatomic sites at risk for infection, and intensified immunosuppression regimens.

However, when examining induction immunosuppression regimens, all HALT+/−D patients received eculizumab, whereas no heart transplant patients or liver transplant patients received it. All patients who received eculizumab were prescribed amoxicillin or penicillin prophylaxis to reduce the risk of infection with encapsulated organisms. Prior meta-analyses have shown that while eculizumab may increase the risk of infections with encapsulated organisms - particularly Neisseria meningitidis - it does not appear to increase the overall risk of other infections in either transplant or non-transplant populations.12 In our study, no patients developed N meningitidis infection, and only one HALT+/−D patient experienced an infection with an encapsulated organism (P aeruginosa). Given the small sample size, further studies are needed to determine whether HALT+/−D patients who receive eculizumab are at higher risk of infections overall.

All HALT+/−D recipients also received peri-operative plasmapheresis (PLEX, median 3 sessions, IQR 2-3), which was used more frequently and intensively than in the heart control group (2/6 recipients received PLEX). PLEX, often with albumin and/or fresh frozen plasma as replacement, can cause humoral immunodeficiency by removing immunoglobulins and has been associated with an increased risk of infection.13 This more intensive desensitization regimen, combined with the inherent complexity of dual-organ surgery and the critically ill pre-transplant status of HALT+/−D recipients, likely contributed significantly to the observed infectious burden. The comparator heart group, while highly sensitized, represented a different clinical phenotype, as they were stable enough to wait for a compatible donor without requiring a combined organ strategy or augmented desensitization. The ideal comparison would be to heart-alone recipients receiving identical intensive desensitization (PLEX/eculizumab), but such a cohort does not yet exist at our center.

While the number of antibiotic days, antiviral days, and antifungal days did not differ significantly across the groups, 3 out of the 4 HALT+/−D patients developed CDIs, and HALT+/−D patients had a significantly higher number of C difficile treatment days. Additionally, no heart transplant patients developed CDIs, and only one out of the 5 liver patients developed a CDI (Supplementary Table 2). Possible explanations include greater exposure to broad-spectrum antimicrobials pre- and post-transplant, prolonged hospitalizations pre- and post-transplant, and more intensified immunosuppression.14, 15 Given these findings, targeted interventions such as earlier diagnostic testing for CDI, enhanced antimicrobial stewardship to limit unnecessary broad-spectrum exposure, or even consideration of prophylactic strategies in high-risk subgroups may warrant further evaluation.

Despite the elevated infectious burden, HALT+/−D recipients did not experience higher rates of rejection within 1 year of transplant compared to the other groups. This supports prior observations of the liver’s immunoprotective role in HALT+/−D but also raises the question of whether infectious risks may offset some of the immunologic advantages. Several factors may have contributed to the higher infectious burden in this group. All HALT+/−D patients in our cohort had prolonged pre-transplant hospitalizations, which may have predisposed them to colonization or increased susceptibility to infection post-operatively. In addition, all HALT+/−D recipients received eculizumab, necessitating extended antibiotic prophylaxis, which could have contributed to the higher incidence of C difficile infection observed in this group. While these findings suggest a potential trade-off between infectious risk and immunologic benefit, we believe the magnitude of infectious risk is small when weighed against the mortality risk these highly sensitized patients would have faced had transplantation been deferred in hopes of an immunologically favorable donor or attempted against multiple donor-specific antibodies.

Limitations

The limitations of our study are related to the novelty and very recent implementation of HALT+/−D at our institution. One significant limitation was the small sample size. At the time of data collection, only 4 HALT+/−D procedures had been performed. Additionally, we only collected data for 1 year post date of transplant for each study participant because it had only been 1 year post transplant for the most recent HALT+/−D procedure at the time of data collection, and we hypothesized that the highest burden of infection risk would be near the time of transplant. This was also a single-center design, which restricts generalizability. As more HALT+/−D procedures continue to be performed, investigations with higher power will allow for further clarity if HALT+/−D predisposes patients to more infectious outcomes when compared to highly sensitized heart transplants and highly sensitized liver transplants.

Conclusion

With the limitation of this small sample size, our study indicates that HALT+/−D patients experience more infection-related hospital days post-transplant as well as more C difficile treatment days when compared to highly sensitized heart transplant patients and highly sensitized liver transplant patients. As HALT+/−D becomes more widely adopted for sensitized patients, further research is needed to determine how best to mitigate infectious risk while preserving the procedure’s immunologic advantages.
