Status of Diabetes Mellitus in Pre-, Peri-, and Postmenopausal Women Accessing Obstetrics and Gynecological Care Services in Nigerian Hospitals
Authors: Uchenna Cosmas Ugwu, Lawretta Eyuche Ozoemena, Vivian Akah Pauluyonwu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Diabetes mellitus (DM) poses significant threats to overall health, contributing to increased morbidity and mortality. This study investigated DM prevalence, knowledge, attitude, and practice (PKAP) among premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women accessing obstetric and gynecological care services in Nigerian hospitals, and explored associations with demographic variables.Subjects and Methods:A hospital-based, cross-sectional survey was conducted between August 2024 and January 2025 using a multistage sampling technique. A total of 1620 women were recruited. Data were collected using an adapted Diabetes Mellitus Assessment Questionnaire, which demonstrated 0.81 reliability index. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 22, with statistical significance set at P ≤ 0.05.Results:Of the 1620 women recruited (aged 40–65 years), 1538 (94.9%) provided valid responses, whereas 82 (5.1%) withdrew voluntarily. Among respondents, 586 (38.1%) were premenopausal, 316 (20.5%) perimenopausal, and 636 (41.4%) postmenopausal. DM prevalence was 21.3%, 64.2%, and 65.3% among premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women, respectively. Good DM knowledge was observed in 62.8%, 69.3%, and 68.9% of premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women, respectively. Attitudes and practices toward DM varied across groups. Statistically, significant association was found between DM knowledge and employment (P = 0.022), whereas none existed on education (P = 0.701), alcohol intake (P = 0.901), or marital status (P = 0.501).Conclusion:The prevalence of DM was notably high, particularly among perimenopausal and postmenopausal women. While DM knowledge was generally good, attitudes and practices varied. The significant association between DM knowledge and certain demographic factors underscores the need for targeted health interventions and gender-responsive DM education programs.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus (DM) poses a major public health challenge, contributing to increased morbidity, mortality, dependence, and healthcare costs.[1234] In Nigeria, adult DM prevalence is estimated at 5.77%, with significant associations across demographic variables.[345] Adequate knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) are essential for DM prevention and control, particularly among menopausal women due to hormonal changes influencing disease risk.[25] This study investigated DM prevalence and KAP among premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women accessing obstetric and gynecological care services in Nigerian hospitals, and explored associations with demographic variables. Findings may inform targeted health interventions and support evidence-based policy development on diabetes management.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

This study was a hospital-based, cross-sectional survey involving 1,620 premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women accessing obstetric and gynecological care services in Nigerian hospitals between August 2024 and January 2025. For the purpose of this study, postmenopause is defined as the period characterized by the absence of menstruation for more than 12 months. Perimenopause refers to the phase marked by irregular menstrual cycles, defined as cycle duration of <23 days, more than 35 days, noticeable changes within 5 days of expected patterns, or menstrual bleeding occurring for <12 consecutive months. Premenopause refers to the period in which no symptoms of perimenopause or postmenopause are present.[6] The study was conducted in accordance with established ethical principles and guidelines, as previously described.[78] Written informed consent was obtained from all prospective participants prior to data collection. No monetary compensation was provided for participation, ensuring that the study was free from financial bias or conflicts of interest. Exclusion criteria included women younger than 40 years or older than 65 years, as well as those with severe Parkinson’s disease, psychiatric conditions, or those in a comatose state.

A multistage sampling technique was employed. In the first stage, Nigeria’s six geopolitical zones served as six clusters. In the second stage, three states were randomly selected from each cluster, resulting in 18 sampled states. In the third stage, two hospitals offering obstetric and gynecological care services were purposively selected from each state, yielding 36 hospitals. From each hospital, 45 eligible premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women were conveniently selected, resulting in a total sample size of 1620 women.

The sample size was calculated using the formula:

N = 2DP/A2,

where N = required sample size, D = DM prevalence (5.77% as reported by Uloko et al.[4]), P = 100 − DM prevalence (94.23%), and A = admissible error (15% of 5.77%, i.e., 0.87).

Substituting into the formula:

N = 2 × 5.77 × 94.23/(0.87 × 0.87) =1430.1 ≈ 1450.

To account for potential sample loss, an additional 10% (145 participants) was added, resulting in a final sample size of approximately 1620 women. This approach aligns with expert recommendations in ensuring adequate sample representation in population-based studies.[9]

A team of 36 trained research assistants (one per hospital) collaborated with the principal investigators to recruit and assess participants after obtaining official approval from the respective Chief Medical Directors. Data were collected using an adapted semistructured instrument, the Diabetes Mellitus Assessment Questionnaire (DMAQ), which demonstrated a reliability index of 0.81. This score exceeds the accepted threshold of 0.70 for adapted instruments in survey research, in line with expert opinion.[10] The content validity of the DMAQ was established by five public health experts, and the tool was adapted from a comprehensive review of existing literature on DM from reputable sources.[2311] The questionnaire, designed in simple English, required approximately 15 min to complete. The DMAQ elicited information on participants’ demographic profiles; DM prevalence status; knowledge of DM etiology, diagnosis, risk factors, complications, and prevention; attitudes toward DM management and treatment compliance; and practices related to self-care, lifestyle and dietary habits, blood glucose monitoring, and preventive behaviors. The classification of DM status was guided by benchmarks from the World Health Statistics report.[12]

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 22 (Armonk, New York (NY), USA).[13] Categorical variables were summarized using frequency counts and percentages. Associations between demographic variables and outcome measures were examined using Chi-square tests, with statistical significance set at P ≤ 0.05.

RESULTS

Out of 1620 women aged 40–65 years who were recruited, 1538 (94.9%) provided valid responses, whereas 82 (5.1%) withdrew voluntarily. Among the respondents, 586 (38.1%) were premenopausal, 316 (20.5%) were perimenopausal, and 636 (41.4%) were postmenopausal. Data on specific age brackets were not reported. More than two-thirds (66.9%) of premenopausal women, a greater proportion (63.9%) of perimenopausal women, and a majority (65.3%) of postmenopausal women held university degrees. In terms of residence, slightly more than one-quarter (27.6%) of premenopausal, less than half (41.1%) of perimenopausal, and over one-third (36.2%) of postmenopausal women lived in rural areas. A large proportion of women across all menopausal stages were employed: 68.6% of premenopausal, 66.5% of perimenopausal, and 75.9% of postmenopausal women. The majority of women identified as Christians: 67.9% of premenopausal, 76.9% of perimenopausal, and 69.3% of postmenopausal respondents. Regarding alcohol consumption, more than two-thirds (70.9%) of premenopausal women reported consuming alcohol, compared to slightly more than one-quarter of perimenopausal (29.1%) and postmenopausal (27.6%) women. Living arrangements varied, with less than one-third of premenopausal (31.0%), perimenopausal (24.4%), and postmenopausal (23.1%) women living alone. Marital status also differed across groups: more than half (62.3%) of premenopausal women were married, compared to slightly more than one-third (38.6%) of perimenopausal and less than half (43.9%) of postmenopausal women. Tobacco use was reported by about one-quarter of premenopausal (28.8%) and perimenopausal (25.6%) women, and a much smaller proportion (10.2%) of postmenopausal women [See Table 1 for more details].

The prevalence of DM among participants was 21.3% in premenopausal women, 64.2% in perimenopausal women, and 65.3% in postmenopausal women. A significant proportion of women across all menopausal groups demonstrated adequate knowledge of DM: 62.8% of premenopausal, 69.3% of perimenopausal, and 68.9% of postmenopausal women. Conversely, poor DM knowledge was observed in 37.2%, 30.7%, and 31.1% of premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women, respectively. Attitudes toward DM varied across groups. Among premenopausal women, 30.0% expressed a positive attitude, whereas the majority (70.0%) demonstrated a negative attitude. In contrast, 77.2% of perimenopausal women had a positive attitude toward DM, with only 22.8% indicating a negative attitude. For postmenopausal women, 36.3% showed a positive attitude, whereas 63.7% had a negative attitude. DM-related practices showed notable differences among the groups. Only 28.7% of premenopausal women reported good DM practices, whereas 71.3% exhibited poor practices. Among perimenopausal women, good DM practice was observed in just 19.9%, with a substantial 80.1% reporting poor practice. In contrast, 67.3% of postmenopausal women demonstrated good DM practice, whereas 32.7% reported poor practice [Further details are provided in Table 2].

DISCUSSION

Drawing from the available literature and within the limits of the investigators’ knowledge, this study is the first of its kind to explore the DM prevalence, knowledge, attitude, and practice (DM-PKAP) among premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women accessing obstetric and gynecological care services in Nigerian hospitals. The survey recorded the following DM prevalence rates: 21.3% in premenopausal women, 64.2% in perimenopausal women, and 65.3% in postmenopausal women. All three groups demonstrated good DM knowledge. However, there were disparities in attitudes and practices. For instance, slightly below one-third of premenopausal women had a positive attitude toward DM, whereas over two-thirds exhibited negative attitudes. More than three-quarters of perimenopausal women had a positive attitude, whereas fewer than one-quarter had a negative attitude. Just over one-third of postmenopausal women had a positive attitude, whereas more than half held negative attitudes. On the other hand, less than one-third of premenopausal women reported good DM practices, whereas more than two-thirds demonstrated poor practices. Only 19.9% of perimenopausal women exhibited good DM practices, whereas over three-quarters reported poor management practice. Conversely, more than two-thirds of postmenopausal women practiced good DM management, whereas slightly under one-third demonstrated poor practices. These findings offer critical insights into the DM burden and behavior patterns among menopausal women in Nigeria, underscoring the need for targeted health interventions and gender-specific DM programs. Numerous studies have documented DM prevalence in Nigeria and other regions.[414] Consistent with our findings, Raj and Angadi.[15] reported good DM KAP among participants in a hospital-based cross-sectional study in Bijapur, Karnataka. Similarly, other studies involving male and female respondents also indicated adequate DM KAP,[2] and research on patients with Type 2 DM showed high knowledge, positive attitudes, and good practices.[16] In contrast, other studies have reported poor DM knowledge, negative attitudes, and poor practices, such as those conducted among diabetic patients in the United Arab Emirates,[17] in rural Bangladesh,[18] and among newly diagnosed Type 2 diabetics in Bangladesh.[11]

The positive outcomes in our study may be attributed to increased lifestyle consciousness, awareness of DM-related complications, access to digital media, technological advancements, and improved care delivery by obstetricians and gynecologists. These findings support previous assertions that DM is a global epidemic, posing significant threats to life through increased rates of disability, morbidity, dependence, and rising medical costs.[19] Addressing DM requires early diagnosis, adherence to treatment regimens, and self-care education across diverse populations.[20]

Our study also captured vital demographic insights. For instance, over two-thirds of premenopausal women, a significant proportion of perimenopausal women, and the majority of postmenopausal women held university degrees, underscoring the value Nigerian women place on education. Furthermore, a larger proportion of participants lived in urban areas, likely due to better access to quality healthcare services or nearness to tertiary care facilities. We equally found that most of the respondents were Christians, employed, and consumed alcohol, reflecting a high level of financial independence and increased access to care services. Furthermore, fewer than one-third of the participants lived alone, indicating the presence of social and family support. Most premenopausal women were married, whereas the perimenopausal and postmenopausal groups had more varied marital statuses, including divorced, widowed, separated, or single. Only 10% of postmenopausal women used tobacco, suggesting healthier lifestyle choices, possibly due to aging. These results emphasize the influence of demographic variables on health behavior and align with similar studies conducted elsewhere.[21222324] Statistically, the Chi-square analysis revealed significant associations between DM knowledge and the variables of employment status, religious belief, and living conditions. No significant associations were found with educational status, place of residence, alcohol intake, marital status, or tobacco use. These findings are consistent with previous studies,[2172021222324] further highlighting the complex interplay between demographic factors and DM knowledge.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of our study lies on the use of a robust research design, well-defined population, sound research questionnaire, and appropriate statistical tools to explore DM-PKAP and its demographic associations. However, the hospital-based sampling of the women is a potential limitation to the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, broader, community-based studies are highly needed to better represent the general population of menopausal women in Nigeria. Furthermore, the use of self-administered questionnaires restricted qualitative feedback and might have affected data accuracy due to self-report bias. Future research should incorporate qualitative methodologies, such as interviews or focus group discussions, to capture more nuanced experiences and beliefs regarding the study phenomenon through DM-PKAP.

Implications for the future

This study presents several important implications. For instance, the high prevalence of DM across all menopausal groups indicates increased vulnerability to morbidity, mortality, medical costs, and diminished quality of life. The gap between knowledge and behavior as evidenced by negative attitudes and poor practices suggests that good knowledge alone is insufficient. There is a pressing need for integrated, gender-sensitive health interventions and public education programs to promote positive behavioural changes. Furthermore, the government agencies and private sector partners must collaborate to empower women through awareness campaigns, community-based interventions, and comprehensive DM education.

CONCLUSION

This study reveals a high prevalence of DM among premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women attending obstetrics and gynecology clinics in Nigerian hospitals, with perimenopausal and postmenopausal women being most affected. While DM knowledge was generally high, attitude and practice lagged behind, particularly among premenopausal and perimenopausal women. Significant associations were found between DM knowledge and variables such as employment status, religious belief, and living conditions, whereas no associations were observed with education, place of residence, alcohol intake, marital status, or tobacco use. These findings emphasize the urgency of public health interventions focused on lifestyle modification, early screening, and education programs to reduce the burden of DM, particularly during the menopausal transition. Future research, especially randomized controlled trials (RCTs), is recommended to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions. In addition, facility-based health programs focusing on DM prevention and management should be implemented to support women throughout the menopausal stages. This research offers a novel contribution to the understanding of DM among women in Nigeria, providing a foundation for future policy, educational programs, and public health initiatives aimed at mitigating the growing burden of DM in this vulnerable population.
