Predicting Nerve Course Variations to Prevent Iatrogenic Lesions: A Surgical Perspective on Sural Nerve Variations
Authors: Edgardo R. Rodríguez-Collazo, Eduardo Badillo Colberg, Lauren L. Schnack, Arshano Mckenzie, Shawn Ali, Derek Talbot
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:There are several documented variations of the sural nerve noted within the literature. This cadaveric study was conducted to provide a surgical perspective to either directly addressing the sural nerve, or avoiding it depending on the injury or pathology being surgically addressed.Methods:Twenty-five preserved cadaveric limbs were dissected from the popliteal fossa to the foot. Three locations of the course of the sural nerve data were analyzed. The first location (M1) was from the head of the fibula to the sural nerve. The second location (M3) was from 2.54 cm proximal to the distal tip of the lateral malleolus to the sural nerve. The third location (M2) was from the shaft of the fibula at the halfway point between measurements 1 and 2 to the course of the sural nerve. The types of variations were noted as well, compared to previous studies, highlighting the abundance of sural nerve variations in the lower limb that clinicians and surgeons need to be aware of and ready to address.Results:The sural nerve courses 4.6 cm posterior from the fibula at the proximal fibula and mid fibula and 4.5 cm at the distal fibula. The 25 sural nerve variations are not reflective of what has been previously published in the literature.Conclusion:The data can be utilized in preoperative planning in addressing or avoiding the sural nerve involving the posterior superficial compartment of the leg.

Takeaways

Question: Is there a zone to avoid during surgical dissection of the lower extremity?

Findings: The sural nerve from the head of the fibula (M1) was 4.6 ± 1.0 cm with a 95% confidence interval (CI); the mean distance of the sural nerve from the midpoint of the fibula (M3) was 4.6 ± 0.8 cm with a 95% CI. Distance of the sural nerve to 2.54 cm proximal to the lateral malleolus (M2) was 4.5 ± 0.7 cm with a 95% CI.

Meaning: The sural nerve courses 4.6 cm posterior to the fibula at the fibular head and midsection of the fibula, and 4.5 cm at 2.54 cm proximal to the lateral malleolus.

INTRODUCTION

The sural nerve (SN) is one of the primary sensory nerves that give sensation to the lower extremity. It supplies innervation to the lower posterolateral aspect of the leg1 and lateral aspect of the foot from the ankle and heel to the fifth digit.2 The underlying arterial supply therein due to its location and course is considered to be from the angiosomes derived from the perforators of the posterior tibial and peroneal arteries.3 Many clinical maneuvers and surgical procedures are performed around the lateral aspect of the distal extremity of the leg, ankle, and foot. The SN is often used for cable harvesting procedures2 and is commonly injured in procedures of the lower extremity due to its highly variable location and branching patterns. This could result in a considerable risk of iatrogenic injuries during any procedure of the lower extremity due to the coursing path of these variably located nerves.

It is generally understood that the SN arises from the tibial nerve as what is known as the medial sural cutaneous nerve (MSCN). It is then joined by the peroneal communicating branch, which is a ramus of the common peroneal nerve (CPN).2 This classic formation is what has been understood for decades on end, but studies throughout time have shown that this classically taught formation is much more complex and diverse than originally devised (Fig. 1). With this in mind, research has been conducted to further understand the variants of the nerve at hand to shed light on the nature of it. With such variability, surgical interventions within the region of its course could potentially, if not cautious, damage the nerve upon an attempt on the procedure.

SN formation on a fresh–frozen specimen. Identified here are the MSCN and LSCN coursing through the lower leg to form the SN more distally. (Photograph courtesy of Dr. Edgardo R. Rodriguez-Collazo, Chicago, Ill.)

In surgical interventions such as open reduction internal fixation to address displaced fractures, SN injury is plausible in the posterolateral approach to the ankle. In regards to flaps in reconstructive plastic surgery of the lower extremity, the nerve is commonly severed, leading to formation of a neuroma, paresthesia, or a decrease in sensation on the lateral aspect of the foot.4 Understanding these anatomical variations are paramount in regard to surgical relevance. It is recommended that care be taken to look for the SN extensively. This information should be used to help plan surgical approaches to minimize iatrogenic injury to the SN.

This cadaveric study was conducted to provide a surgical perspective to the documented variants of the SN and further insight into the same. The final objective is to offer a comprehensive and practical guide for preventing iatrogenic injuries to the SN during posterior compartment procedures of the lower limb, given their identified variants.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Due to availability of specimens at the institution, 25 formalin preserved lower limbs were dissected at the popliteal fossa and distally to the rearfoot. The cadaveric specimens were placed prone. Before the initial incision, the fibular head and distal aspect of the lateral malleolus were identified. A longitudinal incision was made along the length to the fibula upon successful identification of the SN variations. Furthermore, an incision was made in the popliteal region to connect the longitudinal incision, and a horizontal incision was performed near the distal end of the fibula. Subcutaneous dissection was performed thereafter to create a flap and expose the SN extending from the posterior fibula to the posterior central aspect of the lower limb (Fig. 2).

Posterior view of the cadaveric SN dissection approach with the head of the fibula and lateral malleolus as landmarks. (Illustrative overlay courtesy of Shawn Ali, North Chicago, Ill.)

The trajectory of the SN was measured with a digital tool, the Apple Inc. Measure application (Palo Alto, Calif.). Before taking digital measurements, the accuracy of the application was cross-referenced with a standard anatomical measurement ruler (Fig. 3). The measurements obtained from both methods were found to be equal with similar margin of error. Specific measurements were taken: the length of fibula, which was subsequently divided into thirds, and upon exposure of the SN, the distance of the nerve in relation to the posterior aspect of the fibula in each of the thirds was also measured with the lower third distance of the nerve being measured at 2.54 cm above the distal tip of the lateral malleolus. Variations were observed and classified with the use of Orascoptic (Madison, Wis.) surgical loupes. The classifications determined were based on SN branching variation types as reported by Ramakrishnan et al,5 and compared with those as reported by Steele et al.6 Any SN variations that showed convergence were measured from the posterior aspect of the fibula to the length of the distance of where the convergences were identified.

Cross referencing the distance between the posterior border of the fibula to the SN.

The mean distance from the fibula to the SN was calculated at three anatomical locations. Location 1 (M1) was from the head of the fibula to the SN (or MSCN). Location 2 (M3) was from 2.54 cm proximal to the distal tip of the lateral malleolus to the SN, and the third location (M2) was from the shaft of the fibula at the halfway point between the two aforementioned locations to the SN (Figs. 3 and 4). The variability from the means at locations M1, M2, and M3 were also calculated at 1.96 SDs [95% confidence interval (CI)].

DISCUSSION

Variations of the SN

The SN complex is composed of four interrelated components: the MSCN, the LSCN, the peroneal communicating nerve, and the SN.1 Given the variation between patients of the configuration of the SN in the way their components connect to form the nerve in question, researchers recognized a need for a standardized method of classification to describe varying compositions of the SN and its corresponding morphologies.5–7 The classification systems were created to limit the description of the SN components, morphologies, side of union, contributions, symmetry between limbs, and various cutaneous insertions.8

Ramakrishnan et al5 provided a PRISMA-driven meta-analysis of the SN variants and defined further the SN morphology, indicating six general classes. Steele et al6 further expanded on this and identified more types and subtypes upon their endeavors and identified 8–13 variations. Garção and colleagues in 20237 continued and listed 6 types of anatomical patterns of the SN, identifying additional subtypes that were previously not noted by Steele and colleagues.6 The most prevalent sites for the SN formation seen in their study was seen in the middle and lower thirds of the leg.7 The group concluded that the most prevalent SN formation pattern found in their research was the union of the MSCN with the LSCN. Another point noted in their research was that they found there to be differences regarding the geographical subgroup and the subject age.7 The distinct classes that were defined by Ramakrishnan and colleagues5 first were bifurcate derived. Type 1 involved the union of the MSCN and the peroneal communicating nerve, which is the classically taught variant and the most well-known. Type 2 is a union of the MSCN and the LSCN.5 Ramakrishnan and colleagues5 were also able to categorize the independent straight branch variants that are located more distally. Type 3 is characterized by the MSCN continuing without the presence of the peroneal communicating nerve. Type 4 is a straight branch of the peroneal communicating nerve alone. Type 5 is an SN branching directly from the LSCN.5 Finally, type 6 is a straight branch from the sciatic nerve.5

Additions to the distinct classes defined by Ramakrishnan and colleagues5 were identified by Steele et al,5 creating two additional variants and two subvariants. Types 7 and 8 were defined as being the new subvariants identified by Steele et al.6 Type 7 was characterized by a parallel arrangement of the MSCN and LSCN coursing distally. The MSCN terminates as lateral calcaneal nerve branches, however, before terminating, it provides a sural communicating branch that anastomoses with the parallel LSCN to give the formation of the SN. Type 8 had a similarly parallel MSCN culminating as lateral calcaneal nerve branches, but the SN originates independently from the CPN. Steele et al6 stated that there were two consistent morphologies for the type 1A that held true to the verbiage defined by Ramakrishnan et al5; however, they gave significant differentiating visuals to the contribution of their SN complexes (Fig. 8). Garção et al7 subsequently identified more variants; however, it is important to note that they did not have types 7 or 8 reported in the same fashion as Steele et al.6 This includes additions of the subvariants in types 1, 2, and 5.7 With these additional nerve variation findings, this now leads surgeons to be able to address potentially up to 13 nerve variations.

Clinical Significance

SN injury is also plausible in the posterolateral approach to the ankle in surgical interventions, such as open reduction internal fixation, to address displaced fractures. Ghani et al9 utilized the posterolateral approach on 28 cadaveric legs, and found that the SN crossed their incision site in 26 of the 28 incisions. Incisions were made halfway between the medial border of the fibula and the lateral border of the Achilles tendon, extending approximately from the tip of the lateral malleolus.9 The distance from the distal tip of the lateral malleolus to the proximal location in which the SN crossed the incision was measured. The data obtained showed that in 26 of the 28 cadavers, the SN crossed the incision site between 2.7 and 4.5 cm proximal to the tip of the lateral malleolus.9 This shows that in 78.5% of their study, there was a 1.8-cm window where the SN was located, and Ghani et al9 were then able to reasonably state a prediction of where the SN’s location could be. What this also displayed is that 21.5% of the time, the location of the SN would be unknown and could not be predicted due to individual anatomical variations. Understanding these anatomical variations is paramount in regards to surgical relevance. It is recommended that care be taken to look for the SN extensively, not only in the location of the lateral malleolus but closer to the Achilles tendon approximately as well.9 This information should be used to help plan surgical approaches and minimize iatrogenic injury to the SN.

In reconstructive plastic surgery of the lower extremity, the reverse sural artery perforator flap is one of the most commonly used methods of flap procedures for reconstruction.4 In efforts to achieve a sufficient pedicle length, the SN is commonly severed during the flap elevation, leading to formation of a neuroma, paresthesia, or a decrease in sensation on the lateral aspect of the foot.4 Extreme caution must be given to the pedicle, given that it is the pivoting point of the flap. SN injury is one of the most consistent outcomes when attempting a reverse sural artery perforator flap due to the needed pedicle length, making preservation of the SN a challenging task. However, complete SN preservation can minimize donor site morbidity.4 Reconstructive methods of the SN may be used if a neurectomy is necessary to dissect the flap. A nerve transfer, regenerative peripheral nerve interface, or targeted muscle reinnervation may be utilized to prevent end neuroma formation.10–14 Therefore, the purpose of presenting these research data is to preoperatively plan and be familiar with anatomical variations where the goal is to address or avoid the SN.

Kim et al4 created a sural-sparing technique for the reverse sural artery perforator flap procedure that would allow surgeons to reach the aforementioned length with minimization of injury to the SN when executed. This pedicle length gives the artery perforator flap an increase to the rotation arc that would allow the flap to cover most defects within the distal third of the leg with no injury to the nerve fascicles, leading to an expected reduction in the risk of sensory morbidity, pain, and neuroma formation in the lower extremity.4

The goal of this study was to identify the SN variations while revisiting the documented variations in the literature. Once our cohort of cadaveric limbs was assessed, the data were taken to evaluate the course of the SN according to the posterior border of the fibula. The purpose of this information is to utilize these data landmarks in surgical planning. Surgical planning may include addressing the SN directly or attempting to avoid it entirely through an orthopedic or plastic and reconstructive flap procedure.

Understanding the variations of the SN is of utmost importance for the surgeon addressing pathology in the region where the SN courses to minimize potential iatrogenic injury. The SN is regularly at risk due to the location and proximity to commonly used surgical approaches. Generally, potential mechanisms of iatrogenic injury derive from what would be the purpose of a surgical intervention and operating room positioning.2 Other common causes of SN injuries include trauma, compression and other primary pathologies. In understanding the variations being presented, identifying locations could improve the recognition of areas and circumstances in which these injuries occur.

CONCLUSIONS

The SN has many documented variations. The data presented provide a guide for the lower extremity reconstructive surgeon to its course. Through preoperative planning in correlation with the fibula, the course of the SN can be avoided or identified, depending on the surgical intervention. Noting the anatomic variations and the average location, this information can guide the surgeon and aid in preoperative planning.

Objectives of this cadaveric study encompassed a two-prong approach by offering insights into its distinct anatomical course as it relates to surgical clinical practice, and further informing the community at large for more measured practices for optimal incision techniques for both opening and closing the surgical site at hand. If there is a preoperatively identified SN injury, then the data collected and analyzed in this article can give further direction to the course of the SN to address the pathology in reference to the posterior border of the fibula. Note, that if an SN was to be addressed for a chronic pain patient, this would typically take place in the leg, proximal to the zone of injury, in the foot or distal leg. If the SN is anticipated to be encountered, as in a flap procedure, then preoperative planning will have to be conducted as to how to address or avoid the SN and its branches. Although a greater sample size would be needed to identify prevalence of each variant and verify the course of the SN, the data provide a guide with a 95% CI as to the location of the SN at three different locations within the lower limb.
