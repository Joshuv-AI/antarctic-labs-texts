Perceptions of tuberculosis and home-based infection screening among families and providers in Madagascar: a qualitative study
Authors: Voahirana Tantely Annick Andrianantoandro, Mamy Jean Jacques Razafimahatratra, Martine Audibert, Rila Ratovoson, Niaina Rakotosamimananan, Jonathan Hoffmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMadagascar is a country with a high tuberculosis (TB) burden, and the incidence of the disease has remained unchanged since 2013. TB detection largely relies on passive strategies, with little attention given to TB infections (TBIs), which are silent reservoirs that fuel future active TB cases.MethodThis qualitative study investigated how Malagasy communities perceive TB and an innovative approach for TBI screening and care. This approach was developed through the APRECIT operational research project in partnership with the National Tuberculosis Control Program (NTP). Semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted between March and May 2023 with a sample of stakeholders and health workers (26 HWs) at the national, regional and community levels and focus groups with household contacts (46 HHCs) of TB patients.ResultsThe fight against TB remains challenging in Madagascar. Community knowledge of TB is limited, and the disease is perceived as shameful. Individuals with TB and their families face stigmatization within their own family and the community. At the provider level, HWs reported challenges in promptly detecting and treating TB people with TB due to structural constraints within the health system and barriers at the household (HH) level. The introduction of home-based screening for TBI was perceived as a way to interrupt disease progression and help reduce stigma. For HWs, this strategy helps to limit TB transmission. However, HWs expressed concerns about giving treatment to asymptomatic people and the replicability of the strategy at the national level.ConclusionImproving communication about TB and implementing a home-based screening strategy for HHCs to identify individuals at high risk of TB progression have the potential to improve TB control in Madagascar.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24333-4.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the leading causes of death from a single infectious disease worldwide [1]. In 2023, an estimated 10.8 million people fell ill with TB, and 1.253 million TB deaths were recorded [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) asserts that implementing household contact (HHC) tracing of index TB patients (ICs), defined as individuals with microbiologically confirmed pulmonary TB, is a key component of TB control and recommends home visits as a contact investigation strategy [2, 3]. To prevent infection or the progression from infection to disease, the WHO also recommends providing preventive treatment (TPT) to at-risk individuals, including HHCs under the age of five and/or those living with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). This process involves identifying high-risk individuals and performing TB infection (TBI) screening via specific tests, such as interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) or tuberculin skin tests (TSTs) [2, 3].

Madagascar is a country with a high TB burden, and the yearly estimated TB incidence has remained unchanged since 2013. In 2023, the incidence of the disease was estimated at 233 [149–336] per 100,000 people, with 47,723 newly diagnosed people and relapses, and the estimated mortality rate was 38 [23–58] per 100,000 people [4, 5]. The current National Tuberculosis Control Program (NTP) relies on passive case finding (PCF) for detecting people with TB. Patients who are suspected of having TB symptoms are referred to diagnostic and treatment centers (CDTs) when they seek care at health centers.CDT social workers are responsible for patient follow-up. This includes distributing medication, updating patient forms, and tasking community health workers (CHWs) to follow up with individuals lost to follow-up.

The PCF approach has its limitations in terms of reaching the target population, as socioeconomic and geographic factors heavily influence access to healthcare [6–11]. In Madagascar, limited access to healthcare is the main reason for under- and late diagnosis and treatment failure of TB. Geographical remoteness, challenges regarding the health system and social barriers—such as delays in seeking care, limited knowledge of TB and the stigma associated with it [12–17]—engender delays in diagnosis and treatment and increase the number of individuals who are lost to follow-up. Little action has been taken to address TBI, despite the high burden of TBI [18, 19].

In line with the TBI management WHO approach [2], an operational research project was jointly carried out by the Institute Pasteur de Madagascar (IPM), NTP and the Fondation Mérieux (FM) (APRECIT 2019–2023). The objectives of the APRECIT project are fourfold: (i) establish a community intervention model to identify HHCs at high risk of developing TB disease; (ii) assess the incidence of TBI among HHCs; (iii) assess the prognostic value of the IGRA and TST in identifying individuals at higher risk of disease progression as candidates for TPT; and (iv) evaluate the cost-effectiveness of the use of the IGRA or TST alone or in combination [20].

The APRECIT project aimed to propose a new, complementary strategy for the NTP, provided that it was both effective and efficient. For this reason, it is important to understand the views of the stakeholders involved, including beneficiaries and healthcare workers. Our paper is original, as studies on TB management in Madagascar are based mainly on epidemiological studies, and only a few have focused on disease perception or evaluation of the TB program. Our study focuses on how health providers and communities perceive TB and the APRECIT strategy (AS). At the national program and health provider levels, we assessed the perceptions and constraints faced by medical and administrative staff regarding the current strategy and AS implementation within the current NTP. At the community level, we assessed AS beneficiaries’ and their families’ perceptions of TB, the concept of TBI, and its management (home screening for TBI, TPT for TBI individuals at high risk of TB progression, and preference between two screening tests: the IGRA-TST).

Materials and methods

Tuberculosis trends in Madagascar from 2008 to 2023

TB control has been an issue in the country since colonization (1896–1960), but the current NTP has been in place only since 1991. CDTs are present in all 23 regions of the country, but the number of functioning CDTs has barely increased: from 205 CDTs in 2005 to 228 in 2018 and 253 in 2023 [21]. TB diagnosis has shown a positive trend (annual increase in diagnoses) (Fig. 1), although the incidence rate has stagnated since 2013: from 293 per 100,000 people in 2000 to 241 per 100,000 people in 2010 and 233 per 100,000 people since 2013 (remaining at this level through 2023) [21]. The death rate decreased from 5.3% in 2008 to 3.9% in 2023; however, the therapeutic success rate has changed very little, increasing from 80.9% in 2008 to 82.1% in 2023 (Fig. 2) [21].

APRECIT strategy (AS)

The APRECIT study, which was conducted in Madagascar from December 2019 to July 2023, was a prospective cohort study [22]. It involved recruiting ICs and their HHCs and then following up with the HHCs over an 18-month period. During this period, the HHCs underwent home-based TBI screening at baseline (using both the IGRA and TST), at months 6 and 12, and at month 18 (using the IGRA only). The TST involves a subcutaneous injection, whereas the IGRA requires a blood sample. HHCs were identified through an active, community-based strategy specifically established for the study [22]. In collaboration with the CDTs, the IPM oversaw the implementation of this strategy. IPM clinical research assistants (CRAs) were responsible for identifying and enrolling HHCs and for conducting follow-ups. Between December 2019 and December 2020, 389 ICs were recruited, and 1,030 HHCs were identified through community-based interventions [23].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the prognostic value of the IGRA and TST in predicting progression from TBI to TB. This evaluation sought to propose to the NTP the test with the best prognostic value for use as TBI screening and to provide TPT to those individuals at high risk of developing active TB. According to the results of the APRECIT project evaluation (biological and economic studies), the TST with an induration of 14 mm had the best predictive value and was the most efficient strategy for Madagascar [23, 24]. Consequently, it was proposed to the NTP as a highly effective tool.

Study setting

The AS involved five CDTs, all situated in Antananarivo, the capital city within the Analamanga region (Fig. 3). Among these, three are public: CHU_Fenoarivo (in the rural commune of Fenoarivo), DAT/CHUSSPA_Analakely, and CENHOSOA_HOMI (in the urban commune of Antananarivo Renivohitra). The remaining two are private: EKAR Anatihazo Isotry and HL_67ha (also located in the urban commune of Antananrivo Renivohitra) (Fig. 3). CHU_Fenoarivo and DAT/CHUSSPA are known to be among the CDTs used for TB treatment. The 5 CDTs were chosen on the basis of the NTP’s previous data, which showed that these five CDTs had the highest number of people with confirmed TB among people suspected of having TB presenting for consultations.

Study participants

The perception study was conducted between February and April 2023. At the national level, we interviewed institutional stakeholders, including the NPT and nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) involved in the supply chain for TB commodities (the Catholics Relief Service (CRS) and Salama). At the regional level, interviews were conducted with staff from the Regional Public Health Directorate (DRSP) and the District Public Health Services (SSD) of Analamanga. At the community level, we interviewed various HWs from IPM and CDTs and HHCs. The participants in the interviews were selected on the basis of several inclusion criteria (Table 1).

Data collection and analysis

The interviews with the NTP and CDTs focused on general TB control strategies, evaluation of the current strategy, perceptions of the AS and its potential introduction into the NTP. For the CDTs and IPM, the interviews focused on the practical implementation of the AS: perceptions of the home-based screening strategy, the use of IGRA and/or TST, and treatment for TBI. For NGOs, the discussions focused on the organization and distribution of equipment, consumables, and medicines related to TB. The purpose of these surveys was to understand the possibility of expanding the AS according to the local context and to identify the main concerns and challenges of the NTP and CDTs.

The interviews in the FGDs focused on perceptions of TB, home screening for TBI, the use of TST and IGRA tests, adherence to TPT, and direct or opportunity costs borne by families as part of their participation in the APRECIT project.

The first and second authors conducted all the interviews. The interview guides were piloted with four HCs and two HWs who did not form part of the study group. HWs were interviewed face-to-face during their free time in their offices, while FGDs took place at a time and location agreed upon by the community. All the participants were involved voluntarily, informed of the objectives of the surveys, and of their right to participate or withdraw from the survey at any time. All of the participants provided their consent to participate and completed the survey.

The interviews were conducted in Malagasy, and each interview was audio-recorded and lasted for approximately 1 h and 2 min to 1 h and 15 min for in-depth interviews and 1 h and 15 min to 1 h and 30 min for FGDs.

The first and second authors translated the interviews from Malagasy into French using clean verbatim transcripts. The interviews were coded by theme, and for each theme, analysis was carried out to identify the central ideas, the ideas that did not seem to vary, and the differences.

Ethical considerations

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. They also conformed to the ethical standards of the national research committee ‘Comité d’Ethique de la Recherche Biomédicale auprès du Ministère de la Santé Publique CERBM’ (approval no. n°156 MSANP/SG/AMM/CERBM/).

The IC recruitment protocol includes a clause on how to handle the risk of stigma during home visits. The protocol states that when patients sign the informed consent form, they also provide their consent for their household contacts to be investigated at their home. Patients who feared being stigmatized were within their right to opt out of the project. In addition, field teams only discuss the project with HHCs once they are inside the home. This is to avoid gossip, which could lead to the patient and their family being stigmatized.

The staff responsible for data collection are bound by professional secrecy. Additionally, precautions were taken to protect the confidentiality of the participants’ data and biological results. These precautions included ensuring that data containing surnames and first names were not entered into the database; only the identifier given to each participant was recorded. Computer data entry and access to the file were also secured via passwords. For the qualitative study, participant confidentiality was maintained using the CDT name instead of identifying HWs by their status or HHCs by their names.

Results

The results distinguish three key elements: TB perception, new strategy acceptability, and programmatic barriers.

Officials’ and health workers’ opinions

TB perception: the way that tuberculosis is perceived in the community makes it challenging to fight

According to HWs, the community knows what TB is. People are aware that it is a contagious, curable, and fatal disease if it is left untreated. They also know that TB manifests through coughing, emaciation, and hemoptysis (coughing up blood) and that only pulmonary TB is commonly recognized. However, according to HWs, the fight against TB remains difficult because it is still considered a shameful disease and is often associated with poverty. Some people believe that patients and their families remain contagious even after treatment. This misconception contributes to stigmatization against those affected. This phenomenon even occurs among HWs.

“Society classifies TB as a disease affecting poor HHs living in precarious situations. Therefore, some people believe that it does not affect people in good living conditions. However, there are wealthy people with important jobs who weigh over 70 kg and suffer from it…It is often difficult to convince these people who they have TB. At first, they deny it; then, they go to other health centers to confirm that they truly do have TB.” HW_EKAR.

For example, if a child has TB, their parents will advise them not to tell their classmates that they are ill for fear of being rejected.” HW_CHUSSPA.

Discrimination can allow TB patients and families to be discreet, either by moving to other places or by not going to the CDT to pick up their medication. According to the results of IPM follow-up, the most common reason for failure to follow-up is removal (35 individuals at M6, 38 at M12 and 17 at M18) [23].

New strategy acceptability: positive reception of the APRECIT strategy as a way to break the tuberculosis transmission chain

The staff of the NTP and the five CDTs all support the implementation of a system that will be effective in fighting TB spread and progression. They consider that screening for TBI will contribute to breaking the transmission chain and that appropriate interventions can be applied for TBI management. In addition, the introduction of a home screening strategy enables more individuals with TB to be detected.

“This new approach makes it possible to detect TB early and limits its spread. These tests are more accurate, so the risk of false negatives is lower. With sputum tests, there’s a high risk of false negatives because the results depend on the sputum collected by the patient.” HW_HL_67ha.

“Screening for TBI is a good approach, as it helps prevent the progression to active TB in contacts of individuals with TB. It also helps to reassure contacts of TB patients, who are also concerned about having TB Themselves.” HW_DAT/CHUSSPA”.

These tests detect patients with TBI even if they are not yet contagious, which reduces the transmission of TB. This allows for the implementation of measures in contacts of individuals with TB before disease appears.” HW_EKAR.

HWs also adhere to both types of tests used to detect TBI but seem to prefer the IGRA. The TST is perceived as less reliable and does not allow a diagnosis to be made with confidence and certainty because of its interference with BCG vaccines, which are mandatory in the country.

Unlike the TST, the IGRA test is administered in a laboratory. The preference is also based on test performance and other benefits associated with it. In terms of results, laboratory technicians believe that there is less risk of false-negative results with the IGRA. It also allows for the detection of individuals with extrapulmonary TB. From a technical point of view, IGRA is considered less invasive than intubating individuals who want to be tested but are unable to provide sputum. HWs believe that blood samples are easier to obtain than sputum samples are and carry a lower risk of bacterial exposure. In addition, the IGRA test is perceived as more discreet for HHCs than for sputum samples that would have an immediate association with TB (patients are given two containers for collecting their sputum). The samples were taken at home at the indicated times, and the containers were returned to the laboratory.

“HHCs may accept blood samples instead of sputum samples because sometimes asymptomatic patients or those who do not cough do not even obtain sputum. They just give saliva, and sometimes we even end up having to intubate them.” HW_CENHOSOA_HOMI.

Programmatic barriers: current strategy weaknesses make TB control difficult

According to HWs, the first limitation of the PCF is related to its approach to identifying people who have TB. This process has limitations because it heavily depends on the IC’s ability to persuade his or her family to undergo screening, especially if HHCs are hesitant to be screened and treated. This applies mostly to parents of children under five years of age and patients lost to follow-up. Among the five CDTs, CHWs frequently reported that children under five years of age were not receiving TPT or that people with symptoms were not thinking about being tested.

“One disadvantage of this fixed strategy is that few case contacts of individuals with TB come to the health center. Some contacts of TB patients are not convinced by patients’ awareness-raising efforts. TB patients also lack the necessary information and knowledge about TB.” HW_CHU_Fenoarivo.

“One disadvantage of this fixed strategy is that few case contacts of individuals with TB come to the health center. Some contacts of TB patients are not convinced by patients’ awareness-raising efforts. TB patients also lack the necessary information and knowledge about TB.” HW_CHU_Fenoarivo.

“Other symptomatic individuals do not come to the center because they are not convinced by the patient’s sensitization efforts. They only occur when the disease worsens. Additionally, there are children under the age of five who are not receiving chemoprophylaxis because their parents do not bring them to the center.” HW_EKAR.

Additionally, there are children under the age of five who are not receiving chemoprophylaxis because their parents do not bring them to the center.” HW_EKAR.

“Other individuals do not come to the health center for TB screening, even when they show suspicious signs, because they do not believe the patients’ explanations.” HW_DAT/CHUSSPA.

In general, to mitigate this reluctance, doctors inquire about other family members before informing patients of a positive TB test.“Before informing a patient who his sputum examination is positive, I first ask if he has any children under five living with him. Only then do I announce the positive result and explain that all children under five living with him should be brought to the health center for chemoprophylaxis because children are vulnerable to TB. In my experience, some patients lie about having children under five after the results have been announced because they do not want to bring their children to the health center.” HW_EKAR.

The second limitation is related to the definition of “contact of an individual with TB”. Among HWs, there are divergent opinions regarding which HHCs are at risk of disease progression and require treatment. For some HWs, at-risk HHCs are individuals who share the same bed or bedroom with TB patients. With respect to children under five years of age, some HWs consider them vulnerable and in need of treatment, whereas others do not believe that treatment is necessary. For other HWs, all HHCs living in precarious conditions must receive TPT, as their immune systems are much weaker due to malnutrition.

“What I have noticed is that some TB patients have only recently contracted TB, so they may not have transmitted it to those around them. However, the protocol requires us to treat all children living in the same HH as the patients, so we just follow suit.” HW_HL_67ha.

“It is not necessary to provide chemoprophylaxis to HHCs because these people have a good immune system and can fight TB. They should only be treated if they develop symptoms of the disease.” HW_CENHOSOA_HOMI.

The third limitation is related to the resources available for patient follow-up. This concerns referred patients, HHCs to be sensitized, and ICs lost to follow-up. Some CDTs considered reference centers often receive patients who are referred by other CDTs or health centers. For example, from 2020 to 2022, the proportion of patients in the CHUSSPA is expected to be between 27.9% and 63.3% of individuals identified with TB (CHUSSPA report, 2022). At the CHU of Fenoarivo, the CDT referred to 246 and 426 patients in 2021 and 2022, respectively. These individuals are diagnosed as positive for TB but are referred to CDTs near their homes to start treatment. For the two CDTs, there was no follow-up for patients referred to other CDTs. Community follow-up of CDT patients is also problematic. Among the five CDTs, the dropout rate ranged from 1 to 9%, and it occurred mainly during directly observed treatment (DTO). Programmatic barriers: challenges to Aprecit strategy implementation and replicability

From a medical point of view, HWs are concerned about providing treatment to asymptomatic individuals. Given the low rate of disease progression, the potential risk of treatment resistance in the case of false positives, and the risk of asymptomatic individuals dropping out of treatment, NTP and CDT staff are skeptical about initiating TPT for asymptomatic HHCs. In this context, HWs suggested that TPT should be given only to HHCs who are at high risk of disease progression. Therefore, for the NTP, the use of IGRA or TST remains a referral test, and it would be better to prioritize active case finding in the community to detect individuals with TB. Other HWs go even further and suggest that TPT for children under five years should be based only on TBI tests.

“I would prefer this test to be done because I’m concerned about giving children under five six months of chemoprophylaxis when we do not know if they have been contaminated.” HW_CHU_Fenoarivo.

“I would prefer this test to be done because I’m concerned about giving children under five six months of chemoprophylaxis when we do not know if they have been contaminated.” HW_CHU_Fenoarivo.

“With this test, we can decide whether to give chemoprophylaxis to a child. For example, if the result is negative, we do not give it.” HW _ HL_ 67ha.

“With this test, we can decide whether to give chemoprophylaxis to a child. For example, if the result is negative, we do not give it.” HW _ HL_ 67ha.

From a technical point of view, regarding the adoption of the TBI screening strategy, there were no specific comments from CRS, SALAMA, DRSP, or SSD about integrating the TBI inputs and equipment into the current ordering and routing process. However, all HWs at the five CDTs agreed that they do not have the necessary resources in terms of infrastructure, equipment and human resources to replicate the home-based screening strategy at their level and even more so at the national level. For the NTP, the AS scale-up will be difficult due to the low number of CDTs able to provide TBI screening with the equipment required for the IGRA test. The example given is the use of GeneXpert since 2021, when it was recommended by the NTP for active TB screening. As of June 2022, only 25 of Madagascar’s 253 CDTs were equipped with Xpert MTB/RIF devices for TB screening [21]. In addition, most of these CDTs essentially reserve GeneXpert for diagnosing patients suspected of having multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) because of the cost of molecular testing, environmental constraints (e.g., regular and stable electricity and correct temperature), and equipment supply and maintenance constraints (e.g., limited number of tests). Households’ opinions

Participant characteristics

Forty-six participants were involved in the study: nine men and thirty-seven women, with ages ranging from 19 to 75 years. The average age of the focus group participants was 40.3 years for women and 47.1 years for men. In terms of marital status, nine were single (two men and seven women), twenty-six were married (twenty women and six men), and eleven were widowed (one man and ten women). In total, twenty-two participants stopped their studies at the primary level, twenty had secondary education, and only four had pursued higher education. Women’s activities are focused primarily on laundry (eight participants), food sales (eight participants), and housemaid work (six participants). Other jobs held by women include seam stress (two participants), utensil seller (three participants), masseuse, secretary, cashier, and janitor. Men’s work included carpenter (two participants), grocer (two participants), driver, welder, and farmer. Eight were inactive: students (three participants), unemployed (four participants), and retired.

TB perception

Limited knowledge about TB

Among the five CDTs, knowledge of TB focuses on the manifestation and severity of the disease. Knowledge regarding transmission is limited. HHCs consider TB to be a serious, fatal, but curable contagious disease. TB symptoms include a hacking cough, weight loss, loss of appetite, and fever. HHs are also aware that under certain conditions, TB can relapse.

A shameful disease that contributes to discrimination

According to HHCs from the five CDTs, TB is still perceived by the community as a shameful disease. Those who spoke openly about it were either people living with neighbors who were also affected or those who said it was a disease like any other. TB is considered a shameful disease because of the negative stereotypes associated with it (e.g., a disease of the poor, alcoholics, smokers, etc.). People with TB are also discriminated against because of the contagious nature of the disease. This discrimination often begins within the family, by isolating the patient and his or her personal belongings, or among neighbors, by avoiding all physical contact. Discrimination can have harmful effects, ranging from the loss of neighborly help to the loss of jobs or customers, or avoiding marriage with people who have had TB or who have a family history of the disease.

“I was so panicked to learn that a TB patient was living with us that I isolated him and kept his plates, spoons and cups separate during the 6 months of treatment. “I did this because I do not want my children to contract tuberculosis as well. Some HHCs are aware that patients are no longer contagious after receiving medical treatment but continue to stigmatize them. TB stigma and conditional healthcare seeking

Before the Aprecit project, HHCs believed that living in close contact with a TB patient put them at risk of becoming infected with rapid symptom manifestation. Consequently, learning that an HH member has TB usually causes panic among other family members, especially if HHs include children or elderly people with chronic diseases (diabetes, hypertension). In most HHs, screening and care are almost immediate, which is not the case for adults, as it is not considered necessary to seek medical care as long as the illness is considered not serious.

APRECIT strategy: acceptability and preferences of households

Positive reception of home-based screening for TBIs

It was only through monitoring carried out by the CRAs that the HHCs became aware of TBI. They knew that they could carry Mycobacterium TB without showing signs of the disease. They were also aware that they might or might not have developed the disease weeks or even months after infection, depending on their state of health and appropriate behavior to adopt (following the CRA’s instructions). None of the participants, including those who dropped out of the project, found it inconvenient to undergo medical examinations and tests when they were not sick or did not feel sick, given their awareness of the seriousness of TB. Testing for TBI and its follow-up were perceived positively, given the obvious risk of infection from living with a TB patient. Screening allowed them to know their status regarding the disease and to be free from permanent doubts that they experienced whenever they presented symptoms similar to those of TB.

“At first, I was puzzled by the idea of testing my children because they were healthy. We received the first test results, and all three results were negative. On the second test, however, my first child was positive. I decided to continue the follow-up testing because you could test negative at first and then become positive later.” FGD_CHU_Fenoarivo.

Home vs. health center visits

HHCs also argued in favor of home visits rather than health center visits. Among the participants, most were followed-up both at home and at their respective CDT. They also feel safer, freer, and have more time to talk to HWs in their homes than when they come to the CDT to discuss health issues. These moments provide them with opportunities to seek advice from HWs about any ailments the patient may be experiencing.

Preference for the IGRA test over the TST test

Between the TST and the IGRA, all the respondents preferred the IGRA. The choice is linked to the inconvenience associated with the TST. The IGRA is less uncomfortable than the TST is, which has been reported to cause pain, itching, and hardening at the injection site, sometimes leading to physical symptoms such as fever or numbness. The side effects of the TST test can impact a person’s ability to perform daily activities properly.

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that TB is still a difficult disease to fight in Madagascar there are two main reasons for this. First, like other diseases, such as mental illness, epilepsy, cancer, or HIV, TB is linked to stigma. Health-related stigma is defined as “a social process or related personal experience marked by exclusion, rejection, blame, or devaluation resulting from the experience or reasonable anticipation of adverse social judgment about a person or group identified with a particular health problem” [25]. The literature has shown that the stigma process is perpetuated by fear of contagion, a lack of knowledge about the disease, and the perception that those affected are poor and uneducated [26]. Two components of stigma have been identified: personal experiences of stigma within communities or families and self-stigma related to the anticipation of social consequences, with or without personal experience. Such anticipation may lead to a delay in seeking healthcare, impact adherence to treatment, or result in refusal of screening [27].

The second reason is that people do not spontaneously participate in disease prevention and screening programs, and poor adherence to medical regimens contributes to treatment failure. The health belief model (HBM), developed in the 1950 s, was designed to explain health-related behavior. This model was subsequently expanded to include six dimensions: perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy [28, 29].

Healthcare workers (HWs) highlight the stigma process—driven by fear of the disease and anticipation of social consequences—as a major barrier preventing household heads (HHs) from seeking care. They further noted that tuberculosis (TB) is often seen as a disease of poverty, which deepens feelings of shame. This challenge is compounded by existing strategies’ limitations in detecting and following up with all individuals with TB. HHs themselves reported this shame, and combined with the perceived contagiousness of TB, patients and their families frequently face discrimination. Our study revealed this discrimination across various levels: within households, among neighbors, and even from healthcare workers themselves. This pervasive stigmatization can lead individuals with TB to conceal their illness and, in some cases, even to self-discriminate by relocating.

Several studies have revealed the stigma experiences of TB patients and their families [16, 30–35]. The root causes of TB in patients and their families’ stigma include perceptions of TB as a disease affecting the poor [31, 36], limited knowledge about the disease [13–15, 37–41], and its contagious nature. In our study, these different perceptions were found, but the lack of understanding regarding the evolution and transmission of the disease seemed to be a dominant factor. HHs were unaware of TBI, suggested different modes of TB transmission and continued to discriminate against TB patients and their families after treatment. Similarly, despite standardized communication, HHs held different conceptions of the two types of tests used (IGRA/TST). While our study did not identify any cases of TB attributed to supernatural causes, other studies have indicated that some populations still view this disease as the consequence of a failure to observe cultural norms or as a social transgression [41–43]. It is highly probable that similar perceptions exist among the Malagasy population [44–46]. However, the general context in which this study was carried out—namely, a limited number of participants, individuals who had therefore received basic knowledge about TB through the APRECIT project, and those who were exposed to more information, particularly in an urban environment—did not allow these perceptions to be fully explored. These situations therefore argue for the need to propose cues to action, such as strengthening communication and awareness campaigns about TB and addressing not only the medical but also the sociocultural aspects of the disease. Communication should be considered a strategy in its own right, as should screening and treatment efforts. There is generally no communication about TB/TBI at the HH level, apart from very occasional and very targeted awareness-raising by CHWs for those lost to follow-up. A better understanding of the disease by all would reduce discrimination, improve adherence to the community screening strategy (both passive and active), and lead to better utilization of health care services. Therefore, an awareness campaign at the community level is essential to combat TB in Madagascar. This campaign must be better targeted to address the misconceptions about TB and the stigma associated with it.

Since 2012, the WHO guidelines have recommended home visits as a strategy for TB contact investigations [47], and research has shown the effectiveness of this approach in increasing the number of individuals with TB/TBI diagnosed and treated [43–45]. Our results also show that both HWs and HHs support the TBI screening strategy [51, 52]. For HWs, detecting TBI is a way to stop the transmission of the disease by offering treatment to patients at high risk of developing TB; additionally, a community-based approach helps detect more individuals with TB. For HHs, TBI screening enables appropriate behavior to avoid progression to the disease, which they perceive as a source of discrimination. In our case, HHs who were well informed about TBI and the IGRA test were motivated to undergo treatment to mitigate this risk. However, if the NTP uses the TST for scale-up, it is important to adjust the communication about the test to avoid misconceptions, as observed in our study. Therefore, HWs who are responsible for community visits especially need to be equipped with knowledge of TBI/TBI tests to provide adequate information to HHCs. Adequate knowledge transfer between HWs and HHCs regarding TBI during contact investigations facilitates screening and adherence to treatment [52].

In relation to strategy adoption, at the CDT and national levels, debates are emerging about how to scale up this strategy: which test to use, treating asymptomatic people, and providing human resources to support the implementation of this strategy. The first point of discussion related to the performance of IGRA/TST is not new [53–57]. IGRAs have been shown to have comparable sensitivity but higher specificity than TSTs do, which may overcome some of the latter’s limitations (e.g., cross-reactivity with BCG and error in measuring the size of induration). HWs prefer IGRA because it is a new test that is perceived as more efficient, whereas TST is viewed negatively because it interferes with the BCG vaccine. In addition, there is concern about providing treatment to asymptomatic people, which means that there is a need to identify those who are truly at risk of progressing to the disease properly. Indeed, some findings have indicated that asymptomatic and healthy conditions are barriers to TPT treatment [58, 59]. For HWs, the results of AS epidemiological and biological studies are relevant here (specifically, a TST with an induration of 14 mm has been proposed for the NTP as a reference test for TBI screening).

Finally, the most challenging aspect in the introduction of the AS strategy is the implementation of the community-based strategy [39, 60, 61]. For Madagascar, support from the TB program can resolve supply problems. However, the human resources required by CDTs to implement this new approach, with respect to the challenges, constraints and barriers faced by the CDTs and NTP with the current strategy (Table 3), remain challenging. In fact, a home screening strategy cannot simply be juxtaposed with the current strategy; it is inherently a community-based strategy. A community-based strategy requires strong commitment from laboratory staff at CDTs and CHWs, who are responsible for identifying where people live, conducting tests, and raising awareness of the need to adhere to treatment. In the current situation, the feasibility of scaling up such interventions is questionable, as CDTs are understaffed and CHWs are volunteers. In addition, although the home screening strategy is widely accepted by HHs, the question of HW discretion still arises in villages, where information spreads quickly. This problem has been identified in some studies, and it is important to consider it during the conception of a scaling-up plan [39, 62].

To overcome current obstacles and ensure the successful implementation of a complementary strategy for TB screening and treatment, the NTP should consider several concrete proposals: strengthening human resources in CDTs, improving the healthcare worker (HW) approach, and conducting public awareness campaigns to destigmatize TB.

First, the human resources in CDTs should be strategically increased to ensure the effective implementation of the community strategy. Before scaling up this strategy, it is also necessary to assess its impact and refine staffing needs. To do this, the strategy should be tested in two pilot CDTs—one in a rural area and one in an urban area. This would ease the current workload and enable the efficient tracing and follow-up of individuals who have been in contact with TB patients.

At the same time, it is important to provide HWs with enhanced training and sensitization to appropriate methods for engaging with communities in villages. Training should focus on an empathetic and nonstigmatizing approach during screening and treatment activities. Respectful and understanding communication will foster community acceptance of the strategy and encourage participation.

Finally, regular, targeted awareness campaigns should be conducted to educate the public about TB. In addition to campaigns run by CHWs, radio is an effective and widely used means of communication. Key messages should consistently emphasize that tuberculosis is a treatable disease similar to any other disease and should not be stigmatized. Changing community perceptions, reducing the stigma associated with the disease, and encouraging early access to care and screening are all key goals.

Our findings should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. A significant proportion of patients live in urban slums where frequent moves are common. This combined with the timing of our study meant that we were unable to select our participants from the initial sample. We also acknowledge an underrepresentation of rural individuals, as only one of the CDTs was located in a rural area. Furthermore, the overrepresentation of women in the FGDs might introduce a gender bias into the findings.
