Consistent performance between medical experts and non‐expert readers in forced‐choice lesion‐detection tasks with PET images
Authors: Craig K. Abbey, Sangtae Ahn, Muhan Shao, Darrin W. Byrd, Kristen A. Wangerin, Scott D. Wollenweber, Sanaz Behnia, Jean H. Lee, Amir Iravani, Murat Sadic, Delphine L. Chen, Paul E. Kinahan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLabeled data are used to train, validate, and test deep learning model observers (DLMOs) as well as linear model observers, such as channelized Hotelling observers (CHOs), for image quality assessment in many imaging modalities, including PET imaging. Ideally, these annotations would come from clinical readers, but these readers are often difficult to access, and it is unclear whether clinical experience is necessary in simple detection tasks involving a known signal profile at a specified location in an image using patient data for backgrounds.PurposeWe evaluate the potential for using non‐medical observers in simple detection tasks to train DLMOs and CHOs. To this end, we compare nuclear medicine physicians to medically naïve readers using a two‐alternative forced choice task with PET images and simulated low‐contrast lesions.MethodsExperimental conditions span 2 locations in the body (liver and lung), 3 lesion contrasts, 3 implementations of 2 different reconstruction algorithms based on ordered subsets expectation maximization and penalized likelihood. The image readers consist of four board‐certified nuclear medicine physicians and four non‐physician readers. We make direct comparisons of observer performance between the two reader groups and analyze the observer performance data using generalized linear mixed models. In addition, we train two DLMOs using the expert reader data and the non‐expert reader data, respectively, and compare the performance of the DLMOs in predicting the performance of (held‐out) expert readers. The DLMOs are based on a CNN and a combination of CNN and Transformer (SwinT) encoders. We also compare CHOs trained on the expert reader data and the non‐expert reader data.ResultsSimilarities among the expert readers and between expert and non‐expert readers, measured by a concordance metric and Cohen's kappa, were not found to be significantly different (p > 0.11). Analysis of variance found that reader expertise was only marginally significant (p = 0.07) and all interactions involving expertise were non‐significant (p > 0.2). Furthermore, the DLMOs trained on experts and on non‐experts showed similar performance in predicting experts for both CNN and CNN‐SwinT DLMOs (p > 0.26). The CHOs trained on experts and on non‐experts showed similar performance (p > 0.31).ConclusionsWe did not find substantive differences between the average performance of medical experts and non‐experts. This suggests that in simple detection tasks like these, clinical experience is not necessary, and we can use non‐medical observers instead of physicians when training models.

INTRODUCTION

Image reconstruction remains an active area of investigation for PET imaging and other tomographic imaging modalities. Reconstruction algorithms balance data fidelity with prior knowledge, typically smoothness constraints, that control local noise and resolution properties of the reconstructed images. Understanding this tradeoff for optimal task performance is an enduring goal in medical image perception, 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 and a central motivation for the development of computational models of the image reading process—referred to as model observers—that can predict reader performance. 4 , 5 These models can then be used to assess and optimize the choice of reconstruction algorithm using task‐performance as the measure of image quality.

Investigations of model observers have traditionally focused on simplified tasks and linear models that combine image pixel values into a decision variable that is intended to be representative of reader performance. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Simplified tasks are an abstraction of the clinical setting, in which abnormalities are constrained to a limited range of sizes, shapes, locations, and contrasts. These tasks are used because they are convenient, and they appear to be able to qualitatively replicate the impact of imaging effects. A number of studies have shown that model observers are consistent with human observer performance in a variety of these settings. 4 , 5 , 8 , 12 , 13 Additionally, Badano et al. 14 demonstrated that a model observer study using relatively simple detection tasks could replicate the overall performance of a large clinical observation study comparing digital mammography and digital breast tomosynthesis.

More recently, machine learning, and specifically deep learning, approaches have been advanced for the development of network model observers, 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 particularly when the image backgrounds come from patient data with no clearly defined sampling distribution. These models offer the possibility of capturing nonlinear combinations of pixel values in a decision variable, if these are representative of observer choices in training data. Indeed, deep learning model observers (DLMOs) based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been demonstrated to predict human observers better than conventional linear model observers do in some cases. 16 , 17 , 19 , 23 A substantial quantity of data is required to train and validate a neural network, in addition to testing on an independent dataset. As a result, these models create an ever‐greater need for labeled data. Ideally, this data would come from radiologists or clinicians who use images in practice. However, it can be a challenge to collect such data because of the limited availability of these expert readers. For simple detection and discrimination tasks that do not require extensive clinical reasoning, non‐expert readers may be able to alleviate this constraint in model‐observer development.

The underlying assumption of this reader substitution is that the tasks being considered are primarily limited by the perceptual properties of the human visual system rather than the clinical expertise of the readers. However, there has been relatively little testing of this assumption, particularly in PET imaging. We are aware of a few studies that have compared clinical and non‐clinical image readers in simple detection tasks, 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 but none of these are in the field of PET imaging. The findings of the cited studies demonstrate comparable performance, with some evidence favoring clinicians over non‐expert readers at the level of group averages. We note that there is a large literature examining the effects of clinician experience on radiological errors, with several examples in review articles. 28 , 29 But this is fundamentally different than a comparison of expert and non‐expert readers in simple “representative” tasks.

In this study, we report on comparisons of expert and non‐expert readers in a simple lesion detection study that evaluated the detection of a 15 mm spherical lesion embedded in patient backgrounds generated from resampling clinically‐acquired list‐mode PET data. Lesion counts from a simulation procedure are added to create “abnormalities” with a known lesion size and uptake. The imaging conditions evaluated include 6 reconstruction algorithms, locations in two‐different organs, and 3 levels of lesion contrast. Forced‐choice studies are used to evaluate detection performance and to compare these two types of readers. A model‐observer analysis of this image data has been reported previously, 30 and more recently, the data, along with the naïve reader performance, has been used to train deep learning observer models. 31 The contribution of this work has been the assessment of nuclear medicine physicians in the same conditions, and the comparison with non‐expert readers as well as model observers. We compare the different observer groups in psychophysical studies, and evaluate generalization for models trained on medically expert and non‐expert readers.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We used data from a study exploring the impact of noise in PET images with a forced‐choice detection paradigm. 30 , 32 The image stimuli (described below) were generated using list‐mode resampling techniques that allowed for two images to be generated that had independent noise but the same anatomical background, thereby focusing on the impact of noise. A simulation procedure 30 was used to add a small low‐contrast lesion to “signal‐present” stimuli with known ground‐truth status.

Image data

All patient image data were accessed through a data‐use agreement with the institution where the patients were evaluated (University Hospital Zurich) and included de‐identification of patient medical record data. The images consisted of clinical [18F] Fluorodeoxyglucose PET images captured as list‐mode data using a GE Discovery PET/CT 690 scanner (GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA). Simulated lesions were added to target stimuli in the forced‐choice pairs, using a simulation procedure (described below). A total of 81 patient datasets were used for this purpose.

The list‐mode PET data for each patient was divided into 2 independent but identically distributed subsets. One subset was used to generate non‐lesion “signal‐absent” stimuli, and the other was used to generate independent signal‐present stimuli that had a simulated lesion added to them. Simulated lesions in image space were forward projected through the scanner model, where effects of attenuation, scanner geometry, and detector efficiencies were implemented, 30 and then added to sinograms extracted from the signal‐present list‐mode data so that they were included in image reconstruction procedures. The simulated lesions were 15 mm spheres of increased tracer uptake. The lesions were located in the liver and lungs, and each lesion was simulated at 3 uptake levels with source‐to‐background ratios of 1.5, 1.75, and 2.0 to create stimuli with 3 levels of signal contrast. The lesion locations were manually selected to be in regions free from other anatomical structures (e.g., vasculature or bronchia) and away from the edges of the organs or other anatomical boundaries.

Images were reconstructed using ordered‐subset expectation maximization (OSEM) and penalized likelihood algorithms with block sequential regularized expectation maximization (BSREM). Both algorithms have a free‐parameter that controls the level of regularization. For the OSEM images, 33 this parameter represents the number of OSEM iterations, and 3 values were evaluated at 1, 2, and 3 iterations. For the BSREM algorithm, 34 which used a relative difference penalty function, 35 3 values representing the strength of the regularizer were evaluated for β = 250, 350, and 450. Thus, a total of 6 reconstructions were generated for each signal‐absent sinogram, and each signal‐present sinogram (encompassing 2 locations and 3 contrast levels) for every patient.

Region‐of‐interest (ROI) patches were cropped from the 3D reconstructions as stimuli for the psychophysical experiments so that lesions were centered in each image patch. The ROIs were 2D, 30×30 pixels, and located in the same location and orientation within the liver or lungs of a patient image. The ROI patches were normalized by their means and then displayed with a fixed window level and width, which were empirically determined for liver and lung, separately, prior to the observer study. A total of 2916 image pairs (81 patients × 2 locations × 3 contrasts × 6 reconstruction) were created for psychophysical evaluations. Note that for each patient and each organ (liver/lung), the lesion‐present images where the lesion locations are the same were not independent, whereas a lesion‐present image and a lesion‐absent image always had independent noise.

Observer studies

A total of 8 readers participated in the study. Half of these were not medical experts, consisting of image scientists recruited at the authors’ institutions. The other half were board‐certified and practicing nuclear medicine physicians recruited from the Department of Radiology at the University of Washington. The clinical readers all read PET images as part of their routine clinical duties.

The psychophysical procedure consisted of a two‐alternative forced‐choice detection study in which two images were presented, and the readers’ task was to select the image that contained the simulated lesion. The user interface used by subjects was created in‐house using MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). A screenshot of the interface window is shown in Figure 1 showing one of the 81 independent patient backgrounds. Lung and liver images over all patients and experimental conditions were averaged, and the mean image was shown above the lesion‐absent and present images as a reference for the target profile. Readers completed the study in 36 sessions with 81 paired lesion‐absent/present images, which were randomly selected per session. In each session, either liver image pairs or lung image pairs were presented. Example image pairs are shown in Figure 2. Before each session, the readers were trained with five randomly selected image pairs. Feedback indicating the lesion‐present image was given immediately after each selection for 5 training images at the beginning of each session to familiarize the readers with the forced‐choice procedure and the specific condition being tested.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis is used to evaluate the observer performance data using model selection criteria to identify a role for expertise effects. We model the observer performance data using generalized‐linear‐mixed‐effects models 36 with a logistic link function (i.e., logistic regression), as implemented in the fitglme procedure in MATLAB. Mixed‐effects models are appropriate because the data encompass several experimental conditions that constitute fixed effects, in addition to effects from various patients and readers that may be considered as random effects. We consider various combinations of fixed main effects and interactions, as well as random main effects and interactions.

The fixed effects include location in the body (2 levels: liver or lung), target contrast (3 levels), and reconstruction method (6 levels), as well as a potential effect of reader expertise (2 levels: expert or non‐expert). We model these as 3, or potentially 4, main effects in the model, depending on whether expertise is included in the model. When interactions between fixed effects are considered, we model 3 interactions when a reader expertise effect is excluded (all pairs of the 3 main effects) and 6 interactions when expertise is included (all pairs of the 4 main effects). All models (except for the null model described below) will consider readers and patients as random main effects. We will consider the effect of adding random interaction terms between any two effects that contain at least one random effect (e.g., interactions between reader and contrast are considered a random effect). Random interaction terms will be considered with expertise effects excluded (7 interactions: Reader×Contrast, Reader×Location, Reader×Reconstruction, Reader×Patient, Patient×Contrast, Patient×Location, and Patient×Reconstruction) and included (9 interactions, including the previous 7 and Reader×Expertise and Patient×Expertise).

A total of 9 models are considered based on different combinations of fixed effects, random effects, main effects, and interactions. Table 1 shows how each of the 9 models (M0 to M8) are composed. The Null Model contains only an intercept term and serves as a baseline for the impact of various added parameters. The full model (M8) has fixed main effects and interactions as well as random main effects (for readers and patients) and interactions, with all expertise terms included. The full model is used to evaluate the significance of various fixed effects through analysis of variance (ANOVA). A Bonferroni correction is used to adjust p‐values for multiple comparisons. Mathematical descriptions of the models can be found in the Supplementary Materials document.

We separately analyze inter‐rater agreement between the expert readers and between the expert and non‐expert readers as an independent assessment of similarity between the two types of readers. Agreement is measured with two metrics: a concordance metric, which is calculated as the proportion of the agreement between a pair of readers, and Cohen's kappa coefficient, which is a commonly used statistic to test inter‐rater agreement. 37 Both metrics act on pairs of readers, and we use two‐sample t‐tests and Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests to generate inference about the two comparisons and also calculate 95% confidence intervals.

Deep learning model observers

We compare the predictive accuracy of models trained on the expert and non‐expert reader data with two types of DLMOs 23 described schematically in Figure 3. The general structure of DLMOs for the two‐alternative forced‐choice (2AFC) task (Figure 3a) is the use of parallel networks with shared weights for processing each alternative. The outputs of the layers are contrasted and run through a sigmoidal function to form a final scalar decision variable indicating the alternative chosen as the target. One DLMO uses a CNN—a variant of LeNet5, 19 , 31 , 38 with 4 convolutional blocks followed by global averaging and a fully connected layer. The other DLMO (Figure 3b) augments the CNN with a separate Shifted Window Transformer (SwinT) encoder with output that is concatenated with the CNN encoder output before the final fully‐connected layer produces a scalar value for the input image. This architecture has demonstrated improved performance in previous studies. 23

DLMO architecture. (a) DLMO for 2AFC trials. (b) Hybrid CNN‐SwinT network. 2AFC, two‐alternative forced‐choice; CNN, convolutional neural network; DLMO, Deep learning model observer

CNNs have been the most common choice for DLMO networks, and they are capable of inherently learning discriminative features with a strong inductive bias based on the assumption that closer pixels are more related. However, CNNs tend to show limited capability for modeling long‐distance feature dependencies, which may be needed to account for large anatomical structures in the image backgrounds. Transformer networks have shown improved feature extraction based on building long‐range feature interactions and capturing global context. 39 In particular, the SwinT model constructs a hierarchical representation of features by starting from small‐sized patches and gradually merging neighboring patches in deeper Transformer layers. 40 Improved performance of CNN‐SwinT DLMO combining CNN and SwinT encoders compared to CNN DLMO with CNN only has been demonstrated in our previous study. 23 Therefore, we chose CNN‐SwinT DLMO as well as CNN DLMO in this study. Our purpose here is to investigate the impact of training these models on expert and non‐expert subjects.

Model training and evaluation

Training and testing data were partitioned on the basis of patients. The 81 patient datasets were partitioned into 9 subsets (9 patients per subset), of which 7 subsets were used for training, 1 subset for selecting the optimal epoch number for training, and 1 subset for testing. The DLMOs were trained separately using the expert reader data and the non‐expert reader data. The training procedure used the binary cross‐entropy loss function, the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.002, and a batch size of 32. The training data were augmented by image flipping and rotation.

Two training‐testing schemes were carried out. In the first experiment, DLMOs were trained separately on all four expert readers and on all four non‐expert readers and tested on predicting the expert readers. In this case, the same expert readers were used for training and testing expert‐trained models, although training and testing images were different. To test on predicting unseen readers, in the second experiment, for each expert reader to be tested on, DLMOs were trained on the other three expert readers. To match the number of readers used for training, DLMOs were also trained on each of all three possible combinations of three non‐expert readers chosen from four. DLMOs trained on the expert reader data and on the non‐expert reader data were compared in terms of accuracy in predicting expert readers. Prediction accuracy was calculated as the fraction of DLMO outputs that agreed with expert reader data. In the first experiment, prediction accuracies averaged across the four expert readers for testing were compared. In the second experiment, prediction accuracies for each unseen expert reader, as well as the averaged accuracies over the four expert readers for testing, were compared, where the accuracies for the non‐expert‐trained models were averaged across the three combinations of three non‐expert readers for training. A 9‐fold cross‐validation was performed for a statistical analysis of the prediction accuracy, and the mean and standard deviation of the prediction accuracy with 95% confidence interval were calculated. The prediction accuracies between the models trained on the expert readers and on the non‐expert readers were compared using one‐sample t‐tests with adjusted p‐values to account for correlation among cross‐validation rounds. 41

Channelized hotelling observer

We also compare the predictive accuracy of channelized hotelling observers (CHOs) trained on the expert and non‐expert reader data. As in our previous study, 23 we use two types of frequency‐selective channels: a square (SQR) model with four non‐overlapping channels, and a sparse difference‐of‐Gaussians (S‐DOG) model with three overlapping channels (see for details). As in Section 2.5, we train and test the CHOs, where training a CHO comprises constructing the linear CHO template with determining internal noise model parameters (see 23 for details). Here, we use the internal noise model based on a non‐uniform internal noise variance proportional to the external noise variance in the channel. 8 , 42

RESULTS

Comparison of expert and non‐expert readers

As noted above, a total of eight readers–four non‐experts and four with medical expertise in Nuclear Medicine—completed the 36 experimental conditions in the observer performance studies. This resulted in a total of 23,328 psychophysical responses on which to analyze performance and evaluate model‐observer predictions. Looking across the 3 plots, it is clear that increased contrast leads to increased performance, which is expected in studies like these. Within each plot, simulated tumors added to the liver were reliably easier to detect than those on the lungs. Average observer performance across all liver conditions was 83.6% compared to 72.8% for lung conditions. The plots generally increase going from left to right, indicating improved performance for the BSREM reconstruction algorithm. Average performance for the BSREM conditions was 80.3% compared to 76.1% for the OSEM conditions. For the majority of experimental conditions (33 of 36), the non‐expert subjects are observed to have higher average performance than the medical experts. Across all conditions, the average performance of non‐expert subjects is 79.9% compared to 76.5% for the experts.

Model selection results

The 9 models described in Table 1 (Section 2.3) have been fit to the binary observer responses, with results shown in Table 3. For each model, the table lists the number of free parameters in the model (DoF), the final fitted log‐likelihood value, the value of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and the value of the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The number of free parameters is generally increasing (except for M6‐M7) with the model number. In all cases, the log‐likelihood is increasing from the null model and achieves a maximum value with the full model. This is to be expected, since the null model can be considered a nested model within any of the others, and all models may be considered as nested within the full model. This result serves as a check that the model fitting software is finding an appropriate maximum value.

DISCUSSION

The observer data for this study came from prior work investigating different architectures for deep‐learning model observers. That study pooled results from the medically naïve and expert readers to train and test the models. The purpose here was to perform a more in‐depth analysis of reader performance and model‐observer training to see if there are systematic differences between the two populations of observers.

The agreement between expert readers is not found to be significantly different from that between expert and non‐expert readers (Table 2). Furthermore, the DLMOs trained on expert and non‐expert readers show consistent prediction performance for both a commonly used CNN architecture and a more complex architecture combining CNN and Swin Transformer (Tables 4). Similar results are observed when DLMOs are tested on unseen readers (Table 5). These results imply there is no substantive systematic difference between the expert and the non‐expert reader groups in these simple noise‐limited tasks.

In addition, ANOVA results from the mixed model demonstrate relatively little effect of observer type in these tasks. There is a marginally significant main effect of observer expertise, but it is in the opposite direction of what might be expected; the non‐expert subjects are generally observed to have higher levels of performance than the medical experts, although the effect size—a difference of 3.5%—is relatively small. For comparison, the average difference between reconstruction methods (BRSEM and OSEM) is 4.3%, and the average difference between locations is more than 10%. Furthermore, the interactions between expertise and other effects in the data are not significant, suggesting that any difference between the two sets of readers will be relatively consistent across conditions.

The model selection results are divergent, with AIC selecting the full model (M8) with all effects, including expertise effects, and BIC selecting a model (M3) that consists of fixed main effects and random 2‐way interactions with expertise effects excluded. There is a large literature on model selection and the use of AIC or BIC, specifically that we will not attempt to summarize here. For example, see the following. 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 Instead, we focus on the findings of Brewer et al. 47 who compared AIC and BIC in a variety of simulations. They find that AIC tends to select the models with the best predictive accuracy in conditions of low data heterogeneity, while BIC is best for conditions of high data heterogeneity. Here, data heterogeneity is defined as unmodeled effects that will change from dataset to dataset. So, the question of which criterion to use comes down to whether observer performance data has high or low data heterogeneity. This is an open question, but we would incline towards high data heterogeneity due to the potential complexity of human subjects. Observers may be affected by multiple influences involving motivation, perceptual ability, and cognitive biases, and these are rarely, if ever, explicitly modeled in observer performance studies. Thus, we would conclude that the analysis of model selection criteria favors a model that excludes expertise effects.

A remaining question is whether these results can be generalized to other image data, experimental conditions, and detection tasks. Nonetheless, this is the first study dedicated to investigating the question of whether physician readers can be replaced with non‐expert readers when training DLMOs for simple detection tasks in PET imaging. Future research could extend the study to other settings, including other imaging modalities.

CONCLUSION

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the role of clinical expertise in simple non‐clinical tasks of the sort used to evaluate and develop models for image quality assessment. Our results indicate that for simple tasks like these, non‐expert readers can be used as a more accessible source of human response data for FDG‐PET imaging.
