Functional characterization of BRCA1 variants of unknown significance using homologous recombination repair assays
Authors: Paloma Martín-Bejarano, Eva María Sánchez-Tapia, Pérez Jessica, Teresa Martín-Gómez, Rosario Vidal Tocino, Rogelio González-Sarmiento, Ana Belén Herrero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Inherited mutations in BRCA1 are among the primary causes of hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC). Genetic testing has identified numerous pathogenic and benign mutations in BRCA1, but also thousands of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) with unclear functional consequences. Accurate cancer risk prediction for carriers of these VUS requires functional assays to assess their pathogenicity. In this study, we analyzed 16 BRCA1 VUS detected at the Hereditary Cancer Genetic Counseling Laboratory in Salamanca, Spain, with the goal of providing additional data to guide their reclassification. Since homologous recombination (HR) is the primary tumor-suppressive function of BRCA1, we employed two complementary HR repair assays to evaluate HR efficiency. The first, an already established assay, uses a HeLa-DR cell line harboring an HR reporter cassette and involves silencing endogenous BRCA1 followed by complementation with the VUS under study. The second, developed in this work, is also based in complementation assays that restore the green fluorescence protein (GFP) gene, and employs a breast cancer cell line with a similar HR reporter system (HCC1937-HR) that lacks BRCA1 expression, allowing direct complementation. Both assays consistently identified five VUS (p.V11G, p.H888Y, p.G1201S, p.Q1395R, and p.F1734L) as pathogenic due to significantly reduced HR efficiency, whereas the remaining 11 VUS were benign in terms of HR function. Notably, two pathogenic variants (p.H888Y, p.G1201S) were located outside the known functional domains of BRCA1. The five HR-deficient variants were further evaluated for their sensitivity to ionizing radiation, which confirmed the deleterious impact on DNA repair for variants p.V11G, p.H888Y, p.G1201S and p.F1734L. Sensitivity to the PARP inhibitor olaparib revealed hypersensitivity only in cells expressing p.V11G and p.F1734L, suggesting variant-specific mechanistic effects with potential therapeutic relevance. In conclusion, recombination-based functional assays complemented with sensitivity assays are effective tools for assessing the pathogenicity of BRCA1 VUS and can provide valuable information to support clinical decision-making. We propose that these assays can be completed within a timeframe compatible with clinical needs, offering critical insights for genetic counseling and personalized treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-025-02125-3.

Introduction

Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome (HBOC) is a genetic disorder defined by a markedly elevated risk of developing breast and ovarian cancers and a moderate increase in the risk of other malignancies, including prostate and pancreatic cancers [1, 2]. The syndrome is predominantly linked to germline mutations in the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, which confer high lifetime cancer risks. Specifically, BRCA1 mutations are associated with a 48–80% lifetime risk of breast cancer and a 20–40% risk of ovarian cancer, highlighting their clinical significance in cancer predisposition and management [1, 3–6].

Genetic testing for the BRCA1/2 genes is a standard practice to improve clinical management and preventive strategies for carriers and their families. While many of the mutations identified are clearly pathogenic or benign, a substantial proportion are classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS). VUS present a significant clinical dilemma, as their functional consequences are unknown, complicating risk prediction and patient management [7–10]. Therefore, accurate classification of VUS is essential to provide appropriate guidance in genetic counseling and inform clinical decisions, including preventive measures and targeted therapies.

Functional assays are indispensable tools for elucidating the impact of VUS on protein function, as outlined by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines. BRCA1 has a well-established role in maintaining genomic stability and participates in multiple cellular processes, including transcriptional regulation, activation of cell cycle checkpoints, and repair of double-strand breaks (DSBs) through the process of homologous recombination (HR) [1, 4, 11]. Several assays have been developed to evaluate the functionality of BRCA1, which include ubiquitin ligase activity assays, sensitivity to proteases or binding to phosphopeptides [12–17]. Other BRCA1 assays evaluate embryonic stem cell viability [18]drug sensitivity [18] transcriptional activation [19] protein–protein interaction [20, 21] or splicing [22, 23] .The limitation of some of these assays is that they focus on specific domains (RING or BRCT), considered the most important for BRCA1 functions, and some of them have been performed using isolated protein domains and not the full-length BRCA1 protein. To overcome these limitations Dr Parvin´s group established a robust assay system to analyze the function of BRCA1 in HR [21] which is critical for its tumor suppressor function. This assay is based on the use of a cell line, HeLa DR-13.9, that bears an HR reporter cassette to quantify HR efficiency. The reporter contains two altered GFP alleles, the first contains a recognition site for the endonuclease I-SceI and the second is a truncated allele. Upon inducing a double-strand break (DSB) with I-SceI in one allele, cells capable of repairing the break via HR, using the second allele as a template, restore functional GFP expression and emit green fluorescence. This fluorescence can be quantified by flow cytometry, providing a direct measure of HR activity. The primary advantage of the Parvin´s assay is that it focuses on BRCA1’s critical role in HR, allowing the evaluation of variants located in any region of the protein, regardless of their specific domain. However, this method involves silencing endogenous BRCA1 before introducing the studied VUS to monitor functional complementation.

In this study, we aimed to characterize 16 BRCA1 VUS identified in patients undergoing genetic counseling at the Hereditary Cancer Genetic Counseling Laboratory in Salamanca, Spain. We employed two complementary HR repair assays to assess the impact of these VUS on HR efficiency. The first assay was based on the HeLa-DR cell line and the Parvin´s method. The second, developed in this study, utilizes a breast cancer cell line that lacks BRCA1 expression and carries an HR reporter cassette, allowing direct functional assessment of VUS. Both methods allowed the identification of five VUS (p.V11G, p.H888Y, p.G1201S, p.Q1395R, and p.F1734L) with a significantly reduced HR efficiency and 11 VUS with a normal HR function. These findings will help to reclassify these 16 VUS as either pathogenic or benign. Furthermore, we explored the therapeutic implications of HR-deficient variants by assessing their sensitivity to ionizing radiation and to the PARP inhibitor olaparib. Four of the five VUS exhibited increased sensitivity to ionizing radiation, and two also displayed marked hypersensitivity to olaparib, underscoring potential implications for personalized therapeutic strategies.

In summary, our results emphasize the utility of HR-based functional assays combined with sensitivity assays in the clinical interpretation of BRCA1 VUS and highlight their potential role in guiding personalized treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

The following cell lines were used in this work: HeLa DR13-9, kindly provided by Dr. J. Parvin (Department of Biomedical Informatics, Ohio State University, Columbus. Second), and HCC1937, a primary ductal carcinoma cell line obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). This cell line is homozygous for the BRCA1 c.5266dupC (p.Q1756fs) mutation. HeLa DR13-9 was cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). HCC1937 was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The presence of mycoplasma was routinely checked with the MycoAlert kit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) and only mycoplasma-free cells were used in the experiments.

Plasmids

The plasmid encoding the recombination substrate, pDR-green fluorescent protein (GFP) and the BRCA1 expression plasmid pcDNA5-HA-BRCA1 were kindly provided by Dr. Parvin (Department of Biomedical Informatics, Ohio State University, Columbus. Plasmid pHR, encoding another recombination substrate was kindly provided by Dr. Gorbunova (Department of Biology, University of Rochester, Rochester, USA). The I-SceI expression plasmid, pCMV-I-SceI was kindly provided by Dra. Wiesmuller (Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Ulm University, Ulm, Germany), and pCBASceI was also provided by Dr. Parvin (Department of Biomedical Informatics, Ohio State University, Columbus. Plasmid pcDNA3 was purchased in Invitrogen (Invitrogen™ V79020) and pDSRed2-N1, a plasmid encoding the red fluorescent protein DsRed2, was purchased in Clontech.

Site-directed mutagenesis

Site-specific point mutations were generated using the QuickChange II XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit from Agilent Technologies. The sequences of the primers (Invitrogen) are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Sanger sequencing was performed to verify all the mutagenized DNA constructs. One founder mutation was used to validate the method: c.5266dupC.

Homology-directed recombination (HDR) assays

HDR efficiency was analyzed following a method previously described by Ransburgh et al. [21]. Briefly, on day 1, HeLa-DR 13.9 cells, carrying the recombination substrate DR-GFP, were transfected with 5 pmol of BRCA1 siRNA (GCU CCU CUC ACU CUU CAG U), to block endogenous BRCA1 expression, and 0.3 μg of the BRCA1 expression plasmid (WT or mutated) using Lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher). On day 2 the transfected cells were transferred from the 24 well-plates to 6 well-plates and on day 3 the cells were transfected again with 25 pmol of the BRCA1 siRNA, 0.75 μg of the appropriate BRCA1 expression plasmid, and 0.75 µg of pCBASceI, the plasmid encoding the endonuclease I-SceI. 72 h after the second transfection, green fluorescence was measured by flow cytometry using a BD Accuri C6 Plus Flow Cytometer.

All BRCA1 variants were tested in duplicate in at least three independent experiments. In each experiment, the percentage of GFP-positive cells from control siRNA transfections was set equal to 100, and the fraction of GFP-positive cells was determined relative to this control.

HDR efficiency was also analyzed as follows: a derivate of HCC1937 (BRCA1 c.5266dupC) was established by transfection with 1 μg of the pHR plasmid previously linearized by digestion with NheI. G418 was added at 500 μg/ml 72 h post-transfection and a stable pool with the integrated homologous recombination reported cassette, HCC1937-HR, was obtained after 3 weeks of selection. To measure HR efficiency in the generated cell line, 106 cells were cotransfected with 5 μg of pCMV-I-SceI, 5 μg of the appropriate BRCA1 expression plasmid (WT or mutated) and 0.5 μg of pDSRed2-N1 to correct for differences in transfection efficiencies. Transfections were performed using the Amaxa Cell Line Nucleofector Kit V and Amaxa Nucleofector device 2b (Lonza). Program used was A-023. Green (GFP) and Red (DsRed2) fluorescence were measured by flow cytometry using BD Accuri C6 Plus Flow Cytometer. HR efficiency was calculated as the ratio of GFP + to DsRed + cells.

All BRCA1 variants were tested duplicate in at least three independent experiments. In each experiment, the percentage of GFP-positive cells from control transfections was set equal to 100, and the fraction of GFP-positive cells was determined relative to this control.

BRCA1 cloning and lentivirus production

To generate stable cell lines expressing BRCA1 variants, the cDNA of BRCA1 (WT or mutated) was cloned into the lentiviral vector pLVX-Puro (Takara Bio). The cDNA was obtained from the plasmid pcDNA5-HA-BRCA1, which was digested with the enzymes SalI and XhoI to generate compatible cohesive ends, and then ligated to the XhoI digested pLVX-Puro vector. The ligation was carried out at 16 °C overnight. The ligation mixture was then used to transform E. coli DH5α cells. Resulting colonies were selected, and plasmid DNA was purified using the GeneJet Plasmid MaxiPrep Kit (Thermo Fisher). The insertion of the fragment was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and restriction enzyme analysis.

Lentiviruses were generated in HEK-293T cells by transfection using the calcium phosphate method. HEK-293T cells were transfected with 10 µg of the corresponding plasmids (pLVX-puro, pLVX-puro-HA-BRCA1, pLVX-puro-HA-BRCA1-Q1756fs, pLVX-puro-HA-BRCA1-V11G, pLVX-puro-HA-BRCA1-H888Y, pLVX-puro-HA-BRCA1-G1201S, pLVX-puro-HA-BRCA1Q1395R and pLVX-puro-HA-BRCA1-L1734R), along with packaging plasmids pPAX-2 and pMD2-G. The mixture was incubated for 5 min with 2X HBS and added to the cells. After 48- and 72-hours post-transfection, the culture medium containing the lentiviruses was collected and filtered to remove cellular debris.

The HCC1937-HR cell line was infected with the lentiviruses obtained, with polybrene added at 10 µg/ml to enhance infection efficiency. After two rounds of infection, the infected cells were selected using puromycin (1.5 µg/ml) for approximately 2 weeks to obtain stable clones. BRCA1 expression in the lines was verified by Western blot.

Western blot

Cells were trypsinized, washed with PBS and lysed in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (140 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0,1% SDS, 0,1% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM PMSF) containing protease inhibitors (Complete, Roche Applied Science, Indianapolis). Protein concentration was measured using the Bradford assay (BioRad, Hercules, CA). Protein samples (35 µg/lane) were subjected to SDS-PAGE (4–15% polyacrylamide gels, BioRad) and transferred to PVDF membrane (Millipore). After blocking, membranes were incubated with the anti-human antibodies anti-BRCA1 (#9010, Cell signalling) and anti-β-actin (#A5441, Sigma-Aldrich). Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Secondary Antibody, DyLightTM 800 (#SA5-35571, Invitrogen) and Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Secondary Antibody, DyLightTM 680 (#35518, Invitrogen) were used as secondary antibodies at 1:10,000 dilution. Immunoblots were incubated for 1 h at RT and protein bands were visualized following two washes using the LI-COR Odyssey fluorescence imaging system (BioAgilytix, USA).

Reagents and radiation

In this work, we used specific reagents and controlled radiation to carry out our experiments. Olaparib (AstraZeneca, Cambridge, UK) and mitomycin C (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were employed for chemical treatments. Radiation exposure was performed at the Isotope and Radiation Protection Service at the University of Salamanca, ensuring accurate and consistent dosing under controlled conditions.

Apoptosis assay

60,000 cells were seeded per well in 6-well plates. After 24 horas, the corresponding treatment (either the drug or radiation) was applied. Seven days later, apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Immunostep S.L, Salamanca, Spain). Samples were acquired using a BD Accuri™ C6 Plus Flow Cytometer. Data from at least 30,000 cells per condition were processed using the cytometer’s software, and the percentages of live cells were graphically represented. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using GraphPad Prism v.8.0.2 software. For quantitative variables, the mean was used as the central tendency measure, and the standard deviation was used as the measure of dispersion. Graphical representations and analyses included all replicates from the various experiments.

To compare continuous quantitative variables among more than two groups, the Shapiro-Wilk test was initially applied to assess the normality of the data. If the data followed a normal distribution, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. Post-hoc analysis using Tukey’s test was performed following ANOVA to identify specific significant differences between groups, with statistical significance defined as a p value less than 0.05.

Results

Selection of BRCA1 variants and gene location

From 2000 to 2022, the Hereditary Cancer Genetic Counselling Laboratory at the Cancer Research Center (University of Salamanca, Spain) conducted over 2500 genetic studies aimed at detecting germline variants in susceptibility to genes associated with hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome (HBOC).

Between 2001 and 2022, a total of 42 variants of uncertain significance (VUS) were identified in the BRCA1 gene. Their current classification status was updated using different resources: ClinVar (Database) and Varsome (aggregator). Those variants that maintained their classification as VUS in both databases, a total of 17, were selected to conduct functional assays. Detailed information about these variants is shown in Table 1. The selected variants are distributed throughout the BRCA1 protein, both inside and outside of known domains (Fig. 1).

Functional analysis of BRCA1 VUS by homology‑directed recombination repair (HDR) assays

The HDR activity was first assessed following the method developed by Dr Parvin´s group [21] (Fig. 2A). HeLa-DR13-9 cells were transfected with a control siRNA (Non targeting) or a siRNA specific to BRCA1 and two days later cells were retransfected with the same siRNAs plus the plasmids encoding either the WT or a known pathogenic form of BRCA1 (the Ashkenazi founder mutation c.5266dupC), together with the plasmid pCBASceI, which encodes the endonuclease I-SceI. Three days later, the percentage of GFP-positive cells were determined by flow cytometry (Fig. 2).

Transfection with the plasmid encoding the BRCA1 WT protein generated a number of GFP + cells similar to the one obtained in the control siRNA sample. On the contrary, depletion of BRCA1 clearly reduced the number of cells with recombined GFP alleles (Fig. 2B). BRCA1 silenced cells were transfected with the empty pcDNA3 plasmid or the one carrying the pathogenic Ashkenazi founder mutation. These results are consistent with previous observations [21] and indicate that the method is robust for determining the effects of specific BRCA1 mutations on homologous recombination efficiency.

We next analysed the HR efficiency of the 16 VUS previously selected and constructed by site-directed mutagenesis. Consistent with the previous experiment, depletion of BRCA1 using the siRNA targeting the BRCA1 3′-UTR and cotransfection of the empty plasmid or the vector carrying the Ashkenazi founder mutation produced a clear reduction in GFP-positive cells relative to the control (Fig. 2C). Transfection of the BRCA1-specific siRNA along with the wild-type BRCA1 expression plasmid fully restored HDR (Fig. 2C, lane 2), as expected. On the other hand, transfection with mutants S36Y, T231M, L512F, S561Y, L668F, S708T, L771S, L973R, S1262del, H1284R and R1589H gave an HR efficiency very close to 100%, whereas transfection V11G, Q1395R and F1794L resulted in HR efficiencies very similar to the one obtained after transfection with the known pathogenic mutation (Q1756fs). Finally, H888Y and G1201S produced partial or intermediate decrements in homologous recombination. These results suggest that the 5 variants V11G, H888Y, G1201S, Q1395R and F1794L with impaired HR efficiencies may exert a pathogenic effect, whereas the rest of the mutations analyzed seem to be benign.

To further confirm these results, we decided to establish a new functional assay using a breast cancer cell line, HCC1937, that do not express a functional BRCA1, as a model system, so the initial step of BRCA1 silencing would not be necessary. We first introduced the HR reporter construct in the mentioned cell line as described in materials and methods. In the HR reporter, the first exon of GFP-Pem1 contains a 22 bp deletion and two I-SceI sites in an inverted orientation, so that I-SceI digestion leaves incompatible ends. The 22 bp deletion ensures that GFP cannot be reconstituted by an NHEJ event (Fig. 3A). This first copy of GFP-Pem1 is followed by a promoter-less/ATG-less first exon and intron of GFP-Pem1. The intact construct is GFP-negative. Induction of a DSB by transfection with a I-SceI encoding plasmid in a HR-proficient cell line can reconstitute the functional GFP gene by intramolecular or intermolecular gene conversion between the two mutated copies of the first GFP-Pem1 exon. Since the second copy of the GFP gene lacks the first ATG codon and the second exon, crossing over or single-strand annealing cannot restore the GFP activity.

Next, we used the HCC1937-HR cell line to monitor HR efficiency of the different BRCA1 variants (Fig. 3C). The plasmid encoding the Ashkenazi founder mutation Q1756fs was again used as a control. The results obtained with this method were very similar to those previously obtained with the first assay: 5 variants (V11G, H888Y, G1201S, Q1395R and F1794L) exhibited impaired HR efficiencies and the rest showed an HR repair activity similar to the wild type (WT) protein.

Generation of stable HCC1937-HR cell lines expressing BRCA1 variants

To further characterize these mutations, stable HCC1937-HR cell lines expressing WT or mutated BRCA1 were generated. BRCA1 cADN (WT or mutant) was cloned into the lentiviral vector pLVX-puro and the constructions were confirmed by enzymatic digestion and Sanger sequencing.

Viral particles were produced by transfecting HEK293T cells with the lentiviral constructs alongside packaging plasmids and then viral supernatants were collected and used to infect HCC1937-HR cells. After infection, cells underwent puromycin selection for three weeks to establish stable cell lines. BRCA1 expression was then confirmed by Western blot (Fig. 4A), showing high levels of expression for all BRCA1 variants in the modified cell lines. The parental HCC1937-HR cell line carries a homozygous 5382insC frameshift mutation in the BRCA1 gene, the same variant that was overexpressed in the pathogenic control line. This mutation introduces a premature stop codon, resulting in a slightly truncated, low-abundance, and non-functional protein. The truncated BRCA1 variant, p.Q1756fs, was detectable by Western blot since the antibody used targets epitopes located in the N-terminal region of the protein.

As shown in Fig. 4B, cell lines carrying variants p.V11G, p.H888Y, p.G1201S and p.F1734L exhibited heightened sensitivity to ionizing radiation compared to HCC1937-HR-BRCA1 WT. This increase in radiation sensitivity supports the compromised DNA repair capacity caused by these mutations.

Sensitivity to Olaparib

Olaparib, a PARP inhibitor, exploits the HR deficiency of BRCA1-mutant cancers. To evaluate sensitivity, stable cell lines were treated with the drug for seven days, followed by Annexin V/PI apoptosis assays. Results (Fig. 4C) showed that HCC1937-HR carrying the V11G and F1734L mutated proteins were more sensitive to olaparib than the HCC1937-HR-BRCA1 WT, which also confirmed the pathogenic impact of these mutations on HR function.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to reclassify 16 variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in the BRCA1 gene using functional assays designed to assess their impact on DNA repair through homologous recombination (HR), the primary tumor-suppressive function of BRCA1.

To assess their functional impact, we first employed the assay developed by Dr. Parvin´s group [21] based on the HeLa DR-13.9 cell line. This cell line’s high transfection efficiency and prior validation make it an ideal predictive model for evaluating homologous recombination (HR) in BRCA1 variants [24, 25]. Among the 16 VUS analyzed, 11 variants (S36Y, T231M, L512F, S561Y, L668F, S708Y, L771S, L973R, S1262del, H1284R, and R1589H) demonstrated near-normal HR efficiency (~ 100%), indicating minimal or no impact on BRCA1 function. In contrast, five variants (V11G, H888Y, G1201S, Q1395R, and F1734L) showed significantly reduced HR efficiency, comparable to the pathogenic control mutation (Q1756fs).

To validate these findings, we developed a second functional assay using the HCC1937 cell line, derived from a breast carcinoma harbouring the founder mutation BRCA1 c.5266dupC (Q1756fs). This model, with a genetic background more representative of pathogenic BRCA1 mutations found in hereditary cancer patients [26, 27], confirmed the results observed in HeLa DR-13.9: the same 11 variants exhibited intact repair capacity, whereas the remaining five displayed statistically significant HR defects.

Our findings related with the 11 variants showing normal HR efficiencies align with previous studies that have reported these variants as neutral or benign based on computational predictions or functional data [28–38]. For example, S36Y, located in the RING domain, has been previously reported not to disrupt BRCA1-BARD1 binding, supporting its benign classification [28]. However, some of them, like S561Y, L771S, L973R y H1284R have being reported benign, but only by computational predictions based on features such as conservation. These predictions, although informative, are insufficiently accurate to be used in the absence of genetic or experimental evidence [39]. Our results, based in functional assays, will therefore contribute to definitively reclassify these variants as benign.

On the other hand, the five variants—V11G, H888Y, G1201S, Q1395R, and F1734L—that demonstrated statistically significant impaired HR repair, could be pathogenic, as they have lost, at least in part, the BRCA1 tumour suppressor role. We found a previous study that described that the V11G variant reduces its binding affinity to BARD1, leading to a destabilized BRCA1-BARD1 complex [40, 41]. This destabilization results in impaired homologous recombination (HR) repair [42] in agreement with our results. The deficiency in HR fits with the increased sensitivity to IR and olaparib found in this work. Moreover, recent research that evaluated BRCA1 variants performing saturation genome editing in the exons that encode the RING and BRCT domains, also found this variant as pathogenic [39].

The F1734L variant also exhibits a marked loss of HR activity and increased sensitivity to PARP inhibitors in our study, aligning with findings from previous works [43]. The authors showed that the F1734L variant, although retains some protein interactions, such as with CtIP, it fails to activate transcription, a hallmark of its loss of function [44]. The saturation genome editing work also classified F1734L as non-functional, consistent with its impaired HR efficiency and increased sensitivity to genotoxic stress [39].

The Q1395R variant is located just one amino acid away from threonine 1394, a critical phosphorylation site for the ATM and ATR kinases, which are essential for homologous recombination (HR) repair and G2/M checkpoint stability following DNA damage. Foo et al. [45] reported that the Q1395R variant exhibits reduced HR repair capacity and increased sensitivity to olaparib. However, in this work, we did not observe hypersensitivity to this drug. This discrepancy might be explained by the intrinsic high tolerance to olaparib of the HCC1937 cell line [46, 47]. However, we neither observed that this variant conferred a higher sensitivity to ionizing radiation. The BRCA1 Q1395R variant is located within the DNA-binding coiled-coil domain of BRCA1, which might explain the impaired homologous recombination (HR) activity, as observed in our reporter assays. These assays are highly sensitive to subtle defects in HR-mediated DNA repair (Gunn & Stark, 2012). However, this variant may still retain sufficient residual BRCA1 function to support cell survival following ionizing radiation (IR) or PARP inhibition, particularly through compensation by backup repair pathways or partial recruitment of PALB2 [19, 48]. Moreover, the thresholds of BRCA1 activity required to score positive in DR-GFP assays are often higher than those needed to confer resistance to DNA-damaging agents, explaining the discordance between HR deficiency and lack of sensitivity to IR or olaparib of this particular variant [49, 50].

Regarding the H888Y variant, although it has been mentioned in several studies [51–53] no functional data have been provided. It is simply reported as a VUS found in certain patients. Our results indicate that H888Y is a pathogenic variant, with reduced HR-efficiency and increased sensitivity to IR when exposed to a dose of 7.5 Gy, highlighting the need for further studies to elucidate at the molecular level its deleterious effect on HR. Notably, this mutation is not located in the BRCT or RING domains, but it is included in a region critical for BRCA1 binding to RAD51, the essential recombinase in the HR process. Therefore, it would be valuable to determine whether this interaction is compromised [51].

The G1201S mutation is also present in the region critical for binding to RAD51. It is currently listed as benign/probably benign in ClinVar based on several studies [54, 55]. One such study [54] assessed its effect on HR efficiency, but the results were inconsistent: in two replicates, the variant behaved similarly to the wild-type (WT) protein, while in one replicate, it exhibited pathogenic behavior, with HR efficiency below 50%. In our study, we evaluated this variant in over three independent experiments, each with two replicates, consistently observing a significant reduction in HR efficiency. Furthermore, we found that the stable cell line expressing this variant was clearly hypersensitive to ionizing radiation, supporting a defect in the HR pathway and suggesting a pathogenic role. Another study [55] concluded that the G1201S variant did not have a pathogenic effect, focusing on its influence on sensitivity to PARP inhibitors and response to immunotherapy. However, those authors did not assess HR efficiency. Interestingly, although we found cells carrying this variant hypersensitive to IR, we also did not observe increased sensitivity to olaparib. It is possible that the G1201S variant impairs HR to a degree sufficient to sensitize cells to ionizing radiation—which causes immediate and irreparable double-strand breaks—but retains enough residual BRCA1 function to support replication fork stability or partial repair, thereby avoiding synthetic lethality upon PARP inhibition. Such differential responses have been described for other hypomorphic BRCA1 alleles that uncouple HR defects from PARPi sensitivity [50, 56].

These findings underscore the importance of functionally assessing both ionizing radiation and PARP inhibitor sensitivity in BRCA1 variants, to better inform precision medicine approaches and guide therapeutic decisions.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations in interpreting partial loss of HR function in relation to cancer risk. While our assays are sensitive to variations in homologous recombination efficiency, the threshold at which HR impairment translates into increased cancer susceptibility remains poorly defined. Thus, the variants that displayed intermediate or partial defects in HR, might represent hypomorphic alleles that may confer moderate or context-dependent risks of cancer. Additionally, compensatory mechanisms or tissue-specific factors could modulate the phenotypic impact of these variants in vivo.

In conclusion, the functional assays used in this work represent robust approaches for variant reclassification and emphasize their clinical utility in understanding hereditary cancer and improving the genetic interpretation of BRCA1 mutations.
