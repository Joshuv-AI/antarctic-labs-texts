Predictors of hepatitis B vaccination uptake among healthcare workers in Benadir region, Somalia: a multicenter study
Authors: Jamal Hassan Mohamoud, Ahmed Mahad Shiekh Mohamed, Mohamed Hussein Adam, Bashiru Garba, Salad Ahmed Halane, Mohamed Adam Mahamud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a significant public health issue, particularly in low-resource settings, and poses a high occupational risk to healthcare workers (HCWs). Vaccination is a key preventive measure, yet uptake among HCWs in Somalia, especially in the Benadir Region, remains unclear. This hospital-based cross-sectional study assessed HBV vaccination uptake and associated factors among 611 HCWs in the Benadir Region from July 4 to October 10, 2024. Data were collected via an online self-administered questionnaire. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression analyses were used to identify factors influencing vaccination status. Only 25.7% of HCWs were fully vaccinated. Key barriers included lack of vaccine availability (41.2%), absence of mandatory vaccination policies (36.5%), and negative attitudes toward vaccination (63.7%). HCWs in facilities without vaccine availability were 4.1 times more likely to be unvaccinated (aOR = 4.1, 95% CI: 2.56–6.56, p < 0.001). Additionally, those in facilities without mandatory vaccination policies were 1.75 times more likely to be unvaccinated (aOR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.12–2.71, p = 0.012). Educational level and professional specialization also influenced vaccination status. The low HBV vaccination rate among HCWs in the Benadir Region poses a significant occupational risk. To improve uptake, systemic challenges must be addressed by ensuring vaccine availability, implementing mandatory vaccination policies, and promoting positive attitudes toward vaccination.Keywords: Hepatitis b, Vaccination uptake, Healthcare workers, Vaccine availability, Benadir region, SomaliaSubject terms: Health care, Health occupations

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a highly infectious viral pathogen of public health significance, with the potential to cause fulminant hepatic failure1. It is the top cause of liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma2,3. Healthcare workers face an elevated risk of contracting infections due to their occupational exposure to bodily fluids from infected patients and potential needle-related injuries4. By the nature of their work, Healthcare workers face a heightened danger of contracting HBV due to their frequent contact with patients and exposure to bodily fluids5–7. While protective measures such as standard precautions and post-exposure prophylaxis help mitigate the risk of HBV infection among vulnerable groups, including healthcare personnel, the most effective preventive strategy remains active immunization1. Globally, HBV infection rates are estimated at approximately 1.3%, with variations ranging from a low of 0.2% in the USA to a high of 8% in African countries8. Sub-Saharan Africa and East Asia account for over two-thirds of hepatitis cases globally, with these regions experiencing a prevalence rate exceeding 8%9–11.

The year 1982 marked the discovery of a highly reliable vaccine that provides 90–100% defence against the hepatitis B virus (HBV)12,13. Following this, hepatitis B vaccination continues to be a part of the WHO Extended Programme for Immunization14. This emphasizes the importance of vaccinating healthcare workers (HCWs) who are routinely exposed to hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. Full immunization against HBV requires a three-dose schedule, with the second and third doses administered 1 and 6 months after the initial dose, respectively. Following this regimen, a hepatitis B surface antibody test should be conducted 6–8 weeks after the final dose to confirm immunity15.

As part of occupational safety measures, HCW are required to be vaccinated against HBV. According to World Health Organization estimates, HBV vaccine coverage among healthcare professionals varies significantly between countries of different economic statuses. In nations with low and middle incomes, only 18%-39% of healthcare workers are vaccinated against HBV. In contrast, high-income countries report a much higher vaccination rate, ranging from 67 to 79% among their healthcare workforce16. In a study conducted in Bosaso, Puntland, Somalia, it was found that only 16.4% of healthcare workers (HCWs) had received all the recommended doses of the HBV vaccine17. Somalia is classified as a region with a high prevalence of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, with rates exceeding 8%18. Following this regimen, Somalia, the HBV vaccine is now included in Somalia’s national routine immunization program for children. The Pentavalent vaccine, which includes the HBV vaccine, was introduced in the country in 201319.

HCWs are at an elevated risk of HBV exposure, and their vaccination is essential to protect both themselves and their patients. Global health initiatives, such as the World Health Organization’s (WHO) goal of eliminating HBV as a public health threat by 2030, emphasize the importance of vaccinating high-risk populations, including HCWs20.

However, In Somalia, there is currently no national policy for HBV screening or vaccination specifically targeting healthcare workers (HCWs). Although some healthcare facilities may implement their own vaccination policies, the lack of a unified national strategy likely contributes to the low uptake of the HBV vaccine among HCWs. Additionally, vaccine availability is limited and primarily accessible through private hospitals21.

Currently, no studies have assessed the HBV vaccination status and associated factors among healthcare workers in the Benadir region of Somalia. This research seeks to address this critical knowledge gap by providing valuable insights into the vaccination landscape among HCWs in the Somali context. The findings aim to inform targeted interventions and strategies to improve HBV vaccination coverage among healthcare workers in the Benadir region.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the healthcare workers

In total, 611 healthcare workers (HCWs) participated in this study, with a response rate of 95%. Of the HCWs, 57.8% (353) were aged 25 years or older, while 42.2% (258) were under 25 years, with an average age of 28 ± 6.1 years. The gender distribution was nearly balanced, with 53.7% (328) male and 46.3% (283) female participants. In terms of educational background, most participants (72.8%, 445) held a bachelor’s degree, while 20.8% (127) held a master’s or PhD. Regarding professional background, medical doctors (38.1%, 233) comprised the majority of study HCWs, followed by nurses (21.4%, 131), lab technicians (12.9%, 79), and smaller proportions of midwives (5.2%, 32), nutritionists (4.3%, 26), and public health specialists (8.8%, 54). Marital status was also recorded, with 62.7% (383) of participants being unmarried and 37.3% (228) married. Regarding work experience, the majority of HCWs (76.9%, 349) had less than 5 years of experience, while 23.1% (105) had 5 or more years of experience (Table 1).

Health centre characteristics and Hepatitis B vaccine status

A total of 78.1% (477) of healthcare workers reported having undergone Hepatitis B testing; Regarding vaccination uptake, 74.3% (454) had received the Hepatitis B vaccine, while 25.7% (157) had not. Among those who received the vaccine, 17.8% (81) had taken only one dose, 47.6% (216) had received two doses, and 34.6% (157) had completed all three recommended doses.

Among HCWs who did not complete the full vaccination schedule, the most commonly reported reasons were forgetting to receive follow-up doses, 27.5% (82), and being too busy, 27.5% (82). Additionally, 24.2% (71) stated that the vaccine schedule had not yet been reached, while 20.8% (62) were unavailable at the time of the second dose. Moreover, 43.2% (252) of the HCWs reported that their facility did not provide the vaccine, and 36.5% (223) indicated that vaccination was not mandatory at their hospital. Interestingly, 56.8% (347) of the HCWs mentioned that their facility had conducted a Hepatitis B vaccination campaign. Furthermore, 63.5% (388) of the HCWs stated that vaccination was mandatory at their hospitals. Regarding vaccine availability, only 58.8% (359) of the HCWs reported that the vaccine was available at their health facilities (Table 2).

Hepatitis B vaccine statue among HCWs

According to the study findings, 25.7% (157) of healthcare professionals had completed their hepatitis B vaccination series, while 25.69% (157) had not received any vaccination. Interestingly, the majority of participants, 48.6% (297), were partially vaccinated (Fig. 1).

Reasons for not received any doses of Hepatitis B vaccine

The most common reason, reported by 54.1% of respondents, was the lack of hepatitis B vaccination services provided at their healthcare facilities. Concerns about vaccine side effects were cited by 22.9% of healthcare workers, while 21% believed that they had natural immunity to the hepatitis B virus. A small proportion (1.9%) reported being infected with hepatitis B during the initial screening (Fig. 2).

Factors associated with non-vaccination

Multivariate binary logistic regression analysis revealed that several factors were statistically associated with hepatitis B vaccine uptake, including mandatory vaccination policy, availability of the hepatitis B vaccine at healthcare facilities, and healthcare workers’ attitudes. Healthcare workers in facilities without a mandatory vaccination policy were 1.75 times more likely to remain unvaccinated than those in facilities where hepatitis B vaccination was mandatory (aOR = 1.75, 95% CI = 1.12–2.71, p = 0.012). Regarding vaccine availability, healthcare workers in facilities without the hepatitis B vaccine were 4.1 times more likely to be unvaccinated than those in facilities where the vaccine was available (aOR = 4.1, 95% CI = 2.56–6.56, p < 0.001). In terms of attitude, healthcare workers with a negative attitude toward the hepatitis B vaccine were 1.59 times more likely to be unvaccinated than those with a positive attitude (aOR = 1.59, 95% CI = 0.04-2.42, p = 0.030). Unfortunately, factors such as marital status, training in infection prevention and control (IPC), and the hepatitis B vaccination campaign offered at health facilities were not associated with vaccination status (Table 3).

Risk factors associated with incomplete vaccination

The study also found that the educational level and specialization of healthcare workers were associated with the completion of hepatitis B vaccination. In terms of educational background, healthcare workers with a bachelor’s degree were 1.49 times more likely to be partially vaccinated than those with a master’s degree or Ph.D. (aOR = 1.49, 95% CI = 1.14–3.02, p = 0.005). Additionally, nurses were 3.41 times more likely to be partially vaccinated than medical doctors (aOR = 3.41, 95% CI = 1.46–7.92, p = 0.004). However, factors such as age, marital status, work experience, and healthcare workers’ attitudes were not significantly associated with full vaccination status (Table 4).

Discussion

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is regarded as a significant health threat in developing countries, as it can lead to chronic liver cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and hepatic failure22. The study found that only 25.7% of healthcare workers (HCWs) in the Benadir region were fully vaccinated against HBV. This proportion aligns with the reported coverage in developing countries, which ranges from 18 to 39%, but remains significantly lower than the 67–79% observed in developed countries23. This finding is also lower than the vaccination rates reported in other studies, such as those from Kenya (48%), Uganda (57.8%), Tanzania (33.6%), and Sudan (56.6%), as well as a study conducted in Ethiopia, which reported a rate of 44.5%4,24–27. Additionally, compared to high-income countries, our results are lower than those reported in the USA (63.4%), China (60%), and Italy (70.1%)28–30. This situation may be attributed to Somalia’s history of state collapse and its fragile healthcare system, which, despite recent improvements, results in significant disparities in vaccine coverage compared to countries with more robust healthcare infrastructures31. On the other hand, the Hepatitis B vaccine is not readily available at most primary health facilities in Somalia, as this study indicates. The lack of availability is a significant barrier to vaccination, with many healthcare workers reporting that unavailability of the vaccine is a primary reason for not being vaccinated. To address this, the Ministry of Health in Somalia should consider implementing free vaccination programs to increase uptake among healthcare workers in hospital settings. Additionally, widespread information dissemination and regular awareness campaigns are essential to encourage vaccination. Strengthening these efforts is essential to increase coverage and protect the health workforce.

The study revealed that 78.1% of participants had been tested for Hepatitis B at least once in their lifetime. As testing is a necessary step before vaccination, this finding indicates a potential willingness to comply with vaccination and reflects a high level of awareness among participants regarding the risks associated with HBV infection. This aligns with previous studies highlighting the significance of individuals’ willingness to understand their health status, especially in relation to infectious diseases such as Hepatitis B32 .

This study found that the Several factors were found to significantly contribute to non-vaccination among healthcare workers. Interesting finding from this study highlights the influence of mandatory vaccination policies. Healthcare workers in facilities that do not have these policies were 1.75 times more likely to remain unvaccinated (aOR = 1.75, 95% CI = 1.12–2.71, p = 0.012). This result aligns with studies conducted in Kenya, where mandatory vaccination policies have been associated with higher vaccine uptake among HCWs33. Furthermore, vaccine availability was a critical determinant, with HCWs in facilities lacking vaccines being 4.1 times more likely to remain unvaccinated. In contrast, a study in Ghana reported that regular vaccine availability increased coverage by 60%, emphasizing the importance of consistent vaccine supply chains34. These findings suggest that structural improvements, such as mandatory policies and reliable vaccine access, are pivotal in addressing low vaccination rates.

In terms of incomplete vaccination, the study identified associations with educational level and professional specialization. HCWs with bachelor’s degrees were 1.49 times more likely to be partially vaccinated than those with master of PhD. This trend contrasts with findings from India, where higher educational attainment was positively correlated with full vaccination coverage35. Therefore, it is essential to implement periodic refresher training for all hospital staff to increase awareness of hepatitis B vaccine uptake in hospital settings.

The study found that nurses were 3.41 times more likely to be partially vaccinated than medical doctors, which may reflect their increased engagement in vaccination programs (aOR = 3.41, 95% CI = 1.46–7.92, p = 0.004). Similarly, a systematic review conducted in Africa reported higher vaccination rates among nurses, suggesting their closer involvement in primary healthcare delivery36. Additionally, this study highlighted the attitude towards the HBV vaccine as another modifiable factor associated with achieving vaccination coverage. Healthcare workers with a negative attitude were 1.59 times more likely to remain unvaccinated. This finding is consistent with study in Nigeria, where vaccine hesitancy among healthcare workers was attributed to misconceptions about the vaccine’s safety and efficacy37. Further research on hepatitis B vaccination among healthcare workers and in hospitals across Somalia is essential. Implementing a national policy for mandatory vaccination, along with regular training to ensure completion of the full vaccine series, is crucial for improving coverage. Hospital administrators should ensure that all healthcare workers, including newly hired and outsourced staff, are fully vaccinated.

Strength and study limitations

A major strength of this research is that it is the first to examine hepatitis B vaccine uptake among healthcare workers in the Benadir Region, offering important insights into a pressing public health issue in the area. However, one limitation of the study is that it relied on participants self-reporting their vaccination status, rather than verifying it through official vaccination records.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study investigated hepatitis B vaccination among healthcare workers (HCWs) in the Benadir Region, Somalia, revealing that only 25.7% were fully vaccinated. Several factors were found to influence vaccination uptakes, including the availability of the vaccine at healthcare facilities, the existence of mandatory vaccination policies, healthcare workers’ attitudes toward vaccination, and their educational backgrounds. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted interventions to improve vaccination coverage. Strategies such as implementing mandatory vaccination policies, ensuring consistent vaccine availability, and addressing vaccine hesitancy through education and awareness campaigns are crucial to enhancing vaccine uptake.

Methods and materials

Study design, setting and period

This study employed a health facility-based cross-sectional online survey to assess hepatitis B vaccine uptake among healthcare professionals in the Benadir region of Somalia. The Benadir region, which includes 20 districts, is a key area in Somalia’s healthcare system. It is home to a wide range of healthcare facilities, both public and private, including three major public health hospitals and more than 85 private healthcare institutions. These facilities together provide a broad spectrum of healthcare services to meet various medical needs. The research was conducted over a three-month period, from July 4 to October 10, 2024, during which all healthcare workers were invited to participate in the confidential online survey. The study also followed the STROBE guidelines throughout its implementation38.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population consists of all healthcare professionals employed in various departments of healthcare facilities located in Benadir Region. The study included healthcare professionals aged 18 years and older who were actively working across all healthcare facilities in the Benadir region. However, individuals enrolled in internship programs and those who did not provide online written informed consent were excluded from participation.

Study variables

The dependent variable for this study was the uptake of the hepatitis B vaccine. The independent variables included sociodemographic factors such as age, gender, marital status, educational level, work department, area of specialization, and years of work experience. Health facility related factors which including vaccine availability, the facility’s vaccination policy “mandatory/not mandatory”, and the type of health facility, training on IPC, HBV campaign offering and attitudes of healthcare workers toward the hepatitis B vaccine and reasons for not receiving any dose of HBV.

Sample size determination and sampling techniques

The sample size was calculated using the formula for a single population proportion, taking into account a 95% confidence level and a margin of error of 3%. The fully hepatitis b vaccination was obtained from a facility-based study conducted in Bosaso, Puntland states, Somalia, set at 16.4%39. Following the inclusion of a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was determined to be 644. The health facilities were selected purposively which was included public and private hospitals. HCWs were then chosen using a convenience sampling technique.

Data collection tool and quality control

A carefully designed and reliable self-administered questionnaire was developed following an extensive review of the relevant literature. The questionnaire was then subjected to a thorough review by subject matter experts to ensure its validity and reliability. It includes sections designed to capture socio-demographic characteristics, health facility attributes, healthcare workers’ attitudes, vaccination status, and underlying reasons for vaccine uptake. Once validated, the questionnaire was uploaded to KoboToolbox, and healthcare representatives from each selected hospital were instructed to distribute the questionnaire via WhatsApp groups.

For the assessment of attitudes, the questionnaire includes ten Likert 5-scale statements, along with corresponding reverse statements, ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. All responses were carefully tabulated, with the maximum attainable score being 50. Attitude scores were then categorized as positive if exceeding 60% and negative if equal to or less than 60%, aligning with the widely accepted modified Bloom’s cut-off point classification40,41.

Data quality was carefully maintained throughout the study by following well-established protocols for data collection, coding, entry, and analysis. These processes were overseen by experienced research specialists to ensure accuracy and consistency. Before the main data collection began, the questionnaire was thoroughly pre-tested to confirm its reliability and validity, ensuring it was well-suited for achieving the study’s objectives.

Data analysis and management

The data were initially downloaded from KoboToolbox in CSV format and carefully reviewed for completeness. During this process, 33 responses were identified as incomplete and excluded from the analysis. The cleaned and verified dataset was then imported into SPSS for detailed analysis into SPSS version 29.0 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, were used to summarize and explore the data. Variables with a p-value of ≤ 0.20 in the bivariate analysis were included in the final multivariable model, where those with a p-value of ≤ 0.05 were identified as statistically significant predictors of Hepatitis B vaccination uptake. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and the model’s goodness of fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test.
