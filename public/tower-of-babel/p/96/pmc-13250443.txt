Navigating the unknown: Nanobubbles and the hidden complexity of aerophilic surfaces
Authors: Junhong Lü
Published: 2026

The study by Vandereydt, Nath, and Varanasi elegantly delineates the three asymptotic regimes (gas viscous, liquid viscous, and liquid inertial) governing bubble evacuation through permeable interfaces (1). Their framework establishes fundamental design rules for debubbling systems across diverse applications, with orders-of-magnitude acceleration in removal times being highly compelling.

However, this work invites deeper reflection on the nuanced relationship between aerophilic surfaces and surface nanobubbles. The discovery of nanobubbles itself arose from the long-standing puzzle of the unexplained long-range attraction between hydrophobic (aerophilic) surfaces in water (2, 3). In this sense, aerophilic surfaces can be viewed as the “native habitat” of nanobubbles. This historical context adds a critical nuance: the very hydrophobicity engineered to facilitate rapid macrobubble evacuation may simultaneously promote the formation of nanobubble layers on submerged membrane (4). This points to a multiscale performance duality, wherein a design feature enabling a primary function may inadvertently seed a secondary nanoscale phenomenon.

These gaseous domains, typically 10 to 100 nm in height and stable for hours or days, evade conventional imaging yet may influence local hydrodynamics, alter gas exchange kinetics, or act as nucleation sites for new bubbles (5, 6). While the authors’ permeability framework masterfully captures continuum-scale behavior, nanobubble stability involves additional physicochemical factors, such as surface chemistry, nanoscale confinement, and gas supersaturation, that lie below this continuum limit (7, 8). The same microporous structures enabling rapid gas evacuation may create favorable conditions for nanobubble nucleation (9). This interplay suggests that the long-term efficacy and stability of the membrane’s plastron (the entrapped air layer) could be modulated by the dynamic formation and dissolution of these nanoscale entities, a factor not captured by macroscopic scaling laws.

For practitioners utilizing aerophilic membranes, several considerations emerge. First, surface characterization should employ complementary in situ techniques, such as atomic force microscopy, under operating conditions. Second, performance should be monitored over extended timescales, as nanobubble layers may develop gradually. Third, observed deviations from predicted performance should prompt investigation into whether nanobubble formation is a contributing factor rather than attributing discrepancies solely to limitations of the continuum model. Proactively mapping the operational envelope, identifying combinations of gas supersaturation, pressure, and surface chemistry that minimizes parasitic nanobubble formation, will be essential for reliable deployment.

Looking forward, the ability to precisely control nanobubble nucleation on aerophilic surfaces can transform a potential concern into a powerful tool. Emerging applications include using nanobubbles as dynamic, removable nanomasks for surface patterning or leveraging their drag-reducing gas layer for ultralow-friction transport in microfluidics (10). Moreover, deliberately engineered nanobubble arrays could enhance mass transfer in membrane contactors. The convergence of aerophilic surface engineering and nanobubble physics thus opens rich avenues for both fundamental inquiry and technological innovation.

In summary, Vandereydt et al. have expertly characterized continuum-scale bubble behavior. The next frontier lies in understanding the nanoscale phenomena intrinsic to these surfaces, which may ultimately dictate their long-term performance and unlock capabilities yet to be imagined. This requires a concerted effort integrating nanoscale characterization with macroscopic performance to fully navigate the hidden complexity of aerophilic interfaces.
