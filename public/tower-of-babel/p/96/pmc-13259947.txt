Toward a More Complete High-Resolution Human Leukocyte Antigen Reference for Koreans
Authors: Inseong Oh, Eun Young Song
Published: 2026

Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) typing is essential for transplantation and is used in other areas, such as disease-association studies and pharmacogenomics. In clinical practice, accurate HLA typing is essential for donor selection and interpreting donor-specific antibodies. However, conventional molecular typing methods, including sequence-specific oligonucleotide probes, sequence-specific primers, and sequence-based typing, have inherent limitations. These methods generally analyze selected exons, often leaving allelic or phase ambiguities unresolved. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has been increasingly adopted because it enables broader genomic coverage and higher resolution [1–5]. In this editorial, we discuss a recent study by Lee et al. [6] in the context of previously available Korean HLA datasets, and consider its methodological strengths, clinical implications, and remaining gaps for the diagnostic immunology community.

Important NGS-based HLA typing data have been generated in previous Korean studies, although each study had certain limitations. Choe et al. [7] analyzed samples from 128 individuals and demonstrated that NGS could resolve discrepancies remaining after Sanger-based typing. Baek et al. [8] extended their analysis to 11 HLA loci, including HLA-DQ and HLA-DP, in a relatively small cohort. Jung et al. [9] reported frequencies across 11 HLA loci in a larger dataset. Baek et al. [10] provided HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-DRB1 frequency data with six-digit resolution in 26,202 Korean volunteer donors for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Although these findings clearly demonstrate the utility of NGS, larger datasets with comparable resolution are required.

In this issue of Annals of Laboratory Medicine, Lee et al. [6], using NGS, reported eight-digit HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, and HLA-DRB1 alleles and haplotype frequencies in 2,726 healthy Korean volunteers. The GenDX NGSgo assay covered class I loci (HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C) from the 5′-untranslated region (UTR) to the 3′-UTR, including all exons and introns of each allele, whereas, for HLA-DRB1, the amplified region spanned intron 1 to exon 4, including exons 2 and 3. Lee et al. identified 222 alleles across the four loci, with HLA-A*24:02:01:01, HLA-B*15:01:01:01, HLA-C*01:02:01:01, and HLA-DRB1*09:01:02:01 being the most frequent. The overall distribution is broadly consistent with previous reports for Koreans [7–10] and neighboring East Asian populations, particularly Japanese and Chinese populations. These data represent one of the larger high-resolution HLA datasets currently available for Koreans, offering a useful population reference.

A key strength of the study by Lee et al. is its scale. With over 2,700 individuals, the frequency estimates (including those for less common alleles) are likely to be more stable. This consideration is relevant in clinical settings, where rare alleles or haplotypes can complicate donor searches or lead to mismatched interpretations. More robust population data might help reduce such uncertainty.

The dataset has several practical implications for clinical laboratories. Unexpected or low-frequency alleles are occasionally encountered, and their interpretation may not be straightforward when population-specific reference data are limited. A larger Korean reference dataset could improve confidence in allele assignments, support the validation of results, and contribute to donor registry optimization and unrelated donor search strategies. This dataset was generated directly using NGS typing rather than derived from older low-resolution data that were later reinterpreted or upgraded. This approach likely reduces methodological heterogeneity and enhances its value as a contemporary reference for the Korean population.

However, this study had some limitations. The analysis was restricted to HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, and HLA-DRB1 and did not include other loci, such as HLA-DQA1, HLA-DQB1, HLA-DPA1, and HLA-DPB1. Previous NGS data have shown that including these loci can provide additional insight into haplotypic structures and increase the clinical applicability of HLA haplotype analysis [11, 12]. In addition, even an eight-digit resolution may not fully capture functionally relevant variants, because regulatory or intronic differences can influence HLA expression [13].

Despite these limitations, the study [6] represents a meaningful step forward. Although it does not address all remaining gaps in the Korean HLA population data, it provides a more stable and practical high-resolution reference than previously available datasets. Future studies involving expanded locus coverage will strengthen its clinical utility.
