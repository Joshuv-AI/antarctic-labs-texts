Mapping the Severity of Phenylalanine Hydroxylase Deficiency
Authors: S. Haitjema, E. M. van Steenis, C. M. A. Lubout, M. R. Heiner‐Fokkema, J. Zschocke, F. J. van Spronsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSince the 1960s, phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) deficiency can be detected via newborn screening, allowing early start of treatment to prevent severe intellectual disability. Precise determination of PAH deficiency severity continues to be hampered by several factors. Nevertheless, as therapeutic options broaden, precise determination of PAH deficiency severity becomes critical to inform individualized treatment selection. Although various methods (hepatic PAH activity, pretreatment phenylalanine (Phe) levels, Phe loading test, Phe tolerance, in vitro expression analysis, tracer studies, prediction models) have been used to assess and/or classify PAH deficiency, all of them still report inconsistencies in the correlation between genotype and biochemical phenotype. Establishing the genotype–phenotype correlation for different genotypes is complicated by the enormous genetic heterogeneity within the PAH gene. Furthermore, variability in the terminology applied to different phenotype strata further complicates interpretation. Here, we aim to summarize the methods that have been described in the literature to assess the classification of PAH deficiency and propose terminology to describe phenotype levels as a crucial step to overcome these issues.

Introduction

Phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) deficiency, usually referred to as phenylketonuria (PKU; OMIM #261600) is one of the most common inherited metabolic diseases (IMD), with a global prevalence of 1 in 20‐25 000 newborns [1]. It is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner and is caused by pathogenic variants in the PAH that codes for the liver enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH; EC 1.14.16.1). PAH is a homotetramer that normally converts phenylalanine (Phe) to tyrosine (Tyr) with help of the cosubstrate tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) [2]. Impaired PAH function causes elvation of blood Phe concentrations (hyperphenylalaninaemia, HPA) and reduced production of Tyr. The clinical hallmarks of untreated PKU are severe intellectual disability, epilepsy, behavioral and motor problems, and decreased melatonin production [3]. More than 1500 pathogenic PAH variants with different functional effects have been identified [4]. The most severe form of PAH deficiency is caused by biallelic null‐variants that completely abolish protein function, whereas attenuated forms are due to hypomorphic variants [5] (i.e., variants causing partial loss of function) resulting in residual enzyme activity on at least one of the two copies of the PAH gene [6].

Since the 1960s, PKU can be detected via population‐based newborn screening, allowing an early diagnosis and thereby preventing intellectual disability as dietary treatment can be initiated within the first weeks of life [7]. Since 2007, supplementation of BH4 (as sapropterin dihydrochloride) has allowed individuals with attenuated forms of PAH deficiency, associated with certain PAH variants, to follow a less restricted dietary Phe intake by increasing and stabilizing PAH activity [8, 9, 10]. Since 2018, enzyme replacement therapy with recombinant phenylalanine ammonia lyase (Pegvaliase), also suitable for individuals with severe forms of PAH deficiency, has been granted approval by the FDA and EMA for adults with PAH deficiency, and since 2026 this approval has been extended to include teenagers from 12 years of age [9, 11, 12, 13]. More recently, sepiapterin (a precursor of BH4) has been granted approval by the FDA and EMA, offering a pharmacological treatment option for some individuals with classic PKU [14]. It is important to note that “classic PKU” in the respective study was defined as documented blood Phe concentrations ≥ 1200 μmol/L at birth or in the medical history (which can occasionally be reached by individuals classified as mild PKU using other approaches), only individuals with sepiapterin responsiveness were admitted to the randomized treatment study, and genetic data were disregarded. It is unlikely that sepiapterin has an effect for biallelic null variants that completely abolish functional protein. Other emerging therapies including JNT517 and various gene and RNA therapies offer promising alternatives for the future of PKU treatment [15, 16].

With the growing number of treatment options, there is an increasing need to establish a framework of different degrees of PAH deficiency that can aid individualized treatment strategies. Throughout the history of PKU, various methodologies have been used to assess the biochemical phenotype, assess and/or classify PAH deficiency, and to determine the “severity” of PAH deficiency in the individual patient (Figure 1) [17, 18, 19, 20]. All of these methods still have inconsistencies in the correlation between genotype and biochemical phenotype. Direct measurement of enzyme activity is usually not possible as this requires a liver biopsy; thus, most approaches use the measurement—under specific conditions—of blood Phe concentrations, which are influenced by the exact amount of Phe intake (taking into account different recommendations for target Phe concentrations) and other factors such as gastrointestinal reabsorption, protein turnover and other metabolic mechanisms [21]. Also, due to the enormous genetic heterogeneity of PAH deficiency, it is difficult to accurately assess—let alone predict—genotype–biochemical phenotype correlations for all different genotypes [19, 22].

Global overview of used methods to assess and/or classify PAH deficiency.

Non‐invasive methods that can quantify residual PAH activity in vivo and link the genotype to the biochemical phenotype could support personalized dietary management and, potentially, treatment selection as new therapies emerge. The aim of this review is to summarize, in chronological order, the methods that have been described in the literature to assess the biochemical phenotype and/or classify the degree of PAH deficiency. In the context of this review, the biochemical phenotype is used to denote the interplay between PAH enzyme function (enzymatic phenotype) and the measurable concentrations of specific metabolites (metabolic phenotype), reflected in differences in the “severity” of the disease.

Methods to Assess PAH Deficiency

Determination of PAH Activity in Liver Tissue

Following the introduction of PKU newborn screening, Kaufman et al. [23] developed an assay for PAH activity in human liver extracts from open or needle biopsy samples. Subsequently, several studies have used slightly modified versions of the assay to determine PAH activity in liver tissue [24, 25, 26]. Bartholomé and his colleagues found a range of PAH activity in individuals with PKU and attenuated forms of HPA using liver needle biopsy specimens. As expected, complete absence of detectable activity was associated with the most severe form of PAH deficiency, but residual PAH activity did not consistently correspond with specific types of PAH deficiency, highlighting the difficulty in defining “severity” solely based on residual PAH activity [25].

Phenylalanine Loading Test

During the 1970s, Blaskovics et al. [27, 28] described a challenge with a large amount of Phe (180 mg Phe/kg body weight/day) during three consecutive days. The aim of this loading test was to distinguish the “phenylalaninemias” that demand treatment (type I, II, and possibly III) from “hyperphenylalaninemias” not necessitating treatment (type IV and V) (Table 1). At the time, the two categories were distinguished by blood Phe concentrations under normal diet above or below 10–20 mg/dL (±600–1200 μmol/L). A few years later, Güttler et al. [29] described a loading test with 99 mg/kg body weight (0.6 mmol/kg) for 24 h, usually at 6 months of age, to differentiate between the two conditions denoted PKU and “HPA.”

In the early 1980s, Lutz et al. [30] standardized the 3 day protein loading test described by Blaskovics et al. [27, 28], mostly using a cut‐off value of 15 mg/dL (≈900 μmol/L) measured at some point throughout the 72 h after start of loading to distinguish between PKU and “HPA”, with PKU being defined as a condition requiring dietary treatment, whereas “HPA” was generally considered not to necessitate therapy. However, they also described a borderline category of 15–20 mg/dL (≈900–1200 μmol/L) in which differentiation between PKU and “HPA” was unreliable. The sample showed considerable age heterogeneity, including participants from 1 month to beyond 13 years of age, with the majority carried out in children under the age of 10 years. All these early studies were hampered by a lack of reliable criteria for treatment initiation in children, and the fact that blood Phe concentrations at that time regarded to be harmless lie far above target Phe range for children with PKU in different guidelines (European guidelines: 120–360 μmol/L; American guidelines: < 360 μmol/L) [31, 32].

For the German Collaborative Study on PKU, maximum Phe values obtained in the 3 day loading test described by Blaskovics et al. [28], performed at 6 months of age, were used to distinguish type I, in which plasma Phe rose continuously above 20 mg/dL (> 1200 μmol/L) throughout the challenge; type II, in which Phe similarly exceeded 20 mg/dL but then declined spontaneously (particularly on day 4 when Phe intake was restricted); and type III, in which Phe concentrations fluctuated around 10 mg/dL (600 μmol/L) for most of the challenge, with only occasional peaks up to 15 mg/dL (Table 1) [33]. Several years later, Langenbeck et al. [34] re‐evaluated the data in children at 6 months of age and assigned two different kinetic types: Type 1 without time‐dependent activation of Phe disposal, typical for classical PKU, and Type 2 with enzyme activation in many individuals with hypomorphic PKU variants. Notably, some mild variants (e.g., p.L48S) appeared consistently non‐activatable, and the data did not allow for conclusions to be drawn regarding the mechanisms of enzyme activation [34]. To investigate the stability of the biochemical phenotype beyond infancy, Burgard et al. [35] evaluated the 3 day loading test in the same population at 5 years of age. They found that Phe tolerance significantly increased between the age of 6 months and 5 years. The authors suggested this might relate to maturation of Phe transamination, and highlighted that renal elimination of hydroxyphenylacetic acid (OHPAA) increased in parallel with Phe tolerance over this period, pointing to the formation and renal clearance of Phe transamination metabolites as potentially important determinants of Phe tolerance in childhood. However, the substantial spread in data indicates alternative reasons may contribute to these differences as well [35].

Phenylalanine Tolerance

Güttler et al. [36, 37] proposed the Phe tolerance test to determine the severity of PAH deficiency. Here, Phe tolerance is determined at 5 years of age, and defined as the amount of Phe that a patient can tolerate without having blood Phe concentrations above 600 μmol/L (Table 1) [36, 37]. The most detailed classification is based on 4 phenotypes defined as tolerance for Phe at 5 years of age (Table 1), ranging from classical PKU (cPKU) to mild hyperphenylalaninaemia (MHP) [18, 31, 38]. For many years, Phe tolerance at 5 years of age was used to classify the severity of PAH deficiency [18, 39, 40]. In 2009, van Spronsen et al. [41] studied the individual course of Phe tolerance and found that from the age of 2 years onwards, rather than from 1 year, Phe tolerance is already a reliable predictor for the tolerance at 10 years of age. However, a later study by Porta et al. [42] showed that while Phe tolerance remained stable during the first 6 years of life in classic PKU (~200 mg/day), it increased in milder forms of PAH deficiency, suggesting that the course of Phe tolerance over time may be dependent on the underlying degree of PAH deficiency. There is a relatively small body of literature on the Phe tolerance beyond 10 years of age, especially during the growth spurt in teenagers [43, 44, 45]. Pinto et al. [44] showed that 65% of individuals with PKU aged 12 years or older were able to tolerate more natural protein than prescribed, though the liberated Phe target (from < 360 μmol/L to < 480 μmol/L) following the Portuguese guidelines for children above 12 years of age may have contributed to the increase in tolerance independent of age. More recently, Pinto et al. [45] showed that Phe tolerance increases markedly from the beginning of puberty, reaching its peak at the end of adolescence. This, again, should be interpreted with caution as it may partly reflect the relaxation of blood Phe targets in many guidelines from 12 years of age, rather than a true increase in biological tolerance alone [45].

Phenylalanine Pretreatment Levels

One of the most widely used methods to define the severity of PAH deficiency is the pretreatment blood Phe level (Table 1) [46]. These pretreatment blood Phe levels were originally based on data from clinically diagnosed individuals with Phe levels above 1200 μmol/L and elevated phenylpyruvic acid in the urine [47, 48]. Because blood Phe takes time to reach its peak and rises consistently during the first 14 days of life [49], the predictive value of the pretreatment Phe method decreased with the implementation of newborn screening programs, as Phe levels are often already measured between day 2 and 7 after birth [31, 38]. Earlier sampling can result in lower Phe levels, potentially leading to an underestimation of the severity of the biochemical phenotype [21]. For example, an untreated Phe level of 240 μmol/L on day 2 can exceed 1200 μmol/L by day 7, underlining the lack of reliability of this value as a measure of PAH deficiency severity [50]. Despite the recognition that pretreatment blood Phe levels are dependent on factors such as timing of measurement, neonatal catabolism and diet at the time of blood sampling, around 80% of the treatment centers continued to use them for phenotyping individuals, as no better and practical alternative was available [21].

2H5 ‐Phenylalanine Tracer

About one decade after the proposal of Phe tolerance by Güttler et al. [37], Thompson et al. [51] published a study in which the L‐[ring‐2H5]Phe and L‐[ring‐2H4]Tyr tracers were used to measure the conversion of labelled Phe into labelled tyrosine, in an attempt to correlate this with dietary Phe tolerance [51, 52]. Surprisingly, no differences were found in Phe hydroxylation between individuals with PKU and controls. In contrast, using different tracers (L‐[ring‐2H5]Phe and L‐[1‐13C]Tyr), van Spronsen et al. [53] found low conversion of Phe to Tyr in individuals with PKU compared to healthy controls. By performing repeated assessments with a washout period in between, they showed that their method gives consistent data and is, therefore, reliable for use in individuals with PKU. Although this method could potentially mimic the clinical reality of the biochemical phenotype, the authors did not find a correlation between the in vivo hydroxylation rate and the Phe tolerance, possibly due to limited phenotypic heterogeneity in their study population [53].

In Vitro Expression Analysis

Okano and his collegues were among the first to calculate the predicted residual enzyme activity (PRA) using in vitro expression assays of mutant PAH alleles introduced into monkey‐kidney COS (CV‐1 in Origin with SV40 genes) cells [40]. Calculation of the PRA was based on using the in vitro expression results of two DNA variants, where it was assumed that (1) both mutant alleles have equal expression and that (2) both mutant subunits contribute equally to the PAH protein, assuming no substantial negative ‐or positive‐ interallelic interaction. The PRA of the DNA variants was calculated as a percentage of the level of PAH activity in normal PAH cDNA. In their study, Okano et al. [40] found that individuals with a PRA of 10% or less had the biochemical phenotype of classical PKU, whereas PRAs > 15% fell into the biochemical phenotype of mild PKU (Table 1). Interestingly, individuals with a PRA of exactly 15% were distributed across both phenotypes, which was also reflected by their genotypes that had previously been observed to express either classical or mild phenotypes, suggesting a possible transitional zone in phenotype expression [40]. Later studies by Trefz et al. [54] and Burgard et al. [55] used Okano's method to assess the correlation between PRA and diagnostic parameters (fluctuations in Phe values, long term dietary control during treatment, cognitive outcome). Trefz et al. [54] proposed specific PRA‐based thresholds to classify individuals into three groups, where a PRA of < 15% was strongly associated with pretreatment Phe levels of > 1200 μmol/L, indicative of classical PKU. Burgard et al. [55] compared PRA with long‐term clinical outcomes, finding that a PRA < 20% was generally associated with classical PKU. Although using the same method to calculate the PRA, all above mentioned authors used different cut off values to link PRAs to specific biochemical phenotypes (Table 1).

13C‐Phenylalanine Breath Test

The 13C‐Phe breath test (13C‐PBT) allows the measurement of in vivo Phe oxidation by quantifying the conversion of oral 13C‐Phe into 13CO2, which is exhaled and can be measured by taking breath samples and analyzing them using gas chromatography isotope‐ratio mass spectrometry [56, 57, 58, 59]. 13C‐breath tests are widely used for various clinical indications. Four published studies have used the 13C‐PBT in individuals with PKU, showing differences in Phe oxidation either between individuals with PKU and a control group or before and after BH4 administration [56, 57, 58, 59]. In addition, Treacy et al. [58] also found varying oxidation rates across genotypes that were broadly consistent with their PRA, suggesting that the 13C‐PBT could provide complementary functional context to genotype‐based predictions.

Genotype‐Based Prediction Models

Since the cloning of the human PAH gene in 1985 by Ledley et al. [60] and the first identification of pathogenic PAH variants in 1986 by DiLella et al. [61], many studies investigated the correlation between genotype and biochemical phenotype [39, 40, 62, 63]. An early study calculated a predicted PAH activity by averaging the relative levels of PAH activity measured in in vitro expression analyses for the two pathogenic variants found in individuals [40]. The study showed a clear correlation of variants with different PKU biochemical phenotypes, but the concept of averaging residual enzyme functions turned out to be an oversimplification.

In 1994 it was shown that individuals with mild forms of PAH deficiency not requiring treatment (denoted non‐PKU HPA) carried specific hypomorphic PAH variants on at least one allele, irrespective of the other variant on the other allele [64, 65]. This led to the concept of functional hemizygosity, which postulated that in compound heterozygous individuals, the clinical severity of a particular pathogenic variant (in the absence of a functioning second allele, i.e., presence of a null allele that completely removes enzyme function) can be deduced from the biochemical phenotype. Kayaalp et al. [39] used this concept to systemically evaluate the genotype–phenotype correlations with regard to classical and variant PKU and non‐PKU HPA for 73 PAH missense variants. They found that when homozygous, 19 out of 21 variants were linked to single categories whereas 2 out of 21 variants were in two adjacent categories. In a “functional hemizygous” constellation 24 out of 35 variants were consistently in single categories, but 9 out of 35 were in two, and 2 out of 35 in all three phenotypic categories, highlighting the complexity of PAH. They concluded that there are broad correlations between mutant PAH genotypes and HPA phenotypes, but that there may be discordances at least for some variants [39].

Using data from 686 individuals with PAH deficiency, Guldberg and his colleagues used four arbitrary phenotype categories based on dietary Phe tolerance (classical/moderate/mild PKU) or blood Phe concentrations < 600 μmol/L without treatment (MHP) to determine “assigned values” (AV1, AV2, AV4, AV8) for 105 PAH variants in functionally hemizygous individuals [18]. The combined AV values were used to determine the expected phenotype. This model works well for biallelic null variants that are always associated with severe PAH deficiency, and for some variants with high residual function that determine an MHP phenotype when functionally hemizygous. However, hypomorphic variants with limited residual function may be associated with a range of clinical severities from mild to severe PKU. Interestingly, inconsistencies in the classification of some variants based on Phe tolerance were linked to different target Phe concentrations in different countries. For example, individuals “functionally hemizygous” for the “moderate PKU” variant p.R261Q were consistently classified as “classical PKU” in Germany with very low dietary Phe targets, but more likely to be associated with mild PKU in countries that allowed higher Phe concentrations (J. Zschocke, personal observation). It was later shown that the mutant protein has significant residual enzyme function only at relatively high Phe concentrations, which fully explains this observation [66, 67].

Almost two decades later, Garbade et al. [19] assigned allelic phenotype values (APVs) to 88 variants by analyzing the biochemical phenotypes (cPKU, mPKU, MHP) associated with these variants in a PAH‐functional hemizygous state [detailed explanation in Supporting Information S1, Explanation Box]. They used a scale from 0 for cPKU to 5 for mild PKU and 10 for mild hyperphenylalaninemia (Table 1). In addition, they calculated genotypic phenotype values (GPVs) for all individuals in the BIOPKU database (http://www.biopku.org), which showed a significant association with pretreatment blood Phe levels [19]. Both Garbade et al. [19] and later Hillert et al. [1] used the highest APV (APVmax) to determine the GPV. They confirmed the concept developed by Guldberg et al. [18] that a higher APV (and thereby milder phenotype) is dominant over a lower APV [68, 69]. Again this method was quite accurate for severe PAH deficiency, but much less reliable in milder deficiencies.

The above mentioned studies cannot easily take the possible effects of inter‐allellic complementation into account that may influence the biochemical phenotype, especially if the APV of one genetic variant is completely different from the other [70]. Recently, Himmelreich et al. [22] used COS‐7 monkey kidney cells to investigate PAH protein expression and in vitro residual PAH activity of 34 common PAH variants using isotope‐dilution liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry, and compared their results with APV data [71]. They substantiated that, even though in vitro PAH activity and APV are the best indicators for the genotype, they do not always correlate with the expected biochemical phenotype [22].

Protein Stability Algorithms

In the 1990s, increased degradation of partly active PAH mutant protein was identified as a major factor in the pathogenesis of PKU [72, 73, 74, 75]. Erlandsen et al. [76] and Pey et al. [77] showed that the treatment effect of BH4 in attenuated forms of PKU is mediated by its chaperone function that leads to reduced protein misfolding, inactivation and degradation for variants with at least some residual function. Pey et al. [78] systematically examined associations between protein stability and phenotype for missense variants of PAH using FoldX, a protein design algorithm developed to evaluate the effect of a variant on the stability, folding and dynamics of a protein [79]. Wettstein et al. [80] explored multiple prediction tools that use the PAH enzyme stability as a predictor for PKU phenotype. They investigated the correlation of phenotype and BH4 responsivity with protein stability, assessed with several algorithms (FoldX, SIFT Blink, Polyphen‐2 and SNPs3D), residual PAH activity and average APV values, finding a relationship between enzyme stability, PAH activity and APV. In addition, protein stability algorithms may be particularly informative for those (rare) variants for which clinical and biochemical data are limited [68].

BH4 Responsiveness

In about 20%–50% of PAH‐deficient individuals (regional percentages depend on genotype spectra), blood Phe levels and/or dietary Phe tolerance improves after treatment with BH4, acting as a pharmaceutical chaperone for the PAH enzyme [46, 81, 82]. Apart from its therapeutic role in selected individuals, BH4 responsiveness testing can also give an impression of disease severity, as responsiveness becomes less likely with increasing severity of PAH deficiency. Various BH4 loading tests have been proposed to find those BH4‐responsive individuals, and the exact procedures differ. In line with Bernegger et al. [83], the European Guidelines on PKU recommend testing for BH4 responsiveness by blood Phe analysis after the administration of two single doses of 20 mg/kg/day BH4 for 48 h, where responsiveness is defined as a > 30% reduction in blood Phe levels [31, 38]. The American approach, as described in the FDA drug label for sapropterin dihydrochloride, uses a one‐month BH4 trial to determine responsiveness [84]. Apart from the BH4‐loading test, many attempts have been made to predict BH4‐responsiveness based on the patient's genotype [66, 85]. A large study on individuals with PKU listed in the BIOPKU database showed that the results of BH4‐responsiveness tests sometimes differ between individuals with the same genotype. The authors suggested various factors that may explain these discrepancies, including dietary and metabolic fluctuation during the test, and concluded that genetic analysis can predict non‐responsiveness in individuals harboring two null alleles, and can potentially identify BH4‐responders by the presence of at least one potentially responsive PAH variant [85]. Staudigl et al. [66] subsequently showed that apart from the genotype, BH4 responsiveness is significantly influenced by substrate concentrations and BH4 dosage. They suggested a combination of short‐term BH4 loading tests (assessing kinetic effects), long‐term BH4 treatment effects (assessing chaperone effects), and in vivo 13C‐phenylalanine oxidation tests (assessing the effect of BH4 on in vivo PAH enzyme activity) for determining disease characteristics [66]. Interallelic complementation of compound heterozygous variants—which can be both positive or negative [70]—may also be a relevant factor in influencing BH4 responsiveness.

PAH Activity Landscapes

More recently, Danecka et al. [17] underlined the need for a model that allows for two different alleles to assess the PAH enzyme activity. Their findings are built upon earlier work from Staudigl et al. [66] Using COS‐7 cells, Danecka et al. [17] aimed to develop a cellular model quantifying genotype‐specific residual PAH activity, taking PAH protein homeostasis and aspects of the metabolic environment (e.g., absolute Phe concentration and temperature) into account. Using co‐expression of PAH variants in COS‐7 cells, PAH function was determined for 30 common PAH homozygous and compound heterozygous genotypes. Activity landscapes were generated and functional data was compared to clinical phenotypes from the BIOPKU database (http://www.biopku.org). They showed that residual PAH activity strongly correlated with biochemical phenotypes, emphasizing the value of their model for improving genotype–biochemical phenotype correlations. To provide an accessible and interactive tool for the visualization of the relationship between PAH activity and biochemical phenotypes, they created a web application (http://pah‐activitylandscapes.org/). Overall, PAH activity landscape models represent a step forward in understanding the interallelic interplay between variants in PAH deficient individuals.

Discussion

The outcome of PAH‐deficient individuals has improved over the last decades due to improvements in treatment options and monitoring [1]. A factor that hampers further optimalization of individualized treatment and care is the lack of a complete understanding of the variety in “severity” of PAH deficiency, especially since individual biochemical phenotypes are shaped by numerous factors including therapeutic intervention and lifestyle factors. In this review, we aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of the various methods used to assess residual in vitro, in silico or in vivo PAH activity, classify PAH deficiency, and/or correlate those methods to the biochemical phenotype.

Throughout history, various biochemical, genetic, and functional approaches have been used to estimate and predict the biochemical phenotype of PAH deficiency. Table 2 summarizes the strengths and limitations of the methods discussed in this review from an in vitro to a more clinical perspective. Methods close to the genotype have the potential to create a clear understanding of enzyme functionality without interference from other aspects (such as epigenetic factors). Methods closer to clinical day‐to‐day practice have a (high) risk of being influenced by therapeutic and environmental factors that one would like to exclude in order to describe or predict the genotype‐biochemical phenotype relationship.

Strength and limitations—from an in vitro to a clinical perspective.

The use of predicted residual enzyme activity (PRA) derived from in vitro expression analysis has been widely explored [40, 54, 55]. However, different studies have applied different cut‐off values to classify PRA into biochemical phenotypes, hampering standardization. While genotype‐based models such as the APV and GPV are suitable for known variants as a rough indicator of biochemical phenotype, they depend on the reliability of the severity classification in initial individuals with PAH deficiency and fail to incorporate interallelic interactions for variants with residual function. PAH activity landscapes developed by Danecka et al. [17] may better account for the functional interaction between alleles. Genotype‐based methods are generally able to predict severe PKU (in individuals with two null variants) and very mild deficiency variants (MHP in individuals with so‐called MHP variants) but are less reliable for the accurate prediction of phenotype and BH4 responsiveness in individuals with mild to moderate PKU.

Although liver biopsies have contributed to the understanding of PAH deficiency, their invasiveness and the inconsistent correlation between residual enzyme activity and clinical phenotype make this approach unsuitable for routine clinical practice. Non‐invasive methods using stable isotopes have made the measurement of PAH hydroxylation velocity and capacity in real‐life possible. However, the robustness of those methods needs to be investigated before they can be considered the new “gold standard” for in vivo estimation of enzyme capacity. A recent study investigated the reproducibility of the 13C‐PBT in healthy adult volunteers, and its findings suggested an acceptable test–retest reliability [88]. Such research has, however, not yet been performed in individuals with PKU.

Out of the 11 methods discussed in this review, the pretreatment blood Phe level remains the most widely used classification method, even though it is highly unreliable. The European Guidelines on PKU stepped away from this classification due to the inconsistencies mentioned in Table 1 [31, 38]. Similarly, the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines on PKU use “PAH deficiency” as the overarching term, without applying the traditional classification system [32]. Given the amount of data available on pretreatment blood Phe levels, refinement of the pretreatment blood Phe method, including amongst others the day and exact timing of sampling, should be possible to better support patient classification and personalized care. Some clinical studies have chosen to look for the highest Phe measured during lifetime instead of pretreatment Phe. However, many centres do not measure blood Phe levels during intercurrent illness, possibly again missing maximum Phe levels. Phe tolerance is a readily available parameter from day‐to‐day management but is highly dependent on age and recommended Phe target concentrations (which differ between countries) that can be either based on advised or actual intake and also depends on other factors. The BH4 responsiveness test is essential for assessing a possible response to this cofactor therapy, but results may be unreliable due to a range of modifying factors. This again emphasizes the need for alternative methods, such as the 13C‐PBT requiring a single dose of approximately 6 mg/kg Phe. In contrast, previously used Phe loading protocols giving 100–180 mg/kg/day for 3 consecutive days are not generally acceptable as they conflict with the ultimate aim of treatment—to keep Phe concentrations low—and as such cannot be regarded as harmless [29, 30]. An alternative biochemical parameter that warrants investigation is the phenylalanine‐to‐tyrosine (Phe:Tyr) ratio, which theoretically reflects PAH‐mediated conversion more directly than phenylalanine concentrations alone [89]. However, the role of the Phe:Tyr ratio in classifying PAH deficiency has not yet been systematically evaluated.

Although current European and American guidelines emphasize blood Phe level targets over formal severity classification, a precise understanding of the underlying degree of PAH deficiency retains important clinical value. It informs expectations around the required strictness of dietary management; for instance, persistently elevated Phe values in classical PKU may reflect the inherent severity of enzyme deficiency rather than poor dietary adherence, whereas similar elevations in milder forms more likely indicate insufficient dietary compliance. Beyond dietary management, precise characterization of PAH deficiency severity is increasingly relevant for treatment selection: BH4 responsiveness is strongly associated with residual enzyme function, and the efficacy of emerging treatments such as gene therapy and RNA‐based approaches likely depends on the effective degree of PAH deficiency. A reliable framework for severity assessment therefore becomes a prerequisite for individualized treatment decisions.

Beyond the complexity introduced by the 11 methods discussed above to assess or predict the biochemical phenotype, interpretation is further hampered by inconsistent terminology for the definition of various phenotypes. This lack of standardized terms limits comparability across studies, including those linking phenotypes to neurocognitive and other clinical outcomes. For example, Blau et al. [90] defined the metabolic phenotype as the PAH activity determined by the genotype, and the clinical phenotype as the blood Phe levels, whereas Guldberg et al. [18] describe the metabolic phenotype as being classical PKU, mild PKU, moderate PKU, or mild hyperphenylalaninaemia (MHP) based on either dietary Phe tolerance or pretreatment blood Phe levels. Staudigl et al. [66] described similar categories (classical PKU, mild PKU, MHP) as the clinical phenotype, while in Danecka et al. [17], the terms metabolic phenotype, clinical phenotype, biochemical phenotype, and pharmacological phenotype were all used without clear definition. Such variation in nomenclature hampers the clarity and comparability of research on PAH deficiency.

Conclusion

The enormous genetic heterogeneity within the PAH gene causes a large variety of biochemical phenotypes in different individuals with PAH deficiency. So far, no gold standard measure has been able to define the complete range of clinical and functional characteristics of PAH deficiency in an accurate way. In combination with the inconsistent use of phenotype terminologies, this has hampered a better understanding of patient outcomes and reliable classifications required for the evaluation of treatments. Future perspectives should emphasize a unified phenotype terminology for PAH deficiency and the development of solid methods that quantify PAH deficiency and enable early, individualized prediction of therapeutic needs.
