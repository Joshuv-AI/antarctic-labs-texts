Comparative effectiveness and dose–response relationships of exercise for depressive symptoms in children and adolescents: a Bayesian network meta-analysis
Authors: Zilong Zhao, Xuewen Li, Longhui Li, Yang Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression in children and adolescents is a growing public health concern with lasting effects on mental and physical health. Exercise is a promising strategy for prevention and treatment; however, the comparative effectiveness of different exercise types and their doses response remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of different exercise types on depressive symptoms and to explore their dose–response relationships using a network meta-analysis.MethodsSix databases (APA PsycInfo, Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) were systematically searched, supplemented by Google Scholar and citation tracking. Randomized controlled trials involving exercise interventions in children and adolescents were included. Risk of bias was assessed using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. Bayesian network meta-analyses and dose–response analyses were conducted using random-effects models.ResultsA total of 41 trials with 4,563 participants were included. Mind–body exercise (MBE) was associated with the largest estimated reduction in depressive symptoms (SMD = − 1.46, 95% CrI: −2.00 to − 0.88), followed by resistance training (SMD = − 0.90, 95% CrI: −1.68 to − 0.14), aerobic exercise (AE; SMD = − 0.80, 95% CrI: −1.20 to − 0.43), interval training (SMD = − 0.60, 95% CrI: −1.43 to 0.24), and combined aerobic exercise and resistance training (AE + RT; SMD = − 0.54, 95% CrI: −1.24 to 0.13). Subgroup analyses suggested relatively larger estimated effects for AE in clinical populations and MBE in subclinical and healthy populations. Age-related differences were also observed, with AE showing relatively larger estimated effects in children and MBE in adolescents. A nonlinear U-shaped dose–response relationship was identified, with the greatest estimated effects occurring at 947 MET-min/week. Dose–response patterns varied across types, ranging from 690 MET-min/week for AE + RT to 1,130 MET-min/week for MBE.ConclusionsExercise appears to be associated with reductions in depressive symptoms in children and adolescents, although the magnitude of benefit may vary by exercise type and dose. The primary, subgroup and dose–response findings should be interpreted cautiously because of substantial between-study heterogeneity, residual uncertainty, and the overall low-to-moderate certainty of the evidence. Further high-quality studies are needed to strengthen the evidence base, confirm subgroup and dose–response findings, and assess long-term sustainability.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08378-2.Keywords: Adolescent, Children, Depression, Dose–response, Network meta-analysis, Physical exercise

Introduction

Depression among children and adolescents has become a major global public health concern, with substantial personal, social, and economic consequences [1]. Approximately 20% of adolescents worldwide experience depressive symptoms, with higher prevalence and burden in low- and middle-income countries [2]. According to the World Health Organization, depression affects approximately 1.4% of younger adolescents and 3.5% of older adolescents [3]. The COVID-19 pandemic has further exacerbated this issue, with recent evidence indicating that 22.6% of children report depressive symptoms, alongside a shift toward younger age groups [4]. This trend is particularly concerning given that adolescence is a critical period for social, emotional, and cognitive development [5]. Early depressive symptoms are strong predictors of later mental health disorders, with up to 67% of affected adolescents progressing to major depression in adulthood [6, 7]. If left untreated, these symptoms can impair emotional, social, and academic functioning and increase the risk of long-term mental health problems and suicidal behaviours [8, 9]. Over time, they may also contribute to chronic physical and psychological conditions, reduced psychosocial functioning, substance use, and premature mortality [9–11]. These long-term consequences highlight the importance of early and effective intervention.

Current treatments for depression in children and adolescents rely mainly on pharmacological and psychological approaches, yet their overall effectiveness remains limited, reducing disease burden by only about 30% [12, 13]. Psychological therapies can be effective but are often constrained by high costs, limited availability, and moderate remission rates of around 50% [14–16]. Additional barriers include stigma, parental concerns, limited awareness, and logistical challenges, particularly in underserved populations. Pharmacological treatments are also associated with side effects, such as nausea, dizziness, sleep disturbances, and gastrointestinal problems, which may reduce adherence or, in some cases, worsen symptoms or increase suicidal ideation [17, 18]. Adherence remains a further challenge, as many young people and their families are reluctant to engage in long-term medication use [19]. In addition, these treatments often do not address co-occurring physical health conditions [20]. Together, these limitations underscore the need for accessible and acceptable strategies to prevent and manage depressive symptoms during this critical developmental period [21].

Exercise has emerged as a promising, low-cost, and accessible intervention. In addition to alleviating depressive symptoms, it supports the development of healthy behaviours that may persist into adulthood [22, 23]. Exercise is generally safe, widely accessible, and less stigmatizing than conventional treatments, making it an appealing option for young people [24]. A growing body of systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicates that exercise can reduce depressive symptoms in adolescents, with small to moderate effect sizes (standardized mean differences ranging from approximately − 0.48 to − 0.64) [25–28]. Some studies have also examined specific exercise modalities, suggesting that aerobic exercise [29], resistance training [30, 31], and combined aerobic and resistance training [32] may be particularly beneficial.

Despite this evidence, several important gaps remain. Differences across studies may reflect variation in inclusion criteria and analytical approaches. Many studies focus on clinically diagnosed populations and often include individuals with comorbid conditions, which may limit generalizability and overlook the preventive potential of exercise in subclinical or healthy populations. In addition, most previous research has used frequentist statistical methods, which, although widely applied, may be less suitable for small sample sizes and complex comparisons [33]. Many trials in this field are relatively small, which may affect the reliability of findings. Furthermore, although different exercise types have been compared, interventions are typically treated as discrete categories, and the relationship between exercise dose and symptom improvement has not been systematically examined. Evidence on dose—particularly nonlinear dose–response relationships—remains limited. The World Health Organization has emphasized the importance of understanding dose–response relationships between physical exercise and depressive symptoms in its guidelines on physical exercise and sedentary behaviour [34], highlighting the need to identify optimal exercise doses for this population.

Therefore, the present study conducted a systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis to address these limitations and provide a more comprehensive evaluation of exercise interventions for depressive symptoms in children and adolescents. Compared with conventional pairwise meta-analysis, the Bayesian network meta-analysis framework enables the simultaneous synthesis of direct and indirect evidence across multiple exercise types, improving the precision and comprehensiveness of comparative effectiveness estimates, particularly when individual trials are small and head-to-head comparisons are limited. Furthermore, by integrating intervention dose into the analytical framework, this study aimed to explore potential dose–response relationships and identify exercise doses associated with greater improvements in depressive symptoms, thereby providing evidence to inform more precise exercise prescriptions. Specifically, we aimed to compare the comparative effectiveness of different exercise types and to examine whether intervention effects varied by age group and baseline depressive symptom status. In addition, we investigated the dose–response relationship between exercise and changes in depressive symptoms to explore exercise doses associated with greater improvements while acknowledging the uncertainty of the available evidence.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and network meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [35]. A completed PRISMA network meta-analysis checklist is provided in Supplementary material 1. The review protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD420261284576).

Search strategy and selection criteria

A comprehensive search was conducted across six databases (APA PsycInfo, Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) from inception to 1 December 2025. To ensure completeness, Google Scholar and reference lists of relevant reviews were also screened. Only peer-reviewed articles published in English were included; grey literature, unpublished studies, and trial registry entries were excluded. Two reviewers (ZZL and LLH) independently performed the search and study selection in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [36], with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer (YY). The full search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Eligibility was assessed using the PICOS framework. Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) Population: children and adolescents aged 6–18 years, including healthy individuals, those with elevated depressive symptoms, or clinically diagnosed depression; studies involving participants with other diagnosed physical or mental disorders were excluded. (2) Intervention: exercise interventions, including aerobic exercise (AE), resistance training (RT), combined AE + RT, interval training (IT), and mind–body exercise (MBE). (3) Comparator: inactive controls (e.g., no intervention, wait-list, usual care, treatment as usual) or alternative exercise interventions. (4) Outcomes: Depressive symptoms measured at baseline and post-intervention using validated assessment instruments were eligible for inclusion, including depression-specific scales (e.g., Beck Depression Inventory, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, Children’s Depression Inventory, and Depression Self-Rating Scale for Children). For broader mood or psychosocial assessment instruments (e.g., Profile of Mood States-Short Form and Symptom Check List-90-Revised), only depression-related subscale scores were extracted for analysis. Studies that did not report sufficient outcome data were excluded. (5) Study design: only randomized controlled trials were included.

Study selection and data extraction

All references for the studies included in this review were managed using EndNote X9. After removing duplicates, two reviewers (ZZL and LXW) independently screened the remaining records. The initial screening was based on titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies, followed by a full-text review to determine final inclusion. At each stage, consensus between the two reviewers was required. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer (LLH) consulted when necessary.

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers (ZZL and LXW). The extracted information was then cross-checked, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (LLH). For each included study, we recorded the authors, year of publication, population details (country, age, gender, severity), intervention characteristics (type, duration, frequency, session length, adherence), and outcomes along with the measurement tools used. When relevant data were unavailable, they were marked as not reported (NA).

Data coding and management

Interventions were first classified into exercise and control conditions to assess the overall dose–response relationship. A secondary classification grouped exercise interventions into six categories: AE, RT, AE + RT, MBE, IT, and control. Exercise dose was standardized using metabolic equivalents (METs). Intensity values were assigned based on the 2017 Youth Compendium of Physical Activities [37], or the 2011 Adult Compendium when necessary [38]. Weekly exercise volume (MET-min/week) was calculated by multiplying intensity, frequency, and duration. When intervention duration was reported as a range, the mean value was used. For consistency in network meta-analysis and dose–response modeling, doses were categorized into seven groups: 0, 300, 600, 900, 1200, 1500, and 1800 MET-min/week.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2). Five domains were assessed: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Each domain was rated as low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias. Assessments were based on information reported in the original articles and focused on identifying potential sources of systematic bias.

Data preparation and effect size calculation

All depressive symptom outcome measures were harmonized prior to analysis to enable synthesis across different measurement scales. Each intervention arm in a multi-arm trial was treated as a separate arm-level observation while retaining a common study identifier. When studies reported multiple intervention groups based on participant characteristics (e.g., sex or age), pooled means and standard deviations were calculated before effect-size estimation [39]. If multiple scales were used in a study to assess depressive symptoms, outcome selection followed a pre-specified hierarchy. Primary outcomes specified in the original study were prioritized when available. When a primary outcome was not defined, all validated depressive symptom assessment tools were considered to measure the same underlying construct. For studies reporting multiple eligible scales, study-specific standardized mean differences were first calculated separately for each scale. These SMDs were then combined at the study level using an unweighted mean. This approach ensured that each study contributed a single effect estimate to the meta-analysis while maintaining conceptual consistency [36].

The analysis was performed using standardized change scores. For each study arm, the mean change was calculated as the difference between post-intervention and baseline values. When standard deviations of change scores were not available, they were imputed following the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, assuming a within-study pre–post correlation coefficient (r) of 0.8 [36]. Subsequently, both the mean change scores and their corresponding standard deviations were standardized by dividing each by the pooled within-study standard deviation, thereby harmonizing outcomes measured using different depression scales [40]. This approach follows the hierarchical Bayesian framework for continuous outcomes described by Efthimiou et al. (2016) and Dias et al. (2013), and the standardized outcome synthesis approach described by Downing et al. (2025), allowing continuous outcomes measured on different scales to be synthesized within a Bayesian network meta-analysis [40–42]. Treatment effects were expressed on a standardized mean difference (SMD) scale with 95% credible intervals (CrIs).

Bayesian network meta-analysis

Model selection and heterogeneity assessment

A Bayesian network meta-analysis was conducted using the ‘multinma’ package in R Studio [43]. The analysis followed the methodological guidance of Phillippo et al. Treatment effects and study-specific baselines were assigned weakly informative normal priors (mean 0, variance 1002), and between-study heterogeneity was modeled using a half-normal prior (variance 52), as recommended for continuous outcomes in ‘multinma’ [43]. Sensitivity analyses using more informative priors were also performed (variance 12). Four Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains were run with 2,000 iterations each, including a burn-in of 1,000 iterations. Convergence was evaluated using the potential scale reduction factor (R-hat) and effective sample size (ESS). The prior and posterior distributions can be directly compared using built-in functions, and corresponding plots of the prior and posterior distributions can be generated. Model fit was assessed using the deviance information criterion (DIC), residual deviance, and the effective number of parameters. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using τ. Meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. When covariate data were missing, observations were excluded from the relevant analyses [44].

Network structure, transitivity and consistency

Network connectivity was examined using network plots. The assumption of transitivity was assessed by comparing the distribution of potential effect modifiers across treatment comparisons. These included baseline depression level, age, sex, adherence, intervention duration, and intervention dose. Continuous variables were summarized descriptively (means and ranges), and categorical variables were compared across treatment comparisons. Network consistency was evaluated at both global and local levels. Global consistency was assessed by comparing residual deviance, DIC, and between-study variance estimates between consistent and inconsistent models. Local consistency was examined using a node-splitting approach, which compares direct and indirect estimates for specific treatment comparisons.

Relative effects, ranking probabilities, and SUCRA

Relative treatment effects were estimated by comparing each psychological intervention with the control condition. Based on these estimates, interventions were ranked according to their effectiveness, and the probability of each intervention attaining a given rank was calculated. Overall rankings were summarized using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), which ranges from 0% to 100%, with higher values indicating greater effectiveness.

Subgroup analysis and publication bias

Each subgroup was analyzed separately to identify the most effective interventions across different depression levels and age group. Potential small-study effects, including possible publication bias, were explored using ‘comparison-adjusted’ funnel plots. Treatments were ordered according to SUCRA to reflect an a priori assumption regarding the possible direction of small-study effects [43, 44]. Egger’s regression test was applied to the comparison-adjusted (network-centered) effect estimates, based on the deviations of each study from its corresponding comparison-specific pooled effect [43].

Bayesian dose-response meta-analysis

A random-effects Bayesian model was used to examine the dose–response relationship between exercise and depressive symptoms. The analysis assessed three core assumptions: network connectivity, consistency, and transitivity. Network plots of exercise types and dose levels demonstrated adequate connectivity (Supplementary Figs. 25–26). Global consistency was supported by comparisons between the consistency model and the uncorrelated average effect model (Supplementary Table 12), while local consistency was further confirmed using node-splitting analyses (Supplementary Fig. 27). No evidence of violation of the transitivity assumption was identified (Supplementary Table 6). Model fit was assessed using the deviance information criterion (DIC), parameter estimates, and residual diagnostics. To determine the optimal functional form for modeling the dose–response relationship, we compared several models, including Emax, linear, natural cubic splines, non-parametric monotonic function, and quadratic fractional polynomial models [45]. Based on model fit statistics, the quadratic fractional polynomial model provided the best fit and was used to describe the nonlinear relationship (Supplementary Table 13; Figs. 28, 29, 30 and 31). Within the MBNMA framework, exercise dose was expressed in MET-min/week and modeled using predefined dose levels (300, 600, 900, 1200, 1500, and 1800 MET-min/week). The estimated dose associated with the greatest reduction in depressive symptoms was derived as the nadir of the fitted continuous quadratic fractional polynomial dose–response curve. The corresponding dose range was defined as the range of exercise doses with a statistically supported treatment effect, where the 95% credible interval did not include the null effect. Treatment effects were reported as SMDs with 95% CrIs. Analyses were performed using the MBNMAdose package in R, and dose–response curves were visualized using the ggplot2 package.

Certainty of evidence

The overall certainty of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework [46]. Evidence derived from randomized controlled trials was initially rated as high certainty and could be downgraded based on predefined criteria. Certainty was reduced if there were concerns regarding risk of bias, inconsistency of results across studies, indirectness of evidence (including differences in populations, interventions, outcomes, or violations of the transitivity assumption), imprecision of effect estimates (wide credible intervals or estimates close to the null), or evidence of publication bias.

Result

Literature screening process and results

Figure 1 shows the study selection process. The initial search identified 5,051 records. After removing duplicates in EndNote, 3,566 unique records remained. Title and abstract screening reduced this to 52 potentially relevant studies, of which 30 met the inclusion criteria after full-text review. Additional searches of Google Scholar and reference lists identified 16 further records, with 11 meeting the inclusion criteria after full-text assessment. In total, 41 studies were included in the final meta-analysis. Details of excluded studies and reasons for exclusion are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

PRISMA flow chart

Study characteristics

The 41 included studies comprised 4,563 participants. Study details are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Sample sizes ranged from 19 to 526, with an average of 112 participants per study. The mean age was 13.88 years, and 56.86% of participants were female. Most studies included both sexes, although 3 focused only on males and 7 only on females. The studies were conducted across diverse settings, with most from the United States (n = 15), followed by China (n = 7), Brazil (n = 3), Korea (n = 3), the United Kingdom (n = 3), Belgium (n = 2), and one study each from Australia, Canada, Colombia, the Czech Republic, Germany, India, Iran, and the Islamic Republic of Iran.

Interventions varied in design and type. Aerobic exercise (AE) was most common (23 studies), followed by mind–body exercise (12 studies), combined aerobic and resistance training (7 studies), interval training (5 studies), and resistance training (4 studies). Intervention duration ranged from 4 to 60 weeks. Sessions were held 1 to 5 times per week and lasted between 10 and 120 min, with an average duration of 52 min.

Results of risk of bias assessment

Among the 41 studies, 15 were rated as low risk of bias, 22 raised some concerns, and 4 were classified as high risk (Supplementary Table 4). Across specific domains, the randomization process was rated as low risk in 25 studies, with 12 raising some concerns and 4 at high risk. Most studies showed low risk for deviations from intended interventions (27 studies), while 14 raised some concerns. Missing outcome data were considered low risk in 32 studies and raised some concerns in 9. Outcome measurement was low risk in 40 studies and raised some concerns in 1. All studies were judged to be low risk for selective reporting.

Results of Bayesian network meta-analysis

Model selection and heterogeneity assessment

Model fit was compared between fixed-effects and random-effects models. The random-effects model demonstrated a substantially better fit, with a lower deviance information criterion (DIC = 172.4) and smaller residual deviance (88.3) than the fixed-effects model (DIC = 668.6; residual deviance = 622.6). The distributions of DIC values for both models are presented in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2. Under the random-effects model, the residual deviance was close to the number of observed data points, indicating an adequate fit. Accordingly, the random-effects model was selected, and its prior and posterior distributions are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. The estimated between-study variance (τ = 0.88) suggested substantial heterogeneity across studies. To explore potential sources of this variability, meta-regression analyses were conducted using baseline depression level, age, sex, adherence, intervention duration, and intervention dose as study-level covariates. Inclusion of these variables did not materially reduce the between-study heterogeneity, which remained substantial in the posterior distribution after adjustment (Supplementary Table 5).

Network connectivity, transitivity, and consistency

The network included six intervention nodes with a well-connected structure (Fig. 2). Baseline characteristics were comparable across treatment comparisons, including depression severity, age, sex, adherence, and intervention features, supporting the assumption of transitivity (Supplementary Table 6). Consistency analyses showed no meaningful differences between direct and indirect evidence. Model fit statistics were similar between consistent and inconsistent models, and node-splitting analyses did not indicate local inconsistencies (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Network plot of comparisons

Effect size analysis and SUCRA ranking

Figure 3 presents the relative effects of exercise interventions. MBE showed the largest reduction in depressive symptoms (SMD = − 1.46, 95% CrI: −2.00 to − 0.88), followed by RT (− 0.90, − 1.68 to − 0.14) and AE (− 0.80, − 1.20 to − 0.43). IT (− 0.60, − 1.43 to 0.24) and AE + RT (− 0.54, − 1.24 to 0.13) showed smaller and less certain effects. All models showed good convergence (Supplementary Table 7).

Relative effects plot. AE (aerobic exercise), AE + RT (combined aerobic and resistance training), MBE (mind-body exercise), RT (resistance training), IT (interval training)

The intervention ranking probability plot (Supplementary Fig. 5) indicated that MBE ranked highest for reducing depressive symptoms, with a mean rank of 1.19 (95% CrI: 1 to 3). The remaining interventions were ranked as follows: RT (2.82; 95% CrI: 1 to 5), AE (3.11; 95% CrI: 2 to 5), IT (3.90; 95% CrI: 1 to 6), AE + RT (4.13; 95% CrI: 2 to 6), and control (5.85; 95% CrI: 5 to 6). The SUCRA values (Fig. 4) were broadly consistent with these rankings. MBE had the highest SUCRA value (96%), followed by RT (64%), AE (58%), IT (42%), AE + RT (37%), and control (3%). However, given the substantial between-study heterogeneity and the limited explanatory value of meta-regression, ranking probabilities and SUCRA values should be interpreted with caution and regarded as supportive evidence that complement the relative treatment effect estimates rather than providing definitive evidence of superiority.

Results of subgroup analysis

Depression level

At the clinical level of depression, AE produced the largest reduction in depressive symptoms (− 3.90, 95% CrI: −4.18 to − 3.63), followed by MBE (− 2.56, 95% CrI: −3.06 to − 2.06), AE + RT (− 1.36, 95% CrI: −1.70 to − 1.01), IT (− 0.77, 95% CrI: −1.34 to − 0.18), and RT (− 0.54, 95% CrI: −1.23 to 0.18). Among participants with elevated (subclinical) depressive symptoms, MBE showed the largest effect (− 1.62, 95% CrI: −3.52 to 0.31), followed by AE (− 1.02, 95% CrI: −2.47 to 0.38), IT (− 0.93, 95% CrI: −3.91 to 2.06), and AE + RT (− 0.07, 95% CrI: −3.49 to 3.34). In healthy individuals, MBE again demonstrated the greatest benefit (− 1.16, 95% CrI: −1.77 to − 0.57). The remaining interventions showed smaller effects, with RT (− 0.91, 95% CrI: −1.61 to − 0.23), AE (− 0.42, 95% CrI: −0.86 to − 0.02), AE + RT (− 0.25, 95% CrI: −1.10 to 0.58), and IT (− 0.14, 95% CrI: −1.07 to 0.81). In the assessment of transitivity within the clinical and subclinical subgroup networks, some imbalance in key study-level characteristics was observed across intervention comparisons, including proportion of female participants, age, and intervention duration (Supplementary Table 8). Global consistency tests and node-splitting analyses indicated that model assumptions were met. Model fit and consistency results are reported in Supplementary Table 9, node-splitting results in Supplementary Tables 10, and convergence diagnostics and effective sample sizes in Supplementary Table 11. Detailed estimates and network plots are provided in Supplementary Figs. 6–14.

Age group

In children aged 6–12 years, AE showed the largest reduction in depressive symptoms (− 0.52, 95% CrI: −0.90 to − 0.10). The remaining interventions showed smaller effects, including MBE (− 0.50, 95% CrI: −1.25 to 0.03), RT (− 0.49, 95% CrI: −1.36 to 0.39), IT (− 0.36, 95% CrI: −1.18 to 0.47), and AE + RT (− 0.20, 95% CrI: −1.24 to 0.76). Among adolescents aged 13–18 years, MBE demonstrated the largest effect (− 1.78, 95% CrI: −2.76 to − 0.86), followed by AE (− 1.32, 95% CrI: −2.02 to − 0.62), RT (− 1.02, 95% CrI: −2.36 to 0.34), IT (− 0.83, 95% CrI: −2.31 to 0.64), and AE + RT (− 0.67, 95% CrI: −2.08 to 0.71). In the assessment of transitivity within the children subgroup networks, some imbalance in key study-level characteristics was observed across intervention comparisons, including proportion of female participants, age, and intervention duration (Supplementary Table 8). Global consistency and node-splitting analyses indicated that model assumptions were satisfied. Model fit and consistency results are presented in Supplementary Table 9, node-splitting results in Supplementary Tables 10, and convergence diagnostics and effective sample sizes in Supplementary Table 11. Detailed estimates and network plots are provided in Supplementary Figs. 15–20.

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

The comparison-adjusted funnel plot suggested some asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 21). Egger’s regression did not provide strong statistical evidence of small-study effects. However, as studies in a network meta-analysis contribute to different treatment comparisons and no single reference line is available to assess symmetry, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Sensitivity analysis using more informative priors (variance = 12) yielded results consistent with the main analysis (Supplementary Figs. 22–24), supporting the robustness of the findings.

Results of Bayesian dose-response analysis

Overall dose-response relationship of exercise

A U-shaped dose–response relationship was observed between exercise dose and improvements in depressive symptoms (Fig. 5). The greatest effect was observed at 947 MET-min/week (SMD = − 1.20, 95% CrI: −1.73 to − 0.68). Beyond this level, the effect gradually declined. Uncertainty increased at doses above 1,460 MET-min/week (SMD = − 0.76, 95% CrI: −1.51 to 0.00). Results from the split dose–response network meta-analysis are presented in Supplementary Fig. 11.

Dose-response relationship between overall exercise and depressive symptoms. The dark green area represents the 95% credible interval. The red dotted line marks the dose associated with the greatest improvement in depressive symptoms. The blue dashed line shows the range over which the dose-response relationship remains statistically significant, as the credible intervals do not cross the line of null effect

Dose-response relationships of the five exercise types

Figure 6 shows the dose–response relationships for the five exercise types. RT followed an L-shaped pattern, with a minimum of 710 MET-min/week required to achieve a significant improvement (SMD = − 1.59, 95% CrI: −3.21 to − 0.03). In contrast, AE, AE + RT, IT, and MBE showed U-shaped relationships. AE achieved the greatest effect at 960 MET-min/week (SMD = − 1.44, 95% CrI: −2.16 to − 0.70), while MBE peaked at 1,130 MET-min/week (SMD = − 2.16, 95% CrI: −3.50 to − 0.86). Although AE + RT and IT showed peak effects at 690 MET-min/week (SMD = − 0.83, 95% CrI: −2.30 to 0.65) and 1,020 MET-min/week (SMD = − 0.73, 95% CrI: −2.07 to 0.68), respectively, these estimates were imprecise. GRADE evidence quality

The overall certainty of evidence was rated as low to moderate using the GRADE framework. This was mainly due to imprecision and concerns about risk of bias, reflected in wide credible intervals and the inclusion of studies with higher risk of bias. A detailed summary of the quality assessments for direct, indirect, and network estimates is provided in Supplementary Table 14.

Discussion

This study examined how different types and doses of exercise influence depressive symptoms in children and adolescents, and whether these effects vary by age and symptom severity. Overall, MBE was associated with the largest estimated reductions in depressive symptoms in the overall network, followed by RT, AE, IT, and AE + RT according to the relative effect estimates. However, the pattern of effects varied across subgroups. AE was associated with larger reductions in depressive symptoms among clinically depressed populations, whereas MBE demonstrated comparatively larger effects in individuals with subclinical symptoms and in generally healthy populations. Age-related differences were also observed, with AE showing relatively larger effects in children and MBE demonstrating more favorable effect estimates in adolescents. In addition, a nonlinear, U-shaped dose–response relationship was observed, with the largest estimated reductions in depressive symptoms occurring at approximately 947 MET-min/week. Dose–response patterns varied by exercise type, with RT showing a threshold effect and other modalities demonstrating U-shaped relationships.

The finding that MBE showed comparatively larger effect estimates in the overall analysis contrasts with earlier meta-analyses that identified AE or RT as the leading interventions [30, 32]. One likely explanation is the broader population included in this study, which extended beyond clinical samples to include individuals with subclinical symptoms and those without diagnosed depression. MBE—such as yoga, Pilates, and dance—combines physical movement with breathing regulation, attention control, and body awareness [47]. These features align closely with mindfulness-based approaches and may be particularly relevant for early-stage or less severe symptoms, where improving emotional regulation and stress awareness is central [30, 48, 49]. At a biological level, MBE has been linked to regulation of stress-response systems, including the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, and to changes in neurochemical pathways associated with mood regulation (brain-derived neurotrophic factor and inflammatory markers) [50–55]. These indirect mechanisms likely contribute to the beneficial effects of MBE on depressive symptoms.

The subgroup analyses suggest that MBE may be beneficial across a wide spectrum of symptom severity, although the magnitude of its estimated effects varied across populations. It was associated with relatively larger effect estimates in subclinical and healthy populations and continued to show meaningful reductions in depressive symptoms among clinical groups. This pattern may reflect differences in underlying mechanisms: interventions such as MBE may be particularly suited to prevention and early intervention, whereas AE may exert stronger physiological effects in more severe cases [56]. Age-related patterns also warrant consideration. The strong performance of MBE in adolescents may be partly explained by the inclusion of dance-based interventions, which combine physical exercise with music, social interaction, and self-expression [57, 58]. These elements may enhance motivation and adherence, especially during adolescence, when social engagement and identity development are prominent [59–61]. At the same time, the apparent age-related differences should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect variations in study design, intervention delivery, and sample characteristics rather than true developmental effects.

RT was associated with relatively large effect estimates in the overall analysis, consistent with previous evidence supporting its potential benefits [30]. Its benefits likely arise through both psychological and physiological pathways. Improvements in strength and physical competence may enhance self-esteem and self-efficacy, which are closely linked to depressive symptoms [62–64]. In addition, RT has been associated with neurobiological changes, including modulation of stress hormones and increased expression of growth factors involved in brain function [65–70]. However, compared with AE and MBE, the evidence base for RT in younger populations remains relatively limited, with fewer trials and smaller sample sizes. As a result, its apparent effectiveness should be interpreted with some caution.

AE was associated with meaningful reductions in depressive symptoms, particularly among clinically depressed individuals and adolescents. This aligns with a substantial body of evidence supporting its antidepressant effects [71]. Aerobic exercise influences multiple pathways relevant to depression, including neurotransmitter release, neuroplasticity (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), and inflammation [72–75]. Its flexibility in intensity and format may also make it easier to implement across different settings. In addition, many forms of AE are social in nature, which may provide added psychological benefits [76]. Nonetheless, the relatively larger estimated effects observed in clinical samples may suggest differential effects of AE across populations; however, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously and requires further confirmation. It should also be noted that treatment effects in the present study were estimated using study-standardized outcomes within a Bayesian network meta-analysis framework rather than conventional pairwise standardized mean differences. Consequently, the magnitude of these estimates should not be interpreted as directly comparable with effect sizes reported in conventional pairwise meta-analyses.

In contrast, IT and AE+RT were associated with comparatively smaller or more uncertain effect estimates in the present network. These findings should be interpreted in the context of limited study numbers and wide uncertainty intervals for some comparisons, rather than as evidence of inferior effectiveness [77]. For IT, the high-intensity nature of the protocols may limit acceptability and adherence, particularly among younger individuals or those with low baseline fitness [78, 79]. Although IT can produce rapid physiological adaptations, its psychological impact may depend on individual tolerance and perception of effort [80, 81]. Similarly, AE+RT may offer broader benefits across physical and psychological domains [32, 52, 82, 83], but these combined effects may not translate into larger reductions in depressive symptoms when compared directly with single-modality interventions. In both cases, the limited number of studies and variability in intervention design also contribute to uncertainty.

The observed U-shaped dose–response relationship suggests that moderate levels of exercise provide the greatest benefit, while both low and excessively high doses are less effective; similar patterns have been reported in previous studies [29, 84, 85]. Insufficient exercise may fail to produce meaningful physiological or psychological changes, whereas excessive exercise can lead to fatigue, stress, or reduced enjoyment, potentially offsetting the benefits [85, 86]. From a physiological perspective, very high training loads may disrupt hormonal balance and increase inflammatory responses, which are associated with poorer mood outcomes [29, 81, 87]. At a behavioral level, higher doses may reduce adherence, particularly among younger populations with competing academic and social demands [88].

Differences in dose–response patterns across exercise types further underscore the importance of tailoring interventions. AE and MBE appear to require higher cumulative doses to achieve optimal effects, possibly reflecting their reliance on repeated exposure and gradual adaptation [73, 89, 90]. In contrast, RT exhibits a threshold effect, suggesting that a minimum level of engagement is necessary before benefits emerge. For AE + RT and IT, dose–response relationships remain uncertain, likely due to limited data. Nevertheless, the estimated dose ranges observed in this analysis are consistent with the typical intensity and psychophysiological characteristics of these exercise types [31, 79]. These findings highlight the need to consider both type and dose when designing interventions, rather than treating exercise as a uniform exposure.

Practical implications

These findings suggest a potential role of exercise as a scalable and adaptable strategy for the prevention and management of depressive symptoms in young people. Importantly, the results indicate that effectiveness may depend not only on whether individuals are physically active, but also on the type and dose of exercise. Different exercise modalities showed varying magnitudes of association with depressive symptoms. MBE was more frequently associated with larger reductions in non-clinical and subclinical populations, whereas aerobic exercise showed relatively larger associations in clinical populations. However, these patterns should be interpreted cautiously given the overall low-to-moderate certainty of the evidence and potential residual confounding across studies. The dose–response analysis indicated a nonlinear relationship with a model-based peak at approximately 947 MET-min/week. This value should be interpreted as a statistical estimate derived from the fitted model rather than a prescriptive or clinically optimal dose range. Overall, these findings may inform future research and the development of exercise-based interventions, but should not be interpreted as clinical recommendations. Exercise interventions should remain flexible and individualized, taking into account personal preferences, physical capacity, and contextual factors that may influence adherence and feasibility.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered. First, although risk of bias was assessed systematically, many studies lacked detailed reporting, particularly regarding randomisation and outcome measurement. This limits confidence in some of the findings and highlights the need for improved reporting standards. Second, the number of studies within certain subgroup networks is relatively small, with imbalances in multiple potential effect-modifying factors observed between intervention nodes in the subclinical and clinical subgroup networks. In addition, some studies classified within the healthy subgroup included participants with overweight or obesity; however, these characteristics were not consistently reported or separately stratified across trials, which may further contribute to between-study heterogeneity. These imbalances may affect the plausibility of the transitivity assumption and may limit the robustness of the subgroup comparisons. Therefore, the findings from these subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger and more balanced evidence networks are needed to improve the reliability and precision of these estimates and to allow more detailed assessment of key effect modifiers. Third, substantial heterogeneity remained even after meta-regression, suggesting that unmeasured factors such as socioeconomic background, intervention quality, or participant motivation may have contributed to the observed variability. Therefore, the results of both primary and subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution. In the interpretation of findings, greater emphasis was placed on relative treatment effects and their corresponding 95% credibility intervals, whereas SUCRA-based rankings were treated as supplementary and secondary evidence. Future meta-analyses using individual participant data may help better clarify the sources of heterogeneity and improve the precision of effect estimation. Fourth, exercise dose estimates were derived from MET-based calculations and may not fully capture participants’ actual engagement with the intervention. In addition, adherence was indirectly inferred from study attrition rather than directly measured, which may not accurately reflect true intervention exposure. Substantial variability in adherence to prescribed exercise protocols may therefore have influenced the actual dose received across participants. Furthermore, dose–response estimates were model-based and subject to uncertainty arising from model assumptions and specification. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should incorporate more precise and standardized measures of intervention adherence, including objective monitoring approaches, and consider more robust modeling strategies to improve dose estimation accuracy. Finally, the overall certainty of evidence was low to moderate, reflecting imprecision and variability across studies. This limits the strength of conclusions, particularly regarding optimal dose thresholds. High-quality, well-reported randomized controlled trials with standardized intervention protocols are needed to strengthen the evidence base and refine exercise prescriptions.

Conclusion

Based on the current evidence, MBE was associated with the largest estimated reductions in depressive symptoms among the exercise modalities examined. Subgroup analyses suggested that AE may be associated with greater benefits in clinical populations, whereas MBE may be more beneficial in subclinical or healthy populations. Intervention effects also appeared to vary by age, with AE showing greater estimated effects in children and MBE in adolescents. Dose–response analyses suggested that the exercise dose associated with the greatest estimated reductions in depressive symptoms differed across exercise modalities, ranging from approximately 690 MET-min/week for AE + RT to 1,130 MET-min/week for MBE. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the heterogeneity across studies, the low-to-moderate certainty of the evidence, and the uncertainty associated with some subgroup and dose–response estimates. Overall, exercise appears to be a promising approach for reducing depressive symptoms across a range of populations, from individuals with subclinical symptoms to those with clinically significant depression. Nevertheless, the comparative effectiveness of different exercise modalities and the observed dose–response relationships require confirmation in future high-quality studies. Future research should further examine the long-term sustainability of intervention effects, factors influencing adherence, and the biological and psychological mechanisms underlying exercise-related improvements in depressive symptoms. Individualized exercise interventions may represent a useful component of depression prevention and management strategies for children and adolescents, although specific exercise prescriptions cannot be definitively recommended based on the current evidence.
