Study of Light Gas Generation Behavior during the Thermal Chemistry Conversion Process of Cattle Manure
Authors: Xue Zhao, Xiaohe Xiong, Yinan Zhang, Houzhang Tan, Renhui Ruan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the thermal chemistry conversion behavior of cattle manure as a biomass fuel, the effect of three reaction ambiences (N2, air, and CO2) and four temperatures (350, 550, 750, and 950 °C) was studied on a fixed-bed reactor. The tested final products were divided into three categories: nitrogen-containing species (NO, NO2, HCN, NH3), carbon-containing species (HCN, CH4, CO, CO2), and others (SO2, HCl). As the temperature increased, HCN and NO always increased, while NH3 and NO2 increased first and then decreased. This tendency was the same in all three ambiences. HCN was found to be the major component among the four measured nitrogen-containing species, with a maximum mass ratio of 17% under N2 ambience at 950 °C. CO2 and CO are the two major carbon-containing species. The oxygen for CO2 or CO formation can be from either cattle manure itself or the surrounding air. As the temperature increased, the contribution to CO2 formation from the internal oxygen became larger than that from the external oxygen in both N2 and air ambiences. At 950 °C, the CO2 mass ratio in N2 ambience was 55%, while it was only 35% from the external oxygen. Under CO2 ambience, the presence of CO2 can boost CO formation by gasification. The SO2–S and HCl–Cl mass ratios present no apparent relation with ambience. As the temperature increased, the SO2–S and HCl–Cl mass ratios decreased in all three ambiences. The sulfur in cattle manure is mainly organic, while the chlorine exists mainly in inorganic forms, such as NaCl and KCl.

Introduction

As the Carbon Peaking and Carbon Neutrality plan, which is the dual carbon goal plan, is gradually being implemented in China, carbon emission reduction has become one of the most important issues in coal-fired power plants. The Chinese government has suggested three pathways to reduce CO2 emission, viz., biomass co-combustion, green ammonia co-combustion, and carbon capture, to meet the requirement of 50% mean carbon emission intensity reduction before the year 2027 (compared to the year 2023) for coal-fired boiler power units. This means the biomass heat ratio needs to achieve 50% of the total fuel heat put into the furnace. Due to economic restrictions, ammonia co-combustion and carbon capture are not the preferred choices for power-generation enterprises. Hence, the biomass is considered as a promising option for decreasing carbon emissions from coal-fired boilers because of its abundant reserves in China.

As a typical biomass resource, cattle manure is abundant in the northwest of China, such as in Shanxi Province, Inner Mongolia, etc. From the perspective of fuel unitization, the lower heating value of cattle manure is around 2580 kcal/kg, , lower than that of thermal coal (typically 4000 kcal/kg or more). Owing to its high volatile matter content, , cattle manure has been proposed as a reburning fuel for reducing NO x emissions. Yang et al. conducted biomass cofiring tests in a 660 MW coal-fired boiler unit and verified its NO x decreasing effect. In addition, the biomass is a carbon-neutral fuel; hence, biomass cofiring with coal can also contribute to CO2 emission reduction.

On the other hand, due to its high H/C ratio, , cattle manure can also be used to generate hydrogen-rich syngas by pyrolysis or gasification. , Maglinao et al. adopted fluidized-bed gasification and obtained an average heating value of 4.19 MJ/m3 at reaction temperatures from 730 to 800 °C. Constantinescu et al. obtained syngas containing 25 vol % hydrogen in a fixed-bed reactor at reaction temperatures ranging from 700 to 875 °C. These studies focused on relatively high temperature conditions (around 800 °C). In fact, according to kinetic and thermodynamic analysis based on TGA, cattle manure begins to lose weight at around 250 °C. , The composition of cattle manure is similar to that of other biomass materials. Yuan et al. found that hemicellulose, cellulose, and lignin components decompose successively with increasing temperature under pyrolysis conditions. Debiagi et al. proposed an existing biomass characterization method, on the basis of the composition of elemental carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. In terms of pollutant emission, Oshita et al. studied N2O emission behavior during cattle manure combustion in a fluidized-bed incinerator and found that N2O emission can reach 110 ppm (12% O2 base). As for SO2 emission, it has been reported that biomass co-combustion is conducive to SO2 emission reduction owing to the relatively lower content of sulfur. , However, reports on direct cattle manure co-combustion or combustion remain limited. Although previous studies had explored the thermal conversion characteristics of cattle manure, further study is still needed. For instance, the formation behavior of thermal conversion products, especially gas pollutant products such as NO, SO2, and HCl, has rarely been reported. A wider reaction temperature needs to be studied. Hence, this study adopted nitrogen, air, and CO2 ambiences to simulate the pyrolysis, combustion, and gasification processes to further explore the thermal chemistry conversion behavior of cattle manure on a fixed-bed reactor, aiming to provide useful information on the thermal conversion utilization of cattle manure.

Experiments

Cattle Manure Sample Preparation Process

Fresh cattle manure was sampled from a cattle farm in Liuhulan Town in Shanxi Province in China. Before the experiment, the cattle manure was dried in an electric oven at 110 °C for 24 h. Then it was milled to control the dried manure size to be less than 1 mm. The sample-making process is shown in Figure .

Schematic diagram of sample making.

Table lists the proximate and ultimate analyses of the cattle manure. The total moisture content was 40.4%. The dried basis volatile was 48%, indicating good ignition performance. Unburned ash was the second largest component, with a dried basis value of 39.97%. According to ultimate analysis, the largest element was carbon, followed by oxygen, with a value of 21.87%, indicating that cattle manure is a highly oxygenated fuel. In addition, the Hd/Cd ratio was 11.8%, higher than that of coal. The major pollutant elements were nitrogen, sulfur, and chlorine, corresponding to NO x , SO2, and chlorine-containing pollutants. Besides conventional pollutants such as nitrogen and sulfur, the chlorine content was as high as 0.87%, which may increase the risks of slagging, corrosion, etc., during the combustion process.

Experiment Rig

The experiment rig was composed of CO2, O2, and N2 gas bottles, mass flow controllers, an oven, a crucible, a water bath, a filter, and a Gasmet DX 4000 flue gas analyzer, as shown in Figure .

Fixed-bed reactor experimental rig.

The oven was heated to the target temperatures (350, 550, 750, 950 °C). For each condition, 0.4 g of milled cattle manure was weighed and placed in a crucible, which was then placed in a constant-temperature area of the oven. Three ambiences, namely, N2, O2/N2, and CO2/N2, were employed to simulate the pyrolysis, combustion, and gasification processes, respectively. N2 gas was considered inert and nonreactive. The volume flow ratio of both O2:N2 and CO2:N2 was set to 1:4. The total carrier gas volume flow rate was maintained at 4 L/min. The flue gas was recorded by the Gasmet DX4000 and analyzed using a laptop. The experimental conditions are shown in Table ; each condition was tested 3 times, and the average data was adopted as the final result. Each condition was tested for around 8 min.

Characterization

The nitrogen-, sulfur-, and chlorine-containing functional groups in the thermal conversion products were tested by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Escalab Xi+). All of the measured binding energies were referenced to 284.4 eV, which corresponds to the C1s binding energy of the neutral C–C component. Scanning electron microscopy/energy-dispersive X-ray (SEM/EDX, Tescan Maia3lmh) analysis was used to identify the morphology of the products. X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD-6100, Shimadzu) was used to analyze the crystal components of the products.

Results and Discussion

The Nitrogen-Containing Species

The mass ratios of four nitrogen-containing species (NO, NO2, NH3, and HCN) under N2, air, and CO2 ambiences are displayed in Figure . As the temperature increased, under three ambience conditions, the mass ratio curves of four nitrogen-containing species could be roughly divided into two categories. One was where the species mass ratios increased as the temperature increased. The HCN and NO curves fell in this category. The other category was where the species mass ratios increased first and then decreased as the temperature increased, as observed for NH3 and NO2 curves. HCN was the major nitrogen-containing species. High temperature was beneficial to HCN formation. The HCN–N ratio increased as the temperature increased under both N2 and CO2 ambiences. The maximum HCN–N mass ratios were 17 and 11.73% under N2 and CO2 ambience at 950 °C, respectively. In addition, HCN was the NO x precursor. It can be easily oxidized to NO x under O2-rich conditions. Hence, the HCN–N ratio remained stable at 2% in air ambience. However, it should be noted that the HCN–N ratio rose steeply to 6% under air ambience at 950 °C. The size of the dried cattle manure was in the millimeter scale. There was not enough time for the newly generated HCN to be oxidized before being carried away from the high-temperature zone.

The Carbon-Containing Species

Four carbon-containing species, namely, CO2, CO, CH4, and HCN, were analyzed. As shown in Figure , CO2 predominated the product rate among the four carbon-containing species in both N2 and air ambiences. According to Table , the two most abundant elements were carbon and oxygen. This explains why CO2 and CO were the major carbon-containing products, whereas the mass ratios of CH4 and HCN were negligible. In addition, oxygen was either from the surrounding air or from within. The external oxygen came from the air. The oxygen within was from the cattle manure itself, like some functional groups such as carboxyl and carbonyl groups. CO2 was generally considered to originate from the decomposition of carboxyl groups, while CO was a result of the decomposition of carbonyl groups. ,

Product ratios of carbon-containing species under three ambiences.

By comparing the CO2–C in N2 ambience, the contribution of external oxygen to CO2 formation can be estimated, as shown in the shaded area in Figure b. With the temperature increasing, the contribution of internal oxygen to CO2 formation was greater than that of external oxygen. Especially at 950 °C, the CO2 mass ratio in N2 ambience was 55%, while it was only 35% from external oxygen.

Figure c shows the product ratios of carbon-containing species under CO2 ambience. Due to the mixing effect of CO2 from the reaction or from the carrier gas, the CO2 concentration could not be distinguished and was therefore not measured. Besides CO2, CO was the major product. Likewise, the CO ratio increased as the temperature increased. At 950 °C, the CO ratio reached nearly 39%. One source of CO formation was the decomposition of interior oxygen functional groups. The other source was the gasification reaction between the carbon in cattle manure and the CO2 in the carrier gas. Adopting the same method, the shaded area in Figure c represents the contribution of CO formation from the gasification reaction. It can be seen that the role of the gasification reaction became more important as the temperature increased. At 950 °C, the CO mass ratio in N2 ambience was 12%, while it was 26% in CO2 ambience.

SO2 and HCl

Figure displays the SO2–S mass ratio trend versus temperature. Under all three ambiences, the SO2–S mass ratios decreased with the temperature increasing, implying the impact of ambience on SO2 formation was limited. Generally, there are three types of sulfur, viz. sulfate sulfur, element sulfur, and organic sulfur. From Table , it can be seen that the sulfur in cattle manure was mainly organic sulfur. The organic sulfur functional group includes aliphatic thiols, aromatic sulfides, thio ethers, and thiophenes. Generally, aliphatic thiols decompose at temperatures as low as 200–300 °C, sulfides and disulfides at around 350–400 °C, aromatic sulfides at 700–800 °C, and thiophenes at temperatures greater than 800 °C.

SO2–S ratio under three ambiences.

Compared with the atmosphere, temperature had a greater influence on SO2 formation. The SO2 mass rate at 350 °C was larger than that at 950 °C, which implied the sulfur in the cattle manure mainly comprised those components that would decompose at lower temperatures, e.g., aliphatic thiols.

The trend of HCl release is shown in Figure . As the temperature increased, the HCl–Cl mass ratio decreased under all three ambiences. Different ambiences seem to have no distinguishable difference in the HCl mass ratio. There were two chlorine sources: one was from the gastric acid secreted in the cattle stomach and the other was from the herbaceous biomass consumed by the cattle. The formation of HCl was reported to be associated with alkali metal chlorides by the following reaction: 2KCl+nSiO2+H2O(g)→K2O(SiO2)n+2HCl(g) 6

Characterization Analysis

Figure shows the XPS test results for sulfur, nitrogen, and chlorine analyses under nitrogen and air ambiences. For sulfur (Figure a,b), the major sulfur-containing peaks were thio ether (162.2–164 eV), thiophene (164–164.4 eV), sulfone (165–168 eV), and sulfate (169–171 eV). In Figure a, it can be seen that the functional group peak around 164 eV corresponds to thiophene and sulfone. After oxidation in an air ambience (Figure b), the peaks around 164 eV decreased markedly, implying that thiophene and sulfone functional groups were totally or partly oxidized. Meanwhile, it should be noted that the functional group peak around 169 eV remained apparent in either nitrogen or air ambience, regardless of temperature.

Sulfur, nitrogen, and chlorine XPS test comparison between nitrogen and air ambiences.

For nitrogen (Figure c,d), there was a broad band peak ranging from 396.6 to 402.5 eV, including pyridine (398.7 eV), amine (399.7 eV), amide (399.9 eV), nitrile (400.1 eV), and pyrrole (400.7 eV). , In air ambience, the nitrogen functional group disappeared at 550, 750, and 950 °C. At 350 °C, the oxidation reaction was incomplete owing to the relatively low temperature. Hence, the nitrogen functional group remained apparent.

For chlorine (Figure e,f), there was a broad band functional group peak associated with chlorine ranging from 196.3 to 202.3 eV, including inorganic and organic chlorine species. The dominant form was inorganic chlorine, viz. chloride. Note that (Figure f) the chlorine functional group signal vanished at 950 °C in air ambience, indicating oxidation reaction was complete. Chlorine was completely released from cattle manure.

Morphology Analysis

The morphologies of raw cattle manure and thermal conversion products under the three ambiences are shown in Figure . A large number of small fragments was found. To further analyze the element composition, several spots were marked, and the corresponding SEM-EDS results are shown in Figure . The major elements in raw cattle manure were carbon and oxygen, indicating the existence of organic compounds. Under the N2 atmosphere, besides carbon and oxygen, Na, Mg, Si, K, Ca, and P were detected after high-temperature conversion. Under the air atmosphere, Na and Cl were detected. Combined with the XRD analysis shown in Figure , note that Na and Cl in the cattle manure existed in the form of sodium chloride. Under the CO2 atmosphere, the major elements were Ca, O, P, and Mg.

Figure displays the XRD results of cattle manure pyrolysis products under N2 ambience. It was observed that the major physical phases were SiO2, NaCl, and KCl. As the temperature increased, the quartz SiO2 phase peak signal weakened, whereas the tridymite SiO2 phase peak signal enhanced. Meanwhile, the NaCl and KCl peak signals decreased. At 950 °C, the NaCl phase peak signal became barely detectable. In addition, a broad diffuse peak was observed at 2θ ranging from 20 to 40 degrees, indicating the existence of amorphous silicon dioxide.

Conclusion

Cattle manure thermal conversion products were studied under N2, air, and CO2 ambiences. The products were divided into nitrogen-containing species, carbon-containing species, and other species (SO2 and HCl). The main conclusions can be summarized as follows.1)HCN was the major nitrogen-containing species, with a maximum at 17% under N2 ambience at 950 °C. High temperature boosts HCN formation in N2 and CO2 ambience. Under air ambience, HCN was easily oxidized and NO was the major nitrogen-containing species.2)CO2 and CO are two major carbon-containing species. The oxygen can be from either cattle manure itself or the surrounding air. As the temperature increased, the contribution to CO2 formation from the internal oxygen became larger than that from the external oxygen in both N2 and air ambience. At 950 °C, the CO2 mass ratio in N2 ambience was 55%, while it was only 35% from external oxygen.3)The impact of the ambient environment on SO2 and HCl formation was not great. As the temperature increased, the SO2–S and HCl–Cl mass ratios both decreased under three ambiences. Sulfur in cattle manure is mainly organic sulfur, while the chlorine is mainly inorganic, primarily as alkali metal chlorides, such as NaCl and KCl.
