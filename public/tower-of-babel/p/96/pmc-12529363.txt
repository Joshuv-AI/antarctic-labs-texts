Differences between male and female mortality in Colombia: a population-based analysis since 1980
Authors: Oscar Espinosa, Adrian D. Smith, Valeria Bejarano, Johnattan García-Ruiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundGlobally, women have consistently exhibited lower mortality and higher life expectancy than men. While this sex gap is well-documented in high-income countries, evidence from Latin America remains scarce. Colombia's history of armed conflict and inequality presents a unique context to examine sex differences in mortality. This study aims to describe long-term trends in sex-specific mortality in Colombia over the past four decades, examining differences by age group, cause of death, geographic region, and health insurance scheme.MethodsWe analysed Colombian mortality data from 1980 to 2023 using national vital statistics (DANE) and health insurance records (BDUA). Age-standardised mortality rates were estimated across six age groups and 20 causes of death, following OECD guidelines. Mortality rate ratios (MRRs), and years of life lost were computed, while life expectancy was calculated using the Lee–Carter model.FindingsA persistent and widening sex gap in mortality was observed. Male age-standardised mortality declined from 591 to 437 deaths per 100,000 between the 1980s and 2020s, while female mortality declined from 445 to 287. The mortality gap peaked in the 1990s (MRR, 1.58; 95% CI, 1.58–1.58) and remained high in the 2020s (MRR, 1.52; 95% CI, 1.52–1.52). The largest disparities occurred in the 15–59 age group and were primarily due to external causes, where male mortality was up to 874% higher (in the 2000s). In older adults, chronic diseases drove the gap. Conflict-affected regions such as Antioquia, Guaviare, and Arauca showed the highest disparities.InterpretationColombia's persistent sex-based mortality gap likely reflects the interplay of contextual factors such as violence, social inequality, and structural barriers to care. Reducing excess male mortality—particularly among young men—may require targeted, gender-responsive policies that incorporate violence prevention, health system strengthening, and strategies to address social inequities.FundingNone.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Sex-based mortality differences are well-documented in high-income countries, where men consistently exhibit higher mortality and lower life expectancy than women, largely due to behavioural and social factors. However, long-term analyses from Latin America, particularly Colombia—a country marked by decades of armed conflict and structural inequality—remain scarce. We searched PubMed and SciELO (January 1980 to June 2025) for studies on sex differences in mortality in Colombia using terms such as “sex mortality gap Colombia” and “gender mortality Latin America”. While dozens of studies have explored mortality in Colombia, most focus on specific health conditions such as COVID-19, cancer, stroke, HIV/AIDS, and others (often in clinical or regional contexts) and rarely differentiate by sex over study periods longer than 15 years. One exception is a geospatial study covering the period 1998–2014, which analysed seven causes of death and found that male mortality (particularly from external causes and circulatory diseases) exhibited stronger spatial clustering than female mortality. The study also identified shifting mortality hotspots, particularly among men, from the central Andean region toward Orinoquía and the Amazon basin. Aside from this and the well-known contributions of the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, we found no national-level research that systematically examines sex-specific mortality patterns in Colombia over more than four decades and across a broad spectrum of health conditions.

Added value of this study

This is the first study to analyse four decades of differences in cause- and age-specific mortality in Colombia by sex. Using national vital statistics, we quantify how external causes linked to violence have shaped excess male mortality. Our findings highlight sharp mortality differentials across age groups, health insurance schemes, and regions, especially in conflict-affected areas. By combining demographic, epidemiological, and spatial analyses, the study offers a nuanced understanding of how social determinants perpetuate the mortality gap, despite overall improvements in population health.

Implications of all the available evidence

Closing the mortality gap between sexes in Colombia requires societal interventions beyond healthcare alone. Persistent excess male mortality—especially among young adults—appears to be strongly associated with structural factors such as violence, socioeconomic exclusion, and limited access to services in rural and marginalised areas, suggesting opportunities for targeted prevention. Gender-responsive policies, violence prevention, and equitable health system reforms are essential. These findings may inform strategies in other low- and middle-income countries affected by conflict, where excess male mortality often reflects social and institutional failures rather than inherent biological vulnerability.

Introduction

Sex-based differences in mortality have been examined across various populations worldwide, with women consistently exhibiting higher life expectancy and lower mortality rates than men.1, 2, 3, 4 Historically, excess male infant mortality primarily accounted for the life expectancy gap; later in the second half of the 20th century, higher male mortality after age 60 became key.2

Elevated adult male mortality in most societies has been linked to behavioural patterns,5 including greater engagement in high-risk activities—such as reckless driving, substance use, and participation in violent or dangerous occupations—along with unhealthy lifestyles and delayed health-seeking behaviours, all of which contribute to higher rates of accidental and violent deaths.6, 7, 8, 9 Studies that have shown these patterns have predominantly focused on Europe and North America data,2,5,10 while the evidence for sex-based differences in mortality in Latin American countries, including Colombia, remains scarce. The distinct social, political, and historical contexts of this region warrant dedicated examination, as factors such as long-standing inequality, armed conflict, and institutional instability may shape gendered mortality patterns differently than in high-income countries.

Mortality differentials between males and females have significantly increased over recent decades across the world. From 1990 to 2021, the male-to-female mortality ratio for adolescents aged 15–24 years rose from 1.29 to 1.65.11 Over the same period, the male proportion of deaths in this age group varied regionally, ranging from 56.9% in Sub-Saharan Africa to 77.4% in Latin America and the Caribbean.11

In Colombia, the ongoing internal armed conflict has resulted in an increase in violent male deaths over the last century,12 and men had 6 years less life expectancy than women in 2024.13 Aside from the higher frequency of violent deaths in men in the country, the persistence of sex-based mortality differences has not been explored sufficiently, despite large demographic and epidemiological transitions since the late 20th and early 21st centuries could have increased the differences in mortality between the sexes.14,15

Thus, this study aims to describe long-term trends in sex-specific mortality in Colombia from 1980 to 2023. We examine differences in mortality by age group, cause of death, geographic region, and health insurance regime. By situating these patterns within Colombia's unique sociohistorical context, we seek to provide a comprehensive interpretation of the persistent sex mortality gap.

Methods

Data sources

This study used the Vital Statistics database of non-foetal deaths maintained by the Colombian National Administrative Department of Statistics (DANE), which consolidates and reports the occurrence of specific vital events. Deaths are recorded based on certificates completed by medical doctors, authorised healthcare personnel, and forensic pathologists, either in physical or digital formats.16 The database includes variables such as sex, age at death, date of death, and health insurance scheme affiliation, among other characteristics. The study period spanned from 1980 to 2023, encompassing a 44-year interval. For mortality analyses, only national and subnational (departmental) counts of 10 or more deaths were included, following international recommendations.17

Population data were obtained from DANE population projections, which are based on the National Censuses of 1973, 1985, 1993, 2005 and 2018. Leveraging the sex- and age-specific microdata, this study focused on estimating age-standardised mortality rates for the age groups: 0–11 months (<1), 1–4 years, 5–14 years, 15–24 years, 25–59 years, and 60 years and over.18 Data on the population enrolled to the health system (contributory or subsidised schemes) were sourced from the Single Database of Enrollees (BDUA), where available information begins in 2012. The first scheme is for salaried workers and their families, while the second is for socioeconomically vulnerable people and which is subsidised by the state.

Mortality data

The analysis included 20 causes of death (Table S1 in Supplement 1), in accordance with the guidelines of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD),19 following the International Classification of Diseases (9th version between 1980 and 1996, and 10th version between 1997 and 2023). The data on rurality (urban or rural areas) was available since 1985, following the classification developed by Colombia's National Planning Department.20

Given the age groups and causes of death considered in this study, records with missing information on causes outside the defined categories were excluded from the analysis (2.8% records with missing age group, undefined cause of death or indeterminate sex). Additionally, for the geographical and health system analysis, records lacking information on location (0.004% records) or health insurance scheme (3.4% records) were also excluded.

We identified that the values reported for 1991 in age groups <1, 1–4 and 5–14 differed significantly from those of other years (e.g., three deaths in 1991 compared to around 100 in other years). We believe that this is a registration error, and we have expressed this to DANE. We used linear interpolation to impute these values, replacing them with a fitted value derived from the trend line across adjacent years. This adjustment had no meaningful impact on national or age-specific mortality trends.

Statistical analysis

All mortality rates (MRs) were reported per 100,000 population. The rates for all ages were age-standardised using the population in Colombia of the year 2000 as reference. We calculated mortality rate ratios (MRRs) as the MRs in males relative to the MRs in females, for all ages and each age group for the period 1980–2023 at the national level.

Cause-specific MRs were also computed for each cause i (MRi), to enable comparisons between male and female mortality. For each cause i in each age group j, the analysis considered the number of deaths dij, the observed population nj and the reference population Nj. Here, dij represents deaths reported for cause i and age group j, while d.j denotes deaths from all causes in age group j. We used the following formulas:MRi=1N∑j=1gdijnj×Nj,MR=1N∑j=1gd.jnj×Nj=∑iMRi,where N=∑j=1gNj is the total reference population. We also measured life expectancies and years of life lost (YLLs) for each sex. Estimates of the MRR were accompanied by 95% confidence intervals, derived using Poisson-based methods to account for the distributional properties of count data. Estimates of life expectancy at birth and across ages of 0, 1–4 and quinquennial age intervals up to 85 years or more were obtained through application of the Lee–Carter model,21 a demographic forecasting approach used in mortality analysis. YLLs were estimated by adapting the methodology described by Espinosa et al.,22 assuming for this calculation the same life expectancy for both sexes. This assumption was made to provide a standardised comparison of premature mortality burden between sexes, avoiding confounding effects due to inherent differences in life expectancy.

All statistical analyses were conducted using the R programming language (version 4.4.3). Since the data used in this study are publicly available on DANE's official website and are anonymised at the population level, ethical review was not required. This study complies with the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies.23

None.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the study population

Between 1980 and 2023, a total of 8,200,892 non-foetal deaths were recorded in Colombia's national vital statistics database. Of these, 4,724,094 (57.6%) were male and 3,476,798 (42.4%) were female. Across the full dataset, 4.2% of female deaths and 5.5% of male deaths occurred in individuals aged under 15 years, 9.9% of female and 22.5% of male deaths occurred in those aged 15–59, and 28.3% of female and 29.6% of male deaths occurred in individuals aged 60 and over. These patterns varied over time, with an ageing trend in the distribution of deaths over the four decades.

Overview

We found a persistent gap between male and female mortality during the period studied (Fig. 1a). In the 1980s, the MR was 591 deaths/100,000 pop for males and 445 deaths/100,000 pop for females (MRR, 1.33; 95% CI, 1.33–1.33), and although these rates declined by the 2020s to 437 deaths/100,000 pop and 287 deaths/100,000 pop, respectively, (MRR, 1.52; 95% CI, 1.52–1.52) relative mortality differences by sex increased. Differences by age group

Age-specific mortality rates (Fig. 2, Table 2) were consistently higher among males across all age groups. Mortality due to external causes among males in the 15–24 age group was more than 800% higher than in females (in the 1990s MRR, 9.04; 95% CI, 8.81–9.28). This difference was also stark in the 25–59 age group during the 1990s and 2000s, with up to 874% more deaths (MRR, 9.74; 95% CI, 9.54–9.95), and somewhat lower in the 5–14 age group, where differences reached 125% (in the 1990s MRR, 2.25; 95% CI, 2.20–2.30). A more homogeneous pattern of mortality by sex was observed over time within the 0–4 and 60+ age groups, reaching a MRR of 1.24 (95% CI, 1.23–1.25) and 1.29 (95% CI, 1.29–1.29), respectively in the Differences by health insurance scheme

Data available from the 2010s for mortality in the contributory and subsidised health care schemes is shown in Fig. 1c. The gap between sexes is particularly wide within each scheme: in the 2020s, the MRR was 1.51 (95% CI, 1.51–151) in the subsidised scheme and 1.40 (95% CI, 1.40–1.40) in the contributory scheme. This was largely explained by external causes of death, with males experiencing over 395% more deaths (in the 2010s contributory scheme MRR, 4.95; 95% CI, 4.86–5.04). Of note, females in the subsidised scheme had higher mortality than males in the contributory scheme, underscoring the combined effect of gender and socioeconomic inequality within the health system.

Further disaggregation by cause of death and insurance scheme (Supplementary Figure S4) demonstrated sex disparities within the same health insurance scheme for deaths due to infectious and parasitic diseases (mean MRR subsidised 1.97 and contributory 2.04), causes of death not elsewhere classified (mean MRR subsidised 1.39 and contributory 1.60), and genitourinary system diseases (mean MRR subsidised 1.30 and contributory 1.25).

Supplementary Figures S5–S8 show the age group composition of deaths by sex and health insurance scheme for each cause of death. Across both sexes and schemes, the majority of deaths for any cause occurred among older individuals, except for specific conditions of the perinatal period and congenital malformations.

In the subsidised scheme, sex differences were mainly driven by a younger age distribution of deaths among males. In both subsidised and contributory schemes, males aged 15–24 had higher proportions of deaths from blood diseases, average of 6.9% of male vs. 5.7% female deaths in the subsidised scheme (Supplementary Figures S5 and S7); and 2.8% vs. 0.9% respectively in the contributory scheme (Supplementary Figures S6 and S8). A similar pattern was observed for nervous system diseases, with mean proportions of 10.6% for males vs. 6.8% for females in the subsidised scheme, and 5.7% vs. 2.8% in the contributory scheme.

Similarly, among individuals aged 25–59, males accounted for higher proportions of deaths from infectious and parasitic diseases than females, with an average of 51.0% vs. 40.8% in the subsidised scheme, and 47.0% vs. 25.7% in the contributory scheme. The same patterns were apparent for external causes, with an average 56.2% vs. 39.5% (subsidised) and 59.5% vs. 41.6% (contributory). In contrast, females in the 25–59 age group have higher proportions of deaths from neoplasms with a mean 32.7% (female) vs. 22.3% (male) in the subsidised scheme, and 29.0% vs. 21.9% in the contributory scheme–as well as from musculoskeletal diseases–mean of 32.3% vs. 23.3% (subsidised) and 29.3% vs. 23.2% (contributory).

Among individuals aged 60 and over, males consistently recorded lower proportions of deaths from mental and behavioural disorders compared to females, mean of 86.9% vs. 97.3% in the subsidised scheme and 97.8% vs. 99.7% in the contributory scheme.

Differences by location

Males exhibited higher mortality than females across urban areas, and the mortality gap between sexes was greatest in the 1990s. Despite the decreasing trend depicted in Fig. 1d, in 2023 the mortality remained higher for males in both rural and urban areas: 434 deaths/100,000 pop in urban and 375 deaths/100,000 pop in rural areas compared to 283 deaths/100,000 pop and 243 deaths/100,000 pop for females, respectively (MRR urban, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.53–1.53, rural, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.54–1.54).

Supplementary Figure S9 presents maps of MR differences, highlighting the pronounced disparity in Antioquia during the 1990s and 2000s. Additionally, high gaps were observed in Guaviare (MRR 1.79; 95% CI, 1.79–1.79) and Caquetá (MRR 1.76; 95% CI, 1.76–1.77) during the 2000s, and in Arauca (MRR 1.96; 95% CI, 1.96–1.96) and Valle del Cauca (MRR 1.75; 95% CI, 1.75–1.76) from the 2000s, departments historically affected by armed conflict and violence, where excess male mortality may reflect the broader impact of structural and territorial violence.

Discussion

The findings from this study provide insights into the relative contribution of different causes of death to overall mortality differentials between sexes in a country affected by armed conflict over the past several decades. There are many well-established biological and social factors that contribute to disparities in life expectancy and health outcomes between males and females. Some have revealed the impact of chromosomal and hormonal differences in sex-based mortality, while others have pointed to social, cultural, environmental, and behavioural origins of differences.24, 25, 26 Advancing our understanding of sex-based differences in mortality is essential to move towards more equal and fairer health systems and societies; yet, in Latin America, comprehensive studies of long-term sex disparities remain scarce. By examining sex-specific mortality trends from 1980 to 2023, our study addresses a regional gap and provides long-term descriptive evidence of how mortality patterns by sex have evolved in Colombia, a country affected by prolonged conflict and social transformations.

In the last four decades, overall mortality declined substantially in Colombia for both sexes over the study period. General improvements in living standards, driven by periods of economic growth, poverty reduction, and enhanced access to basic services like sanitation, have played a key role.27 However, despite marked overall social and economic progress, the relative sex gap in mortality remains high, primarily due to men's greater risk of dying from external causes.

Two relevant social factors offer explanations for differential sex-based mortality in Colombia. First, persistent sex-based differences seem to be intricately tied to the country's socio-political context, reflecting Colombia's struggle with violence as a public health crisis. In the 15–24 age group, males experienced approximately 804% more deaths from external causes in the 1990s, representing 98.4% of the male excess deaths. This decreased to 652% in the 2000s, but still accounted for 97.9% of the male excess in that age group. Differences were even more pronounced in the 25–59 age group, reaching 864% in the 1990s (89.7% of male excess deaths) and increasing slightly to 874% in the 2000s (84.2% of male excess deaths).

Secondly, socioeconomic determinants of health underlie mortality differences. Mortality data controlled by health insurance schemes show pronounced disparities between the contributory and subsidised schemes. Females in the subsidised scheme exhibit higher MR than males in the contributory scheme. For most causes of death, particularly worrisome in non-communicable diseases, mortality rates under the subsidised scheme exceed those in the contributory scheme for both males and females (Supplementary Figure S4), indicating systematic differences in the quality, timeliness, and comprehensiveness of care received by beneficiaries of different insurance schemes.

Decades of armed conflict and structural inequality in Colombia are the backdrop to these findings. The period from the mid-1980s through the 1990s and early 2000s witnessed a brutal escalation of the internal armed conflict, involving complex interactions between leftist guerrilla groups (FARC, ELN), right-wing paramilitary organisations (AUC, often linked to landowners and drug traffickers), powerful drug cartels (Medellín, Cali), and state security forces.28 Homicide rates surged in the late 20th century and at one point were among the highest in the world.29 Our geographical analysis (Supplementary Figure S9) further illustrates the spatial dynamics of violence, with pronounced mortality disparities in departments like Antioquia during the 1990s, and later in Guaviare, Caquetá, Arauca, and Valle del Cauca, areas historically affected by armed conflict and narcotrafficking.30 While our data shows males overwhelmingly make up homicide statistics, it is essential to acknowledge that females have also been victims of violence in ways that may not manifest in a population-level early mortality, but that still represent significant trauma and harm.31

Our analysis also underscores how social determinants intersect with these sex differences. Mortality was higher in populations of lower socioeconomic status (approximated by enrolment to the subsidised insurance scheme) for both men and women. Prior evidence confirms that Colombians in the subsidised health system have worse health outcomes and higher mortality, linked to poverty-related conditions and barriers to care.32 This suggests that the high burden of early male mortality is concentrated in disadvantaged groups. For instance, rural and low-income communities—those most affected by conflict—have faced both higher exposure to lethal violence and restricted access to health care. During much of the conflict, rural areas experienced higher homicide rates than urban centres, potentially reflecting the territorial control dynamics of armed groups and the focus of conflict in these regions.33

This study had limitations. Firstly, paper-based mortality records from earlier decades may suffer from under-registration or misclassification, especially in remote and conflict-affected areas. Some conflict-related deaths were likely recorded simply as homicides in civil records, making it difficult to distinguish war-related mortality in the data.34 We addressed this through statistical methods; however, data gaps may have affected the precision of our estimates for certain regions and time periods. In recent decades, DANE, together with the Ministry of Health and Social Protection, has made significant progress in strengthening death registration, positioning it as a reference model for low- and middle-income countries.35

Second, our analysis of health insurance schemes was limited to data available since 2012, which constrains our understanding of how health system reforms have influenced mortality patterns over the full study period. Despite these challenges, working with long-term national mortality records remains essential for understanding historical mortality trends in Latin America, where comprehensive longitudinal data are rare. Our study contributes by reconstructing and interpreting these trends with a critical awareness of the data's limitations, rather than avoiding the complexity they entail.

Third, while we interpret our findings in light of structural factors such as armed conflict and institutional changes, our analysis remains primarily descriptive. We acknowledge that the lack of individual confounder information, beyond age and location, limited our ability to perform multivariate analyses to support causal inferences. Future research using causal inference analytical frameworks could help to disentangle the complex pathways linking sex, violence, health system, policy, and mortality.

Fourth, the absence of data on ethnicity throughout the full study period. While ethnicity is a key health determinant in Colombia, especially for Raizal and Romani populations, complete information was not available (data have been only systematically recorded since 2018). This precluded an intersectional analysis of mortality disparities by ethnic-racial group.

Colombia's mortality patterns demand a dual policy response that curbs violence while closing structural health system gaps. The 2002–2026 administration's “Total Peace” strategy has opened talks with guerrillas, paramilitaries and urban gangs, but field data indicate that many groups have used negotiations to expand territorial control, perpetuating young-male homicide risk.36 Violence-prevention programmes, rapid judicial response and economic alternatives to illicit markets must accompany any accord to protect youth from early mortality. Properly financing the recently decreed 2025 National Rural Health Plan could extend preventive and emergency strategies to conflict-affected municipalities where risk remains highest.37

Conclusion

Drawing on four decades of national vital statistics, this study offers the first long-term description of sex-specific mortality in Colombia, a country where armed conflict and major health system reforms unfolded simultaneously at the end of the twentieth century. We document a sustained decline in all-cause mortality for both sexes since 1980, a sign that broad social development translated into life-saving gains. Yet the pace of improvement diverged between sexes. External causes of death kept male mortality stubbornly high through the 1990s and 2000s, and while the gap narrowed after, young and middle-aged men continue to die at rates incongruent with the country's level of development.

Our findings suggest that excess male mortality is not solely attributable to biological factors but likely reflects an interplay between gendered vulnerabilities and broader social conditions, an interpretation consistent with existing literature, though not directly tested in our analysis. The steep gradient by health insurance scheme shows that when access, quality and timeliness of care improve, the gap reduces. At the same time, geography matters: the departments that bore the brunt of conflict still display elevated mortality, suggesting that post–accord violence, narcotrafficking, and weak state presence keep risk environments alive. Closing the sex gap in Colombia and other countries affected by armed conflicts will require simultaneous investment in security, poverty reduction, and sex-responsive health services, particularly emergency and rehabilitation capacity in rural areas.
