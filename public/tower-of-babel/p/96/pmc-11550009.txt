Chemical profiling of fungal metabolites via the OSMAC approach: Novel identification of Brianthein W from an endophytic fungus, Hypomontagnella monticulosa Zg15SU
Authors: Anisa Lutfia, Erman Munir, Yurnaliza Yurnaliza, Mohammad Basyuni, Hirosuke Oku
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Our study unveils a novel briarian diterpene, marking a groundbreaking discovery from an endophytic fungus, hypomontagnella monticulosa.•The compound (1) demonstrated remarkable anticancer activity, with IC50 values below 0.1 µg/mL against HCT-116, NBT-T2, and Panc-1 cell lines.•Utilizing an OSMAC approach, we identified a potent marine-derived compound in the MeOH extract of H. monticulosa, shedding light on its unique metabolic potential.

Introduction

Natural products are promising bioactive compounds that are increasingly studied for applications in pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, and the food industry. Medicinal plants are well-known for forming beneficial mutualistic relationships with microorganisms, providing a unique and suitable microenvironment for endophytes that produce unknown metabolites (Strobel 2003; Strobel 2018). Among these microorganisms, endophytic fungi surpass the majority of bacterial colonizers in species diversity and functionality. They serve as reservoirs of bioactive natural products, which can be extensively exploited for use in antibiotics, medicines, and various agricultural and industrial applications (Strobel and Daisy 2003; Zhang et al., 2006). The Zingiberaceae family is a group of medicinal plants regularly used in traditional herbal medicine, particularly in Indonesia (Ramadhanil et al., 2019). Associative endophytes have been reported from various sources, leading to the progressive development of studies and exploration of diverse endophytic fungi inhabiting different environments (Anisha and Radhakrishnan 2017; Ginting et al. 2013). Many endophytic fungi in uncommon Zingiberaceous species may also have interrelationships with other endophytic fungi, contributing to the co-synthesis of desirable phytochemicals within the host plant's tissues. However, studies on the phytochemical constituents produced by both endophytes and their host are still ongoing to fully elucidate the stable and mutual relationships involved (Aly et al., 2011). Preliminary investigations have reported the occurrence of a potential endophytic fungus, identified as Hypoxylon monticulosum (recently renamed Hypomontagnella monticulosa), isolated from Zingiber griffithii, a Zingiberaceous species from North Sumatra with limited available information. The fungus produced a detectable amount of scalarane sesterterpenoid during batch fermentation, a unique secondary metabolite typically found across various organismal taxa, but predominantly in marine sponges and nudibranchs (Dewick, 2009). Furthermore, this compound is notably recognized as a chemotaxonomic marker among sponge species (Gonzalez, 2010). Previously, sesterterpenoids were primarily regarded as phytotoxic compounds produced by endophytic fungi to colonize plants (Cimmino et al., 2014). However, the purified compound has been observed to exert anticancer properties against human cancer cell lines Panc-1 (pancreatic cancer), NBT-T2 (bladder cancer), and HCT116 (colon cancer) (Lutfia et al., 2021). A recent investigation revealed that novel metabolites can be produced by an endophytic strain through environmental modifications of fermentation conditions in axenic culture. These strategies may range from altering the medium and growth conditions to co-cultivating with one or more microbial strains to induce genetic machinery responses or act as elicitors (Marmann et al., 2014; Williams et al., 2008). This phenomenon was later termed One Strain, Many Compounds (OSMAC) (Bode et al., 2002). By applying the OSMAC approach, numerous unique and novel compounds have been isolated and identified as potential bioactive compounds in various bioassays, particularly those produced by endophytic and marine microorganisms (Hewage et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2016; Romano et al., 2018).

In this study, the OSMAC approach was used to discover novel compounds. The fermentation conditions were modified by growing an axenic culture of H. monticulosa in two variants of two broth media, namely, potato dextrose and Czapek-Dox broths, in the absence or presence of host rhizome extract. The cultures were subsequently extracted with MeOH and subjected to GC–MS analysis to identify any conserved compounds. Further purification using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and structural elucidation using 1H/13C NMR yielded a briarane diterpenes from this newly reported microbial source.

Materials and methods

Fermentation condition and extraction

The endophytic fungus, Hypomontagnella monticulosa Zg15SU, was initially isolated from the rhizome of Zingiber griffithii Baker, a wild specimen collected from Tahura Tongkoh Bukit Barisan, North Sumatra, Indonesia (Lutfia et al., 2021). The stock culture was subcultured onto Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA; Merck, Germany) by transferring the edge of the fungal colony with aerial hyphae to the fresh medium. The culture was incubated under ambient conditions for 3 days. Batch fermentation was conditioned in two different basal media, e.g., potato dextrose broth (PD) and Czapek-dox Broth (CZ) with (PD1, CZ1) or without plant extract (PD2, CZ2) as inducers. A 1 % (w/v) plant extract, prepared from a stock of Zingiber griffithii rhizome extract stored from prior research, was incorporated into the growth medium (Lutfia et al., 2021). Approximately 500 g of rhizome powder, sourced from air-dried rhizome segments, were soaked and macerated in ethyl acetate (EtOAc) (w/v) for 3 days with continuous agitation. The resulting macerates were filtered and separated using a separatory funnel and Whatman #1 filter paper. The extract was then concentrated under reduced pressure. Also, a standard medium for inducing bioactive-producing fungi was prepared following the suggestion of Wickerham medium (Wi) with composition (g/L): malt extract (3 g), yeast extract (3 g), peptone (5 g), NaCl (30 g), and glucose (10 g) (Kumar and Kaushik 2013). Three pieces of active-growing mycelial plugs were inoculated into 100 mL of broth, each placed in a 500-mL Erlenmeyer flask. The cultures incubated at ambient temperature for four weeks without agitation. After the incubation period, biomass or mycelial mats were filtered, pooled, and dried at room temperature. The metabolite extraction followed the liquid-state fermentation using a 100 % solvent system of MeOH (Marcellano et al., 2017). Mycelial mats were manually crushed and macerated using 100 mL solvents and maintained for three days under agitation. The macerates were separated using a Buchner funnel placed with a Whatman # 1 filter paper. The crude extracts were then concentrated in vacuo and stored in 4 °C for further experimentation.

Analysis of volatile metabolites by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS)

GC–MS analysis was performed on a GC–MS-QP2010 Ultra system (Shimadzu Europa GmbH, Dusseldorf, Germany) instrument fitted with RTX-5MS (30 × 0.25 × 0.10 m) column. The volume of injection was 1 µL. The oven temperature was maintained at 80.0 °C for 1 min and programmed to increase to reach 280 °C at the rate of 5 °C/min. The total flow was 9.0 mL/min within the pressure of 65 kPa. Helium (99.999 %) was used as the carrier gas with a flow rate of 3 mL/min at a linear speed of 36.8 cm/sec. The samples were injected in splitless mode. The scanning range of the spectral mass was set at 40 to 600 (m/z), with a scan-interval of 0.5 s.

Identification and multivariate analysis of identified compounds

Interpretation of the GC–MS mass-spectra was compared to the library of the National Institute of Standard and Technology (NIST), US, with >62,000 patterns. The compound names and molecular formulas were confirmed by comparing them with the library database. MeOH extract showing the largest number of identified compounds was summarized to give some information regarding the biological activities from each compound. Principal component analysis (PCA) and agglomerative hierarchical clustering (AHC) analysis were performed based on 40 identified chemical compounds to signify the difference among MeOH extracts (Lv et al., 2015). Both PCA and HCA were performed using XLSTAT statistical computer software package, version 14 (Addinsoft, New York, USA).

Antimicrobial test

The crude MeOH extracts from induced and non-induced H. monticulosa cultures were assayed for their bioactivities following the principle of disk diffusion assay (Balouiri et al., 2016). Stock culture from each indicator strains namely Candida maltose NBRC 1977, Escherichia coli JM 109, and Micrococcus luteus NBRC 13,867 were picked up from three colonies and dissolved in LB broth and grown at 35 °C for 24 hr. The microbial lawns were prepared by mixing a portion or 2 mL (1:10) of active-growing cultures into 18 mL molten LB agar until it solidified. One milligram from each MeOH extract from different culture conditions was dissolved into 1 mL acetone and pipetted ± 5–10 µL into sterile disks. The disks were placed proportionally on top of microbial lawns. The plates were incubated at 35 °C for 24 h Any visible clear zones surrounding the disks indicated the antimicrobial activities and were recorded in millimeter (mm) measured using a digital caliper. The anti-microbial test was performed in triplicate. An Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) test was conducted to compare the means of the inhibitory zones among different treatments (Wi, CZ1, CZ2, PD1, and PD2) for each microorganism using Minitab® ver. 19.1 (USA). This test determines whether there are any statistically significant differences between the means of the groups. Following the ANOVA, Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test was applied to identify specific differences between group means.

HPLC purification

Crude MeOH extracts of H. monticulosa (1.2 g) were partitioned using EtOAc:H2O (v/v). The EtOAc layer (1.16 g) was separated by open column chromatography (OCC) on NP-SiO2/n-Hex–EtOAc gradient to yield further five fractions (0.4, 3.5, 1.7, 116.2, 722.3 mg). The 5th fraction (722.3 mg) was the largest portion of the extract and was further purified using HPLC with an RP-C18 column, employing an n-hexane–EtOAc (10:2) solvent system to yield a briarian diterpene, the brianthein W (1).

Structural elucidation

Optical rotations were recorded on a Jasco DIP-1000 polarimeter (Jasco, Tokyo, Japan). The IR spectra were measured using a Hitachi T-2001 spectrophotometer. High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HRESIMS-ESI) spectra were recorded on a Jeol JMS-T1000GCV spectrometer (Jeol, Tokyo, Japan). Both 1D and 2D NMR spectra in CDCl3​ were recorded on a Bruker Avance III-500 spectrometer (Bruker Biospin, Massachusetts, USA) at 500 MHz. The 1H, 13C NMR, DEPT, COSY, HMBC, and HSQC spectra were obtained, with 13^{13}13C NMR chemical shifts deduced by analyzing HSQC and HMBC spectra. Chemical shifts are reported in ppm (δ), using tetramethylsilane (TMS, δH 0.00) or the CdCl3​ signal (δC 77.1) as standards (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Massachusetts, USA). Column chromatography was performed on Merck silica gel 60 (SiO2_22​) and Merck precoated TLC silica gel 60 F254 plates (Merck-Millipore, Massachusetts, USA). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was conducted in reversed-phase mode on a Hitachi l-4000H apparatus, utilizing an RP-C18 column, with a Shimadzu LC20A pump, an SPD-20A UV detector (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), and a Shodex RI-101 detector (Showa Denko, Tokyo, Japan), and appropriate solvent systems for purification.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxic effect of compound (1) on different human tumor cell lines, viz. Panc-1 (pancreatic cancer), NBT-T2 (bladder cancer), and HCT116 (colon cancer)was determined by 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazole-2yl)−2, 5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay using TACS™ MTT Cell Proliferation Assay (MTT-CPA) kit (Trevigen®, USA). Briefly, 100 µL cells of known concentration (1.0 × 104 cell density) were placed into three wells for each diluted concentration of compounds. Ten µL of MTT reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2–4 hrs at 37 °C. Subsequently, 100 µL detergent was added into each well following the appearance of the purple precipitate. The plate was incubated in the dark at 18–24 °C for periods ranging from 2 hr to overnight. Negative control was prepared from untreated cells, MTT, and solubilizing buffer (10 % SDS in 0.1 HCl). The absorbance was then determined by an ELISA reader at 570 nm. The inhibitory concentration (IC50) value was extrapolated from the concentration-response plot using linear regression analysis.

The MeOH extract derived from the Wickerham medium, later termed as MeOH-Wi was then subjected to further HPLC purification to discriminate the most potent bioactive metabolite for anticancer assay and to explore the possibility of finding a novel compound. MeOH-Wi was composed of neophytadiene (19.37 %), diethyl phthalate (16.38 %), 1H-purin-6-amine, N-((3-fluorophenyl)methyl)- (15.43 %), and octadecamethylcyclononasiloxane (11.89 %) with other minor components (<10 %). The relative abundance of mentioned components was approximately the same across MeOH extracts, with the exception to diethyl phthalate, which was greatly increased in potato dextrose and Czapek-Dox medium. These results corresponded with the outcome of the OSMAC-based approach in which different nutritional composition and fermentation conditions may yield different chemical compounds produced by a microbial strain.

The bioactivity of some identified compounds from MeOH-Wi was summarized in following the categorization of chemical groups (Table 2). The chemical group composition of MeOH-Wi was fatty acids (21.74 %), followed by terpenoids (17.38 %), cyclosiloxane (13.04 %), aldehydes (13.04 %), alkenes (8.7 %), hydrocarbons (8.7 %), esters (8.7 %), alkaloids (4.35 %), and an unclassified compound, 9,9-Dimethyl-3,7-diazabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane. Fatty acids, that formed the largest component number five and were identified as 9-octadecanoic acid, decanoic acid, N-lauroylsacrosine, oleic acid, and undecanoic acid. Antimicrobial activity of MeOH extracts

A preliminary bioactivity test was performed on three different pathogens resulted in very distinct activities among tested MeOH extracts in three replications (Table 3). The strongest antimicrobial activity was observed from MeOH-Wi extracts against all pathogens among different tested extracts (Table 2). The most sensitive pathogen in this study was M. luteus (Gram-positive bacteria) (±11 mm), followed by Candida maltosa (Yeast) (±9 mm) and E. coli (Gram-negative bacteria) (±5 mm). Based on the ANOVA results, significant differences were observed among the extract groups optimized using the OSMAC approach for Candida maltosa (F4,10 = 5.06; P = 0.017) and Micrococcus luteus (F4,10 = 17.68; P = 0.000). However, when comparing microorganism groups using the same extract, only the Wickerham group exhibited significant differences (F4,10 = 8.15; P = 0.019). MeOH-Wi extract from the standard fermentation medium was very enriched with chemical cocktails, especially those with antimicrobial properties as previously summarized in Table 2. Structural elucidation

Compound (1) was purified as colorless crystal with molecular formula: C24H33O8 (m/z = 449.20987, Δ −0.05 mmu) based on ESIMS (Fig. 3). FT-IR (Kbr) spectrum revealed an absorbance of hydroxyl group (-OH) at 3389 cm-1, -C-H stretching at 2932 cm-1, C = O group at 1731 cm-1, C = C and C—O group at 1374, 1242 dan 1018 cm-1. 1H and 13C spectrum of compound (1) (Table 4) is similar to a briarian diterpene compound, brianthein W isolated from a marine sponge, Briaerum sp. (Sheu et al., 1996). 1H NMR spectrum showed seven signals of C—H at 5.13 (1H, br d, H-2), 5.21 (1H, br d, J 8.5, H-6), 5.62 (1H, br d, J 8.0, H-7), 3.08 (1H, m, H-10), 5.94 (1H, d, J 10.0, H-12), 6.10 (1H, d, J 10.0, H-13), and 4.90 (1H, br d, J 6.0, H-14), three signals of CH2 at 2.26 and 1.67 (1H,1H m, H-3), 2.55 and 2.24 (1H, 1H, m, H-4), and 2.85;2.64 (1H, 1H, m, H-9), four signals of methyl at δ 1.00, 1.87, 1.98, and 1.11 for H-15, H-16, H-18, and H-20, with the other two signals at δ 2.08 and 2.15 for the acetyl group. The difference in the chemical shift (δ) in C-11(δC 81.4(C)), C-12 (δH 5.94 d J = 10.0 Hz; δC 136.2 (CH)), and C-13 (δH 6.10 d J = 10.0 Hz; δC 126.2 (CH)). HMBC spectrum of compound (1) showed a long-range coupling through three bonds between H-13 (δ 6.10 d, J = 10.0 Hz) with acetate carbonyl (δC 169.6) and between H-3 (δ 2.26 m) with carbonyl (δC 169.7). HMBC correlations were also observed between H-14 (δ 4.90 br d, J = 6.0 Hz) with C-15 methyl, H4–6 (δ 2.55 m; 5.21 br d, J = 8.5 Hz) with C-16, and H-12 (δ 5.94 d, J = 10 Hz) with C-2 . According to spectrum, all structure never been published before and compound (1) is a newly compound reported from a fungal source. The cytotoxicity of compound (1) was tested and plotted for calculating the inhibitory concentrations (IC50) as shown in Fig. 4. The IC50 of compound (1) was 0.077, 0.080, and 0.102 µg/mL against the growth of HCT-116, NBT-T2, and Panc-1, respectively. Discussion

In recent days, the effort for the finding of novel medicinal compounds to combat microbial infection and suppression of the incidence of cancer has been focused on natural products, especially those derived and synthesized by microbes. Traditionally, medicinal plants have provided a suitable environment for symbiotic endophytes whose success in colonization is attributable to the co-synthesis of protective bioactive metabolites (Jia et al., 2016). Indonesia, as one of the tropical countries and as a biodiversity hotspot, makes a significant contribution to the diversity of medicinal plants, including some notable Zingiberaceous species. Following our initial study, we have persevered and progressed with elaborative efforts for exploiting the further potential of an endophytic fungal strain, Hypomontagnella monticulosa, an endophytic ascomycetous fungal species, for producing new metabolites by applying the OSMAC-based approaches in batch fermentation (Romano et al., 2018).

In this study, the use of different fermentation or growth media was made successfully to produce distinct chemical profiles based on GC–MS analysis of MeOH extracts. The use of Wickerham medium for batch fermentation of H. monticulosa has proved its excellence in inducing the fungal strain to secrete more new metabolites in other media. A similar study also reported the use of Wickerham medium for fermentation of Alternaria sp., an endophytic fungal isolate from Ziziphus jujuba which resulted in the discovery of 21 natural products, which included 19 polyketides and one alkaloid (Orfali et al., 2017). Moreover, the Wickerham medium has been considered as the standard medium for cultivating marine-derived fungi for obtaining their secondary bioactive products (Kjer et al., 2010). The presence of high NaCl content might affect the production of some compounds by our fungal strain in the Wickerham medium. A previous study has reported that griseofulvin, a polyketide compound was produced only when the fungal strain was grown in Wickerham medium as a response to high Cl+ concentration (Fan et al., 2019). Another study also reported that the high glucose content in the medium may repress the transcription of some bioactive products. This was revealed in the case Penicillium sp. It yielded poor metabolite diversity (Assaf et al., 2020). In contrast, our study found the most diverse metabolites when Wickerham medium was used.

The addition of plant or rhizome extract from the host of H. monticulosa, the Zingiber griffithii failed to exhibit or induce a more diverse chemical composition during fermentation. Recent evidence has revealed that the incorporation of plant extract facilitated a higher number of culturable endophytic bacteria and greater diversity compared to synthetic medium, which was considered as a very rich environment for bacterial growth (Eevers et al., 2015). However, the case was not always consistent with the study on our fungal strain in which we presumed that the effect of adding plant extract will impact on the chemical diversity. Therefore, in the future investigation, we would like to test whether the addition of fungal extract of H. monticulosa might increase the chemical pools synthesized by its host or any Zingiberaceous species as a model for studying the co-synthesis of bioactive metabolites (Soliman and Raizada, 2013).

MeOH-Wi produced an array of bioactive metabolites as summarized from the available references. The resulting antimicrobial and bioactivities of MeOH-Wi emphasized the synergistic mechanism of chemical cocktails present in the extract. Fatty acids constituted the largest chemical group in the extract and may be responsible for the antimicrobial activity (Tanvir et al., 2018). Linoleic, linolenic, and oleic acid have the potential as antifungal agents against plant pathogenic fungi Crinipellis perniciosa, Pyrenophora avenae, Pythium ultimum, and Rhizoctonia solani. These fatty acids reduce the biomass of pathogens and inhibit their growth as seen in vitro study (Walters et al., 2004). Decanoic or capric acid (C10) and other long- and short-fatty acids demonstrated considerable antimicrobial activities in a dose-response experiment (IC80) against human oral pathogens such as Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Candida albicans, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Streptococcus gordonii, S. mutans, and S. sanguis (Huang et al., 2011), Undecanoic acid supplemented with quinic acid of Syzygium cumini were reported to decrease biofilm formation and virulence factor of Candida spp., a human fungal pathogen (Muthamil et al., 2018). Although identified in higher amounts and various types, the majority of fatty acids found in this study may be the result of disruption of mycelial tissue of H. monticulosa during batch fermentation. The mycelial tissue was much disrupted during the extraction and the fatty acids may not be considered as secondary metabolites.

The second most abundant chemical group identified from MeOH-Wi was terpenoids consisting of monoterpene, diterpene, and two sesquiterpenes. Although in trace amounts, their significant contribution to bioactivities may be note-worthy. Hexahydrofarnesol (3,7,11-Trimethyl-1-dodecanol), a sesquiterpene compound was reported to inhibit the squalene synthesis through enzymatic inhibition of squalene synthetase, a pathway in reducing the incidence of atherosclerotic disorders in human (Montellano et al., 1977). Isochiapin-B, another recently identified sesquiterpene was first reported in the essential oil of Citrus aurantium. It possesses both antimicrobial and antioxidant properties with potential use as natural preservative (Değirmenci and Erkurt, 2020). Neophytadiene, a diterpene compound was reported to exhibit anti-inflammatory based in vivo test using Wistar rats and egg-albumin as a phlogistic agent. The compound was majorly derived from the essential oil of the leaf of Jatropha curcas (Adeosun et al., 2017). Also, Neophytadiene was identified from bio-oil volatiles, which exhibited pesticidal activity against Leptinotarsa decemlineata, the Colorado potato beetle (CPB), in an in planta test (Caceres et al., 2015). We did not find any available references explaining the bioactivity of p-Menth-6-en-2,8-diol, cis as the only monoterpene identified in our study.

The GC–MS data of MeOH-Wi only gave an initial depiction of the chemical composition within the extract. Further purification using HPLC and structural elucidation using 1D and 2D NMR was conducted to find and identify any significant secondary metabolite compound traced in a considerable amount. Surprisingly, a diterpene compound or compound (1) was traced as the largest component of the purified extract. Compound (1) was later compared to the reference structure of brianthein W, a briarian diterpene mostly known to be synthesized by a marine sponge, Briareum spp., along with other compounds, such as briarellins and asbestinins (Sung and Chen, 2002). On the total of terpenoids studied from endophytic fungi, sesquiterpenes and diterpenes were the two most identified groups produced by the microorganisms (de Souza et al., 2011). However, some chemical shift based on 13C NMR analysis was noted and given in explanation to distinguish our finding with the reference. Compound (1) was known to exhibit anticancer activities against human cancer cell lines with IC50 lower than previous reports using (0.76<IC50<50) P-388, KB, A-549, and HT-29 cell lines (Sheu et al., 1996). Moreover, the diterpenoids also showed a wide range of bioactivities such as anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and antifouling which reflected its importance for the development of pharmacological compounds in the future (Majhi, 2020; Putra et al., 2017). Progressive finding on other new briarein- and eunicellin-type was still promoted solely from the marine sponges as the sources of the natural products. Hence, we consider our results interesting since different niche occupied by the endophytic fungus inhabiting the plant environment did not limit its genetic machinery in the biosynthesis of a distinct metabolite which predominantly exists in marine organisms. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on finding a briarian diterpene originating from an endophytic fungus, especially by H. monticulosa.
