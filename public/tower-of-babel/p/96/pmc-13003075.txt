Polyacetylenes as antibacterial, antibiofilm, and anti-quorum-sensing agents: Sources, chemistry, bioactivities, and 13C-NMR data
Authors: Reham M Samra, Fatma M Abdel Bar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rise of multidrug-resistant bacteria now poses significant challenges, rendering many conventional medicines ineffective and necessitating the search for new antimicrobial agents. Polyacetylenes, both natural and synthetic, possess diverse biological activities, with their remarkable antibacterial potential being particularly significant. This review offers an in-depth evaluation of the therapeutic roles of polyacetylenes as antibacterial, antibiofilm, and anti-quorum-sensing agents, summarizing the tested compounds, their natural sources, the assays employed, and the bacterial strains investigated. It also provides an overview of their biosynthesis, distribution, and pharmacokinetic properties, along with a comprehensive analysis of their chemical classification and 13C-NMR data. Despite decades of research on polyacetylenes, no previous review has systematically integrated their antibacterial mechanisms, biofilm-inhibitory effects, quorum-sensing disruption, and structure–activity relationships (SAR). Our analysis revealed that antibacterial and antibiofilm activities are highly dependent on chain length, conjugation, and specific functional substituents, with halogenation, carbonyl and cinnamate incorporation, and stereochemical configuration significantly modulating potency beyond the previously assumed requirement for hydroxylation. This work fills that gap by compiling information on about 90 polyacetylenes that were reported between 1947 and January 2025. It draws attention to their dual antibacterial mechanism, involving disruption of membrane and downregulation of key QS-related genes, leading to attenuated virulence, suppressed biofilm formation, and marked decrease in bacterial burden. In conclusion, polyacetylenes represent promising antibacterial, antibiofilm, and anti-quorum-sensing candidates, offering valuable scaffolds for developing new therapies against multidrug-resistant infections.Keywords: Polyacetylenes, Antibiotic resistance, Antibiofilm, Quorum-sensing, SAR, Asteraceae

Introduction

Polyacetylenes are fascinating natural and synthetic lipophilic metabolites characterized by the presence of multiple carbon–carbon triple bonds. They occur across diverse biological sources, including plant families (mainly Asteraceae, Araliaceae, and Apiaceae), fungal taxa (mainly basidiomycetes), insects, humans (in small amounts), and various marine species, such as sponges, cnidarians, algae, tunicates, and marine microbes (Negri 2015; Singh et al. 2020). Some food plants that contain polyacetylenes in their edible parts include parsley, lettuce, celery, carrot, artichoke, aubergine, parsnip, caraway, fennel, and tomato (Negri 2015).

Besides their unique carbon–carbon triple bonds, polyacetylenes are characterized by the presence of a diversity of substituents such as oxygenated, halogenated, or sulfur-containing groups, contributing to their structural and functional variability (Legrave et al. 2015). The diversity of structures is attributed to differences in chain length, degree of unsaturation, the nature of attached substituents, alkene stereochemistry, and the configuration of hydroxyl groups. This trend suggests that such structural complexity underpins the broad spectrum of bioactivities attributed to these compounds (Legrave et al. 2015). Interestingly, several reports converge on the observation that polyacetylenes exhibit cytotoxic, anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, antimicrobial, antiviral, neuroprotective, and serotogenic activities (Singh et al. 2020). Moreover, accumulating evidence indicates that their biological actions stem from the reactivity of their triple bonds, which can capture nucleophilic groups such as thiols (Santos et al. 2022a).

The procedures required for extracting, separating, and structurally elucidating these structurally diverse compounds pose significant challenges for scientists due to their instability, particularly their sensitivity to salinity, oxygen, light, heat, and pH (Legrave et al. 2015). The unique IR and NMR spectral patterns of acetylenic functions make it possible to identify polyacetylenic compounds. Regarding IR spectra, acetylenic groups exhibit characteristic absorption bands in the range from 2000–2500 cm−1 (C≡C stretching), while terminal acetylenic groups show an additional diagnostic band for acetylenic C-H stretching (≡C-H) near 3300 cm−1, arising from the sp-hybridized carbon atom. Moreover, comparative spectral evidence indicates that alkynes can be easily identified in the NMR spectra, with chemical shifts between 1.8–3.1 ppm for terminal acetylenic group in 1H-NMR, and corresponding carbon signals in the 80–90 ppm range in 13C-NMR (Legrave et al. 2015).

The increasing resistance to existing antimicrobial drugs, and the subsequent reduction in their effectiveness, has increased the worldwide imperative to discover and develop new antimicrobials. The increase and spread of multidrug resistance have made bacterial infections a serious concern, prompting intensive research efforts to discover novel antimicrobial agents. Multidrug-resistant bacterial strains account for nearly 50% of infections acquired in healthcare settings worldwide. Representative pathogens include those that produce expanded-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBL) like Acinetobacter baumannii, Escherichia coli, Helicobacter pylori, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae, as well as penicillin-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae (PRSP) and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Shigella spp. (Abass et al. 2024).

Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that bacterial biofilms and quorum-sensing (QS) are accountable for over 60% of all bacterial infections. Bacterial biofilms consist of bacteria coated with an extracellular mucus comprising proteins, polysaccharide matrix, and lipids. Bacteria form biofilms when they encounter environmental pressures such as oxidative stress, high osmotic pressure, food deficits, or exposure to antimicrobial agents. The antibiotic resistance of biofilms increases by roughly 1000 times, posing a significant challenge for conventional therapeutic interventions. Interestingly, quorum sensing further intensifies this problem, as it enables cell-to-cell communication and coordinates pathogenic behaviors in bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus mutans, P. aeruginosa, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Haemophilus influenzae bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus mutans, P. aeruginosa, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Haemophilus influenzae (Kumar et al. 2017).

Polyacetylenes demonstrated antibacterial and anti-biofilm activities against pathogens such as K. pneumoniae and S. aureus (Emad et al. 2020; Aljarba et al. 2024), as well as downregulated genes encoding QS and biofilm (Aljarba et al. 2024), underscoring their potential as promising leads for the development of new antimicrobial therapies.

This review focuses on the existing knowledge about natural and synthetic polyacetylenes with tested antibacterial activity. It covers biosynthesis, sources, antibacterial, anti-biofilm, and anti-quorum sensing activities, and presents the available 13C-NMR data for the compounds examined. Herein, polyacetylenes are classified according to the length of their alkynyl chains and the presence of specific heteroatoms into several categories, including C7-C25 acetylenic compounds, thiophene polyacetylenes, dioxaspiro polyacetylenes, and miscellaneous polyacetylenes. Drawing on literature published up to January 2025, this review provides a comprehensive reference for advancing research on polyacetylenes as emerging antibacterial, antibiofilm, and anti-quorum-sensing agents.

Research methodology

To collect and analyze scientific information on polyacetylenes with antibacterial, antibiofilm, and anti-quorum-sensing properties, a systematic literature search was conducted. Reputable scientific databases, such as PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar, and other sources (e.g., SpringerLink, Wiley, and ResearchGate), were searched for articles published until January 2025, the cutoff date for literature inclusion in the current review. Terms including “polyacetylenes”, “polyacetylene compounds”, or “polyacetylenic compounds” were used in the search strategy with Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT), combined with the keywords; “antibacterial”, “antimicrobial”, “antibiofilm”, “anti-quorum sensing”, “quorum-sensing inhibition”, “biosynthesis”, “natural compounds”, “mechanism of action”, “toxicological properties”, “pharmacokinetic properties”, “structure–activity relationship (SAR)”, and “13C-NMR”. A total of 209 articles were initially retrieved. Of these, 158 articles were left for preliminary screening after duplicates were eliminated and studies unrelated to the targeted biological activities or planned structural characterization methodology of polyacetylenes were excluded. Based on predetermined selection criteria, collected publications were further refined to include studies reporting: (i) natural (or derivatized) polyacetylenes with investigated antimicrobial activities; (ii) distribution and biosynthetic pathway, (iii) experimental evaluation of antibacterial, antibiofilm, or anti-quorum-sensing activities; (iv) SAR and mechanism of action; (v) toxicological and pharmacokinetic properties; and/or (v) availability of 13C-NMR spectral data. Finally, 95 research articles were chosen and added to the final review after this evaluation.

Distribution and traditional medicinal uses of plants containing polyacetylenes

Polyacetylenes are common plant-derived secondary metabolites that serve as important chemotaxonomic markers and ecological agents such as phytoalexins, allelopathic compounds, and antifeedants, and contribute to plant defense mechanisms against pathogens and herbivores. Their occurrence is especially concentrated in at least 24 plant families, notably Asteraceae, Apiaceae, Araliaceae, Campanulaceae, Olacaceae, Pittosporaceae, and Santalaceae (Konovalov 2014a, 2014b; Xie and Wang 2022). Their biosynthesis originates from unsaturated fatty acids, and they can be found in various plant organs, often as part of essential oils or in combination with other bioactive molecules (Konovalov 2014a, 2014b). In addition to their ecological functions, polyacetylenes have attracted considerable research interest for their broad spectrum of biological activities. These bioactivities underpin the traditional and modern herbal medicinal uses of many polyacetylene-containing plants (Konovalov 2014a; Lin et al. 2016; Xie and Wang 2022; Jo et al. 2023; Lai et al. 2023).

Polyacetylene-containing plants have been utilized in folk and traditional medicine for centuries by various cultures, reflecting their broad spectrum of bioactivities, particularly their application as anti-infective therapy, a practice now supported by modern research. As an example, Launaea capitata (Asteraceae) has been historically utilized in ethnomedicine for addressing infectious conditions. Recent investigations supported this traditional use, demonstrating that its polyacetylene glycosides exhibited strong antibacterial and antibiofilm effects against pathogens like K. pneumoniae, including the ability to disrupt biofilm formation and downregulate bacterial quorum-sensing genes (Aljarba et al. 2024). Similarly, polyacetylenes isolated from Tanacetum vulgare (tansy) roots have demonstrated notable antibacterial effects against plant pathogenic bacteria, supporting the plant’s historical use in treating infections (Móricz et al. 2018b). Comprehensive reviews highlight that polyacetylenes from various medicinal plants possess broad-spectrum antimicrobial, antibacterial, antifungal, and even antiviral properties, which likely contribute to their traditional use in managing infectious diseases (Negri 2015; Móricz et al. 2018b). In addition, polyacetylenes from Cussonia zimmermannii, a Tanzanian medicinal plant, have shown activity against protozoan pathogens such as Trypanosoma and Leishmania spp., aligning with its ethnobotanical use for treating parasitic infections (Senn et al. 2007). These findings underscore the scientific basis for the traditional anti-infective uses of polyacetylene-rich plants, highlighting their promise as candidates for developing new antimicrsobial drugs (Senn et al. 2007; Negri 2015; Móricz et al. 2018b; Aljarba et al. 2024). Biosynthesis of polyacetylenes

In most eukaryotes, biosynthesis of polyacetylenes occurs in the cytosol, but in plants, it proceeds in the stroma of plastids, where the fatty acid precursors originate. As described by (Minto and Blacklock 2008), Singh et al., (2020), the biogenesis of alkynyl moieties proceeds through two distinct pathways (Fig. 1). In pathway A, fully elongated (full-length) acyl lipids undergo desaturase-mediated modification to yield structures bearing alkynyl groups. In contrast, pathway B introduces the alkynyl functionality during the de novo assembly of fatty acids, in which the acyl chain is progressively extended via sequential head-to-tail Claisen-type condensations of malonyl-derived units, ultimately giving rise to the characteristic triple-bonds of polyacetylenes.

Biosynthetic pathway of polyacetylenes

These biochemical pathways generate three important intermediates: tariric acid, stearolic acid, and crepenynic acid (Fig. 1). These intermediates serve as essential precursors in the formation of numerous naturally occurring polyynes, which are converted through subsequent transformations into a wide array of natural polyacetylenes (Negri 2015). With the help of desaturase/acetylenase enzymes, subsequent unsaturation reactions introduce additional alkyne moieties, often in conjugated forms. Desaturation at the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) happens on C16–C18 acyl chains, which are also capable of undergoing oxidative cleavage processes and chain elongation (Minto and Blacklock 2008). Notably, elongation pathways that produce acyl chains of C20 and longer are comprehensively discussed in (Leonard et al. 2004). The incorporation of sulfur atoms into a conjugated diyne moiety results in the generation of thiophene rings through a sequential multistep reaction. A plausible biosynthetic pathway for thiophene acetylenes has been proposed in (Zhang et al. 2014a). This category of compounds is predominantly synthesized by plant species within the Asteraceae family, as well as certain fungi (Negri 2015). Additionally, oxidation reactions are crucial in incorporating functional groups, including hydroxyls, ketones, and epoxides, as well as shortening the polyacetylenes' carbon chains. Moreover, hydroxyl groups may undergo esterification, either through intramolecular reactions or by coupling with various carboxylic acids. Also, glycosylation of hydroxyl groups with different polysaccharide enhances the diversity of structures.

Chemistry and bioactivities

C7 Polyacetylenes

Four C7 polyacetylenes (1–4) with tested antibacterial activity were reported in literature, as seen in (Fig. 2, Table 2). One synthetic and three natural compounds were established. Compounds 1 and 2 were isolated from Gymnopilus spectabilis and are believed to be biosynthesized from the crepenynate pathway. Crepenynic acid acts as a central monoacetylenic precursor in this fungal biosynthetic pathway, undergoing successive desaturation steps and related enzymatic transformations that give rise to short-chain polyacetylenes, such as hepta-4,6-diyn-3-ol (2) and its chlorinated analogue, 7-chloro-hepta-4,6-diyn-3-ol (1). Because of this pivotal role, crepenynic acid was established as a crucial branching intermediate from which many fungal polyacetylenes are derived (Aqueveque et al. 2006; Minto and Blacklock 2008). Pronounced antibacterial effects of compounds (1) and (2) against Bacillus brevis, Bacillus subtilis, Streptococcus pyogenes, and S. aureus have been attributed to the high chemical reactivity of their acetylenic functionalities (Aqueveque et al. 2006). Similarly, the antimicrobial reputation of Bidens pilosa L. aligns with its production of phenylhepta-1,3,5-triyne (3), a bioactive polyacetylene recognized for its antibiotic potential (Alvarez et al. 1996).

C8 Polyacetylenes

Two C8 polyacetylene compounds (5 and 6) with antibacterial activity have been identified in mushrooms (Fig. 3, Table 3). Several edible mushrooms, notably Lentinula edodes (shiitake mushroom), possess documented medicinal properties, including antibacterial effects, supporting their integration into clinical applications (Qi et al. 2023). Among this subclass, lentinamycin (6) derived from L. edodes, exhibited pronounced growth-inhibitory activity, particularly against Gram-positive bacteria and other clinically relevant strains, as revealed from paper disk and agar diffusion assays (Fukushima-Sakuno 2020).

C9 polyacetylenes

This subclass includes two compounds (7 and 8) as shown in Fig. 4 and Table 4. The active component (8) was identified as an amide formed from the C-9 ene-triyne carboxylic acid and p-aminobenzoic acid. This metabolite exhibited pronounced selectivity for Gram-positive bacteria, showing remarkable potency against these strains while displaying reduced effectiveness against Gram-negative bacteria. Compound 8 displayed extremely potent activity against S. aureus, with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) below 0.001 µg/mL, and against S. pyogenes, with an MIC of 0.008 µg/mL. However, the reduced analogue lacking the ene-triyne functionality, and thus the reactive acetylenic moiety, failed to inhibit S. aureus, highlighting the essential role of the reactive triple bond system in mediating antibacterial effects (Parish et al. 2004). Additional antibacterial activity was observed against Bacillus megaterium (MIC value, 4 µg/mL) and S. pneumoniae (MIC value, 8–16 µg/mL). Among Gram-negative bacteria, compound 8 showed no detectable effect on K. pneumoniae, E. coli, or P. aeruginosa. However, inhibitory activity of Haemophilus influenzae was reported with an MIC value of 0.5 µg/mL (Parish et al. 2004).

C10 polyacetylenes

A total of 13 compounds (9–21) of this category were encountered in the literature from plants, fungi, and through synthesis (Fig. 5, Table 5). Konovalov (2014b) noted that Asteraceae species commonly contain aliphatic polyacetylenes with carbon chain lengths of C10, C13, C14, and C17. Six compounds from this subclass were extracted from Asteraceae species then tested for their antibacterial activity. The antibacterial potential of compound 10 was tested against K. pneumoniae and S. aureus, where it exhibited slight to moderate inhibition of biofilm-forming isolates (Emad et al. 2020; Aljarba et al. 2024). The bioactivity of polyacetylene derivatives 15 and 19 from Bellis perennis, and their structural analogues (16–18) have been investigated against panels of clinically significant bacterial species. Only compounds 15 and 17 exhibited antimicrobial potential (Avato et al. 1997). In this study, linear polyacetylenes were found to require an accessible reactive functional group to influence microbial growth through metabolic interactions (Avato et al. 1997). Therefore, the two active 15 and 19 compounds structurally resemble the bioactive polyacetylenes from fungi with carboxylic or hydroxyl groups. Alternatively, additional substituents, including esters or hydrocarbons, appeared ineffective (Avato et al. 1997).

C14 polyacetylenes

This sub-class includes twenty-one compounds (29–49, Fig. 9). Six of which (30, 37–41) are cinnatriacetins containing a cinnamate moiety with fourteen fourteen-carbon long chain. These cinnatriacetins were isolated from the Japanese edible mushroom 'kanzotake', Fistulina hepatica. They share the same skeleton but differ in the geometry of double bonds at C5 and C2` along with the substitution at the benzene ring. Compound 30 is a geometrical isomer of 40 at C2`-C3`. These compounds showed varying activities active against Gram-positive as well as negative bacterial strains. Research revealed that the existence of cinnamic acid residues in these compounds can enhance the stability of polyacetylene intermediates that are free and reactive. These residues act as effective protecting groups, and their ester moiety can be enzymatically cleaved under specific conditions, releasing cinnamic acid and returning polyacetylene to its natural reactivity (Whaley et al. 2023). Additionally, three polyacetylene glycosides, designated as coreosides (44–46), were obtained from the Coreopsis tinctoria Nutt. Buds. These compounds possess C14 polyacetylene backbones bearing “cis” double bonds at position C6-C7. Guo et al., (2017) reported that C14 polyacetylene glycosides, particularly those containing cis double bonds, are rarely obtained from members of the Asteraceae. The three isolated glycosides were evaluated for their antimicrobial properties against nine pathogenic microorganisms, and their MIC values are presented in Table 9. Among them, compound 44 exhibited remarkable efficacy and demonstrated promising potential as a novel antibacterial agent targeting Bacillus anthracis, the causative pathogen of a globally distributed and highly fatal zoonosis (Guo et al. 2017). Recent findings have highlighted the significant antibacterial and antibiofilm properties of a C14 polyacetylene glucoside (47) derived from the whole plant of L. capitata. This compound demonstrated inhibitory effect against a group of clinical K. pneumoniae strains, showing MIC values between 16 and 128 µg/mL. Furthermore, it effectively reduced the biofilm formation of strong and moderate bacterial isolates, achieving a decrease from 84.21% to 42.1%, compared to an extract, which showed a reduction of 68.42%. Additionally, the compound was found to downregulate several quorum-sensing genes, including wzm, luxS, wbbm, and mrkA. In vivo studies indicated its antibacterial efficacy through the enhancement of liver and spleen histological structure, a reduction in TNF-α immunoreactivity, as well as a decrease in bacterial burden and inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and IL-6 (Aljarba et al. 2024).

C17 Polyacetylenes

Eleven C17 Polyacetylenes (52–62) exhibiting antibacterial activity have been identified mainly from species within the Apiaceae and Araliaceae families (Fig. 11, Table 11). An earlier investigation assessed the antibacterial properties of three representative compounds (52, 57, and 62) against a spectrum of bacterial species, including both Gram-positive and negative strains. Among them, falcarinol (57) displayed the greatest inhibition, with MIC values ranging from 12.5 to 37.6 μg/mL, followed by acetoxyfalcarinol (52). However, panaxjapyne (62) exhibited no detectable activity (Hinds et al. 2017). Although these tested polyacetylenes analogues share a terminal methyl substituent, panaxjapyne (62) lacks a double bond at C1, a structural characteristic that may account for its inactivity (Schinkovitz et al. 2008). Additionally, four polyacetylenes (55, 59–61) were obtained from Panax ginseng hairy root cultures, which served as an efficient system for isolating secreted antimicrobial metabolites due to their growth in confined liquid media. Among the tested metabolites, 60 and 61 exhibited superior antibacterial effects compared to compounds 55 and 59, indicating that keto substitution contributes to improved antimicrobial efficacy in polyacetylenes (Fukuyama et al. 2012).

C18 polyacetylenes

This sub-class comprises four polyacetylenes (63–66) (Fig. 12, Table 12). Thiophene polyacetylenes

Ten thiophene acetylenes (72–77 and 80–83, Fig. 16) were isolated from the Xanthopappus subacaulis herb. The antibacterial properties of the isolated compounds were evaluated against Gram-positive and negative bacterial varieties. Compound 82, possessing a threo configuration, displayed pronounced activity toward B. subtilis, while its isomer 81, characterized by an erythro configuration, demonstrated broad-spectrum antibacterial effects toward Erwinia carotovora, B. cereus, E. coli, and S. aureus. Halogenated compounds 72, 73, 80, and 83, each bearing a chlorine (Cl) substituent at C-14, exhibited moderate antibacterial effects against B. subtilis, B. cereus, E. carotovora, S. aureus, and E. coli. However, compounds 74–77, in which the C-14 chlorine is replaced by a hydroxyl group, showed no detectable antibacterial activity against any of the tested strains (Table 16). Zhang et al., (2014a) concluded that the presence of a Cl substituent at C-14 is crucial for the antibacterial activity of this class of thiophene acetylenes.

Four structurally diverse polyacetylenes originating from distinct ecological niches have been documented in this subclass, all of which were evaluated for their antibacterial activity (Table 18, Fig. 18). Antibacterial mechanisms of polyacetylenes

The antibacterial mechanism of polyacetylenes is primarily attributed to their chemical reactivity, which is augmented by the existence of several acetylenic bonds. Such acetylenic bonds can trap nucleophiles, such as thiols, disrupting bacterial cellular processes (Santos et al. 2022a). ​​Additionally, specific structural features, such as chloride groups in thiophene acetylenes, carboxylic or hydroxyl groups, and keto-type functionalities, further contribute to their antibacterial efficacy. These functional groups are readily accessible for metabolic reactions, influencing bacterial growth and biofilm formation.

Although mechanistic studies on the antibacterial activity of natural polyacetylenes remain limited, recent investigations have explored synthetic analogues. Notably, pyrrolidinium-pendant polyacetylenes with dynamic helical conformations were shown to bind directly to the lipid bilayer in the bacterial cell membrane (Zhou et al. 2023). This interaction results in structural destabilization and increased membrane permeability, resulting in the efflux of vital intracellular constituents such as ions and ATP. The consequent disruption of cellular homeostasis ultimately results in bacterial cell death (Zhou et al. 2023).

Bacteria employ two major QS systems to regulate behaviors such as virulence and biofilm formation: the type I system, which involves N-acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs) and the LuxI/LuxR system for intraspecies communication, and the type II system, which uses autoinducer-2 (AI-2) produced by LuxS for interspecies signaling. K. pneumoniae lacks a typical type I QS system and does not synthesize AHLs. Instead, it employs SdiA, a LuxR homolog that senses AHL signals originating from neighboring microorganisms. SdiA-mediated control downregulates fimA and luxS expression and maintains the fimS switch in an inactive configuration, thereby reducing fimbrial production and attenuating biofilm formation. Conversely, the K. pneumoniae type II system, mediated by the LuxS/AI-2 pathway, functions as a pivotal regulatory axis for biofilm architecture and lipopolysaccharide production. Downregulation of luxS leads to suppressed expression of mrkA (a fimbrial subunit critical for biofilm development) and the LPS-associated genes wbbM and wzm. This indicates that the LuxS/AI-2 system coordinates surface structure biosynthesis and biofilm dynamics (Wang et al. 2022; Li and Ni 2023). Notably, treatment with polyacetylenes significantly downregulates these QS-related genes (i.e., luxS, mrkA, wbbM, and wzm), highlighting their potential to disrupt QS-regulated virulence pathways, Fig. 19 (Aljarba et al. 2024).

Mechanism of antibacterial action of polyacetylenes in Klebsiella pneumoniae

Polyacetylenes exhibit antibacterial activity through two primary mechanisms disruption of bacterial membranes and blockade of quorum-sensing (QS) pathways. Polyacetylenes disrupt QS regulation by downregulating key QS-related genes, including luxS, mrkA, wbbM, and wzm. This interference impairs AI-2–mediated signaling, reducing biofilm-associated gene expression (mrkA) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) biosynthesis (wbbM and wzm). Additionally, polyacetylenes suppress luxS expression, further weakening interspecies communication via the type II QS system. These effects diminish biofilm development, alter cell surface architecture, and attenuate bacterial virulence, contributing to the antibacterial activity of polyacetylenes against K. pneumoniae.

Structure–activity relationship (SAR)

The polyacetylenes reviewed in this work demonstrated broad-spectrum antibacterial properties against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, although their activities varied markedly according to their structural features. Generally, compounds bearing extended conjugation and multiple acetylenic (C≡C) bonds exhibited superior potency, as demonstrated by octa-2,3-diene-5,7-diyne-1-ol (6), (E)−4-(nona-2-en-4,6,8-triynamido)benzoic acid (8), (R)−1,2-dihydroxy-trideca-3,5,7,9,11-pentayne (26), falcarindiol (56), xestospongiamide (66), and compound 87, which displayed low MIC or IC50 values indicating strong antibacterial potential. In particular, compound 8 was as the most active derivative, with MIC values range of 0.001–0.008 µg/mL against S. aureus and S. pyogenes, while the pentayne derivative 26 showed comparable activity with MICs values of 0.5–1 µg/mL against MRSA and VREF, suggesting that increased chain length and polyunsaturation enhance reactivity and antimicrobial potency. Conversely, shorter or glycosylated polyacetylenes, such as coreosides E–F (44–46), were significantly less active (MICs ≥ 68–93 µM), suggesting that sugar residues may impair membrane permeation and weaken antimicrobial activity.

Additionally, the incorporation of a chlorine substituent in thiophene acetylenes has been shown to improve the antibacterial efficacy. This structural impact is clearly demonstrated when comparing compound 1 (7-chloro-hepta-4,6-diyn-3-ol) with its non-halogenated analogue compound 2 (hepta-4,6-diyn-3-ol). The chloro derivative exhibited noticeably stronger antibacterial effects, producing inhibition zones of 13–18 mm against B. brevis, S. pyogenes, and S. aureus, whereas the hydroxy analogue generated noticeably smaller zones of 9–10 mm against the same bacterial strains under identical assay conditions. This enhanced activity suggests that chlorination may improve bacterial membrane interaction or target affinity, whereas the absence of the chloro substituent in compound 2 results in weaker antibacterial action.

The presence of functional groups that are readily accessible for metabolic reactions, such as carboxylic or hydroxyl groups, may be essential for influencing microorganism growth. Within the falcarinol-type polyacetylenes, antibacterial potency is strongly influenced by the degree and position of hydroxyl substituents. Falcarindiol (56), a more polar derivative bearing two hydroxyl groups, exhibited superior antibacterial effects, with MIC values of 2.6–10.4 µg/mL against M. luteus, B. subtilis, and S. aureus. In comparison, falcarinol (57), a less polar compound with a single hydroxyl group, showed relatively lower antibacterial activity, with MICs of 12.5–37.6 µg/mL for B. cereus, S. aureus, and MRSA, and 16.4 µM against Mycobacterium spp. The position, number, and nature of oxygenated substituents critically alter antibacterial potency. A clear SAR is evident within the panaxacol-type polyacetylenes (compounds 55, 59–61), where dihydropanaxacol (55) exhibited moderate effects with MICs of 125–250 µg/mL, whereas the introduction of a hydroxyl group at C-1 in 1-hydroxydihydropanaxacol (59) markedly diminished activity, yielding MIC values of 500–1000 µg/mL. Conversely, keto-analogues demonstrated considerably improved activities as in 17-hydroxypanaxacol (60), which exhibited MICs of 31–125 µg/mL, notably 31 µg/mL against MRSA, while panaxacol (61) produced consistent MIC values of 63 µg/mL across the tested panel. While excessive hydroxylation attenuates potency as in case of compound 60 compared to 61. These comparative data indicate that keto substitution enhances antibacterial performance, likely by optimizing the balance between polarity and lipophilicity and improving interactions with bacterial membranes. Thus, carbonyl incorporation represents a favorable modification in polyacetylene scaffolds for antimicrobial development, whereas hydroxylation exerts variable effects that depend on the number and positional context of the hydroxyl groups.

Another noteworthy observation emerges from the antibacterial activities of the two stereoisomeric forms of the thiophene polyacetylene derivative, xanthopappin D (compounds 81 and 82), which clearly demonstrate the influence of the C-10/C-11 configuration on bioactivity. The 10,11-erythro isomer (81) exhibited markedly stronger antibacterial effects, with MIC values of 7.25–15.5 µg/mL against B. cereus, S. aureus, and E. carotovora, whereas 10,11-threo-xanthopappin D (82) showed substantially reduced potency, displaying MICs as high as 62.5–125 µg/mL against most of the same bacterial strains. This difference suggests that the erythro configuration, in which the OH groups at C-10/C-11 lie on the same side, provides a more favorable molecular conformation for membrane interaction or target engagement compared to the threo arrangement, where substituent orientation on opposite sides diminishes biological activity. These data generally highlight stereochemistry as a crucial structural determinant governing the antibacterial efficacy of polyacetylenes.

Additionally, it seems that adding residues of cinnamic acid gives free reactive polyacetylene intermediates more stability. By serving as a protective group, the cinnamate moiety increases the stability of otherwise reactive polyacetylene intermediates. Its ester linkage can be enzymatically broken to liberate the active polyacetylene, regulating reactivity and maintaining antimicrobial potential (Whaley et al. 2023).

Both our SAR findings and those reported by Skeen et al. converge on the principle that antibacterial activity in polyacetylenes is closely linked to structural parameters such as chain length, conjugation, and the presence of rationally positioned hydroxyl groups and extended alkynyl systems that display enhanced potency (Skeen et al. 2024). However, whereas Skeen and colleagues focused primarily on variations in alkynol placement relative to the diyne core, our study investigates a broader chemical landscape. We demonstrated that additional features, including halogenation, carbonyl substitution, glycosylation, cinnamate residues, and stereochemistry, either strengthen or diminish antibacterial effects, thereby revealing structural impacts not addressed in their work. Additionally, Skeen et al., (2024) concluded that the optimal carbon chain length is narrow and critical, and only diynes with 10–13 total carbon atoms retained bacterial growth inhibition. However, our results suggest that polyynes from various classes, including C8, C9, C13, C17, and C18 were remarkably bioactive. Also, the presence of a hydroxyl group was suggested by Skeen et al. as a crucial requirement for activity, but our results showed that other functional groups such as amide groups (such as in compounds 8 and 66) or bromo substituents (such as in compounds 66 and 87) noticeably enhanced activity in the absence of hydroxylation.

Thus, SAR findings in this review indicate that polyacetylenes with superior antibacterial effectiveness tend to share certain structural features, including multiple acetylenic bonds with extended conjugation, limited polar substitution (Keto groups and rational hydroxylation), amide or halogen groups, and favorable stereochemical orientations. In contrast, excessive hydroxylation, glycosylation, or disruption of conjugation generally diminishes antibacterial activity. Toxicological and pharmacokinetic properties of polyacetylenes

Polyacetylenes have demonstrated the capacity to suppress pathogenic bacterial proliferation and impede biofilms development, supporting their prospects as templates for new antimicrobial interventions (Shamim et al. 2023). The toxicological and pharmacokinetics of polyacetylenes can influence both their efficacy and safety profiles, necessitating thorough investigation during drug development. While their natural origin often suggests lower toxicity and greater safety, some polyacetylenes are known to possess significant cytotoxic and neurotoxic properties, necessitating careful toxicological evaluation before clinical application. For instance, bupleurotoxin and oenanthotoxin from the Bupleurum genus exhibited strong neurotoxicity, primarily by inhibiting GABA receptor-mediated signaling in the brain, which can lead to epileptic seizures and cerebral lesions in animal models (Zhang et al. 2014b; Lin et al. 2016). This neurotoxicity underscores the need for careful toxicological evaluation to avoid adverse effects in potential drug candidates. Other polyacetylenes, like falcarinol from Apiaceae vegetables and certain compounds from Panax ginseng and Tridax procumbens, display pronounced cytotoxic effects against various cancer cell lines, indicating their anti-tumor potential; however, their concomitant toxicity toward normal cells remains a significant limitation (Zidorn et al. 2005; Konovalov 2014b; Kim et al. 2022; Tan et al. 2024; Greger 2025; Zhou et al. 2025). The toxic effects of polyacetylenes are not limited to neurotoxicity and cytotoxicity; some also show phototoxic, antimicrobial (considering bacterial flora), and allelopathic activities, which may impact ecological interactions and human health (Konovalov 2014b; Xie and Wang 2022; Greger 2025). Despite their traditional medicinal use, the substantial toxicity of these polyacetylenes necessitates careful evaluation, especially for therapeutic use. Understanding the mechanisms underlying their toxic effects helps guide the design of safer analogs and informs risk assessment. The molecular mechanisms underlying the toxicity of polyacetylenes often involve interference with key cellular pathways, such as apoptosis induction or inhibition of neurotransmitter receptors (Zhang et al. 2014b; Lin et al. 2016; Tan et al. 2024; Greger 2025).

Despite their promising therapeutic potential, studies on pharmacokinetics and safe dosage parameters of polyacetylenes remain limited. Radix Bupleuri (RB), the principal component of the TCM formulation Xiaoyao Powder (XYP), consists of the dried roots obtained from Bupleurum scorzonerifolium and B. chinense, with polyacetylenes identified as key bioactive constituents. In a pharmacokinetic study conducted in rats, four polyacetylenes isolated from RB were investigated, among which bupleurynol and (2Z,8E,10E)-pentadecatriene-4,6-diyn-1-ol demonstrated rapid absorption and slow elimination. These compounds exhibited approximately linear systemic exposure over a dosage range of 22.5–90 g per kg of the crude plant material, indicating a relatively predictable pharmacokinetic behavior within this range (Liu et al. 2015). However, comprehensive data regarding the toxicity and safe dosage of polyacetylenes in humans remains insufficient. Further in-depth investigations are essential to establish definitive safety guidelines, elucidate metabolic pathways, and assess potential long-term effects (Xie and Wang 2022). Current evidence highlights the potential of polyacetylenes as key pharmacologically active constituents in herbal medicines; however, rigorous evaluation of their pharmacokinetic properties and safety profiles is imperative prior to their clinical application (Liu et al. 2015; Xie and Wang 2022).

A terminal methyl group directly attached to an acetylenic carbon, such as in polyacetylene patterns, PA1 and PA4 (Fig. 21) exhibits a markedly upfield 13C signal (δC 4.5 and 3.1, respectively), while methyl groups linked to an olefinic carbon resonates more downfield as in the polyacetylene pattern, PA6 (Fig. 21), which appeared at δC 17.4. In addition, terminal hydroxymethyl substituents occur next to a triple bond appear upfield as in patterns PA7 and PA11, which resonate approximately at δC 51.5, whereas those attached to a double bond, appear downfield as in PA13 appearing at δC 78.1. Further substitution of the terminal hydroxymethyl groups next to a triple bond result in a downfield shift of its carbon signal as in patterns PA8, PA10, and PA11, which appear at a range of δC 58.6–63.5. The introduction of a chloro substituent at the terminal acetylenic carbon, as observed in pattern PA9, causes substantial deshielding of this carbon, shifting it from δC 68.7 in pattern PA8 (represented by compound 2) to δC 102 in pattern PA9 (represented by compound 1).

The presence of a carbon signal at ~ 143–150 ppm and another at ~ 106–109 ppm together with acetylenic carbons suggests possibly a terminal alkene or conjugated double bond attached to the polyacetylene chain, as in patterns PA4-PA6, and PA13. The attachment of carbonyl groups to these double bonds generates an α,β-conjugated system, leading to shielding of the α-carbon and deshielding of the β-carbon, as obvious in patterns PA12 (δC-α 118.4 and δC-β 139.7) as compared to patterns PA4-PA6 (δC-α ~ 147–150 and δC-β ~ 106–109, respectively). Whereas the introduction of a bromo substituent at the terminal olefinic carbon, as in pattern PA14, resulted in nearly identical chemical shift values for both vinylic carbons, each resonating at approximately δC 117–119 as observed for compounds 66 and 87.

Similarly, the thiophene-linked acetylenes show distinct and reproducible patterns, enabling reliable structural identification as depicted in pattern PA15, that shows carbon resonances for the thiophene moiety at δC 121.4, 127.2, 129.0, and 134.7, together with the characteristic and consistent chemical shifts of the polyyne chain.

During the process of reviewing previously reported NMR data for polyacetylenes, a clear discrepancy was identified in the 13C-NMR assignments of 1-phenylhepta-1,3,5-triyne (3). The compound was originally isolated from Bidens pilosa and described by Alvarez et al. (1996), who provided complete 13C-NMR data for this compound. However, when these values were compared with the independently reported and unambiguously assigned 13C-NMR data published later by Kimura et al. (2008), major inconsistencies became evident. The ChemDraw-based structural analyses (Fig. 22a-b), along with reported 13C-NMR characteristics of polyacetylenes, clearly illustrate this discrepancy. In the Alvarez group’s report, the carbon resonances attributed to the polyacetylene chain ranged from δC 58.9 to 75.1 ppm, but critically, the terminal methyl group is reported at δC 78.2 ppm (Alvarez et al. 1996), which is implausible for a terminal alkyne-linked methyl, which usually appears at δC 3–5 ppm (Lai et al. 2023). By contrast, the data from Kimura et al. displayed a terminal methyl signal at δ 4.7 ppm, which is entirely consistent with the literature. The ChemDraw prediction (Fig. 22b) further supports the correctness of the Kimura data as the calculated shifts align closely with the experimentally validated values for 1-phenylhepta-1,3,5-triyne (3), especially for C-7, the terminal methyl carbon. In contrast, the Alvarez et al. values match more closely the pattern expected for 1-phenylhepta-2,4,6-triyne, a constitutional isomer of 3 (Fig. 22a). These findings suggest that the compound isolated by Alvarez et al. was most likely misassigned, and that their NMR data correspond to a different triyne isomer, not 1-phenylhepta-1,3,5-triyne as claimed. It is worth noting, however, that Kimura et al., despite providing accurate NMR values, did not assign individual chemical shifts for carbons C-2 to C-6 within the polyacetylene chain. Therefore, we used ChemDraw-predicted chemical shift values to allocate the unassigned resonances reported by Kimura et al., allowing a coherent and structure-consistent assignment of carbons C-2 to C-6, as illustrated in Fig. 22a-b.

Conclusion

Polyacetylenes are of considerable interest due to their extensive pharmacological activities, which draw the attention of chemists toward further backbone derivatization and the evaluation of these compounds as novel therapeutic agents. This review comprehensively addresses the lead polyacetylenes and their antibacterial, anti-biofilm, and anti-QS activity properties, in addition to discussing their biosynthesis, sources, SAR, toxicological and pharmacokinetic properties, and 13C-NMR data. Our derived SAR demonstrates that no single structural motif dictates polyacetylene bioactivity; rather, antibacterial performance arises from an interplay of chain length, diyne conjugation, and targeted substituents. Activity is expanded beyond previously thought hydroxyl-driven models by structural interventions such as amide, bromo, and cinnamate incorporation. Findings support the view that despite significant progress, there is still considerable work required to advance antimicrobial polyacetylenes. Notably, the limited availability of polyacetylenes from natural sources underscores the need for efficient, scalable, and stereoselective synthetic strategies. Such developments would expand access to structural analogues and facilitate deeper SAR investigations, ultimately accelerating their progression as antimicrobial candidates. Furthermore, future research must include well-designed animal studies and clinical trials to validate efficacy and ADMET characteristics, as in vitro findings alone are insufficient to predict in vivo behavior. Finally, to enable rational optimization and informed therapeutic development, it will be crucial to further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying their antibacterial, anti-biofilm, and anti-QS activities.
