Immune Dysregulation and Community Health Factors in Maternal and Childhood Leprosy: A Partial Least Squares-Structural Equation Modelling Study
Authors: Flora Ramona Sigit Prakoeswa, Anang Endaryanto, Cita Rosita Sigit Prakoeswa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeprosy remains a significant public health challenge in endemic regions, particularly among vulnerable populations. Immune dysregulation and community health factors, including environmental conditions and socio-economic status, may play a crucial role in the transmission and progression of the disease. This study aims to estimate the magnitude of the effects of immune dysregulation and community health status on the occurrence of leprosy among mothers and children in endemic areas.Materials and MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted. This study collected patient data from mothers and children with leprosy in endemic regions and non-leprosy subjects in endemic and non-endemic areas. This involved assessing immune dysregulation, focusing on T helper 1 (Th1; interferon-γ [IFN-γ]), T helper 2 (Th2; interleukin-4 [IL-4]), regulatory T cells (Treg; forkhead box P3 [FoxP3+]), and T helper 17 (Th17; interleukin-17 [IL-17]) markers. Because immune parameters were available only in a sub-sample, these variables were incorporated into the structural equation modelling as exploratory immune constructs. Community health status was evaluated through maternal nutrition, household environment, family education, economic status, household smoking habits, village endemicity, presence of Bacille Calmette–Guérin (BCG) scars in children, access to healthcare, and perinatal risk factors. The results were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and SmartPLS 4 (SmartPLS GmbH, Bönningstedt, Germany).ResultsOne hundred six subjects were included in this study. Path analysis revealed significant effects within the studied factors. The health environment had a significant negative impact on village endemicity (β=-0.294; P<0.001). Leprosy in the family was significantly influenced by health environment (β=0.146; P<0.001), family education (β=-0.289; P<0.001), family economic status (β=-0.237; P<0.001), smoking in the family (β=0.191; P<0.001), access to health facilities (β=-0.051; P<0.001), and village endemicity (β=0.305; P<0.001). Mother immunity was significantly influenced by village endemicity (β=0.138; P<0.001), leprosy in the family (β=-0.554; P<0.001), maternal nutritional status (β=0.191; P<0.001), and maternal BCG scar (β=-0.149; P<0.001), which was noteworthy but should be interpreted cautiously. Child immunity was significantly affected by village endemicity (β=-0.148; P<0.001), leprosy in the family (β=-0.280; P<0.001), mother immunity (β=-0.156; P<0.001), child BCG scar (β=-0.021; P<0.001), perinatal risk (β=0.067; P<0.001), and mother leprosy (β=0.429; P<0.001). Maternal leprosy was significantly influenced by maternal immunity (β=-0.208; P<0.001). Finally, Child leprosy was significantly affected by mother immunity (β=0.441; P<0.001), child immunity (β=-0.374; P<0.001), and mother leprosy (β=-0.165; P<0.001).ConclusionThis study highlights the intricate interplay between immune dysregulation and community health factors in the occurrence of leprosy. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to address these factors and reduce leprosy prevalence.Keywords: Cross-sectional studies, Cytokines, Immune system diseases, Leprosy, Socioeconomic factors

INTRODUCTION

Leprosy remains a significant public health burden in many developing countries, particularly affecting communities with poor health status and limited access to healthcare. While effective and affordable multidrug therapies have the potential to cure infections, failures in detection and treatment can lead to the development of stigmatising leprosy-associated grade-2 disabilities (G2Ds) [1,2]. By recent estimates, 7% of the more than 200,000 new cases of leprosy detected each year occur in individuals who have already developed G2Ds by the time of diagnosis [1,3]. The burden of leprosy is reflected not only in its direct impact on individuals but also in the social and economic challenges it creates for affected communities. Leprosy disproportionately impacts mothers and children due to their unique vulnerabilities, such as compromised immunity and poor living conditions [4]. Furthermore, understanding the factors that contribute to the transmission and progression of leprosy in these populations is crucial for formulating effective prevention and intervention strategies.

Community health status, encompassing environmental, socio-economic, and healthcare-related factors, plays a crucial role in determining the spread of infectious diseases such as leprosy [5]. Previous studies have examined the relationship between community health status, for example, air pollution, and immunity status. Air pollution is a significant health problem worldwide, especially in developing countries [6]. Environmental pollution is a significant threat to health. Biological air pollution is mainly caused by plant pollen, fungi, bacteria, and viruses [7]. Exposure to environmental pollution can alter the microRNA-145 profile, thereby affecting cytokine production, including interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2), and coagulation factor III/tissue factor (F3) [8]. Furthermore, miRNA changes were reported to influence the Th1/Th2 balance in asthma patients [9]. These conditions are often prevalent in leprosy-endemic regions, exacerbating the community's susceptibility to the disease. In particular, individuals living in areas with high levels of air pollution and poor nutrition are at greater risk of immune dysregulation [10], which may increase their vulnerability to Mycobacterium leprae infection.

Immune dysregulation is a possible key factor in the development of leprosy [11,12]. The immune system's ability to regulate its response to pathogens and maintain homeostasis determines whether an individual becomes resistant or susceptible to infection [13]. Immune dysregulation has different definitions in different diseases: autoimmunity with dominant Th1 and T helper 17 (Th17) activity, allergy with dominant Th2 activity, and neoplastic or infectious cases with low Th1 and Th17 activity. In leprosy, Th1, Th2, Treg, and Th17 cells play crucial roles in the immune response [14,15]. Furthermore, other studies have shown that to fight leprosy infection, Treg homeostasis and Th1 and Th17 protection are required [14,16]. Any imbalance in these cells, particularly due to environmental or genetic factors, can lead to immune dysregulation [17]. This dysregulation may impair the body's ability to fight off M. leprae, allowing the bacteria to proliferate and cause disease. Therefore, understanding the immunological mechanisms underlying leprosy susceptibility is essential for developing targeted interventions to prevent transmission.

The present study used partial least squares-structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM), to examine how immune dysregulation and community health factors are associated with maternal and childhood leprosy in vulnerable populations. The conceptual framework assumed that distal community and household determinants, such as the health environment, education, economic status, smoking exposure, and access to health facilities, may influence household exposure to leprosy and village endemicity. Previous studies have shown that poor socioeconomic and environmental conditions, crowding, and household exposure are important determinants of leprosy risk [18,19]. These factors may subsequently influence maternal immunity, child immunity, and leprosy outcomes. Child-specific factors, including Bacille Calmette—Guérin (BCG) scar and perinatal risk, were also considered relevant to child immunity. Thus, the SEM was specified from distal to proximal pathways, with environmental and family characteristics as upstream determinants, village endemicity and leprosy in the family as intermediate factors, and maternal immunity, child immunity, and leprosy outcomes as downstream variables. Although several individual determinants have been reported previously, the value of this study lies in examining these factors simultaneously within mother-child pairs in endemic and non-endemic settings.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study design

This study employed an observational-analytic, cross-sectional design to explore the interplay between immune dysregulation and community health status in leprosy incidence. The study was conducted in both leprosy-endemic and non-endemic areas, specifically in 10 districts of Tuban Regency, East Java, Indonesia, including Bulu, Tambakboyo, Kerek, Jetak, Soko, Jenu, Temandang, Tuban, Palang, and Sumurgung. Data collection was carried out in March 2020. Subjects included children with leprosy, their mothers, and healthy control subjects from both endemic and non-endemic areas. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

2. Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Dr. Soetomo Hospital, Surabaya, under approval number 1664/KEPK/XI/2019 (dated 19 November 2019). All participants provided informed consent before data collection.

3. Study population

The study population consisted of mother-child pairs from both endemic and non-endemic regions, specifically grouped into five distinct groups based on their health status and geographic location: Group 1, children with leprosy and healthy mothers (from endemic areas); Group 2, healthy children and mothers with leprosy (from endemic areas); Group 3, children and mothers both with leprosy (from endemic areas); Group 4, healthy children and mothers (from endemic areas), and Group 5, healthy children and mothers (from non-endemic areas). The inclusion criteria required children aged 5–18 years, and the mothers were the children's biological mothers. Leprosy diagnoses were made according to the guidelines set by the Indonesian Ministry of Health and World Health Organization recommendations. Exclusion criteria included patients with severe type 1 or 2 leprosy reactions, pregnant mothers, and those in poor general health. The sample size was calculated using the formula for comparing two proportions for the five groups, focusing on cytokine differences in interferon-γ (IFN-γ) between healthy and leprosy-affected subjects. The formula for sub-samples uses the Compare Two Means procedure. The value of μ1−μ2 is taken from the difference in IFN-γ cytokines between healthy subjects and leprosy subjects, according to a previous study from Nada et al. [20]. A total of 106 mother-child pairs were included. Sampling was performed consecutively, and data were managed through coding, editing, and database entry.

4. Data collection

Data were collected through interviews, questionnaires, environmental observations, and blood sampling. Demographic data were sourced from health records, including leprosy patient medical cards at the local health centres (Puskesmas), maternal and child health records, and family cards. This section involved collecting information about family education, economic status, smoking habit, access to healthcare facilities, and maternal nutritional status. Anthropometric measurements (height, weight, and body mass index [BMI]) of mothers were taken. In addition, the environmental assessment included evaluating conditions in the house, such as ceilings, walls, floors, lighting, temperature, humidity, room density, and ventilation. Additional factors, such as access to clean water, waste disposal, and sanitation, were also recorded. Subsequently, blood samples (5 cc) were collected from participants to assess serum albumin and cytokine levels, including IFN-γ (Th1), interleukin-4 (IL-4) (Th2), forkhead box P3-positive cells (FOXP3+) (Treg), and IL-17 (Th17), using flow cytometry with Sysmex XN-3000 and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. These immune analyses were available only for a sub-sample of 41 mother-child pairs because complete laboratory data were available only for these participants.

5. Measurement and structural modelling

The measurement and structural models comprised several latent variables and their corresponding indicators. The model structure was specified a priori based on biological plausibility and public health reasoning. Environmental and socio-economic factors were positioned as upstream determinants because they may influence household living conditions, exposure to infection, and access to preventive and curative services. This ordering is consistent with evidence linking deprivation, housing conditions, and household contact with leprosy occurrence [18,19]. Leprosy in the family and village endemicity were modelled as intermediate contextual factors, followed by maternal immunity, child immunity, and leprosy outcomes. Accordingly, the SEM was intended as an exploratory, theory-guided framework rather than a definitive causal model.

The Health Environment latent variable was measured by indicators such as house crowding, ventilation, temperature, lighting, access to clean water, sanitation, and overall house conditions. Maternal nutritional status was reflected by body weight, height, BMI, and serum albumin. Family education and family economic status included indicators such as parental education, employment, per capita income, and income adequacy. Smoking in family was measured by father and mother smoking habits, access to health facilities by mother and child access to healthcare services, and Perinatal risk by exclusive breastfeeding and antenatal care. Village endemicity, mother bacille BCG scar, child BCG scar, and leprosy in the family were included as direct constructs. mother immunity and child immunity were measured through Th1 (IFN-γ), Th2 (IL-4), Treg (FoxP3+), and Th17 (IL-17) cytokine levels, while mother leprosy and child leprosy were measured directly by diagnosis. After model refinement, several constructs were retained as single-indicator measures, including smoking in family (mother smoking), access to health facilities (mother’s access), and perinatal risk (exclusive breastfeeding), for model parsimony.

6. Hypotheses development

This study aims to estimate the magnitude of the effects of the 21 proposed hypotheses. Based on the conceptual framework and previous evidence linking environmental deprivation and household exposure with leprosy risk, the hypotheses were organised from distal to proximal pathways, beginning with environmental and socio-economic determinants, followed by household and village-level leprosy context, then maternal and child immunity, and finally mother and child leprosy [18,19].

We proposed 21 hypotheses (Fig. 1): the health environment significantly influences village endemicity (H1). Each of the health environment (H2), family's education (H3), family's economic status (H4), smoking in the family (H5), access to health facilities (H6), and village endemicity (H7) has a significant influence on the occurrence of leprosy in the family. Subsequently, each village's endemicity (H8), leprosy in the family (H9), the mother's nutritional status (H10), and the mother's BCG scar (H11) significantly influenced the mother's immunity. Additionally, each village endemicity (H12), leprosy in the family (H13), mother immunity (H14), child BCG scar (H15), perinatal risk (H16), and mother leprosy (H17) have a significant effect on child immunity. Moreover, maternal immunity has a significant impact on mother leprosy (H18). Lastly, each of the mother's immunity (H19), the child's immunity (H20), and the mother's leprosy (H21) has a significant effect on the child's leprosy.

7. Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and SmartPLS 4 (SmartPLS GmbH, Bönningstedt, Germany). Normality was first assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Because this study was exploratory, involved a relatively complex model with multiple latent variables, and used a modest sample size, PLS-SEM was selected as the main analytical approach. PLS-SEM was used to evaluate theory-guided associations among environmental, socio-economic, immunological, and leprosy-related variables rather than to establish a definitive causal model. Because immune parameters were available only in a sub-sample, the mother immunity and child immunity constructs were treated as exploratory and interpreted with caution.

The measurement model was evaluated using factor loadings, Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability, and average variance extracted (AVE). Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed using AVE, the Fornell-Larcker criterion, the Heterotrait-Monotrait ratio, and cross-loadings. Multicollinearity was examined using the variance inflation factor, with values below 3.30 indicating acceptable collinearity. To improve parsimony and reduce the risk of unstable estimates, indicators with low loadings were removed during model refinement; as a result, several constructs were retained as single-indicator measures in the final model. The structural model was evaluated using path coefficients (β) and P-values. Statistical significance of the structural paths was assessed using bootstrapping in SmartPLS 4 (SmartPLS GmbH, Bönningstedt, Germany).

RESULTS

A total of 106 pairs of mothers and children from leprosy-endemic and non-endemic areas participated in this study. Data on environmental, socio-economic, healthcare, and individual health-related variables were collected from all subjects. Cytokine, blood profile, and blood chemistry data were available for a sub-sample of 41 mother-child pairs. Among the overall study participants, 19 pairs (17.9%) were in Group 1 (children with leprosy, healthy mothers), 24 pairs (22.6%) in Group 2 (healthy children, mothers with leprosy), 3 pairs (2.8%) in Group 3 (both children and mothers with leprosy), 33 pairs (31.1%) in Group 4 (healthy children and mothers from endemic areas), and 27 pairs (25.5%) in Group 5 (healthy children and mothers from non-endemic regions). In the sub-sample of 41 pairs, 10 pairs (24.4%) belonged to Group 1, 10 pairs (24.4%) to Group 2, 2 pairs (4.9%) to Group 3, 11 pairs (26.8%) to Group 4, and 8 pairs (19.5%) to Group 5.

1. Measurement model

The measurement model in this study includes several latent variables, each measured by specific indicator variables, with some variables removed due to lower significance, as indicated in Figure 2. The Health Environment latent variable was significantly represented by house crowding (factor loading = 0.791), ventilation (0.757), temperature (0.781), house light (0.734), the intensity of house light (0.775), house floor (0.754), house ceiling (0.751), and the overall house health criterion (0.837). In contrast, indicators such as house walls and windows were removed due to lower loadings. The nutrition status of the mother was measured using body weight (0.856), height (0.762), and BMI (0.888), with the albumin serum level removed. The family's education construct included the father's education (0.621), the mother's education (0.613), and the child's education (0.907). At the same time, the family's economic status was captured by the father's job (0.991), family income (0.964), and income per capita (0.996), with the mother's job removed.

The smoker in family latent variable was represented solely by the mother smoking (1.000), with the father smoking removed. Access to health facilities was measured only through the mother's access (1.000), and perinatal risk was represented by exclusive breastfeeding (1.000). Additionally, some variables were directly measured without latent indicators, such as village endemicity (1.000), mother BCG scar (1.000), child BCG scar (1.000), and leprosy in the family (1.000). In addition, the mother immunity and child immunity latent constructs were measured using Th1 (IFN-γ) (0.873 for mothers, 0.851 for children), Th2 (IL-4) (0.913 for mothers, removed for children), Treg (FoxP3+) (removed for both), and Th17 (IL-17) (0.657 for mothers, 0.634 for children). The final constructs for mother leprosy (1.000) and child leprosy (1.000) were directly measured by the diagnosis of leprosy in the respective individuals. 2. Structural model

The structural model revealed significant effects within the studied variables. The nutrition status of mothers improved mother immunity (β=0.191, P<0.001), while the presence of BCG scars in mothers had a decreasing impact on mother immunity (β=-0.149, P<0.001). The health environment positively contributed to the occurrence of leprosy in the family (β=0.146, P<0.001) and negatively influenced village endemicity (β=-0.294, P<0.001). Family education (β=-0.289, P<0.001) and economic status (β=-0.237, P<0.001) were associated with a decrease in leprosy within the family. Smoking in the family had a positive effect on leprosy in the family (β=0.191, P<0.001), while access to health facilities had an adverse impact (β=-0.051, P<0.001). Perinatal risk had a positive effect on child immunity (β=0.067, P<0.001). Village endemicity positively influenced leprosy in the family (β=0.305, P<0.001) but negatively affected child immunity (β=-0.148, P<0.001) and positively influenced mother immunity (β=0.138, P<0.001). Child BCG scar was associated with decreased child immunity (β=-0.021, P<0.001). Leprosy in the family had a substantial negative impact on both the mother's (β=-0.554, P<0.001) and the child's (β=-0.280, P<0.001) immunity. Mother immunity negatively influenced mother leprosy (β=-0.208, P<0.001) but positively influenced child leprosy (β=0.441, P<0.001). Child immunity had a decreasing effect on child leprosy (β=-0.374, P<0.001), and mother leprosy negatively influenced child leprosy (β=-0.165, P<0.001). DISCUSSION

This study provides exploratory insight into the interplay between immune dysregulation, community health status, and maternal and childhood leprosy in endemic and non-endemic areas. Several significant associations were identified, highlighting the roles of nutritional status, environmental factors, family characteristics, and healthcare access. However, because of the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Although many of the individual factors identified are already recognised in the leprosy literature, the contribution of this study lies in integrating them into a single analytical framework linking community conditions, maternal immunity, child immunity, and leprosy outcomes. Thus, the SEM should be viewed as a theory-guided, hypothesis-generating model rather than a final causal representation of leprosy occurrence. Subsequently, an important limitation in interpreting the immune findings is that immune dysregulation and leprosy status were measured at the same point in time. Therefore, it cannot be determined whether immune dysregulation preceded leprosy, contributed to disease progression, or developed as a consequence of the disease itself. The T-cell-related markers assessed in this study should therefore be interpreted as immunological associations rather than direct evidence of mechanisms for treatment development.

The positive association between maternal nutritional status and immunity underscores the critical role that proper nutrition plays in maintaining a healthy immune system. Adequate nutrition provides the necessary micronutrients and energy required for the optimal functioning of immune cells [21,22], which is essential for both preventing and controlling infections such as leprosy. Malnutrition may weaken the immune system [23,24], reducing the body's ability to mount an effective defence against infections like M. leprae. Additionally, malnutrition may alter gut microbiota, which plays a significant role in regulating immune responses, further contributing to immune dysfunction [25,26].

A noteworthy and somewhat counterintuitive finding was the negative association between maternal BCG scar and maternal immunity. Although BCG vaccination is generally considered protective against mycobacterial diseases, its relationship with leprosy may be influenced by age at vaccination, genetic predisposition, and environmental exposure [27,28]. Because BCG scar status is an imperfect proxy for vaccination history, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. A previous study from the same endemic area in Tuban reported that BCG status was associated with leprosy only in certain group comparisons and suggested that this relationship may be influenced by waning protection and co-existing factors [29]. Previous studies also show that, in general, a single dose of BCG provides higher protection against leprosy in young individuals. The BCG protection wanes over time but can last for 10–30 years [30]. As individuals age, the efficacy of BCG wanes, which may explain why the vaccination was less protective for mothers in this study. Therefore, this negative association calls for further investigation into the long-term effects of BCG vaccination on leprosy immunity, particularly in high-risk populations. Subsequently, continuous monitoring and possibly booster doses of BCG could be considered as strategies to maintain immunity in older populations.

The health environment emerged as a critical factor influencing leprosy in families and village endemicity [31]. Poor living conditions, inadequate housing, and lack of sanitation create an environment conducive to the spread of infectious diseases, including leprosy [4]. This finding aligns with the global public health literature, which highlights the role of environmental factors in the transmission of infectious diseases [32]. The negative impact of the health environment on village endemicity suggests that improving environmental conditions could significantly reduce leprosy prevalence in endemic areas. In addition, another study showed that environmental factors, such as a 7-fold increase in the risk of new leprosy cases, as is often the case in leprosy-endemic areas, are associated with burning or burying waste rather than with access to integrated waste collection and disposal facilities [33]. It emphasises the role of environmental sanitation in controlling the spread of the disease. Other environmental exposure also plays a significant role. Frota et al found that environmental contamination, particularly through water sources, could facilitate the spread of M. leprae [34]. This prolonged exposure likely contributes to the heightened immune dysregulation seen in endemic regions, where immune responses are often skewed towards ineffective Th2-mediated immunity rather than the more protective Th1 response.

Family education and economic status were both associated with a decrease in leprosy occurrence within families. Higher levels of education are often associated with better hygiene practices, greater healthcare access, and greater disease awareness, which collectively reduce the risk of leprosy transmission. Similarly, improved economic status is associated with better access to healthcare, improved living conditions, and better nutrition, all of which contribute to stronger immune defences and lower susceptibility to infection. On the other hand, families with lower socio-economic status were more likely to experience leprosy, as poverty is often linked to poor access to healthcare, inadequate nutrition, and exposure to unhygienic living conditions. Studies by Kerr-Pontes et al and Pescarini et al have consistently shown that poverty and low education are risk factors for leprosy, as they limit individuals' ability to access timely medical care and adopt preventive health behaviours [18,19]. In addition, smoking in the family was found to have a positive effect on leprosy occurrence, which may be attributed to the immunosuppressive effects of smoking. Previous research has shown that smoking weakens immune responses and increases susceptibility to infections [35,36], including mycobacterial diseases like leprosy. The finding that access to health facilities had a decreasing impact on leprosy occurrence highlights the importance of timely diagnosis and treatment, emphasising the need for improved healthcare access in endemic areas to control the spread of leprosy.

The study also revealed several critical insights into immune regulation in leprosy. The perinatal risk associated with exclusive breastfeeding was positively associated with child immunity, suggesting that early-life factors, such as maternal health and perinatal care, play an essential role in shaping children's immune systems. Previous studies show that exclusive breastfeeding may support the immune system of infants by providing immunological factors like secretory IgA, bioactive molecules for immune maturation, dynamic composition with immunoglobulin A, higher leukocyte values for enhanced immune response, and immunomodulatory agents such as cytokines, lactoferrin, and lysozyme, which protect against pathogens and aid in immune development [37,38]. However, village endemicity negatively influenced child immunity, likely reflecting the cumulative burden of environmental and socio-economic factors that impair immune development in endemic areas. Subsequently, leprosy in the family had a substantial negative impact on both maternal and child immunity, indicating that the presence of leprosy within the household affects the immune systems of other family members, possibly due to prolonged exposure to M. leprae or shared environmental and genetic factors. This finding reinforces the need for household-based interventions in leprosy control programs.

Maternal immunity was negatively associated with maternal leprosy, suggesting that stronger maternal immune responses may protect against the disease. This immune imbalance aligns with a previous study by Ivanova and Orekhov, which noted that a strong Treg and Th1 response is critical for maintaining immune homeostasis and protection [39]. In leprosy patients, particularly those with a more severe form of the disease, the failure of Treg to regulate immune responses can lead to unchecked inflammation, exacerbating the disease. Therefore, the immune profile observed in this study among leprosy patients highlights the importance of maintaining a balance between immune cell types to prevent disease progression. However, maternal immunity positively influenced child leprosy, suggesting that maternal immune dysregulation may contribute to increased child susceptibility. Child immunity, on the other hand, conferred protection against child leprosy, highlighting the importance of robust immune responses in preventing the onset of the disease. Last, the negative impact of maternal leprosy on child leprosy suggests that children of mothers with leprosy are at higher risk of contracting the disease, possibly due to close contact and shared environmental conditions. This underscores the need for targeted interventions that address both maternal and child health in leprosy-endemic areas.

The findings of this study emphasise the critical need for comprehensive public health interventions that address not only individual-level factors, such as immune dysregulation, but also broader community-level determinants, including nutrition, education, and environmental conditions. A prior Indonesian study has shown that weak family support may adversely affect psychosocial well-being in people with leprosy, suggesting that leprosy control should also consider family and social support dimensions [40]. Improving maternal nutrition and living environments in leprosy-endemic areas could strengthen immune responses and reduce the transmission of M. leprae. Public health programs must integrate nutritional support, housing improvements, and sanitation measures to reduce exposure to environmental factors that exacerbate immune dysregulation and increase vulnerability to leprosy. Additionally, educating families on hygiene practices, smoking cessation, and disease prevention is essential to reducing both individual and household-level risks. Furthermore, equally important is enhancing healthcare access in endemic areas to ensure timely diagnosis and treatment of leprosy. Household-based leprosy control strategies, particularly those aimed at protecting children and mothers from exposure, could significantly curb the familial spread of the disease. By improving access to healthcare services, including vaccination and early detection programs, public health initiatives can prevent the development of severe complications, such as disabilities and further transmission within families and communities. At the same time, these community health factors are not specific to leprosy and may influence many other health outcomes. Therefore, reducing leprosy prevalence will require broader and sustained social, environmental, and health-system efforts rather than disease-specific measures alone. In this respect, the present findings may be most useful for understanding the public health context of leprosy in resource-limited endemic settings, rather than for providing direct clinical or mechanistic guidance. These comprehensive interventions, targeting both individual immunity and the social determinants of health, are crucial to effectively reducing the burden of leprosy in vulnerable populations.

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish temporal order or causality, particularly for the immune findings, where it remains unclear whether immune dysregulation is a cause or consequence of leprosy. Reliance on self-reported data may also have introduced bias. In addition, BCG scar status may have misclassified vaccination history because a visible scar does not always develop after vaccination. The SEM included multiple constructs and pathways in a relatively modest sample, which may have affected model stability and the precision of some estimates; this issue is especially relevant for Mother Immunity and Child Immunity, because immune parameters were measured only in a sub-sample. Although PLS-SEM was appropriate for this exploratory analysis, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger longitudinal studies. Moreover, because several identified social and environmental determinants are already well recognised, the originality of this study lies mainly in the integrated modelling of these factors in mothers and children rather than in identifying new individual risk factors.

In conclusion, this study highlights the interplay between immune dysregulation and community health status in maternal and childhood leprosy in endemic regions. Maternal nutrition, living conditions, and healthcare access were associated with immunity and susceptibility to M. leprae infection. These findings support a multifaceted approach to leprosy prevention that combines attention to immune function with improvements in community health. Although the identified factors are not entirely novel, the study adds value by demonstrating their interrelationship within a single mother-child framework. Because the SEM was exploratory, the findings should be confirmed in larger and longitudinal studies.
