CCL chemokines as prognostic and immunotherapeutic markers in prostate cancer: a comprehensive analysis
Authors: Yao Bai, Chaojin Liang, Jie Gu, LuoWu Wang, Ke Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to delineate the prognostic significance of CCL family expression in Prostate Cancer (PCa) and explore their immunomodulatory roles to assess therapeutic potential.MethodsWe identified CCL family expression profile utilizing ONCOMINE, and their relationship with the pathological stage and Gleason Score of PCa patients. The genetic alterations and protein-protein interactions were investigated in PCa. Subsequently, Transcriptional regulatory networks were established using TRRUST database. The immunocyte infiltration expression profile was investigated based on the CCL expression. Go and KEGG analysis was employed to further explore the biological functions and pathways. Finally, we verified the CCL gene expression using in vitro PCR experiments.ResultsThe findings revealed a notable elevation in the expression levels of CCL11/14/16/18/19 in PCa, whereas the expression of CCL2/7/8/17/23/25 were significantly decreased in PCa compared to the expression levels in normal tissues.High expression of both CCL3 and CCL4 was significantly associated with shorter disease-free survival.CCL8/22/24 and CCL3/11/17/18/19/22 was associated with high pathological N stage and high Gleason Score, respectively. High expression of CCL11/17/18/22/24 and CCL3/11/17/18/19/22 was associated with high pathological N stage and high Gleason Score, respectively.ConclusionsThe findings may provide insights and evidence on the potential application of CCL chemokines as immunotherapeutic treatment targets and prognostic biomarkers for PCa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04265-y.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most prevalent and deadly cancers globally, which primarily affects older men. With approximately 1.1 million patients diagnosed annually, PCa is the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death in men worldwide [1]. Chronic inflammation and inflammatory factors, such as C-C motif chemokine ligand (CCL), play a vital role in the induction and progression of PCa [2, 3]. Although previous studies have reported effective diagnostic biomarkers and efficient therapy, immune prognostic and immunotherapeutic biomarkers remain poorly characterized.

Chemokines, a family of small cytokines, are known for their chemotactic properties, directing the migration of immune cells in multiple tumors [4]. They participate in both anti-and pro-tumorigenic immune responses [5]. The CCL family, in particular, has been implicated in various biological and pathological processes of cancers, including chemotaxis, inflammation, cellular migration, and anti-tumor immunological responses to tumor-associated antigens [6, 7]. Given the significant role of CCL chemokines in cancer biology, we have chosen to focus on specific members of the CCL family that have been previously associated with PCa progression and immune responses.

Recent studies have reported that CCL chemokines are implicated in tumorigenesis, proliferation, and metastasis in various cancers [8, 9]. While prior fragmented studies on individual CCLs exist, a comprehensive analysis integrating prognostic evaluation with immune microenvironment characterization for the entire CCL family in PCa is lacking. This study therefore aimed to conduct a systematic profiling of CCL expression, genetic alterations, transcriptional regulation, and immune infiltration associations, seeking to identify prognostic biomarkers and elucidate the immunomodulatory functions of CCLs in PCa [10].

Materials and methods

Expression profile of the CCL family in PCa

The expression profile of the CCL family was explored in ONCOMINE 4.5 database and validated in UALCAN. ONCOMINE (www.oncomine.org) is an online data mining database, comprising various gene expression datasets, thus it is used for genome-wide expression analyses [11]. Threshold parameter settings were p < 0.05, a fold change of 1.5, and a gene rank of top 10%. Students’ t-test was employed to compare the expression levels of CCL chemokines between PCa group and normal group. p < 0.05 represented a statistically significant difference. UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html) is a comprehensive, interactive web resource for the analysis of cancer OMICS data and miRNA gene expression analysis using TCGA datasets [12].In this study, the “Visualize heatmapmodule was used to analyze CCL family in PCa. Student’s T-test was employed to generate a p-value. The p-value cutoff was 0.05.

Correlation between the CCL chemokines expression and the pathological stage and prognostic value of PCa

Disease-free survival (DFS) analysis of single CCL chemokines in PCa and normal tissue was performed using the GEPIA2 database (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index) [13]. Based on the “PARD” dataset, we used the “survival analysis” module to analyze DFS. Following the methods: Disease-Free Survival, Group cutoff: Median, P-value: 0.05. Student’s t-test was used to compare the survival rate between PCa and normal groups, p < 0.05 denoted a statistically significant difference. The relationship between the CCL chemokines expression and the pathological stage (N) and Gleason Score of PCa was performed in UALCAN. “TCGA Gene” module was used for the subsequent analysis.

Genetic alterations

cBioPortal (www.cbioportal.org), an exploratory analysis tool for exploring large-scale cancer genomic data sets that hosts data from large consortium efforts, like TCGA and TARGET [14]. Based on TCGA database, genetic alterations, co-expression, and the network module of CCL chemokines were obtained from cBioPortal. Genetic alterations 494 of PCa samples (TCGA) were analyzed utilizing this tool. mRNA expression z scores (RNA Seq V2 RSEM) were received using a z score threshold of ± 2.0. A z score threshold of ± 2.0 was used for protein expression.

Protein-protein interaction and Gene-gene interaction

GeneMANIA (http://www.genemania.org) was used to explore genes that are related to particular gene (s) of interest using correlation data. For example, protein and genetic interactions, pathways interactions, co-expression, co-localization, and protein domain similarity [15]. CCL chemokines protein-protein interaction analysis was performed using GeneMANIA software. In this study, STRING database (https://string-db.org/) was also employed to perform protein-protein interactions to elucidate the interactions among different CCL chemokines expression levels, CCL chemokines, and 25 over-expressed and 25 under-expressed genes in PCa [16].

Transcriptional regulatory networks

TRRUST (https://www.grnpedia.org/trrust/) is a database used to explore the transcriptional regulatory networks of humans and mice [17], which contains 8444 transcription factor (TF)-target regulatory relationships of 800 human TFs. TRRUST was used to explore key regulated factors of differentially expressed CCL chemokines in PCa. The Fisher’s exact test and the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure were used during the analysis.

Correlation between CCL chemokines expression and immune infiltration

TIMER (http://timer.cistrome.org/) web server enables systematical analysis of immune infiltrates (B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells) across diverse cancer types [18]. Herein, we used the “gene module” to visualize the correlation between CCL chemokines expression and immune infiltration level in PCa. The module “Survival” was employed to explore the clinical relevance of CCL chemokines and immune subsets in PCa.

Biological function prediction

The gene ontology (GO) and Genes and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genomes (KEGG) analyses were employed to explore the biological function of differential expression CCL family in PCa. We utilized the R package “clusterProfiler” and “org.Hs.eg.db” to perform GO enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway analysis [19]. Results with p.adj < 0.05 & qvalue < 0.2 were considered as significant. Benjamini-Hochberg was employed for the adjusted p-value to get the optimal False Discovery Rate (FDR). R package “ggplot 2” was employed for the approach of the database for annotation, visualization, and integrated discovery.

Cell culture

The human prostate cancer cell lines PC3 and LNCap were cultured in RPIM 1640 medium (HyClone, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37℃ with 5% CO2. Cells were digested and passed at a ratio of 1:6 when reaching 90% confluence. Logarithmic growth phase cells were applied for subsequent experiments.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

We extracted total RNA from the human benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and PCa (PC3 and LNCaP) cells employing TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). 1 µg of total RNA for the synthesis of single-stranded cDNA was prepared with the help of the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Dalian, China). Then, we applied reverse transcription quantitative PCR to analyze the expression levels of lncRNA of hub genes using a 7,500 PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cycling conditions were as follows: denaturation at 95 ℃ for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95 ℃ for 20 s and extension at 60 ℃ for 20s. The GAPDH gene was used as internal control. The primer sequences are shown in Table S1.

Validation of protein expressions of hub genes by the HPA database

The Human Protein Atlas database (HPA) (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) [20] is dedicated to providing pathological and gene expression information on a variety of human proteins. The protein expression of CCL genes in PCa was determined using immunohistochemistry (IHC) from the HPA “tissue” and “pathology” modules.

Statistical analysis

Unpaired Student’s T-test was employed to compare normally distributed data between the two groups. The Wilcoxon test was applied to compare non-normally distributed data. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare mean values among multiple groups, while Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for nonparametric data. The R package “ggplot2” was mainly used to visualize data. Benjamini–Hochberg was used to convert the P values to FDRs for identifying differential expressed genes. The Kaplan–Meier was employed to generate and visualize survival curves for the subclasses. Log-rank test was utilized to determine the statistical significance. All the tests were two-sided and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.For high-throughput analyses (e.g.differential expression, GO/KEGG enrichment), the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR). A corrected p-value (q-value) of < 0.05 was considered significant unless otherwise specified.

Clinic prognostic value of CCL chemokines in PCa patients

To further elucidate clinical outcomes associated with different expression levels CCL chemokines in PCa, we generated DFS curves using GEPIA. Univariate survival analysis using GEPIA showed that PCa patients with high levels of CCL3 (p = 0…6.036, HR = 1.6) and CCL4 (p = 0.032, HR = 1.6 ) were associated with shorter DFS (Fig. 2A and B). We then assessed the association between the expression of differentially expressed CCL chemokines and the pathological stage (N) of PCa patients. The results revealed a significant correlation between the expression of CCL11/14/17/18/21/22/24 and pathological N stage (Fig. 3). Moreover, the higher expression of CCL3/11/17/18/19/22 was related to high Gleason Score (6–10) (Fig. 4). Upregulation of CCL3 and CCL4 was significantly associated with a shorter disease-free survival (DFS) in PCa patients. This finding indicates that these chemokines may serve as predictive markers for disease recurrence.

Genetic expression alteration, neighbor gene network, and interaction network analyses with CCL chemokines in PCa patients

We performed a comprehensive evaluation of the molecular characteristics of CCL family. The genetic alterations of CCL family were exhibited in Fig. 5A. CCL 7/8/11/15/17/22 was the top six detected CCL genetic alteration in 484 PCa samples. The mutational landscape of the CCL family was presented in Fig. 5B. Genetic alterations were detected in 7% (34/489) patients and the amplification was the most frequent mutation among CCL family (Fig. 5B). To further explore the relationship between CCL chemokines and alterative genes in PCa, 25 over-expressed genes in PCa (PCA3, AMACR, DLX1, LOC1001380872, ACSM1, TFF3, HPN, OR51E2, FABP5, SIM2, C2orf72, COMP, LOCC145837, SPON2, SFTPA2, COL9A2, APOC1, TARP, ISG15, CGREF1, SDK1, C15orf48, PRR16, VSTM2L and FEV) and 25 under-expressed genes (ACTC1, ANO1, SCGB1A1, GPX2, CLU, QPRT, C8orf84, AOX1, PENK, PCP4L1, CYP4B1, GSTM3, GOS2, S100A14, GSTM2, APOBEC3C, GATA3, DUOX1, JPH4, ANGPT1, CCK, MYLK, GSTP1, SLC14A1 and UNC5B) were retrieved from STRING database (Fig. 5C and D). A PPI network of CCL chemokines was constructed using STRING database to explore the potential interactions (Fig. 5E), analysis of the PPI network showed 17 nodes and their interactions. Differentially expressed CCL chemokines were associated with CXC chemokines, chemokine receptor binding, and chemokine activity (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, a PPI network of the top 25 over-expressed genes, 25 under-expressed genes, and 9 transcription factors in PCa and CCL chemokines were constructed using cBioPortal. Analysis of the PPI network showed that alterative genes (PENK, DUOX1, ISG15, ANGPT1, TFF3, GSTP1 and SGB1A1) were significantly correlated with CCL chemokines with modulatory functions in PCa (Fig. 5G). We next investigate the correlation among the differentially expressed CCL chemokines. High correlations were observed between CCL2 and 7/8; CCL14 and 16/23; CCL16 and 23; CCL17 and 19 (Figure S3).

Discussion

This study explored the role of CCL chemokines in PCa tumorigenesis and prognosis. CCL3/4 expression levels were negatively correlated with DFS of PCa patients (Fig. 2). The expression of CCL11/17/18/22/24 was associated with the metastasis of pathological N stage (Fig. 3). Furthermore, higher expression of CCL3/11/17/18/19/22 was related to high Gleason Score (6–10) (Fig. 4). CCL chemokines were significantly correlated with infiltration levels of various immune cells, including B cells, CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells (Figs. 6 and 7). Taken together, our findings suggest that certain CCL chemokines may serve as potential prognostic and immunotherapeutic biomarkers in PCa.

Our findings position CCL chemokines as central orchestrators of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in prostate cancer. Mechanistically, the association of CCL3 and CCL4 with poor disease-free survival may be linked to their role in recruiting immunosuppressive myeloid cells, akin to the established CCL5–CCR5 axis in metastatic castration-resistant PCa (mCRPC) [21, 22]. Recent studies suggest that such chemokine networks can establish feed-forward loops that sustain T cell dysfunction and macrophage M2 polarization, thereby facilitating immune evasion [23]. The transcriptional coupling of CCLs with NF-κB and STAT3 further supports their role in coordinating malignant progression and therapy resistance.

Translationally, this dual role as prognostic biomarkers and druggable targets underscores their clinical potential. Our data supports the development of multi-CCL gene signatures for risk stratification, complementing existing clinical parameters.

Moreover, several CCR antagonists (e.g., targeting CCR2, CCR4, CCR5) are in preclinical or early clinical development [24, 25]. In prostate cancer, combining CCR inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade or androgen receptor-targeted therapy may disrupt immunosuppressive niches and restore anti-tumor immunity [26]. Additionally, measuring circulating CCL levels could serve as a minimally invasive liquid biopsy for monitoring disease. Prospective validation of CCL-based biomarkers in well-annotated cohorts receiving modern therapies is therefore an essential next step toward clinical implementation.

Our comprehensive analysis delineates the distinct expression profiles, prognostic significance, and immunomodulatory roles of specific CCL chemokines in prostate cancer (PCa). While confirming their involvement in PCa biology, our findings assume greater significance when viewed within the rapidly evolving framework of PCa immunology and the search for novel therapeutic targets. The association of CCL3 and CCL4 with poorer disease-free survival extends beyond simple correlation. These chemokines are potent recruiters of immunosuppressive myeloid cells. Their upregulation may establish a feed-forward loop in the tumor microenvironment (TME) similar to the well-characterized CCL5–CCR5 axis in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), which fosters resistance by recruiting regulatory cells and suppressing cytotoxic immunity [21, 22]. This model provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for the poor DFS associated with high CCL3/4 expression and connects our findings to active research on overcoming immunotherapy resistance in PCa.

Furthermore, the correlation of several CCLs (e.g., CCL2, CCL8, CCL11) with macrophage and dendritic cell infiltration highlights their role in shaping the immune landscape. The CCL2-CCR2 axis is a canonical pathway for recruiting tumor-promoting (M2-like) macrophages [27]. Our data suggests that a broader CCL network may coordinately regulate myeloid influx in PCa. This is clinically pertinent, as targeting CCR2 or tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) is being explored in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors [28]. The transcriptional link we identified between CCLs and key hubs like NF-κB and STAT3—central regulators of chemokine production, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune evasion—strengthens the argument for their functional role in disease progression [29, 30].

The translational potential of these axes’ hinges on their druggability. While chemokines themselves are challenging targets, their receptors (e.g., CCR2, CCR4, CCR5) are G-protein-coupled receptors amenable to pharmacological inhibition. Small-molecule antagonists or antibodies against these receptors are in development for oncology [24, 25]. Our study provides a bioinformatic rationale for investigating such agents in PCa subsets characterized by high specific CCL/CCR axis activity, potentially in combination with androgen receptor-directed therapy or immunotherapy to remodel the TME.

The differentially expressed CCL chemokines were identified and their relationship with DFS in PCa was explored. Our study showed that 5 CCL chemokines (CCL11/14/16/18/19) are highly expressed in PCa, whereas 6 CCL chemokines (CCL2/7/8/17/23/25) are downregulated in PCa compared with the levels in normal prostate tissue (Fig. 1B). This suggests that these chemokines may play a crucial role in the tumorigenesis and progression of PCa. However, it is important to note that our study has certain limitations. For instance, we compared PCa cells with BPH cells instead of normal human prostate epithelial cells. Future in vivo and in vitro experiments are necessary to comprehensively validate our findings. Previous studies reported that CCL2 is highly expressed in PCa cells [28], the discrepancy regarding CCL2 expression between our study and previous reports may be attributed to differences in sample types, experimental platforms, or tumor heterogeneity. Notably, our results showed that CCL3 and CCL4 expression levels were negatively correlated with DFS of PCa patients. To our knowledge, the relationship between CCL3/4 expression levels and DFS in PCa patients has not been reported previously. Similarly, previous studies demonstrated that CCL3 and CCL4 expressions are implicated in proliferation, invasion, and migration of prostate cells and are associated with a high risk for biochemical recurrence of PCa [31].

Further, the correlation between CCL chemokines and transcription factor targets in PCa was explored. Notably, 9 key transcription factors (RELA, NFKB1, IRF3, REL, STAT6, SPI1, STAT1, STAT3, and JUN) were highly associated with CCL chemokines in PCa. Activated RELA and NFKB1 can regulate immune responses and promote the progression of PCa [32]. A previous study showed that IRF3 plays a key role in apoptosis of PCa cells and inhibits prostate tumor growth [33]. In addition, RELA, NFKB1, and IRF3, REL are all crucial targets that are associated with the recurrence of PCa. Furthermore, STAT family genes are implicated in PCa progression and metastasis by inducing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition [34]. Our study presents evidence of a potential correlation between JUN and CCL chemokines in PCa tumorigenesis for the first time. In summary, findings from the present study provide insights into the role of CCL chemokines and transcription factors that may collaboratively contribute to driving PCa progression.

Genetic alterations and transcription factors have a complex and multifaceted impact on tumorigenesis, metastasis and recurrence of PCa. We employed the PPI network to screen the genes (top 25 over-expressed and 25 under-expressed) and transcription factors most closely associated with CCL chemokines in PCa. Notably, 7 alterative genes (PENK, DUOX1, ISG15, ANGPT1, TFF3, GSTP1, SGB1A1) showed a direct connection with CCL chemokines and 9 transcription factors in PCa. Consistent with our findings, previous studies report that all these alterative genes are involved in tumorigenesis or recurrence of PCa [35–37]. However, limited studies report the association of these alterative genes with CCL chemokines in PCa. The present study validates previous findings and provides potential value that CCL chemokines may be utilized as treatment targets and prognostic biomarkers for PCa.

Recent studies report that immune cells are implicated in the progression of PCa, and immunotherapy is an effective treatment method [38]. Our study showed that 6 types of immune cells (B cells, CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells) were significantly associated with CCL chemokines in PCa. Chemokines are small, secreted cytokines and exhibit chemotactic properties, directing the migration of immune cells in multiple tumors. They participate in both anti-and pro-tumorigenic immune responses. Interestingly, in the tumor microenvironment, the interaction between cancer cells, immune cells, and stromal cells plays essential roles in neoplasia. Moreover, all these cells can secrete chemokines, which contribute to their modulation in tumors. Studies revealed that chemokines and their corresponding receptors are key regulators in recruiting immune cells and driving the inflammatory response to a specific signature [39]. These findings confirm the value of CCL chemokines as a prognostic biomarker and provide extensive evidence of the changes within the immune microenvironment in PCa.

Limitations and future directions

While our integrative bioinformatics approach provides a comprehensive overview of CCL chemokines in PCa, several limitations should be acknowledged.While our study provides valuable insights into the role of CCL chemokines in PCa, it is not without limitations. This study has certain limitations. First, the prognostic value of CCL3 and CCL4 is derived from univariate analyses and require validation via multivariate models in prospective cohorts. Second, the use of BPH cells as controls, rather than normal prostate epithelial cells, may introduce bias in expression comparisons. While suggestive, whether they remain independent of established clinical parameters—such as Gleason score and pathological stage—requires validation by multivariate Cox regression in prospective cohorts. The use of PCa cell lines and BPH cells as controls, instead of primary human normal prostate epithelial cells, may introduce certain biases. Future in vivo and in vitro experiments are necessary to comprehensively validate our findings. Additionally, the exploration of the mechanisms by which CCL chemokines influence PCa progression and immune response warrants further investigation. (2) High-Resolution Immune Mapping: Utilizing single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics to precisely define the immune cell subsets recruited by specific CCLs within the PCa TME. (3) Functional and Preclinical Testing: Conducting in vitro and in vivo functional studies to establish causality, such as examining the effects of CCL overexpression/knockdown on immune cell recruitment and tumor growth. Preclinically testing small-molecule antagonists or antibodies targeting relevant CCRs (e.g., CCR2, CCR5) alone or in combination with immune checkpoint blockade or androgen receptor-targeted therapies is crucial to assess therapeutic potential. Conclusions

In conclusion, our study highlights the potential of CCL chemokines as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in PCa. The significant associations between CCL chemokine expression and clinical outcomes, along with their immunomodulatory effects, suggest that these molecules are embedded within established NF-κB/STAT3 pathways and myeloid recruitment circuits, and could play a pivotal role in the development of novel immunotherapeutic strategies for PCa.Future studies should validate these findings in prospective clinical cohorts and explore the therapeutic potential of targeting specific CCL/CCR axes in combination with existing immunotherapies.
