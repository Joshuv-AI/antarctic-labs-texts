Yellow fever in Latin America and the escalating risks in a changing eco-epidemiological landscape: a review
Authors: Esteban Ortiz-Prado, Jose G Prieto-Marin, Juan S Izquierdo-Condoy, Jorge Vasconez-Gonzalez, Wilder Andrés Villamil-Parra, Ginés Viscor, Óscar A Niño-Méndez, Jorge Enrique Correa-Bautista, Marta Rusiñol, Doménica Cevallos-Robalino, Juan-Carlos Navarro, Jorge A Villalobos-Madriz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryYellow fever (YF) remains a major public health concern in Latin America. We characterise the early-2025 outbreak, compare it with prior waves—especially Southeast Brazil (2016–2018)—and examine environmental, structural, and policy-related drivers. We reviewed regional surveillance data and mortality records from January to April 2025, comparing them to historical outbreak data. Case fatality rate (CFR), geographic spread, and population vulnerability patterns were analyzed. We also reviewed published literature and health alerts to contextualize contributing factors. In 2025, 301 confirmed cases and 124 deaths were reported (CFR 41.1%), mostly in Bolivia, Brazil, Colombia, and Peru, concentrated among unvaccinated populations at the forest–urban interface. Transmission remains sylvatic, but peri-urban spillover risk is rising. Deforestation, climate variability, cross-border migration, and political instability heighten vulnerability; surveillance is fragmented and vaccine uptake insufficient in high-risk groups. YF is re-emerging through intertwined biological, ecological, and socio-political forces. Preventing another large-scale epidemic requires strengthened, integrated surveillance, including Non-Human Primates (NHP) epizootics, equity-centred immunisation in remote areas, and coordinated regional action focused on prevention, early detection, and health-system resilience.Keywords: Yellow fever, Latin America, Outbreak, Vaccination, Surveillance

Background

Yellow fever (YF) is an acute viral hemorrhagic disease transmitted by infected mosquitoes, endemic to tropical regions of Africa and South America.1 Despite the availability of a highly effective vaccine, periodic outbreaks continue to occur in Latin America, often fueled by ecological disruption, low immunization coverage, and sylvatic transmission cycles involving non-human primates (NHP).

Search strategy and selection criteria

Although a systematic search was not conducted, references for this Review were identified through targeted searches of official data from the World Health Organization (WHO) and Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) databases, as well as PubMed and Scielo using search terms such as “yellow fever”, “fiebre amarilla”, “febre amarela”, “outbreak”, “epidemics”, “surveillance”, “vaccination”, “disease burden”, “transmission”, “ecological risks”, “Latin America”, “South America”, “eco-epidemiological”, and “non-human primates” from January 1960 until December 2025. Articles were also identified through searches of the authors’ own files, reference lists of relevant publications, and health organization reports (e.g., PAHO epidemiological updates). Papers published in English, Spanish, and Portuguese were reviewed, with a focus on peer-reviewed studies, surveillance reports, and policy documents, particularly those addressing epidemiological aspects of disease burden, transmission dynamics, and ecological risks. The final reference list was generated on the basis of originality, relevance to the scope of yellow fever resurgence in Latin America, and contribution to understanding epidemiological, ecological, and policy drivers, with an emphasis on studies of disease burden, transmission, and ecological risks.

In 2025, 301 confirmed YF cases and 124 associated deaths have been reported across Latin America, yielding a case fatality rate (CFR) of 41.1%.2 In the current episode, four countries (Bolivia, Brazil, Colombia, and Peru) have accounted for 289 of the 301 confirmed cases and 115 of the 124 reported deaths.3 This situation surpasses, in terms of both lethality and geographic spread, the most comparable recent epidemic: the 2018 outbreak in São Paulo, Brazil, which had a case fatality rate (CFR) of 35.4% (Table 1). That outbreak remains the largest yellow fever epidemic in the Americas in the 21st century to date.

The current epidemiological profile, marked by low vaccine coverage in forest-adjacent populations and persistent sylvatic transmission, closely mirrors that of the 2017–2018 event.4,5 During that outbreak, Brazil recorded 498 autochthonous cases and 176 deaths, primarily among unvaccinated individuals at the forest–urban interface, resulting in a mortality frequency of 35.4%.6 Its rapid spatial expansion (∼3.3 km/day) was fueled by vector amplification during the rainy season and multiple introductions of South American genotype I strains into densely populated regions.7

During the current outbreak, most fatalities occurred among unvaccinated individuals, underscoring the critical importance of immunization.8 In response, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) has issued alerts emphasizing enhanced surveillance, georeferenced epizootic mapping, and targeted vaccination in ecological corridors.3,7 Contributing factors include vector proliferation due to deforestation and climate variability, the persistence of sylvatic reservoirs, and increased cross-border human mobility.

Given the region's ecological vulnerability and mobility patterns, there is a real risk of renewed epidemic expansion into densely populated but historically under-vaccinated urban areas.9 Without rapid implementation of early detection systems, mass immunization campaigns, and intersectoral coordination, To prevent another large-scale yellow fever crisis in Latin America, lessons from last year's outbreak, including earlier detection through integrated human–non-human primate surveillance, faster risk communication, and timely targeted vaccination at the forest–urban interface and along mobility corridors, must guide immediate and coordinated action from the tropical medicine and public health research community, emphasizing strategies to avert new surges.

Epidemiological patterns, cyclical dynamics and epizootics

YF in South America shows quasi-cyclical (∼3–7-year) sylvatic waves tightly coupled to epizootics in highly susceptible Neotropical NHPs particularly howler monkeys (Alouatta spp.) and to rainfall and temperature-driven vector dynamics involving Haemagogus and Sabethes.10 These waves recur seasonally, with higher incidence between December and May, as susceptible populations are renewed through births and migration into areas with heterogeneous vaccination coverage. Periodic spillover beyond the Amazon has been documented, including expansion into previously unaffected municipalities of Southeast Brazil during 2016–2018 and recent foci in São Paulo (Brazil) and Tolima (Colombia) in 2024–2025.11,12 Lessons from 2017 to 2018 include the operational value of NHP-epizootic surveillance and predictive corridor models to target vaccination; however, local declines of Alouatta populations may reduce epizootic-signal sensitivity, and anomalously high temperatures in 2023–2024 could modify vector activity and challenge model calibration.13 Urban transmission remains unconfirmed; moreover, evidence on Aedes aegypti's competence for sustained YFV transmission is geographically variable and method-dependent, warranting cautious interpretation.14,15

Vaccination strategies: fractional-dose use during emergencies

When vaccine supply is insufficient during large outbreaks, WHO/PAHO have authorized off-label fractional dosing (∼1/5 of the standard 0.5 mL; ≥1000–3000 IU) to rapidly expand coverage, as implemented in the DRC and Brazil.16 Across WHO-prequalified 17D vaccines, fractional doses show non-inferior immunogenicity at ∼28 days and 1 year, with sustained neutralizing titers reported up to ∼8–10 years for 17DD and 17D-204 substrains.17 But important caveats remain: early antibody kinetics may be lower by day 10 (relevant to International Health Regulations (IHR) travel timelines), protection durability data are limited in specific groups, and children vaccinated in infancy can exhibit faster waning even after a full dose (supporting caution in assuming long-term protection after fractional dosing).18,19 Current WHO policy restricts fractional dosing to emergency settings and it does not qualify for an International Certificate of Vaccination (“yellow card”), unless a subsequent full dose is given.17,20 Operationally, programs should ensure precise dose calibration and administration (subcutaneous [s.c.] versus intradermal [i.d.] injection, which requires specific training), clear eligibility criteria, robust documentation, management of multi-dose vial wastage within the reconstitution window, and post-campaign effectiveness, safety, or serologic monitoring.21 Nevertheless, in supply-constrained emergencies, fractional dosing remains a credible dose-sparing tool to achieve high coverage while maintaining protection.

Vaccination, duration of protection and booster policies

Although WHO (2013) endorsed a single-dose strategy for most adults,22 Brazilian cohort data show waning immunity after 17DD primo vaccination: neutralizing antibody titers (PRNT) and memory B/T-cell subsets decline over 5–13 years in adults, with seropositivity falling below ∼90% beyond 5–9 years.23 In children vaccinated at 9–12 months, PRNT seropositivity and cellular memory wane earlier, with seropositivity <60% by ≥4 years post-dose, supporting the need for boosters in high-risk settings.18 Historically, Brazil's Ministry of Health recommended a booster at 10 years for adults and at 4 years for those primo vaccinated in infancy; during the 2016 shortage, the program temporarily adopted a single-dose policy to maximize coverage.20 In adults who received ≥2 doses, seropositivity remained higher and more persistent (≈86–100% across 1–10+ years since the last dose), suggesting selected individuals may benefit from revaccination while prioritizing primo vaccination of susceptibles.19 Consistent with these cohort findings, a systematic review identified “time since vaccination” as a recurrent correlate of vaccine failure and highlighted specific risk groups (pregnancy, early infancy, severe malnutrition), reinforcing the rationale for targeted booster policies in high-risk settings.24

Trends in Yellow fever case-fatality rates in South America (1960–2025)

Historical analysis from 1960 to 2025 reveals a cumulative burden of 9692 reported YF cases and 3445 deaths in South America, corresponding to a regional CFR of 36%. Brazil and Peru had the highest disease burden, with 3559 (37%) and 3361 (35%) cases, respectively, followed by Bolivia (1568 cases; 16%), Colombia (774 cases; 8%) and Venezuela with 78 cases of YF.25 Country-level CFR vary: Brazil 35.2%, Peru 41%, Bolivia 33%, Colombia 25%, and Venezuela 41%.25 Colombia's lower CFR likely reflects broader case ascertainment and expanded laboratory confirmation. Apparent values > 100% such as the 136% cited for 2021 are artefacts of period misalignment in preliminary reports (deaths and cases assigned to different epidemiological periods) rather than true lethality; once aligned to the same period, the estimate is well below 100% and within Colombia's recent national range.26

During the early surveillance period (1960–1979), 1494 cases were documented in South America, yet no deaths were officially recorded, likely due to diagnostic limitations and severe underreporting (Fig. 1).2 In contrast, the 1980s showed dramatic increases in CFR, peaking at 90% in 1987. Significant outbreaks occurred in 1995 (524 cases) and 2018 (1323 cases), reflecting improved detection of severe cases but also gaps in timely treatment and vaccination coverage.

Improved detection in 2016–2018 reflects a combined effect of surveillance scale-up (indicator- and event-based), expanded laboratory capacity (PCR/IgM availability and shorter turn-around), updated case definitions and risk communication to clinicians, and the systematic use of NHP epizootic reports to trigger targeted human case finding. Concomitantly, multiple states experienced true incidence surges, so increases cannot be attributed to ascertainment alone.

More recent data (2020–2025) reveals an upward trend. During this period, 370 cases and 149 deaths were reported, with an average CFR of 40%. Brazil (140 cases; CFR 64%), Peru (105 cases; CFR 47%), and Colombia (78 cases; CFR 45%) were the most affected (Fig. 2). Ecuador reported three cases and two deaths in 2025 (CFR 67%), in line with earlier national patterns (five cases and three deaths since 2000).

During the 15 first weeks of 2025 alone, a total of 193 cases and 77 deaths (CFR 40%) were reported across the region, reinforcing concerns of resurgence. Contributing factors include post-COVID vaccine hesitancy, deforestation-driven vector expansion, and climate change, all of which have facilitated transmission into previously unaffected areas such as Tolima (Colombia). Structural inequities, including limited healthcare access in rural Amazonian districts such as Morona Santiago (Ecuador), further elevate mortality risk. Reactive policies, such as mandatory vaccination certificates, are insufficient to address these underlying vulnerabilities. These findings underscore the urgent need for robust surveillance systems and equity-centred interventions to prevent urban spillovers while also reducing case fatality rates in outbreak and endemic areas.

Surveillance and clinical practice, early suspicion and differential diagnosis

During large dengue waves, severe febrile jaundice or hemorrhagic presentations should trigger parallel evaluation for YF and other etiologies (e.g., leptospirosis, spotted fever rickettsioses, and Plasmodium falciparum malaria in the Amazon). Prompt notification, linkage with reports of NHP die-offs, and accelerated testing pathways are essential to prevent misclassification and delays in care, particularly in forest–urban interface settings and underserved Amazonian districts.7,14 When evaluating suspected vaccine failure, verification of vaccination timing, age at primary dose, pregnancy status, nutritional status, and immunosuppression is recommended in light of documented risk modifiers.24

Key factors contributing to the recent outbreaks

Limited access to vaccine in forested areas

Despite the proven efficacy of the YF vaccine, most cases in recent outbreaks have occurred among unvaccinated individuals.27 Remote rainforest communities face substantial logistical challenges, including rugged terrain and inadequate health infrastructure, which limit routine immunisation efforts.28,29 However, innovative delivery strategies such as drone-assisted transport have shown promise in overcoming these barriers by shortening delivery times and maintaining cold-chain integrity.30

Seasonal mosquito proliferation

Rainy seasons create ideal breeding conditions for vector species such as Haemagogus spp, Sabethes spp and Aedes aegypti.31, 32, 33 Studies have consistently demonstrated that mosquito abundance significantly increases during rainy periods, heightening the risk of YF transmission.34

Climate change and expanding transmission zones

Shifting precipitation patterns and rising temperatures, hallmarks of climate change, are expanding the geographic range of mosquito vectors into previously unaffected regions. This ecological shift increases the number of people at risk, particularly in highland or peri-urban regions where Aedes aegypti and Haemagogus spp. are establishing new habitats.35,36

Inadequate health promotion on mosquito-bite prevention

Effective communication strategies are essential to promote protective behaviors. However, recent studies highlight gaps in public health campaigns, especially in reaching remote and vulnerable populations with actionable prevention advice.37 Additionally, the proliferation of misinformation on social media has further undermined public trust in scientific evidence and reduced uptake of preventive measures.38,39

Human migration and cross-border spread

Human mobility, particularly involving unvaccinated individuals, has historically contributed to the regional spread of infectious diseases. The ongoing migration in Latin America, including mass displacement from Venezuela, presents challenges for YF control by increasing the number of susceptible individuals moving across borders.25,40,41

Political instability and security challenges in health delivery

Political instability significantly undermines health service delivery and disease control efforts. Historical evidence illustrates that conflict and governance breakdowns have facilitated the resurgence of infectious diseases in multiple contexts, such as malaria and measles in Uganda, and HIV/AIDS in Guinea-Bissau.42,43 In the current Latin American landscape, political turbulence compromises health system performance by disrupting resource allocation, deprioritizing vaccination programs, and compromising operational continuity. Compounding this instability, the presence of organized crime, including drug trafficking networks, further restricts access to high-risk, remote regions. In many such areas, healthcare workers face safety threats, and public health operations, including immunization and vector surveillance campaigns, are frequently suspended or obstructed. These dynamics not only impair national outbreak responses but also pose a broader threat to regional health security by enabling unchecked transmission of YF and other arboviruses.44,45

Moreover, isolated indigenous and rural communities are particularly vulnerable under these conditions. These populations often lack consistent access to healthcare, preventive information, or protective interventions, yet they inhabit ecologically sensitive zones where YF circulates in sylvatic vectors and NHP.14,46 Their exclusion from coordinated response efforts deepens structural inequities and magnifies the human toll of outbreaks.

Non-human primates as sentinels in yellow fever surveillance

The detection of dead or sick NHPs has proven to be a critical early warning indicator of YFV circulation in endemic regions. For example, in Colombia's Tolima department, necropsies conducted by Cortolima (Corporación Autónoma Regional del Tolima) confirmed YFV infection in 19 out of 23 deceased primates, which triggered intensified public health responses in neighboring municipalities.47 Similarly, the Colombian National Academy of Medicine has emphasized the importance of prompt community reporting of monkey deaths, as such events signal active viral transmission and require immediate intervention.48 The PAHO has recognized Colombia's community-based surveillance model as a regional benchmark.49 This approach integrates primate mortality data into geospatial monitoring systems, guiding targeted vector control measures and strategic vaccination campaigns.50

This integrated model of syndromic ecological surveillance not only enhances early detection capabilities but also highlights the dual benefit of wildlife conservation not only for biodiversity preservation but also for public health protection.

Growth of urban developments towards enzootic zones, ecological fragmentation, and illicit activities

According to the sylvatic pattern of YF, a gradient of viral dissemination from rural towards urban areas has been observed, which increases the likelihood of overlapping of infected jungle vectors and the human population.51 Also, the transmission dynamics of YF and other arboviral diseases are increasingly shaped by illegal human activities within forest ecosystems, particularly unregulated gold mining and deforestation.52 Empirical studies have demonstrated that such activities disrupt ecological stability and significantly increase human exposure to sylvatic vectors, including Haemagogus spp and Sabethes spp.53, 54, 55

In French Guiana, for instance, undocumented gold miners working in heavily degraded forest areas have exhibited high levels of exposure to zoonotic pathogens, including YFV, due to prolonged environmental exposure, poor housing conditions, and lack of vector control or healthcare access.54 Similarly, in the Ecuadorian Amazon, Izurieta and collaborators41 documented a 13% YF attack rate among military personnel operating in regions undergoing rapid deforestation, underscoring the role of landscape alteration in facilitating viral spillover.56

Further evidence from Wilk-da-Silva and collaborators.42 and Ilacqua and collaborators43 demonstrates that forest fragmentation and increased edge density are positively correlated with elevated YFV circulation, suggesting that disturbed habitats created by illegal mining and logging may serve as ecological amplifiers for arboviral transmission.57,58 These findings are supported by broader reviews,59 which identify anthropogenic environmental change as a central driver in the resurgence of sylvatic YF, particularly in areas with limited immunization coverage and weak surveillance infrastructure. Illegal extractive activities and associated ecological degradation create high-risk environments for yellow fever outbreaks by increasing contact rates among humans, enabling the proliferation of sylvatic vectors, and non-human primate reservoirs.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis relies on secondary data from national surveillance systems, which may be affected by inhomogeneous inter-countries data collection protocols, underreporting, inconsistent diagnostic criteria, and reporting delays. These factors could impact the accuracy of incidence, mortality, and geographical distribution estimates. Second, the absence of comprehensive genomic sequencing data limited our ability to characterize viral genotypes circulating in some regions, constraining our understanding of transmission dynamics. Similarly, limited and uneven data on vaccination coverage in high-risk populations prevented a more precise assessment of its protective effect. Finally, the study did not incorporate socioeconomic variables or health system access indicators, which could have provided deeper insights into the observed disparities in disease burden across countries. We did not produce novel geospatial distribution models. Instead, we compiled reported human outbreaks and documented NHP epizootics and mapped descriptive incidence by jurisdiction to visualize spread and surveillance gaps.

Conclusions

The resurgence of YF in Latin America reflects a convergence of biological, environmental, and socio-political vulnerabilities that continue to undermine regional health security. Despite the availability of a safe and effective vaccine, gaps in coverage, particularly in remote and underserved areas, alongside deforestation, climate variability, and political instability, have created conditions ripe for viral reemergence.

Addressing this crisis requires strengthened surveillance systems, equitable vaccine deployment, and intersectoral coordination to mitigate ecological drivers of transmission. The scientific community must prioritize research on vector dynamics, vaccine strategies, and health system resilience. YF is no longer a historical concern, it is a contemporary public health threat demanding urgent, science-based, and coordinated one health action.

Building upon the aforementioned points, it can be concluded that the persistent threat of YF in Latin America underscores the urgent necessity for a comprehensive and collaborative response with a focus on transnational health problems, determinants, and solutions that involve multiple disciplines and promote interdisciplinary collaboration in the context of global health. Overcoming existing vulnerabilities demands not only the equitable expansion of vaccination and the strengthening of surveillance, but also proactively addressing the underlying environmental and socio-political factors that facilitate the disease's spread. Sustained investment in scientific research and the consolidation of resilient health systems are fundamental pillars for preventing future outbreaks and safeguarding regional public health from this and other emerging threats.
