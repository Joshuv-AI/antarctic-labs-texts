Adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and risk of chronic kidney disease among middle-aged and older adults: insights from two nationwide cohort studies
Authors: Zenghui Zhang, Shaojie Han, Xunda Ye, Huimin Zhu, Zegui Huang, Yangbo Xi, Chuxian Guo, Zhaoyu Liu, Jun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdherence to the EAT-Lancet diet has been associated with a lower risk of adverse health outcomes. However, its relationship with chronic kidney disease (CKD) risk remains unclear. We aimed to investigate the association between adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and CKD risk among middle-aged and older adults.MethodsThis study included 19,991 participants from the US NHANES cohort for cross-sectional analysis and 119,820 participants from the UK Biobank cohort for longitudinal analysis, all aged 40 years or older. Dietary data were collected using 24-hour dietary recall questionnaires, and a modified EAT-Lancet diet index was constructed based on the EAT-Lancet reference diet. Multivariable poisson regression models and Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to examine the associations between the EAT-Lancet diet index and CKD risk.ResultsIn the US NHANES cohort, 4,640 (23.21%) participants were diagnosed with CKD. Within the multivariable model, the relative risk for CKD in the highest adherence group of the EAT-Lancet diet index was 0.78 (95% CI: 0.70, 0.87) compared to those in the lowest adherence group. During a mean follow-up of 13.19 years, 3,658 (3.05%) incident cases of CKD were identified in the UK Biobank cohort. After adjusting for covariates, the Q2, Q3, Q4, and Q5 groups exhibited significantly lower risks for incident CKD compared to the Q1 group, with hazard ratios (95% CI) of 0.94 (0.85, 1.03), 0.85 (0.77, 0.94), 0.82 (0.74, 0.91), and 0.71 (0.64, 0.79), respectively. A clear dose-response relationship between EAT-Lancet diet index and CKD risk was observed in two cohorts.ConclusionsAdherence to the EAT-Lancet diet is associated with a lower prevalence and risk of CKD in middle-aged and older adults, with a dose-response relationship. These findings highlight the importance of adopting the EAT-Lancet diet to lower the risk burden of CKD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01236-z.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a chronic progressive condition characterized by structural or functional abnormalities of the kidneys lasting for more than three months [1]. Due to its high prevalence, poor prognosis, and substantial medical costs, CKD has emerged as a significant public health challenge globally [2]. According to data from the 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study, the incident cases of CKD have increased from 7.80 million in 1990 to 18.99 million in 2019 globally, while the disability-adjusted life years have risen from 21.50 million to 41.54 million [3]. It is estimated that about 30 million adults have CKD in the United States, representing more than one in seven of the all population [4], while the prevalence of CKD is generally greater than 10% in Eastern and Central European countries [5]. Notably, the burden of CKD increases with age, particularly affecting middle-aged and elderly populations [6]. Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors and implementing primary prevention strategies are crucial measures to reduce the risk burden of CKD in these age groups.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the development and progression of CKD are closely linked to metabolic, lifestyle, and environmental factors [7]. Key risk factors include hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, which are often associated with unhealthy dietary patterns high in fat and sugar [8, 9]. Given the irreversible nature of CKD progression, promoting lifestyle changes, especially adopting healthy dietary patterns, is essential for preventing CKD [10]. In 2019, the EAT-Lancet Commission convened experts from various fields to develop a global planetary health reference diet aimed at promoting both human health and environmental sustainability, known as the EAT-Lancet Diet [11]. This dietary pattern emphasizes the consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, while strictly limiting animal protein, fat, and sugar intake. Extensive research evidence has shown that adherence to the EAT-lancet diet is associated with a lower risk of adverse health outcomes such as type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD) and all-cause mortality [12–16]. However, whether adherence to the EAT-Lancet Diet can effectively lower the risk of CKD, particularly among middle-aged and elderly individuals who bear a heavy CKD burden, remains to be determined.

To fill this knowledge gap, we intend to utilize data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to examine the association between adherence to the EAT-Lancet dietary pattern and risk of CKD via conducting a cross-sectional study. Furthermore, the data from the UK Biobank cohort were used to prospectively investigate the longitudinal relationship between adherence to the EAT-Lancet Diet and incidence of CKD in middle-aged and elderly populations.

Methods

Study design and population

NHANES is a comprehensive program designed to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. Its research design employs a stratified, multi-phase sampling method to ensure sample representativeness [17]. The survey covers various counties across the United States, with 15 counties visited annually, and approximately 5,000 individuals surveyed each year. Data collected include demographic and socioeconomic information, dietary habits, health-related issues, as well as results from physical examinations and laboratory tests, which are released in two-year cycles. For this study, we utilized data from 70,190 participants across seven NHANES cycles (2005–2018). Participants lacking complete dietary questionnaires, self-reported CKD data, or younger than 40 years were excluded. Ultimately, 19,991 participants were included for cross-sectional analysis (Figure S1). NHANES was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the National Center for Health Statistics, and all participants provided written informed consent.

The UK Biobank is a large, population-based prospective cohort study that recruited over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years from 22 assessment centers across England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010 [18, 19]. At baseline, participants completed a touch-screen questionnaire and brief oral interview, underwent a physical examination, and provided biological samples. For our prospective cohort analyses, we firstly excluded participants under 40 years of age at baseline or those without completed two 24-hour online dietary questionnaire data. Additionally, participants diagnosed with CKD based on International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10) coding (see Table S1), having an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m², or a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g at baseline were excluded. Furthermore, participants with CKD diagnosed prior to dietary assessment was excluded. A total of 119,820 participants were included in the final analysis (Figure S2). The UK Biobank study received approval from the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (11/NW/0382), and all participants provided written informed consent upon enrollment. This research adhered to the UK Biobank’s data access procedures and was authorized under application number 300,908.

Dietary assessment and construction of the EAT-Lancet diet index

For dietary assessment in NHANES, participants provided detailed dietary data through consecutive two 24-hour recall questionnaire during a computer-assisted, face-to-face interview, and the average value of these two 24-hour recalls was used to calculate EAT-Lancet diet index. The questionnaire was primarily designed based on the United States Department of Agriculture’s (USDA) food pattern. To align with the EAT-Lancet dietary pattern, we replaced the food items in equivalent amounts according to previous studies [20, 21], as shown in Table S2. We used the conversion factors established by Conrad to estimate the grams of food consumed by NHANES participants based on their food pattern equivalents intake of each EAT-Lancet component [21]. In the UK Biobank, a web-based 24-hour dietary questionnaire was administered at the assessment center to collect detailed dietary information [22]. About 300, 000 participants were invited via e-mails between February 2011 and April 2012, with the request to complete four 24-hour dietary questionnaires. However, not all the participants who received the email invitation completed the diet questionnaire, some of them completed twice, and a small number of people completed three times or more. Therefore, we included the participants with completed at least two diet questionnaire survey for the construction of the EAT-Lancet score. Food intake amounts were calculated as the mean of all available measurements. We developed an EAT-Lancet diet index based on these food components, including whole grains, tubers and starchy vegetables, vegetables, fruits, dairy products, protein sources, legumes, added fats, and sugars, following prior study [23], as detailed in Table S3.

For the construction of the EAT-Lancet diet index in this study, each component was assigned points from 0 to 10 proportionally up to the recommended intake, and proportionally from 10 to 0 when exceeding it. The total possible score ranged from 0 to 130 points, with higher scores indicating greater adherence to the EAT-Lancet reference diet, as shown in Table S4.

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome of interest in this study was incident CKD. In the NHANES cohort, CKD was defined as a self-reported history of CKD or dialysis, eGFR less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m², or UACR ≥ 30 mg/g; meeting any one of these criteria constituted a diagnosis of CKD [24]. In the UK Biobank, health outcomes for each participant were determined by linking hospital admission and death registry records. The incidence of CKD during follow-up was identified using ICD-10 codes, as detailed in Table S1. For longitudinal analysis, participants in the UK Biobank were followed from baseline enrollment until the occurrence of CKD, death, or the censoring date (October 31, 2022), whichever occurred first.

Assessment of covariates

As previously detailed [17–19], standardized questionnaires and laboratory protocols were employed to gather information on the following covariates in US NHANES and UK Biobank cohort: age (continuous variable), sex (female or male), income (< 18,000, 18,000–30,999, 31,000–51,999, 52,000–100,000, >100,000), ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic Black, or other) educational attainment (college degree or no), smoking and drinking status (yes or no), body mass index (BMI) (continuous variable), physical activity levels (at goal or no). Physical activity at goal is defined as ≥ 150 min/week of moderate intensity, ≥ 75 min/week of vigorous intensity, or an equivalent combination. The data of comorbidity, including diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary heart disease, stroke, and heart failure (yes or no), were collected based on ICD codes, self-reports, or medication use. In addition, the covariates in UK Biobank also included region of assessment (Midlands, North, Scotland, South, or Wales), systolic blood pressure (continuous variable), sleep duration (continuous variable), Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) use (yes or no).

Statistical analyses

The study population from NHANES and the UK Biobank was divided into five groups according to quintiles of the EAT-Lancet diet index, respectively. NHANES data were analyzed following the NHANES statistical guidelines. Baseline characteristics in the US NHANES were described using weighted means with standard errors of mean (SEM) for continuous variables and unweighted frequencies with weighted percentages for categorical variables. For the UK Biobank, continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were reported as percentages. Categorical variable comparisons were conducted using chi-squared tests, and one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for continuous variables.

The modified poisson regression model with binary outcome data was employed to investigate the cross-sectional association between adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and the prevalence of CKD. The results are presented as relative risk (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Model 1 adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 further adjusted for ethnicity, education level, income, smoking and drinking status, BMI, physical activity at goal, and history of diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary heart disease, stroke, and heart failure, building upon Model (1) In the UK Biobank, the cumulative incidence of CKD across groups stratified by the EAT-Lancet diet index was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with comparisons made using log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to calculate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CI for the longitudinal associations between the EAT-Lancet diet index and CKD risk. The covariates adjusted for in the models 1 were consistent with those in NHANES, but in the UK Biobank cohort, additional adjustments included region of assessment, systolic blood pressure, sleep duration, total energy intake, and use of NSAIDs in the model (2) The missing values of covariates was shown in the Table S5-S6, and the multiple imputation with chain equation method was used to handle missing values of covariates in the main analysis. Additionally, restricted cubic spline poisson regression models or Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to evaluate the nonlinear relationship between the EAT-Lancet diet index and CKD risk, with nonlinearity assessed using likelihood ratio tests.

Subgroup analyses in both the US NHANES and UK Biobank were conducted by stratifying data according to age (< 65 vs. ≥65 years), sex (male vs. female), smoking status (non-smoker vs. smoker), drinking status (non-drinker vs. drinker), ethnicity (non-Hispanic white vs. other), college degree (yes vs. no), physical activity (at goal vs. below goal), BMI (< 28 vs. ≥28 kg/m²), and history of diabetes, dyslipidaemia, and hypertension (yes vs. no). Sensitivity analyses were also performed to verify the reliability of the primary analysis results. First, we conduct a sensitivity analysis of CKD based on self-reported history of CKD or dialysis in NHANES. Second, given the correlation between diet and Inflammatory response, additional adjustments were made for systemic inflammatory response index in the sensitivity analysis. Third, considering the significant association between CKD and CVD [25], participants with a history of CVD (including coronary heart disease, stroke, or heart failure) were excluded from the analysis. Fourth, taking into account the impact of covariates imputation on the results, the analysis was repeated without addressing the missing values of covariates. Fifth, excluding participants within the first 2 years of follow-up in UK biobank to account for reverse causation. Finally, we excluded participants with fewer than three completed dietary questionnaires to repeat the analysis. All statistical analyses in this study were conducted using R software version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Result

Baseline characteristics of the study population in the US NHANES and UK biobank

Tables 1 and 2 present the baseline characteristics of participants stratified by quintiles of the EAT-Lancet score in the US NHANES and UK Biobank cohorts. The mean age of participants was 59.7 years in the NHANES cohort and 56.0 years in the UK Biobank cohort. Among the participants included in the analysis, less than half were males (48.9% in NHANES, 44.2% in UK Biobank). The EAT-Lancet diet index ranged from 0 to 95 in NHANES and from 0 to 90 in the UK Biobank, with the distribution shown in Figure S3. In both cohorts, compared to those in the lowest quintile, participants in the highest quintile were more likely to be female, have a higher income level and proportion with a college degree, and meet physical activity goals. They also had a lower proportion of Non-Hispanic Black participants, smokers, as well as lower BMI levels and a lower prevalence of comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary heart disease, stroke, and heart failure. In UK biobank cohort, participants in the highest quintile have a higher energy intake level, but lower proportion of NSAIDs use compare with those in the lowest quintile. Longitudinal association between the EAT-Lancet diet and incident CKD risk in the UK biobank

During a mean (SD) follow-up of 13.19 (1.34) years, 3,658 (3.05%) incident cases of CKD were identified in the UK Biobank cohort. Cumulative incidence curves demonstrated that participants in the Q5 group had a significantly lower risk of developing CKD compared to those in other groups (log-rank test, p < 0.001, Fig. 3). Within the multivariable model, the Q2, Q3, Q4, and Q5 groups exhibited significantly lower risks for incident CKD compared to the Q1 group, with HR (95% CI) of 0.94 (0.85, 1.03), 0.85 (0.77, 0.94), 0.82 (0.74, 0.91), and 0.71 (0.64, 0.79), respectively (Table 4). There was a significant linear negative association between the EAT-Lancet diet and the risk of incident CKD (p for non-linearity = 0.318, Fig. 1.B), with each 10-point increase in the score associated with a 10% lower risk of CKD. Discussion

In this study, we conducted both a cross-sectional analysis involving 19,991 participants from the US NHANES cohort and a longitudinal analysis with 119,820 participants from the UK Biobank cohort. Our findings reveal a significant inverse association between the EAT-Lancet diet index and the risk of CKD among middle-aged and older adults. This association also exhibits a clear dose-response relationship, with each 10-point increase in the EAT-Lancet diet index corresponding to a 6–10% reduction in CKD risk. Additionally, these findings remain robust across diverse subgroups and sensitivity analyses in US NHANES and UK Biobank cohort.

Previous studies have examined the association between individual dietary elements or locally recommended dietary patterns and the risk of CKD in the general population. For instance, calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium are essential minerals that play crucial roles in maintaining proper kidney function [26]. A prospective observational cohort study including 188,510 participants from the UK Biobank found that lower dietary magnesium intake is associated with a higher risk of developing CKD in adults with normal kidney function [27]. However, focusing on a single dietary element cannot fully capture the complex interactions between diet and disease development. Therefore, considering a comprehensive healthy dietary pattern is necessary for prevention of CKD. A prospective study revealed that higher adherence to the Alternative Mediterranean Diet (aMED), the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) score, and healthful plant-based diet index patterns was associated with a lower risk of CKD in British adults [28]. This highlighted the importance of maintaining a healthy plane-eating diet to reduce the risk of CKD. In addition, using data from the Chronic Renal Dysfunction Cohort (CRIC) study, Hu et al. calculated four dietary pattern scores, including the 2015 Healthy Eating Index, the 2010 Alternative Healthy Eating Index, aMED, and the DASH score, to investigate their associations with risk of CKD progression [29]. They found a negative association between healthy diet scores and risk of CKD progression, with the strongest association observed for the aMED.

The aMED, originating from countries surrounding the Mediterranean Sea such as Spain, Italy, and Greece, emphasizes traditional dietary culture and balanced nutrition, which is beneficial for preventing CVD [30]. In contrast, the EAT-Lancet diet focuses on global sustainability by considering the environmental impacts of food production, such as greenhouse gas emissions and water use, aiming to provide sustainable food security worldwide [11]. While numerous previous studies have demonstrated the significant protective effects of the EAT-Lancet diet against adverse health outcomes [16, 31], evidence specific to CKD has been limited. In the current study, we utilized data from two large national cohorts (US NHANES and UK Biobank) to explore, for the first time, the association between the EAT-Lancet diet and CKD risk in middle-aged and elderly individuals. Both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses revealed that adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet was associated with a lower prevalence and long-term risk of CKD. These findings extend existing research on the EAT-Lancet diet and adverse health outcomes, identifying a planetary healthy eating patterns that can be scaled up globally to prevent the burden of CKD.

High protein intake has been shown to exacerbate kidney damage and increase the risk of CKD [32]. Previous studies have demonstrated that whole-plant dietary patterns can reduce overall protein intake, thereby slowing CKD progression and providing significant renal protection [33]. However, adhering strictly to a whole-plant diet may not be sustainable in the long term, and chronically low protein intake can lead to malnutrition or other adverse health outcomes [34, 35]. Animal protein consumption promotes an acidic environment and inflammation in the body, whereas plant proteins exhibit alkalinizing and anti-inflammatory properties, which are beneficial for kidney health [36]. The EAT-Lancet diet recommends not only the intake of plant-based components such as fruits and vegetables but also appropriate levels of protein intake. Specifically, it advocates replacing animal proteins (such as red meat and ultra-processed meats) with plant-based protein sources like nuts, legumes, and whole grains to achieve a balanced nutritional composition. Sustainable healthy eating patterns should provide lasting benefits. Our longitudinal analysis of UK Biobank cohort confirmed that adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet index reduces the long-term risk of CKD, with each 10-point increase in the index associated with a 7% reduction in CKD risk.

The important practical significance of this study is the discovery of a new global healthy dietary pattern, the EAT-Lancet diet, which can significantly reduce the risk of CKD. CKD is one of the leading causes of premature death globally, with its incidence steadily increasing [37]. However, due to the absence of overt symptoms in the early stages, CKD often remains undiagnosed. It is estimated that 94% of patients with mild to moderate kidney dysfunction and approximately 48% of those with severe renal insufficiency remain undiagnosed in US, which can lead to advanced chronic renal failure and associated complications later in life [38]. This significantly increases the risk of mortality and poses a substantial public health burden. Improving dietary habits is cost-effective in many countries, and recommending the EAT-Lancet diet not only benefits individual health but also supports the sustainable development of the earth’s environment [39, 40]. The findings of this study provide robust evidence for the primary prevention of CKD and offer valuable information for local governments and public health departments to implement interventions and systemic changes.

This study possesses several notable strengths, including that we are the first to utilize data from two large cohort studies, the US NHANES and the UK Biobank, to investigate both cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and the risk of CKD among middle-aged and older adults. Notably, consistent results have been observed across different cohorts and perspectives. Additionally, the US NHANES cohort is nationally representative, and the UK Biobank cohort has stable follow-up, which enhances the reliability of our findings. However, this study also has some limitations that must be acknowledged. First, CKD diagnosis in the US NHANES primarily relies on self-reported data, whereas CKD events in the UK Biobank are mainly identified through ICD coding. Given the concealment of CKD diagnosis, both methods may underestimate its prevalence and incidence, which might weaken the ability to detect the association. Nevertheless, our study still identified a strong reverse association. Second, although we adjusted for a comprehensive set of confounding factors, the observational design of this study limits our ability to establish causality and fully rule out residual confounding from other variables, such as environmental and genetic factors. Third, dietary data were collected using a 24-hour dietary recall questionnaire, which may be subject to recall bias. In addition, the count of solid fats and oils does not truly reflect the added saturated fats and added unsaturated oils, and using g/day for scoring may cause confusion among different dairy products. Furthermore, only baseline dietary information was available, and long-term changes in diet and other covariates were not assessed. Future studies should investigate association between the change of dietary patterns and CKD risk. Finally, although data were drawn from two large cohorts in the US and the UK, regional dietary differences and potential selection bias due to voluntary participation may affect the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Therefore, caution should be exercised when applying these results to different demographic or cultural contexts.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our results indicate a significant association between adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet and a lower prevalence and risk of CKD in middle-aged and elderly populations, with evidence of a dose-response relationship. These findings further emphasize adoption of the EAT-Lancet diet to prevent and improve the risk burden of CKD.
