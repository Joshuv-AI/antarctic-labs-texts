Reallocating 24-hour movement behaviors and cardiometabolic health in adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yuxuan Zhang, Jiaqiao Zhang, Jiaxin Yue, Fan Liu, Xiuxiu Huang, Dandan Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examined the associations of reallocating time among 24-hour movement behaviors (24HMB) on cardiometabolic health outcomes (anthropometric indicators, blood pressure, glucose, lipids) in adults.MethodsSeven databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, ProQuest, Web of Science, CINAHL and SPORTDiscus) were systematically searched through August 2024. Studies were eligible if they assessed the association between 24HMB and cardiometabolic outcomes. Study quality was evaluated using established assessment tools. Meta-analyses were performed when at least three studies provided comparable 30-minute isotemporal substitution estimates for a specific outcome.ResultsTwenty-one eligible studies (62,056 participants) were included. Reallocating 30-minute of sedentary to moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) was associated with lower body mass index (BMI) (β = -0.53, 95% CI -0.81 to -0.26), lower waist circumference (β = -1.42, 95% CI: -2.19 to -0.56), higher high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (β = 0.02, 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.03), and lower triglyceride levels (β = -0.06, 95% CI: -0.11 to -0.01). Replacing sedentary behavior with sleep (β = -0.13, 95% CI -0.19 to -0.07) was associated with lower BMI. Substituting sedentary behavior with either sleep (β = -0.66, 95% CI: -1.19 to -0.14) or light physical activity (β = -0.40, 95% CI: -0.57 to -0.22) was linked to smaller waist circumferences. Substituting sleep with MVPA was associated with lower BMI (β = -0.31, 95% CI -0.46 to -0.15) and waist circumference (β = -1.46, 95% CI -2.37 to -0.55). Conversely, replacing MVPA with sleep (β = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.27 to 0.37) was associated with higher BMI. Furthermore, greater adherence to the 24HMB guidelines (the number of meeting behavioral recommendations of 24HMB guidelines) was associated with lower risks of obesity, hypertension and diabetes.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that the associations between 24HMB reallocations and cardiometabolic health were direction-specific. The most consistent benefits were observed when time was reallocated from sedentary behavior to MVPA, which was associated with lower BMI, smaller waist circumference, and better blood lipids.Trial registrationThis study has been registered in PROPERO (CRD42024565071).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01885-4.

Introduction

Cardiometabolic diseases, including cardiovascular disease and diabetes mellitus, are the leading causes of mortality worldwide [1]. Risk factors such as overweight, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and reduced insulin sensitivity significantly contribute to the development of these conditions [2, 3]. Adopting a healthy lifestyle plays a crucial role in lowering the risk of cardiometabolic disease risk [4, 5]. Among lifestyle factors, physical activity (including moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity (MVPA) and light intensity physical activity (LPA)), sedentary behavior (SB), and sleep are collectively referred to as 24-hour movement behaviors (24HMB). These components have each been independently linked to various cardiometabolic outcomes in previous studies [6–8].

Existing evidence indicated that increasing the time spent in physical activity can improve cardiometabolic health through multiple mechanisms, including the reduction of oxidative stress and inflammation, as well as the promotion of favorable structural and functional remodeling of the cardiovascular system [9, 10]. In contrast, prolonged and uninterrupted SB has been associated with increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, largely due to its contribution to elevated body fat, visceral adiposity, dyslipidemia, and systemic inflammation [11, 12]. Additionally, both short (≤ 6 h) and long sleep duration (≥ 8 h) may negatively impact cardiometabolic health by modulating endocrine function, activating the sympathetic nervous system, accelerating atherosclerosis, and amplifying inflammatory responses [13, 14]. However, these findings are largely based on studies that examined each component of 24HMB in isolation, without accounting for their inherent interdependence. Since the total time in a day is fixed at 24 h, an increase in time spent on one behavior necessarily results in a decrease in another [15]. Therefore, it is essential to investigated how the reallocation of time across the full spectrum of 24HMB influences cardiometabolic health.

The first integrated 24HMB guideline was published in Canada in 2016. It provided specific, integrated recommendations for the entire day, including at least 150 min of MVPA per week, 7–8 h of regular sleep, and SB limited to 8 h or less per day [16]. Following this, an increasing number of studies have examined the overall impact of 24HMB on cardiovascular health [17]. Evidence suggests that the adherence to 24HMB guideline is positively associated with improved cardiovascular outcomes [18, 19]. More recently, research has begun to explore how changes in the allocation of time among 24HMB components affect cardiometabolic health, particularly in the middle-aged and older populations [20, 21]. Several studies have indicated that replacing SB with MVPA is associated with the improvements in various cardiometabolic outcomes. However, the magnitude of these effects has varied across studies [21, 22]. In contrast, the effects of reallocating time from SB to other behaviors, such as LPA or sleep, remain less clear. While some studies indicated that the substituting SB with LPA or sleep may confer modest benefits for cardiometabolic health in the middle-aged and older adults, these associations are generally weaker than those observed with MVPA [22, 23]. Furthermore, other studies have reported that such substitution produce insignificant or even adverse effects [21, 24, 25]. These inconsistencies may be attributes to variations in participants’ characteristics, methods of assessing 24HMB, and analytical approaches, all of which complicate the interpretation of findings. Therefore, a systematic review is warranted to synthesize current evidence on the effects of time reallocations among 24HMB on cardiometabolic health outcomes.

A systemic review to date has summarized the associations between 24HMB compositions and cardiometabolic biomarkers [26]. While it supported a positive relationship between MVPA and cardiometabolic health, it did not include a meta-analysis and failed to quantify the associations (e.g., effect sizes or regression coefficients), particularly with regard to the impact of reallocating time from one behavior to another. Therefore, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to address these gaps. Our objective was to synthesize existing evidence on the association between 24HMB and cardiometabolic health, and to quantify the effects of different behavioral substitutions patterns on cardiometabolic outcomes using performing meta-analyses.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline [27]. The protocol has been registered in PROPERO (CRD42024565071).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was independently conducted by two authors from database inception to August 2024. Seven electronic databases were searched: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, ProQuest, Web of Science, CINAHL and SPORTDiscus. The search strategy mainly included terms related to 24HMB, physical activity, SB, sleep and cardiometabolic health. The detailed search strategy is provided in Table S1. To ensure comprehensive coverage, the reference lists of systematic reviews published in the past three years were also manually screened for additional relevant studies.

Study selection

Study selection was independently performed by two authors. Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (a) Participants were adults or older adults; (b) All observational and intervention studies were eligible if they reported associations between 24HMB and cardiometabolic health indicators; (c) The exposure of interest was 24HMB (measured by self-report or wearable accelerometers), including physical activity, SB, and sleep; (d) The outcomes included at least one of the following cardiometabolic health markers: anthropometry indicators (BMI, waist circumference or hip circumference), blood pressure (systolic blood pressure (SBP) or diastolic blood pressure (DBP)), blood glucose indicators (insulin level, blood glucose, or Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)), blood lipids (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), triglycerides, or total cholesterol). Studies were excluded if they: (a) lacked extractable data; (b) focused on higher-education students (such as university or college students, regardless of age), whose 24HMB patterns are strongly shaped by academic schedules and therefore not generalizable to the broader adult population; (c) paid attention to pregnant women, given the unique physiological changes during pregnancy. Additionally, in cases where multiple articles reported overlapping results from the same cohort, only the study with the larger sample size was retained.

Data extraction

Relevant data was extracted independently by two authors using a standardized table developed by our research team. The following information was collected: (a) study characteristics (first author, publication year, study design, and setting); (b) participant characteristics (sample size, age, and gender distribution); (c) exposure (methods of measuring 24HMB); (d) outcomes (cardiometabolic health indicators and the corresponding effect sizes associated with 24HMB).

Quality assessment

The risk of bias was independently assessed by two authors using appropriate tools based on study design. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was applied to evaluate the quality of cohort studies [28], while the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist was used for assessing cross-sectional studies [29]. The NOS consists of eight items scored on a nine-point scale across three domains: selection, comparability, and outcome. The JBI checklist includes eight specific questions, with each item rated as “Yes” if the criterion was met, “No” if not met, or “Unclear” if insufficient information was provided.

Statistical analyses

Meta-analyses were conducted when more than three studies reported the effects of 24HMB time reallocations using isotemporal substitution on the same outcome [30]. To minimize variability arising from differences in substitution durations, only studies that applied a 30-minute isotemporal substitution were included in the meta-analyses. A random-effects model was fitted using the restricted maximum likelihood estimator to pool the effect size (β) and its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) [31]. The generic inverse-variance method was applied, which is appropriate for pooling regression coefficients. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values categorized as low (< 25%), moderate (25–75%) or high (> 75%) [32]. All meta-analyses were performed using the metafor package in R (version 4.3.1), a comprehensive and widely used tool for synthesizing various types of effect sizes [33]. Although we initially planned to assess publication bias, this was not undertaken because each meta-analysis included fewer than ten studies. Under these conditions, formal statistical tests and funnel plots lack sufficient power and would yield unreliable results. Regarding subgroup analyses, due to the number of included studies for each specific outcome was very small and not enough to ensure robust and meaningful subgroup analyses. Therefore, to avoid potentially misleading results, we did not perform subgroup analyses.

Results

Study selection

A total of 4419 records were identified after removing duplicates, and 88 studies were deemed potentially relevant based on the initial screening of titles and abstracts. Following full-text review, twenty-one articles published between 2013 and 2024 met the inclusion criteria. Detailed information on the study selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

The twenty-one included studies encompassed a total of 62,056 participants, with individual study sample sizes ranging from 75 to 15,253 [18, 20–24, 34, 36–49]. Most study populations were from European and North American countries, and no restrictions were placed on participants’ health status. Among the included studies, eight targeted the adult population, defined as individuals aged 18 to 59 years, and all employed a cross-sectional design [21, 22, 34, 36–40]. Six studies specifically focused on older adults, defined as those aged 60 years or older (four cross-sectional and two cohort studies) [18, 23, 41–44]. A further seven studies included participants spanning both adult and older adult age groups (six cross-sectional and one cohort studies) [20, 24, 45–49]. Among the twenty-one included studies, the majority (n = 19) used device-based measures (accelerometers or multi-sensor monitors). However, five of these device-based studies supplemented the sleep duration data only with self-reported measures. The remaining two studies relied on questionnaires to measure all 24HMB components. Regarding analytical approaches, several studies employed regression analyses to assess the associations between individual movement behaviors or adherence to 24HMB guidelines and cardiometabolic outcomes. The majority, however, used isotemporal substitution models. One study utilized cluster analysis to group participants and conducted comparisons based on the identified clusters (Table 1; Fig. 2). Meta-analyses were performed on eight studies that used 30-minute time reallocations as the substitution unit and provided extractable data. Specifically, a meta-analysis was conducted on seven studies for BMI [21, 23, 24, 37, 42, 45, 48], six for waist circumference [21, 23, 24, 42, 45, 46], three for blood pressure [23, 24, 46], three for fasting blood glucose [23, 46, 48], three for HDL [23, 46, 48], and three for triglycerides [23, 46, 48]. Among the studies we included for the meta-analysis, except for one study that measured sleep duration solely through questionnaires [45], all the other studies employed a device-based approach to measure 24HMB.

Quality assessment

Among the eighteen cross-sectional studies, four studies did not clearly specify the criteria for sample inclusion. Three studies were rated as having a high risk in exposure measurement due to the use of self-reported sleep measurement. With respect to confounding factors, two studies failed to adjust for potential confounders, while another three studies were rated as unclear because they did not report on confounding factors that could impact cardiometabolic health outcomes. Additionally, two studies did not utilize appropriate methods to address confounding factors. For all other quality assessment items, the cross-sectional studies generally demonstrated a low risk of bias. Among the three cohort studies, two studies showed a high risk of bias regarding the representativeness of the exposed cohort, and none reported how the non-exposed cohort was selected. Detailed results of the quality assessment are presented in Fig. 3, Table S2.

Associations between 24HMB and cardiometabolic health outcomes

Associations between 24HMB and anthropometry

BMI was reported as an outcome in sixteen studies [18, 21, 23, 24, 34, 36–43, 45, 47, 48], including specific populations such as individuals with metabolic syndrome [23], patients with type 2 diabetes [41, 48], and women post-breast cancer surgery [45]. Three studies found that a higher number of behaviors meeting the 24HMB guideline recommendations was associated with lower BMI [18, 39, 40]. Fourteen studies reported significant associations between 24HMB time-use compositions and BMI, using either regression analyses [21, 23, 24, 34, 36–38, 41, 42, 45, 47, 48] or isotemporal substitution models [21, 23, 24, 34, 36–38, 41–43, 45, 47, 48]. Regression analyses showed that higher MVPA levels and lower SB levels were associated with lower BMI. Isotemporal substitution models consistently indicated that replacing SB with MVPA was associated with lower BMI in most studies. Additionally, over half of the studies suggested that substituting SB with LPA or sleep was also associate with lower BMI. Detailed results are presented in Table 2, Figure S1. A meta-analysis involving seven studies examined BMI outcomes [21, 23, 24, 37, 42, 45, 48]. Substituting 30 min of SB with MVPA [21, 23, 24, 37, 42, 48] (β = −0.53, 95% CI −0.81 to −0.26, P<0.001, I2 = 92.8%), or with sleep [23, 24, 48] (β = −0.13, 95% CI −0.19 to −0.07, P<0.001, I2 = 0%), was significantly associated with lower BMI. Likewise, substituting 30 min of sleep with MVPA [21, 24, 42, 45, 48] (β = −0.31, 95% CI −0.46 to −0.15, P<0.001, I2 = 48.5%), was also associated with lower BMI. Conversely, replacing 30 min of MVPA with sleep [24, 42, 48] (β = 0.32, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.37, P<0.001, I2 = 0%) was associated with higher BMI. Replacing 30 min of MVPA with SB [24, 42, 48] (β = 0.55, 95% CI −0.05 to 1.16, P = 0.07, I2 = 54.9%) was linked to higher BMI, although this association did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4).

Fifteen studies included waist circumference as an outcome [18, 20–24, 34, 36, 39–42, 45, 46, 49], including specific populations such as individuals with type 2 diabetes [20, 41, 49], metabolic syndrome [23], and women post-breast cancer surgery [45). Three studies found that meeting at least one component of the 24HMB guidelines was associated with lower waist circumference [18, 39, 40]. Twelve studies reported a significant association between 24HMB time-use composition and waist circumference, based on regression analyses [21, 23, 24, 34, 36, 41, 42, 46, 49] or isotemporal substitution models [20–24, 34, 36, 41, 42, 45, 46, 49] indicated that higher levels of MVPA levels were associated with lower waist circumference. Replacing SB, LPA, or sleep with MVPA was also linked to lower waist circumference (Table 2). Substituting SB with sleep also linked with lower waist circumference in over half of the studies. However, the associations of other substitution patterns on waist circumference were inconsistent. Detailed results regarding the associations between 24HMB and waist circumference are presented in Table 2, Figure S2. A meta-analysis of six studies [21, 23, 24, 42, 45, 46] that conducted 30-minute isotemporal substitution analyses showed that replacing 30 min of SB with MVPA [21, 23, 24, 42, 46] (β = −1.42, 95% CI −2.19 to −0.65, P<0.001, I2 = 95.9%), LPA [21, 24, 42] (β = −0.40, 95% CI −0.57 to −0.22, P<0.001, I2 = 0%), or sleep [21, 24, 42] (β = −0.64, 95% CI −1.19 to −0.10, P = 0.02, I2 = 72.6%) was significantly associated with lower waist circumference (Fig. 4). Additionally, replacing 30 min of sleep with MVPA [21, 24, 42, 45] (β = −1.46, 95% CI −2.37 to −0.55, P <0.05, I2 = 87.8%) also was associated with lower waist circumference.

Associations between 24HMB and blood pressure

Nine studies investigated blood pressure outcomes [18, 23, 24, 34, 38–40, 44, 46], including specific populations such as individuals with metabolic syndrome [23]. Only two studies adjusted for the use of antihypertensive medications in their analyses [23, 34]. Three studies examined the relations between adherence to 24HMB guideline and blood pressure, and found that meeting at least one of the 24HMB guidelines was associated with a lower risk of hypertension. (Table 1) [18, 39, 40]. Five studies reported significant associations between 24HMB time-use composition and blood pressure, based on regression analyses [23, 24, 34, 38, 46] and isotemporal substitution models [23, 24, 34, 38, 46]. However, the regression analyses revealed no significant associations between 24HMB components and blood pressure in most studies. Detailed results are presented in Table 2, Figure S3 ~ S4. A meta-analysis of three studies employing isotemporal substitution models showed that none of the time reallocations led to significant changes in either SBP and DBP (Fig. 4) [23, 24, 46]. In addition, one study explored the association between clustered 24HMB patterns and clinic-measured blood pressure in older adults with chronic diseases [44]. Individuals in the “active and non-sedentary” group exhibited lower systolic blood pressure.

Associations between 24HMB and blood glucose

Nine studies examined blood glucose related outcomes, including fasting glucose, fasting insulin and HbA1C levels [20, 22, 23, 34, 37, 40, 42, 46, 48], including specific populations such as individuals with metabolic syndrome [23] and type 2 diabetes [20, 48]. Only three studies adjusted for the use of antidiabetic medications in their analyses [23, 34, 48]. One study assessed the relationship between adherence to the 24HMB guideline and type 2 diabetes, reporting that not meeting any of the 24HMB components was associated with higher odds of type 2 diabetes [40]. Eight studies reported significant association between 24HMB time-use compositions and fasting blood glucose based on either regression analyses [23, 34, 37, 42, 48] or isotemporal substitution models [20, 22, 23, 34, 37, 42, 46, 48] (Table 2). Regression analyses indicated that increased engagement in LPA was associated with lower fasting insulin levels. Isotemporal substitution analyses further demonstrated that replacing SB with either MVPA or LPA was associated with lower fasting glucose levels in some studies. Additionally, substituting SB with MVPA was negatively associated with HbA1c levels. Detailed findings are presented in Table 2, Figure S5 ~ S7. However, a meta-analysis of three studies employing isotemporal substitution models found that none of the substitution patterns resulted in statistically significant changes in fasting glucose levels (Fig. 4) [23, 46, 48].

Associations between 24HMB and blood lipids

Ten studies investigated blood lipid outcome [21–23, 34, 37, 40, 41, 46–48], including specifically targeting individuals with type 2 diabetes [41, 48] and metabolic syndrome [23]. One study explored the association between adherence to the 24HMB guideline and triglycerides levels, finding that participants who met only one component of the guideline had higher odds of elevated triglycerides compared to those meeting all three components [40]. Nine studies examined the association between 24HMB time-use composition and blood lipids using either regression analyses [21, 23, 34, 37, 41, 47, 48] or isotemporal substitution models [21, 23, 34, 37, 41, 46, 48] (Table 2). The results from regression analyses revealed inconsistent associations between 24HMB and triglyceride levels. No significant associations were found between 24HMB and, HDL, LDL, or total cholesterol in most studies. However, five studies showed that replacing SB with MVPA was associated with increased HDL levels. Details results on the association between 24HMB and blood lipids are presented in Table 2, Figure S8 ~ S11. Our meta-analysis of three studies indicated that replacing SB with MVPA was significantly associated with higher HDL levels (β = 0.02, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.03, P<0.001, I2 = 19.4%) [23, 46, 48] and lower triglycerides levels (β = −0.06, 95% CI −0.11 to −0.01, P = 0.023, I2 = 78.4%) [23, 46, 48]. No other significant associations were observed (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study systematically reviewed the existing evidence and examined the association between 24HMB and the cardiometabolic health indicators, drawing on twenty-one studies comprising a total of 62,056 participants. Overall, the regression analyses indicated that longer time spent in MVPA was consistently associated with lower BMI, waist circumference, fasting insulin, and triglycerides levels, whereas greater time in SB was linked to higher BMI. Associations with LPA were less consistent, although longer LPA duration was related to lower fasting insulin levels. In contrast, the associations between sleep duration and cardiometabolic health outcomes were mostly non-significant. Our meta-analysis confirmed that substituting either SB or sleep with MVPA was associated with lower BMI and waist circumference. Notably, substituting SB with MVPA was further linked to favorable changes in triglycerides and HDL levels. In contrast, substituting SB with sleep or LPA showed only modest associations with lower BMI or lower waist circumference. Additionally, stronger adherence to the 24HMB guidelines was generally associated with more favorable cardiometabolic outcomes, including lower BMI, smaller waist circumference, reduced triglyceride levels, and lower risks of obesity and hypertension.

The quality assessment of the included studies revealed several methodological limitations. First, the majority of studies did not adequately report key characteristics of the study population or specify exclusion criteria, limiting the generalizability and interpretability of their findings. Second, although the use of objective measurement methods to assess 24HMB is an advantage in many studies, differences in equipment types, wearing time specifications, and data processing algorithms across various studies may have led heterogeneity in exposure measurements. Additionally, several studies did not adequately control for confounding factors that could have influenced cardiometabolic outcomes. To enhance the rigor and applicability of future research, studies should clearly define and report inclusion and exclusion criteria, along with detailed participant characteristics, to facilitate accurate interpretation and ensure relevance to specific populations. Furthermore, future studies are encouraged to adopt objective methods with standardized protocols, or combine them with validated self-report tools to provide complementary insights and improve the comprehensiveness of 24HMB assessments. Given that blood pressure, glucose, and lipid levels are influenced by factors such as existing medical conditions, medication use, and dietary habits, it is essential that future studies comprehensively account for these potential confounders when examining associations with cardiometabolic health outcomes.

Regarding anthropometric outcomes, our meta-analysis demonstrated that replacing SB and sleep with MVPA was consistently associated with lower BMI and waist circumference. Additionally, substituting SB with either LPA or sleep was linked to smaller waist circumference, while substituting SB with sleep was associated with lower BMI. These findings offer multiple viable strategies for improving elevated BMI and waist circumference. While most previous studies have primarily emphasized increasing physical activities to reduce these anthropometric indicators, relatively few have specifically targeted reductions in SB [50, 51]. Notably, for older adults, reducing SB may represent a more feasible and acceptable behavioral target due to their physical limitations and lower receptiveness to engaging in MVPA [52].

Our meta-analysis did not find a significant association between 24HMB and blood pressure. Several factors may account for this lack of significance. First, blood pressure is highly sensitive to a wide range of variables, including measurement techniques, use of antihypertensive medications, lifestyle behaviors, and underlying health conditions [53]. Notably, most of the studies included in our analysis did not adequately adjust for these confounding factors, which may have contributed to the non-significant findings. Furthermore, blood pressure naturally fluctuates throughout the day, and variations in measurement timing can significantly impact the results [54]. To reduce measurement error and more accurately assess the relationship between 24HMB and blood pressure, the use of 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring is recommended. Although our meta-analysis did not yield statistically significant results, two individual studies suggested that replacing SB with LPA was associated with a reduction in blood pressure [22, 34]. This association may be attributed to the adjustment for covariates such as a history of hypertension and the use of antihypertensive medications in those studies. Additionally, recent research from the ProPASS Consortium indicated that spending more time engaging in physical activity or sleep was associated with lower blood pressure [35]. Alterations in the composition of 24HMB time use were significantly associated with HbA1C and fasting insulin levels. However, the relationship between reallocating 24HMB time and fasting blood glucose remained non-significant, likely due to the influence of various confounding factors such as the use of hypoglycemic medication and differences in diabetes type [55, 56]. Additionally, the inherent day-to-day biological variability of fasting glucose, combined with methodological differences across studies in fasting duration, protocols, and blood-draw timing, could have introduced measurement errors that obscured potential associations. Accordingly, future studies investigating the association between 24HMB and blood glucose regulation should carefully account for critical confounders, including diabetes type and antidiabetic treatment. It is noteworthy that HbA1c, as a longer-term marker of glycemic control, offers greater stability and may serve as a more reliable indicator of the relationship between 24HMB patterns and glucose regulation over time compared to fasting glucose levels [57]. A notable gap in the current evidence base is the lack of research examining the association between 24HMB and postprandial blood glucose. While studies focusing on physical activity suggest that post-meal activity may favorably influence postprandial glucose, it remains unknown how the integrated allocation of all 24HMB components across the day influences these glycemic excursions [58]. Therefore, future research should investigate the impact of time-specific movement behavior substitutions on glycemic outcomes, with particular emphasis on postprandial glucose responses.

Our results revealed that replacing SB with MVPA has a beneficial association on HDL and triglyceride levels [59]. Similar to fasting glucose, triglyceride levels are also sensitive to recent dietary intake and fasting duration; variations in these pre-analytical conditions across studies may have contributed to the heterogeneity in findings. Further regression analyses suggest that these improvements are primarily attributable to the increase in MVPA, rather than the decrease in SB itself. This conclusion is consistent with previous studies, which have reported minimal or non-significant effects of SB reduction alone on HDL and triglyceride levels. In fact, a prior meta-analysis also failed to detect statistically significant effects of SB reduction on blood lipids [60]. These findings suggest that simply reducing SB may not be sufficient to elicit meaningful improvements in lipid profiles. Instead, increasing levels of physical activity—particularly MVPA—appears to play a more critical role in managing blood lipid levels effectively.

In our review, the associations between sleep and cardiometabolic outcomes were mostly insignificant, which contrasts with finding from previous evidence [8]. One possible explanation is the nonlinear nature of the relationship between sleep and cardiometabolic health [61]. Critically, most studies treated sleep as a continuous variable without examining the potential non-linear relationships. However, most of the studies included in our review did not differentiate between short and long sleep durations, potentially obscuring the effects of sleep on cardiometabolic outcomes. Furthermore, sleep quality (beyond duration), also plays a critical role in influencing cardiometabolic health. To more accurately assess the impact of sleep on these outcomes, future research should stratify analyses by sleep duration categories (e.g., short, adequate, and long sleep) and incorporate measures of sleep quality. This approach will allow for a more nuanced understanding of how different sleep patterns influence cardiometabolic risk.

Our study has several limitations. First, all included studies were observational, which precludes drawing causal inferences between 24HMB reallocations and cardiometabolic outcomes. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed across several outcomes in our meta-analyses, likely stemming from methodological inconsistencies among the included studies, such as differences in population characteristics, measurement approaches or definitions for 24HMB components, and analytic strategies. Specifically, heterogeneity in participants’ ages and baseline health status may contribute to variability in the estimated associations between 24HMB time allocations and cardiometabolic outcomes. Furthermore, divergent measurements and definitions for 24HMB components across studies (e.g., different definitions for 24HMB and varying intensity thresholds for MVPA) pose challenges to the synthesis and interpretation of evidence across studies. Inconsistent analytical approaches (e.g., traditional isotemporal substitution models and compositional data analysis) may further limit the comparability of effect estimates across studies, thereby influencing the robustness of the pooled meta-analytic results. Besides, inconsistency in adjustment for covariates such as diet, comorbidities, and medication use (Tables S3 ~ S8) could compromise the comparability of effect estimates. Third, the generalizability of our findings to specific subgroups may be limited, given the underrepresentation of populations such as the oldest-old adults and individuals with complex health profiles (e.g., metabolic syndrome or type 2 diabetes) in the current literatures. In addition, the limited number of eligible primary studies prevented subgroup analyses and restricted the investigate on of potential non-linear relationships between sleep duration and outcomes. Consequently, possible variations in outcomes related to these factors could not be examined.

Our study offers important implications for both research and clinical practice. From a research perspective, future researchers should focus on developing integrated 24HMB intervention programs and designing behavioral substitution strategies among 24HMB components to maximize health benefits and improve the feasibility of interventions. Priority should be given to populations most likely to benefit from changes in movement behaviors, such as individuals capable of MVPA but not meeting the recommendations. Future research should also examine clinically relevant indicators that remain under-studied, such as postprandial glucose and 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure. Furthermore, to address the heterogeneity observed in this review, future studies should adopt standardized protocols for objective 24HMB assessment and apply consistent adjustment for key confounders, including medication use. From a clinical standpoint, healthcare professionals should adopt a holistic view of 24HMB, emphasizing the importance of balanced time allocation across movement behaviors. This includes encouraging individuals to reduce SB by substituting it with other beneficial activities, or promoting the replacement of various behaviors with MVPA, tailored to individual characteristics and preferences Furthermore, our findings strongly support the integration of the comprehensive 24HMB framework into national health guidelines, moving beyond fragmented, behavior-specific recommendations. Policymakers are encouraged to develop evidence-based, holistic behavioral guidelines grounded in the 24HMB model to optimize cardiometabolic and overall health outcomes at the population level.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that the composition of 24HMB is associated with several key cardiometabolic health indicators. The evidence, derived primarily from time reallocation models, indicates that reallocating time from SB to MVPA is consistently associated with beneficial changes in anthropometric measures (BMI and waist circumference), specific blood glucose markers (HbA1c and fasting insulin), and lipid profiles. In contrast, associations involving reallocations to LPA or sleep were less consistent and generally more modest, and no significant overall association was observed with blood pressure. Overall, our findings imply that increasing MVPA and reducing SB may be promising approaches for improving cardiometabolic health, which deserve further verification in future interventional studies. Furthermore, more studies should be conducted to further clarify the effects of the time reallocations between LPA and sleep on cardiometabolic outcomes. It is essential to fully understand the association of 24HMB with cardiometabolic health, and maximize the cardiometabolic health benefits by promoting the time allocation balance among 24HMB components.
