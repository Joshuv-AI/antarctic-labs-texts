XND1-centered network regulates salt tolerance by integrating root xylem plasticity and Na+ unloading in Arabidopsis
Authors: Bingli Ding, Yafei Shi, Runling Zhang, Mengyu Liang, Xiaohu Sun, Yupu Huang, Christophe Maurel, Ning Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAlthough xylem is the primary conduit for long-distance transport in plants, its potential role in regulating ion homeostasis and salt tolerance has remained largely unexplored. Here, we demonstrate that the transcription factor XND1 (Xylem NAC Domain 1) regulates salt tolerance through actions on xylem structure and function, thereby highlighting the unrecognized importance of xylem in salt stress response. In particular, our findings reveal an XND1-centered network that orchestrates the salt stress response by integrating root xylem developmental plasticity and sodium unloading. These findings establish a previously unrecognized genetic link between these two processes and provide insights for developing salt-tolerant crops.

Soil salinity is an increasingly severe problem that adversely affects global crop production (1–3). Salt can induce osmotic stress, leading to a reduction of water uptake, as well as ion toxicity caused primarily by the excessive accumulation of sodium ions (Na+), thereby disturbing Na+/potassium (K+) homeostasis in plants (4, 5). Plants have developed mechanisms to control Na+ concentrations, including regulations of ion uptake and transport (2, 6, 7).

In particular, plants encode a family of Na+-transporters called HKT (High-affinity K+ Transporters), which play a crucial role in the protection from excessive Na+ accumulation by facilitating the removal of Na+ from xylem, primarily in roots (8–11). HKT1 was first recognized in wheat as a high-affinity K+/Na+ cotransporter (12, 13). In Arabidopsis, AtHKT1 is the only member of the HKT family and has been identified as a Na+ transporter that contributes to salt tolerance by controlling root/shoot Na+ distribution (8, 14, 15). In the root stele, AtHKT1 restricts Na+ delivery to aerial parts, principally by unloading Na+ from the xylem to parenchyma cells, thereby enhancing Na+ exclusion and plant salt tolerance (11, 16). Additionally, AtHKT1 is considered to be involved in Na+ recirculation, potentially by facilitating the loading of Na+ into the phloem within the shoots (17). AtHKT1 has also been reported to play a protective role in plant fertility by reducing Na+ accumulation in stamen filaments. Thus, AtHKT1 plays diverse roles in regulating salt tolerance in plants (18, 19).

The regulation of AtHKT1 involves a potentially intricate mechanism. Expression of AtHKT1 is negatively regulated by small RNA-mediated DNA methylation in its promoter region (20). In addition, a Calmodulin-binding protein CAMTA6 induces the expression of AtHKT1 in response to salt stress, although the precise regulatory mechanism remains to be determined (21). AGL16, a MADS-box transcription factor, directly binds to the promoter of AtHKT1 and represses its expression to negatively regulate salt stress response (22). Moreover, the activity and stability of AtHKT1 under salt stress can be modulated through direct interactions with PP2C49 and SOS3 (23, 24). In crops, regulators of HKT transporters have been demonstrated as crucial components of salt tolerance and promising breeding targets (25, 26).

Root xylem formation of Arabidopsis exhibits a notable plasticity in response to environmental cues (27). Water deficit triggers ABA signaling to activate VASCULAR RELATED NAC DOMAIN (VND) transcription factors, and promote changes in both root xylem cell fate and differentiation rate (28–30). The structure properties of xylem vessels, which function as conduits responsible for long-distance ion transport, can significantly affect salt uptake and translocation in plants (27). Salt stress inhibits gibberellin signaling, leading to discontinuous differentiation and consequently gaps within the protoxylem. Incomplete root xylem formation can therefore impede Na+ transport and confer an advantage for plants under salt stress (31). More specifically, VND6 is involved in the formation of protoxylem gaps under salt stress, by an as yet unknown mechanism (31). These effects are possibly supported by VNI2 (VND-INTERACTING 2), a NAC transcription factor that regulates root xylem development through interaction with VNDs, and is induced by high salinity in an ABA-dependent manner (32–35).

The xylem-specific transcription factor XYLEM NAC DOMAIN 1 (XND1) plays a crucial role in negatively regulating xylem differentiation, primarily through interactions with diverse proteins (36–39). XND1 facilitates plant resistance against vascular pathogens by mediating flagellin-induced xylem plastic development (40). In contrast, it exerts a negative impact on plant drought response, likely attributed to its inhibitory effects on root water transport (40). The possible role of XND1 under other abiotic stress conditions remains, however, largely unexplored. In this study, we investigated the role of XND1 in root xylem plasticity in response to salinity, and uncovered an unexpected involvement in the regulation of HKT1-mediated Na+ unloading. Thus, this work uncovers a central molecular link that integrates xylem plasticity and Na+ unloading under salt stress.

Results

XND1 Positively Impacts Salt Stress Tolerance.

Analyses of XND1 promoter-driven GUS lines and reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) assays revealed that, in roots, XND1 is mostly expressed in the stele and further induced in response to a salt treatment (100 mM and 200 mM NaCl) (Fig. 1 A and B). To explore the potential regulatory role of XND1 in plant response to salt stress, we analyzed three previously described allelic T-DNA insertion mutants: xnd1-3 and xnd1-5 as loss-of-function alleles, and xnd1-4 as a gain-of-function allele (40). Soil-grown seedlings of the mutant and wild type (Col-0) lines were subjected to a 200 mM NaCl treatment. While all genotypes exhibited similar growth performance under normal conditions, xnd1-3 and xnd1-5 showed under salt treatment an enhanced etiolation compared to Col-0, whereas the gain-of-function allele xnd1-4 showed reduced etiolation (Fig. 1C). The quantification of Soil Plant Analysis Development (SPAD) values and plant survival, consistently indicated that under plant exposure to salt, both chlorophyll content and survival rates were significantly decreased in xnd1-3 and xnd1-5 compared to Col-0, while they were notably increased in xnd1-4 (Fig. 1 D and E). Furthermore, under salt treatment, the accumulation of Na+ in the shoots was significantly higher in xnd1-3 and xnd1-5 compared to Col-0 and xnd1-4 (Fig. 1F). The overall data suggest that XND1 exerts a protective role during plant salt stress response, potentially through inhibition of Na+ accumulation.

XND1 Mediates the Formation of Protoxylem Discontinuities in Roots Under Salt Stress.

Given that the expression of XND1 is salt-inducible (Fig. 1 A and B), we investigated its potential role in root xylem plasticity under salt stress. Under normal conditions and in all genotypes investigated, protoxylem vessels were continuous over root length (Fig. 2 A and B). By contrast, protoxylem discontinuities, characterized by noticeable gaps that are believed to hinder Na+ transport and thereby enhance plant tolerance to salt stress (31), were observed in both Col-0 and xnd1-4 seedling roots, following a 48-h exposure to 150 mM NaCl. By comparison, the formation of such gaps was significantly reduced in xnd1-3 and xnd1-5 (Fig. 2 A and B). Our findings confirm that salinity triggers discontinuous protoxylem differentiation. XND1 appears to play a pivotal role in this process, potentially contributing to the inhibition of long-distance Na+ transport and to a reduced accumulation of Na+ in aerial tissues of plants under salt stress.

XND1 Directly Targets the Sodium Transporter HKT1.

AtHKT1 (hereafter referred to as HKT1) is a vital Na+ transporter that is expressed in the vasculature and affects long-distance Na+ transport by facilitating the unloading of Na+ from the root xylem into adjacent parenchymal cells (11). Here, we examined the expression levels of HKT1 in Col-0 and xnd1 alleles under normal and salt stress conditions. In the former conditions, expression of HKT1 was similar in all genotypes. Under salt stress, expression of HKT1 in Col-0 was markedly (5.3-fold) induced by salt stress. Yet, this induction was significantly higher in xnd1-4 compared to Col-0, while it was substantially decreased in xnd1-3 and xnd1-5 (Fig. 3A). To further examine the potential regulatory role of XND1 on HKT1 expression, we introduced a HKT1pro:GUS construct into Col-0, xnd1-4, and xnd1-5 plants. GUS staining in plants under salt stress revealed, with respect to Col-0, an increased expression in xnd1-4, contrasting with a reduced expression in xnd1-5 (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The results confirm that XND1 plays an essential role in the transcriptional regulation of HKT1 expression in response to salt stress.

A potential interaction between XND1 and the HKT1 promoter was investigated using a Y1H assay and supported by growth of transformed yeast under selective conditions (Fig. 3C). Direct binding of XND1 to the promoter region of HKT1 was then explored through EMSA (Fig. 3D). More specifically, the addition of XND1 induced a mobility shift of labeled probes corresponding to the XCP1 promoter region, which contains binding sites for XND1 (41). In addition, mobility shift was markedly inhibited by unlabeled probes. The overall data establish that XND1 binds to the SNBE (secondary wall NAC binding element) motif of the HKT1 promoter fragment, located approximately from −964 to −945 base pairs relative to the start codon (Fig. 3D). In addition, the regulatory effects of XND1 on HKT1 were analyzed using luciferase reporter assays in Arabidopsis protoplasts and N. benthamiana leaves (Fig. 3 E–G). The activity of HKT1 promoter was significantly enhanced in the presence of XND1 (Fig. 3 F and G). These findings substantiate the idea that XND1 directly binds to the promoter of HKT1 and activates its transcription.

To investigate the potential functional interplay between XND1 and HKT1 in response to salt stress, plants carrying a loss-of-function allele of HKT1 (hkt1) were crossed with the gain-of-function xnd1-4 allele to generate a double mutant. Seedlings of the single and double mutant lines and the wild type (Col-0) were subjected to NaCl-induced salt stress on agar plates. The germination rates, seedling establishment, and growth were significantly decreased in hkt1 and xnd1-4 hkt1 compared to Col-0, while they were notably increased in xnd1-4 (Fig. 3 H–M). The results indicate that XND1 functions upstream of HKT1, thereby supporting the hypothesis that XND1 enhances HKT1-mediated Na+ unloading, in addition to discontinuous protoxylem differentiation to favor plant acclimation to salt stress.

MYBS2 Interacts with XND1 to Antagonistically Regulate HKT1.

To identify regulators associated with XND1 during plant salt stress response, we performed a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screen and uncovered a robust interaction between XND1 and MYBS2 (Fig. 4A), a salt stress-regulated MYB transcription factor (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). This interaction was further validated through GST pull-down (Fig. 4B), split firefly luciferase complementation (Fig. 4C), and coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays (Fig. 4D). The results collectively demonstrate that XND1 physically interacts with MYBS2. While the two proteins showed colocalization within the nucleus (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), XND1 was also observed in the cytoplasm, in agreement with previous studies (39).

To further investigate the potential roles of MYBS2 in the salt stress response, we collected two loss-of-function T-DNA insertion lines of MYBS2 (mybs2-1 and mybs2-2) and developed transgenic lines overexpressing MYBS2 under the control of the CaMV35S promoter (Fig. 5A). The salt tolerance of corresponding soil-grown seedlings was investigated. By reference to Col-0, mybs2-1 and mybs2-2 showed, under salt stress, reduced etiolation and higher chlorophyll content (Fig. 5 B and C) whereas the overexpression lines showed an opposite phenotype. In addition, the survival rates of the overexpression lines were notably decreased compared to those of Col-0 and mybs2 mutants (Fig. 5D). Consistently, the accumulation of Na+ in the shoots was significantly reduced in mybs2-1 and mybs2-2, but enhanced in the overexpression lines (Fig. 5E). The overall findings indicate that MYBS2 negatively acts on salt stress tolerance.

Furthermore, EMSA using the HKT1 promoter showed that, while the addition of XND1 caused a pronounced mobility shift of the labeled probes, a reduced mobility shift was observed upon addition of MYBS2 (Fig. 5F). Yet, MYBS2 alone was not able to bind to the probe. (Fig. 5F). The data suggest that MYBS2 attenuated the binding of XND1 to the promoter of HKT1 and thereby antagonistically regulates XND1-dependent expression of HKT1.

The antagonistic effects of XND1 and MYBS2 on HKT1 expression were further analyzed through luciferase reporter assays in Arabidopsis protoplasts and N. benthamiana leaves. We observed that the enhanced activity of the HKT1 promoter observed upon expression of XND1 was notably reduced when MYBS2 was cointroduced into the system (Fig. 5 G–I). Collectively, the data suggest that the negative effects of MYBS2 on plant stress tolerance may be due in part to its physical interaction with XND1 which in turn antagonistically regulates HKT1-mediated xylem unloading.

XND1 Is Activated by VNI2 in Response to Salt Stress.

To investigate additional regulators of XND1, we conducted a yeast one hybrid screen using a 2091 bp XND1 promoter fragment that can be activated under salt stress, as shown in luciferase reporter assays (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The screening revealed three potential transcriptional regulators (SI Appedix, Table S1), including VNI2, a NAC transcription factor known to be up-regulated in response to salt stress (Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A) (33). EMSA indicated that VNI2 directly binds to two SNBE motifs (SNBE1, SNBE2) present in the XND1 promoter (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, luciferase reporter assays conducted in Arabidopsis protoplasts and N. benthamiana leaves revealed that the promoter of XND1 was significantly activated by VNI2 (Fig. 6 C–E). These findings indicate that VNI2 directly interacts with the XND1 promoter region, thereby activating its transcription.

The regulatory functions of VNI2 were further investigated, using vni2 loss-of-function mutants, as generated via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing, and transgenic lines overexpressing VNI2 under the CaMV35S promoter (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B and C). These lines first revealed a positive effect of VNI2 on expression of XND1 under control condition (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). Single (vni2) and double (vni2 xnd1-4) mutant lines as well as VNI2 overexpression lines were subjected to a NaCl treatment on agar plates or in soil (Fig. 6 F–H and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). In accordance with previous studies (33), the germination and cotyledon greening rates under salt stress on agar plates were significantly lower in vni2 loss-of-function lines compared to both Col-0 and VNI2 overexpression lines (Fig. 6 F–H and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B and C). The chlorophyll content, in soil-grown plants under salt stress, was significantly reduced in the vni2 mutant lines but increased in the overexpression lines relative to the Col-0 control (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D and E). Notably, the germination rate and cotyledon greening in the vni2 xnd1-4 double mutant showed a significant improvement compared to the vni2 single mutant, suggesting that enhanced expression of XND1 in xnd1-4 can mitigate the defects of vni2 in the salt stress response (Fig. 6 F–H). The collective data demonstrate that VNI2 activates XND1, thereby enhancing plant tolerance to salt stress.

OsXND1 Positively Impacts Salt Stress Tolerance in Rice.

We further analyzed the phylogenetic relationships between NAC transcription factors in Arabidopsis and rice, and identified potential orthologs of XND1 in rice (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). OsNAC028 and OsNAC079 are two rice homologs of XND1, exhibiting 51.46 and 50.74% protein sequence identity, respectively. RT-qPCR assays revealed that both genes were mainly expressed in roots and induced by salt treatments, with OsNAC028 showing a notably higher level of induction (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A–D). We further explored the potentially conserved role of OsNAC028, which was designated as OsXND1 due to its high sequence identity and functional resemblance to XND1, in the context of salt stress response. Analyses of OsXND1 promoter-driven GUS lines confirmed that, similar to Arabidopsis XND1, OsXND1 was predominantly expressed in the root stele and showed enhanced expression under salt exposure, consistent with the RT-qPCR results (Fig. 7A and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B).

(F) A working model showing that the XND1-centered network orchestrates plant salt stress response via integrating root xylem plasticity and Na+ unloading. On one hand, XND1 regulates xylem development-related genes, either directly or indirectly, thereby mediating the formation of root protoxylem gaps resulting from discontinuous differentiation. On the other hand, XND1 directly targets and activates the sodium transporter HKT1, thereby facilitating the removal of Na+ from root xylem. The potential role of OsXND1 in regulating root xylem plasticity and salt stress tolerance was further examined. Transgenic lines overexpressing OsXND1 under the control of the Ubiquitin promoter were generated (SI Appendix, Fig. S8E) and subsequently subjected to salt stress treatment. Under normal conditions, protoxylem vessels were continuous over root length in both the wild-type control and OsXND1 overexpression plants (Fig. 7 B and C). Following a 48-h exposure to 100 mM NaCl, discontinuous differentiation characterized by noticeable gaps was significantly more pronounced in the protoxylem of OsXND1 overexpression seedling roots compared to that of the wild-type control, (Fig. 7 B and C). Furthermore, the overexpression lines showed reduced etiolation and significantly higher survival rates compared to the wild-type control after salt treatments (100, 150, and 200 mM NaCl) (Fig. 7 D and E and SI Appendix, Figs. S8C and S9). The results demonstrate that OsXND1 contributes to salt-induced root xylem plasticity and positively regulates salt stress tolerance in rice, pointing to a conserved function of XND1 in dicot and monocot plants.

Discussion

As the primary conduit for water and ion transport in plants, xylem contributes essentially to the regulation of root hydraulics and drought tolerance (40). However, its specific role in regulating ion homeostasis and salt tolerance remains largely unexplored. Here, we report that the transcription factor XND1 regulates salt tolerance by coordinating xylem developmental plasticity and sodium unloading, highlighting the unrecognized importance of xylem in salt stress response.

XND1 Integrates Xylem Plasticity with HKT1-Mediated Na+ Unloading.

XND1 has long been known as a negative regulator of root xylem development (36, 40). Here, we found that its expression in the root stele was notably induced by salt (Fig. 1 A and B). Importantly, we closely investigated the role of XND1 in xylem development during plant response to salt stress, and revealed that, in these conditions, XND1 critically contributes to the formation of root protoxylem gaps due to a discontinuous differentiation of vessels (Fig. 2). As xylem serves as the primary conduit for long-distance root-to-shoot ion transport, we propose that such discontinuities limit Na+ transport to shoots and potentially confer an advantage for plants under salt stress. An additional key finding was that XND1 directly targets the promoter of sodium transporter HKT1 and positively modulates its expression, thereby enhancing Na+ removal from the root xylem (Fig. 3). Thus, XND1 integrates root xylem developmental plasticity with HKT1-mediated Na+ unloading to positively regulate plant tolerance to salt stress.

These ideas are supported by genetic evidence: Loss-of-function mutants of XND1 (xnd1-3; xnd1-5) or HKT1 (hkt1) showed a lower tolerance to salt stress than wild-type (Col-0) whereas a gain-of-function of XND1 (xnd1-4) showed an enhanced tolerance. In addition, the salt stress tolerance of the xnd1-4 hkt1 double mutant was comparable to that of hkt1, but significantly lower than that of xnd1-4 and Col-0 (Fig. 3 H–M). This suggests that the HKT1-mediated pathway may play a predominant role, at least under our experimental conditions, in the XND1-regulated response to salt stress.

In molecular terms, our work adds an XND1-mediated transcriptional regulation of HKT1 to previously described posttranslational regulation mechanisms (23, 24). Since XND1-induced xylem discontinuities may lead to an accumulation of Na+ in the xylem below the gaps, our findings indicate that it may be critical to enhance HKT1 in the same tissues, to efficiently remove Na+ and prevent its over accumulation. At the whole plant level, XND1 inhibits Na+ accumulation in shoots, thereby conferring enhanced salt tolerance in Arabidopsis (Fig. 1).

Notably, functional characterization of OsXND1 (OsNAC028) in rice indicates the generality of these mechanisms and a regulatory role under salt stress that is conserved across species (Fig. 7). The functional characterization of OsNAC079, an additional rice ortholog, has yet to be determined. Given that the two rice orthologs of XND1 have probably duplicated recently (SI Appendix, Fig. S7) and potentially exhibit functional redundancy, OsNAC079 may also share functional similarities with XND1. It is noteworthy that XND1 may function effectively across a relatively broad range of salt concentrations (Figs. 1, 3, and 7 and SI Appendix, Fig.S9). However, whether its specific role varies under different concentrations remains to be elucidated in future studies.

An XND1-Centered Regulatory Network in Plant Response to Salt Stress.

While a role for the transcription factor MYBS2 in sugar signaling has previously been documented (42), its involvement in the context of salinity came as a surprise. Here, we found that MYBS2 functions as a regulator of salt stress responses, in association with XND1 (Figs. 4–6). MYBS2 functionally interacts with XND1, to antagonistically regulate HKT1-mediated xylem unloading and salinity responses (Figs. 3 and 4). More specifically, DNA binding assays revealed that MYBS2 substantially reduced the capacity of XND1 to bind to its target region in the HKT1 promoter (Fig. 5). By contrast, MYBS2 seems not to bind to this region (Fig. 5), although unexplored promoter regions may be recognized. Thus, we propose that MYBS2 diminishes the binding affinity and subsequent activation role of XND1 on HKT1 expression, thereby negatively regulating xylem Na+ unloading. Whether MYBS2 also antagonizes XND1-dependent regulation of xylem-related genes and thereby xylem development remains to be determined.

In the future, it will be crucial to understand how the two antagonistic regulators, MYBS2 and XND1, can be coordinated to maintain expression of HKT1 at a physiologically appropriate level. While expression of both XND1 and MYBS2 is upregulated by salt (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2), additional translational and posttranslational regulatory mechanisms may also modulate their functions, thereby fine-tuning the expression of HKT1. Since MYBS2 is also involved in the control of glucose-responsive gene expression and potentially plays a role in growth regulation (42), the MYBS2–XND1 interaction might critically contribute to a balance between salt stress responses and plant growth.

Finally, the present work allowed to identify the NAC transcription factor VNI2 as an additional component of the transcriptional cascade involving XND1 (Fig. 6). Previous studies have shown that VNI2 negatively regulates xylem development through its interaction with and inhibition of VND7, a pivotal positive regulator of xylem development (32, 35). Our findings suggest that VNI2 may also exert its function through direct regulation of XND1. We also showed that VNI2 is significantly induced by high salinity conditions, in line with the role of XND1 in salt stress responses (Fig. 6 and SI Appendix, Figs. S5 and S6) (33). Thus, the positive regulatory effect of VNI2 may counteract the inhibitory effect of MYBS2 on XND1 expression to fine-tune the dual effects of the latter during plant salt stress response. Since VNI2 is also responsive to abscisic acid (ABA) (33), the VNI2-XND1 module may help integrate this stress signal in these processes.

In summary, our findings reveal an XND1-centered network that orchestrates plant salt stress response through integration of root xylem developmental plasticity and Na+ unloading, thereby establishing a previously unrecognized genetic link between these two processes (Fig. 7). Given that XND1 is also recognized for its roles in regulating plant responses to drought and vascular pathogens (40), the XND1-centered regulatory network potentially serves as a genetic hub coordinating multiple stress responses. Thus, our work highlights the high significance of regulators of vascular development for plant resilience to diverse environmental challenges and points to important targets for crop improvement.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials.

The Arabidopsis ecotype Columbia-0 (Col-0) was used as the wild-type control in this study. The T-DNA insertional mutants mybs2-1 (SALK_205581), mybs2-2 (SALK_206518), and hkt1 (wiscDSLox353H10) were obtained from AraShare (http://www.arashare.cn). T-DNA insertional mutants xnd1-3, xnd1-4, xnd1-5, and XND1pro:GUS transgenic lines were described previously (38, 40). The xnd1-4 hkt1 double mutants were obtained by genetic crossing. The vni2 loss-of-function mutants (vni2-1 and -2) and vni2xnd1-4 double mutant were generated by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing in the respective backgrounds of Col-0 and xnd1-4. The MYBS2-overexpressing lines and VNI2-overexpressing lines were generated by cloning the coding sequence (CDS) of MYBS2 (AT5G08520) or VNI2 (AT5G13180) into the pCAMBIA1300-GFP vector, followed by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation into Col-0. The OsXND1-overexpressing lines were generated by cloning the CDS of OsXND1 (LOC_Os02g34970) into the pCAMBIA1301U-Flag vector under the control of a maize ubiquitin promoter, followed by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation into rice variety Zhonghua11 (ZH11). HKT1pro:GUS lines were generated by transforming the Col-0, xnd1-4, and xnd1-5 backgrounds with the pDX2181 vector containing a 2 kb promoter fragment of HKT1 (AT4G10310). All primer sequences used in this study are provided in SI Appedix, Table S2.

Plant Growth Conditions and Salt Stress Treatments.

Arabidopsis seeds were surface sterilized through immersion in 75% ethanol for 2 min, followed by a 15-min treatment with 10% sodium hypochlorite solution. Subsequently, the seeds were washed five times with sterile water. After stratification at 4 °C for 3 d, the seeds were sown on a 1/2-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium (pH 5.7), containing 1% [w/v] sucrose and 0.7% [w/v] agar, and germinated under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod at 100 μmol m−2 s−1 at 22 °C.

For the analysis of seed germination and cotyledon greening on agar plates, sterilized seeds were cultured on 1/2-strength MS medium, either with or without specified concentrations of NaCl. Following a stratification period of 3 d at 4 °C in darkness, the plates were horizontally positioned in the growth chamber. Germination and cotyledon greening rates were recorded daily for 7 d. The seedlings were collected and analyzed for root length and fresh weight. Each replicate for the fresh weight measurement was derived from a pooled sample consisting of five seedlings.

For phenotypic analysis of soil-grown plants, 10-d-old seedlings cultured on 1/2-strength MS medium were transplanted into soil and grown for an additional 2 wk. The soil was irrigated with 200 mM NaCl, and the chlorophyll content of the leaves, as indicated by Soil Plant Analysis Development (SPAD) values, was measured after a 10-d treatment using a SPAD-502 m (Konica Minolta, Japan). Survival rates were determined approximately 25 d after the treatment.

For the phenotypic analysis of rice under salt stress, 15-d-old ZH11 and OsXND1-overexpressing lines were cultivated hydroponically under a 12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod at 28 °C and treated with 150 mM NaCl. The phenotypic changes under stress conditions were observed approximately 7 d after the treatment, and the survival rates were determined approximately 15 d after the treatment.

Measurements of Na+ Content.

For the determination of ion content, seedlings were subjected to treatment with or without 200 mM NaCl prior to harvest. Samples were dried at 100 °C for 24 h before measuring the dry weight. Approximately 0.1 g of dried samples were digested in 10 mL of 2% HNO3 overnight, followed by sequential heating to 120, 140, and 160 °C for 1 h each until the samples became clear. Each sample was then adjusted to a final volume of 10 mL with 2% HNO3. The supernatant was filtered for the determination of Na+ concentrations using an ICP-MS (Thermo ScientificTM iCAP-RQ).

Analysis of Root Xylem Morphology.

For the analysis of xylem phenotypes in Arabidopsis or rice roots, 4- or 5-d-old seedlings subjected to 48-h NaCl treatment were stained overnight with 0.2 % basic fuchsin (Sigma, B0904). The samples were subsequently subjected to three washes in ClearSee with gentle shaking, in accordance with the previously described protocol (43). Root xylem morphology was examined between the protoxylem initiation and the metaxylem initiation using a confocal laser-scanning microscopy (Zeiss LSM 980), with excitation at 561 nm and detection of fluorescence emission within the range of 600 to 650 nm.

Y2H Assays.

For the Y2H screening, the 1–180 amino acid coding region of XND1 (AT5G64530) was cloned into the pDEST32 vector as a bait, considering the strong self-activation of the full-length CDS (38). The Arabidopsis transcription factor library was screened according to the previously described method (44).

For the Y2H assay between XND1 and MYBS2, the coding region of XND1 corresponding to amino acids 1–180 was inserted into the pGBKT7-Leu vector as the bait. The CDS of MYBS2 was inserted into the pDEST22 vector as the prey. Both constructs were cotransformed into the yeast strain Y2H and cultured on synthetic defined (SD) medium lacking leucine and tryptophan. To identify positive transformants, SD medium lacking leucine, tryptophan, adenine, and histidine was utilized. Furthermore, 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT) was added to inhibit potential self-activation.

LCI Assay.

The CDS of XND1 and MYBS2 were respectively cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-nLUC and pCAMBIA1300-cLUC vectors. These constructs were subsequently transformed into agrobacterium strain GV3101 and transiently expressed in 30-d-old N. benthamiana plants via infiltration. Following a 3-d incubation period, the infiltrated leaves were treated with a solution containing 100 mg/mL D-Luciferin potassium salt (Solarbio, IL2330) in darkness for 10 min. Luminescence activity was then detected using the Tanon-5200 chemiluminescent imaging system (Tanon, China). The empty vectors were utilized as negative controls in the experiment.

GST Pull-Down Assay.

The CDS of XND1 and MYBS2 were respectively cloned into pGEX-6P-1 and pET15 vectors. The GST-tagged XND1 and 6xHis-tagged MYBS2 proteins, along with the GST control protein, were expressed and purified from E. coli BL21 (DE3). Subsequently, the 6xHis-MYBS2 protein was mixed respectively with GST and GST-XND fusion proteins, followed by a 4-h incubation at 4 °C with glutathione sepharose beads (Sigma, G4510). After incubation, the beads were washed three times with a washing buffer containing 20 mM Tris (pH7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.5 mM PMSF. Finally, the input samples and the eluted proteins were detected by immunoblotting using anti-GST (ABclonal, AE001) and anti-His (ABclonal, AE003) antibodies.

Co-IP Assay.

The CDS of XND1 and MYBS2 were cloned into the pUC19-Flag and pM999-GFP vectors, respectively. These constructs were cotransformed and transiently expressed in Arabidopsis protoplasts derived from rosette leaves of 22-d-old seedlings. The protoplast preparation and transformation were performed as previously described (45). Proteins extraction was performed using a Co-IP buffer composed of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, and protease inhibitor cocktail. The extracted proteins were incubated with Anti-GFP magnetic beads (Proteintech, gtma) at 4 °C for 4 h under gentle shaking. After incubation, the beads were washed five times, resuspended, and mixed with SDS sample buffer. Immunoblotting analysis was conducted on both input samples and eluted proteins using anti-Flag antibodies (Abclonal, AE005) and anti-GFP antibodies (Roche, 11814460001).

Luciferase Reporter Assay.

To investigate the regulation of promoter activities of HKT1 and XND1, reporter constructs containing firefly luciferase were generated by cloning HKT1 promoter (2000 bp) and XND1 promoter (2091 bp), respectively. The CDS of XND1, MYBS2, and VNI2 were cloned into an effector vector under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter. The construct harboring Renilla luciferase under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter was utilized as the internal control. The reporter, effector, and internal control constructs were cotransformed into Arabidopsis protoplasts, which were subsequently cultured for 12 to 16 h at 23 °C in the dark. The protoplast preparation and transformation were performed as previously described (45). Luciferase activities were measured using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1501) and the Multimode Reader Platform (Tecan Spark, Switzerland).

For the luciferase reporter assay in tobacco, the HKT1 promoter (2000 bp) and XND1 promoter (2091 bp) were respectively cloned into the pGreenII0800-LUC vector to generate the respective reporter constructs. The effector and reporter constructs were coexpressed in N. benthamiana leaves for 2 to 3 d, after which luminescence activity was measured using the Tanon-5200 chemiluminescent imaging system (Tanon, China).

Quantitative Gene Expression Analysis.

Quantification of gene expression using RT-qPCR was performed essentially as previously described (40). The 2(−ΔΔC(T)) method was employed to determine the relative expression levels (46), using ACTIN2 (AT3G18780) and OsACTIN1 (LOC_Os03g50885) as the internal controls in Arabidopsis and rice, respectively. Primer sequences are provided in SI Appedix, Table S2.

Y1H Assays.

The HKT1 promoter (2000 bp) and XND1 promoter (2091 bp) were respectively cloned into the pAbAi vector as bait constructs, which were further linearized with the restriction enzyme BstBI and then transformed into Y1H Gold strain. The colonies were selected on SD medium lacking uracil (SD-Ura) and validated using PCR. The prey vectors were further transformed into the strains containing the bait construct, and selected on SD-Ura-Leu medium with Aureobasidin A (AbA). The screening of a prey cDNA library of Arabidopsis was conducted using the bait construct containing the XND1 promoter, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions for the Matchmaker Gold Y1H Library Screening System (Clontech).

EMSA.

The 6xHis-tagged XND1 and MYBS2 proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) using the pET15 vectors containing the CDS of XND1 and MYBS2, respectively. The recombinant proteins were purified using Ni-NTA beads (Sangon Biotech, C600792). DNA probes were synthesized and labeled with Cy5 at the 5′-end (AuGCT Biotech). The labeled probes were mixed with the purified proteins in EMSA Binding Buffer (Beyotime, GS005), incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and then separated via electrophoresis at 4 °C in the dark. The fluorescence was captured with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-RAD).

GUS Staining.

4- or 5-d-old seedlings, either untreated or treated with 100 mM NaCl for 1 d, were analyzed using GUS staining. The staining solution composed of 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), 1 mg/mL X-Gluc, 0.5 mM potassium ferricyanide, 0.5 mM potassium ferrocyanide, 10 mM EDTA, and 0.1% Triton X-100. After 3 h of staining, the seedlings were washed multiple times in 75% ethanol and then examined under an Olympus BX53 microscope.

Statistical Analyses.

Statistical analyses were conducted using Student’s t test via Microsoft Excel, one-way ANOVA, or Fisher’s exact test through SPSS STATISTICA 17. The data used for statistical analyses are provided in Datasets S1–S12.
