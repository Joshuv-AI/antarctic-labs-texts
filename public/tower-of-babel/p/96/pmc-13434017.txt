Development and external validation of a machine learning–based Cox model for predicting in-hospital survival in classic heatstroke: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Donglin Li, Xinyi He, Yanlin Zhou, Fake Liu, Yu Wang, Zongqian Wu, Chuan Zhong, Haiyang Guo, Tao Liu, Shengjie Tang, Haiyang Hu, Qin Li, Haining Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClassical heatstroke (CHS) is a life-threatening condition necessitating accurate prognostic tools for risk stratification. The potential of machine learning to predict clinical outcomes in CHS remains largely unexplored. Our objective was to develop and externally validate machine learning–based Cox model for predicting survival in hospitalized CHS patients. This retrospective multicenter study analyzed data from 538 CHS patients admitted to eight hospitals in western China between June 2022 and September 2023, with four institutions constituting the training cohort and four geographically distinct hospitals forming the external validation cohort. An innovative machine learning framework integrating nine algorithms with 54 combinatorial implementations was employed to identify robust prognostic features. Significant predictors from the optimal algorithm combination were incorporated into a multivariable Cox regression model, visualized as a clinical nomogram. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and calibration curves, while survival differences were assessed with Kaplan–Meier analysis. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) + supervised principal components (SuperPC) combination yielded the highest C-indexes (0.921 training; 0.811 validation). The nomogram achieved AUCs of 0.91, 0.85, and 0.86 for 10-, 20-, and 30-day survival in the training set, and 0.710, 0.80, and 0.80 in external validation. Calibration curves indicated strong agreement between predicted and observed 10-day survival probabilities, and Kaplan–Meier analyses confirmed significant survival stratification between risk groups. We developed and validated an accurate machine learning integration-based prognostic tool for CHS inpatients. Introduction

Heatstroke (HS) is a life-threatening medical emergency characterized by profound hyperthermia (core temperature typically exceeding 40.5 °C), central nervous system (CNS) dysfunction, and multiorgan failure, which categorized into two forms: classic and exertional heatstroke.1,2 Exertional heatstroke (EHS) was defined as heatstroke occurring during or immediately following strenuous physical activity, typically in younger, otherwise healthy individuals.1–3 Classic heatstroke (CHS) is primarily caused by prolonged high temperatures exposure and inadequate heat dissipation.1,2,4 Heatwaves, driven by global warming and urban heat islands caused by urbanization, are the main external factors contributing to a rise in fatalities, outpacing other severe weather conditions.1,4–8 Various internal factors, including physiological decline, comorbid medical conditions, and social isolation, render older individuals particularly susceptible to the impacts of prolonged heat exposure.4,9–11 The populations most vulnerable to CHS are those with diminished heat adaptation, inability to perform self-care, and chronic health conditions.4,9 Due to the continuous and unprecedented high temperatures caused by global warming in recent years, severe CHS cases increased substantially, with the mortality rate from heat stroke among older adults reportedly exceeding 50%.12,13 Prompt recognition of CHS patients with an elevated risk of mortality can facilitate the development of preventive and therapeutic management strategies.

Risk stratification models facilitate the optimization of healthcare resource allocation–including interventions, bed capacity, and staffing–by providing early warnings to healthcare teams regarding hospitalized patients at elevated risk of clinical deterioration. Previous studies have demonstrated similar prediction accuracy using traditional regression methods incorporating multiple clinical factors.14,15 However, conventional statistical methods commonly used by clinicians overlook complex interactions among variables that could be crucial in predicting future disease progression and may be better captured by more advanced modeling techniques. In recent years, machine learning (ML), a branch of artificial intelligence (AI), has garnered considerable attention for developing clinical predictive tools for disease diagnosis, staging, and prognosis.16–18 It has been effectively utilized to identify concealed patterns within intricate data, enabling more accurate predictions of clinical outcomes compared to traditional statistical approaches, particularly when handling extensive datasets.19 ML algorithms have emerged as a compelling approach for data mining endeavors, owing to their capacity to forecast individual patient outcomes by evaluating numerous predictor variables and their complex, high-dimensional interrelationships.20,21 As far as we are aware, no previous research has employed ML to predict mortality risk in CHS patients.

Machine learning approaches can enhance prognostication by integrating diverse predictors in non-linear patterns and utilizing multiple interactions to improve accuracy.22 Nevertheless, the numerous variables and intricate scoring mechanisms restrict their application to only the most advanced informatics settings. Even with adequate infrastructural support, the "black-box" nature of many algorithms often hinders interpretability, limiting their comprehensibility among both clinicians and patients.23 A hybrid approach that combines the strengths of machine learning with simpler, clinically interpretable risk prediction models may strike a balance between accuracy and interpretability, while also facilitating subsequent implementation. We conducted a prognostic study using data from a retrospective cohort of CHS patients treated at 8 hospitals in Western China. Initially, we developed and compared 54 machine learning algorithms of varying complexity and variable ranges to predict mortality risk in CHS. To balance accuracy, interpretability, and feasibility, we performed multivariate Cox regression analysis using variables selected from the best-performing machine learning models and constructed nomograms for visualization.

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective, multi-center study enrolled patients from eight hospitals across Sichuan Province in Western China between June 2022 and September 2023. The machine learning-based models were developed using data from patients diagnosed with CHS across four hospitals and subsequently underwent external validation with an independent dataset collected from four additional hospitals. The present study was conducted in full compliance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis plus artificial intelligence (TRIPOD-AI) guidelines.24.

Eligibility criteria comprised: (1) individuals aged 18 or older, and (2) participants who fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for CHS, including documented history of heat exposure, hyperthermia, and neurological abnormalities. Exclusion criteria consisted of: (1) a diagnosis of exertional heatstroke (EHS was excluded from this study due to its distinct pathophysiology, different risk factor profile, and generally more favorable prognosis compared to CHS), (2) the presence of irreversible conditions with a direct impact on mortality25,26: end-stage malignancy with active progression; end-stage chronic organ failure (Child–Pugh Class C cirrhosis, New York Heart Association Class IV heart failure with left ventricular ejection fraction < 20%, or end-stage chronic kidney disease on dialysis for > 6 months prior to admission); and severe traumatic brain injury with Glasgow Coma Scale = 3 prior to heat exposure, and (3) incomplete or missing clinical data. Ethical approval and informed consent

All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations of Declaration of Helsinki, International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects, and Ethical Review of Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects. The study received approval from the ethics committees or institutional review boards of the eight participating hospitals. Given its anonymous and retrospective nature, the ethics committees granted a waiver of informed consent.

Data collection

Data collection included a broad spectrum of variables derived from the participating CHS patients, spanning sociodemographics, clinical characteristics, hematological parameters, therapeutic regimens, and outcomes. Baseline assessments captured key sociodemographic variables: anthropometric measures (age, gender, height, weight, body mass index [BMI], body surface area); substance use (alcohol and tobacco); and social determinants (marital status, mode of admission, self-care ability, living conditions [e.g., ventilation, air conditioning], and social isolation). Clinical data encompassed a range of comorbidities and parameters, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), coronary artery disease, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, body temperature, peripheral oxygen saturation, diarrhea, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), length of hospital stay, and cerebral edema. The treatment details encompassed the administration of antibiotics and the application of mechanical ventilatory support. Additionally, outcome measures, specifically hospital discharge or mortality, were recorded. Given the limitations of long-term follow-up data, 10-day in-hospital mortality was selected as the primary model endpoint. Laboratory investigations comprised a comprehensive metabolic and hematologic profile, including glucose, arterial blood gas parameters (pH, bicarbonate [HCO₃-], and base excess [BE]), and a complete blood count with differentials (leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocyte, eosinophil, basophil, and monocyte counts and percentages; erythrocyte count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV, MCH, MCHC, red cell distribution width [RDW-CV and RDW-SD], and platelets). Additional hemostatic and inflammatory markers consisted of lactate, fibrinogen, prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), D-dimer, procalcitonin (PCT), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP). Renal function was evaluated via urea nitrogen, creatinine, and uric acid. Electrolyte panel included potassium (K+), sodium (Na+), chloride (Cl-), and calcium (Ca2+). Cardiac injury markers encompassed troponin, myoglobin, and CK-MB. Hepatic and biliary parameters included AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyltransferase, total protein, albumin, globulin, direct, total, and indirect bilirubin, the albumin-to-globulin (A/G) ratio, and cholinesterase. Lipid profile assessment involved triglycerides, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL). To minimize sampling bias, the research team maintained clear and effective communication with healthcare personnel and meticulously verified the gathered data in collaboration with them.

Missing data processing

In our study, 34 patients (4.8% of the initial cohort) were excluded due to incomplete data, primarily involving laboratory parameters (specifically cholinesterase, lactate, and CK-MB) that were not uniformly obtained in patients with rapid clinical deterioration or early transfer. No imputation was performed, as we opted for complete-case analysis to avoid introducing bias from non-random missingness. This decision is now explicitly stated and justified.27,28.

To enhance predictive precision, mitigate overfitting, and reduce computational demands, dimensionality reduction was performed prior to modeling. Multiple machine learning algorithms—Lasso, supervised principal components (SuperPC), partial least squares Cox regression (plsRcox), elastic net (Enet), Ridge, CoxBoost, random survival forest (RSF), stepwise Cox, and survival support vector machine (survival-SVM)—were employed for both feature selection and model construction. These nine algorithms were combined into 54 distinct configurations within the training dataset using tenfold cross-validation to support variable selection and model development. All models were evaluated using external validation datasets. The C-index was calculated for each model in both the training and external validation sets, and predictive performance was ranked based on average C-index values. Algorithm combinations exhibiting strong performance and clinical translational potential were selected. Consequently, robust prognostic features for overall survival in CHS patients were identified.

Subsequently, factors selected by the optimal machine learning algorithm combination were analyzed using a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model. Based on these prognostically significant factors, we developed an in-hospital survival prediction model presented as a nomogram. The model’s generalizability was evaluated using an external validation cohort from four independent hospitals. Discriminative performance was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC). Calibration plots were used to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes in both training and validation cohorts. Beyond AUC-based discrimination, the model’s independent predictive value was examined across different risk score thresholds. Using the median risk score from the training cohort as a cutoff, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups. This threshold was then applied to the external validation cohort, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated. All analyses were conducted in R (version 3.6.3), with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of enrolled patients with CHS

From the initial database of 673 patients, we excluded individuals diagnosed with EHS (n = 47), those with irreversible underlying conditions impacting mortality (n = 54), and those with incomplete data (n = 34, 19 patients [55.9%] had incomplete laboratory panels due to rapid clinical deterioration requiring immediate transfer to intensive care before blood sampling could be completed; 9 patients [26.5%] died within 6 h of admission, and key laboratory values—particularly cholinesterase and lactate—were not obtained before death; 6 patients [17.6%] were transferred from outside hospitals with incomplete medical records). Consequently, following the inclusion criteria, 538 patients were enrolled in the study. The training and external validation sets consisted of 291 and 247 patients, respectively. The median age was 73 years (IQR: 66–80), and 293 (54%) were male. Most patients (85%) arrived by ambulance, while 79 (15%) were self-admitted. The mean body surface area and body BMI were 1.69 m2 and 21.6 kg/m2, respectively. A majority (68%) lived in poorly ventilated or non-air-conditioned environments, 34% had impaired self-care ability, and 21% were socially isolated. Median body temperature on admission was 40 °C (IQR: 39–41 °C). Additionally, tachycardia (heart rate > 100 bpm) was observed in 71% of patients, and severe hypoxemia (oxygen saturation < 80%) was recorded in 9% at admission. Patients had a median GCS score of 8 (IQR: 5–10) on admission. Unusual laboratory findings were observed across the entire cohort, including prolonged prothrombin time, elevated blood glucose, neutrophilia, and increased creatinine levels. More than half of the patients exhibited increased levels of inflammatory biomarkers, including procalcitonin, D-dimer, myoglobin, CK-MB, aspartate transaminase, troponin, and direct bilirubin. Management focused on symptomatic and conservative treatment, primarily alleviating hyperthermia. Among hospitalized patients with CHS, antibiotic therapy was the most frequently administered treatment (74%), followed by mechanical ventilation (41%). In the cohort, 53 patients were diagnosed with disseminated intravascular coagulation, 130 with acute respiratory distress syndrome, and 139 with multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. Among the cohort, 73 patients reported diarrhea. On imaging, brain edema was evident in 8 cases. The median duration of hospital stay was 6 days. Overall, there were 149 deaths (66 in the training set and 83 in the validation set), with 389 patients discharged following treatment. Feature selection based on integrative machine learning

To establish robust prognostic indicators for CHS, we integrated 54 machine learning algorithms to assess prognostic signatures derived from the analysis of multiple variables. We developed 54 predictive models using a ten-fold cross-validation method in the training cohort and calculated the C-indexes for both the training and validation cohorts, as shown in Fig. 2. The C-indexes of the training set and the validation set varies from 0.726 to 0.921 and 0.511 to 0.811 respectively, and the average C-indexes ranges from 0.619 to 0.866 (Fig. 2). Among these models, the optimal one was a combination of Lasso and SuperPC, which achieved the highest average C-index (0.921) and outperformed all other models in the validation datasets. The optimal algorithm screened out 12 key features: self-care ability, social isolation, heart rate, body temperature, peripheral oxygen saturation, GCS, lactate, CK-MB, uric acid, myoglobin, cholinesterase and diarrhea.

A total of 54 machine learning prediction models were constructed through a ten-fold cross-validation process. The C-index for each model was subsequently calculated by aggregating its performance across all validation datasets.

Model development based on multivariate Cox regression analyses

We performed multivariate Cox regression analysis on the 12 independent prognostic factors selected by the optimal ML model, developing a predictive model to estimate in-hospital survival probability (Fig. 3). Although body temperature, self-care ability, peripheral oxygen saturation, lactate, and myoglobin were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) in the multivariate Cox regression analysis, they were retained in the model due to their clinical relevance and did not diminish its discriminative performance. The risk score for each participant was calculated using a linear combination of the 12 predictor variables, weighted by their respective regression coefficients. The formula was as follows: risk Score = (− 0.668 × social isolation) + (0.445 × self-care ability) + (0.157 × body temperature) + (1.091 × heart rate) + (0.076 × peripheral oxygen saturation) − (0.324 × GCS) + (0.114 × lactate) + (0.00175 × uric acid) + (1.218 × CK-MB) + (0.473 × myoglobin) − (0.00033 × cholinesterase) − (0.782 × diarrhea). A nomogram was utilized to graphically represent this model (Fig. 4).

Prognostic model based on risk score for survival analysis

The risk score for each patient was computed as a weighted sum of predictors, with weights corresponding to their respective regression coefficients from the Cox proportional hazards model. Patients were stratified into two subgroups using the median risk score as the threshold: a low-risk group (risk score ≤ -0.097797511) and a high-risk group (risk score > -0.097797511). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated significantly prolonged survival in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group across both the training and validation cohorts (Fig. 5).

Establishment and validation of a nomogram

To facilitate clinical translation, a nomogram was constructed based on the multivariate Cox regression model. The nomogram revealed that the GCS and cholinesterase were the predominant factors influencing prognosis, followed by uric acid, lactate, body temperature, heart rate, CK-MB, and heat rate. Diarrhea, social isolation, self-care ability, myoglobin and peripheral oxygen saturation moderately influenced survival outcomes. Each subcategory of these variables was assigned a score on a standardized point scale. The sum of all points was calculated and aligned with the nomogram’s total points scale, allowing the probability to be read by drawing a vertical line to the survival axis. Calibration plots demonstrated strong concordance between predicted and observed 10-day survival outcomes–a level of agreement that was consistent with the model’s performance at 20 and 30 days (plots not shown)–indicating robust calibration. Analysis of the ROC curves showed AUCs of 0.91, 0.85, and 0.86 for the 10-, 20-, and 30-day intervals, respectively, in the training set, and 0.71, 0.80, and 0.80 in the external validation set (the 20-day and 30-day results are exploratory analyses with limited reliability due to small sample sizes at these time points). These findings indicated the model’s strong discriminative performance. (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Classic heatstroke is a life-threatening disorder caused by sustained exposure to high environmental temperatures and insufficient thermoregulation, leading to high rates of illness and death and imposing significant demands on public health and medical infrastructure.1–3 In this multicenter study, we developed and externally validated a prognostic model incorporating machine learning algorithms using a large cohort of patients with CHS. Notably, the model incorporates multiple variables to deliver personalized, patient-specific predictions of in-hospital survival. It offers valuable guidance for implementing preventive and therapeutic strategies for heat stroke patients in clinical settings.

The primary cohort, drawn from a wide geographical distribution, ensures representativeness and generalizability to Chinese patients with CHS. To identify robust prognostic features for CHS, we developed a novel computational framework integrating nine ML algorithms and their 54 combinations. Further validations in external validation datasets revealed that the optimal model was a combination of Lasso and SuperPC. The strength of integrative approaches lies in their ability to construct a model with reliable prognostic performance for CHS utilizing various machine learning algorithms and their combinations. These algorithms integration also help reduce variable dimensionality, making the model more simplified and translational. Utilizing this framework, we successfully developed a consensus model that exhibits strong predictive accuracy and practical translatability. Through multivariate COX analysis and Kaplan–Meier curve analysis, we established that the prognostic model can stratify the overall survival (OS) risk for CHS patients while independently predicting these outcomes. To offer a practical tool for quantifying survival in CHS, we developed a nomogram and conducted external validation. The nomogram demonstrated reliable discrimination, with the ROC curve and AUCs indicating strong predictive accuracy. Additionally, the calibration curve supported the nomogram’s precision, revealing a close alignment between predicted and actual survival rates.

Machine learning algorithms currently serve as a powerful approach for analyzing multivariate and high-dimensional data. However, as techniques like artificial intelligence become increasingly intertwined with extensive variable sets, overfitting emerges as a critical challenge that must be addressed during model development. Models that exhibit strong performance on training data often struggle to achieve effective generalization across additional validation sets, a phenomenon that is far from rare. To mitigate the issues arising from overfitting in the training cohort, we adopted the average C-index from the two cohorts as the ranking criterion. The study revealed that models trained with Lasso and SuperPC exhibited outstanding performance across both the training and validation sets. Two previous studies have developed a survival prediction model for CHS patients using traditional regression analysis methods.14,15 However, their single feature selection method has its inherent limitations. Machine learning techniques offer substantial potential to enhance prognostic accuracy by integrating a broad spectrum of predictors through nonlinear modeling and leveraging complex variable interactions.22 While machine learning excels at leveraging vast amounts of data for automated predictions, simplifying the model remains crucial, especially as the number of features increases. We streamlined our approach by selecting features identified by the optimal machine learning model for multivariate COX regression analysis, creating a more concise prediction model. The results demonstrated that this simplified nomogram maintained strong performance across both the training and validation sets.

However, the performance drop (e.g., AUC from ~ 0.91 to ~ 0.71) suggests overfitting and limited generalizability. This attenuation reflects several factors.

Model complexity: Although we employed nine algorithms with regularization techniques (Lasso, Ridge, Elastic Net), integrating 54 algorithmic combinations introduced complexity that may have captured training-specific noise. The Lasso + SuperPC combination, despite being the best-performing, remained vulnerable to overfitting when applied to geographically distinct validation cohorts. Case mix differences: The training and validation cohorts were drawn from geographically distinct hospitals in western China. Differences in patient acuity, diagnostic practices, and treatment protocols between sites likely contributed to the performance drop. Notably, the validation cohort had a higher mortality rate (33.6% vs. 22.7% in the training cohort), reflecting greater disease severity. Feature dimensionality: With 538 patients and numerous candidate predictors, the patient-to-predictor ratio was suboptimal for complex machine learning models. Although we applied dimensionality reduction (Lasso and SuperPC) to mitigate this, the final model retained 12 predictors—a relatively high dimensionality given the event count (149 deaths).

Our optimal machine learning model found that self-care ability,9 social isolation,9 body temperature,1,29–31 heat rate,3 GCS32–34 and diarrhea1,35,36 were associated with poor prognosis of CHS, consistent with prior studies.14,15 In addition, lactate, peripheral oxygen saturation, uric acid, CK-MB, myoglobin and cholinesterase were also selected. Elevated lactate levels are an independent predictor of mortality in severe heatstroke and are also associated with an increased risk of acute kidney injury (AKI), DIC, and myocardial injury.37 Kaplan–Meier survival analysis confirmed that patients with increased lactate levels had significantly higher mortality.37 A prospective cohort study by Pease et al.38 reported universally elevated lactate among ICU-admitted heatstroke patients, with non-survivors showing markedly higher values. Lactate is also included in the prognostic scoring system for severe heatstroke developed by Yang et al.39, underscoring its clinical relevance. As a product of anaerobic metabolism, lactate serves as a marker of tissue hypoperfusion.40 Severe heatstroke often leads to dehydration, reduced effective blood volume, and subsequent organ hypoperfusion and hypoxemia.37 Rhabdomyolysis is another critical complication, potentially arising from dysregulated thermoregulation, fluid and electrolyte loss, and direct thermal damage to muscle cells, disrupting energy metabolism.41 This process is frequently accompanied by elevated CK, CK-MB, and myoglobin, indicating myocardial injury.42,43 The role of uric acid in heatstroke remains underrecognized, though elevated levels may reflect associated rhabdomyolysis. Butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) has been inversely associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients on venoarterial ECMO after cardiac surgery44, and recently with adverse outcomes in COVID-19.45 However, its prognostic value in heatstroke has not been studied and warrants further investigation.

Accurate prediction of patient deterioration can help clinicians assess risks during hospitalization by identifying individuals who may require ICU-level care before their condition worsens. During periods of increased admissions, hospitals may utilize predictive models to optimize the allocation of limited resources–such as inpatient beds–by assigning low-risk patients to lower-acuity care settings. This approach has motivated the development of robust prognostic tools for predicting outcomes in CHS. Nevertheless, despite these endeavors, the generalization performance–namely, how well these models perform across new patient populations–is frequently neglected during their development and assessment. To achieve this, we crafted a risk stratification model incorporating twelve commonly gathered personal and clinical variables from patients’ electronic health records to predict clinical deterioration. This model demonstrated outstanding discriminative accuracy across external validation cohorts, while maintaining robust calibration performance. As a tool for assessing clinical risk, this model could be integrated into the electronic health record alongside vital signs, offering clinicians a concise overview of a patient’s condition. A healthcare facility might opt to leverage the model to assist a rapid response team in identifying and evaluating patients at elevated risk of clinical decompensation. Ultimately, how the model is integrated into clinical decision-making should be determined by local priorities, resource constraints, treatment availability, and the institution’s acceptable thresholds for false-positive and false-negative results.

External validation may reveal limitations in a model’s generalizability when the validation cohort differs substantially from the development cohort–including variations in clinical conditions, individual traits, clinical workflows, and other factors. Achieving feature consistency across patient populations and institutions remains challenging, even in the most basic settings. For instance, variations in clinical workflows between hospitals might lead to differing documentation habits or monitoring approaches–such as intermittent versus continuous pulse oximetry–which could ultimately influence the effectiveness of these variables. Notwithstanding the probable variations in clinical practices across hospitals, our proposed model exhibited strong performance across institutions, indicating that these variables reflect certain elements of illness severity that hold broad applicability. The model’s robust generalizability may stem from several strategic design decisions. Initially, we trained it using a development cohort drawn from multiple hospitals. Furthermore, the feature selection approach, which integrates multiple machine learning algorithms, along with the multi-institutional external validation strategy, contribute to the model’s exceptional generalization capabilities. However, as highlighted by the American Heart Association’s scientific statement on artificial intelligence in heart disease and recent guidance from Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA),46,47 the ultimate value of clinical prediction models lies not in diagnostic performance metrics alone, but in their ability to improve patient-centered outcomes when implemented in real-world care. Our model, while demonstrating acceptable discrimination in external validation, represents an early-stage predictive tool; future work must therefore focus on prospective validation and implementation-phase evaluations to assess whether integrating this model into clinical workflows translates into meaningful improvements in patient outcomes—such as reduced mortality, shorter time to critical interventions, or more efficient resource allocation. A recent example of such a complete validation pipeline was reported by Park et al.,48 who developed an AI-based cardiac arrest prediction model that progressed from retrospective development through prospective testing and ultimately demonstrated clinical benefit in a non-randomized controlled trial. This trajectory serves as a benchmark for the stage of development of our own model and outlines the pathway required to achieve clinical utility.

Feature selection techniques integrating multiple machine learning algorithms might yield features that may not immediately align with clinical intuition, yet still capture critical elements of a patient’s condition, aiding in outcome prediction.20 An additional advantage of adopting more sophisticated machine learning methods lies in their capacity to identify and emphasize “significant” features as described above. Until recently, predictive models typically depended on input features selected by clinicians, deemed significant based on prior studies. This method is susceptible to bias and may restrict the selection of input variables due to reliance on subjective human assumptions. This limitation can be partially mitigated by incorporating a broad set of potential predictor variables and allowing machine learning algorithms to identify the most informative features.49,50 These factors may not necessarily align with those deemed most important by clinicians and could bring attention to aspects that were previously overlooked. We expand upon this research by investigating a broader range of classifiers, such as random forest and elastic net, in conjunction with feature reduction techniques. We believe that applying this methodology to our dataset provides a robust approach to selecting the most suitable model.

Additional strengths of our study include its multicenter design and a substantial cohort of over 500 patients from multiple cities in Western China, enhancing its generalizability to the Chinese population. The models were developed using easily accessible clinical data, eliminating the need for complex pre-processing or access to imaging data. Although previous studies have demonstrated the value of clinical data in building prognostic models for CHS, none have systematically compared multiple feature reduction methods in combination with diverse machine learning classifiers, as implemented in our study. Moreover, while existing approaches typically rely on a limited number of features for survival prediction, our modeling strategy incorporates the comprehensive use of available variables to enhance predictive accuracy. We believe this approach could be adapted across various health systems, including those in resource-limited environments, by utilizing local clinical datasets to enhance surveillance stratification for CHS patients.

Limitation

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the retrospective design of our study, there is a potential for selection bias stemming from its inherent limitations. Second, although our sample size (n = 538) is comparable to or larger than many heatstroke prediction models, it remains modest for machine learning approaches. With only 149 outcome events, the effective sample size is at the lower bound for reliable model development. Despite using regularization and dimensionality reduction to mitigate overfitting, the performance decline from training to external validation (AUC: 0.91 to 0.71) partly reflects sample size limitations relative to model complexity. Larger, more diverse cohorts are needed to validate and refine our model. Third, our model was not benchmarked against conventional severity scores such as SOFA, APACHE II, and SAPS II, which previous heatstroke studies have shown to be significantly associated with mortality.51–53 This indirectly supports our model’s relevance, as it identified similar predictors (GCS, lactate, organ dysfunction markers). Future prospective studies should include such benchmarking to better establish the added value of machine learning-based models. Fourth, while our model demonstrates promising discriminative performance in retrospective external validation, prospective validation in a multicenter setting is required to assess its real-world predictive accuracy before clinical implementation. Future studies should also evaluate implementation-phase outcomes, including the model’s impact on clinical decision-making, time to intervention, and ultimately patient-centered outcomes such as reduced mortality and morbidity. Such prospective validation will be necessary to establish the model’s clinical utility and facilitate its integration into routine critical care practice. Fifth, the long-term (day-20, day-30) prediction models are constrained by substantial loss of follow-up data and should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective studies with complete long-term follow-up are needed to validate these results.

Conclusion

In this study, we evaluated multiple machine learning algorithms and feature selection techniques to construct prognostic prediction models for patients with CHS. By integrating artificial intelligence with conventional statistical approaches, our models achieved robust performance, effectively balancing predictive accuracy with clinical interpretability. These models demonstrated consistent performance across both training and external validation cohorts. Utilizing of this integration method may offer promising clinical decision support for both practitioners and patients in personalized risk-stratification and surveillance.
