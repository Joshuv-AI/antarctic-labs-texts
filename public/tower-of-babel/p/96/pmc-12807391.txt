Analyzing genomic variation in cultivated pumpkins and identification of candidate genes controlling seed traits
Authors: Chieh‐Ying Lee, Jaw‐Fen Wang, Chia‐Hui Chang, Chih‐Wei Tung
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPumpkins are important vegetable crops widely grown worldwide, and seeds are considered a popular nutraceutical food and an excellent source of protein, oil, and vitamins. Seed size is one of the most important targets for commercial breeding in Cucurbita species; studies have shown that pumpkin seed size variation has a similar trend with fruit size, shape, and seed yield. However, few studies have been conducted to identify genetic loci controlling seed‐related traits in cultivated pumpkins. This study analyzed the genomic characteristics of pumpkin breeding materials of 321 Cucurbita accessions collected worldwide, including Cucurbita moschata, Cucurbita maxima, and Cucurbita pepo, using extensive single nucleotide polymorphisms obtained from the genotyping‐by‐sequencing method, significant genetic variations were identified within and between Cucurbita species. Four major cultivar fruit types were further revealed in C. moschata species, and significant differentiation patterns were detected in several chromosomal regions. A total of 15 significant loci associated with pumpkin seed traits were mapped through a genome‐wide association approach; 32 genes previously reported to be associated with seed size regulation in Arabidopsis and Oryza sativa were located in the intervals defined by linkage disequilibrium. Through this study, we gained a deep understanding of the genomic variation distribution across Cucurbita species. The available genetic resources and the associated genetic contents could be used in commercial pumpkin breeding and will facilitate molecular marker‐assisted selection in pumpkin seed trait improvement.

INTRODUCTION

Cucurbita spp., known as pumpkins, are important vegetable crops belonging to the Cucurbitaceae family that are widely grown around the world. The group includes five major domesticated species. Among them, Cucurbita moschata, Cucurbita maxima, and Cucurbita pepo are the main cultivated species (Chomicki et al., 2020). The production of this vegetable was approximately 27 million tons in 2020, according to the Food and Agriculture Organization Statistics (FAOSTAT), and the main production countries are China and India. Several features contribute to the importance of pumpkin. First, it has high nutritional value and thus provides numerous health benefits. It is rich in carotenoids, vitamins, and minerals such as potassium (K), calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), magnesium (Mg), and copper (Cu) (Nagar et al., 2018; Priori et al., 2016). Pumpkin seeds are also a popular nutraceutical food and an excellent source of protein, oil, and vitamins. Recent research has revealed that almost 70% of pumpkin seed oil is made of unsaturated fatty acids, monounsaturated fatty acids, and vitamin E (Jarret et al., 2013; Veronezi & Jorge, 2012). Second, it has diverse uses. All pumpkin plant parts are edible, including mature and immature fruits, young stems, tendrils, flowers, and seeds (Sanjur et al., 2002; Zizumbo‐Villarreal et al., 2016). Third, pumpkin is adaptable to a wide range of production systems and highly tolerant to soil‐borne diseases. Thus, it is largely used as a rootstock for other cucurbits, such as cucumber and watermelon (Davoudi et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2021). For the above reasons, the popularity and economic value of Cucurbita are increasing.

Seed size is a key agronomic trait related to evolutionary fitness in plants (Y. Guo et al., 2020). It is also one of the most important targets for commercial breeding in Cucurbita (Y. Guo et al., 2020; Y. Wang et al., 2019). Generally, seeds with larger sizes have faster germination rates and produce stronger seedlings, which contribute to better nutrient competition and better tolerance for environmental adaptability (Gomez, 2004). Studies have shown that pumpkin seed size variation has a similar trend with fruit size and shape as well as seed yield. Paris and Nerson (2003) evaluated C. pepo seed traits of a set of germplasms with diverse fruit shapes and found an inverse relation to the ratios of seed length to width. Cultivar types with long fruit shapes, such as cocozelle, zucchini, straightneck, and crookneck, have smaller seeds, while cultivar types with short or round fruit shapes, such as pumpkin, vegetable marrow, acorn, and scallop, have larger seeds. Darrudi et al. (2018) studied 32 Cucurbita accessions collected in Iran and found that seed weight is positively correlated with fruit weight and flesh thickness, seed yield is positively correlated with fruit yield in C. pepo, and fruit cavity diameter is positively correlated with seed weight, length, and width in C. moschata. Moreover, pumpkin seed size was positively correlated with oil content but negatively correlated with protein content (Meru et al., 2018). Genetic studies on pumpkin seed size are very limited. Y. Wang et al. (2019) found that seed length (SL) and width displayed continuous variation in the F2 segregating population of C. maxima and identified 10 genomic regions on six chromosomes associated with seed traits. Therefore, more research is needed to fully explore seed‐related traits.

Genomic research in pumpkin is progressing slower than that in other cucurbit crops. The first version of the Cucurbita reference genome was generated for C. maxima, C. moschata, and C. pepo (Montero‐Pau et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2017) after cucumber (Huang et al., 2009), melon (Garcia‐Mas et al., 2012), and watermelon (Guo et al., 2013). Recently, next‐generation sequencing (NGS) technology and genotyping‐by‐sequencing (GBS) approaches have accelerated genome‐wide single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) development with the advantages of high‐throughput and cost‐effective properties (Poland & Rife, 2012). SNP markers provide a powerful tool for studying genetic variation to utilize potential resources for crop improvement. Until recently, most of the genetic diversity studies of Cucurbita were limited by the small number of materials from certain geographic regions or by the low density of molecular markers (Formisano et al., 2011; Gong et al., 2012; Kaźmińska et al., 2017). The situation has recently changed due to technological developments in genomics. For example, H.‐Y. Lee et al. (2020) used 610 germplasms and 2071 SNPs to define three subgroups and to generate a core collection in C. moschata; note that 1500–32,000 high‐quality SNPs were detected in each of the USDA pumpkin germplasm collections of three species with size ranging from 314 to 829 accessions (Hernandez et al., 2023).

SNP markers are also effective in exploring the genetic architecture of important traits through mapping approaches. SNP markers have been utilized in pumpkin to study several traits, including dwarf vine (Zhang et al., 2015) and fruit traits (Montero‐Pau et al., 2017; Zhong et al., 2017). Quantitative trait loci (QTLs) mapping was the approach used in these studies. However, there are several disadvantages of the QTL mapping approach, including limited allele diversity due to its biparental nature, time‐consuming preparation of the mapping population, and the low resolution of the mapped QTLs. Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) based on linkage disequilibrium (LD) mapping have recently become a key method used to study phenotype variation associated with the genome. GWASs can identify candidate genomic regions harboring genes significantly associated with target trait variations attributed to historical recombination events (Alqudah et al., 2020). In pumpkin, the GWAS approach has recently been used to identify novel genes associated with powdery mildew resistance (Alavilli et al., 2022) and dwarfism (the bush phenotype) in C. pepo (Hernandez et al., 2023). Thus, a GWAS could be an effective approach to study seed size traits in pumpkin.

In this study, we explored the genomic characteristics of pumpkin breeding materials. A total of 321 Cucurbita accessions, including C. moschata, C. maxima, and C. pepo, were investigated. The objectives of this research are to (1) develop SNP markers from three commercial Cucurbita species and analyze the population structure of Cucurbita and (2) determine the genetic regions associated with pumpkin seed morphology traits and locate candidate genes. The results provide important information for commercial pumpkin breeding and will further promote the use of molecular marker‐assisted selection in pumpkin breeding.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials

A total of 321 pumpkin accessions were provided by Known‐You Seed Company. These materials consist of 151 germplasms belonging to three cultivated species (C. moschata, C. maxima, and C. pepo.) and 170 inbred lines developed by company breeders; the complete material list is provided in Supporting Information. The germplasm was collected from 22 countries: 110 originated in Asia, eight were from Europe, six were from the Americas, four were from Africa and Oceania, and 23 had unknown countries of origin. The accessions have been maintained since 1968. The species identity of each germplasm was verified by growing at least 10 plants in the field during the multiplication process, purified by self‐pollination for at least four generations. In C. moschata, 76 accessions were collected from 14 countries and 42 were inbred lines. In C. maxima, 39 accessions were collected from five countries, majority were from Japan, and 103 were inbred lines. In C. pepo, 32 accessions were collected from nine countries and 29 were inbred lines.

Seed phenotypes evaluation

The seeds used in the study were produced and stored according to the standards set by the company for commercial sale. Therefore, the uniformity was high. A total of five seeds per accession were used to measure seed‐related traits, including SL, seed width (SW), seed area (SA), and length‐width ratio (L/W). Images of seeds were collected in JEPG (Joint Photographic Experts Group) format with 3000 × 4000 pixels and analyzed using ImageJ software (Abràmoff et al., 2004). The image analysis was executed as follows: (a) image conversion to grayscale type (8 bits); (b) size transformation with a known scale into millimeters (mm); (c) selection of an area for measurement; (d) target area separation by threshold adjustment; and (e) measurement with the analyze‐particle function.

DNA extraction and SNP identification by GBS

GBS libraries were prepared following the protocol described in Elshire et al. (2011). Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves using the Qiagen Plant DNeasy kit. One hundred nanograms of genomic DNA for each accession was digested using the methylation‐sensitive restriction enzyme ApekI. DNA fragments were ligated to 96 barcode adapters that were assigned to each accession after digestion. DNA samples were pooled and amplified by PCR to generate GBS libraries. The libraries were sequenced by the Hi‐Seq 2500 platform with a single‐end method, which was performed by the Biodiversity Research Center of Academia Sinica in Taiwan. The raw reads were deposited in the NCBI SRA with the accession number PRJNA1005018.

The GBS reads from 321 pumpkin accessions were aligned independently to the two reference genomes of C. moschata var. Rifu and C. maxima var. Rimu; both assemblies were downloaded from the Cucurbit Genomics Database (http://cucurbitgenomics.org/). SNP calling was performed using the TASSEL‐GBS pipeline, following the procedure described by Glaubitz et al. (2014).

Population structure and genetic analysis

The genetic structure among the three cultivated pumpkin species was investigated with the model‐based estimation software ADMIXTURE v1.3 (Alexander et al., 2009), and the most informative value of K was explored using the cross‐validation method. The model was run for population numbers (K) of 1–10 between and within all Cucurbita species. Principal component analysis and LD analysis were performed using TASSEL v5.2.8 (Bradbury et al., 2007). The number of principal components (PCs) was used as a variable in the GWAS. LD (r 2) was calculated for a sliding window size of 100 SNPs. The LD decay distance was measured as the physical distance (kb) where the average r 2 value dropped to half of its maximum. The calculated LD block intervals were used to search for candidate genes for seed traits. The pairwise fixation index (Fst) was used to estimate genetic divergence with the R package hierfstat. The fixation index is a measure of the allele frequency variation between populations, ranging between 0 and 1, and a higher Fst indicates greater differentiation.

Genome‐wide association studies of seed‐related traits

GWASs were conducted with TASSEL v5.2.8, and a generalized linear model (GLM) was used. The formula for the GLM was y = Xβ + e, which includes the vector of phenotypic data (y), the matrix of genotype and population structure (X), the vector of genotype and population structure (β), and the vector of residuals (e). The population structures (Q) were obtained by performing principal component analysis (PCA). For both species, we tested GLMs with and without population structure as a covariate. The fit of different models for each phenotype dataset was determined based on Q–Q plots. The significance of associations among markers and traits was given as the p value in each GWAS, and the Bonferroni correction was used to determine the significance threshold at a significance level of 5%. The genomic regions containing the significant SNPs were further decided by the LD decay distance in each chromosome and considered candidate intervals. Genes located in candidate intervals were annotated according to the reference genome of C. moschata var. Rifu and descriptions were acquired from the Cucurbit Genomics Database (http://cucurbitgenomics.org/; Yu et al., 2023).

RESULTS

Genome‐wide SNP identification among three Cucurbita species

A total of 321 Cucurbita accessions, including 142 C. maxima, 118 C. moschata, and 61 C. pepo accessions, were sequenced using the GBS method. The high‐quality reads were aligned to the C. moschata var. Rifu reference genome, and a total of 78,424 SNPs were detected with a missing rate less than 5% (Supporting Information). Of these, 31,456 high‐quality SNPs were selected using the criteria of a minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 0.05 and zero missing data. The number of SNP markers on each chromosome ranged from 1004 SNPs (chromosome 20) to 3104 SNPs (chromosome 4), with an average of 1572 SNPs per chromosome (Figure 1a). Among them, the SNP number and the average distance between two SNPs within each species were 20,730 and 11.8 kb in C. maxima (Figure 1b), 18,782 and 13.1 kb in C. moschata (Figure 1c), and 13,888 and 17.6 kb in C. pepo (Figure 1d). Other than chromosomes 9, 10, and 20, the high‐quality polymorphic SNPs were evenly distributed on other chromosomes in each species. The homozygosity of the tested materials was examined using a high‐quality SNP set. All accessions were highly fixed, with a minimum homozygosity of 85%, and only two accessions had heterozygosity over 10%. Therefore, we confirmed that all the accessions used in this study were highly fixed and suitable for population structure and GWASs.

The genome‐wide SNP distribution in the Cucurbita chromosomes showed a 0.1 Mbp window size. (a) 31,456 SNPs in 321 Cucurbita accessions; (b) 20,730 SNPs in 142 C. maxima accessions; (c) 18,782 SNPs in 118 C. moschata accessions; and (d) 13,888 SNPs in 61 C. pepo accessions.

The high‐quality GBS reads were also aligned to the C. maxima var. Rimu reference genome; a total of 76,814 SNPs were detected with a missing rate less than 5% (Supporting Information). The SNP number and distribution in three Cucurbita species are highly similar with the results from the alignment to the C. moschata var. Rifu genome; the population structure detected in Cucurbita species is nearly identical using the SNPs from two alignment datasets (Supporting Information). Therefore, we only presented the genetic analysis and GWAS results based on the SNPs derived from alignment to the C. moschata var. Rifu reference genome.

Population structure and genetic relationships in cultivated Cucurbita

Using 31,456 high‐quality SNPs, population structure analyses were performed among the three Cucurbita species. First, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted. The first two PCs explained over 90% of the total variation. Three clear genetic groups were yielded, each representing C. maxima, C. moschata, and C. pepo. The PCA results showed no admixture between Cucurbita species (Figure 2a). LD analysis was conducted to assess the effect of selection among the Cucurbita species. LD decay was measured as the physical distance at which the LD decreased to half of its maximum value. Among the three species, C. moschata showed the fastest LD decay of 49 kb (r2 = 0.25), followed by C. pepo with 118 kb (r2 = 0.32), and C. maxima exhibited the extended LD decay of 350 kb (r2 = 0.35; Figure 2b). The results implied that the numbers of historical recombination events in the species were very different, which could have resulted from artificial selection or inbreeding. For example, the fastest LD decay in C. moschata might be due to a higher genetic diversity in C. moschata collections or more gene flow events over the commercial breeding history in the species. Therefore, we further analyzed the population structure of C. moschata to examine whether there were subpopulations within the 118 accessions.

The PCA results indicated that the C. moschata accessions were slightly structured (Figure 2c). The structure was further analyzed using ADMIXTURE software. After determining the best K value, we identified four potential ancestors in C. moschata (Figure 2d). More than half of the accessions had mixed ancestral compositions, indicating possible gene flow or introgression between ancestors. The admixture proportions of each accession were in agreement with their position in the PCA plot. The accessions with a highly mixed ancestral composition were located in the middle of the PCA plot and spread across the groups. A total of four genetic groups were further defined when the proportion of a single ancestry was larger than 50% in each accession, and only one accession was ungrouped due to a lower major ancestry proportion of 46% (Figure 2d, marked with an asterisk). The level of genetic difference among the four groups was estimated using the population divergence index (Fst). Higher Fst values indicate greater genetic differentiation. Fst between groups in C. moschata ranged from 0.17 to 0.33 (Figure 2e). Group 4 had the highest differentiation degree, as its Fst values with the other three groups were the highest. Groups 2 and 3 were highly aggregated and overlapped in the PCA plot. The lowest Fst value was also observed between these two groups. Collectively, these results supported the presence of four potential genetic groups in C. moschata, and Group 4 was the most genetically differentiated group.

Distribution of categorical traits in genetic groups of C. moschata corresponding to cultivar fruit types

To evaluate whether the four genetic groups coincided with the distribution of categorical phenotypes of multiple fruit traits, 96 field‐grown accessions with high germination rates and multiple replicates were investigated. Categorical phenotypes for each trait were recorded in each genetic group, and the accessions with a 100% single ancestry component tended to belong to one particular type (Table 1). In Group 1, the majority of accessions had brown, pear‐shaped, marbled, and wart‐free fruits, and the average fruit ratio was 1.12. Group 2 can be easily identified by its flat fruit shape (fruit ratio of 0.65); moreover, it is the only group with warts on the fruits. Group 3 has diverse fruit shapes, albeit mostly with marbled skin, no warts, and no grooves. Group 4 is genetically most distant from the others. It also has a quite different morphology compared with the other groups, and the majority of accessions showed early flowering, cream‐colored skin, and the longest fruit shape (fruit ratio of 1.64). Our results revealed that the four groups were not only distinct in genetic composition but also highly different in fruit morphology and growth habits. Therefore, we named Groups 1–4 as Pear Marble, Flat Warts, Round Marble, and Cream Club, respectively (Figure 2f).

Identification of candidate SNPs associated with seed size traits

The high‐quality SNPs generated in this study were further examined for their application in a genome‐wide association study using four continuous seed morphology traits. Data on SA, SL, SW, and seed length‐width ratio (L/W) were collected from the accessions of C. moschata and C. maxima. Due to their small sample size, C. pepo accessions were not included in this analysis.

Overall, a clear difference in seed morphology between the two species was observed (Figure 3a). The seeds of C. maxima were larger and rounder than those of C. moschata. The average SA, SL, and SW in C. maxima were 133.9 mm2, 17.3 mm, and 10.5 mm, respectively, compared with 89.2 mm2, 15.2 mm, and 8.2 mm in C. moschata. The average L/W was 1.65 in C. maxima and 1.90 in C. moschata. The distribution of seed morphology data was more concentrated in C. moschata, especially those of SA and SW. The relationships between the seed morphology traits were similar in the two species (Figure 3b). Strong positive correlations were detected between SA, SL, and SW (r > 0.90), followed by SL and SW (r > 0.70). The only negative correlation was detected between SW and L/W in both species.

In C. maxima, 11,190 SNPs were used, and a total of 106 SNPs exceeded the 5% significance level adjusted by the Bonferroni threshold (−log10 p value > 5.460; Figure 4a). The number of significant SNPs was 45 for SA, 55 for SL, 50 for SW, and 6 for L/W, spread over 16 chromosomes (Table S1). To understand the top SNP effect on each trait, phenotypes among the genotypes at each SNP are shown in a box plot (Figure 4b), and T tests were used to confirm the phenotypic significance between different genotypes. The genotypes CC, AA, and TT at SNPs S16_1725753 (R 2 = 0.28), S10_3092310 (R 2 = 0.27), and S19_9126541 (R 2 = 0.29) showed smaller average SA, SL, and SW values compared with the genotypes TT, CC, and TT, respectively. The CC genotype in SNP S4_3279298 (R 2 = 0.21) had a smaller L/W on average than the AA genotype.

In C. moschata, 11,213 SNPs were used, and 209 SNPs exceeded the 5% significance level adjusted by the Bonferroni threshold (−log10 p value > 5.350; Figure 4c). The number of significant SNPs was 156 for SA, 121 for SL, 76 for SW, and 20 for L/W, spread over 19 chromosomes (Table S1). Phenotypes among the genotypes were also compared (Figure 4d). The genotypes CC and AA at SNPs S17_6272933 (R 2 = 0.44) and S1_12559324 (R 2 = 0.37) showed smaller average SA, SL, and SW values than the genotypes TT and GG, respectively. The GG genotype at SNP S14_15518091 (R 2 = 0.21) had a larger L/W than the AA genotype on average (Table S2).

A few physically linked SNPs were detected in either species for all four seed traits. These results suggested that common QTLs or key regulatory genes could be present that control seed morphology. These SNPs are located on chromosome 4 in C. maxima (Figure 4a, marked with an arrow) and chromosomes 6, 11, 14, and 17 in C. moschata (Figure 4c, marked with an arrow). Then, a total of 15 candidate intervals were further defined with the LD decay distance on each chromosome, where adjacent significant SNPs were located (Table 2). In these intervals, a total of 659 predicted genes were then identified using the reference genome of C. moschata var. Rifu in CuGenDB (Table S3). Among them, 32 genes have been reported to be associated with seed size regulation in Arabidopsis and Oryza sativa (Table 2). Interestingly, E3 ubiquitin‐protein ligase and leucine‐rich repeat receptor‐like protein kinase family proteins were both found in C. maxima and C. moschata. They might play a key role in seed morphology variation in Cucurbita. Further experiments are needed to confirm the functions of these genes in Cucurbita.

DISCUSSION

SNP discovery in Cucurbita species

Cucurbita species are among the most important vegetables cultivated globally because of their economic and nutritional value. However, genetic studies are still very limited for developing new varieties. In this study, we used 321 diverse commercial materials of C. maxima, C. moschata, and C. pepo to generate 78,424 confident SNPs by the GBS method with a missing rate less than 5%, which were comparable to the number of SNPs detected in other studies. For example, Nguyen et al. (2020) identified 37,869 SNPs from 41 pumpkin cultivars. H.‐Y. Lee et al. (2020) used 610 C. moschata germplasms from the RDA and USDA and only developed 2071 SNPs. In a recent study, Hernandez et al. (2023) detected over 60,000 SNPs in 1677 pumpkin accessions, with the missing rate of up to 40%. There could be two main reasons to explain why we were able to identify significant amounts of SNPs in 321 pumpkin accessions. First, the materials we investigated are highly fixed, with a minimum homozygosity of 85%. Second, the materials covered three species with diverse market types used in Asia, Europe, and America (López‐Anido, 2021). We have further demonstrated in this study that such a large number of SNPs is not only useful to understand genetic structure but is also successfully applied in association studies for commercial Cucurbita species. The applications of the generated SNPs are numerous, including the use of marker‐assisted selection to introduce important target genes efficiently.

Genetic divergence in commercial Cucurbita species

We first used the 31,456 SNPs for genetic structure analysis between three Cucurbita species. The PCA results could explain 90% of the genetic variation between species using the first two components. Gong et al. (2012) analyzed the genetic variations between nine Cucurbita species using 74 SSR markers, but the results could only explain 30% of the genetic variation. The much larger genetic variations observed in our study could be due to the larger marker number and the more uniform distribution of the markers throughout the genome. LD decay reflects population history and is known to be affected by mutation, population size, mating systems, gene flow, and selection (Gupta et al., 2005). Here, we performed the LD decay analysis on domesticated Cucurbita using 142 C. maxima, 118 C. moschata, and 61 C. pepo accessions. Among the three species, C. moschata showed the rapid LD decay of 50 kb, and C. maxima had the extended LD decay of 350 kb. Studies of melon and watermelon have shown that the LD decay distance of wild species (less than 10 kb) is much shorter than that of domesticated species (ranging from 40 to 300 kb; Guo et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020). The extreme case was 1.2 Mb in the cultivar group of Melon ssp. agrestis, suggesting that this group may have experienced a severe bottleneck during breeding improvement (Liu et al., 2020). Therefore, our results suggest that more introgressions were selected and fixed through artificial selection in C. maxima. On the other hand, the shorter LD in C. moschata reflects a lower level of inbreeding or higher genetic diversity among the three species.

Furthermore, SNP markers were used to analyze the population structure of C. moschata. A total of four genetic groups with different degrees of differentiation were determined. Combining seven major phenotypes, we defined four potential cultivar types in C. moschata. Based on their representative fruit types, they were named Pear Marble, Flat Wart, Round Marble, and Club Cream. Grouping within C. moschata was observed in earlier studies using either only phenotypic or genotypic data. Our research is the first report systemically defining the cultivar type in C. moschata. Robinson and Decker‐Walters (1997) first proposed three horticultural types among North American commercial cultivars based on fruit morphology. Gomes et al. (2020) assessed 91 accessions in Brazil, most of which were local landraces, and proposed 16 groups based on fruit traits, seed traits, and days until flowering. Recently, two reports from Korea revealed potential genetic groups within C. moschata using SNP data. The results showed that the Fst between Asian and American materials was 0.20 (H.‐Y. Lee et al., 2020). The Club Cream type in our study is similar to the North American type described by Robinson. This type is genetically the most different from the other Asian materials, as indicated by the larger Fst value of 0.3. This observation is in agreement with the results of E. S. Lee et al. (2022) and Hernandez et al. (2023). The Pear Marble type we used was mainly from Northeast Asia, while the Flat Wart and the Round Marble types were mainly from Southeast Asia, and the average Fst between these groups was 0.20. E. S. Lee et al. (2022) also found such differentiation between materials coming from these two regions (Fst = 0.15).

GWASs identified potential SNP markers and candidate genes associated with pumpkin seed traits

Pumpkin seeds are edible and highly nutritional, making them an important target for commercial breeding (Darrudi et al., 2018). We performed seed morphology GWASs in Cucurbita and successfully used 142 C. maxima and 118 C. moschata accessions to explore genomic regions associated with SL, SW, SA, and L/W. Genetic studies on pumpkin seed traits are limited. A previous report using F2 QTL mapping for SL, SW, and seed weight in C. maxima identified 10 potential regions on six chromosomes, mainly on chromosomes 6 and 17 (Y. Wang et al., 2019), and we identified 199 new SNPs distributed over 13 chromosomes associated with SL and SW. This could be due to the larger and more diverse plant materials used in our study.

A total of 14 consensus seed size QTLs of watermelon were reported by Y. Guo et al. (2020), four of which were repeatedly detected in multiple populations on three chromosomes, and their syntenic regions were located on chromosomes 6 and 17 in C. maxima. Comparing our GWAS results in C. maxima and C. moschata, significant signals were found to be associated with seed size traits (SL, SW and SA) on chromosomes 11, 17 and 19 in both species. These data suggested that more genomic regions could also be involved in seed size control in Cucurbita species.

Many genes and regulatory pathways that control seed traits have been reported in model plants such as Arabidopsis and rice. The reported pathways include the ubiquitin pathway (Li et al., 2019), the IKU pathway (A. Wang et al., 2010), hormone signaling pathways (Assefa et al., 2019), and other regulators (Si et al., 2016). In cucurbits, seed size is quantitatively inherited, and the genetic mechanism is still unknown. Based on gene annotation and previous reports, we identified 32 candidate genes that could be responsible for seed morphology traits in Cucurbita, 12 of which were E3 ubiquitin protein ligase genes and zinc finger protein genes. These genes were also proposed by Y. Guo et al. (2020), who found that E3 ubiquitin protein ligase genes and zinc finger protein genes may play important roles in controlling seed size in cucurbits. Therefore, sequence variation and functional experiments of these genes in Cucurbita materials with different seed morphologies will be interesting research targets for further understanding the genetic mechanisms of seed traits in pumpkin.

CONCLUSIONS

A set of 31,456 high‐quality SNPs was developed from 321 commercial materials in three Cucurbita species. The genetic structure analysis reflected the genetic differentiations between three Cucurbita species and defined four cultivar types within C. moschata together with phenotype data. GWASs were also performed in C. maxima and C. moschata to identify potential candidate genes related to seed morphology traits. The findings from this study provide novel information on the genetic architecture of seed traits. The results in this study will be helpful in accelerating crop improvement through the generated resource of the genomic framework for future germplasm use and selecting useful alleles related to important traits by MAS or genomic selections for prebreeding. More molecular experiments are also needed to understand the genetic mechanisms regulating seed traits in Cucurbita.
