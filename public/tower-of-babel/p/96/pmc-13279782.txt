Association of wearable sensor-based gait analysis with phenoconversion trajectories in idiopathic REM sleep behavior disorder
Authors: Shanshan Cen, Hui Zhang, Yuan Li, Yuan Yuan, Qihao Zhang, Wei Mao, Yanning Cai, Piu Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdiopathic rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) is a well-established prodromal manifestation of α-synucleinopathies, with two principal phenoconversion trajectories: parkinsonism-first and dementia-first. Although subtle gait abnormalities have been observed prior to phenoconversion, their predictive value remains unclear. We investigated whether wearable sensor–based gait parameters are associated with phenoconversion and its clinical trajectories in iRBD. Sixty-eight polysomnography-confirmed iRBD patients and 61 healthy controls were enrolled at baseline. The iRBD cohort was followed for a mean of 3.68 years, with 21 patients converting to neurodegenerative diseases; 38 patients completed follow-up gait assessment. Participants performed one-minute walking trials under normal, fast, and dual-task conditions while wearing six inertial sensors. At baseline, iRBD patients exhibited significant gait abnormalities compared with controls. Several gait parameters were more strongly associated with subsequent conversion to Parkinson’s disease (PD) than to dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), including shorter stride length, greater swing time variability, reduced arm swing range, increased arm swing variability, and lower peak arm swing velocity. Longitudinally, converters showed a steeper decline in stride length and greater increases in dual-task cost. Wearable sensor–based gait assessment may serve as a digital biomarker for predicting phenoconversion in iRBD, particularly along the parkinsonism-first trajectory.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Idiopathic REM sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) has been identified as one of the earliest and most specific prodromal manifestations of α-synucleinopathies. Longitudinal cohort studies have shown that individuals with iRBD have a cumulative risk of phenoconversion that increases over time, reaching up to 80% over 5 to nearly 30 years, with an estimated annual phenoconversion rate of ~6–8%1. Among those who convert, Parkinson’s disease (PD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) are the most common outcomes, with roughly equal risks of developing either condition, whereas conversion to multiple system atrophy (MSA) appears to be relatively rare2. Based on the initial clinical presentation, phenoconversion can be classified into parkinsonism-first or dementia-first pathways1,3.

There is a critical need to develop reliable biomarkers for identifying individuals with iRBD who are at risk of disease phenoconversion. Such biomarkers would support the implementation of preventive or early intervention strategies and enhance the design of clinical trials for disease-modifying therapies by enabling the enrichment of cohorts with individuals at high risk of near-term phenoconversion4. Moreover, given the approximately equal risk of PD and DLB among individuals with iRBD, and the marked differences between these trajectories in clinical course, treatment strategies, and possible underlying mechanisms, the ability to reliably distinguish individuals who are likely to develop dementia-first versus parkinsonism-first phenoconversion could allow for more precise subject stratification in future targeted therapeutic trials5.

In parallel, research on wearable-derived digital biomarkers has expanded rapidly in recent years. Using 7-day wrist-worn accelerometer recordings from the UK Biobank, Schalkamp et al. demonstrated that wearable accelerometry data can specifically identify Parkinson’s disease up to seven years before clinical diagnosis6. These findings strongly support the potential of wearable sensors as sensitive digital biomarkers for PD and its prodromal stages.

To date, several cross-sectional studies have shown that subtle gait abnormalities are already present in patients with iRBD7–10. Some investigations have employed instrumented gait analysis systems, which provide high-precision measurements but are costly9,11, while others have utilized wearable devices or smartphone-based approaches, which are more portable and cost-effective7,8,10,12,13. However, findings across studies have been heterogeneous, likely reflecting differences in sensing technologies, assessment environments (laboratory versus real-world), and intrinsic characteristics of iRBD cohorts. A fundamental limitation of gait studies in iRBD is the inherent uncertainty regarding the timing of phenoconversion and the specific diagnosis that will ultimately emerge, which can vary widely across individuals and may span years to decades14. Consequently, long-term longitudinal studies are required to clarify the prognostic value of gait digital biomarkers15.

In the present study, we aimed to explore the potential of wearable sensor-based gait parameters for predicting phenoconversion in a single-center iRBD cohort. Specifically, we sought to address two key questions: (1) whether gait parameters could differentially associate with distinct phenoconversion trajectories (parkinsonism-first versus dementia-first), and (2) how gait parameters change longitudinally, thereby characterizing their progression trajectories.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

At baseline, 68 PSG-confirmed iRBD patients and 61 HCs were included in the final analysis. Of the 76 iRBD patients initially enrolled, 5 were excluded due to missing data, and 3 were lost to follow-up, leaving 68 iRBD patients for analysis. These patients were prospectively followed for an average of 3.68 ± 3.09 years. During follow-up, 21 patients converted to neurodegenerative disease (converters), whereas 47 remained non-converted (non-converters). Among the converters (mean follow-up 3.20 ± 2.71 years), 10 developed PD, 10 developed DLB, and 1 developed MSA (Table 1 and Fig. 1).

iRBD patients underwent annual clinical evaluations and were instructed to complete gait examination every 2 years or at the time of phenoconversion. Gait examination was not performed between 2018 and 2019 due to technical issues, and data collection was further impacted during the COVID-19 pandemic. Overall, 38 iRBD patients (14 converters and 24 non-converters) had complete gait examination at both baseline and the most recent follow-up visit, enabling longitudinal analyses.

At baseline, HCs and iRBD patients were comparable in age at enrollment, sex, BMI, and cognitive performance. iRBD patients showed higher MDS-UPDRS-III score and markedly higher RBDQ-HK scores compared with HCs (both p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Within the iRBD cohort, sex, BMI, age at RBD onset, RBD duration, MDS-UPDRS-III score, and MMSE score were similar at baseline across phenoconversion groups. However, age at enrollment (p = 0.011), MoCA score (p = 0.015), and RBDQ-HK score (p < 0.001) differed significantly. DLB-converters were older than both non-converters (p = 0.039) and PD-converters (p = 0.012). Notably, DLB-converters exhibited lower MoCA scores than non-converters (p = 0.019), indicating worse baseline cognitive performance. In addition, non-converters reported lower RBDQ-HK scores compared with both PD-converters (p = 0.034) and DLB-converters (p = 0.001), whereas RBDQ-HK scores did not differ between the two converter groups (Table 1).

Since only one iRBD patient converted to MSA, this subgroup was excluded from subgroup analyses.

Cross-sectional comparisons of gait parameters

At baseline, iRBD patients showed gait abnormalities compared with HCs during normal walking. Specifically, iRBD patients walked more slowly, with reduced stride velocity (p < 0.001) and prolonged stride time (p < 0.001). They also demonstrated altered gait phase, including increased stance time (p = 0.016) and reduced swing time (p = 0.016). Greater gait variability was also observed, including increased swing variability (p = 0.039). Similarly, arm and trunk movements were reduced in iRBD patients, with lower peak arm swing velocity (p < 0.001) and reduced trunk horizontal range of motion (p = 0.002). Both peak horizontal (p < 0.001) and sagittal trunk velocities (p = 0.003) were also decreased compared with HCs (Supplementary Table 1).

Within the RBD cohort, gait performance declined under dual-task walking compared with normal walking, with reduced stride velocity (p = 0.009) and prolonged stride time (p = 0.042). This decline was most pronounced in DLB-converters. No significant baseline differences in gait parameters were observed between converters and non-converters. Stride velocity showed a trend toward a condition × group interaction (p = 0.065), suggesting a possible differential response to dual-task walking across phenoconversion groups. Dual-task cost in gait parameters did not differ significantly across groups (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 2–3).

Group differences emerged in arm asymmetry. Across all walking conditions, PD-converters exhibited greater peak arm swing velocity asymmetry than non-converters during normal (p = 0.017), fast (p = 0.042), and dual-task walking (p = 0.010). No significant differences were observed in dual-task cost of arm parameters across groups (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 2–3).

For trunk movement, no significant group differences or group × condition interactions were observed. However, when dual-task cost was considered, group differences emerged in trunk control, including trunk horizontal range of motion (p = 0.007) and peak horizontal trunk velocity (p = 0.035). PD-converters showed smaller dual-task cost in trunk movement compared with both non-converters (p = 0.007) and DLB-converters (p = 0.028), while no differences were observed between non-converters and DLB-converters (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 2–3).

Baseline gait parameters associated with phenoconversion

Baseline MDS-UPDRS-III score was significantly associated with phenoconversion to α-synucleinopathies (adjusted HR = 1.550, 95% CI = 1.314–1.828, p < 0.001).

Overall, baseline gait parameters reflecting reduced pace and diminished arm swing were most strongly associated with phenoconversion. During fast walking, reduced stride velocity (adjusted HR = 0.923, 95% CI = 0.861–0.989, p = 0.024), lower peak arm swing velocity (adjusted HR = 0.988, 95% CI = 0.980–0.997, p = 0.008), and decreased arm range of motion (adjusted HR = 0.938, 95% CI = 0.886–0.994, p = 0.030) were associated with a higher risk of phenoconversion.

In addition, during dual-task walking, lower peak arm swing velocity (adjusted HR = 0.989, 95% CI = 0.979–0.998, p = 0.017) and greater dual-task cost in stride length asymmetry (adjusted HR = 1.014, 95% CI = 1.001–1.028, p = 0.040) were associated with phenoconversion (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 4).

Baseline gait parameters associated with PD and DLB phenoconversion

Distinct gait features were associated with phenoconversion to PD and DLB.

Conversion to PD was associated with multiple gait parameters across walking conditions. During normal walking, greater swing variability (adjusted SHR = 1.129, 95% CI = 1.006−1.268, p = 0.040) and reduced arm range of motion (adjusted SHR = 0.901, 95% CI = 0.814−0.997, p = 0.043) were associated with an increased risk of PD conversion. During fast walking, shorter stride length (adjusted SHR = 0.878, 95% CI = 0.778−0.990, p = 0.033), lower peak arm swing velocity (adjusted SHR = 0.989, 95% CI = 0.978−0.999, p = 0.030), and greater arm range of motion variability (adjusted SHR = 1.075, 95% CI = 1.003−1.152, p = 0.042) were associated with a higher risk of PD conversion.

Under dual-task walking, lower peak arm swing velocity (adjusted SHR = 0.986, 95% CI = 0.972−1.000, p = 0.049) and reduced arm range of motion (adjusted SHR = 0.895, 95% CI = 0.802−0.998, p = 0.045) were also associated with increased PD risk. In addition, greater dual-task cost in trunk control was associated with PD conversion, including increased dual-task cost in trunk horizontal range of motion (adjusted SHR = 1.074, 95% CI = 1.012−1.139, p = 0.018) and peak horizontal trunk velocity (adjusted SHR = 1.058, 95% CI = 1.005−1.112, p = 0.030) (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 5).

Longitudinal gait parameter changes in iRBD cohort

Gait performance progressively deteriorated during follow-up in the iRBD cohort.

Stride length declined significantly under all walking conditions (normal: p = 0.002; fast: p < 0.001; dual-task: p < 0.001). This decline was more pronounced during dual-task walking in converters, who showed a faster reduction in stride length (p = 0.001) and a greater increase in dual-task cost (p = 0.006) compared with non-converters. Stride velocity also decreased over time during fast walking (p = 0.005) and dual-task walking (p < 0.001). However, the rate of decline in stride velocity did not differ between converters and non-converters. Similarly, stride time increased over follow-up during dual-task walking (p = 0.020), accompanied by a reduction in swing time (p = 0.007) and a prolongation of stance time (p = 0.007). Gait variability and asymmetry worsened progressively. Stride length variability increased over follow-up (p = 0.002), and both swing and stance asymmetry increased during normal walking (swing: p = 0.024; stance: p = 0.017) and dual-task walking (swing: p = 0.001; stance: p = 0.002). Stride length asymmetry likewise increased (p = 0.004). For these variability and asymmetry measures, longitudinal trajectories were similar between converters and non-converters (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables 7).

Group differences were also observed in arm movement. Across all walking conditions, non-converters consistently exhibited larger and faster arm movements than converters, reflected by higher peak arm swing velocity and greater arm range of motion (all p < 0.05). Over time, peak arm swing velocity declined during fast (p = 0.005) and dual-task walking (p = 0.007), while arm range of motion decreased across all walking conditions (all p < 0.05). Dual-task cost of both peak arm swing velocity and arm range of motion increased progressively over follow-up (both p < 0.001). Arm swing variability and asymmetry also worsened over time; however, the rate of change did not differ significantly between converters and non-converters (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables 7).

Trunk movement also differed between groups but showed similar longitudinal progression. Across walking conditions, non-converters showed greater horizontal trunk range of motion and velocity than converters (all p < 0.05). Sagittal trunk velocity differed between groups during fast walking only (p = 0.020). Over time, dual-task cost in trunk control increased, particularly for horizontal trunk velocity (p = 0.008). Dual-task cost of sagittal trunk range of motion also increased during follow-up (p = 0.014). However, longitudinal trajectories of trunk parameters were similar across phenoconversion groups (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables 7).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the potential of wearable-based gait analysis to predict phenoconversion in individuals with iRBD toward distinct trajectories of α-synucleinopathies. Our results demonstrated that selected gait parameters were more strongly associated with parkinsonism-first phenoconversion (greater arm range of motion variability, shorter stride length, reduced arm range of motion and peak arm swing velocity, among others) than with dementia-first phenoconversion in our RBD cohort. Moreover, baseline comparisons showed that individuals who later converted to PD exhibited greater peak arm swing velocity asymmetry compared with both DLB converters and non-converters. Importantly, longitudinal analyses revealed a faster decline in stride length during dual-task walking, as well as a greater increase in dual-task cost of stride length, among converters compared with non-converters.

It is essential to evaluate predictive biomarkers separately for each phenoconversion pathway. This heterogeneity may partly explain the inconsistent findings reported in previous digital biomarker studies in RBD cohorts14. Therefore, instead of treating phenoconversion as a single outcome, our study exploratorily examined whether wearable sensor-derived gait parameters differentially predict parkinsonism-first versus dementia-first phenoconversion. The results demonstrated that temporal-spatial gait parameters were specifically associated with parkinsonism-first phenoconversion, whereas none of these gait measures predicted dementia-first phenoconversion. This finding likely reflects the greater sensitivity of wearable gait analysis to early motor abnormalities.

In contrast, dementia-first phenoconversion may be more optimally predicted by neuropsychological assessments, as well illustrated in the study by Joza et al. and Marchand et al.16,17. Nevertheless, previous work has shown that impaired gait performance, particularly under dual-task conditions, can predict dementia or mild cognitive impairment (MCI)18,19. Dual-task paradigms require individuals to perform a secondary cognitive task simultaneously with walking, thereby challenging attention networks20,21. In individuals with dementia or MCI, who already exhibit attentional network dysfunction, gait performance typically deteriorates under dual-task conditions due to impaired automaticity21.

In our study, we additionally quantified dual-task cost (DTC) to capture this effect20. A greater dual-task cost in stride length asymmetry was the only DTC parameter associated with dementia-first phenoconversion. Notably, a higher DTC in stride length asymmetry in our study reflected a reduction in asymmetry during dual-task walking (serial subtraction of 100 − 3). Although baseline differences did not reach statistical significance, individuals who later converted to PD tended to exhibit greater stride length asymmetry during dual-task walking, whereas those who later converted to DLB showed a tendency toward lower asymmetry. A plausible explanation for this pattern is that PD-related motor dysfunction is typically characterized by greater gait asymmetry compared with DLB. Accordingly, the observed DTC findings may reflect underlying differences in motor asymmetry between phenoconversion trajectories rather than a specific dementia-related effect. Furthermore, higher dual-task costs in trunk horizontal range of motion and peak horizontal trunk velocity were associated with an increased risk of parkinsonism-first phenoconversion. These results suggest that trunk movement amplitude and velocity markedly decrease under dual-task conditions, indicating worsened rigidity and impaired axial control during cognitively demanding walking in individuals who later convert to PD22.

Longitudinal data are essential for determining whether gait parameters can reliably reflect disease progression and thus serve as progression-sensitive digital biomarkers. However, longitudinal studies using wearable sensors in RBD cohorts remain limited. Del Din et al. conducted a prospective longitudinal study to identify gait parameters as potential early diagnostic markers of PD. In that study, 696 healthy individuals were followed longitudinally with four assessments at 2-year intervals. The results demonstrated that reduced gait pace, including step length and step velocity, began to deviate from that of non-converters approximately four years prior to clinical diagnosis23. Consistent with these findings, our study showed that converters exhibited a significantly faster decline in stride length during dual-task walking, as well as a greater increase in dual-task cost of stride length, compared with non-converters. Stride length is known to be strongly modulated by dopaminergic function22,24,25; therefore, its accelerated decline among converters may reflect underlying dopaminergic dysfunction. Moreover, the increase in dual-task cost indicates a reduction in gait automaticity under cognitively demanding conditions, and thus cholinergic deficits may plausibly contribute to the observed changes26. Together, these longitudinal changes suggest that wearable sensor–based gait parameters may serve as potential digital biomarkers for monitoring disease progression during the prodromal phase.

We hope that this study could inspire future investigations to design more optimized protocols for digital biomarker research, especially in RBD cohorts. First, a recent cross-sectional study has employed real-world designs in which participants wore an accelerometer for seven consecutive days under free-living conditions, demonstrating that iRBD patients exhibit measurable gait alterations compared with healthy controls7. In contrast, laboratory-based assessments, such as those used in our study, provide a more controlled and standardized environment8. A key advantage of real-world assessment is its ability to capture gait in a natural, free-living environment while reducing participant burden and the costs of frequent travels27,28. However, real-world protocols do not permit dual-task paradigms, which typically require standardized laboratory conditions29.

Second, substantial heterogeneity exists in sensor placement across current studies30. For example, in the study by Del Din et al., the sensor was placed over the fifth lumbar vertebra, allowing capture of lower-limb gait parameters7,23. This modality may be particularly suitable for real-world monitoring, as participants are unlikely to tolerate wearing multiple sensors for prolonged periods in home settings28. However, such placement does not allow assessment of arm parameters, which are recognized as important prodromal markers31–33. In our study, baseline comparisons revealed that PD-converters exhibited greater peak arm swing velocity asymmetry compared with both DLB-converters and non-converters, suggesting that motor asymmetry may emerge earlier in the upper limbs. This observation is consistent with clinical experience, in which PD symptoms often manifest initially in the upper limbs34. Although wrist-worn sensors are widely used because of their convenience and high compliance30, they are limited in their ability to capture inter-limb asymmetry30, a key hallmark of PD35. Therefore, when designing future digital biomarker protocols, careful trade-offs are necessary to maximize the sensitivity for detecting prodromal changes.

Our study has several limitations. First, sample size is a main limitation of this study. Only 21 participants converted to α-synucleinopathies during follow-up, resulting in a relatively small number of PD and DLB converters in the subgroup analyses. This limited sample size restricts the precision and reliability of the estimated hazard ratios, particularly for subgroup outcomes. Future studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up periods, and a greater number of phenoconversion are warranted to validate these findings and to further clarify digital biomarkers associated with distinct phenoconversion trajectories. Second, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied given the exploratory nature of this study, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. In addition, a de novo PD comparison group was not available. The absence of a disease anchor limits direct contextualization of gait abnormalities relative to established PD. Future studies incorporating early-stage PD cohorts would help clarify the specificity of the identified gait features.

In summary, this study demonstrates that wearable sensor–based gait analysis may predict phenoconversion in individuals with RBD while differentiating distinct phenoconversion trajectories. Longitudinal changes, particularly a faster decline in stride length under dual-task conditions, were more strongly associated with phenoconverters. These findings support the potential of wearable-based gait analysis as digital biomarkers for early risk stratification and progression monitoring in prodromal α-synucleinopathies.

Methods

Participants

Sixty-eight patients with iRBD aged ≥50 years were consecutively recruited from the Department of Neurology at Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University, between October 2012 and December 2023. The diagnosis of iRBD was confirmed by video-polysomnography (vPSG) based on the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, Third Edition (ICSD-III)36. Comprehensive clinical evaluations were conducted to exclude secondary causes of RBD, including other neurodegenerative disorders, narcolepsy, structural lesions involving the brainstem, and drug-induced RBD. Healthy controls (HCs) were community-dwelling adults aged ≥50 years from Beijing. HCs with possible RBD symptoms were excluded through clinical interviews and questionnaire screening using the RBD Questionnaire-Hong Kong (RBDQ-HK; total score ≤ 18)37. HCs were included as a normative reference for gait parameters and were used only for baseline cross-sectional comparisons. All participants underwent neurological evaluations to exclude any symptoms or signs of dementia or Parkinsonism. Participants with severe mobility impairments due to orthopedic conditions or other neurological disorders were also excluded. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital of Capital Medical University (No. [2012]006, No. [2022]047).

Clinical assessments and follow-up visits

Demographic information and detailed clinical examination were assessed for all participants at baseline visit, including the Movement Disorders Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). iRBD patients were prospectively followed through in-person evaluations conducted by an experienced movement-disorder specialist. Follow-up visits were scheduled annually or whenever patients reported new cognitive, motor, or autonomic symptoms. Phenoconversion was recorded when patients developed Parkinsonism or dementia. The date of phenoconversion and the date of the last clinical visit were recorded. A diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease (PD) followed the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) clinical diagnostic criteria38. Multiple system atrophy (MSA) was diagnosed according to the 2008 criteria for probable MSA39. Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) was diagnosed based on the 2017 Fourth Consensus Report of the DLB Consortium40. De novo mild cognitive impairment was not considered as phenoconversion in this study.

Wearable sensor-based gait examination

All participants were instructed to walk back and forth along a 10-meter corridor for 1 minute while wearing six inertial sensors (128 Hz; Opal, APDM Inc., Portland, OR, USA) placed on the bilateral wrists and ankles, sternum, and lumbar region. Three walking conditions were assessed in the iRBD cohort: (i) normal walking at a comfortable pace, (ii) fast walking at a voluntarily accelerated pace, and (iii) dual-task walking, performed at a normal pace while continuously subtracting 3 from 100. HCs were included for cross-sectional comparison under the normal walking condition only, as the dual-task paradigm differed between groups. Patients with iRBD were asked to discontinue clonazepam 24 h before gait testing. Gait parameters were automatically extracted using the Mobility Lab version 1 (APDM). Coefficient of variation was calculated as 100 × (SD /mean), where the mean value represents the average of the left and right sides. Asymmetry was defined as the mean absolute difference between alternating left and right side for each gait cycle. Extracted gait features were categorized into established domains, including pace, rhythm, variability, asymmetry, postural control, and additional parameters describing arm and trunk. Dual-task cost (DTC) was calculated as (single-task − dual-task)/single-task × 100.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Stata version 18.

Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed prior to group comparisons. Demographic and clinical variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Gait parameters were compared using repeated-measures ANOVA (group × walking condition) among nonconverters, dementia-first converters, and parkinsonism-first converters (corresponding to DLB-converters and PD-converters, respectively). Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni correction. All analyses were adjusted for age.

To examine the associations between baseline gait parameters and overall phenoconversion to α-synucleinopathies, Cox proportional hazards regression was first applied for each gait parameter. To further evaluate phenoconversion risk while accounting for competing outcomes, competing risk regression analyses were subsequently performed. Dementia-first phenoconversion was modeled as the event of interest with parkinsonism-first phenoconversion treated as the competing event, and vice versa. The time of baseline gait examination was used as the baseline time, and the interval between baseline gait examination and phenoconversion was used as the follow-up duration. All survival analyses were adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. Sensitivity analyses were performed using a simplified model adjusting for age alone to assess the robustness of the primary findings.

Longitudinal changes in gait parameters were examined using generalized estimating equations (GEE). The actual time interval between baseline and follow-up assessments was included in the analysis, with additional adjustment for age, sex, and BMI.

Standardization for heatmap visualization

To facilitate visualization of cross-sectional and longitudinal group differences, standardized z-scores were calculated using the non-converter group as the reference.

For cross-sectional analyses, z-scores were computed for each gait parameter and walking condition as (mean_converter − mean_non-converter) / SD_non-converter.

For longitudinal analyses, z-scores were calculated as (Δ_converter − Δ_non-converter) / SD_baseline_non-converter, where Δ represents the change in each gait parameter from baseline to follow-up.
