Angiopoietin-like protein 4 deficiency promotes tumor immune infiltration and CD8+ T cell cytotoxic activity to inhibit tumor progression
Authors: Linjun Liang, Yingzi Liu, Yue Shi, Peng Zhang, Nguan Soon Tan, Liang Li, Yong Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The tumor microenvironment (TME) exhibits immunosuppressive characteristics, hindering CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor effects and restricting the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors in some cancers. ANGPTL4, a secreted glycoprotein, is implicated in the regulation of lipid metabolism, angiogenesis, and inflammation, but its role in tumorigenesis, metastasis, and the modulation of the immune microenvironment requires further investigation. We compared the differences in tumor proliferation kinetics between in vitro and in vivo models of ANGPTL4 gene knockout, to determine the role of the TME in the cancer-promoting mechanism mediated by ANGPTL4. The absence of ANGPTL4 in tumor cells or the host can inhibit tumor proliferation and metastasis. Furthermore, ANGPTL4 absence was associated with extracellular matrix remodeling and the immune responses. We identified a correlation between the absence of ANGPTL4 and low expression of Ki-67 and vimentin. ANGPTL4 exhibited spatially variable expression in tumor tissues. Finally, we confirmed ANGPTL4 deficiency slowed tumor growth and markedly increased intratumoral CD8+ T cell infiltration. Transcriptomic analyses further revealed upregulation of adaptive immune pathways, including IFN-γ production, and T cell priming in ANGPTL4-deficient tumors. Together, these results show that the loss of ANGPTL4 reprograms the TME toward a more immune-active state, enhancing CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses.

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME), which consists of tumor cells, immune cells, stromal cells, extracellular matrix, and signaling molecules, forms a complex ecosystem, with immunosuppressive characteristics constituting one of the core mechanisms underlying tumor progression and treatment resistance.1 CD8+ T cells, as the central effector cells of anti-tumor immunity, exert their anti-tumor effects by directly killing tumor cells and secreting effector molecules, such as IFN-γ and granzyme B.2 However, the intricate immunosuppressive factors within the TME, including hypoxia, acidosis, and immunosuppressive cytokines, lead to inadequate infiltration, functional exhaustion, or even apoptosis of CD8+ T cells, ultimately facilitating tumor immune evasion.3,4 Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been successfully used to reverse tumor immune evasion and improve clinical outcomes by blocking the inhibitory signals to CD8+ T cells.5 Nevertheless, for many cancer types, such as non-small cell lung cancer and primary liver cancer, ICIs exhibit low clinical response rates and high drug resistance rates.6,7 This dilemma suggests that tumor cells regulate CD8+ T cell function through non-canonical pathways, highlighting the urgent need to determine novel regulatory mechanisms to develop combined intervention strategies.

Angiopoietin-like protein 4 (ANGPTL4) is a member of the angiopoietin-like protein family, belonging to the secreted glycoprotein category.8 ANGPTL4 originates primarily from the adipose tissue, liver, macrophages, and tumor cells, and comprises an N-terminal coiled-coil domain and a C-terminal fibrinogen-like domain.9,10 Functionally, ANGPTL4 is involved in lipid metabolism, angiogenesis, and the regulation of inflammation. It plays a dual role in the protection and pathogenesis in metabolic diseases (such as diabetes), respiratory infections, and cardiovascular diseases.11,12,13 Furthermore, the pleiotropic functions of ANGPTL4 in tumorigenesis, metastasis, and immune microenvironment regulation have garnered considerable attention.

ANGPTL4 affects the proliferation and survival of tumor cells and regulates the TME to promote tumor invasion and metastasis. Tumor-derived ANGPTL4 activates NADPH oxidase-dependent O2- generation by binding to integrin receptors. The high ratio of O2-/H2O2 further triggers the PI3K/PKBα and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathways, thereby significantly enhancing the resistance of tumor cells to anoikis.14 ANGPTL4 disrupts tight junctions between endothelial cells, leading to vascular leakage and structural disorders that promote trans-endothelial migration and distant metastasis of tumor cells.15 Clinical data analysis has revealed a significant correlation between high ANGPTL4 expression and reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration as well as poor prognosis in patients with various solid tumors.16,17

In hepatocellular carcinoma, ANGPTL4 promotes the polarization of M2 macrophages. M2 macrophages secrete inhibitory cytokines, such as IL-10 and TGF-β, which suppress CD8+ T cell function and recruit regulatory T cells, ultimately leading to immune evasion.18 However, it remains unclear whether ANGPTL4 directly participates in the anti-tumor immune responses of CD8+ T cells.

We therefore compared tumor proliferation kinetics in ANGPTL4-knockout models under both in vitro and in vivo settings, to delineate the specific contribution to ANGPTL4-driven tumor progression. We revealed that ANGPTL4 operates through a dual mechanism: intrinsically promoting cancer cell proliferation and extrinsically shaping an immunosuppressive TME. This role was underscored by spatial single-cell proteomics, which revealed a negative correlation between ANGPTL4 expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration. Furthermore, CD8+ T cell-intrinsic ANGPTL4 deficiency significantly enhanced anti-tumor immunity and delayed tumor growth. Transcriptomic analyses identified that ANGPTL4 suppresses T cell effector programs by limiting cGMP-PKG and cAMP signaling pathways, thereby constraining IFN-γ production and T cell priming. We therefore infer that ANGPTL4 drives tumor progression not only by directly stimulating cancer cells but also by orchestrating a profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironment that restricts CD8+ T cell recruitment and functional activation. This comprehensive understanding positions ANGPTL4 as a promising therapeutic target for reprogramming the TME and enhancing anti-tumor immunity.

Results

ANGPTL4-deficient tumor cells show decreased cell proliferation and metastasis

We used the CRISPR-Cas9 system to generate mouse Lewis lung carcinoma knockout cells (LLC-KOs) and control cells (LLC-NCs) in LLC cells. Changes in cell proliferative capacity were analyzed through CCK-8 and clone formation assays. The CCK-8 results indicated that after 72 h of incubation, cell viability in the LLC-KO decreased by 38.1% compared to that of LLC-NC (p < 0.01) (Figure 1A). The number of clones formed in the LLC-KO group was reduced to 50.5% of that in the LLC-NC group (p < 0.01), and the clone size was significantly smaller (Figures 1B and 1D). ANGPTL4 has been reported to act on vascular endothelial cells and promote tumor metastasis.15,19 We conducted a transwell assay to evaluate the regulatory effect of ANGPTL4 on the invasive behavior of tumor cells. The results showed that the number of tumor cells passing through the basilar membrane was significantly lower in the LLC-KO group than in the LLC-NC group (Figures 1C and 1E). Quantitative analysis using crystal violet staining revealed that the optical density (OD) value of the LLC-KO group was 0.13 ± 0.03, which was 50.0% lower than that of the LLC-NC group (0.26 ± 0.01) (∗∗p < 0.01, Figure 1C). This finding suggests that the absence of ANGPTL4 impairs the ability of tumor cells to penetrate the matrix barrier, thereby inhibiting tumor invasion.

To verify whether the effects of ANGPTL4 on tumor proliferation and metastasis were consistent in vitro and in vivo, we subcutaneously transplanted LLC-KO or LLC-NC cells into 6-week-old C57BL/6 mice (n = 5). After 4 weeks of tumorigenesis, subcutaneously transplanted tumors were successfully established in all mice (Figure 1F). The growth rate of subcutaneous tumors in the LLC-KO group was significantly lower than that in the LLC-NC group (p < 0.01) (Figure 1G). At day 28, the tumor weight in the LLC-KO group was significantly lower than that in the LLC-NC group (p < 0.01) (Figure 1H).

We established lung metastasis models by injecting LLC-KO or LLC-NC cells into C57BL/6 mice and ANGPTL4−/− C57BL/6 mice via tail vein injection (n = 3 or 4 per group). Lung tissues were collected on day 28 to analyze metastatic nodules. In C57BL/6 mice, the number of visible lung nodules was lower in the LLC-KO group than in the LLC-NC group (Figure 1I). However, in ANGPTL4−/− C57BL/6 mice, there was no significant difference in the number of lung nodules between the LLC-KO and LLC-NC groups (Figure 1J). These findings indicate that tumor-derived ANGPTL4 promotes metastasis, while loss of ANGPTL4 in the host also limits tumor spread. In ANGPTL4−/− hosts, additional tumor-cell deletion of ANGPTL4 conferred no further reduction in metastatic burden, suggesting overlapping rather than additive effects between tumor and host sources of ANGPTL4 protein. This pattern supports a model in which ANGPTL4 from both compartments cooperatively shapes the TME to favor tumor progression.

ANGPTL4 regulates matrix organization and immune infiltration

To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of ANGPTL4 in the TME, we conducted transcriptome sequencing of tumor tissues from LLC-KO and LLC-NC transplanted groups of mice (Figure 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis revealed a significant upregulation of various biological processes related to the tumor microenvironment in the LLC-KO group, including extracellular matrix organization, cell-matrix adhesion, collagen metabolic processes, and cellular response to extracellular stimuli (Figure 2B). Within the immune-related functional modules, GO enrichment analysis indicated that the absence of ANGPTL4 led to the upregulation of pathways, such as the regulation of T cell proliferation, immune response, activation of immune response, cytokine production, immune effector process, and production of molecular mediators of the immune response (Figure 2B). The findings were further validated by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) results, which showed a positive regulatory trend in extracellular matrix organization, interferon-γ signaling, and T cell priming pathways in the LLC-KO group (Figures 2C–E). Spatial analysis of immune cell infiltration in the TME

To investigate the immune regulatory role of ANGPTL4 in the TME, we designed a PhenoCycler-Fusion spatial single-cell protein detection panel consisting of 15 antibodies to visualize and analyze the tumor immune landscape and spatial distribution patterns in the subcutaneous tumor tissues of wild-type C57BL/6 mice (Figures 3A and 3B). After completing cell segmentation and measuring marker expression intensity, the LLC-NC counted 118,348 cells, and the LLC-KO counted 103,466 cells. The proportion of ANGPTL4+ cells was 18.90% in the LLC-NC and 0.12% in the LLC-KO (Figure 3H). Subsequently, eight fields of view were randomly selected from either LLC-NC or LLC-KO images for quantitative analysis (Figures 3C–3G), especially for the comparison of ANGPTL4-positive vs. negative cells (Figures 3I–3M). Compared with LLC-NC, the number of ANGPTL4+ cells in LLC-KO was significantly reduced (Figure 3C), and the number of Ki-67+cells was also significantly decreased (Figure 3D). In the co-expression analysis, 17.43% of Ki-67+cells in LLC-NC co-expressed ANGPTL4 (Figure 3I), while in LLC-KO, the proportion of co-expressing cells was 0.07%, further supporting the inhibitory effect of ANGPTL4 deficiency on cell proliferation. Compared with LLC-NC, the expression of vimentin (Figure 3F) and CD31 (Figure 3G) in LLC-KO was also lower. The low expression of Vimentin may reflect a reduction in the mesenchymal characteristics of tumor cells, while the low expression of CD31 may indicate a decrease in tumor angiogenesis. In LLC-NC, the proportions of cells co-expressing ANGPTL4 with vimentin+, CD31+, and Col1A1+were 19.42%,7.26%, and 18.25%, respectively, while in LLC-KO, the proportions of co-expressing cells were 1.51%,0.07%, and 0.25%, respectively (Figures 3K–3M). Additionally, Ter119-labeled cells were widely present in the central region of the tumor in LLC-KO (Figures 3A and 3B). Although LLC-NC mice had a significantly higher proportion of immune cells than LLC-KO mice, their tumor growth rate was faster (Figures 1F and 3E). This contradictory phenomenon suggests that the immune cells in the LLC-NC group may not have effectively performed their immune surveillance function and were instead in an immunosuppressive state. This immunosuppressive state may impair the ability of immune cells to recognize and eliminate tumor cells, thereby promoting the rapid growth of tumors. Simultaneously, the proportions of ANGPTL4+ in CD45+ cells were 32.60% (LLC-NC) and 0.14% (LLC-KO), respectively (Figure 3J). This finding indicates that ANGPTL4 expression is widespread in immune cells in the LLC-NC group, and that this expression may be closely related to the functional state of immune cells. Considering the high proportion of immune cells and attenuated immune surveillance function in the LLC-NC, we hypothesized that ANGPTL4 might regulate immune cell function, leading to an immunosuppressive state, thereby influencing tumor progression.

Next, we analyzed the relationship between ANGPTL4 and immune cells in LLC-NCs. Immune cells were divided into seven major cell types using relevant markers (Figures 4A and 4B), namely helper T cells (Th), cytotoxic T cells (Tc), regulatory T cells (Tregs), M1 macrophages (M1), M2 macrophages (M2), neutrophils (NEU), and mixed leukocytes (LEU). The spatial distribution of ANGPTL4 expression in LLC-NCs was uneven, with distinct areas of high and low expression (Figures 3 and 4C). Based on the expression levels of ANGPTL4, LLC-NC cells were divided into four regions (Figure 4D). Our findings indicate a negative correlation between the expression level of ANGPTL4 and the number of CD45+ cells (Figure 4E). Subsequently, after identifying cell subpopulations in each region, we discovered that in Area 2, where ANGPTL4 expression was low, there was an increase in Tc, Th, M1 , and LEU. Conversely, immunosuppressive M2 macrophages decreased, whereas there were no significant differences in Treg cells or neutrophils (Figure 4F; Table S1). In the experiments, immune infiltration detected using pathological methods was consistent with the infiltration status inferred from the gene expression profiles, suggesting that ANGPTL4 modulates immune infiltration within the tumor microenvironment and regulates the anti-tumor activity of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. Although total CD45+ frequency was higher in LLC-NC, subset analysis showed a shift toward immunostimulatory Tc, Th, and M1, and away from M2 in low-ANGPTL4 regions, indicating that immune composition, not total immune cell load, tracks anti-tumor activities. ANGPTL4 deficiency promotes anti-tumor activity of CD8+ T cells

C57BL/6 mice with a conditional knockout of ANGPTL4 in CD8+ T cells (ANGPTL4CD8-/-) and their wild type counterparts (ANGPTL4f/f) were subjected to subcutaneous tumor implantation to investigate the regulatory role of ANGPTL4 in CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. The results demonstrated that compared to the ANGPTL4f/f control group, the tumor growth rate was significantly delayed in ANGPTL4CD8-/- mice (Figure 5A), with tumor volumes on day 18 at 338.5+-82.12 mm3 (ANGPTL4CD8-/-) vs. 577.9+-106.8 mm3 (ANGPTL4f/f) (Figure 5B), indicating that the absence of ANGPTL4 markedly enhanced the anti-tumor activity of CD8+ T cells. We isolated CD8+ T cells from subcutaneous tumor tissues of ANGPTL4 CD8-/- and ANGPTL4 f/f mice for bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis (|log2 FC| > 2, p < 0.05) revealed that compared to the ANGPTL4 f/f group, 429 genes were significantly upregulated, while 523 genes were downregulated in the ANGPTL4 CD8-/- group. GSEA revealed significant enrichment of gene sets related to adaptive immune response (GO:0002250, NES = 2.64, p < 0.001), type II interferon production (GO:0032729, NES = 2.42, p < 0.001), T cell activation (GO:0050870, NES = 2.33, p < 0.001), and positive regulation of interleukin-2 production (GO:0032743, NES = 2.26, p < 0.001) in the CD8+ T cells of the ANGPTL4 CD8-/- group, compared to their wild-type counterparts (Figure 5E). This suggests that the absence of ANGPTL4 promotes T cell priming and proliferation, systematically enhancing the effector functions of CD8+ T cells. Further KEGG pathway analysis identified significant upregulation of the cGMP-PKG signaling pathway (mmu04022, enrichment factor = 3.18, p = 0.004) and the cAMP signaling pathway (mmu04024, enrichment factor = 2.46, p = 0.016) in the ANGPTL4 CD8-/- group, compared to their wild-type counterparts (Figure 5F). Validation of key genes showed increased Pik3r5 (a node gene in the cGMP-PKG pathway) and Adcy1 (an effector in the cAMP pathway) expression in ANGPTL4-deficient CD8+ T cells, compared to their wild-type counterparts.

Discussion

Our results reveal that the absence of ANGPTL4 in tumor cells or the host can inhibit tumor proliferation and metastasis. The deletion of ANGPTL4 was associated with extracellular matrix remodeling and the immune response. Single-cell spatial proteomics revealed a correlation between the absence of ANGPTL4 and low expression of Ki-67 and vimentin. ANGPTL4 exhibited spatially variable expression in tumor tissues. Regions with low ANGPTL4 expression showed an increase in Tc, Th, M1, and LEU, and a decrease in immunosuppressive M2 macrophages. Finally, we confirmed that tumor growth was slowed without ANGPTL4, with a significant increase in CD8+ T cell tumor infiltration. Sequencing results suggest an augmentation in adaptive immune response, IFN-γ production, and T cell priming. Therefore, the absence of ANGPTL4 alters immune infiltration in the TME and modulates the anti-tumor response of CD8+ T cells. Blocking ANGPTL4 expression in CD8+ T cells enhances their anti-tumor activity and inhibits tumor growth.

The immune system regulates the occurrence, development, and treatment response of tumors.21 The immune system can recognize and destroy newly formed tumor cells or inhibit tumor metastasis and diffusion. However, this can also reshape tumor cells, leading to immune evasion.22,23 This process involves precise regulation of the balance between tumor promotion and suppression, suggesting that the co-evolution of the tumor parenchyma and microenvironment is a core driving force for the progression of malignant tumors.24,25 A deeper understanding of the interactions between tumor and immune cells within the TME can offer new therapeutic strategies.

ANGPTL4 is highly expressed in various tumor cells, including ovarian, lung, and colorectal cancer cells. Associating with multiple signaling pathways, including ERK1/2 and JAK2-STAT3, ANGPTL4 enhances tumor cell survival, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis.15,26,27 For instance, in ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma, ANGPTL4 promotes tumor cell proliferation and migration by activating the JAK2/STAT3 pathway. Simultaneously, it interacts with endothelial cell-specific molecule-1 (ESM1), reprograming lipid metabolism and accelerating angiogenesis.27 In this study, we constructed an ANGPTL4 knockout model and observed that the absence of ANGPTL4 significantly inhibited tumor growth. This result is consistent with previous studies indicating that high ANGPTL4 expression in tumor cells promotes malignant progression. Therefore, it can be inferred that the lack of ANGPTL4 in tumor cells can effectively block their intrinsic cancer-promoting signaling network, including critical aspects, such as proliferation, metabolism, and microenvironment remodeling, thereby suppressing the malignant behavior of tumor cells.

This study systematically compared the differences in tumor proliferation kinetics between in vitro and in vivo models of ANGPTL4 gene knockout, revealing the critical role of the TME in the cancer-promoting mechanism mediated by ANGPTL4. In vitro cell proliferation experiments demonstrated that while ANGPTL4 knockout significantly inhibited the proliferation rate of tumor cells, it did not completely halt cell proliferation or colony formation. Upon further observation of in vivo tumor growth, we found that the inhibitory effect of ANGPTL4 knockout on tumor growth was more pronounced in vivo than in vitro. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fundamental differences in tumor cell growth regulatory mechanisms between in vitro cultures and in vivo environments. In vitro cultures cannot fully represent the complex interactions between tumor, immune, stromal, and vascular endothelial cells that occur in vivo.28 In the in vivo environment, tumor development is not solely determined by the autonomous proliferative potential of malignant cells but is closely related to the dynamic interactions within the TME. The TME drives the spatiotemporal evolution of tumor biological behavior through multidimensional regulation involving stromal cells (such as cancer-associated fibroblasts), immune infiltration, extracellular matrix remodeling, and metabolic signaling networks.25,29

Two mouse models of metastatic tumors were used to investigate the regulatory role of ANGPTL4 in the TME: a tumor cell-specific ANGPTL4 knockout model (LLC-NC vs. LLC-KO) and a systemic host ANGPTL4 knockout model (C57 vs. ANGPTL4-/- C57). By comparing tumor progression in these two models, we observed that both the autonomous secretion of ANGPTL4 by tumor cells and the presence of ANGPTL4 in the host microenvironment significantly promoted the proliferation and metastasis of tumor cells. These findings suggest that ANGPTL4 promotes tumor progression at the cellular level and acts within the host tumor microenvironment, synergistically driving malignant tumor behavior.

Using full-slice imaging technology combined with digital pathological analysis, we discovered that ANGPTL4 exhibits significant spatial heterogeneity of expression within tumor tissues and that its expression level is negatively correlated with the degree of immune cell infiltration. We conducted a detailed regional classification of tumor slices based on the expression levels of ANGPTL4. Regions with low ANGPTL4 expression showed a significant increase in the proportion of CD8+ T cells and M1 macrophages, and a decrease in M2 macrophages. These findings suggest that ANGPTL4 inhibits the recruitment of CD8+ T cells to tumors. ANGPTL4 affects immune cell infiltration by regulating chemokine expression and stability.30 In a DSS-induced colitis model, the absence of ANGPTL4 led to a significant upregulation of chemokines (such as CCL2, CCL11, and CXCL10) in the colonic tissue, resulting in massive immune cell infiltration into the lamina propria and a marked increase in the number of neutrophils and macrophages.30 ANGPTL4 can bind to integrin β1 and β5, activating the FAK-Src-PAK1 signaling pathway to induce keratinocyte migration, promoting wound healing.19 This indicates that ANGPTL4 is crucial in regulating cell migration via integrin signaling. Furthermore, ANGPTL4 modulates connections between vascular endothelial cells through integrin signaling, affecting vascular permeability and integrity.15 However, it remains to be proven whether the absence of ANGPTL4 enhances the ability of CD8+ T cells to penetrate the vascular barrier or extracellular matrix.

Compared to control mice (ANGPTL4f/f), mice with a specific CD8+ T cell ANGPTL4 gene knockout (ANGPTL4 CD8-/-) exhibited significantly slower tumor growth rates (Figures 5A and 5B). This suggests that ANGPTL4 may play an inhibitory or negative regulatory role in the anti-tumor immune response of CD8+ T cells, and its absence can restore the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells against tumor cells. Further observations revealed a profound enhancement in the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into tumor tissues. This implies that under conditions of ANGPTL4 deficiency, CD8+ T cells can more effectively penetrate tumor tissues, corroborating previous experimental findings showing a negative correlation between ANGPTL4 expression and the infiltration of certain immune cell types. Additionally, sequencing results indicated significant enrichment of gene sets related to adaptive immune responses, IFN-γ production, and T cell priming in the ANGPTL4-deficient group. As a crucial immunomodulatory factor, IFN-γ promotes the priming and proliferation of T cells, exerting a profound immunomodulatory effect.31 These data suggest that ANGPTL4 plays an immunosuppressive role in the TME by inhibiting the priming and effector functions of CD8+ T cells.

In this study, we constructed mouse models with tumor cell-specific and systemic ANGPTL4 deletion and observed that both tumor cell-autonomous secreted and host microenvironment-derived ANGPTL4 drive tumor progression. ANGPTL4 is heterogeneously expressed in the TME, and areas of high ANGPTL4 expression are associated with reduced tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells and decreased anti-tumor activity of CD8+ T cells. Specific knockout of ANGPTL4 in CD8+ T cells enhances their infiltration and increases IFN-γ-driven T cell priming and tumor-killing efficiency. This discovery reveals the dual role of ANGPTL4 in tumor progression, acting as both a “tumor cell-autonomous driver” and a “host microenvironment regulator”.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatments

Mouse LLC cells were maintained in Li Liang lab of the Southern University of Science and Technology. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (C11995500BT, Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (10019-141C, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15140122, Gibco), placed in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. When the cell confluence reached 80%–90%, subculturing was performed using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (25200072, Gibco). The cells were centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 1 min and reseeded at a 1:3 ratio. The medium was replaced with fresh medium every 48 h. Cell morphology and viability (>95%) were monitored daily by microscopy. Cells from passages 3 to 10 were used for all experiments.

ANGPTL4 knockout LLC cells (LLC-KO) were generated using a CRISPR-Cas9 system (Hanheng Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). LLC cells were infected with target and control viruses for 24 h at the recommended multiplicity of infection of 30. Cells in good condition were selected for continued culture 48 h post-infection. After obtaining the required number of cells, they were treated with 0.50 μg/mL puromycin (HY-B1743A; MedChemExpress) to screen for successfully infected cells. Sanger sequencing was performed to detect indel mutations in the target gene, and monoclonal cell strains were obtained using the dilution method. Finally, quantitative PCR (qPCR) and western blotting were performed to confirm loss of protein expression.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 1,000 cells per well, with 200 μL of complete medium added to each well. The plate was placed in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. Subsequently, at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, 20 μL of CCK-8 reagent (GK10001, GLPBIO) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h, avoiding bubble formation during this period. Finally, the absorbance (OD value) was determined at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader, and the cell proliferation curve was plotted.

Clone formation assay

Cells in logarithmic growth phase were harvested and digested with trypsin to prepare a single-cell suspension. Each experimental group was inoculated with 1 × 103 cells per well in a six-well culture plate and gently shaken to mix evenly. The plates were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 7–10 days, with regular observation of clone growth and medium replacement 1–2 times during incubation.

After discarding the culture medium, the cells were gently washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. The plates were rinsed with running water and allowed to dry. Clones with a diameter of ≥50 μm were counted visually under a microscope. The experiment was performed in triplicates.

Mouse strains

All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment (temperature 22 ± 1°C, 12-h light/dark cycle), and the experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southern University of Science and Technology (approval No.: SUSTech-JY202209008). Female mice aged 6–8 weeks were used in all experiments. The mouse strains included C57BL/6, ANGPTL4-/- C57BL/6, ANGPTL4f/f C57BL/6, and ANGPTL4CD8-/- C57BL/6 mice. C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Vital River Laboratories. The ANGPTL4-/- C57BL/6 mice are systemic ANGPTL4 knockout mice, while the ANGPTL4f/f C57BL/6 mice have loxP sequences inserted on both sides of a specific exon of the ANGPTL4 gene. ANGPTL4CD8-/- C57BL/6 mice were specific for CD8+ T cell ANGPTL4 knockout. All three genetically engineered mice were obtained from Li Liang Laboratory of the Department of Pharmacology at the School of Medicine, Southern University of Science and Technology. Mice used in these experiments underwent PCR genotyping.

In vivo models

For the subcutaneous tumor model, LLC-NC or LLC-KO cells (5 × 105 cells/mouse) were inoculated subcutaneously into the right flank of 6-week-old female mice. Tumor volumes were measured every 3 days using the formula V = (length × width2)/2. On day 28, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were harvested and weighed. For the lung metastasis model, LLC-NC and LLC-KO cells (1 × 106 cells/mouse) were injected into mice via the tail vein. The mice were euthanized on day 28, and lung tissues were counted for the surface metastatic nodules.

Transcriptome sequencing

Tumor tissues were rapidly collected, and frozen in liquid nitrogen under sterile conditions. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol, and RNA integrity was confirmed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and Nanodrop 2000 detection (RIN ≥8.0, A260/280 = 1.8–2.2). The sequencing library was constructed using the NEB Next Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina Nova Seq 6000 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads. Raw data were quality controlled using FastQC, aligned to the mm10 genome using HISAT2, and quantified transcripts using StringTie. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 (|log2FC| ≥ 1, p < 0.05), and enrichment analysis of GO terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was performed with clusterProfiler.

PhenoCycler-Fusion

Subcutaneous tumor tissues from mice were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin to prepare 4 μm pathological sections. After dewaxing, antigen retrieval (pH 9.0 EDTA buffer, 95°C for 20 min), and serum blocking, an iterative staining strategy was employed, with 3–4 antibodies labeled in each round (including 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole [DAPI] for nuclear staining). Tyramide signal amplification was combined with fluorescent dyes (Cy3/Cy5/AF488) and chemical elution for repeated staining cycles. The antibody panel included antibodies against CD45, CD3e, CD8, FOXP3, Ly6g, F4/80, CD206, vimentin, Col1A1, CD31, Ter119, Ki-67, and ANGPTL4, and was stained together with DAPI. The tissue sections were scanned using the PhenoCycler (AKOYA Biosciences) at 20× magnification. Spectral unmixing was applied to eliminate the fluorescence spillover, allowing the acquisition of multichannel high-resolution images. Cell segmentation was performed using QuPath with the nucleus-cytoplasm expansion algorithm. Immune cell phenotyping was based on fluorescence intensity as follows: cytotoxic T cells (CD3+CD8+), helper T cells (CD3+CD8-), Treg cells (CD3+FOXP3+), M1 macrophages (F4/80+CD206-), M2 macrophages (F4/80+CD206+), mixed leukocytes (CD45+CD3-), red blood cells (Ter119+), vascular endothelial cells (CD31+), and fibroblasts (Col1A1+). The spatial proximity of ANGPTL4+ regions to immune/stromal cells was assessed using CellNeighbor analysis.

Statistical analysis

R (v.4.1.2) and GraphPad Prism 9.5 were used for all statistical tests. Statistical comparisons were conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, unless otherwise noted. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
