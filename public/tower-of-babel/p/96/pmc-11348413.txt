Total Synthesis and Anti-inflammatory Activity of Asperjinone and Asperimide C
Authors: Kittisak Thongpat, Natthawat Milehman, Worarat Rojanaverawong, Pannita Holasut, Sunhapas Soodvilai, Chutima S. Vaddhanaphuti, Kwanruthai Tadpetch
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Total syntheses of two γ-butenolide natural products, asperjinone (1) and asperimide C (2) in both racemic and chiral forms have been accomplished utilizing Basavaiah’s one-pot Friedel–Crafts/maleic anhydride formation protocol as a key strategy. Our syntheses verified the revised structure of 1 proposed by Williams et al. and the structure and absolute configuration of 2 reported by the Li group. This work also discloses the unprecedented anti-inflammatory activity of 1. Synthetic 1 exhibited significant anti-inflammatory activity in renal proximal tubular epithelial cells (RPTEC) by suppression of gene expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 under LPS-induced renal inflammation condition and was superior to (S)-1, rac-2, 2, and a positive drug control, indomethacin. Moreover, compound 1 inhibited downstream signaling of inflammation by significantly reducing iNOS and COX-2 gene expression and total NO production. The anti-inflammatory activity of asperjinone (1) renders it a potential and promising candidate for developing novel anti-inflammatory agents against inflammation worsening acute kidney injury.

Maleic anhydride and maleimide-containing natural products are naturally occurring γ-butenolide secondary metabolites isolated from fungi and other microorganisms. This family of natural compounds has been shown to exhibit various and promising biological activities.1 A subgroup of these natural products possessing aryl substituents at the 3- or 4-positions are less commonly found, and their biological activities are underexplored. Asperjinone (1), a nor-neolignan natural product containing a maleic anhydride core, was isolated from the EtOAc extract of Aspergillus terreus (C9408-3) by Liaw et al. in 2012.2 The originally proposed epoxide-substituted isoprenyl side chain of asperjinone was later revised to a 3-hydroxybenzopyran using computer-assisted structure elucidation methods by Williams and co-workers (Figure 1).3 It should be noted that the absolute configuration and biological activities of 1 were not determined due to the limited amount isolated (1.0 mg). In 2018, Li and co-workers reported the isolation and anti-inflammatory activity of a maleimide analogue of asperjinone, namely asperimide C (2), from a tropical endophytic fungus Aspergillus terreus SC1550 (Figure 1).4 Compound 2 exhibited potent inhibitory effects on nitric oxide production in lipopolysaccharide-mediated RAW 264.7 cells with an IC50 value of 0.78 ± 0.06 μM without cytotoxicity (indomethacin was used as a positive control with IC50 = 37.5 ± 1.6 μM). There has been no report on the synthesis of compounds 1 and 2, which prompted us to synthesize both compounds in both racemic and chiral forms in order to verify the structures of the natural products and the absolute configuration of 2 and to evaluate their anti-inflammatory activities.

Structures of asperjinone (1) and asperimide C (2).

The retrosynthesis of racemic asperimide C (rac-2) and racemic asperjinone (1) is depicted in Scheme 1. Racemic asperimide C (rac-2) would be prepared from 1 via imide formation. We envisioned that asperjinone (1) could be prepared in one step from acrylate 3 and chromanol 4 using Basavaiah’s protocol.5 Although the Basaviah group reported this transformation with simple substrates such as benzene and monosubstituted benzene substrates, the more elaborate substrate such as chromanol 4 has not been showcased. We expected that chromanol 4 would be a viable substrate and the alkoxy moiety of 4 would be a more powerful directing group to direct the desired regioselectivity in the Friedel–Crafts alkylation with acrylate 3.

Retrosynthesis of rac-Asperimide C (rac-2) and rac-Asperjinone (1)

Our synthesis began with the preparation of Friedel–Crafts alkylation precursors 3 and 4 (Scheme 2). A synthesis of compound 3 was achieved in three steps starting from Friedel–Crafts acylation of phenol with ethyl chloroglyoxylate to give ethyl 2-(4-hydroxyphenyl)-2-oxoacetate (5) in 54% yield, which was converted to TBS ether 6 using TBSCl in 96% yield. The key Morita–Baylis–Hillman (MBH) reaction between 6 and 2 equiv of methyl acrylate in the presence of DABCO (1 equiv) at ambient temperature furnished the adduct 3 in 35% yield (43% yield based on unreacted 6). It is worth noting that protected phenol 6 undergoes faster MBH reaction compared to 5, which took more than 10 days with incomplete consumption of the starting 5. Synthesis of chromanol 4 was also done in two steps via treatment of phenol with NaH and prenyl bromide in toluene to give ortho-prenylated product 7 in 78% yield.6 We have found that an alternative route via cross metathesis of ortho-prenylated phenol and 2-methyl-2-butene gave slightly lower yield of 7. Subsequent oxidation of 7 using m-CPBA in the presence of p-toluenesulfonic acid in CH2Cl2 resulted in the formation of the desired chromanol 4 in 64% yield.7

With the desired precursors 3 and 4 in hand, we proceeded with Friedel–Crafts alkylation/maleic anhydride formation using Basavaiah’s protocol (Scheme 3). Treatment of 3 and 1.7 equiv of 4 with 4 equiv of methanesulfonic acid in dichloroethane at 80 °C for 6 h furnished the requisite asperjinone (1) in 37% isolated yield after purification by reversed-phase preparative HPLC.8 Although the isolated yield of 1 was modest, we were quite satisfied considering that this protocol could affect three transformations: Friedel–Crafts alkylation, hydrolysis, and anhydride formation in a one-pot fashion. In addition, it proved that this protocol was amenable to the more elaborate substrate, as we did not observe the products from side reactions such as alcohol elimination. The 1H and 13C NMR data of synthetic 1 were identical to those reported for the natural product by the Liaw group, thus verifying the revised structure of asperjinone proposed by Williams et al. Conversion of asperjinone to rac-asperimide C was smoothly accomplished by heating with 6 equiv of urea at 150 °C for 2 h which gave rac-2 in 53% yield.9 The 1H and 13C NMR data of synthetic rac-2 were also identical to those reported for asperimide C by the Li group. It is worth mentioning that attempts to directly form rac-asperimide C from the nitrile analogue of 3 (prepared from 6 and acrylonitrile) was unsuccessful since under the same reaction conditions only resulted in the Friedel–Crafts alkylation product without formation of the maleimide core.

Our next task was to enantioselectively synthesize (S)-asperjinone ((S)-1) and asperimide C (2) with the established protocol to confirm the absolute configuration of the natural product. We chose to install a chiral epoxide via a chiral diol, which would in turn be accessible from Sharpless asymmetric dihydroxylation. The synthesis of (S)-1 and 2 thus began with protection of the phenol moiety of 7 with a benzyl group to give benzyl ether 9 in 91% yield (Scheme 4). Sharpless asymmetric dihydroxylation of 9 using AD-mix-β in the presence of methanesulfonamide at 7–10 °C furnished the chiral diol 10 in 78% yield and 90% ee (determined by chiral HPLC). Selective mesylation of chiral diol 10 occurred smoothly to provide mesylate 11 with the desired regioselectivity in 88% yield. Subsequent treatment of alcohol 11 with NaH in THF afforded chiral epoxide 12 in 87% yield. The next task was to remove the benzyl protecting group using hydrogenolysis conditions (H2, Pd/C in EtOAc). We noticed that under these conditions the in situ opening of the epoxide by the free phenol occurred to result in the formation of regioisomeric mixture of dihydrobenzopyran and dihydrobenzofuran alcohols. After extensive experimentation, it was discovered that adding 0.5 equiv of acetic acid promoted the formation of the requisite chiral chromanol (S)-4 in 72% yield. The absolute configuration of (S)-4 was confirmed to be S via Mosher’s ester analysis (see Supporting Information), which was in accordance with the stereochemical outcome predicted by the Sharpless mnemonic for asymmetric dihydroxylation.10 Chromanol (S)-4 was then subjected to Friedel–Crafts alkylation with MBH alcohol 3 under the same reaction conditions used in synthesis of 1 to give (S)-asperjinone ((S)-1) in 25% isolated yield after sequential purification by silica gel column chromatography and reversed-phase preparative HPLC. Alternatively, (S)-1 obtained from silica gel column chromatography which contained a small amount of the ortho adduct could be directly converted to asperimide C by treating with urea at 150 °C to give 2 in 20% yield after purification by reverse phase preparative HPLC. The specific rotation of synthetic 2 ([α]D25 +6.1 (c 0.61, MeOH)) was closely similar to the reported value [α]D20 +4.4 (c 0.62, MeOH) for natural 2, thus verifying the S-absolute configuration proposed by the Li group.

Because no biological activity of asperjinone was reported and its maleimide analogue, asperimide C, showed excellent anti-inflammatory activity, we therefore evaluated the anti-inflammatory activity of compounds 1 and 2 in both racemic and chiral forms in human renal proximal tubule epithelial cells (RPTEC). This RPTEC cell line was chosen in this study because the inflammation in the kidneys is considered as a main cause of acute kidney injury that could lead to morbidity and mortality.11 The renal inflammation could be evidenced by the generation and release of several pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6).12 These cytokines could induce renal inflammation by binding to their receptors12 as well as death receptors13 at the plasma membrane and further activate JAK/STAT/NFκB pathways14 which then cause the inflammation, cell apoptosis, and cell death leading to acute kidney injury. Hence, the cytotoxicity of 1, (S)-1, rac-2, and 2 or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was initially tested in RPTEC using cell viability assay. The cells were exposed to 1, (S)-1,rac-2, and 2 or indomethacin as a positive drug control in the presence and absence of LPS for 24 h. Results showed that all compounds had no cytotoxic effects on RPTEC (Figure 2, left panel). Likewise, the viability of cells that were treated with LPS in the presence or absence of tested compounds showed no differences (Figure 2, right panel), which confirmed that LPS and all the tested compounds had no effects on the RPTEC cell viability.

To investigate the anti-inflammatory activity of 1, (S)-1, rac-2 and 2, the expression level of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes i.e. TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 were measured using qPCR assay. The level of all the genes treated with 10 μg/mL of 1, (S)-1, rac-2, 2 (26 μM), and indomethacin (28 μM) are shown in relative fold change (RFC) compared to untreated groups. As shown in Figure 3A, only 1 and indomethacin significantly reduced the expression of TNF-α gene at approximately 38% for both. In addition, 1 was the only candidate that could ameliorate the level of IL-1β by 24%, whereas rac-2 displayed increased level of IL-1β expression and the chiral compounds (S)-1 and 2 did not affect this gene expression (Figure 3B). Compounds 1, (S)-1, and 2 had no effects on the expression of the IL-6 gene compared to the untreated cells while rac-2 showed the opposite effect by increasing the IL-6 gene expression (Figure 3C). Furthermore, when inflammation was induced by LPS, all these genes had up-regulated expression compared to that of the untreated cells (Figure 3D–F). Nonetheless, 1 markedly down-regulated the expression of the TNF-α gene for approximately 67% compared to LPS-treated cells whereas (S)-1, rac-2, 2, and indomethacin mitigated TNF-α gene expression by 57%, 44%, 58%, and 64%, respectively, compared to LPS-treated cells (Figure 3D). Similarly, IL-1β gene expression was significantly reduced by 1 and indomethacin at 61% and 56%, respectively, whereas (S)-1, rac-2, and 2 equally decreased the IL-1β gene expression by 52% compared to LPS-treated cells (Figure 3E). Similar to the results of TNF-α and IL-1β gene expression, synthetic 1 and indomethacin displayed a higher degree of alleviation of pro-inflammatory IL-6 gene expression by 55% and 57%, respectively, compared to the alleviation by (S)-1 (48%), rac-2 (44%), and 2 (39%) (Figure 3F). From these results, it is obvious that synthetic 1 could suppress TNF-α and IL-1β gene expression under physiological conditions whereas other compounds and indomethacin did not. Additionally, under LPS-induced inflammation conditions, compound 1 could reduce the expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 to a higher degree compared to other tested compounds and the positive control, indomethacin.

Because the NFκB pathway also activates the release of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) resulting in the generation of NO and inflammation,15 we then further determined downstream NFκB signaling including the level of iNOS gene expression, total NO production, and its end products: nitrite (NO2–) and nitrate (NO3–). As shown in Figure 4A–D (left panel), all four compounds had no effects on the level of iNOS gene expression as well as total NO, NO2–, and NO3– under noninduction by LPS which represents physiological conditions. As expected, under LPS-induced inflammation conditions, compounds 1, (S)-1, rac-2, 2, and indomethacin at 10 μg/mL significantly reduced iNOS gene expression by 67%, 47% 57%, 52%, and 59%, respectively, as shown in the right panel of Figure 4A. In addition, under LPS-induced inflammation conditions, 1, (S)-1, rac-2, and indomethacin markedly and equally decreased NO2– by 45% while 2 showed significantly lower effect (24%) when compared to that of LPS-treated cells (Figure 4B, right panel). Nonetheless, none of these compounds had a significant effect on NO3– concentration in both basal and LPS-induced inflammation conditions (Figure 4C). The total NO level which is indicated by the summation of NO2– and NO3– concentration in the cells treated with 1, (S)-1, rac-2, and indomethacin showed significantly lower degree than that of LPS-treated cells for approximately 35%, 33%, 37%, and 38%, respectively, while 2 decreased total NO production for only approximately 18% (Figure 4D, right panel). Thus, asperjinone (1) seems likely to be a better candidate than the other tested compounds for development as an anti-inflammatory agent due to its higher potency to inhibit downstream signaling of inflammation.

Further evaluation of the anti-inflammatory activity was performed on cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) gene expression, as the NFκB cascade and NO also activate the release of COX-2, involved in the generation of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and inflammation.16 As shown in Figure 5A, under basal conditions, 1 decreased the expression of COX-2 gene by approximately 20% whereas rac-2 up-regulated and (S)-1, 2, and indomethacin had no effects on this gene. However, under LPS-induced conditions, the level of COX-2 gene expression was alleviated by 1, (S)-1, rac-2, 2, and indomethacin for approximately 61%, 49%, 53%, 52%, and 61%, respectively (Figure 5B). Hence, this study further verified that asperjinone (1) has higher potency than others due to its strong anti-inflammatory effects under LPS-induced inflammation conditions.

In conclusion, the total syntheses of racemic asperjinone (1), (S)-asperjinone ((S)-1), racemic asperimide C (rac-2), and asperimide C (2) have been accomplished utilizing Basavaiah’s one-pot Friedel–Crafts/maleic anhydride formation protocol as a key strategy. Our syntheses verified the revised structure of 1 proposed by Williams et al. and the structure and absolute configuration of 2 reported by the Li group. This work also discloses the unprecedented anti-inflammatory activity of asperjinone and possibly this class of natural products. Synthetic 1 exhibited significant anti-inflammatory activity in RPTEC cells by suppression of gene expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 under LPS-induced inflammation conditions and was superior to (S)-1, asperimide C (rac-2 and 2), and the positive control, indomethacin. Moreover, 1 inhibited downstream signaling of inflammation by significantly reducing the iNOS and COX-2 gene expression and the total NO production. This novel anti-inflammatory activity of asperjinone (1) renders it a potential and promising candidate for developing anti-inflammatory agents against inflammation worsening acute kidney injury.

Experimental Section

General Experimental Procedures

Unless otherwise stated, all reactions were conducted under a nitrogen atmosphere in oven- or flamed-dried glassware. Solvents were used as received from suppliers or distilled prior to use using standard procedures. Anhydrous solvents were obtained using an INERT PureSolv Solvent Purification System. All other reagents were obtained from commercial sources and used without further purification. The specific rotations were recorded on a JASCO P-2000 polarimeter. Infrared (IR) spectra were recorded on a PerkinElmer 783 FTS165 FT-IR spectrometer. 1H, 13C, DEPT, and 2D NMR spectroscopic data were recorded on 300 or 500 MHz Bruker FTNMR UltraShield spectrometers. 1H NMR spectra are reported in ppm on the δ scale and referenced to the internal tetramethylsilane. High-resolution mass spectra were obtained on an ultraperformance liquid chromatography–high resolution mass spectrometer (Agilent LC-QTOF 6500 system), Mae Fah Luang University. Column chromatography was performed on silica gel 60 (0.063–0.200 mm, Merck). Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was performed on silica gel 60 F254 (Merck) and visualized under a UV lamp (254 nm) or with cerium ammonium molybdate stain. Reversed-phase preparative HPLC purification was performed at room temperature on Buchi PrepPure C18 (250 × 20 mm, 10 μM, 100 Å) columns using a Buchi Pure C-850 FlashPrep HPLC system (BÜCHI Labortechnik AG) that was eluted with deionized H2O (solvent A), MeOH (solvent B), and MeCN (solvent C) at a flow rate of 15 mL/min. Continuous monitoring of the effluent was achieved with an integrated multiple wavelength UV detector which was set from 254 to 400 nm. The enantiopurity of (R)-diol 10 was determined by HPLC on an Agilent series 1200 equipped with a diode array UV detector using a CHIRALPAK AS-H column (15 cm) and a guard column (1 cm).

Racemic Asperimide C (rac-2)

Biological Experimental Procedures

Cell Culture

Human renal proximal tubule (RPTEC) cell line was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic and grown in an incubator containing 95% O2, 5% CO2, at a temperature of 37 °C. The cells were then seeded at the concentration of and 5 × 104 cells/well in a 96-well plate and a total of 2 × 105 cells in 60 × 15 mm Petri-dish, respectively, and cultured for 3 days for further experiments. Indomethacin, a nonsteroidal inflammatory drug (NSAID), was used as a positive control in each experiment.

Determination of Cell Viability

The viability of the RPTEC cells was detected with a 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,3-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Briefly, the confluent cells were incubated in the presence or absence of 1 μg/mL of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and asperjinone (1), (S)-asperjinone ((S)-1), rac-asperimide C (rac-2), asperimide C (2), or indomethacin, a positive drug control, at a dose of 10 μg/mL for 24 h. Subsequently, MTT solution was added to the samples at the final concentration of 0.5 μg/mL for the next 4 h, after which the assay solution was removed. The cells were then dissolved to become the formazan precipitate by DMSO:ethanol (v/v) at a ratio of 1:1, and the samples were measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Biotek).

Determination of the Proinflammatory Gene Expression

The expressions of proinflammatory genes in RPTEC cells were detected using the quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) technique. The cells were incubated with or without 1 μg/mL LPS in the presence or absence of 10 μg/mL of 1, (S)-1, rac-2, 2, or indomethacin for 24 h. The total RNA was extracted from 10 cm Petri dishes containing RPTEC cells using TRIzolTM Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturer’s instruction. The total RNA was subsequently converted to first-strand cDNA by adding commercial first-strand cDNA reactions (Bio-Rad). The expressions of target genes were amplified using primer sets as presented in Table 1 together with the SYBR green real-time PCR master mix (Bioline).

Determination of Nitric Oxide (NO) Production

The culture media in each condition was detected based on the Greiss reaction using a commercial nitrate/nitrite colorimetric assay kit (Cayman). The absorbances of total nitric oxide (NO) and its final product, nitrite (NO2–), in each sample were determined at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Biotek). The level of nitrate (NO3–), another end product of NO, was subsequently calculated by subtracting nitrite from total NO.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software version 6.0. The data were expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). The sample comparison was analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s posthoc test. P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
