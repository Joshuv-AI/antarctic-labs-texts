A catalyst-free bioorthogonal reaction for malononitrile addition to azodicarboxylates
Authors: Honggui Huang, Xingyu Liu, Qianqian Qi, Hannan Chen, Haiyan Zhang, Kaisong Zhang, Xinyan Xu, Yunting Zhao, Xiang Zhou, Wenbo Liu, Tian Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeveloping bioorthogonal reactions that enable fundamental exploration of biological processes remains a key pursuit of chemical biology. We report here malononitrile addition to azodicarboxylate as a distinct class of catalyst-free bioorthogonal reactions. Our findings demonstrate that malononitriles react rapidly with azodicarboxylates in both organic and aqueous environments at ambient temperature, without requiring catalysts, bases, or additives. The facile modification of malononitriles facilitates their incorporation into biomolecules such as RNA and proteins. This malononitrile addition to azodicarboxylate reaction exhibits high robustness in complex biological systems, attributed to its distinct reactivity through a concerted transition state. Furthermore, we find that the malononitrile addition to azodicarboxylate reaction is compatible with other well-established bioorthogonal reactions (such as copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition and tetrazine ligation), enabling integration with click reactions for simultaneous site-specific labeling. These features position it as a valuable tool for various biological applications.Subject terms: Chemical biology, Organic chemistry

Introduction

Bioorthogonal reactions are highly valued across chemistry and biology for their ability to react selectively and efficiently within biological systems without interfering with natural processes1–6. Since its inception, bioorthogonal chemistry has undergone remarkable advancements, allowing researchers to introduce non-native molecules into complex biological systems with precision7–13. For instance, bioorthogonal reactions have enabled scientists to track the dynamic distribution of specific proteins in living cells and to investigate intercellular signaling without compromising cell integrity14–19.

Following extensive exploration, a variety of bioorthogonal reactions have been developed, including prominent examples such as Staudinger ligation20,21, copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC)22,23, strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC)24,25, and tetrazine ligation26,27, among others28,29 (Fig. 1a). While these achievements have revolutionized the methodologies used for in situ studies of biological processes, their practical application still faces challenges, often constrained by specific application ranges, reaction conditions, the availability of reactants, and biocompatibility30–32. In light of these limitations, scientists have been working to develop novel bioorthogonal reactions, particularly those that can be safely utilized in biological environments3,7,33. Building upon our extensive experience with malononitriles34–37, we investigated their potential to expand the repertoire of bioorthogonal reactions.

This study introduces our development of malononitrile addition to azodicarboxylate (MAAD) as a distinct class of catalyst-free bioorthogonal reactions and its biological applications. The reaction features high selectivity and proceeds rapidly in both organic and aqueous solutions at room temperature. The inherent stability and good solubility in water of both malononitriles and azodicarboxylates, coupled with their demonstrated biocompatibility and low toxicity, even at elevated concentrations, underscore their suitability for bioorthogonal applications. Importantly, the ease of modifying malononitriles facilitates their incorporation into biomolecules (Fig. 1b). This operationally simple reaction exhibits robust performance in complex biological systems due to its distinct reactivity, proceeding through a concerted transition state (Fig. 1c). Additionally, this MAAD reaction is compatible with azides and alkynes (vide infra), making it possible to combine this reaction with click reaction to achieve multiple site-specific labeling concurrently. These attributes render it an ideal tool for biological studies and potential therapeutic applications.

Results and discussion

Reaction development and optimization

Our group has focused on the synthetic applications of malononitriles as a versatile platform to access structurally diverse molecules34–37. Both malononitriles and azodicarboxylates are stable, widely available, highly soluble in water, and low-toxicity even at high concentrations (vide infra)38–40. These bio-compatible properties led us to envisage that the addition of nucleophilic malononitriles to electrophilic azodicarboxylates could constitute a class of bioorthogonal reactions. To explore this possibility, we began by investigating the reactivity of benzyl malononitrile (M1) and diisopropyl azodicarboxylate (DIAD, A1). Upon simply mixing M1 and A1 in THF at 20 °C, we observed rapid formation of the adduct P1 without the need for any catalyst, base, or additional reagents. Real-time monitoring using online Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirmed that the reaction was completed within 90 seconds under standard conditions (THF, 25 °C, 0.1 mmol/mL; Fig. 1d), with a second-order rate constant of k2 = 0.703 M-1s-1 (see Fig. 1f and Supplementary Figs. 1–3). A variety of physiologically relevant conditions were examined (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The reaction completed in approximately 65 minutes in pure water, primarily due to the limited aqueous solubility of azodicarboxylate substrates. Nevertheless, the reaction proceeded smoothly across a range of pH conditions (pH 3.4–10.4, Supplementary Table 1), in the presence of different ionic strengths (1.0 equivalent KCl or NaCl), and in the presence of 10 mg/mL BSA (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Table 2). Furthermore, we examined the influence of biological thiols commonly encountered in cellular environments, such as glutathione (GSH) and L-cysteine. As shown in Fig. 1g, the MAAD reaction retained favorable yields under these conditions, indicating good compatibility with reductive biological matrices. Collectively, these findings highlight the robustness and bioorthogonality of the MAAD reaction across a wide range of biologically relevant environments.

A brief examination of substrate compatibility disclosed that the reaction accommodates a wide variety of substrates. As demonstrated in Fig. 1e, both malononitriles (M) and azodicarboxylates (A) with various substituents (R1 and R2) delivered the corresponding products (P1–P10) in quantitative yields. We further conducted a systematic evaluation of how various substituents influence the reactivity of the MAAD reaction. Substrates bearing aryl (e.g., M4) and allyl (e.g., M5) substituents displayed comparable reactivity to the standard substrate M1, with no significant differences in reaction rate or product yield. Substrates containing a furyl heterocycle (e.g., M6) also participated efficiently in the MAAD reaction. However, the isolated yield was moderately reduced ( ~ 73%), which we attribute to the lower chemical stability of the furan-containing product during purification. These results collectively indicate that the MAAD reaction possesses excellent functional group tolerance and structural compatibility.

Notably, the reaction proceeded smoothly in most common organic solvents, spanning a wide polarity range (e.g., THF, DMF, DMSO). This provides flexibility in substrate selection based on their solubility profiles. Given that biological environments are typically well-buffered (e.g., via phosphate or bicarbonate systems), we next evaluated the performance of the MAAD reaction in buffered aqueous solutions. Intriguingly, employing a mixture of organic solvent (e.g., THF, DMSO, MeCN) and PBS (1:20) as the reaction medium, the MAAD reaction was completed within 20 minutes. Throughout this process, no decomposition of the products was observed. To assess the chemical stability of the MAAD reaction product under physiologically relevant conditions, we dissolved the synthesized adduct P1 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH = 7.4) and incubated it at room temperature for 24 hours. The product was then recovered by rotary evaporation, yielding 11.7 mg from an initial 11.8 mg. Comparison of the recovered product with the original P1 by 1H-NMR spectroscopy showed no detectable differences, indicating that the MAAD adduct exhibits excellent chemical stability under physiological conditions.

Exploring MAAD reaction for RNA labeling in vitro and in cellulo

Exploring the MAAD reaction for RNA labeling, it is critical to recognize the significance of RNA as a vital carrier of genetic information that plays key roles in gene expression and cellular regulation41–43. To understand its intricate structure and functions better, effective RNA labeling techniques are indispensable. In recent years, the technique of RNA 2’-OH specific acylation has gained prominence for selectively marking the ribose 2’-OH in the RNA backbone using acylating reagents44–47. We synthesized malononitriles with various acylating functionalities (M8–M13, Supplementary Fig. 4a, b) and tested their modification on RNA using a short-chain model (R-21nt, Supplementary Fig. 5). While M8-M10 exhibited poor reactivity (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c), M11-M13 demonstrated high incorporation efficiency, yielding RNA-M11, RNA-M12, and RNA-M13 quantitatively under 100 mM conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5d). The MAAD reaction between malononitrile-incorporated RNA and dibenzyl azodicarboxylate (A2) at 37 °C for 15 minutes revealed high reactivity for RNA-M11 and RNA-M13, whereas RNA-M12 showed significantly lower conversion (Supplementary Fig. 5b and 5d). Consequently, RNA-M11 was selected for further evaluation of azodicarboxylate effects (Fig. 2a).

All nine azodicarboxylates (Supplementary Fig. 4c) examined exhibited high reactivity except for A4 and A7 (Supplementary Fig. 6). Notably, a more than 4-fold increase in efficiency was observed with bisazodicarboxylates A8 and A9 compared to their monomeric azodicarboxylate counterparts, with reactions reaching saturation within 40 seconds at 128 μM (Supplementary Figs. 6d and 7). These results were further confirmed by Electrospray Ionization-Mass Spectrometry (ESI-MS), as shown in Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 8. The addition products of RNA-M11 and A1 were accurately identified (peaks c-f, Fig. 2c). The molecular weight of the native R-21nt remained unchanged following treatment with A1. Additionally, no side reactions between either the malononitriles or A1 and the inherent functional groups of RNA were observed, demonstrating orthogonality.

A hallmark of bioorthogonal chemistry is its capacity to introduce molecular moieties that retain biological function within complex systems29,48,49. Accordingly, we prepared azodicarboxylate derivatives bearing biotin or BODIPY (azo-biotin and azo-BODIPY, Supplementary Fig. 9a). The incorporation of biotin group by the MAAD reaction, using malononitrile modified RNA and azo-biotin, was demonstrated with high efficiency, as assessing by the binding strength of biotin with streptavidin-HRP (SA-HRP) post-reaction via a dot blot assay (Fig. 2d, e). Notably, no false-positive signals were detected with native RNA. BODIPY-labelled RNA was also successfully obtained, as evidenced by the PAGE analysis (Fig. 2f).

In cellulo validation of the MAAD reaction involved transfecting purified RNA-M11 into cells, followed by 24-hour treatment with azo-BODIPY. Laser confocal microscopy revealed bright RNA fluorescence spots (Fig. 2g, h), while control cells with native RNA showed no fluorescence (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 9c). The bioorthogonality of the MAAD reaction was confirmed by the selective coupling of azo-BODIPY with RNA-M11, using Cy5-labeled RNA as the M11 substrate (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Neither malononitrile modification nor azo-BODIPY addition affected Cy5 emission. Numerous fluorescence spots were observed in the cytoplasm, confirming successful labeling since RNA-M11 lacks a nuclear localization sequence. Only modified RNA-M11 appeared in the BODIPY channel, with spots nearly overlapping those in the Cy5 channel, indicating that the BODIPY signals originated from the MAAD reaction.

Exploring MAAD reaction for protein labeling in vitro and in cellulo

Building on the established proof-of-concept, we expanded the application of the MAAD reaction to proteins, employing two globular proteins, bovine serum albumin (BSA) and lysozyme (LYZ) as templates (Fig. 3a). The malononitrile moiety was successfully introduced into BSA upon reacting with malononitrile M14. The purified BSA-M14 was subsequently treated with azo-BODIPY at 37 °C for 15 minutes, followed by analysis through SDS-PAGE. Coomassie staining revealed both native and modified BSA, with fluorescent signals exclusively observed in the lanes corresponding to azo-BODIPY-treated BSA-M14. As expected, the fluorescence intensity increased proportionally with the concentration of azo-BODIPY. Notably, no fluorescent signals were detected for unreacted BSA-M14 or native BSA, either before or after treatment, as depicted in Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 10a. Similar results were obtained when applying the MAAD reaction to lysozyme (Supplementary Fig. 10b-c), demonstrating the general compatibility of this method.

Furthermore, the capability of the MAAD reaction for protein labeling within a cellular environment was demonstrated. Utilizing the specificity of the Cetuximab antibody to recognize antigens on the surface of A549 cells50, we modified the Cetuximab antibody with M14 to introduce malononitrile fragment. The modified Cetuximab-M14 was then incubated with A549 cells, followed by treatment with azo-BODIPY, resulting specific incorporation of fluorescent labeling (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 9d).

The Application of MAAD in Temporal Suppression Over RNA Processes

The MAAD reaction’s efficiency and simplicity make it ideal for manipulating biomolecule structure and function. Incorporating malononitrile minimally affects RNA structure, preserving its bioactivity. However, adding an azodicarboxylate introduces steric hindrance and polarity shifts, disrupting RNA and enabling precise control over its function (Supplementary Fig. 11a). R-21nt was modified with various malononitrile levels (RNA-M11), treated with 64 µM A9, and then paired with R-21nt-FAM-c to form double-stranded RNA (dsRNA). RNA-M11 with up to 150 mM M11 retained stable pairing (Supplementary Fig. 11b), while A9 treatment of those with >50 mM M11 significantly inhibited pairing (Supplementary Fig. 11c). When we replaced the modified RNA with R-21nt-c and used FAM-labeled R-21nt as the complementary strand for validation, similar results were obtained (Supplementary Fig. 11d, e). Denaturing PAGE analysis indicated that only modified RNA responded to A9, showing increased molecular weight and reduced migration, while native RNA remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 12).

After demonstrating the MAAD reaction’s capacity to alter RNA secondary structure, we explored its potential in regulating RNA function, focusing on reverse transcription efficiency. Using R-32nt-M11 as the template for RevertAid RT, nearly half of the primers extended fully, indicating compatibility with malononitrile modification (Supplementary Fig. 13a). However, the MAAD reaction with A9 severely inhibited RevertAid RT activity, except for RNA-M11 with 50 mM M11 modification, leaving most DNA primers unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 13b). A concentration-dependent inhibition was observed: 8 µM A9 led to ~50% extension failure, while 64 µM nearly abolished extension (Supplementary Fig. 13c). These effects were confirmed with M-MuLV RT (Supplementary Fig. 14). Denaturing PAGE showed significant changes only for modified R-32nt-M11 after azodicarboxylate treatment (Supplementary Fig. 15), and the reaction with A9 completed in just 20 seconds (Supplementary Fig. 15d). These findings support our hypothesis that the MAAD reaction can effectively modulate RNA structure and function, providing a powerful tool for temporal suppression over RNA processes.

CRISPR/Cas9, a programmable DNA endonuclease, depends on single-guide RNA (sgRNA), whose activity is linked to its structural conformation51–54. This led us to explore whether the MAAD reaction could disrupt sgRNA structure and, in turn, CRISPR/Cas9 activity (Fig. 4a). We modified sgRNA with varying concentrations of M11 to incorporate malononitrile, finding that Cas9 tolerated modifications below 35 mM (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 16a). Following A9 treatment, malononitrile-modified sgRNA was detectable. Notably, 10 mM modified sg-GAPDH showed over 50% reduction in cleavage efficiency, while 30 mM nearly abolished activity (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 16b). A9 was chosen for further investigation due to its superior inhibitory performance. We speculate that this efficiency may stem from its intrinsic structural potential to form intramolecular cross-links, increasing steric hindrance, an effect preliminarily validated in our previous studies55,56. Comparing various azodicarboxylates (A1-A9, Fig. 4d-m and Supplementary Fig. 17), we found rigid phenyl ring structures (A2, A5-A7) had better inhibitory effects than alkyl-substituted variants (A1, A3, A4), with bisazodicarboxylates A8 and A9 showing substantial activity increases. Notably, Brd0539 (a Cas9 small molecule inhibitor)57 exhibited weak inhibition, while A6 and A9 showed minimal cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 18). The MAAD reaction’s versatility was confirmed with sg-SLX4IP and sg-HPRT1, yielding IC50 values of 10.09 µM and 12.35 µM, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 19-20). These results underscore the MAAD reaction’s potential for precise control of CRISPR/Cas9 activity in vitro.

CRISPR/Cas13a is notable for its specific cleavage of target RNA guided by gRNA, similar to Cas9, though they serve different functions58–60. Our MAAD reaction method for controlling RNA structure is compatible with RNA-protein complexes like CRISPR/Cas13a (Fig. 4n). We modified the gRNA of CRISPR/Cas13a (crRNA/Cas13a) with varying M11 concentrations and assessed cleavage activity. Cas13a tolerated malononitrile modifications (Supplementary Fig. 21a and 21c), but tolerance decreased with increased steric hindrance; 64 µM A9 nearly completely inhibited crRNAs modified with 100 mM and 120 mM M11 (Fig. 4o and Supplementary Fig. 21a). A9 showed a 5.4-fold increase in activity compared to the monomer A6 (Fig. 4p, Supplementary Fig. 21b and 21d). Testing Cas13a’s collateral cleavage activity with a fluorescent reporter system61 revealed that while malononitrile modification reduced activity, it still cleaved non-target RNA within 10 minutes at M11 concentrations below 120 mM (Supplementary Fig. 22a-22e). The addition of A9 further amplified the inhibitory effect on crRNA modified with 120 mM M11 (Supplementary Fig. 22f). Denaturing PAGE confirmed that changes in Cas13a activity stemmed from structural alterations in modified crRNA-M11 due to azodicarboxylate treatment (Supplementary Fig. 23).

Exploring the orthogonality of MAAD reaction

The capability for precise RNA manipulation sets the stage for investigating the orthogonality of the MAAD reaction alongside other established bioorthogonal reactions. A key feature of the MAAD reaction is its compatibility with these established methods, such as the inverse electron demand Diels-Alder reaction (IEDDA) and strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) (Supplementary Fig. 24a). Three different RNAs (R-21nt, R-32nt, and R-44nt) were labelled with norbornene (for IEDDA)27,62, malononitrile (for MAAD), and azide (for SPAAC)24, respectively. The simultaneous combination of the MAAD reaction with each of the two labelled RNAs did not interfere with the reaction outcomes of the other (Supplementary Fig. 24b).

Building on these findings, we designed a ternary CRISPR/Cas9 in vitro cleavage experiment to further validate the orthogonality of the MAAD reaction. In this setup, three types of modified sgRNA were prepared: sg-HBEGF was modified by introducing an azide group using the AZDE compound (for SPAAC); sg-SLX4IP was modified by attaching a norbornene group using the NOR compound (for IEDDA); and sg-GAPDH was modified by incorporating a malononitrile group using the M11 compound (for MAAD) (Fig. 5a). We then examined four different combinations of modified sgRNA: (1) azide-modified sgRNAs + norbornene-modified sgRNAs + unmodified sgRNA, (2) azide-modified sgRNAs + malononitrile-modified sgRNAs + unmodified sgRNA, (3) norbornene-modified sgRNAs + malononitrile-modified sgRNAs + unmodified sgRNA, and (4) azide-modified sgRNAs + norbornene-modified sgRNAs + malononitrile-modified sgRNAs. For these conditions, we treated the samples with either DMSO or a combination of DBCO, Tz (Supplementary Fig. 25), and Azo compounds, followed by the introduction of three target DNA sequences for the CRISPR/Cas9 cleavage reaction. We quantified the intact products using qPCR, and the results (Fig. 5b-d) demonstrated that the SPAAC, IEDDA, and MAAD reactions exhibited excellent orthogonality, with no signs of interference. Moreover, the treatment with DBCO, Tz, and Azo compounds did not affect the activity of the native sgRNAs. These findings indicate that the MAAD reaction is highly compatible with classical SPAAC and IEDDA reactions, enhancing the current toolbox of bioorthogonal reactions and allowing for the simultaneous or sequential labeling of multiple target molecules, thereby facilitating a multidimensional analysis of complex biological processes.

The Application of MAAD in Reducing CRISPR-Cas9 OFF-target Effects

CRISPR/Cas9 is a powerful genomic editing tool, but prolonged activation can lead to OFF-target effects, potentially resulting in diseases and tumors, thus limiting its clinical use63,64. After confirming that the MAAD reaction can effectively control Cas9 cleavage activity in vitro, we aimed to regulate its efficiency in cells to achieve specific temporal suppression and reduce OFF-target effects. We transfected varying concentrations of modified sg-SLX4IP into HeLa-OC cells stably expressing Cas965. Four hours later, the medium was replaced, and cells were treated with 64 μM A9 for 24 hours. The insertion and deletion (indel) ratios were quantitatively analyzed using the T7 endonuclease I (T7EI) assay66–68. Consistent with in vitro results, the intracellular CRISPR/Cas9 system tolerated some malononitrile modifications on sgRNA; 45 mM modified sg-SLX4IP retained over 60% editing efficiency compared to native sg-SLX4IP (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 26a). Following A9 treatment, editing efficiency significantly decreased, with indel ratios dropping 1.6-fold (49.4% to 30.7%), 3.2-fold (40.4% to 12.6%), and 9.1-fold (31.8% to 3.5%) for 35 mM, 40 mM, and 45 mM modified sg-SLX4IP, respectively. In contrast, the activity of native sg-SLX4IP remained unaffected by A9, demonstrating the specific recognition of malononitrile-modified sgRNAs without impacting unmodified strands, highlighting the MAAD reaction’s good bioorthogonal performance.

We observed that complete preservation of sgRNA cleavage activity and full inhibition by A9 treatment, as seen in vitro, were not achieved in cells. We speculate this is because sgRNA forms an effective gene editing complex with Cas9 upon transfection, making it difficult for A9 to reverse edits after the target site is modified. To test this, we added A9 1 hour after transfection, significantly enhancing its inhibitory effect on modified sgRNA (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 26b). This finding suggests that the bioorthogonal reaction between sgRNA-M11 and azodicarboxylate provides a strategy for temporally modulating CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing activity in living cells, potentially minimizing prolonged activation-associated OFF-target effects.

To evaluate MAAD’s impact on OFF-target effects of sgHEKs4-M11, we employed the GUIDE-seq technique69,70, using the Cas9 inhibitor Brd053957 as a control (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 26c-d). We first analyzed gene editing efficiency using the T7EI assay before and after modification, noting that Brd0539 affected both native and modified sgHEKs4 due to its direct action on Cas9. In contrast, A9 selectively inhibited only the modified sgHEKs4-M11, showing significantly better effects on 25 mM and 30 mM modifications compared to Brd0539 (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 26d). The 30 mM modified sgHEKs4 retained about 65% activity without azodicarboxylate treatment, but exhibited a 4.5-fold decrease after A9 treatment, which was applied 4 hours post-transfection. Based on this, we chose this condition for the GUIDE-seq experiment. Results showed that A9 significantly reduced OFF-target effects, increasing the ON-target ratio from 31.1% to 78.7% (nearly 2.5-fold) and improving the ON-target/2MM OFF-target ratio from 0.45 to 4.75 (approximately 10.6-fold) (Fig. 6d-f). While Brd0539 improved OFF-target effects for both modified and unmodified sgHEKs4, its selectivity was inferior to A9. Importantly, A9 minimally impacted OFF-target effects of native sgHEKs4.

To further validate the OFF-target suppression observed in GUIDE-seq, we performed T7EI cleavage assays on a representative two-mismatch OFF-target site (GA). Consistent with GUIDE-seq results, treatment with A9 significantly reduced indel frequency at the GA site in the modified sgRNA group, while no notable effect was observed for the native sgRNA (Supplementary Fig. 27). These data further support that MAAD treatment can selectively suppress prolonged Cas9 activity and mitigate OFF-target editing at sensitive loci. These results indicate that the MAAD reaction enables temporal control of CRISPR/Cas9 editing while effectively reducing OFF-target effects, enhancing the technology’s potential applications.

Taken together, these results indicate that MAAD provides a catalyst-free bioorthogonal platform for cellular protein labeling, programmable modulation of RNA structure and function, and temporally controlled CRISPR/Cas9 editing with reduced OFF-target activity, thereby broadening the toolkit for biological research and applications.

Methods

Cell culture

HEK293T and HeLa-OC cells (kindly provided by Prof. Wen-Sheng Wei, Peking University, China) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified environment. The cells were passaged with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, phenol red. To ensure mycobacteria-free cultures, cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination using the MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector Kit (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Malononitrile modification of RNA

For a standard modification step, native RNAs (20 μM) were reacted with the indicated concentration of the indicated malononitrile compound in a 10-μL solution containing 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5 @ 25 °C), 6 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NaCl, 60% DMSO and 200 mM DMAP (4-dimethylaminopyridine). The whole mixture was incubated on a shaker (200 rpm) at 37 °C for 12 h and then quenched by the addition of 2.0 μL NaOAc (3 M, pH 5.0 @ 25 °C), 75 μL ethanol and 1.0 μL glycogen (5 mg/μL). Each sample was then stored in a −80 °C freezer for at least 2 h and the pellet was collected by centrifugation (4 °C, 12000 × g 1 h). The pellet was rinsed with 300 μL cold 75% ethanol and collected again by centrifugation (4 °C, 12000 × g 20 min, repeated twice). Finally, the malononitrile-modified RNA was resuspended in 20 μL of DEPC-treated water and stored at −20 °C or −80 °C.

MAAD bioorthogonal reaction on RNA

The MAAD reaction was performed in a 10 μL aqueous solution, containing 50 ng malononitrile-modified RNAs, and the indicated concentrations of the indicated azodicarboxylate. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min and analyzed using a 12% or 20% denaturing PAGE gel. Electrophoresis was conducted on a vertical electrophoresis apparatus (DYCZ-22A, Liuyi Instrument Factory, Beijing, China) at room temperature (400 V, 4 h) and the results were visualized using a ChemiDocMP imaging system.

Dot blot assay

To demonstrate the MAAD reaction on the RNA system, 1 μg M11-modified R-21nt was incubated with 200 μM Azo-biotin in 100 μL aqueous solution at 37 °C for 30 min. Products were purified using the RNA Clean & Concentration Kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 100 ng of native R-21nt or M11-modified R-21nt (before/after Azo-biotin treatment) were added dropwise to a nylon membrane, allowed to air dry and then UV crosslinked twice (HybriLinker™ HL-2000 Digital Hybridisation, UVP). The membrane was blocked in 5% BSA at 37 °C for 1 h, washed three times with 1×TBST and blotted with high sensitivity streptavidin-HR at 37 °C for 1 h. The membrane was then washed three times with 1×TBST, incubated in ECL chemiluminescent substrate and imaged using the ChemiDocMP imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). The membrane was also stained with methylene blue.

Malononitrile modification of protein

BSA/Lysozyme (10 mg/mL) was incubated with 30 mM M11 in PBS solution to a final volume of 500 μL. After shaking at 200 rpm and 37 °C for 30 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C for 3 min, and 450 μL of the supernatant was transferred to ultrafiltration tubes. The products were purified by rinsing three times with PBS solution, with 10-min intervals between each rinse. Finally, the M11-modified BSA/Lysozyme was collected from the bottom of the ultrafiltration tubes, transferred to centrifuge tubes, and diluted to 50 μL.

RNA imaging

HEK293T cells (6 × 105) were seeded in 20 mm glass-bottom dishes overnight before transfection and washed twice with warm DPBS (Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline). Then, 500 μL of warmed DMEM was added to each well. For transfection, 1 μg of M11-modified sg-SLX4IP was diluted in 120 μL of DMEM, and 5.0 μL of Lipofectamine 3000 transfection agent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 120 μL of DMEM was added to the diluted RNA mixture. After a 15-min incubation at room temperature, the RNA complexes were transferred to the wells containing the cells and growth medium. Cells were cultured for 6 h, washed three times with warm DPBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde fixative solution (Beyotime, P0099-100ml) for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, the cells were washed three times with warm DPBS, 5 minutes per wash. For SPAAC labeling of transfected DNA/RNA, 8 μM DBCO-Cy5 was added to each well, and the reaction proceeded for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Cells were then washed three times with warm DPBS (5 minutes per wash), stained with Hoechst 33342 (1 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, 14533) for 5 min, and washed again three times with warm DPBS (5 minutes per wash). Cell images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 DIVE microscope equipped with a HC PL APO CS2 63×/1.40 OIL objective, as well as HyD and HyD-RLD detectors. A 405 nm laser was used to image Hoechst 33342, a 488 nm laser to image the FAM fluorophore, and a 552 nm laser to image the Cy5 fluorophore.

Cetuximab labeling and imaging

Cetuximab antibody (2 mg/mL in PBS) was modified by reacting with 200 equivalents of M14 compound at room temperature for 2 hours. The reaction mixture was then purified by ultrafiltration (3 kD cutoff, 15,000 × g, 5 rounds). The concentration of the modified antibody (Cetuximab-M14) was quantified using BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime).

For in cell labeling, 1×105 A549 cells were plated in 35-mm glass-bottom dishes. Once reaching ~80% confluency, cells were incubated with 30 μg/mL Cetuximab-M14 for 30 minutes, followed by three washes with cold PBS. Then, 50 μM azo-BODIPY was added and incubated at 37 °C for 20 minutes. Cells were washed again, stained with Hoechst 33342 (1 μg/mL, 10 min), and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus FV3000).

Structural manipulation of RNA through MAAD reaction

M11-modified RNAs (R-21nt) were prepared using the general modification protocol described above. The complementary RNA (fluorescently labelled R-21nt-c-FAM) was used as a probe to study the hybridization properties of the M11-modified RNAs (R-21nt). For the RNA hybridization experiment, the M11-modified R-21nt was treated with a range of concentrations of dimeric azodicarboxylate A9 at 37 °C for 15 min, and then annealed with the R-21nt-c-FAM in 1 × reaction buffer (50 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8 @ 25 °C) at 37 °C for 2 h. Each sample was then analyzed on a 20% native PAGE gel. Electrophoresis was performed on a vertical electrophoresis unit at room temperature (400 V, 2 h) and the results were visualized using a ChemiDocMP imaging system.

Control of RNA reverse transcription by MAAD reaction

Reverse transcription was carried out in two different reaction buffers: one containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.3 @ 25 °C), 75 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2 and 10 mM DTT for M-MuLV and one containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.3 @ 25 °C), 50 mM KCl, 4 mM MgCl2 and 10 mM DTT for RevertAid RT. For this assay, native or M11-modified RNA templates (R-32nt) were preincubated with the indicated concentrations of azodicarboxylate A9 and the indicated enzyme in 1× reaction buffer at 37 °C for 15 min. The extension reaction was initiated by rapid mixing with a solution containing the fluorescence-labelled DNA primer (RT-primer1, Supplementary Table 4) and dNTPs in 1× reaction buffer. The final concentrations of each component in a typical 10 μL reaction were as follows: 20 U M-MuLV/RevertAid RT, 100 nM RT-primer1, 500 nM R-32nt and 200 μM dNTPs. After incubation for 2 h at 42 °C, reverse transcription was quenched by the addition of 5 volumes of stop buffer (95% formamide, 25 mM EDTA, pH 8.0 @ 25 °C) and each sample was analyzed on a 20% denaturing PAGE gel. Electrophoresis was performed on a vertical electrophoresis unit at room temperature (400 V, 1.5 h), and results were visualized using a ChemiDocMP imaging system.

In vitro transcription of sgRNA

All sgRNA used in this study were synthesized by in vitro transcription. Separate shorter overlapping sequences were designed to generate the template dsRNA. A short PCR was performed to anneal and fill in the overlapping sequences using Pyrobest™ DNA Polymerase. Transcription reactions were performed at 37 °C for 12 h according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After DNA digestion with DNase I at the end of transcription, the transcribed RNA products were purified using the NaOAc/phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation methods. The purified sgRNAs were resuspended in DEPC-treated water and stored at −20 °C or −80 °C.

Control of in vitro CRISPR/Cas9 cleavage by MAAD reaction

For this experiment, 50 ng of M11-modified sgRNAs were incubated with 0.2 μM Cas9 enzyme and the indicated concentrations of the azodicarboxylate compound in 1× NEBuffer™ 3.1 (100 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 100 μg/mL BSA, pH 7.9 @ 25 °C) at 37 °C for 15 min. Cas9-mediated DNA cleavage reactions were initiated by rapidly mixing equal volumes of a solution containing target DNA fragments (50 ng) in 1× NEBuffer™ 3.1 and incubating at 37 °C for the specified times. Reactions were quenched by heating at 75 °C for 5 min, and each sample was analyzed on a 1.5% agarose gel containing 1.5× Super GelRed for visualization. The GeneRuler 100-bp DNA Ladder was used as a DNA size marker in this experiment.

LbuCas13a expression and purification71

The LbuCas13a expression plasmid (Addgene #83482) carrying an N-terminal His6-MBP tag and a TEV protease cleavage site was transformed into E. coli Rosetta (DE3). A 16 mL overnight starter culture was used to inoculate 4 L LB and grown at 37 °C (300 rpm) to OD600 ≈ 0.6. Expression was induced with IPTG (isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside, Sigma, final 500 µM), and cultures were shifted to 16 °C for 16 h.

Cells were harvested and lysed by sonication in 20 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, pH 7.5 (25 °C). All purification steps were performed at 4 °C. The clarified lysate was applied to Ni Sepharose; protein was eluted with 100 mM imidazole. The His6-MBP tag was removed by TEV digestion. The sample was further purified by cation-exchange chromatography (SP column; elution buffer 20 mM Tris-HCl, 1 M NaCl, pH 7.5) and by size-exclusion chromatography (Superdex 200 Increase 10/300) in 20 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.5. Protein was concentrated to 20 mg/mL and stored at −80 °C.

Control of in vitro CRISPR/Cas13a cleavage by MAAD reaction

For in vitro Cas13a RNA cleavage, 5 ng of M11-modified crRNAs were incubated with 5 nM Cas13a enzyme and the indicated concentrations of azodicarboxylate A9 in 1× Cas13a buffer (20 mM HEPES, 50 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 5% glycerol, pH 6.8 at 25 °C) at 37 °C for 15 min. Cas13a-mediated RNA cleavage reactions were initiated by rapidly mixing equal volumes of a solution containing 20 ng of the target RNA (Cas13a target, Supplementary Table 5) in 1× Cas13a buffer, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Reactions were quenched by adding 5 volumes of stop buffer (98% formamide, 2% SDS), and each sample was analyzed on a 20% denaturing PAGE gel.

For the kinetic fluorescence study, RNA cleavage reactions were initiated by rapidly mixing equal volumes of the Cas13a/crRNA complex with a solution containing target RNA and reporter RNA (Cas13a reporter, Supplementary Table 5) in 1× Cas13a buffer. The final concentrations of all components, excluding A9, were as follows: 50 nM purified LbuCas13a, 25 nM gRNA, 25 nM target RNA, 125 nM quenched fluorescent RNA reporter, and 0.5 U RiboLock RNase inhibitor. The fluorescence increase over time was measured using an F-4600 FL Spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan) in kinetics mode at room temperature. Reactions were monitored for 10 minutes with measurements taken every second. The excitation and emission wavelengths were set to 496 nm and 520 nm, respectively, using a 1-cm path-length cell. Slit width settings were 10 nm for both excitation and emission.

Cell survival assay

For investigating the cytotoxicity of azodicarboxylate compounds, HeLa-OC cells were seeded in 96-well plates at time zero (5000 cells per well). After 24 h, azodicarboxylate compounds was added at final concentrations of 16-1024 μM (8-128 μM), and the cells were allowed to culture for another 24 h. Subsequently, MTT (20 μL, 5 mg/mL in DMSO) was introduced to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 4 h. Formazan crystals were dissolved by adding 150 μL of DMSO. Optical density (OD) values at 570 nm were measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M5, Molecular Devices) to assess cell viability.

Results are expressed as a percentage of cell viability, calculated based on the absorbance ratio between treated and untreated controls (cells incubated with culture medium). In both conditions (treatment and control), the absorbance of the corresponding medium without cells was subtracted to account for potential interference from the compounds.

CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing in HeLa-OC cells

HeLa-OC cells (2 × 105 per well) were seeded in 12-well plates 12 h before transfection. Native or modified sgRNAs were delivered using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 4 h, medium was replaced with complete medium containing the indicated concentrations of azodicarboxylate A9. Cells were cultured in the dark at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 24 h. Genomic DNA was isolated (FastPure Blood/Cell/Tissue/Bacteria DNA Isolation Mini Kit). Target loci were amplified from 200 ng DNA using PrimeSTAR HS DNA Polymerase with primers listed in Supplementary Table 3. Amplicons were purified (Zymo DNA Clean & Concentrator). T7E1 digestion (from 100 ng DNA) was performed per the manufacturer’s protocol; reactions were quenched with SDS-containing loading dye and analyzed on 1.5% agarose gels stained with 1.5 × Super GelRed (100 V, 1.5 h). A GeneRuler 100-bp DNA Ladder was used as size standard.

GUIDE-seq analysis

A stable Cas9-expressing HeLa line (HeLa-OC) was used. Cells (1.5 × 105 per well) were plated in 12-well plates and cultured overnight. The next day, cells were washed 3× with warm DPBS, rinsed with serum-free DMEM, and 800 µL serum-free DMEM was added per well. sgRNA complexes were prepared by diluting 1.2 µg sgRNA in 50 µL DMEM and mixing with an equal volume containing 2.5 µL Lipofectamine 3000 in 50 µL DMEM. dsODN complexes were prepared by diluting 2 µL dsODN (100 µM) and 2 µL P3000 in 50 µL DMEM and mixing with 2.5 µL Lipofectamine 3000 in 50 µL DMEM. After 4 h, medium was replaced with complete medium containing 64 µM azodicarboxylate A9, and cells were cultured in the dark at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 48 h. Genomic DNA was extracted (FastPure Blood/Cell/Tissue/Bacteria DNA Isolation Mini Kit). Target regions were amplified from 200 ng DNA using ApexHF HS DNA Polymerase FL Master Mix; PCR products were purified (Gel/PCR Purification Kit). T7E1 digestion and NdeI restriction were performed on 100 ng amplicons and analyzed on 1.5% agarose gels with 1.5× Super GelRed (100 V, 1.5 h).

For GUIDE-seq library preparation69,70, genomic DNA was fragmented to ~300 bp using Hieff NGS® Fast-Pace DNA Fragmentation Reagent (YEASEN, #12609ES96), followed by end-repair and A-tailing. Fragments were ligated to P5 adapters (T4 DNA ligase) and subjected to two rounds of PCR with Phanta HS Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase to append indexed P7 adapters. Libraries were pooled and sequenced at Wuhan Biobank. For data analysis, we utilized the open-source guideseq software available at https://github.com/aryeelab/guideseq. This analysis included alignment to the GRCh37 human genome (release 44, downloaded from GENCODE, https://www.gencodegenes.org/human/release_44lift37.html), ensuring comprehensive and accurate assessment of the GUIDE-seq results.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were statistically analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). Normally distributed data sets were analyzed using an analysis of a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data was excluded from the analysis. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times. Biological replicates refer to independent experiments conducted using freshly prepared reagents.
