Pneumocystis jirovecii associated with Lung Virome in patients with severe pneumonia
Authors: Yuanxiu Huang, Xin Wei, Xiaohan Huang, Chen Peng, Baoyue Lin, Xindie Ren, Guojun He, Xuewu Zhang, Chao Jiang, Lingtong Huang, Silan Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryPneumocystis jirovecii (P. jirovecii) threatens immunocompromised patients, yet its role in the lung microbiome remains unclear. We performed metagenomic sequencing on bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from two retrospective cohorts: a multicenter study of 1,737 patients with pneumonia and a validation cohort of 72 intubated ICU patients. Participants were categorized into P. jirovecii infection, colonization, or negative groups based on qPCR and clinical characteristics. Our analysis revealed minimal bacterial differences but significant viral and fungal variations. Notably, negative patients with P. jirovecii exhibited the lowest viral diversity. Even after adjusting for potential confounders, including immunosuppression and disease severity, P. jirovecii status remained most strongly associated with virome alterations. Furthermore, in cytomegalovirus-positive individuals, P. jirovecii positivity was significantly associated with higher 28-day mortality. These findings suggest that P. jirovecii colonization represents a distinct intermediate state in the lung microbial ecosystem between infection and negative, shedding light on its potential role in pneumonia pathogenesis and outcomes.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, medical specialty, immunology, respiratory medicine, medical microbiology

Introduction

Pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP) is a common lung infectious disease caused by Pneumocystis jirovecii (P. jirovecii) and often occurs in immunosuppressed patients,1,2 such as HIV-infected individuals, post-transplant patients, and patients with cancer receiving immunosuppressive therapy.3,4 Although available anti-infective treatments can alleviate the condition, the treatment of PCP remains challenging and has a high recurrence rate.5 P. jirovecii can colonize the host for long periods of time by altering its major surface glycoproteins and evolving with its host, and so forth. Once immune function decreases, P. jirovecii can be reactivated to cause PCP,6 which has a strong tendency toward the lungs.7

The lung microbiome is a complex and dynamic ecosystem comprised of interacting communities of bacteria, fungi, viruses, and so forth.8 Although the number of microorganisms in the lung is much smaller than in the intestines, the stability of respiratory microbiota is considered necessary to prevent pathogen expansion. Several studies have shown that lung microbiota can affect immune cells and play an important role in the development of lung diseases and autoimmune diseases through various mechanisms.9,10,11 During disease states, the lung microbiota undergoes drastic changes, optimizing the injured respiratory tract for adaptation, potentially leading to an imbalance between beneficial and harmful microorganisms.

The bacterial microbiome of the lungs has been the main focus of research to date.12,13,14,15 At present, a small number of studies have evaluated the correlation between PCP and lung bacterial microbiota and found that PCP was not related to bacterial microbiome.11 However, the fungal/viral microbiome, two “neglected” members of our body’s ecosystem, warrants further investigation. No studies evaluated the correlation between PCP and fungal and viral microbiota. In this study, we hypothesized that specific microenvironments exist in the lungs of patients with P. jirovecii colonization and infection, providing necessary conditions for the reproduction of P. jirovecii. By analyzing microbial richness, community composition, and functional changes, we can gain a deeper understanding of the association between lung microbiota and PCP to search for new therapeutic targets. This is of great significance for improving the quality of life and survival rate of immunosuppressed patients.

In this study, we constructed two multicenter cohorts. In Cohort 1, we reviewed the data and clinical information of 1737 patients who had performed Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) clinical metagenomics in 17 medical centers and discovered P. jirovecii infection was significantly associated with the viral species composition of the lung microbiome in patients with pneumonia. In Cohort 2, microbiota analysis of BALF from 72 patients with a lung infection, including P. jirovecii infection (n = 17), P. jirovecii colonization (n = 19), and matched P. jirovecii negative (n = 36) was performed by metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) to deeply investigate the association between P. jirovecii infection and lung microbial community. We also retrospectively assessed the evolution of the lung microbiome in the three groups with the clinical characteristics.

Result

Correlation between P. jirovecii colonization and P. jirovecii infection and fungal and viral microbiota in cohort 1

Among a total of 1897 patients who performed commercial clinical metagenomics tests, fungal species were identified in 958 patients. This process revealed three phyla, including Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Mucoromycota, along with 41 species. The top four detected fungal genera were Candida spp. (n = 594), Aspergillus spp. (n = 306), Pneumocystis spp. (n = 169), and Nakaseomyces spp. (n = 106) (Table S1). Among them, P. jirovecii has been widely detected in every city with different proportions (Figure 1A). Among 1897 patients, 21 patients were under 18 years old, 139 patients were lost to follow-up or abandoned treatment, and 1737 patients were ultimately included in Cohort 1 (Figure 1B).

We retrospectively collected data from a multicenter cohort covering 1737 patients who had performed clinical metagenomics. Propensity Score Matching, Subgroup, and Secondary Analysis. Propensity score matching was performed after matching with age, sex, and clinical characteristics. After matching, three groups of patients were included in this Cohort: P. jirovecii infection (n = 90), P. jirovecii colonization (n = 62), and matched P. jirovecii negative (n = 302), with two patients in the negative group being matched repeatedly (Figure 1B). The baseline characteristics of patients with P. jirovecii infection and colonization compared to negative controls before and after matching are presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. Our results showed that when all microbiota without P. jirovecii were included in the PCoA analysis, there were significant intergroup differences between the three groups (analysis of similarities Adonis test, R2 = 0.04, p = 0.001) (Figure 2A). The PCoA map of P. jirovecii infection group partially overlapped with that of the P. jirovecii colonization group. The relationship between community compositions and environmental factors was analyzed using RDA, which showed that samples were distinctly grouped and displayed, and the most significant factor affecting the viral group was P. jirovecii (Figure 2B). By Spearman correlation analysis, our multicenter Cohort showed a correlation between P. jirovecii detections and different viral, fungal, bacterial, and other species (Figure 2C). The detection rates of Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5, and torque teno virus were progressively higher in the P. jirovecii negative and P. jirovecii colonization groups.

No differences in bacterial microbiota composition were observed between groups in cohort 2

To validate the correlations between P. jirovecii infection and virome, a total of 72 hospitalized patients with severe pneumonia were recruited as Cohort 2 after a strict pathological diagnosis and exclusion process (Figure 1B). The clinical characteristics, demographic and routine laboratory test results of patients are summarized in Table 3. We obtained the final non-redundant catalog of 94382 genes with an average length of 637.21 bp from 72 BALF samples. At the bacterial level, the alpha diversity index showed no significant differences among the three groups, except for the Chao1 index (Figures S1A–S1C). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) of Bray-Curtis distances indicated no significant differences between P. jirovecii infection, P. jirovecii colonization, and P. jirovecii negative groups (analysis of similarities Adonis test, R2 = 0.04, p = 0.077) at the bacterial level (Figure S1D). We analyzed the relative proportions of preponderant taxa in the BALF bacterial community and found that it consisted mainly of Achromobacter deleyi, Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Achromobacter insuavis, Achromobacter xylosoxidans, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Figure S1E).

Fungal and viral microbiota differed among groups in cohort 2, with distinct patterns by immune status

The bioinformatics analysis resulted in various fungal OTUs. P. jirovecii negative group showed the lowest Fungal diversity, as estimated by alpha diversity analyses based on the Shannon diversity index, when compared with P. jirovecii colonization and P. jirovecii infection groups (Padj <0.05). The Chao1 index (reflected species richness) and Simpson indices (species diversity) of these three groups were also significantly different (Padj <0.05) (Figures 3A–3C). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distance showed a clear separation of each experimental group. The results of an analysis of similarities (Adonis test, R2 = 0.16,p = 0.001) also indicated significant differences in microbial composition between the three groups, which was similar to the previous conclusion obtained in Cohort 1 (Figure 3D). The dominant fungi observed at the species level were Aspergillus flavus, Candida albicans, Fusarium oxysporum, Rhizopus oryzae, Rhizopus delemar and Melampsora pinitorqua, and so forth in the three groups (Figure 3E). As expected, there was a significant difference in the relative abundance of P. jirovecii among the three groups (Figure 3F), in addition, Fusarium oxysporum showed a significant variation in relative abundance among the three groups (Figure 3G), further supporting the distinct fungal community shifts associated with P. jirovecii status.

For virome analysis, the P. jirovecii negative group had the lowest alpha diversity calculated by Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices. (Padj <0.05) (Figures 4A, S2A and S2B). Moreover, total viral relative abundance was lowest in the P. jirovecii negative group compared to the other two groups (Figure 4B). For β-diversity, PCoA analysis using Bray-Curtis distances revealed significant variations in the lung viral microbiome at the species level (Adonis test, R2 = 0.08, p = 0.004) (Figure 4C). In both immunocompetent and immunosuppressed patients, the P. jirovecii-negative group had the lowest viral α-diversity. Among immunocompromised patients, the colonization group showed intermediate diversity (between infection and negative groups) by Shannon index (Figures S2C and S2D). For β-diversity, viral composition did not differ significantly in immunocompetent patients. However, in immunosuppressed patients, the colonization group exhibited an intermediate viral profile (Figures 2E and 2F). According to the species abundance table and annotation table, dominant taxa were selected for classification. The dominant microbiota in the P. jirovecii infection and P. jirovecii colonization groups were Simplexvirus humanalpha1 (HSV-1), Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5, Anelloviridae sp., Lymphocryptovirus humangamma4, and so forth. (Figure 4D). Correlation analysis of virus and fungal microbiome and differences in KEGG among the three groups in cohort 2

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate relationships at species of Fungi and Viruses (Figure 5A). There were significant positive correlations across P. jirovecii infection-enriched species. P. jirovecii was positively correlated with Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5, Torque teno virus, and Fusarium oxysporum was positively correlated with Apiotrichum montevideense (Padj≤0.01). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed distinct microbial functional profiles between groups. Compared with the colonization group, the P. jirovecii infection group showed significant enrichment in pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, chemical carcinogenesis, diabetic cardiomyopathy, and neurodegenerative diseases. In contrast, the infection group showed significantly reduced abundance in pathways related to metabolic functions, including metabolic pathways, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and microbial metabolism. Similarly, compared with the P. jirovecii-negative group, the infection group also showed increased abundance in disease-related pathways, including diabetic cardiomyopathy, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Huntington disease, and decreased abundance in general metabolic functions, such as metabolic pathways, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and microbial metabolism (Figure 5B).

Relationship between bronchoalveolar lavage fluid microbiota composition and clinical indexes

To investigate the relationship between the fungal and viral microbiota composition and host health status, Spearman correlations were conducted between the individual species abundance and clinical indexes (Figure S3). We found multiple clinical indicators were closely related to the lung microbiota, including age, immunosuppression, connective tissue disease, renal insufficiency, solid tumor, transplantation and respiratory dysfunction, and so forth. Among the top 30 most abundant genera, Pneumocystis, Anelloviridae sp. was positively correlated with immunosuppression, renal insufficiency, and transplantation; Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5 and Alphatorquevirus were positively correlated with immunosuppression and transplantation; Pneumocystis and Geoglossum showed a negative correlation with respiratory dysfunction; Malassezia, Saccharomyces, Massospora, Epichloe, and connective tissue disease showed a negative correlation.

The effect of bacterial, fungal abundance, and clinical indexes on viral community was investigated by redundancy analysis (Figure 5C). The first two components explained 55.11% of the total variance in viral composition. The influence of P. jirovecii, Candida albicans, and Achromobacter spp. abundance, immunosuppression, transplantation, and age were considered to explain the distinction of the viral composition among the P. jirovecii infection, colonization, and negative groups (Padj <0.05). P. jirovecii had the greatest effect on the viral community (R2 = 0.39, Padj = 0.012). Similar to Cohort 1, the samples were clearly grouped, suggesting that P. jirovecii colonization may be the transition stage from P. jirovecii negative to P. jirovecii infection.

Additionally, we selected representative Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5 and Lymphocryptovirus humangamma4 and explored the effects of virus and P. jirovecii positivity on 28-day all-cause mortality for 1737 cases in Cohort 1. The 28-day mortality of individuals who are CMV-positive but not treated with acyclovir, ganciclovir, or valganciclovir (n = 226) was significantly associated with P. jirovecii positive (Figure 6A). However, P. jirovecii positivity was not associated with 28-day mortality in patients with CMV-negative or CMV-positive receiving anti-herpesvirus therapy in Cohort 1 (n = 1511) (Figure 6B). Similar results were found in the individuals who are EBV-positive in Cohort 1 (Figures 6C and 6D). After adjusting for key clinical confounders using IPTW, the association between P. jirovecii positivity and 28-day mortality remained significant in patients with CMV-positive (Figure 6E). In contrast, this association was not observed in the other groups (Figures 6F–6H).

Discussion

P. jirovecii is a fungal respiratory pathogen that causes life-threatening pneumonia (PCP) in patients with risk factors (i.e., related to immunosuppression and fragile medical conditions). Treatment options are limited, and the mortality rate remains high.16,17 P. jirovecii colonization occurs in some individuals, and it has been found that even a low abundance of colonized P. jirovecii can stimulate the immune response to cause lung injury. The role of P. jirovecii colonization in certain lung diseases has attracted increasing attention.18 The human lung is colonized by dynamic microbial communities,19 and the lung micro-environmental homeostasis is important to resist invasion by potential pathogens.

Studies have shown that even with dominant taxa, the lungs of underlying patients with lower respiratory disease have various bacteria, fungi, viruses, and other microorganisms. The composition of the microbiota may influence the behavior and virulence gene expression of major pathogens and host susceptibility to infection.20 There is increasing evidence that lung microbiome alterations are associated with a variety of diseases. For example, bacteria were significantly increased in the lower respiratory tract of patients with cystic fibrosis,21,22 chronic obstructive lung disease,23,24 and bronchiectasis.25 However, the comprehensive landscape of bacterial, fungal, and viral microbiota has not been investigated in P. jirovecii-infected or colonized patients.

Fungi and viruses participate in shaping the lung microenvironment26 and play an important role in the lungs of patients with severe pneumonia.27 How P. jirovecii interacts with the virome and fungal community in the lung remains largely unknown. Our findings were consistent with previous studies in that the bacterial compositions of P. jirovecii infection, P. jirovecii colonization, and P. jirovecii negative groups were not statistically significant.28 Taxa, including other known lung pathogens, such as Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, were detected in the BALF samples tested. An increased proportion of opportunistic pathogens may lead to microbial translocation and secondary infections.

However, P. jirovecii infection and colonization were significantly associated with the alterations in the structure of the fungal and viral communities in the lung, manifested largely by presenting P. jirovecii and Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5 as the predominant species, compared to the P. jirovecii negative group. The viral and fungal microbiome of the P. jirovecii colonization group was intermediate between the P. jirovecii infection and patients with P. jirovecii negative. Therefore, the characterization of different species at different stages of P. jirovecii infection may be a potential biomarker. Importantly, stratified analyses by immune status revealed divergent associations. In immunocompetent patients, viral diversity was increased in those with P. jirovecii colonization compared with patients with P. jirovecii-negative, whereas no such difference was observed in infection cases. In contrast, among immunocompromised patients, viral diversity was markedly elevated in the infection group compared with negatives, with colonization showing intermediate but non-significant changes. These results indicate that the effect of P. jirovecii on lung virome composition is modulated by host immune status. One possible explanation is that in immunocompetent individuals, colonization may be sufficient to trigger virome alterations without progression to full-blown infection, whereas in immunocompromised patients, infection itself is the predominant driver of virome dysbiosis. This highlights the importance of considering host immune background when interpreting microbiome data in severe pneumonia.

Herpesviruses in ICUs have been widely reported, including Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5, herpes simplex virus, and Epstein-Barr virus, among others.29 Even in immunocompromised patients, the reactivation of Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5 is often found in critically ill patients.30,31 Co-infection with Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5 and other pathogens was detected in more than half of the patients with P. jirovecii infection.32 We also found that total viral relative abundance was significantly elevated in the lungs of patients with P. jirovecii infection compared with the negative group, manifested mainly by Simplexvirus humanalpha1, Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5, Lymphocryptovirus humangamma4, and multiple respiratory viruses. These strongly suggested that P. jirovecii can coexist with other pathogenic microorganisms, and infection may alter the local and systemic environment, thereby affecting the host’s lung microbiota. Moreover, we observed the 28-day mortality of individuals who are CMV-positive or EBV-positive was significantly associated with P. jirovecii positivity. Several prospective studies reported Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5 or Lymphocryptovirus humangamma4 reactivation was associated with significantly increased morbidity.33,34 Although it is difficult to establish a link between detected viruses and observed clinical symptoms, the mNGS can aid ICU clinicians in identifying mixed lung infections in patients and making sound treatment decisions.35 There are still many unresolved key issues in the field of co-infection. Cytomegalovirus humanbeta5 is considered a herpes virus associated with immunosuppression and has been shown to weaken innate immunity in the lungs by reprogramming alveolar macrophages.36 It remains unclear whether this will further weaken the body’s ability to clear P. jirovecii. A better understanding of the complex relationships between hosts and viruses, bacteria, and fungi, such as P. jirovecii will help us to cope with this difficult issue. After applying IPTW to reduce confounding, the association between P. jirovecii positivity and 28-day mortality remained significant in CMV-positive patients but was no longer evident in EBV-positive patients. This suggests that, unlike CMV, EBV reactivation may have a limited impact on short-term mortality. EBV reactivation in ICU settings is often transient and asymptomatic, and its clinical relevance remains controversial. Differences in viral load, detection timing, and host immune status may partly explain these findings. Therefore, EBV status might not be a decisive factor in the prognosis of P. jirovecii-positive patients, though further prospective studies are needed.

We discovered that the up-regulated genes in P. jirovecii infection group were significantly enriched in Oxidative phosphorylation, and the down-regulated genes were significantly enriched in Metabolic pathways, Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, Microbial metabolism in diverse environments, Biosynthesis of amino acids, and so forth. Nevertheless, recent studies of the complete genome sequence of P. jirovecii have shown that it has lost Carbohydrate, Lipid, Cofactor metabolism, and many other metabolic pathways, but may retain those critical for survival in the host environment, such as relying on glucose utilization through oxidative pathways to produce energy.37,38

In summary, our study highlights the complexity of the interactions between lung microbiota after P. jirovecii infection. In our two cohorts, even after adjusting for factors such as immunosuppression, disease severity, and various microorganisms previously shown to be associated with immunosuppression, P. jirovecii remains the most important microorganism associated with the virome changes. P. jirovecii colonization may induce alterations in the lung microenvironment and disrupt the lung microbiota, which may provide opportunities for invasion by potential pathogens. The present study will enhance our understanding of the collective mechanisms that contribute to P. jirovecii infection-associated pneumonia, which is an important step toward developing approaches for the treatment and management of polymicrobial infections.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the BALF samples came from multiple physicians who approached these procedures differently, so the procedure itself was not fully standardized. In addition, when analyzing the microbiota of low-biomass specimens, contaminated DNA in DNA extraction kits and other laboratory reagents may affect the results.39 We cannot guarantee that all pollutants were removed, although the negative controls were established; however, in general, the number of unique reads of contaminating bacteria was low. Additionally, we found qPCR and NGS results yielded inconsistent detection (n = 2) in the P. jirovecii negative group, which may be related to the selection of primers for the fungal variable region.40 However, overall P. jirovecii reads were very scarce in these two samples. qPCR and NGS cannot directly distinguish between P. jirovecii infection and colonization, and a combination of clinical characteristics and test results needs to be considered in clinical practice. Fourth, RNA viruses were not included in the virome analysis, as only DNA was extracted from archived BALF samples. This was due to the retrospective design and lack of RNA preservation, limiting our ability to assess RNA viruses potentially involved in acute infections. Finally, underlying diagnoses, comorbidities, and several other therapeutic factors could have an impact on the lung microbiome. Unfortunately, due to the species specificity of P. jirovecii, we have been unable to validate our results in animal experiments.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study approval and ethical statement

This retrospective study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine and other participating medical centers (No. IIT20230222A). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patient enrollment and grouping

The diagnosis of PCP or P. jirovecii colonization was independently determined by physicians at each participating center and reviewed by Lingtong Huang as we described before.47 Discrepancies were resolved by Hongliu Cai. Diagnostic criteria for PCP included: (1) positive P. jirovecii detection by clinical metagenomics or PCR; (2) consistent clinical manifestations; (3) compatible chest CT results. Exclusion criteria included confirmed COVID-19, invasive aspergillosis, or absence of host risk factors with persistently negative BDG tests. In Cohort 1 (n = 1,737), patients with pneumonia undergoing commercial metagenomics testing in 17 centers were classified as infection (n = 95), colonization (n = 65), or negative (n = 1,577). In Cohort 2 (n = 72), mechanically ventilated ICU patients were grouped as infection (n = 17), colonization (n = 19), or matched negative controls (n = 36). All participants were Han Chinese, with no significant differences in age or sex among groups.

Clinical data collection

Patient data, including laboratory test results and clinical manifestations, were retrospectively obtained from medical records. Variables recorded for analysis were age, sex, and main clinical symptoms at the time of diagnosis. The definition of immunosuppression is as mentioned before.3 The definition of severe pneumonia aligns with the description previously. White blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, C-reactive protein, oxygenation index, and procalcitonin level were also collected, when available. Clinical outcome was defined as either death or survival at 28 days after ICU admission.

Method details

Sample collection

Samples were obtained by electronic fiberoptic bronchoscopy with instillation and subsequent aspiration of sterile saline solution (NaCl 0.9%), which were collected in sterile plastic tubes.48 Then the precipitate was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4°C, collected, resuspended in sterile PBS, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4°C. The final pellet was used for DNA extraction.

DNA extraction and PCR

Total DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (Tiangen) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. After washing the centrifuge column, 100μl DNA was collected in 1.5ml tubes by centrifugation. DNA integrity and size were monitored by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentrations were measured with a Thermo NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific™). Extracted DNA samples were then directly used for PCR or stored at −20°C for future experiments. Additional QPCR testing was performed on P. jirovecii.

Metagenomic sequencing and data processing

A Paired-end library was constructed using NEXTFLEX® Rapid DNA-Seq (Bioo Scientific, USA). Metagenomic sequencing was performed on the Illumina Novaseq (Illumina, USA). Nuclease-free water served as negative control. The original data was filtered and quality checked with software fastp (v0.20.0).41 Low-quality and N-containing reads were removed to obtain high-quality data sequences for the subsequent analysis. Clean reads were aligned to the human genome sequences (homo sapiens) using BWA (v0.7.17) software to remove reads with high alignment similarity. Taxonomic classification of bacteria, fungi and viruses was assigned to metagenomic reads using Kraken2 (v2.1.1)43 and a comprehensive custom database of bacterial (370,424), fungal (7485), viral (41,963), archaeal (6269), and protozoal (994) genomes from the union of NCBI RefSeq and GenBank database. A confidence threshold of 0.05 was applied. Species were retained for further analysis if ≥100 reads in ≥3 samples (bacteria/fungi) or ≥10 reads in ≥3 samples (viruses).49 Clean reads were de novo assembled with MEGAHIT (v1.1.2),44 The predicted open reading frames (ORFs) with lengths being or over 100 bp were retrieved and translated into amino acid sequences using prodigal (v2.6.3).45 The predicted genes with a 95% sequence identity (90% coverage) were clustered using CD-HIT46 and the longest sequences from each cluster were selected as representative sequences to construct a non-redundant gene catalog. We used SOAPaligner software42 (http://soap.genomics.org.cn/) to compare the high-quality reads of each sample with the non-redundant gene set (default parameter: 95% identity) and calculate the abundance information of genes in the corresponding samples. The KEGG annotation was conducted using Diamond (https://github.com/bbuchfink/diamond, version 0.8.35) against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) with an e-value cutoff of 1e-5.

Clinical metagenomics testing and propensity matching

Clinical metagenomics assays were conducted in certified laboratories (CAP or Chinese National Health Commission EQA). A species/genus was considered positive if RPM-r ≥ 5 (sample RPM/negative control RPM). Propensity score matching (MatchIt R package) was applied on clinical variables (age, sex, pneumonia type, organ dysfunction, immunosuppression) using nearest-neighbor matching (1:2 ratio, caliper 0.2).

Mapping of fungal detection

Taxonomic IDs were assigned using taxizedb (v0.3.1)50 and NCBI taxonomy database. Phylogenetic visualization was generated with ggtree (v3.4.4), and geographic mapping used the Aliyun DataV. GeoAtlas platform with the sf R package (v1.0-14).

Diversity and correlation analysis

Beta diversity was calculated with Bray-Curtis dissimilarity using the vegan R package (v2.6-4), and PERMANOVA was performed with adonis2. Pearson correlations between fungal and viral species were calculated with Hmisc (v5.0-1) and visualized with corrplot (v0.92) and Cytoscape (v3.9.1). Co-occurrence analysis was performed with the cooccur R package (v1.3) and visualized with ggcorrplot2 (v0.1.2).

Survival analysis

Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to adjust for confounders in analyses of P. jirovecii status and 28-day mortality stratified by CMV or EBV infection. Propensity scores were used to derive stabilized weights, incorporating key clinical covariates including SOFA score, solid tumor, hematological malignancy, and chronic kidney disease. Covariate balance after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMD < 0.1 considered acceptable. The resulting weights were incorporated into weighted Cox proportional hazards models to estimate the association between P. jirovecii status and 28-day mortality in the viral subgroups.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Comparisons of microbial taxa between groups used paired or Welch’s t-tests, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and Chi-squared tests, with q value adjustment for multiple testing. Thirty-six species were identified as significantly different among groups. The variance inflation factor (VIF) threshold was set at 4. Correlation with clinical parameters was assessed by Spearman analysis and visualized as heatmaps. Redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed with log10-transformed abundances of bacteria, fungi, and viruses using the vegan package. All statistical analyses were performed using R language and environment (R 2022, http://www.rproject.org) version 4.2.0 and the add-on packages.
