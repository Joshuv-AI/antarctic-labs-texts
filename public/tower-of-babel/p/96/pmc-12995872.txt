Finerenone Utilization for Chronic Kidney Disease and Diabetes
Authors: Wei Lin, Adam Schweber, Yunwen Xu, Alexander R Chang, Youssef MK Farag, Amrita Mukhopadhyay, Saul B Blecker, Leora I Horwitz, Morgan E Grams, Jung-Im Shin
Published: 2026

Finerenone, a nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (ns-MRA), received Food and Drug Administration approval in July 2021 for use in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and type 2 diabetes (T2D). With cardiorenal benefits supported by randomized trials,1 ns-MRAs are recommended in the 2022 KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) guidelines.2 However, data on the real-world utilization patterns of finerenone among people with diabetes are scarce.

We evaluated electronic health records data from 3 regional U.S. health systems. Among patients with diagnosed diabetes, finerenone initiation, as well as steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (s-MRAs), renin-angiotensin-system inhibitors (RASis), glucagon like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAsf), and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is), were abstracted from outpatient prescriptions records after July 9, 2021 (finerenone approval date) up to December 31, 2024. Prespecified demographic and clinical factors possibly associated with finerenone initiation were assessed within each system using multivariable logistic regression and pooled using random-effects meta-analysis. The baseline cardiovascular disease (CVD) and CKD progression risk was determined using the KDIGO categories (low, moderate, high, and very high risk); the distribution of risk categories was compared across patients initiating different medication classes. The study was approved by Institutional Review Board of Johns Hopkins and New York University.

What is the clinical question being addressed?

How has finerenone been adopted and utilized in real-world clinical practice among individuals with diabetes?

What is the main finding?

Finerenone prescription was extremely low but did appear to be specifically targeted toward patients with higher risk of cardiovascular disease and chronic kidney disease progression.

There were 245,763 (mean age: 67.8 years; 48.1% females; 48.3% non-Hispanic White; 16.2% non-Hispanic Black; 16.0% Hispanic), 205,194 (mean age: 68.2 years; 49.0% females; 90.8% non-Hispanic White; non-Hispanic Black 3.9%; Hispanic 4.1%), and 128,212 individuals (mean age: 62.2 years; 54.0% females; 54.8% non-Hispanic White; 35.7% non-Hispanic Black; 4.4% Hispanic) with diabetes seen across the 3 health systems; 0.3% (717), 0.1% (157), and 0.04% (57) initiated finerenone during the study period (from July 9, 2021 to December 31, 2024). Among 931 new finerenone users, 77.3% were covered by Medicare, 15.8% by commercial insurance, and 2.4% by Medicaid. The number of new finerenone users increased over time in all systems: 15.6% (95% CI: 10.3% to 21.0%) relative increase per quarter, 19.2% (8.5% to 30.9%), and 15.9% (6.8% to 25.7%), respectively (Figure 1A). Overall, only 0.6% of patients recommended to initiate finerenone (diabetes, estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] ≥25 mL/min/1.73 m2 and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥30 mg/g) (N = 91,084) received a finerenone prescription. Factors negatively associated with finerenone initiation among eligible patients were older age (meta-analyzed adjusted OR per 10 years older: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.84 to 0.97), female (OR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.61-0.88), and heart failure (OR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.42-0.86). In contrast, non-White race (OR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.04-1.52), use of other guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for CKD (RASi, GLP-1RA, or SGLT2i: 1 concomitant medication, OR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.29-2.13; ≥2 medications, OR: 3.82; 95% CI: 2.97-4.91), lower eGFR vs eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (eGFR 45-59 mL/min/1.73 m2, OR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.59-3.31; eGFR 25-44 mL/min/1.73 m2, OR: 3.30, 95% CI: 2.24-4.48) and albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥300 mg/-g (OR: 3.91; 95% CI: 2.47-6.19) were positively associated with finerenone initiation.

Patients who received a finerenone prescription were at much higher baseline risk of CVD and CKD progression than those who received GLP-1RA, SGLT2i, RASi, or s-MRA prescriptions, with consistent patterns across systems. When categorized by KDIGO risk, the proportion of finerenone initiators at high and very high risk was 78.3% (79.4%, 69.2%, and 86.5%, respectively, in each system). In contrast, initiators of GLP-1RA, SGLT2i, RASi, and s-MRA who were classified as high and very high risk represented 18.3%, 28.3%, 24.4%, and 37.9%, respectively (Figure 1B).

There are multiple possible reasons for the extremely low rates of finerenone prescription. Prior authorization requirements undoubtedly play a role. Some health plans mandate prior failure of an SGLT2i treatment.3 Others require no pre-existing heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (similar to the eligibility criteria of clinical trials),3 which may explain our observation that patients with heart failure were less likely to initiate finerenone. Safety profiles may deter prescription of finerenone, as hyperkalemia was reported in 12.0% of trial participants in the active arm.1 Interestingly, we observed that non-White patients—of whom 44.5% were non-Hispanic Black and 26.0% Hispanic—were more likely to initiate finerenone, despite prior evidence that Black or Hispanic patients were less likely to be prescribed other novel GDMT such as SGLT2i and GLP-1RA.4 Provider perception of higher risk of CVD and CKD among non-White patients may play a role.

There was a striking difference in risk profiles among patients prescribed finerenone compared to other GDMT. Finerenone is specifically approved for patients with T2D and CKD, whereas RASi, SGLT2i, and GLP-1RA have broader indications in T2D, encompassing lower-risk patients. In addition, prior authorization programs often require strict albuminuria and eGFR thresholds for patient approval.3 In our study, a greater number of concurrent GDMT was associated with a higher likelihood of finerenone initiation. Given the traditional treatment sequence—initiating and maximizing RASi therapy, followed by the addition of SGLT2i, reassessment after 3 to 6 months, subsequent initiation of ns-MRA, and consideration of GLP-1RA5—the use of a greater number of concomitant GDMT likely reflects more advanced or treatment-refractory disease.

This study leveraged data from 3 large regional health systems covering diverse patient populations, enhancing generalizability. However, the small number of finerenone initiators and the lack of information on why patients were or were not prescribed finerenone warrant cautious interpretation of the regression analysis. Albuminuria was frequently not tested, impeding baseline risk assessment. Overall, 32.3% of patients initiating finerenone were not tested for albuminuria 3 years before medication initiation. Consequently, finerenone uptake estimates likely reflect among measured eligible population rather than the entire eligible population. Lastly, different GDMT initiators in our study were not mutually exclusive, but our goal was to assess baseline risk at the time of different medication initiation even within the same person.

In conclusion, finerenone prescription was extremely low across 3 health systems, but did appear to be specifically targeted toward patients with higher risk of CVD and CKD progression. Improving access and accumulating evidence supporting favorable risk-benefit ratios might increase the adoption of finerenone.
