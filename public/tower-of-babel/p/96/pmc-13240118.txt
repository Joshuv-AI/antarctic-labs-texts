Preventing Cerebral Air Embolism Following Central Venous Catheter Removal: The Role of Tissue Adhesives
Authors: Ananda Shakya, Pieter Bothma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTissue adhesives are a simple yet effective solution to achieve adequate sealing of the tract. Early hyperbaric treatment is essential and definitive treatment for gas embolism.

Kanga Bejan et al. [1] deserve recognition for bringing to attention a rare but serious case of air embolism following central venous catheter (CVC) removal. Their report highlights that appropriate precautions were taken, including removing the line in the Trendelenburg position and asking the patient to perform Valsalva maneuver, both of which are standard and important practices. However, there are few aspects of management that merit further reflection. The use of petroleum‐based jelly at the insertion site, for example, may not be the most reliable way to prevent air entry. On the other hand, achieving an airtight seal from a tight dressing is practically difficult to achieve due to movement, sweating, etc., even after hemostasis is achieved. Furthermore, the dressing is not impermeable and any movements that cause decrease in central venous pressure (CVP) like coughing or standing can cause air embolism [2]. Coughing may separate soft tissues along the catheter tract [3] and even the small tract left behind is enough for the air to be drawn in due to negative intrathoracic pressure [4], and given the devastating sequalae, the risk is too high.

Hence, we would like to suggest tissue cyanoacrylate (tissue adhesive) as a simple and practical solution for reliably achieving adequate closure of the residual orifice. Suturing the incision site is a viable solution [5]. Tissue adhesive is advantageous over suturing as it is quicker, less painful, and easy to apply, even by nursing or outreach teams with minimal additional training. Our own local audit showed that it is both effective and safe, with no complications on immediate or long‐term follow‐up. In addition to that, cyanoacrylate adhesive has been shown to promote less intense and more organized inflammatory reaction, with greater collagen deposition and greater tensile strength and facilitating faster healing [6].

The second point we would like to highlight is the management of air embolism in this patient. Early administration of 100% oxygen and positioning the patient in left lateral decubitus (Durant's maneuver) and Trendelenburg position is appropriate and known initial steps [7]. What we would like to emphasize is early consideration of hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT). There is good evidence that the sooner HBOT is started, the better the outcomes [8, 9]. In this case, symptoms developed early, yet the patient has ultimately passed away on Day 24, suggesting there may have been an opportunity to escalate treatment. It is uncertain whether access to HBOT was a restrictive factor. Current guidance from the Intensive Care Society [10] recognizes HBOT as a definitive treatment and provides recommendations for its timely use.

Overall, this case outlines the essence of effective application of correct preventive measures to undertake while also highlighting the importance of early escalation to HBOT when complications arise.
