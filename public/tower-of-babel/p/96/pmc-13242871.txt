Lessons From Deep Eutectic Solvents to Design High Entropy Electrolytes for Electrochemical Energy Storage
Authors: Wenjia Zhang, Xusheng Zhou, Manuel Cerro‐Carracedo, Paula García‐Balaguer, Bernabé Gómez‐Herrera, Boren Xu, María C Gutiérrez, Francisco del Monte, M Luisa Ferrer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increased interest that high‐entropy electrolytes (HEEs) are lately receiving makes their design (in terms of number of different components and ratio among them) challenging. Most of current efforts to bring some light into the combinatorial design space of HEEs rely on developing machine models but it is yet necessary identifying solvent descriptors that can accurately capture key solvent properties. Thus, there is an urgent need of alternative strategies offering convenient and powerful approaches for designing HEEs. Here, we hypothesized that deviations from ideality may indeed be a fingerprint of solvent mixtures with complex and nonadditive molecular interactions between components. While little attention has been paid to the nonlinear correlations in HEEs, deviations from ideality have been largely studied in deep eutectic solvents (DESs). The aim of this perspective is to demonstrate that these deviations serve as a definitive fingerprint of the solvation architecture of not only DESs but also HEEs, allowing these thermodynamic anomalies to be used as a proxy for determining the entropic landscape of some specific HEE compositions.Keywords: deep eutectic solvent, deviation from ideality, excess properties, high‐entropy electrolytes, nonideal mixtures, solvation structure, supercapacitors

Introduction

The state‐of‐the‐art for high‐entropy electrolytes (HEEs) has rapidly evolved from a conceptual transfer of the high‐entropy materials paradigm to practical liquid and solid electrolyte systems: researchers have shown that deliberately increasing compositional complexity (multiple salts, solvents, or ionic species) raises configurational entropy and can reshape solvation structures and interfacial chemistry in ways that improve electrochemical performance [1]. Interestingly, this increase in the number and diversity of molecules in the solvation structures has been observed that contributes to more negative Gibbs energies dominated by the entropic contribution [2]. Recent experimental and modeling studies report that HEEs can promote more robust solid/cathode electrolyte interphase (SEI/CEI) formation, tune ion‐coordination environments to improve coulombic efficiency and rate capability, and break traditional tradeoffs between overpotential and reversibility—benefits that have been demonstrated in lithium and zinc battery chemistries [3]. Overall, the field is moving from proof‐of‐concept studies toward targeted design rules that combine experiments, atomistic modeling, and data‐driven approaches to exploit the unique advantages of compositional complexity for safer, longer‐lived, and higher‐performance energy storage devices.

The challenge is finding a universal strategy to design HEEs given their complex composition with multiple solvents, salts and additives. This compositional diversity creates a vast combinatorial design space that is nearly impossible to explore exhaustively through traditional trial‐and‐error experimentation. Machine learning might offer a highly convenient and powerful approach for designing HEEs. Machine learning models can efficiently analyze large datasets, identify complex nonlinear relationships between composition and performance, and predict promising formulations with minimal experimental input [4, 5, 6]. By learning from both experimental and computational data, these models can guide targeted synthesis, reduce the number of required experiments, and accelerate the discovery of optimal electrolyte systems [7, 8]. Consequently, machine learning may transform the design of HEEs from an empirical, labor‐intensive process into a data‐driven, predictive, and scalable scientific strategy.

Still, the search for suitable solvent descriptors to enable machine learning‐driven design of HEEs remains a major challenge in materials science [9, 10]. Identifying descriptors that can accurately capture key solvent properties—such as polarity, donor number, viscosity, dielectric constant, and molecular interactions—is difficult because these parameters often exhibit strong nonlinear correlations and context‐dependent behavior in mixtures [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Moreover, many traditional molecular descriptors fail to fully represent collective solvation effects or dynamic interactions that influence ionic transport and stability. As a result, efforts are still needed to identify physically meaningful, and computationally efficient descriptors that bridge molecular‐scale structure and macroscopic performance, enabling reliable machine learning models for rational electrolyte design.

Interestingly, little attention has been paid in HEEs to the nonlinear correlations mentioned above that arise from deviations from ideality and are indeed a fingerprint of solvent mixtures with complex and nonadditive molecular interactions between components [17, 18, 19, 20]. In an ideal mixture, the interactions between unlike molecules are the same as those between like molecules, leading to predictable, linear relationships between composition and properties. However, real solvent systems often deviate from this ideal behavior due to differences in polarity, hydrogen bonding (HB), dipole moments, or molecular size. These deviations—manifested as, for example, excess enthalpies, volume changes, or activity coefficients—provide insight into specific interactions such as preferential solvation, clustering, or repulsion between components [21]. Understanding and quantifying these deviations is crucial for the rational design of complex electrolyte systems, including HEEs. Particularly interesting is determining the compositions where nonideal interactions more profoundly influence ion solvation structures, transport properties, and electrochemical stability.

Here in, we hypothesized that deep eutectic solvents (DESs) may provide a valuable model system for studying HEEs [22]. Most common DESs are formed by combining a hydrogen bond donor and acceptor (HBD and HBA, respectively), with the strong specific interactions established between them—such as HB, ion–dipole forces, and charge delocalization—being responsible of significant deviations from ideal mixing behavior [23]. In particular, we are interested in not only binary but also ternary and quaternary DESs [24, 25, 26], that are inherently more complex than binary ones and in which the highly interactive molecular structures may serve as experimentally accessible and theoretically rich platforms for probing the relationship between molecular interactions, structure, and thermodynamic behavior—insights that are directly relevant to the design of complex HEEs. Moreover, special attention will also be paid to DESs obtained by addition of a molecular solvent (e.g., H2O) [27, 28] for which the whole set of dilutions allows an easy visualization of deviations from either ideality in different properties [29] and/or conventional trends in Brillouin and 1H NMR spectroscopies [30, 31, 32, 33]. Within this context, the aim of this perspective is demonstrating that these deviations serve as a definitive fingerprint of the solvation architecture, allowing these thermodynamic and spectroscopic anomalies to be used as a proxy for determining the entropic landscape of HEEs. The reason of focusing on the solvation architecture of DESs rather than directly in the entropic contributions is because of the limited number of DESs and DESs dilutions for which these contributions to the Gibbs energy of mixing are described. Nonetheless, it is worth noting that most recent ones also considered a nonideal scenario [34, 35, 36, 37] thus supporting the strategy followed in this perspective.

Solvation Structure and Nonideal Behavior in Binary DESs

As an archetype of binary DESs, we started with “reline”, a DES composed of choline chloride and urea in a 1:2 molar ratio (ChCl:2U, Figure 1). It has been widely described that, when anhydrous, the ChCl:2U mixture melts at ca. 303 K [38], while the corresponding ideal mixture would have a melting point close to 373 K (Figure 1). Note that this temperature depression (ΔT 2) is defined as the difference between the ideal (T E, ideal) and the real (T E) eutectic point and not as the difference (ΔT 1) between the linear combination of the melting points of the pure components and T E [39]. This strong decrease of the melting point is a finger print of DESs and, in the particular case of ChCl:2U, has been assigned (at least partially) to the negative deviations from ideality in the U solubility curve [40]. Actually, U molecules form a wide range of HBs in ChCl:2U, from soft contacts to stronger associations, the latter being responsible for the deviation from ideality [41]. This U incorporation into the flexible HB network of ChCl:2U occurs by adoption of a nonplanar (sp3) shape, slightly more pyramidal than in H2O [42, 43] and far from the planar (sp2) structure adopted in the crystal [44].

Interestingly, H2O and U also form a eutectic mixture at a U concentration of ca. 33 wt% (this is, ca. 0.13 in U molar fraction or U:6.7H2O in molar ratio) with a melting point at ca. 261.2 K (Figure 2) [45]. In this case, the decrease of the melting point is also ascribed to the formation of HBs between water and urea, as the preferred coordination positions of U and H2O widely overlap (Figure 2a). This overlapping determines that the average number of HBs formed between water molecules decreases from ca. 3.6 for bare water to ca. 3.0 at the eutectic composition (Figure 2b) [46, 47].

(c) Average number of HBs normalized per H2O molecule. Blue, HBs to H2O; green, HBs to U; black, sum. Reprinted from ref. [45].

ChCl and H2O can also form a eutectic mixture (ChCl:4H2O in molar ratio, Figure 3a) by the preferred interaction of H2O with the hydroxyl group of Ch and the Cl– anion [48, 49, 50, 51]. Actually, MD simulations have revealed that there is an enhanced tendency of water molecules to get closer to the hydroxyl group of the cation with increasing water contents [50]. In this case, the melting point was at ca. 218 K (Figure 3a) with the DSC scans displaying none transition for ChCl:3.3H2O and ChCl:4.2H2O (Figure 3b) [49, 51]. Thus, according to the above‐mentioned definition of DES based on the magnitude of ΔT 2, the ChCl:4H2O mixture could be considered as a DES (resembling the so‐called unconventional DESs) [52] with a ΔT 2 of ca. 20 K while the mixture of water and U should never be considered as a DES but as a regular eutectic mixture (the T E, ideal for the mixture of water and U is ca. 262.3 K) [53]. Moreover, the application of Brillouin spectroscopy to the study of mixtures of ChCl:2H2O with different H2O contents revealed how the hypersonic velocities (νH) and the opto‐acoustic dispersion function (D 180–function) began to deviate from the conventional ultrasonic velocities (νU) and the refractive index for water contents of ca. 18 wt% (ChCl:4.1H2O in Figures 3b and S1) [49]. Discrepancies between νH and νU have been ascribed to a relaxation phenomenon, when transitioning from a purely dissipative fluid to an ‘‘elastic medium’’ (see Appendix I for details on the physical insights of ultrasonic and hypersonic velocities) [54]. Interestingly, the excess logarithmic viscosity (ln η E) also exhibited the maximum deviation form ideality for water contents of ca. 18 wt% (Figure 3d). Finally, the 1H NMR spectra of mixtures of ChCl:2H2O also followed some particular pattern, with the coalescent signal of the exchangeable proton of water molecules and the hydroxyl group of ChCl becoming two separate signals for basically the same H2O contents (e.g., ChCl:4.4H2O in Figure S2) than those where data obtained by Brillouin spectroscopy deviated from data obtained by conventional means (e.g., ChCl:4.1H2O in Figure S1) [49]. It is worth noting that this transition of the exchangeable proton signals, from coalescent to separated, has been observed previously in aqueous dilutions of DESs when transitioning from the so‐called DES‐in‐water regime to the water‐in‐DES one [31, 55], in which the number of HBs that H2O molecules formed with themselves decreased from ca. 3.6 (i.e., corresponding to bulk H2O with a tetrahedral structure) [56] to ca. 2 [57].

Both in ChCl:2U and in ChCl:4H2O, the Cl– anion plays a capital role in the formation of HBs. The analysis of the solvation structure of the Cl– anion is also of relevance within the context of this perspective given the participation of electrolyte ions in all the mechanisms describing electrochemical energy storage, from chemical insertion in batteries to physical absorption in supercapacitors. Interestingly, the radial distribution functions (RDFs) of the centers of mass of Cl–Ch and Cl–U for ChCl:2U and of Cl–Ch and Cl–H2O for ChCl:3.33H2O obtained from, respectively, EPSR analysis of neutron scattering experiments performed with ChCl:2U and MD simulations performed with ChCl:3.33H2O provided quite similar intermolecular coordination numbers in the first solvation shell of Cl− anions (Figure 4a) [50, 57].

Solvation Structure and Nonideal Behavior in Ternary and Quaternary DESs

Given the similar complexity of the solvation structure found in different binary DESs, one could hypothesize whether this complexity (in number and/or diversity of species) could increase in ternary DESs. This was indeed confirmed in ternary DES derived from ChCl:2U and obtained by incorporation of resorcinol (R, 3 equivalents) as a second HBD so to obtain ChCl:2U:3R [59, 60]. The complexity of the solvation structure of ChCl:2U and ChCl:2U:3R was analyzed on the basis of the site–site coordination numbers obtained by EPSR analysis of neutron scattering experiments (Figure 4b). In this case, to allow the formation of Cl–R interactions, the coordination numbers of Cl–Ch and Cl–U for ChCl:2U:3R decreased as compared to those for ChCl:2U. However, the total number of molecules in the first solvation shell of Cl– anions (sum of Ch, U, and R) was higher for ChCl:2U:3R than for ChCl:2U.

With the same rationing, quaternary DESs could exhibit a solvation structure around the Cl– anions with even further complexity than ternary ones. As for ChCl:4H2O, where water was playing the role of an HBD, quaternary DESs could be obtained by addition of certain amounts of water to ChCl:2U:3R. Thus, our group studied by neutron scattering experiments and EPSR analysis the quaternary mixture obtained by addition of 11 equivalents of H2O (e.g., ChCl:2U:3R:·11H2O) [58], for which both the divergence between νH and νU, and the transition of the exchangeable proton signals from coalescent to separated began (Figures 4c, S2) [58, 61]. Interestingly, the coordination numbers exhibited the same trend as for ChCl:2U:3R and ChCl:2U, decreasing those of Cl–Ch, Cl–U, and Cl–R for ChCl:2U:3R:11H2O as compared to those for ChCl:2U:3R, but with a contribution of Cl–H2O that ultimately led to a net increase of the final number of molecules in the first solvation shell of Cl– anions in ChCl:2U:3R:11H2O (Figure 4b).

Solvation Structure and Nonideal Behavior in HEEs

After identifying the correlation in DESs between deviation of ideality and complexity of the solvation structure, we looked at the HEE prepared by combination of two ionic liquids (ILs) sharing the cation (e.g., 1‐ethyl‐3‐methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate and 1‐ethyl‐3‐methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide, this is EMIMBF4 and EMIMTFSI, respectively) in a mixture of H2O, dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO), and acetonitrile (CH3CN) with a 2:1:1.5 molar ratio (e.g., 2H2O:DMSO:1.5CH3CN). The molar ratio between H2O and DMSO was thus selected because they form a eutectic mixture at this specific composition [62, 63]. Meanwhile, the CH3CN content was determined considering previous works describing binary mixtures of H2O and DMSO, H2O and CH3CN, and DMSO and CH3CN [64], as well as the balance between the ionic conductivity and the flammability of the resulting ternary mixture [65, 66, 67].

The key question was what EMIMBF4:EMIMTFSI ratio (in the 2H2O:DMSO:1.5CH3CN solvent mixture) best performs as the electrolyte of a supercapacitor cell (see Appendix II in Supporting Information for details of electrode preparation and electrochemical measurements) and whether this performance correlates with the complexity of the solvation structure and/or the deviation of ideality. Among the different mixtures, the best performance in terms of energy and power density (as consequence, at least partially, of the improvement of the ionic conductivity, ion dissociation balance, and electrode‐specific interfacial compatibility; see Figure S4) as well as of capacity retention upon cycling was found for 0.69EMIMBF4:0.81EMIMTFSI:2H2O:DMSO:1.5CH3CN (Figure 5a,b) [68]. This mixture also exhibited the solvation structure with the largest number and diversity of species (Figures 5c,d and S5), confirming its high‐entropic nature. Interestingly, the deviations from ideality of the excess molar volumes (VE) and excess viscosities (ln ηE) were also maxima for this specific mixture (Figure 5e,f), with negative deviations for VE and positive for ln ηE as corresponds to mixtures formed by strong intermolecular interactions including charge transfer, dipole–dipole interactions, and HBs [29, 69].

Summary, Final Remarks, and Future Directions

DESs are characterized by the formation of HB complexes between one of the components (acting as HBA) and the other one (acting as HBD). These extensive intermolecular interactions promote a structural reorganization of the solvation structure of Cl– anions that serves as the microscopic origin of the significant deviations from ideality observed in their macroscopic thermodynamic properties. Interestingly, the complexity of the solvation structure of Cl– anions (in number and diversity of species) dictates the magnitude of the nonideal behavior; the more complex is the solvation structure, the more the system deviates from ideal mixing patterns.

In HEEs, the disordered heterocomplexation within the solvation structure of ions is the responsible of breaking the tradeoff between conductivity and interfacial stability, this is the most remarkable feature of HEEs. The challenge is finding a universal strategy to design HEEs because their complex composition (with multiple salts sharing either the anion or the cation, and solvents) creates a vast combinatorial design space that is nearly impossible to explore exhaustively through traditional trial‐and‐error experimentation. While considerable efforts focus on the search of suitable solvent descriptors to enable machine learning‐driven design of HEEs, little attention has been paid to the physical insights coming out from excess properties. This perspective pointed to excess properties in HEEs as a simple way to measure the structural and energetic changes that occur upon mixing and thus, find the eventual ratio among components that best promotes ionic mobility and favors desolvation at the interface.

The mixture used in this perspective as an example to validate this correlation between deviation from ideality and solvation structure in HEEs was composed of two ILs and H2O, DMSO and CH3CN as solvents/cosolvents. Future works should explore the use of different salts and/or different cosolvents besides H2O. For instance, works in progress from our group are currently exploring the use of double salts soluble in H2O, sharing either the anion or the cation, and with common monovalent or multivalent anions and cations (e.g., Cl–, ClO4 3–, CH3CO2 –, etc. and Li+, Na+, K+, Zn2+, Fe3+, etc.). Moreover, the use of number of cosolvents miscible with H2O and with different HB capabilities (e.g., ethylene glycol, glycerol, γ–butyrolactone, sulfolane, etc.) could be explored. Meanwhile, it is worth noting that the electrochemical energy storage of choice in this perspective was a supercapacitor, but one could also visualize the application of this approach for the design of HEE for other types of electrochemical energy storage systems like hybrid capacitors, aqueous Zn and Fe batteries, or aqueous redox flow batteries, among others.
