Hepatic Fascioliasis Mimicking Hydatid Cyst and Complicated by Portal Vein Thrombosis: A Case Report From Ethiopia
Authors: Binyam Mulatu Habte, Yoseph Mulatu Habte, Makida Mulatu Habte, Selamawit Gudeta Jima, Alazar Sitotaw, Mekoya Mengistu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFascioliasis is a zoonotic helminthic infection often underdiagnosed in humans, particularly in endemic regions like Ethiopia. In its hepatic migratory phase, it can mimic other liver pathologies such as hydatid cysts or pyogenic liver abscesses, making diagnosis difficult. We report a case of a 48‐year‐old Ethiopian woman with a three‐month history of right upper quadrant abdominal pain, intermittent fever, and vomiting. She frequently consumed raw lettuce and tomatoes. Laboratory evaluation revealed leukocytosis with marked eosinophilia. Abdominal ultrasound showed a heterogeneous hepatic lesion, raising suspicion for hydatid cyst or early liver abscess. Triphasic CT revealed multiple confluent hypoattenuating lesions in segment VII and thrombosis of the right anterior portal vein. Although serologic, parasitologic, and histopathologic confirmation was unavailable, the diagnosis of hepatic fascioliasis was strongly supported by clinical findings, radiologic features, endemic exposure, and rapid response to triclabendazole. Anti‐parasitic therapy and anticoagulation resulted in clinical and hematologic improvement. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of hepatic fascioliasis in low‐resource settings and the importance of considering it in patients with hepatic lesions, eosinophilia, and portal vein thrombosis. To our knowledge, this is the first reported Ethiopian case of hepatic fascioliasis complicated by portal venous thrombosis.

Introduction

Fascioliasis is a zoonotic parasitic infection caused by two species of trematodes, namely Fasciola hepatica and Fasciola gigantica , and mainly affects the liver. Humans acquire the infection by ingesting raw aquatic plants or vegetables contaminated with encysted metacercariae [1].

The clinical presentation of fascioliasis consists of two phases: the acute hepatic phase involving fluke migration through the liver parenchyma, and presenting with right upper quadrant pain, fever, and eosinophilia; the chronic biliary phase includes symptoms associated with biliary obstruction, such as cholangitis and cholelithiasis. Diagnosis in the acute phase can be challenging due to non‐specific symptoms and frequently negative stool examinations. Imaging often reveals subcapsular hepatic tracks or ill‐defined lesions, which may mimic pyogenic liver abscess or hydatid cyst [2].

Vascular complications such as portal vein thrombosis (PVT) are extremely rare in fascioliasis and have been reported in isolated cases [3]. This leads to diagnostic delay, particularly in resource‐limited settings where advanced imaging or serological testing may not be readily accessible.

We present a case of a 48‐year‐old woman with acute fascioliasis mimicking a hydatid cyst and complicated by portal vein thrombosis, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and the importance of considering parasitic infections in resource‐limited settings.

Case History/Examinations

A 48‐year‐old female patient from Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, presented with dull aching right upper quadrant abdominal pain of 3 months' duration. The pain progressively worsened over the past week and was associated with multiple episodes of bilious vomiting and low‐grade fever. She also has a history of frequent consumption of raw tomatoes and lettuce; otherwise, no history of jaundice, weight loss, or diarrhea.

On admission, she was conscious (E4V5M6); her vital signs were that she was febrile, with a temperature of 38.6°C, blood pressure of 128/76 mmHg, and heart rate of 84/min. On abdominal examination, she had tenderness over the right upper quadrant without guarding or rebound tenderness. There was no hepatosplenomegaly or ascites. Other examination findings were unremarkable.

While serologic testing and ERCP could have provided diagnostic confirmation, these were not performed due to unavailability in our resource‐limited setting. The absence of these tools reflects a broader challenge in many low‐income regions, where advanced diagnostics are either inaccessible or cost‐prohibitive, and clinicians must rely on clinical, laboratory, and radiologic findings to make timely therapeutic decisions. Liver biopsy was not performed due to its invasive nature, associated procedural risks, and the anticipated low diagnostic yield in the setting of a parasitic infection with characteristic clinical and radiologic features.

The patient was initially started on empiric antimicrobial therapy with intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole for presumed pyogenic liver abscess. After radiologic and clinical suspicion of fascioliasis was raised, she was given a single dose of Triclabendazole 10 mg/kg. Following the detection of portal vein thrombosis, anticoagulation was started with unfractionated heparin and warfarin, later transitioned to oral rivaroxaban.

The patient demonstrated gradual clinical improvement. Abdominal pain and fever resolved, and her eosinophil count began to decline over the following days and normalized in weeks. She was discharged in stable condition with follow‐up arranged.

Case Discussion

Fascioliasis, caused primarily by the liver fluke Fasciola hepatica , is a parasitic infection of growing importance, particularly in regions where humans are exposed to contaminated freshwater vegetation. While the disease burden is concentrated in certain endemic areas of Latin America, Africa, and the Middle East, under diagnosis remains a challenge even in areas where zoonotic transmission is possible [3, 4].

Although fascioliasis is frequently overlooked in clinical practice in Ethiopia, it is likely more prevalent than reported. Several case reports from Ethiopian tertiary centers have confirmed human infection, and veterinary data consistently show high fasciola infection rates in livestock, suggesting endemic transmission [5, 6].

This case underscores the diagnostic challenges posed by hepatic fascioliasis, particularly when its manifestations mimic other common hepatobiliary pathologies such as hydatid cyst or pyogenic liver abscess, and when it is further complicated by rare vascular events like portal vein thrombosis.

The infection typically follows a biphasic course: an initial hepatic (parenchymal) phase marked by larval migration through liver tissue, followed by a chronic biliary phase as adult flukes inhabit the biliary ducts [7]. The patient described in this report presented during the hepatic migratory phase, a stage where clinical features are highly non‐specific and frequently misinterpreted. Symptoms such as right upper quadrant pain, low‐grade fever, and vomiting are commonly attributed to more prevalent conditions, including bacterial liver abscesses and parasitic cysts like echinococcosis. The presence of peripheral eosinophilia, however, serves as an important diagnostic clue that should raise suspicion for helminthic infections [2].

In our case, the patient's presentation prompted an initial differential diagnosis that included pyogenic liver abscess and hydatid cyst. A pyogenic liver abscess was suspected first due to the presence of fever, leukocytosis, and hepatic tenderness. However, the absence of other systemic symptoms and the absence of response to antibiotics reduced its likelihood. Hydatid cyst was also considered given the endemicity and radiologic appearance of the liver lesion, yet no daughter cysts or calcifications were noted on imaging.

Imaging plays a pivotal role in reaching a correct diagnosis. The ultrasound in this case revealed a heterogeneously echogenic lesion with no internal vascularity and adjacent portal vein thrombosis, prompting an initial differential that included hydatid disease and liver abscess. However, triphasic contrast‐enhanced CT provided more clarity: the liver showed multiple hypoattenuating and subcapsular lesions without enhancement or central liquefaction—features that are characteristic but not pathognomonic of the hepatic phase of fascioliasis [8]. The suggestive radiologic pattern of irregular, poorly defined hepatic lesions combined with eosinophilia should prompt early consideration of parasitic migration, particularly when stool tests are negative and serology is unavailable or pending [7].

While invasive procedures such as liver biopsy and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) can offer diagnostic confirmation in hepatic fascioliasis, their use is often limited by both practical and clinical considerations. Liver biopsy may reveal eosinophilic infiltration, granulomatous inflammation, or necrosis; however, it carries procedural risks and generally offers a low diagnostic yield, as the migrating larvae often do not localize in biopsied tissue [9]. In our case, biopsy was deemed unnecessary due to the characteristic radiologic features and pronounced eosinophilia, which strongly supported a presumptive diagnosis. Similarly, ERCP can aid in detecting adult flukes within the biliary tree during the chronic phase, but it was not available in our setting and is less helpful during the early hepatic (migratory) phase [10]. The unavailability of these procedures reflects the diagnostic limitations common in resource‐constrained environments, where clinical acumen and imaging become critical to timely and effective management.

A distinguishing feature in this case was the presence of segmental portal vein thrombosis (PVT), a rarely reported complication of fascioliasis. While PVT is well documented in association with hepatic malignancies, cirrhosis, and hydatid cysts, its occurrence in fascioliasis is infrequent and believed to result from localized inflammation, endothelial disruption, or mass effect from larval migration and perivascular eosinophilic infiltrates [3]. The recognition of PVT in the setting of fascioliasis is critical because it can alter management, requiring anticoagulation in addition to anti‐parasitic therapy. In our patient, early initiation of anticoagulation likely mitigated further thrombotic propagation.

The diagnostic delay in this case emphasizes the importance of integrating epidemiological context, eosinophilic laboratory patterns, and imaging findings. Despite the absence of definitive stool ova or serologic confirmation, the convergence of radiological and hematological findings supported the presumptive diagnosis and guided successful therapy. This approach is increasingly validated in the literature, particularly in endemic areas where advanced diagnostic tools may be limited [11].

Once hepatic fascioliasis is suspected or confirmed, anti‐parasitic therapy is the cornerstone of treatment. The drug of choice is Triclabendazole, a benzimidazole derivative highly effective against both immature and adult fasciola species. Triclabendazole is typically administered at a dose of 10 mg/kg as a single dose or divided into two doses 12 h apart [11, 12, 13]. A repeat dose may be required in refractory cases. Its mechanism of action involves inhibition of microtubule assembly and disruption of parasite energy metabolism. It's generally well tolerated, with mild gastrointestinal side effects [13].

This case report has several limitations, as we were unable to obtain serologic and parasitological confirmation (e.g., identification of Fasciola eggs in stool or bile). Additionally, serologic testing, which is a valuable non‐invasive diagnostic tool, was not available in our setting. These limitations were primarily due to constraints in diagnostic resources, including a lack of access to advanced serologic assays and endoscopic procedures such as ERCP.

Despite the absence of definitive confirmation, the diagnosis was strongly supported by the clinical presentation, striking peripheral eosinophilia, and imaging findings highly suggestive of hepatic parasitic infection. The patient's residence in an endemic area and her rapid clinical and hematologic response to triclabendazole provided additional support for the diagnosis.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges clinicians face in resource‐limited settings, where empirical clinical judgment must often guide decision‐making in the absence of confirmatory laboratory tools. It also illustrates two important clinical lessons: first, that fascioliasis should remain a differential consideration in hepatic lesions with eosinophilia, even in the absence of a classic exposure history; and second, that vascular complications such as portal vein thrombosis (PVT), while rare, may serve as an early radiologic clue for considering fascioliasis. Early recognition and management of such complications are essential to improving outcomes and avoiding unnecessary surgical intervention.
