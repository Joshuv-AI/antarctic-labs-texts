Wavelength-scale noise-resistant on-chip spectrometer
Authors: Jianbo Yu, Hsuan Lo, Wenduo Chen, Changyan Zhu, Yujin Wu, Fakun Wang, Chongwu Wang, Congliao Yan, Cuong Dang, Bihan Wen, Hui Cao, Yidong Chong, Qi Jie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Performant on-chip spectrometers are important for advancing sensing technologies, from environmental monitoring to biomedical diagnostics. As device footprints approach the scale of the operating wavelength, previous strategies, including those relying on multiple scattering in diffusive media, face fundamental accuracy constraints due to limited optical path lengths and noise-sensitive random transmission matrices. Here, we demonstrate a wavelength-scale, CMOS-compatible on-chip spectrometer that overcomes the challenges by exploiting inverse-designed quasinormal modes in a complex photonic resonator. These modes extend the effective optical path length beyond the physical device dimensions, producing highly decorrelated spectral responses. We show that this strategy is theoretically optimal for minimizing spectral reconstruction error in the presence of noise. The fabricated spectrometer occupies a lateral footprint of only 3.5 times the free-space operating wavelength, with a spectral resolution of 10 nm across the 3590-3760 nm mid-infrared band, which is suitable for various molecular sensing. The design of this miniaturized noise-resistant spectrometer is readily extensible to other portions of the electromagnetic spectrum, paving the way for lab-on-a-chip devices, chemical sensors, and other applications.

Introduction

Optical spectrometry is essential to all scientific fields that touch upon the interactions of light and matter1, and in recent years, the growing demand for compact integrated devices has driven the development of highly miniaturized spectrometers2–12. Spectrometers based on filtering13,14, dispersive components15–17, and Fourier transform interferometry18,19 have been integrated into on-chip platforms, reducing the footprint required for spectrometry to several hundred operating wavelengths. A recent breakthrough has come from so-called reconstructive spectrometers20–22, in which input spectral information is encoded in the intensities of space- or time-division output channels via a spectral transmission matrix, and retrieved with the help of reconstruction algorithms. Reconstructive spectrometers are able to resolve a broad set of spectra with only a few sampling channels23,24, offering significant advantages in terms of compactness, application specificity, and cost-effectiveness.

An ideal reconstructive spectrometer should capture as much spectral information as possible during the encoding process, as well as being resistant to noise during reconstruction. A common strategy for effective spectral encoding is to reduce the correlation of the spectral transmission matrix by raising the in-device optical path length through multiple scattering and interference in disordered media20. With strong scattering (which shortens \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{l}}}}_{t}$$\end{document}lt), well-functioning disordered spectrometers have been realized with L as small as tens of operating wavelengths, and mean optical path lengths substantially exceeding L21,25,26. This approach begins to fail, however, as the device size decreases toward the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L\sim {{{\mathcal{l}}}}_{t}$$\end{document}L~lt regime, where no path-length enhancement occurs. Moreover, the inherently random transmission matrices in such systems make the reconstruction sensitive to noise, further limiting accuracy. An alternative strategy is to exploit high-Q quasinormal modes, whose effective optical path lengths can exceed the mean values predicted by diffusive scattering theory27. Yet, there are many open questions about this approach, including how best to engineer such quasinormal modes in a compact device, and what properties to optimize for the purposes of robust spectral reconstruction.

In this work, we demonstrate a reconstructive spectrometer that operates robustly at a wavelength-scale footprint. The device’s design is guided by a rigorous derivation of the optimal spectral transmission matrix that minimizes the reconstruction mean squared error (MSE) in the presence of noise, accompanied by the use of inverse design to engineer a photonic microcavity with quasinormal modes yielding a spectral transmission matrix of the desired form. Unlike approaches relying on optical diffusion, which is subject to the aforementioned scaling limits, we aim to work with special modes that are outcoupled to only one or a few output ports and possess high effective optical path lengths, allowing for robust spectral reconstruction even in a cavity on the order of a few wavelengths. As a proof of concept, we realize an on-chip spectrometer with a lateral footprint of only 3.5 times the free-space operating wavelength, an order of magnitude smaller than previous reported on-chip spectrometers. Our device achieves a spectral resolution of 10 nm over the 3590–3760 nm mid-infrared range, enabling the detection of molecules with mid-infrared absorption peaks such as formaldehyde and methane28. These results are obtained at a relatively high noise level of 7.9% in the output intensity, which is promising for device integration with low-power on-chip light sources. This work establishes a design paradigm for ultracompact, CMOS-compatible spectrometers, which can serve as the foundation for next-generation integrated photonic sensing platforms.

In a typical photonic structure containing random scatterers, each mode couples to many output channels, resulting in an unstructured transmission matrix (Fig. 1, middle panel). For this case, the FOM defined in Eq. (3) is not close to its maximum, and the spectral reconstruction is relatively sensitive to measurement noise. However, after optimizing the structure using the above FOM, the transmission matrix will acquire a banded structure, which is predicted to give more robust and accurate spectral reconstruction (Fig. 1, bottom panel). The optimized transmission matrix is non-square, with its populated elements clustered along a diagonal-like band stretching between two corners of the matrix. Physically, this means that the input signal at each frequency is directed to one or a small number of adjacent output ports, distributed evenly over the operating frequency range. On-chip spectrometer design

As proof of concept for the above theory, we use inverse design to develop an on-chip spectrometer featuring the optimized banded transmission matrix described above. The spectrometer utilizes a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) platform and features a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$13\,\mu {m}\times \,13\,\mu m$$\end{document}13μm×13μm inverse designed scattering region, along with one input and 15 output waveguides. Each waveguide is 0.5 μm in width and 1.5 μm in height, and supports a single TM mode across the wavelength range of 3600–3820 nm. This range is divided into 85 equally spaced discrete wavelength channels, by which the spectral transmission matrix is defined. (The number of wavelength channels is chosen by balancing spectral reconstruction fidelity against the computational cost of inverse design; we verified that as the number of wavelength channels varies from 55 to 100, the optimal transmission matrix retains its characteristic banded structure, as shown in the Supplementary Information.) An adjoint-method inverse design procedure30,31 is used to determine the permittivity distribution in the scattering region so that the transmission matrix closely matches the target one. The functionality of the designed spectrometer is validated through FDTD simulations, with results shown in Fig. 2b. The upper panel displays the field distributions under monochromatic input at a few representative wavelengths. For comparison, some corresponding cavity eigenmodes are presented in the lower panel. The cavity supports around 40 eigenmodes within the target wavelength range, with Q-factors ranging from several tens to several hundred (see Supplementary Information). Through inverse design, certain of these high-Q eigenmodes outcouple selectively to one or a few output ports (as for the other eigenmodes, they couple to many output ports or decay by side scattering). As a result, each output port exhibits a Lorentzian-like transmission profile (Fig. 2c). When exciting the system at a wavelength between two adjacent transmission peaks, we observe the output shifting smoothly from one port to another, as shown in the middle plot in the upper panel of Fig. 2b. The inter-channel crosstalk observed here plays a crucial role in the optimization, enabling simultaneous transmittance maximization and correlation minimization as guided by our FOM; this differs notably from conventional filters or demultiplexers, where crosstalk is generally treated as an undesirable design defect (see Supplementary Information). The transmission spectrum for each channel has a quality factor of 200. These high-Q quasinormal modes with extended lifetimes increase the effective optical path length to more than three times that of the random spectrometers (see Supplementary Information).

To evaluate the distinctiveness of this spectrometer’s transmission matrix, we compare its FOM with those of 5000 random structures. These random structures have the same footprint and input/output configuration as the designed spectrometer, except that the scattering region contains randomly distributed air holes (see Supplementary Information). The distribution of their FOMs is shown in Fig. 2d. The FOM for our design is 0.12, somewhat lower than the theoretical maximum of 0.38 but two orders of magnitude above the highest FOM among the 5000 structures in the random sample.

We then use our design, along with the random spectrometer with the highest FOM, to reconstruct 10,000 Gaussian spectra with randomly selected bandwidths ranging from 40–200 nm and center wavelengths between 3600 and 3820 nm. The spectra are numerically input to the respective transmission matrices, and Gaussian noise is added to the output channels, with noise levels quantified by the standard deviation σσ. The mean reconstruction MSE across 10,000 spectra at various noise levels from 0 to 20% is shown in Fig. 2e. For both spectrometers, the mean MSE increases with increasing noise levels. However, the mean MSE of our inverse-designed spectrometer is more than one order of magnitude lower than that of the optimal random spectrometer across all noise levels, providing a clear indicator of superior robustness against noise.

Spectral reconstruction

We now employ the fabricated spectrometer to reconstruct various types of spectra. Before tackling these underdetermined inverse problems, we observe that our theoretical analysis has been based entirely on pseudo-inverse reconstruction, which essentially imposes a minimum L2-norm constraint without incorporating any structural priors such as sparsity or smoothness. When structural prior information about the spectrum is available (e.g., the spectrum is broadband and smooth), reconstruction accuracy can be further improved by incorporating appropriate regularization terms. Specifically, it is appropriate to use L1 regularization for sparse spectra, L2 regularization for broadband spectra, and a derivative-based term to enforce spectral smoothness. It is also worth noting that even though regularization can suppress noise during spectral reconstruction, it is distinct from and cannot substitute for the transmission matrix optimizations we have discussed. Regularization is applied post-measurement by constraining the solution space, which enhances robustness by compromising spectral fidelity and diversity. In contrast, optimizing the transmission matrix improves data quality at the measurement stage, which enables accurate reconstruction with weaker regularization and thereby better preserves the fidelity and diversity of the reconstructed spectra.

Discussion

We have proposed and experimentally demonstrated a wavelength-scale on-chip spectrometer based on a silicon nanophotonic platform, achieving exceptional robustness to experimental noise alongside ultra-compact integration. Our theoretically-guided inverse design methodology generates compact optical structures hosting long-lived quasinormal modes with outcoupling characteristics that are optimal for spectral reconstruction. By working in this regime, we bypass the limitations of previous reconstructive spectrometers based on disordered cavities20, chaotic cavities42, multimode waveguides43, waveguide meshes36, etc., whereby the enhancement of the transport mean free path degrades as the device size approaches the wavelength scale. In future work, it would be interesting to explore whether a similar inverse design approach could be applicable to spectrometers based on tunable two-dimensional41 or superconducting materials44.

Our device has achieved a spectral resolution of 10 nm across the 3590–3760 nm wavelength range, enabling the detection of numerous molecular fingerprints in the mid-infrared region. The compact lateral footprint is only 3.5 times the operating wavelength, an order of magnitude smaller than previous reported on-chip spectrometers. The device also performs well under high noise conditions (7.9% intensity noise in our experiments), indicating its suitability for integration into chips with low-power light sources. The spectral resolution can be further improved by increasing the number of detection channels. For example, utilizing the thermal-optical effect of silicon45, the transmission matrix can be dynamically tuned by adjusting the temperature of the device, allowing for greater device flexibility and precision in spectral reconstruction. Additionally, considering the wide transparent window of silicon, the operation wavelength range of the spectrometer could be extended from 1.1 μm to 8 μm, covering wide near-infrared and mid-infrared regions, by removing the buried oxide layer to minimize the absorption loss46.

The proposed design strategy is broadly applicable and can be extended to a variety of material platforms, including aluminum nitride, silicon nitride, lithium niobate, chalcogenide glasses, germanium, high-resistivity silicon, and polymers, enabling spectral coverage from the ultraviolet to the Terahertz. Fully on-chip spectral detection may also be achievable by monolithically integrating laser sources and photodetectors with the spectrometer, allowing both calibration and spectral measurements to be performed by reading the photocurrent and processing it using an on-chip or external processor. Such devices hold strong promise for a wide range of applications in chemical analysis, biosensing, material science, environmental monitoring, and lab-on-chip photonics.

We note that a theoretical work on noise-robust spectral reconstruction, involving similar ideas, was posted while this manuscript was under preparation47.

Methods

Sample fabrication: A 400-nm-thick resist (ZEP 520 A) was spin-coated on the silicon-on-insulator substrate and baked for 2 min at 180 °C. The spectrometer pattern was obtained by electron-beam lithography (EBL). The development was performed in the developer (ZED N50) for 2 min followed by rinsing in isopropanol for 40 s. After that, the pattern was etched using deep reactive ion etching, with the remaining photoresist removed by oxygen plasma ashing.

Optical characterization

A Mid-IR tunable laser was used for characterizing the transmission matrix of the spectrometer. The output intensity was recorded by an IR camera (FLIR A6703) with the detection wavelength range of 3–5 μm.

Simulations

The transmission spectrum and the electric field distribution were calculated using the software Lumerical FDTD.
