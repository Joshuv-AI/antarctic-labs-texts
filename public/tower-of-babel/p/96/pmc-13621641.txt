Associations between government-supported institutional long-term care and health outcomes among socioeconomically disadvantaged older adults in China
Authors: Qiufeng Gao, Yuxin Jiang, Xintong Wang, Bei Zhou, Yaojiang Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough Long-Term Care (LTC) benefits aging populations, evidence on government-supported LTC for socioeconomically disadvantaged older adults in low- and middle-income countries remains limited. This study examines how government-supported institutional LTC is associated with physical and mental health outcomes among welfare-dependent older adults in rural China.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted among 313 welfare recipients aged 60 and above in Shaanxi Province. Health outcomes were assessed using validated measures of physical health (self-rated health, pain, functional limitations) and mental health (depression, anxiety, loneliness). Regression models and propensity score matching were used to examine associations, and potential mechanisms were further explored.ResultsGovernment-supported institutional LTC was significantly associated with better physical and mental health, particularly higher self-rated health (ATT = 0.177, p = 0.005; β = 0.079, p = 0.006) and lower anxiety (ATT=-0.288, p < 0.001; β=-0.146, p = 0.016). Longer stays were associated with more favorable health patterns. Mechanism analyses suggested that these associations may relate to age-friendly environments, improved healthcare access, enhanced social participation, and better nutritional conditions. Heterogeneity analyses indicated the associations were stronger among older, illiterate individuals and those with family guardians, particularly male guardians.ConclusionsThis study provides robust empirical evidence of associations between government-supported institutional LTC and better health outcomes among socioeconomically disadvantaged older adults, highlighting its potential role in enhancing well-being in rural settings. These findings emphasize the importance of expanding equitable, integrated, and high-quality LTC services to support sustainable, person-centered aging care for vulnerable populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-02940-w.Keywords: Formal care, Health outcomes, Vulnerable populations, Publicly funded intervention

Background

The rapid growth of the aging population has placed significant strain on healthcare systems globally, especially for Socioeconomically Disadvantaged Older Adults (SDOAs) in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), such as China. By 2050, 80% of the world’s older adults will reside in LMICs, where limited healthcare and social infrastructure—combined with socioeconomic disadvantages—continue to exacerbate health disparities [1, 2]. In China, which is home to nearly one-fifth of the global aging population, this challenge is particularly acute. In 2018, approximately 17.75 million older adults reported limitations in activities of daily living, a figure expected to more than double by 2050 [3]. Rural SDOAs exhibit even poorer health outcomes, including lower hypertension treatment rates, higher disability prevalence among the illiterate, and more severe depressive symptoms—especially among women and individuals with limited education [4, 5].

In response to demographic shifts, the demand for formal Long-Term Care (LTC) services has increased rapidly, particularly in rural China, where traditional family-based caregiving has weakened due to the one-child policy, urbanization, and substantial labor mobility [6]. As informal support declines, older adults with limited caregiving alternatives increasingly rely on formal services, exposing systemic inefficiencies such as hospital bed shortages and rising medical costs [4]. Although institutional LTC is a cornerstone of aging care in high-income countries [7], its development in China has lagged behind—especially relative to home- and community-based care—leaving rural older adults with limited and often inadequate support [6]. To address these disparities, the government has introduced fully funded formal LTC programs, with resource-intensive institutional care (i.e., social welfare facilities) targeting vulnerable groups such as childless individuals, those with disabilities, and low-income groups [8].

While substantial evidence suggests that LTC interventions can reduce healthcare utilization, lower medical costs, and improve health outcomes, most existing studies originate from high-income countries and report mixed findings [9]. Some reviews find no significant impact of institutional LTC on quality of life [10], whereas others document benefits such as improved self-reported health and higher-quality end-of-life care [11, 12]. Evidence comparing institutional and home-based care is similarly inconclusive across a range of outcomes including physical function, mortality risk, happiness, and healthcare utilization [13, 14]. Concerns have also been raised regarding the potential for institutional care to disrupt daily routines and reduce autonomy, particularly in low-quality settings [15]. In LMICs, these uncertainties are compounded by systemic constraints—including insufficient funding, workforce shortages, and fragmented service delivery—raising questions about whether institutional LTC can effectively meet the needs of disadvantaged older adults [16].

In China, evaluations of government-supported LTC remain limited, with little evidence focusing specifically on SDOAs in rural areas. Existing studies are mixed: some studies report no significant effect of institutional LTC on disease management among cognitively impaired older adults [17], while others highlight potential benefits including substitution of informal care, improved cognitive function, enhanced life satisfaction, and reduced depressive symptoms [18, 19]. Rural regions face constraints—particularly cultural expectations related to filial piety and the limited availability of informal support networks—which disproportionately affect SDOAs who rely heavily on under-resourced public services [2]. Moreover, government-supported LTC institutions in rural areas often provide only basic services, raising concerns about their capacity to meet diverse and complex needs [6]. Despite ongoing policy efforts, the impact of institutional LTC on the health outcomes of rural SDOAs remains unclear, underscoring the need for targeted empirical research.

Using data from Shaanxi Province in northwest China, this study draws on the Social Determinants of Health (SDoH) framework to examine the associations between government-supported institutional LTC and the health outcomes of SDOAs in rural areas, as well as the potential pathways through which LTC may influence health outcomes [20]. Specifically, the study has three objectives. First, we examine associations between the use of public nursing facilities (i.e., Jing-lao-yuan) and multiple physical and mental health outcomes, using regression models and Propensity Score Matching (PSM). Second, we explore potential pathways underlying these associations, focusing on age-friendly environments, healthcare accessibility, social participation, and nutrition. Third, we assess heterogeneity in these associations across subgroups defined by age, education levels, and guardian types.

Methods

Sampling and data collection

This cross-sectional survey was conducted from May to June 2024 in a county in Shaanxi Province, northwest China, which was formerly designated as a poverty-stricken county. The survey targeted older individuals aged 60 and above from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds, including those in the “three-no” group (wubaohu—no ability to work, no income, and no family support) and individuals from low-income households, all of whom were eligible for government-supported nursing homes at low or no cost. Detailed sampling procedures and participant selection criteria were provided in the Supplemental Text (Additional file 1).

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software to determine the minimum required sample size. Assuming a t-test with a medium effect size, a significance level of 0.05, statistical power of 0.80 [21], and an allocation ratio of 1:2 between residents of institutional LTC residents and home-based individuals, a minimum of 144 participants (48 institutional residents and 96 home-based individuals) was required. Given the planned village-level clustering adjustment, we aimed to recruit at least 200 participants. Ultimately, 313 individuals were enrolled, including 107 residents from all public nursing facilities and 206 home-based individuals from eligible villages, ensuring adequate statistical power.

Measurements

The empirical analysis was guided by the SDoH framework, which organized variables into individual, environmental, resource-related, and behavioral domains relevant to health outcomes in later life. Specifically, we conceptualized a framework in which institutional LTC use was associated with health outcomes, conditional on baseline characteristics, and was potentially linked through multiple intermediate domains.

Within this framework, selected theoretical perspectives were used to inform the operationalization of key domains. The ecological theory of aging, the behavioral model for vulnerable populations, and activity theory guided the measurement of environmental conditions, resource accessibility, and behavioral engagement, respectively [22–24]. Individual characteristics, family characteristics, and health behaviors were treated as confounders, as they may jointly influence both the likelihood of receiving institutional LTC and health outcomes, consistent with prior literature [25, 26]. Accordingly, variables were grouped into the independent variable, dependent variables, covariates, and mechanism variables. The independent variable captured the use of public nursing facilities (i.e., Jing-lao-yuan), and dependent variables measured physical and mental health. Covariates included individual characteristics, family characteristics, and health behaviors. Mechanism variables included age-friendly environments, healthcare accessibility, social participation, and nutrition. These mechanism variables are not modeled as formal mediators, but rather were used to explore potential pathways underlying the observed associations.

Independent variables

The independent variable measured the provision of government-supported LTC. Institutional LTC was available in public nursing facilities, whereas home-based individuals had limited or no access to formal care due to the underdevelopment of home- and community-based LTC services in rural China, as documented in our investigation and previous studies [6]. This distinction between institutional and non-institutional care enables a comparison of differences in health outcomes across care settings among older adults in rural areas. In addition to the binary indicator of institutional LTC use, we further measured the duration of institutional stay, defined as the self-reported cumulative number of months of the older adult had lived in the government-supported LTC facility prior to the survey; when available, these reports were verified with administrative records.

Responses ranged from “very good” to “very poor” and were dichotomized into healthy (“very good” or “good”) and unhealthy (“moderate”, “poor”, and “very poor”).

Covariates

Control variables are defined within the individual-level domain of the SDoH framework. These included individual characteristics, family characteristics, and health behaviors. Individual characteristics comprised age, gender (1 = male), years of schooling, disability status (1 = yes), and chronic disease status (1 = yes). Family characteristics included marital status (married, single, and widowed/divorced), number of children, and household savings. Health behaviors covered participation in the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme (NRCMS) (1 = yes), and unhealthy behaviors (2 = both smoking and alcohol consumption, 1 = either, 0 = none). These variables are included as confounders, as they may jointly influence both long-term care utilization and health outcomes.

Age-friendly environments. Assessed using a composite score from the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey, based on eight housing indicators (e.g., bathrooms, running water, natural gas) and categorized into accessibility, sanitation facilities, and residential quality. Activity space types (e.g., reading room, outdoor areas) were also evaluated for their support of older adults’ mobility and daily activities.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18.0 with a significance threshold of 0.05. Continuous variables were summarized as means ± standard deviations (SD) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. For the main analysis, all health outcomes measured as categorical variables were analyzed using logit regression with village-level clustered standard errors to examine their associations with government-supported institutional LTC. To strengthen internal validity and reduce potential selection bias arising from non-random LTC participation, PSM with normal kernel density estimation was employed to estimate the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT), though it remains unable to account for unobserved confounders. As robustness checks, categorical outcomes were replaced with their corresponding continuous measures, and OLS regressions were applied to evaluate the consistency of association patterns across model specifications. To minimize recall bias in the reporting on the duration of institutional stay, standardized interview wording was used, and responses were cross-validated with facility records when possible. Mechanism and heterogeneity analyses followed the same model-selection principles, applying logit models for categorical outcomes and OLS models for continuous outcomes. Heterogeneity analyses were conducted by including interaction terms between institutional LTC and subgroup indicators.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Table 1 summarizes descriptive statistics for the unmatched sample (N = 313) and the matched sample (N = 305) by care settings. In the unmatched sample, the overall mean age was 71.5 years (SD = 7.0), and 83.4% of participants were male. Individuals in institutional LTC were slightly older (72.4 vs. 71.0 years; %bias = 19.0) and more likely to be male (95.3% vs. 77.2%; %bias = 54.5). After matching, group differences were substantially reduced (age: %bias = 7.7; gender: %bias = 0.1). Approximately 80.2% of participants had not completed primary school, nearly 20.0% were disabled, and 69.0% had at least one chronic disease, with similar distributions across groups. Marriage rates were low (26.8%), and although institutional LTC residents were less likely to be married, matching effectively reduced this imbalance. A large difference in the number of children (0.6 vs. 1.6; %bias = -64.9) was also substantially minimized after matching (%bias = -3.2). Participation in NRCMS was high (> 92.0% in both groups), and unhealthy behaviors showed minimal variation between groups. Overall, PSM effectively reduced standardized mean differences, generating well-balanced comparison groups.

The impact of government-supported institutional LTC on health outcomes

Main results

Table 2 displays the estimated associations between institutional LTC and health outcomes across three complementary approaches: (1) logit regression in the full sample, (2) PSM-based ATT estimates, and (3) logit regression applied to the matched sample. After adjusting for covariates and accounting for village-level clustering, institutional LTC was consistently associated with better physical health. Across all models, institutional LTC was significantly associated with improved SRH (β = 0.881, p = 0.001; ATT = 0.177, p = 0.005; β = 0.895, p < 0.001). Associations with pain and functional limitations did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05), although a consistent pattern suggested lower reported pain but a higher likelihood of functional limitations among institutionalized SDOAs.

Institutional LTC was also associated with better mental health, particularly reduced anxiety symptoms. After adjusting for covariates, institutional LTC was significantly associated with lower anxiety in both logit regression (β = -1.251, p = 0.047) and PSM models (ATT = -0.288, p < 0.001). Associations with depression and loneliness were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), although trends indicated slightly lower loneliness and a slight tendency toward higher depression symptoms among institutional LTC recipients. The baseline results capture average associations of institutional LTC, while potential heterogeneity across subpopulations is examined in subsequent analyses.

Robustness analyses

To assess the robustness of our estimates with respect to the matching procedure, we additionally implemented alternative propensity score matching algorithms, including radius matching and k-nearest neighbor (KNN) matching. The results, reported in Table A2, remain consistent in terms of direction, magnitude, and statistical significance.

To assess robustness further, we replaced categorical health outcomes with their continuous counterparts and re-estimated associations using OLS regression (Table A3). The positive association with SRH remained consistent, and pain levels were significantly associated with institutional LTC (β = -0.464, p = 0.007; β = -0.454, p = 0.009), suggesting a potential association between institutional LTC and lower pain levels. Associations with ADL or IADL counts remained non-significant (p > 0.05), although negative coefficients suggested a potential reduction in functional limitations. Mental health outcomes were also robust, reinforcing the observed associations between institutional LTC and reduced anxiety and loneliness.

Association with duration of institutional LTC

Table 3 presents the estimated associations between the duration of institutional LTC and health outcomes, using logit regression. Given the substantial variation in residents’ length of stay—from a few months to more than 20 years—the analysis examined whether longer durations of residence within institutional LTC were cross-sectionally associated with differences in health status. After adjusting for covariates, longer institutional LTC duration was significantly associated with better SRH (β = 0.079, p = 0.006) and lower anxiety symptoms (β = -0.146, p = 0.016). Although associations with pain, functional limitations, depression, and loneliness did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05), the patterns suggested that longer stays were related to fewer reports of pain and loneliness, alongside a higher likelihood of reporting functional limitations and depression symptoms.

Mechanism analysis

Table 4 reports the association between government-supported institutional LTC and four mechanism dimensions—age-friendly environments, healthcare accessibility, social participation, and nutrition. For age-friendly environments (Panel A), institutional LTC was significantly associated with improvements in overall age-friendly facilities (p < 0.001), with marked increases in accessibility and sanitation facilities (p < 0.001), although no significant change was observed in residential quality (p = 0.213). Moreover, the availability of activity spaces increased significantly (p < 0.001). In the realm of healthcare accessibility (Panel B), institutional LTC significantly increased the availability of diverse care services (p < 0.001)—including lifestyle guidance, medical visits, and personalized care—while improving health service accessibility (p = 0.002) and access to both medical and mental health information (p = 0.007).

Heterogeneity analysis

To examine potential variations in the associations between government-supported institutional LTC and health outcomes across different subgroups of SDOAs, we conducted heterogeneity analyses based on age, education level, and guardian characteristics by estimating interaction models between institutional LTC and subgroup indicators. The results are shown in Fig. 1. Figure 1A showed that the associations between institutional LTC and several health indicators differed by age. Among older SDOAs, institutional LTC was associated with a lower likelihood of reporting pain (p < 0.05), depression (p < 0.05), and loneliness (p < 0.01), as well as fewer functional limitations (p < 0.001), compared with non-institutional LTC. Figure 1B indicated heterogeneity by education level. Among SDOAs receiving institutional LTC, those with at least a primary education were more likely to report anxiety (p < 0.001), whereas the associations with pain and loneliness were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). These findings suggest that higher educational attainment was not consistently associated with better adaptation to institutional LTC.

Discussion

This study addresses a critical gap in the literature by providing empirical evidence of associations between government-supported institutional LTC and the physical and mental health of SDOAs in rural China. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive evaluation of formal LTC among this vulnerable population. By employing both regression and PSM models, we examined the associations between institutional LTC and health outcomes, while also exploring underlying mechanisms and heterogeneity across subgroups defined by individual and guardian characteristics.

Sample characteristics

SDOAs consistently exhibited markedly poorer health outcomes than the general older population, highlighting the urgent need for tailored LTC support. Compared to national-level data, SDOAs reported substantially lower SRH (66.8% vs. 54.9%) [32], higher prevalence of pain (80.5% vs. 44.1%) [33], and more functional limitations (56.6% vs. 37.5%) [16]. These disparities reduce their capacity for self-management and increase dependence on government-supported LTC services. Regarding mental health, although depression prevalence was comparable to national estimates [34], levels of loneliness were nearly 1.8 times higher and anxiety symptoms were almost five times more prevalent [33, 34]. These findings underscore the severe psychological vulnerabilities facing SDOAs and reinforce the strong link between socioeconomic disadvantage and poor mental well-being. The challenges observed in this population appear to arise from an interplay between physical and psychological factors [35], where poor physical health exacerbates psychological distress, while economic constraints and low health literacy limit access to essential preventive and medical care [36]. These intersecting disadvantages contribute to persistent health inequities across the life course.

Associations suggestive of causal relationships

Our findings align with emerging evidence that government-supported institutional LTC is associated with better health outcomes for older adults in resource-limited settings, with longer residence showing more favorable patterns [11, 37]. Similar programs serving financially disadvantaged older adults in the United States have demonstrated improvements in SRH and functional outcomes [37]. We also observed reduced anxiety symptoms among institutional LTC residents, suggesting that formal care may alleviate psychological distress relative to informal care arrangements. SDOAs often face multiple barriers to adequate care—including limited informal support, low health literacy, and financial constraints [2]. In response, China has expanded government-supported institutional LTC since 2006 to provide stable housing, daily assistance, and basic medical care for vulnerable older adults [38]. Although many residents enter LTC with pre-existing conditions [39], access to trained staff and assisted living services may help maintain or improve SRH and pain outcomes, particularly for individuals lacking family support.

Despite these advantages, government-supported institutional LTC was not significantly associated with improvements in functional limitations or depression symptoms. Prior research shows that functional capacity may continue to decline over time even in structured formal care settings, as reflected in decreasing ADL scores over time [40]. Targeted rehabilitation programs may therefore be needed to preserve functional independence. Similarly, transitioning into institutional LTC may heighten the risk of depression symptoms, especially without care to mental health interventions [41]. Movement restrictions in the sampled public nursing facilities—implemented to ensure safety—may further contribute to negative mood states. A meta-analysis of neurobiological and non-pharmacological interventions reported largely non-significant associations with depression, illustrating the complexity of treating mood disorders in LTC settings [42]. Nevertheless, collaborative care models that reduce stigma and enhance accessibility show promise in addressing depression among vulnerable older populations [43]. At the same time, the non-significant overall associations for functional limitations and depression may reflect heterogeneity across subgroups, as indicated by the interaction analyses.

Mechanism

Drawing on theoretical frameworks and our empirical findings, this study identified four key pathways through which government-supported institutional LTC may have enhanced the well-being of SDOAs: age-friendly environments, improved healthcare accessibility, stronger social participation, and better nutritional support. Each mechanism reflected a distinct dimension along which institutional settings help reduce vulnerability and supported physical and psychological resilience. First, the age-friendly environment emerged as an important pathway supporting physical well-being. Consistent with the Ecological Theory of Aging [22], institutional LTC facilities reduce the mismatch between environmental demands and personal competence [44]. Regression results showed that residents experienced significantly fewer environmental constraints and had access to more activity spaces than home-based peers. These findings suggested that institutional LTC minimized environmental stressors—such as mobility barriers and physical strain—and promoted greater independence in daily activities [45].

Second, healthcare accessibility constituted a central mechanism. According to the Behavior Model for Vulnerable Populations [23], healthcare use depends on predisposing, enabling, and need-based factors. Our analyses indicated that institutional LTC residents had access to a broader range of care services and more medical and mental health information. These patterns suggest that institutional LTC integrated professional care, routine assessments, and health education, enhancing residents’ health literacy and facilitating appropriate healthcare use and chronic disease management [46, 47]. Supported by targeted policies for vulnerable populations, such integration may have contributed to improved SRH and reduced functional limitations [48].

Third, social participation functioned as a key psychosocial mechanism by mitigating mental health risks associated with isolation. In line with Activity Theory [24], opportunities for engagement in social and cognitive activities supported psychological well-being. Empirical results showed that institutional residents participated in more recreational and group activities than home-based individuals, reflecting stronger social integration. Such structured interactions likely strengthened emotional resilience and a sense of belonging [49, 50], which corresponded with lower psychological distress and higher life satisfaction among institutionalized older adults [51].

Fourth, nutritional well-being played a critical role as a physiological pathway influencing both physical resilience and emotional stability. Regression analyses revealed that institutional LTC residents had healthier BMI levels and more diverse diets, reflecting the benefits of balanced diets and routine nutritional monitoring [52]. Adequate nutrition not only supports neurochemical stability [53], but also reduces food insecurity and irregular eating patterns, helping maintain consistent energy levels essential for mobility and cognitive function [54]. Given that malnutrition is a major contributor to morbidity in older adults, the structured nutritional programs in institutional LTC were essential for preventing long-term health risks.

Collectively, these mechanisms suggested that institutional LTC functioned not merely as a custodial environment but as an integrated system addressing environmental, medical, social, and nutritional needs, thereby enhancing both physical and psychological resilience among SDOAs.

Heterogeneity

Subgroup analyses revealed significant heterogeneity in the associations between institutional LTC and health outcomes. SDOAs who were older or had limited literacy appeared to benefit more from the structured support available in LTC facilities. For these individuals, institutional LTC may provide essential medical monitoring, mobility assistance, and daily living support [39]—services particularly relevant for those with advanced age and low educational attainment. Given that low education is linked with long-term economic disadvantage [55] and a higher risk of poverty [56], these individuals often lack the resources to manage their health independently. In this context, institutional LTC may serve a compensatory role by offering integrated disease management and targeted health education, thereby improving both physical and mental well-being.

Family guardianship was associated with improved physical outcomes, including reduced pain, potentially through more continuous and individualized care that facilitates preventive monitoring, timely health management, and a more secure living environment [57, 58]. However, in the context of strong filial piety norms, intensive family caregiving may also be linked to increased psychological burden and reduced self-efficacy and autonomy, leading older adults to withdraw from activities they are capable of performing and thereby experiencing higher levels of anxiety [59]. In rural China, where traditional gender norms shape caregiving expectations, male guardians (typically sons or close male relatives) were often responsible for providing practical support [60], whereas female relatives may offer more emotional support [61]. Our findings suggest that male guardianship was associated with reduced pain and fewer functional limitations, while female guardians were linked to lower loneliness. Moreover, for SDOAs, the involvement of any family guardian—regardless of gender—may function as a supportive factor by facilitating care coordination within LTC settings [62]. Finally, although stronger guardian-recipient relationships were correlated with better emotional well-being [63], their association with the health benefits of formal care appeared less pronounced than that of age, education, and guardianship status.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size (313 older individuals), restricted geographic coverage, and imbalanced gender composition limit the generalizability of the findings beyond the study setting, and caution is warranted when extending the results to other socioeconomically disadvantaged older adult populations in different contexts. Second, although analytical strategies such as PSM were applied to reduce selection bias based on observable characteristics, unobserved confounders may still bias the estimated associations, and the cross-sectional design does not allow for establishing temporal precedence or causal interpretation. Third, the study did not capture variations in institutional care quality, which may meaningfully affect health outcomes. Fourth, despite standardized interviewer training, several self-reported measures were subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially affecting precision. Future studies should incorporate objective indicators and multi-source data verification to address these issues.

Despite these limitations, this study makes several important contributions. It provides robust evidence on the associations between government-supported institutional LTC and health outcomes among SDOAs—a group often underrepresented in the literature. By examining duration of institutional stay, the study shed light on how longer residence may shape health outcomes. In addition, by unpacking four key mechanisms—age-friendly environments, healthcare accessibility, social participation, and nutrition—the study deepens our understanding of how institutional LTC may reduce vulnerability and promote well-being.

Building on our findings, LTC represents a potentially effective policy approach for improving the health of SDOAs in rural settings. Evidence from LMICs suggests that integrating basic healthcare services with social and community-based care can improve physical and psychological outcomes. For example, community-based LTC programs in Thailand and emerging evidence from long-term care settings in sub-Saharan Africa demonstrate the feasibility and potential benefits of LTC interventions in resource-limited settings [64, 65]. First, consistent with the role of environmental conditions identified in our analysis, governments should prioritize improving the physical environment of LTC facilities and surrounding community spaces. Enhancing accessibility and safety—such as through age-friendly infrastructure and barrier-free design—can reduce environmental stressors affecting vulnerable older populations [66, 67]. Second, given the importance of healthcare accessibility, policymakers should strengthen the integration of healthcare services within LTC institutions by incorporating routine health monitoring, medication management, and preventive assessments. These measures are essential for improving chronic disease management in under-resourced rural settings [6, 18]. Third, in line with the observed benefits of social participation, LTC programs should expand structured social and recreational activities and strengthen community linkages to support mental health and functional capacity [19, 50]. Fourth, as nutrition represents a key pathway, governments should institutionalize standardized nutritional support systems, including dietary monitoring and balanced meal provision, to improve both physical and psychological well-being [19, 68]. Finally, policymakers should establish comprehensive assessment and monitoring systems to evaluate functional status, mental health, and nutritional risks, thereby enabling more targeted resource allocation. Given that these interventions may yield greater benefits among highly vulnerable groups, priority should be given to older adults with limited family support or higher levels of dependency. Together, these measures point toward the development of a more targeted, integrated, and equitable LTC system.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that government-supported institutional LTC was associated with better physical and mental health among SDOAs in rural China. By providing a stable living environment, accessible healthcare, opportunities for social engagement, and structured nutritional support, institutional LTC helped mitigate multiple dimensions of vulnerability. These findings underscore the importance of expanding equitable, integrated, and person-centered LTC services in rural settings. Strengthening age-friendly environments, medical–LTC coordination, social programs, and structured nutritional management may further enhance the well-being of older adults with limited family and community support.
