Stability and biocompatibility of hBD3 with different chiral configurations and their therapeutic efficacy in periodontitis
Authors: Miaomiao Zhang, Can Ni, Ziqing Fan, Lingjun Li, Min Wang, Yuan Wei, Wenrong Yang, Chao Liu, Fuhua Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryPeriodontitis is characterized by progressive destruction of the periodontal attachment apparatus caused by dental plaque accumulation, which can lead to tooth loosening and permanent loss. Human β-defensin 3 (hBD3) exhibits broad-spectrum antibacterial activity and exerts critical immunomodulatory functions. Modifying amino acid chirality represents a novel strategy for the development of next-generation antibacterial agents. In this study, D-hBD3 and L-hBD3 were synthesized via solid-phase peptide synthesis, with recombinant hBD3 (r-hBD3) as control, and their therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in experimental periodontitis mice. Our findings demonstrated that D-hBD3 exhibited remarkable stability, potent antibacterial activity in vitro, and in vivo therapeutic efficacy comparable to conventional antibiotics, r-hBD3 and L-hBD3. Mechanistically, D-hBD3 regulated immunity by downregulating pro-inflammatory TNF-α, upregulating anti-inflammatory IL-10, promoting intestinal macrophage polarization to M2 phenotype, and selectively inhibiting oral pathogens while preserving intestinal microbiota homeostasis. Collectively, this study provides critical evidence supporting D-hBD3 as a cost-effective therapeutic candidate for periodontitis.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory condition, characterized by the progressive destruction of periodontal supporting tissues. This condition manifests as attachment loss and alveolar bone resorption, accompanied by the formation of periodontal pockets and gingival bleeding, ultimately resulting in tooth loss.1 Currently, clinical treatment primarily depends on mechanical debridement techniques, such as scaling and root planing. However, these methods are associated with challenges, including the potential for incomplete removal of debris. No single therapy for periodontitis has been identified as having a significant advantage over others. Clinical practice indicates that the efficacy of nearly all mechanical periodontal treatments can be enhanced to varying degrees when combined with antibacterial therapies.2,3,4,5 Antimicrobial agents can be administered either locally or systemically to manage periodontitis. Systemic antimicrobial agents offer the advantage of reaching all oral surfaces and fluids effectively. Furthermore, these agents contribute to the eradication of infection by inhibiting periodontal pathogens that infiltrate subepithelial periodontal tissues as well as other extradental sites.6

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) represent a diverse and multifunctional class of small molecules that are derived from the innate immune system. In comparison to traditional antibiotics, these compounds typically exhibit a broader spectrum of activity and a reduced likelihood of inducing drug resistance.7 Defensins and cathelicidins represent the two most significant classes of antimicrobial peptides found in the human body. Antimicrobial peptide LL-37 has been substantiated by research to possess the ability to inhibit the formation of biofilms formed by Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans,8 underscoring its potential application value in the treatment of periodontitis.9 Defensins, a class of cationic antimicrobial peptides characterized by their rich disulfide bond content, are categorized into α, β, and θ types and exhibit potent antibacterial and immunomodulatory properties. Owing to its superior antibacterial spectrum and activity compared with other defensins, human β-defensin 3 (hBD3) has been extensively utilized in antibacterial research.10 hBD3 exhibits antibacterial properties against oral microorganisms, including both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria, as well as Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains, in addition to Candida species.11 Furthermore, hBD3 is involved in the regulation of immune inflammatory responses and the osteogenic differentiation processes of cells.12,13 Research has demonstrated that hBD3 can be stably expressed in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells. The transplantation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell sheets cotransfected with hBD3 and connective tissue growth factor effectively promoted periodontal tissue regeneration in nude mice.14 Nevertheless, several limitations remain regarding its clinical application, including high manufacturing costs, unknown systemic toxicity, susceptibility to proteolytic enzymes, and a short half-life in vivo. Molecular chirality significantly influences intermolecular interactions and the supramolecular self-assembly of peptides.15 The interaction between bacteria and material surfaces—whether through molecular chirality or supramolecular chirality—plays a crucial role in modulating antibacterial properties. Compared with L-enantiomers, D-enantiomers of antimicrobial peptides typically exhibit greater efficacy against multidrug resistant bacteria.16 This enhanced effectiveness is often attributed to the superior proteolytic resistance of D-peptides. For example, a study comparing the activities of L- and D-enantiomers of the designed antimicrobial peptide GL13K (derived from human parotid secretory protein) revealed that D-type GL13K demonstrated faster self-assembly kinetics and heightened antimicrobial activity under alkaline pH conditions.17 Another investigation developed small cationic peptides P4C and P5C, which are composed entirely of D-type amino acids. These peptides exhibited complete resistance to proteases, were noncytotoxic and nonhemolytic, and displayed stronger activity against ESKAPE pathogens than did their L-type counterparts.18 hBD3 plays a crucial role in both innate and adaptive immunity, restricting the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines in macrophages stimulated by TLR4.19 hBD3 inhibited the polarization of RAW 264.7 cells toward the M1 phenotype and attenuated the activation of the nuclear factor-κB signaling pathway.20 Previous research has demonstrated that hBD3 exhibits anti-periodontitis biological activity, and this effect may be closely associated with its regulation of the macrophage polarization process.

In this study, we synthesized L/D-hBD3 to systematically compare its antibacterial activity, stability, and immunomodulatory functions with (r-hBD3). The therapeutic effects of each type of hBD3 and antibiotics (amoxicillin + metronidazole) were evaluated using a periodontitis mouse model. Additionally, we analyzed their impacts on oral and intestinal microbiota. The immunomodulatory mechanisms were also preliminarily investigated. This research aims to provide a theoretical foundation for the development of novel chiral antimicrobial peptides and offers insights and strategies for the treatment of periodontitis.

Discussion

The excessive and inappropriate use of antibiotics in clinical practice has resulted in an increase in pathogen resistance, a reduction in the efficacy of existing antibiotics, and an increase in the prevalence of multidrug resistant pathogens. To address this pressing issue, researchers must focus on the development of new antibacterial agents, with hBD3 emerging as a promising candidate. Compared with other defensins, hBD3 has a broader antibacterial spectrum and enhanced antibacterial activity. hBD-3 demonstrates effective antibacterial properties against a diverse range of oral microorganisms.11 On the other hand, hBD3 may play a significant role in the osteogenic differentiation of cells. Periodontal pathogens exhibit sensitivity to the antibacterial properties of periodontal ligament cell (PDLC) sheets that have been modified with the hBD3 gene. Furthermore, these cell sheets mitigate bone resorption induced by inflammation in a canine model of periodontitis.21 Our previous research conducted in the laboratory demonstrated that gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) combined with hBD3 have a protective effect on the progression of experimental periodontitis in rats. In addition to promoting bone repair, these compounds also play a significant role in inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and mitigating the inflammatory damage associated with periodontitis.22 However, several limitations are associated with the clinical application of natural hBD3, including insufficient availability, high manufacturing costs, susceptibility to proteolytic degradation, and a short in vivo half-life. The chiral distribution of amino acids within the sequence results in distinct conformations. Chirality is a pervasive phenomenon in nature. Notably, materials derived from chiral peptides can be intentionally engineered to modify their physicochemical properties through the strategic adjustment of peptide sequences. Compared with the L-type structure, the D-type structure is more stable, and D-type structure responds differently to various stimuli, including elevated temperature, ionic strength, pH, and denaturants.23 Therefore, we aimed to modify the chiral configuration of hBD3 to increase its stability and other biological functions, while also assessing its therapeutic potential for periodontitis.

In vitro experiments demonstrated the successful synthesis of L-hBD3 and D-hBD3 peptides exhibiting optical activity. CD analysis revealed that the secondary structure spectra of L-hBD3 and D-hBD3 were essentially mirror-symmetrical, thereby confirming their anticipated enantiomeric relationship. A slight asymmetry was observed in the circular dichroism spectrum. This phenomenon may be attributed to racemization, as well as amino acid insertions and deletions, and side chain reactions.24 The optical purity of the synthetic product was determined to be 95%, indicating the presence of trace amounts of residual enantiomers or impurities (approximately 5%) during the synthesis process. This finding is consistent with the characteristics inherent to current solid-phase peptide synthesis technology.25,26 We examined the biological functions of r-hBD3, L-hBD3, and D-hBD3. The experimental results indicated that the three variants of human β-defensin 3 exhibited no statistically significant differences in terms of cytotoxicity and hemolytic activity. Notably, at a treatment concentration of 100 μg/mL, r-hBD3 demonstrated a slight trend toward decreased cell viability; however, this difference was not statistically significant when compared to L-hBD3 and D-hBD3. These findings suggest that despite the differing preparation methods—chemical synthesis versus recombinant expression—the three hBD3 variants maintain a high degree of consistency in their fundamental biocompatibility characteristics. The results of the enzymatic stability experiment indicated that D-hBD3 exhibited significantly greater resistance to proteolytic degradation compared to its L configuration, a characteristic that aligns closely with its exceptional performance in antibacterial assays. In experiments targeting P.g, D-hBD3 demonstrated enhanced antibacterial activity at both 25 μg/mL and 50 μg/mL concentrations. Not only does D-hBD3 exhibit superior structural stability, but its antibacterial efficacy is also markedly better than that of r-hBD3 and L-hBD3. This finding corroborates existing research conclusions.17,18

In this study, a murine model of experimental periodontitis was successfully established through the combination of ligature placement and infection with P.g. The therapeutic effects of three variants of hBD3 were systematically evaluated under various systemic administration regimens. The commonly used clinical antibiotic regimen comprising AMX/MTZ served as the positive control. According to the results obtained from micro-CT imaging and periodontal histological analysis, the degree of alveolar bone resorption in all treatment groups—including those receiving the three hBD3 variants as well as the AMX/MTZ group—was significantly lower than that observed in the PD group. Additionally, there was a notable decrease in osteoclast numbers alongside an increase in collagen fiber content, indicating partial repair of periodontal tissue damage. TNF-α is a pro-inflammatory cytokine that plays a crucial role in the onset and progression of periodontitis.27,28 In contrast, IL-10 serves as a key anti-inflammatory cytokine. It exerts its effects by inhibiting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and regulating osteoblast-mediated bone formation, thereby influencing overall bone function.29 The research findings indicate that L-hBD3 and D-hBD3 play a crucial role in modulating the inflammatory microenvironment of periodontal tissues. In comparison to the PD group, there was a significant reduction in the expression level of TNF-α, accompanied by a notable increase in IL-10 levels. These results suggest that L-hBD3 and D-hBD3 can effectively restore the balance between typical pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors in cases of periodontitis.

The analysis of the microbial community surrounding the second molar indicated that hBD3 can specifically modulate the microbiota structure associated with periodontitis in mice. This finding reveals a dual mechanism of action, characterized by an increase in beneficial bacteria (Firmicutes_D) and a reduction in pathogenic bacteria (Actinobacteria/Proteobacteria). Notably, D-hBD3 exhibited a significantly enhanced capacity to inhibit pathogenic bacteria compared to traditional antibiotics. In the oral microbiological detection, P.g inoculated in the experimental group of a ligature-induced periodontitis model was not effectively detected. This phenomenon may be attributed to several factors: Firstly, the existing detection methods exhibit insufficient sensitivity for low-load P.g. Secondly, the relatively short observation period of the experiment may have influenced the detection results. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that in a simple ligature-induced periodontitis model, P.g can be identified in gingival tissue by the 45th day of experimentation.30 suggesting that extending the observation period could enhance the pathogen detection rate.

Although most courses of antibiotic treatment do not cause apparent adverse reactions, antibiotics can induce significant alterations in the intestinal microbiota, thereby exerting both short-term and long-term effects on health.31 This study investigated the effects of AMX/MTX, as well as the application of hBD3 as an adjunctive therapy for periodontitis, on the intestinal microbiota of mice. These findings indicated that the administration of AMX/MTX significantly reduced the diversity of the intestinal flora, notably increasing the levels of pathogenic bacteria belonging to Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria, and substantially decreasing Firmicutes_D.32 In contrast, the application of hBD3 did not significant alter the diversity of the intestinal flora. D-hBD3 plays a vital role in inhibiting oral pathogenic microorganisms and maintaining microbial homeostasis. Previous studies have indicated that periodontitis may induce an imbalance in intestinal flora through alterations in the salivary microbiota.33 In this study, significant changes were observed in the oral microbiota of mice within the PD group, while no notable alterations were detected in their intestinal flora. This discrepancy may be attributed to the limited sample size and relatively short duration of the experimental period, as potential changes in the intestinal flora have not yet fully manifested. Research has demonstrated that the use of antibiotics can result in the overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria due to a decrease in species diversity.34 AMPs play a crucial role in maintaining a balanced microbiota. Defensins, the most abundant antimicrobial peptides associated with the intestinal mucosa, serve as a barrier defense mechanism to protect the host from pathogen invasion.35 Research has shown that β-defensins possess antimicrobial activity against a diverse range of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative intestinal pathogens.36 The intestinal flora exhibited partial recovery 28 days after the cessation of AMX/MTZ treatment; however, it had not yet been fully restored to its baseline state. Notably, the levels of the pathogenic bacteria Proteobacteria remained elevated. The effects of antibiotic therapy on the intestinal microbiome are significant and far-reaching. Distinct chiral variants of hBD3 do not exhibit toxicity in healthy organs.37 Histological analysis indicated a trend toward a decreased ratio of intestinal villus length to crypt depth in the AMX/MTX group, which may compromise intestinal barrier function and elevate the risk of pathogen colonization.38

In addition to its antibacterial properties, hBD3 also has immunomodulatory effects.39,40 Our previous study demonstrated that hBD3 can polarize macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, which contributes to its anti-inflammatory characteristics.41 The results from this study revealed that D-hBD3 significantly increased the proportion of M2 macrophages and elevated serum levels of IL-10, which is beneficial for promoting systemic anti-inflammatory effects in periodontitis treatment.19 Further investigations are needed to elucidate the mechanism through which D-hBD3 exerts its anti-inflammatory effects in the treatment of periodontitis by facilitating the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype.

From the perspective of production costs, there are notable differences in the pricing of the three hBD3 peptides. The cost of r-hBD3 (100 μg) is approximately $ 302, whereas L-hBD3 and D-hBD3, both with the same specifications, are priced at $86 and $130 respectively. In comparison to r-hBD3, both L-hBD3 and D-hBD3 exhibit significant price advantages, with L-hBD3 being the most cost-effective option. In conjunction with the findings from this study, it is evident that D-hBD3 not only offers a competitive price—approximately 57% lower than that of r-hBD3—but also retains excellent biological activity. In conclusion, D-hBD3 demonstrates significant potential as a next-generation cost-effective therapeutic agent for the treatment of periodontitis, owing to its lower cost, unique stability, diverse therapeutic mechanisms, and promising prospects for clinical translation.

This study systematically evaluated the biological characteristics of various configurations of hBD3 variants (L-hBD3, D-hBD3, and r-hBD3) and their potential applications in the treatment of periodontitis. The primary findings of this research are as follows: D-hBD3 exhibited significant performance advantages, demonstrating enhanced stability and antibacterial activity. It effectively regulates the imbalance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses in vivo, specifically modulating the oral microecological environment while maintaining intestinal flora homeostasis. These results not only further substantiate the considerable advantages of D-hBD3 as a candidate drug for periodontitis treatment but also provide a robust theoretical foundation and practical basis for developing new therapeutic strategies centered on D-type antimicrobial peptides.

Limitations of the study

The sample size of the in vivo experimental animals in this study was limited, and there were significant individual differences among the experimental subjects. This variability may result in discrepancies in certain outcomes both within and between groups. Although the findings indicated no significant difference between the use of chiral hBD3 and AMX/MTZ for treating periodontitis, further comprehensive studies are necessary to fully elucidate the therapeutic potential of chiral hBD3 for this condition. Additionally, whether chiral hBD3 can serve as a viable alternative to antibiotics in clinical applications warrants further investigation and exploration.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

The animal study described in this article was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Aniphe Biolaboratory Inc. (Approval Number: JSAB25006M). All methods employed in the study were in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations established by the committee. This study adhered to the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines for preclinical animal studies. ARRIVE Statements include the following: (I) Identification of the institutional and/or licensing committee that granted approval for the experiments, along with any relevant details. (II) Confirmation that all the experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Aniphe Biolaboratory, Inc., housed in an SPF-level rearing environment provided by the same laboratory, and maintained under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. The mice were acclimatized in a temperature-controlled room maintained at 23 ± 3°C with relative humidity ranging from 40% to 70%, allowing for adaptive rearing over a period of 1 week.

Experimental periodontitis model and groups

Forty-two male six-week-old C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to six groups (N = 7 per group). The mouse experimental periodontitis model was established by ligating the second maxillary molar with a 4-0 silk thread (Jinghuan Medical Appliance, Yangzhou, China) and inoculating it with Porphyromonas gingivalis (; obtained from the American Type Culture Collection [ATCC], accession number: ATCC 33277) at a dosage of 1 × 10ˆ9 colony-forming units (CFU) per mouse (50 μL per mouse). The P.g was anaerobically cultured on blood agar plates (hopebio, China). The cultures were placed in anaerobic culture bags (hopebio) with anaerobic gas producting bags (hopebio), sealed, and incubated at 37°C. The modeling process lasted for two weeks. Then the ligature was removed and dental plaque around the teeth was cleaned as thoroughly as possible using a probe. Subsequently, different types of hBD3 and antibiotics were applied respectively in mice. According to different intervention methods, the groups are classified as follows:

(1) Control group, the mice without periodontitis.; (2) Periodontitis (PD) group, the mice with periodontitis received a daily gavage of phosphate buffer saline (PBS) for 7 days; (3) Amoxicillin + metronidazole + periodontitis (AMX+MTZ) group, the mice with periodontitis received dental plaque removing intervention and intragastric administration of 8 mg/mL amoxicillin (AMX, MedChemExpress, USA) and metronidazole (MTZ, MedChemExpress) daily for 7 days; (4) r-hBD3+ periodontitis (r-hBD3) group, the mice with periodontitis received dental plaque removing intervention and intragastric administration of 100 μg/mL r-hBD3 daily for 7 days; (5) L-hBD3+ periodontitis (L-hBD3) group, the mice with periodontitis received dental plaque removing intervention and intragastric administration of 100 μg/mL L-hBD3 daily for 7 days; (6) D-hBD3+ periodontitis (D-hBD3) group, the mice with periodontitis received dental plaque removing intervention and intragastric administration of 100 μg/mL D-hBD3 daily for 7 days.

Method details

Circular dichroism

L/D-hBD3 was synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis, achieving a purity greater than 95%. The peptide was subsequently dissolved in a 10 mM phosphate buffer (PBS, CORNING, USA) at pH 7.4 to obtain a final concentration of 100 μg/mL. To eliminate aggregates, the solution was centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 10 min. The supernatant obtained was immediately utilized for circular dichroism (CD) (J-810, JASCO, Japan) measurements.

Cytotoxicity assessment

At 14 to 16 days of gestation, female C57BL/6J mice were euthanized, after which their entire bodies were immersed in 75% alcohol. The embryos were carefully extracted from the maternal body, and the outer membranes of the embryos were removed. The heads and internal organs were subsequently excised, after which the remaining embryonic tissue was minced and placed in an appropriate trypsin solution. This solution was then digested in a water bath at 37°C, and the resulting cells were transferred to high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) for culture at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. The viability of mouse embryonic fibroblasts was assessed via a Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated overnight. Subsequently, recombinant human β-defensin 3 (r-hBD3; Nanjing Jin Yibai Biological Technology Co. Ltd., China), L-hBD3, and D-hBD3 were introduced into the culture medium at concentrations of 25 μg/mL, 50 μg/mL and 100 μg/mL. After a 24 h incubation period, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and further incubated for an additional 2 h. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm via a microplate reader. The cell viability assay was conducted in triplicate or more to ensure the reliability of the results.

Hemolytic activity assessment

First, whole blood from the mice was centrifuged at 1500 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were then resuspended in PBS and subjected to centrifugation at 1500 ×g for an additional 5 min, after which the supernatant was discarded once more. This process was repeated three times until the supernatant after centrifugation exhibited no discernible color. Subsequently, red blood cells were collected and diluted with PBS to create an 8% (v/v) red blood cell suspension. The concentrations of the r-hBD3, L-hBD3, and D-hBD3 solutions were 100 μg/mL. In a 96-well plate, 150 μL of each antimicrobial peptide solution was combined with 150 μL of the prepared 8% red blood cell suspension and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Following incubation, the mixed solutions were centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min, after which the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at a wavelength of 540 nm. Red blood cells suspended in a buffer containing only PBS served as a blank control representing zero percent hemolysis (0%), whereas those treated with a solution of Triton X-100 (Solarbio, Beijing, China) at a concentration of 1% constituted the positive control group representing complete hemolysis (100%). The hemolytic activity was calculated via the following formula: hemolysis rate (%) = [(Ap - A0)/(A100 - A0)] × 100%, where A0 denotes the absorbance from the blank control group consisting solely of PBS, A100 represents the absorbance from the positive control group containing Triton X-100, and Ap corresponds to the absorbance from the experimental groups.

In vitro antimicrobial assays

Freshly cultured P.g was grown in PBS (50 μL; OD600 = 0.5, containing approximately 10ˆ5 CFU) supplemented with r-hBD3, L-hBD3, or D-hBD3 at concentrations of 25 μg/mL, 50 μg/mL and 100 μg/mL. The total reaction volume was maintained at 100 μL, and the cultures were incubated at 37°C with constant shaking at a speed of 200 rpm. After 1 h of incubation, the bacterial mixture of P.g was further diluted by a factor of 5,000. Subsequently, 100 μL from each diluted sample was plated onto blood agar plates (with three independent repetitions). Following overnight incubation at 37°C, colony-forming units (CFUs) of P.g were counted accordingly.

Trypsin degradation experiment

Aliquots of 200 μL of three solutions—r-hBD3, L-hBD3, and D-hBD3—each with a concentration of 100 μg/mL were combined with 400 U/L trypsin (Solarbio) and incubated in a constant temperature incubator at 37°C for 12 h. The mixture was then shaken thoroughly, and 80 μL of the resulting solution was transferred into a sterilized EP tube. Subsequently, 20 μL of 10% acetic acid (XILONG SCIENTIFIC, China) was added and mixed well, followed by low-temperature centrifugation at 4°C at 12,000 ×g for 15 min. A volume of 10 μL from the supernatant was collected for component analysis using an Agilent 1260 HPLC system. The HPLC washing conditions used were as follows: a linear gradient from 5% to 40% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% TFA) at a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min. The number of charged particles and their mass-charge ratios were determined via an Agilent 6410 LC-MS system equipped with an ESI source. Independent experiments were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Micro-CT

Maxillary samples from C57BL/6J mice were immersed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 48 h, followed by micro computed tomography (micro-CT) scanning (Skyscan. 1176 scanner, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany). The scanning parameters included a voxel resolution of 18 μm, a source voltage of 65 kV, and a source current of 385 μA. Postscan, three-dimensional (3D) image reconstruction and two-dimensional (2D) sectional images were generated in the mesiodistal and buccolingual orientations at various sites via CTvox and DataViewer software. The CTAn software was subsequently employed to quantify bone-related parameters, specifically measuring the distance from the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar bone crest (ABC) on both the mesial and distal sides surrounding the maxillary second molars.

ELISA

The concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-10 (IL-10) in mouse serum were assessed using the appropriate enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (NeoBioscience, Shenzhen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histological analysis

The maxillary bone samples were immersed in a 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) solution for 5 weeks. Subsequently, the samples underwent a series of processes including dehydration, embedding, and sectioning, with the thickness of the sections set at 5 μm. Hematoxylin-eosin (HE, Servicebio) staining was then performed to evaluate histological changes and assess the degree of inflammation. Concurrently, Masson’s trichrome staining and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining were employed to analyze bone resorption, osteoclast activity, and the extent of tissue destruction. Samples of small intestinal tissue as well as organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for a duration of 24 h. Subsequently, these samples underwent dehydration, embedding, and sectioning processes before being stained with HE to facilitate the observation of histological changes. The results from these staining procedures were collected and analyzed using a scanner in conjunction with CaseViewer software. For quantitative assessment, five random fields of view were selected from each sample to objectively analyze osteoclast activity in the maxilla and the ratio of intestinal villus length to crypt depth.

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence

Immunohistochemical analysis involved an overnight incubation of maxillary bone sections at 4°C with primary antibodies (IL-10 rabbit antibody or TNF-α rabbit antibody, Servicebio), followed by a 1 h incubation at room temperature with secondary antibodies. For quantitative assessment, ImageJ software was utilized to evaluate three specific regions (the mesial, distal, and furcation regions of the maxillary second molar) in each section. Immunofluorescence analysis was performed after fixation and permeabilization of intestinal tissue sections; the samples were blocked with 5% normal goat serum containing 1% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, a mixture of primary antibodies (CD206 rabbit antibody and CD86 antibody, Abcam, UK), diluted at a ratio of 1:100, was added and incubated overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber. Following washing with PBS buffer, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa Fluor 594 and anti-mouse IgG-Alexa Fluor 488, Abcam), diluted to a concentration of 1:500, were applied and incubated at room temperature for one and a half hours in the dark. Thereafter, DAPI (Abcam) was utilized to stain the nuclei, after which the sections were mounted using an anti-fade mounting medium. Images were collected sequentially in single channels utilizing a confocal microscope. Finally, the fluorescence signals corresponding to M1 and M2 macrophages were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software, allowing for the calculation of the M1/M2 ratio.

qRT‒PCR

The tissue of the small intestine was immersed in a solution designed for RNA preservation (Servicebio). Total RNA was extracted from the intestine via the Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA utilizing the HiScript III RT SuperMix Kit (Vazyme). Relative quantification results were analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCt method. The experiment was conducted in triplicate, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the internal reference for standardization. The primer sequences used are listed in Table S1.

Analysis of oral and intestinal microbiota

On the seventh day post-administration, microbial samples were collected from the second molar region of the maxilla in mice using 4-0 ligature thread. Concurrently, fecal samples were also obtained. A subsequent collection of fecal samples was performed on the twenty-eighth day following administration. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen after collection and stored at −80°C for long-term preservation. DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing of the V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene were conducted by Shanghai Personalbio Technology Co., Ltd.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All the data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Statistical analyses were conducted via GraphPad Prism version 8.0 software. Statistical significance was assessed via a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. The statistical method utilized is detailed in the corresponding figure legends. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the significance levels are indicated as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
