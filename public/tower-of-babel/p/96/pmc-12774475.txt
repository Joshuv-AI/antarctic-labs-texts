Endogenous vasopressin and vasopressin receptor 2 in bladder as anti-diuretic / anti-spasmodic targets for the treatment of multifactorial nocturia
Authors: Irina Zabbarova, Pradeep Tyagi, Youko Ikeda, Xiangyu Yan, Visishta Ginjupally, Sophia Percy, Amanda Wolf-Johnson, Shachi Tyagi, Marcus Drake, Karl-Erik Andersson, Alan Wein, Christopher Fry, Anthony Kanai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Nocturia, the urge to wake from sleep to void, is targeted sub-optimally by existing drugs that modulate circadian dysregulation of urine production in kidney without alleviating detrusor overactivity, a key aspect in the multifactorial etiology of nocturia. This study reports age-related upregulation of arginine vasopressin (AVP) synthesis in mouse urinary bladder to counter the concurrent decline in the kidneys’ ability to concentrate urine, another prevalent symptom of nocturia.Methods:Adult/aged C57Bl/6 mice of both sexes were anesthetized with isoflurane for bladder permeability experiments and used awake for cystometries with saline and soybean oil. Bladder overactivity was induced by chronic acrolein instillation. AVP and VR expression was examined by Western blot and immunofluorescence.Results:Aged mice showed lower urine osmolality relative to adults that coincided with upregulation of urothelial pre-pro AVP synthesis and VR2 expression that would complement the endocrine action of AVP in promoting bladder reabsorption of free water from stored urine. This was evident from the amplified systemic uptake of tritiated water following bladder instillation of AVP or vasopressin receptor 2 (VR2) agonist, desmopressin (dAVP). Fedovapagon, a small molecule VR2 agonist, exerted anti-diuretic action in kidney, surpassing the efficacy of AVP or dAVP in free water reabsorption from stored urine in bladder and dampened detrusor overactivity.Conclusion:Fedovapagon, with its anti-diuretic actions on kidney, augments the therapeutic benefit activating VR2 receptors in urothelium to promote water reabsorption and relax the detrusor. Therefore, bladder VR2 activation by fedovapagon exemplifies a mechanistic paradigm for addressing the multifactorial etiology of nocturia.Keywords: Arginine vasopressin, Bladder overactivity, Desmopressin, Fedovapagon, Aging

Introduction

Nocturia, the urge to urinate that interrupts sleep, has been reported in men and women across all ages and affects a significant number of younger adults with the prevalence increasing with age [1]. Clinically, nocturics are characterized by increased diuresis, natriuresis, non-dipping blood pressure during sleep and involuntary detrusor contractions (nocturnal urgency) [2,3]. The multifactorial pathophysiology of nocturia involves both urologic (e.g., detrusor overactivity) and non-urologic factors (e.g., nocturnal polyuria due to heart failure, hypertension, sleep apnea, diabetes insipidus) [4].

The diurnal changes in urine production [5] are regulated through various hormones including arginine vasopressin (AVP) acting on three G protein-coupled receptors; vasopressin receptor (VR)1a, VR1b (also known as VR3), and VR2. VR1a/VR1b activate phospholipase through Gq protein-coupling to cause a rise in intracellular Ca2+ through transient receptor potential (TRP) channels and VR2 activates adenylyl cyclase through Gs protein to induce smooth muscle relaxation and facilitate diffusion of H2O through insertion of aquaporin 2 (AQP2) channels into the cytoplasmic membrane [6]. VR2 until recently was believed to function mainly in the renal collecting duct cells to reabsorb water and maintain plasma osmoregulation [6], but we previously described that there is a an age-related upregulation in functional expression of VR1a and VR2 in the kidney and bladder mucosa of rats [7] and humans [8], and here, that the expression of pre-proAVP and VR1b as well as VR2 in the bladder mucosa of mice are involved in osmoregulation.

The rise in urine osmolality with the transition of urine from the renal pelvis to the bladder [9] and the reduction/stabilization of the bladder volume of healthy adults during sleep [10] is informed by the rapid uptake of 3H water and 14C urea from the uninjured bladders of mice and rabbits [11] replicating the systemic uptake of instilled 3H water from human bladder [12]. The systemic reuptake of water, urea and Na+ from bladder is mediated by the expression of Na+-K+-ATPase [13] and mineralocorticoid receptors [14] on the basolateral membrane, carbonic anhydrase isozymes I and III in middle layer and deeper layer of urothelial cells, and epithelial sodium channel (ENaC).

AQPs comprise a family of channels that regulate water diffusion [15,16] but may also be involved in movement of small nonelectrolytes such as urea. Recent studies have shown that the rodent urothelium expresses AQP1, AQP2 and AQP3 (there are AQP3, 4, 7, and 9 in human). Additionally, AQPs (e.g., AQP1) have been localized to the endothelial cells lining the capillaries and arterioles of the bladder wall [17]. These channels and transporters facilitate urine reabsorption by the bladder for maintaining urine production rate of kidneys above oliguria threshold in healthy humans electing water fasting lasting ≥12 h [5,18]. Unlike kidneys, water influx into the urothelium is independent of urine osmolality, as water molecules from the bladder lumen traverse umbrella cells by passive paracellular diffusion into extracellular space, from where it is ultimately transported to intravascular space via AQP2 expressed on the basolateral side of umbrella cells [15, 16] and endothelial cells lining of capillaries and arterioles of the bladder wall. AQP2 upregulation in the urothelium with distension [15] during sleep emulates the dehydration evoked upregulation of AQP2 in rodents [17,19] and in humans [20]. The ATP needed to fuel Na+ transport from the basolateral side of umbrella cells and increased transcription and phosphorylation of AQP2 channels and urea transporters are generated by oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria rich umbrella cells [21], whose excessive oxygen demand can only be met by unequal distribution of bladder blood flow between the mucosa and detrusor. Thus, paracrine AVP/VR2 signaling together with autoregulation of the mucosal blood flow is integral to bladder barrier function. Accordingly, the premise of this study was to demonstrate that VR2 signaling in the bladder urothelium enhances water reabsorption from urine stored in the lumen. We postulate urine reabsorption from bladder to be a complementary mechanism with the kidney to regulate bladder volume and urine osmolality during sleep and during bouts of restricted water intake and dehydration [5,18]. Material and methods

Animals

Adult (12–24 weeks) and aged (>22 months) male and female C57Bl/6J mice were purchased from Jackson laboratories (strain #000664, Bar Harbor, ME). Study animals were maintained in a centralized climate-controlled animal unit at the Department of Laboratory Animal Resources, University of Pittsburgh. Animals were socially housed and maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle (7:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m.), food and water provided ad libitum. All animal procedures received ethical approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Pittsburgh (protocol # 23124202, 23053115) and were carried out by experienced personnel, with no systemic casualties.

Test drugs

dAVP acetate (Selleck Chemicals LLC, Houston, TX), AVP, fedovapagon and lixivaptan (MedChemExpress LLC, Monmouth Junction, NJ) were dissolved in saline (dAVP acetate) or DMSO to form stock solutions and stored as aliquots at −30 °C.

24 h urine collection

Mice were placed unrestrained in metabolic cages with a waste collection system that separates urine from feces and keeps it at 2–4 °C to minimize evaporation (Columbus Instruments, Columbus, OH). The voiding activity was recorded for 24 h using associated OxyMax software.

Mobile filling cystometries (CMGs) using an implanted access port

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (5 % for initiation and 1.5–2 % for maintenance). A bladder catheter was implanted via a laparotomy, led under the skin to the back of the animal and connected to an externalized port (Instech Labs, Plymouth Meetings, PA). Muscle and skin were sutured separately, and mice allowed to recover with prophylactic antibiotics and analgesics with recordings commencing ~2 weeks after surgery. Prior to each CMG recording, the bladder was emptied. The external surface of the access port was connected to a programmable syringe pump and pressure transducer with the animals freely mobile during their CMGs recorded using LabChart (AD Instruments, Colorado Springs, CO). The bladders were filled with 0.01 mL/min room temperature saline or soybean oil (MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO) with or without fedovapagon (100 nM).

Acrolein instillation

Male mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and catheterized through the urethras with PE10/30G needle catheters. Bladders were then emptied and instilled with 150 μL of 1 mM acrolein solution (Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA). The instillations lasted 10 min and were repeated 2 more times, 7 days between instillations.

In vivo systemic reabsorption of solute-free and bound water from bladder

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and catheterized. Bladders were then emptied and instilled with 150 or 300 μL of saline containing 5 μCi/l 3H water and 5 μCi/l14C urea with or without AVP (100 nM) or dAVP (100 nM). The tail was snipped 1 mm from the tip and 10 μL of blood withdrawn before, immediately after and then every 15 min for 1–2 h (total volume did not exceed 200 μL), immediately mixed with 2 mL of scintillation fluid and disintegration per minute (DPM) measured using a liquid scintillation counter (PerkinElmer, Akron, OH).

In a different experimental setting, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. One ureter was isolated, cut from the bladder and diverted to a collection tube for collecting fresh urine (the cut end near the bladder suture closed). The other ureter was left intact to deliver urine to the bladder, to be collected 2 h later. Experiment was repeated after intraperitoneal (1 mg/kg, IP) administration of fedovapagon and urine samples analyzed for volume and electrolyte content.

Urine and serum ion concentration and osmolarity measurements

Na+, K+ and Cl− concentrations were measured from freshly withdrawn 24 h urine or serum samples from mice treated with AVP receptor agonists/antagonists or vehicle (DMSO or saline) with an EasyLyte analyzer (Medica Corporation, MA) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Urine osmolarity was measured using a Wescor Vapro 5520 osmometer.

Vascular illumination of the mouse bladder

Mice were given 500 units of heparin 30 min before isoflurane anesthesia. The jugular vein was exposed and a PE10 catheter inserted and secured. Fluorescent microbeads (0.02 μm diameter diluted 1:2 with phosphate buffered saline and 500 units heparin for 150 μl total volume, Thermofisher) were slowly infused and allowed to circulate for 15 min. The mouse was sacrificed while under anesthesia and the bladder isolated. The bladder was fixed overnight in 4 % paraformaldehyde, followed by 24 h in 30 % sucrose in phosphate buffered saline before embedding for cyrosectioning. Bladders were sectioned 100 μm thick and visualized using confocal microscopy (Olympus FV3000, Waltham, MA).

Immunofluorescence

Bladders were isolated, fixed overnight in 10 % buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned transversely, 3 μm thick on a microtome. Sections were processed for immunofluorescence. Briefly, tissue sections were subjected to citrate buffer antigen retrieval and incubated in primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. For full list of used antibodies see Supplemental Table S1. For fluorescent labeling, signal amplification was performed using tyramide based kits (Alexa Fluor Tyramide SuperBoost kit, Thermofisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA) according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. Labeling was visualized, and images were captured using an FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus) and recorded using confocal montage microscopy.

Western blot and ELISA

Bladder and urine samples were collected immediately following sacrifice of the animals and snap frozen with liquid nitrogen. Blood was also collected for plasma and serum samples. Tissue protein lysates were isolated and used for Western blot analysis using methodology as previously described [22]. The antibodies and dyes utilized in this study are listed in supplementary data. Urine, plasma and serum were analyzed using AVP ELISA (EIA-VAS-1, RayBiotech, PeachTree Corner, GA) according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out on n = 3 to 5 mice (see legends for each figure). The data sets were assessed for normal distribution using Prism. The appropriate statistical tests were used based on the assessment results. Student’s t-test or one-way Anova were used for normally distributed data while non-normal distributions were analyzed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The difference between groups was considered significant when p < 0.05. Cohen’s effect sizes for all significantly different data corresponded to large between-group differences (>0.8). Data from experiments and image analysis were summarized as mean ± standard deviation. The mean between-group differences with 95 % confidence intervals (CI) are included in the figure and table legends.

Results

Immunolocalization of AVP and its receptors in the mouse bladder wall

We characterized the VR and AVP expression in adult and aged female C57Bl/6 mouse bladders to determine their localization and relative expression. Fig. 2A shows immunolocalization of pre-proAVP in bladder tissue from aged female mice where it is densely localized in the apical urothelial cells with only scattered expression in the lamina propria, and adult mice where it is more localized to basal cells/lamina propria. The age-related pre-proAVP upregulation in urothelium could be a compensatory mechanism to the decline in AVP secretion from the pituitary gland [23]. VR1a (Fig. 2B) is concentrated in the apical cells while VR1b (Fig. 2C) is conveniently localized to the intermediate and basal cells such that released mature AVP, 6 % excreted unchanged in urine or directly from plasma (66–93 % of bladder blood reaches mucosa [24,25]) can readily bind to these receptors. Age-related difference in urothelial pre-proAVP was supported by measurements of mature AVP in adult and aged mouse urine, serum and plasma samples showing a trend towards increased urinary AVP without differences in blood levels between the age groups (Supplemental Fig. S1). Somewhat removed is the VR2 subtype (Fig. 2D) which is predominately localized to basal cells in aged bladders with minimal expression in adults. VR1b are also found in detrusor smooth muscle (Fig. 2E). The relative protein level of pre-proAVP in adult and aged mouse bladders was measured using Western blot and supported the immunofluorescence findings. Namely, there is a greater expression of pre-proAVP in aged mouse bladder mucosa compared to younger mice (Fig. 2F) with no significant difference in the detrusor (Fig. 2G).

Systemic reabsorption of solute-free and bound water from bladder

To determine the functional properties of bladder VR signaling, mice were anesthetized, and their bladders were instilled with 150 or 300 μL of saline containing 3H water (Fig. 3A) and 14C urea (Fig. 3B). These experiments demonstrated the positive association between water and urea absorption with distention/bladder volume (Fig. 3C) and that this association is attenuated by the age-related decline in vascularization and perfusion (Fig. 3D and E). While AQP2 expression increases with age, age-related decline in urea transporters in urothelium [26] can shed light on the differences in plasma profile of 3H water (Figs. 3A) and 14C urea (Fig. 3B). Bladder distension physically dilates the tight gap junction [27] to accelerate the paracellular diffusion of water from lumen into the extracellular space of the urothelium and the resultant decline in osmolarity of extracellular fluid activates AQP2 expression [15] for facilitated diffusion of water from extracellular space to the intravascular space, regardless of gender. Instilled volumes were determined from the mean voided volumes recorded in metabolic cage experiments (~150 μL for adult/300 μL for aged males).

Because bladder distension to 1 mL confounded the effect of intravenous dAVP in anesthetized rat [15], we selected much lower bladder volume of ~150 μL to tease out the effect of intravesical AVP and dAVP on the systemic reuptake of 3H water after instillation of 0.1 % DMSO (control), AVP (100 nM) or dAVP (100 nM, Fig. 4A–D). The % dose of 3H water absorbed at a single time point of 15 min in adult and aged mice of both genders (Fig. 4E) is consistent with the kinetics of water reabsorption determined in human bladder without ureter ligation [12] and in rabbit bladder with ureter ligation [11]. In the absence of ureter ligation, peak plasma levels of xenon gas instilled in human bladder peaked at 5 min [28] and renal excretion at 30 min of radioisotopes absorbed from bladder generated spurious results about bladder permeability [29]. Our mice study without ureter ligation mimics clinical study and we compared different treatment groups at 15 min to exclude the confounding effect from progressively higher renal excretion of 3H water at later timepoints.

Relative to control settings, both AVP and dAVP significantly enhanced the rate of rise in DPM (Fig. 4A–D) and more than doubled the % dose of absorbed radionuclide (Fig. 4E) in aged male mice compared to 60 % at the same time point noted with AVP in aged female mice. The absence of a significant effect of the agonists on water reabsorption in adult male mice at the 15 min timepoint indicates that the mucosal perfusion rate and vascularization density is the primary determinant in water reabsorption [30], which is also affirmed by significantly lower 3H water absorption by vehicle treated aged male mice relative to adult male mice. If significantly higher mucosal perfusion in males masks the influence of dAVP on urine reabsorption, adult male mice would be an appropriate model to tease out the differential effect of urine reabsorption and detrusor relaxant effect of fedovapagon in future studies. Aged females reabsorbed significantly higher dose of instilled water than adult males at the 15 min timepoint in both presence and absence of AVP/dAVP (#p < 0.05).

In vivo reabsorption of urine from the bladder

To demonstrate that water reabsorption alters urine composition at the level of the bladder, we analyzed the mouse urine electrolyte composition of stored urine versus urine collected directly from the ureter (Fig. 5). Over a 2 h period, urine volume collected from bladder was ~20 % lower than urine volume collected simultaneously from the transected ureter (Table 1). Moreover, a significantly higher Na+ concentration (108 ± 5 vs. 74 ± 20 mM) in urine collected from bladder relative to the severed ureter (Fig. 5A–C) is consistent with solute-free water reabsorption from stored urine to reflexively counter the rise in plasma osmolality ensuing from a decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) under surgical anesthesia as evident from a modest >5 % rise in plasma Na+ levels to ~150 mM (Fig. 5C) from ~140 mM reported in control mice [31].

The effect of fedovapagon on bladder relaxation and detrusor overactivity

Cystometrograms in awake mice demonstrated that prior administration of the VR2 antagonist, lixivaptan, abolished the effect of intravesical administration of fedovapagon (100 nM), especially the decrease in baseline pressure as well as contractile activity (Fig. 6). dAVP administration in similar experiments did not have this effect (not shown). The lipophilicity and lower molecular weight of fedovapagon compared to dAVP ensured predictable absorption across the urothelium [32]. The anti-diuretic effect in kidney together with anti-spasmodic effect in bladder was evoked by intramuscular injection of fedovapagon (1 mg/kg) in acrolein instilled mouse (a model of bladder overactivity) with the decrease in baseline pressure, pressure threshold and non-voiding contractions in conjunction with increase in intercontractile intervals and increase in bladder capacity compared to sham controls (Fig. 6D and E, Supplemental Table S2).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that AVP-VR2 mediates solute-free water reabsorption in the mouse bladder, which is amenable to modulation by AVP levels excreted in urine and by plasma levels of AVP. Moreover, a small molecule VR2 agonist, fedovapagon, surpasses the anti-diuretic action of AVP. The pharmacological actions of systemic and intravesical fedovapagon are consistent with VR2 activation triggering 3′,5′-cAMP/PKA pathway for increased transcription and phosphorylation of AQP2 channels and their insertion into the collecting ducts and the basolateral surface of umbrella cells in the bladder epithelium. The anti-diuretic action of fedovapagon is self-evident from publicly available evidence of ten completed phase I to III clinical trials between 2008 and 2018 recording a 50 % reduction in the number of nocturnal voids in men with benign prostatic hyperplasia (NCT02637960) where its reported site of action was the renal collecting ducts. We now demonstrate that the anti-diuretic action of fedovapagon in kidney is augmented by direct action on VR2 receptors in the bladder to accentuate urine reabsorption, detrusor relaxation and alleviate nocturnal urgency as a novel treatment paradigm with multipronged action to target multifactorial etiology of nocturia.

It has been previously shown that rodent and human bladders express VR subtypes and the coincidence between age-related upregulation of VR [22,33] and presence of pre-proAVP in the aged mouse urothelium (Fig. 2) implies that paracrine AVP/VR2 signaling in urothelium could be compensatory to the age-related decline in AVP secretion from the pituitary gland [23], a component in the multifactorial etiology of nocturia. Furthermore, the bladder is also endowed with the capacity for a paracrine AVP/VR2 signaling mechanism that has also been described in the kidney [34]. However, more recent findings suggest that endogenous pre-proAVP of the kidney plays a redundant role in urine concentration [7]. An enhanced rate of urine reabsorption in presence of AVP or dAVP (Fig. 4) implies that the activation of AVP/VR2 axis in kidney and bladder lowers plasma osmolality by increasing the urine osmolality during dehydration and sleep [5,18]. AVP released by the pituitary gland activates VR2 receptors to increase water reabsorption via AQP2 on the apical membrane of collecting ducts in the kidney, which is aided by paracrine signaling of AVP produced locally in the bladder mucosa. Osmolality of plasma and urine are related [5,18], homeostasis of plasma osmolality with dehydration for 12 h results from a dramatic rise in urine osmolality whereas water loading or diuresis decrease urine osmolality [35].

A linear rise in absorption of radiolabeled water and urea from the bladder into the blood with distention of mouse bladder (Fig. 3) corroborate previously reported results in rat with ligated ureter [36] in accordance with the rise in paracellular diffusion of instilled dyes and drugs with human bladder distension from 30 to 60 mL [37]. It is therefore reasonable to infer that paracellular diffusion of water from the bladder lumen into extracellular space of the urothelium precedes AQP mediated systemic reuptake into the intravascular space [15,16]. Confocal imaging of bladder sections of mice transcardially perfused with fluorescent beads revealed that the density of blood vessels to the bladder mucosa was much lower in aged mice compared to younger adults (Fig. 3D versus 4E).

The reduction in urine volume due to reabsorption in mice recapitulates the previously reported temporary volume reductions in the bladders of sleeping healthy human adults [10]. Furthermore, transient reductions of urine volume during sleep were hypothesized to coincide with the spikes in plasma levels of AVP and aldosterone [38]. The coincidence implies that hormonal fluctuations can adjust the rate of free water reabsorption in kidney as well as in bladder throughout sleep for maintenance of plasma osmolality, as previously demonstrated in water loaded and water deprived rats [39,40]. The circadian release of AVP during sleep of healthy adults is a key player of nocturnal dips in blood pressure which makes bladder hemodynamics favorable for increased compliance with the upregulation of AQP2 and Piezo 1 together with TRPV4 downregulation [41] for increased reabsorption of solute-free and solute-bound water from urine. Accordingly, age-related decline in kidney function is expected to increase the production of dilute urine and concurrent age-related hypertension and cardiovascular diseases [42] would compromise microcirculation of the bladder mucosa to negatively impact reabsorption of urine during sleep. Despite bladder outlet obstruction raising AQP1 expression [43], compensatory hypertrophy of detrusor can lower mucosal perfusion to lower urine reabsorption with prolonged bladder distension. Accordingly, aging-associated pathologies (e.g. benign prostatic enlargement, metabolic syndrome) could impact urine reabsorption due to compromised vascular perfusion [44].

Aged mice exhibited increased responsiveness to VR agonists compared to their younger counterparts (Fig. 4E) and aged males [30], showed decreased urine reabsorption under control conditions presumably due to reduction in nitric oxide signaling [45] and oxidative stress impacting the activity of AQP [46]. Taken together with immunolocalization findings (Fig. 2), it could be posited that there is decreased systemic AVP release from the CNS in aging leading to upregulation of VR expression and compensatory pre-proAVP production in the urothelium. However, the temporal changes in systemic AVP were not investigated in this study and are a subject for further investigation.

Our studies in mice replicated the findings of increased osmolality of urine from bladder relative to renal pelvis in humans [9] as urine volume collected in the bladder over 2 h was ~20 % lower than urine simultaneously collected from the cut ureter (Fig. 5). The findings are consistent with reabsorption of solute-free water from urine stored in the bladder. Furthermore, intramuscular administration of fedovapagon significantly reduced urine production in kidneys (~74 % reduction in volume versus control conditions) and enhanced urine reabsorption from the bladder (~82 % reduction in volume versus control conditions), supporting a role for VR2 activity in both organs. Our findings are consistent with reports from preclinical [47] and clinical trials that showed a significant anti-diuretic effect peaking at 2 h after oral fedovapagon administration [36]. The present studies support fedovapagon action in bladder augmenting the well-studied anti-diuretic effect on the collecting ducts in kidney.

Unlike for dAVP, the decrease in baseline pressure as well as contractile activity in vivo (Fig. 6B) following intravesical administration of fedovapagon prompted isovolumetric cystometry with soybean oil, with negligible reabsorption, to demonstrate that the effect of fedovapagon on basal and peak bladder pressure was not solely due to an increase in water reabsorption, but also due to bladder relaxation (Fig. 6C). The effect of AVP on the detrusor has been described previously, with the predominant effect being a VR1 mediated contractile response [48]. There have been indications that dAVP may promote detrusor relaxation [8], however, clinically this has not been reported. The lack of effect of dAVP on bladder activity in patients could be due to its comparable affinity to VR2 and VR1b (EC50 24 nM versus 11 nM). If both receptors are activated equivalently by dAVP, it could be hypothesized that these conflicting pathways would result in no net effect. Although, fedovapagon is reported to be selective for VR2, its effects on VR1 do not appear to have been investigated. Although it is a limitation of this study, our experiments show that significant bladder relaxation induced by fedovapagon is most likely mediated by VR2 as it can be effectively blocked with lixivaptan (Fig. 6B). Overall, fedovapagon exhibits the anti-diuretic effects in kidneys with urine reabsorption in bladder as well as dampening bladder overactivity (Fig. 6E).

The relationship between morning urine osmolality and the urine production rate by the kidney at night is governed by an inverse relationship between bladder filling rate and reabsorption from bladder, previously established by direct radionuclide cystography [49]. Because the transcellular surface of umbrella cells forbids water influx, paracellular diffusion of water from the bladder lumen can occur only along ~27 % of the surface area of umbrella cell borders [5,18]. Therefore, the dogma of a “water-tight” bladder may be only valid in polyuria because solute-free water reabsorption from bladder raises the osmolality of stored urine, which gets more pronounced at lower filling rates [46] from the kidney in healthy individuals not loaded with water or suffering from polyuria. These findings also contest the belief of the kidney being the sole determinant of urine osmolality, which is also supported by a mounting body of clinical evidence that indicates the bladder is a critical site in the homeostasis of plasma osmolality and in raising osmolality of urine post-production in kidney.

The data presented support the presence of AVP and VRs in the mouse bladder to promote solute-free water reabsorption, and a dual-action therapeutic, fedovapagon, that relaxes the detrusor and abolishes bladder overactivity to reduce intravesical pressure and achieve multipronged action in treatment for nocturia without polypharmacy.
