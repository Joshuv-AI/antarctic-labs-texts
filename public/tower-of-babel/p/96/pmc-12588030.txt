Effects of footwear and insoles on balance and gait parameters in athletes with ankle injuries: protocol for a systematic review
Authors: Zeinab Gasavi Nezhad, Mokhtar Arazpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAnkle injuries are highly prevalent in athletes and often lead to chronic instability, impaired postural control and altered gait mechanics that compromise performance and delay return to sport. Although specialised footwear and insoles are widely used to enhance stability and normalise gait, their true efficacy in athletic populations remains uncertain, warranting a systematic synthesis of current evidence.Methods and analysisThis protocol follows Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols guidelines. We will include randomised, quasiexperimental and observational studies published up to 30 April 2025 that involve athletes with acute or chronic ankle injuries and evaluate footwear or insole interventions on balance and/or gait parameters. Five electronic databases (PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL) will be searched with tailored Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords. Two reviewers will independently screen records, extract data and assess methodological quality using the Effective Public Health Practice Project tool, which evaluates six domains: selection bias, study design, confounders, blinding, data collection methods and withdrawals/drop-outs, providing a global rating of strong, moderate or weak. Certainty of the body of evidence will be graded using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework. Results will be synthesised narratively, grouping findings by intervention type, outcome measure, sport and assessment method.Ethics and disseminationNo ethical approval is required as the review uses previously published data. Findings will be shared via peer-reviewed publication and scientific conferences.PROSPERO registration numberProspectively registered in PROSPERO under CRD420251031083.

Introduction

Ankle injuries are among the most prevalent musculoskeletal injuries in athletes, with epidemiological studies reporting incidence rates as high as 11.5–15.3 per 1000 athletic exposures in high-risk sports such as basketball, soccer and volleyball.1 These injuries predominantly result from high-impact movements, sudden directional changes or inadequate neuromuscular control, leading to acute ligament sprains, chronic ankle instability (CAI) and recurrent injuries.2 Up to 40% of athletes with ankle injuries develop CAI, which is characterised by persistent balance deficits, altered gait mechanics and prolonged recovery times.3 Such impairments hinder athletic performance and increase susceptibility to secondary injuries, compensatory movement patterns due to fatigue and delayed return to sport (RTS).4 Compromised postural control and asymmetrical gait further exacerbate functional limitations, underscoring the need for targeted interventions to address biomechanical and neuromuscular deficits. Footwear and insoles have emerged as critical tools for injury prevention and rehabilitation, offering mechanical support, proprioceptive enhancement and alignment correction to mitigate instability and optimise movement patterns.5 However, their efficacy in restoring balance, normalising gait parameters and facilitating RTS in athletes remains inadequately characterised, necessitating a systematic synthesis of evidence tailored to athletic populations.

Existing evidence highlights the role of footwear modifications, such as high-top shoes and motion-control designs, in reducing ankle inversion during dynamic tasks, while customised insoles improve plantar pressure distribution and arch support.6 Systematic reviews investigating footwear and insole interventions for ankle instability have reported heterogeneous outcomes, with a notable lack of focus on athletic populations. For instance, Khaliliyan et al demonstrated that textured insoles enhanced static balance in non-athletic individuals with CAI, yet the study excluded dynamic tasks critical to sports performance, such as jumping, cutting or rapid directional changes.7 Similarly, Tang et al observed moderate improvements in postural stability with insole use but acknowledged limitations in extrapolating these findings to high-intensity athletic activities.5 These gaps highlight the inadequate generalisability of existing evidence to sports contexts. Critically, Paradise et al conducted a meta-analysis of footwear interventions in military tactical athletes and found no prophylactic benefits of prescribed orthoses in preventing lower extremity injuries, despite modest reductions in injury recurrence. However, the authors cautioned that such findings may not apply to athletes requiring high-velocity agility manoeuvres or explosive movements.8 This aligns with the updated Cochrane review by Handoll et al, which underscored the scarcity of high-quality randomised trials evaluating footwear interventions specifically for athletes with CAI.9 Collectively, these studies emphasise a pivotal research gap: current evidence disproportionately prioritises non-athletic or mixed cohorts, neglecting the biomechanical complexities of sports involving dynamic stability during pivoting, landing or repetitive loading.

Objective and protocol rationale

The systematic review evaluates the effects of footwear and insoles on balance and gait parameters in athletes with ankle injuries. The primary objective is to synthesise existing evidence on the efficacy of these interventions in addressing sport-specific biomechanical challenges. Secondary objectives include identifying gaps in the literature and guiding future randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to optimise injury prevention and rehabilitation protocols. Focusing exclusively on athletic populations, this review clarifies the applicability of footwear and insole interventions in real-world sports contexts, where demands such as agility, speed and repetitive loading necessitate tailored solutions. The findings will inform evidence-based guidelines for clinicians and researchers, ultimately enhancing rehabilitation strategies and reducing the burden of ankle injuries in athletes.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review protocol has been developed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines to ensure methodological rigour, transparency and reproducibility. It was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251031083 prior to commencing the review process.

Project status and projected timeline key dates

This systematic review is currently in progress. The protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO on 30 April 2025. The comprehensive literature search across all specified databases was completed on 31 May 2025, followed by the completion of study selection, data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment. As of 10 September 2025, the review is in the final stage of data synthesis and evidence grading using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. The full systematic review manuscript is anticipated to be finalised and submitted for publication by 30 November 2025. All steps have been conducted in strict adherence to the preregistered protocol.

Eligibility criteria

The following eligibility criteria were established for the selection of studies to be included in this systematic review.

Study types

Inclusion: peer-reviewed primary research articles published up to 30 April 2025, irrespective of language. Eligible study designs include experimental studies (RCTs, quasiexperimental studies) and observational studies (eg, cross-sectional, cohort, case-control) that evaluate the effects of footwear or insoles on balance and gait parameters in athletes with ankle injuries.

Exclusion: conference abstracts, editorials, letters to the editor, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses and studies lacking full-text access or sufficient methodological details for quality assessment and data extraction will be excluded.

Participants

Inclusion: studies involving athletes (amateur or professional) of any age or sex with a history of ankle injuries, including acute sprains or CAI.

Exclusion: studies focusing primarily on non-athlete populations (eg, sedentary individuals, military personnel outside athletic contexts) or studies that do not explicitly report outcomes for the athletic subgroup will be excluded.

Intervention types

Inclusion: interventions involving modifications to footwear (eg, high-top shoes, motion-control shoes, minimalist footwear) or the use of different insole types (eg, customised orthotics, textured insoles, cushioned insoles) will be considered.

Exclusion: studies evaluating orthotic devices beyond footwear and insoles (eg, ankle braces, splints, taping techniques) will be excluded unless footwear or insoles are explicitly analysed as standalone interventions with separate outcome data.

Comparator types

Inclusion: studies employing the following comparisons will be included:

Intervention groups (eg, footwear/insole use) compared with control groups (eg, no intervention, placebo insoles, standard footwear).

Comparisons between different designs or types of footwear or insoles (eg, textured vs flat insoles, customised vs prefabricated orthotics).

Exclusion: studies comparing interventions unrelated to footwear or insoles (eg, physical therapy alone) will be excluded.

Outcome measures

Inclusion: studies that provide a quantitative assessment of at least one balance parameter (eg, postural stability, centre of pressure displacement, sway area/velocity, single-leg stance time, time-to-stabilisation) and/or at least one gait parameter (eg, stride length, step length, step width, gait speed, step symmetry, ground reaction forces (GRFs), cadence, kinematic gait analysis, eg, joint angles, range of motion).

Exclusion: studies focusing solely on anatomical, physiological or metabolic outcomes that are not directly related to balance or gait function (eg, muscle activation patterns without corresponding kinematic analysis, pain scores without functional gait outcomes) will be excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

To identify relevant studies, a systematic and exhaustive search will be conducted across five electronic databases (PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL) to capture peer-reviewed literature published up to 30 April 2025. The search strategy will integrate natural language terms, controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH) and Boolean operators (AND/OR/NOT) to optimise sensitivity and precision. Key search concepts will include:

Footwear/insoles: shoe, foot orthosis, custom insole, athletic footwear, textured insert.

Balance/gait: postural stability, gait analysis, stride parameters, GRF, dynamic balance.

Ankle injuries: ankle sprain, CAI, lateral ankle injury.

Athletes: athlete, sports performance, competitive sport, team sport.

Filters will exclude non-primary research (eg, reviews, editorials) and prioritise studies involving athletic populations (amateur/professional) with ankle injuries. No restrictions will be applied to language, publication year or geographic origin to minimise bias.

Study selection

All records retrieved from the database searches will first be imported into EndNote X9 for systematic reference management. Duplicate entries will be eliminated using the software’s automated deduplication function, and a subsequent manual check will be performed to confirm complete duplicate removal. The de-duplicated citations then will undergo a two-stage screening process, carried out independently by two reviewers (ZGN and MA) to ensure rigour and minimise bias.

Title and abstract screening

Each reviewer will independently assess the titles and abstracts of all unique records against the predefined eligibility criteria. Any study deemed potentially relevant by at least one reviewer will be advanced to the next stage.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies will be assessed using the Quality Assessment Tool for Quantitative Studies developed by the Effective Public Health Practice Project (EPHPP). This tool is particularly appropriate for the current review, as it allows for the evaluation of diverse quantitative study designs, including both experimental and observational methodologies.10 11

The EPHPP tool evaluates six key domains: selection bias, study design, confounders, blinding, data collection methods and withdrawals and drop-outs. Each domain will be rated as ‘strong,’ ‘moderate’ or ‘weak’ based on standardised criteria provided by the tool. An overall global rating will be assigned to each study, where a single ‘weak’ domain results in a ‘moderate’ global rating, and two or more ‘weak’ ratings lead to an overall ‘weak’ score.10 11

Two reviewers (ZGN and MA) will independently apply the EPHPP tool to all eligible studies. Any disagreements in scoring will be discussed until consensus is reached. Additionally, the initial inter-rater agreement rate—representing the percentage of items rated identically by both reviewers before discussion—will be calculated to help gauge the consistency and reliability of the assessment process.

Grading the quality of evidence

The certainty and strength of evidence for each prespecified outcome will be appraised using the GRADE framework. GRADE provides a transparent and reproducible method to judge the overall body of evidence by examining five key domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. Each outcome will be assigned one of four quality levels—high, moderate, low or very low—based on these domain assessments. By default, RCTs will start as high-quality evidence, whereas observational studies will begin at a lower tier; subsequent downgrading or upgrading will occur depending on identified study limitations or strengths.12 13

Two reviewers (ZGN and MA) will independently conduct the GRADE evaluations for all outcomes. Any discrepancies in their initial judgments will be reconciled through discussion until mutual agreement is achieved.

Synthesis of results

A narrative synthesis will be undertaken to integrate findings on the effects of footwear and insoles on balance and gait parameters in athletes with ankle injuries. Owing to anticipated heterogeneity in study designs, intervention characteristics, outcome metrics and sporting contexts, a formal meta-analysis will not be conducted. Instead, extracted results will be grouped according to intervention type (eg, cushioning vs stability-focused insoles), specific balance or gait outcomes assessed (such as centre-of-pressure sway or stride length), the athlete’s sport discipline and the measurement tools or protocols used. By organising the evidence in this way, the review will provide a structured, comprehensive narrative that highlights both consistencies and divergences across studies, thereby offering a clear overview of the existing research landscape.

Ethics and dissemination

This systematic review does not require ethical approval, as it relies on data from previously published studies and does not involve new data collection or direct interaction with human participants. The results will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed scientific journal and may also be presented at relevant conferences to share findings with researchers, clinicians and policymakers, promoting evidence-based practice.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in this study.

Amendments to the protocol

Any changes to the original protocol arising during the study due to unforeseen circumstances or new evidence will be fully documented, including the rationale and potential impact on study outcomes. These changes will be transparently reported in the final publication and updated in the study registration database.

Discussion

Ankle injuries are a frequent concern in athletes, often resulting in chronic instability and compromised performance. Footwear and insoles are commonly employed to address these issues, yet their specific effects on balance and gait parameters in athletic populations remain poorly understood. This systematic review protocol seeks to bridge this evidence gap by synthesising data tailored to athletes with ankle injuries. By adhering to PRISMA-P guidelines and employing a comprehensive search strategy, the review ensures a robust evaluation of the literature. However, challenges such as variability in study designs, intervention types and outcome measures may hinder data synthesis and the feasibility of a meta-analysis. Despite these limitations, the anticipated findings hold promise for informing clinical practice by pinpointing effective interventions to enhance rehabilitation and reduce injury recurrence. Additionally, the review will identify critical areas for future research, including the need for standardised outcome measures and sport-specific studies, thereby advancing evidence-based approaches in sports medicine.
