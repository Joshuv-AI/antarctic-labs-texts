Factors Affecting the Appropriate Angiography Prescription in Iranian Hospitals
Authors: Atieh Sadat Bigdeli, Leila Riahi, Fakhreddin Hejazi, Aniseh Nik Ravan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundInappropriate angiography prescriptions impose substantial financial and clinical burdens on healthcare systems. This study aimed to develop a comprehensive model for appropriate angiography prescription by examining supply‐and demand‐side factors in public and private hospitals in Iran.MethodsData were collected from domestic and international databases covering the period from 1998 to 2023 and analyzed through exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis.ResultsResults indicated that physician density and information asymmetry were the most influential factors in the public sector, while physician income and patient insistence dominated in the private sector. Patient preferences and intolerance of uncertainty were key demand‐side determinants.ConclusionsThe findings support policy interventions targeting behavioral and structural drivers of inappropriate prescriptions.Keywords: appropriate prescription, clinical and non‐clinical feature, Iran, private center, public center

Introduction

Healthcare expenditure growth over the past decade has been a significant concern for healthcare policymakers in Iran [1, 2]. Therefore, implementing cost‐mitigating measures, ensuring equitable access, and promoting cost‐effective and beneficial treatments are imperative as part of the objectives of universal health coverage [3, 4, 5]. Cardiovascular diseases are the most common cause of death in many countries, including Iran, and they constitute a major source of disability, incurring substantial costs for diagnosis and treatment [6, 7]. One of the medical technologies for cardiovascular diseases is the angiography device. Therefore, the analysis and careful utilization of diagnostic and therapeutic angiography are crucial for the patient's well‐being and the healthcare system [8].

Appropriate prescriptions are those that are made based on clinical guidelines and based on the patient's needs in order to improve the patient at the lowest cost. On the other hand, if the prescription is made based on the patient's insistence due to information asymmetry and the doctor's profiteering, it is considered an inappropriate prescription. Because it has the least effectiveness at the highest cost in the patient's treatment process [9].

There are two dimensions in the provision of healthcare services: supply and demand. One of the characteristics that distinguish the health sector from other economic sectors is that the doctor makes the demand on behalf of the patient, and since doctors are also suppliers, they lead to an opportunity for excessive demand from suppliers [9, 10]. In addition, in the health sector, the doctor has the ability to influence treatment decisions based on the dynamics of information power. Therefore, physicians may encourage patients to use healthcare services where the therapeutic value is less than the cost [11, 12]. Especially when patients are covered by supplementary insurance, doctors' income depends on the number of services they provide [13]. Under these conditions, doctors usually attract more patients with adequate insurance coverage, and insured patients are more likely to seek services [14]. Therefore, evidence‐based guidelines provided by medical associations can lead to systematic guidance for patients and physicians, facilitating better‐informed decision‐making and shaping healthcare demand. In Iran, the above reasons are very effective in creating disproportionate prescriptions. Therefore, creating and presenting applicable clinical guidelines is very helpful [15, 16].

In addition, the study of doctor's behavior plays a fundamental role in healthcare demand. Significant factors on doctor behavior, patients with different initial health conditions, uncertainty about treatment effects, and unawareness of patient preferences [17, 18]. Therefore, examining the patient's non‐clinical characteristics increases the patient's tolerance of uncertainty and preferences and prevents excessive and disproportionate service provision [19, 20, 21]. It is worth mentioning that patients depend on the provider to communicate and obtain the patients' consent to accept the opinion and choose the treatment options, which the doctor does not receive special attention in practice in Iran [18].

One of the prominent features of health care services in supply is that according to the laws, free access to service providers, especially in the public sector, is impossible. This, in turn, increases the cost of services [22] because doctors tend to provide more services to compensate for their violated rights or direct dependencies on other providers, such as equipment companies, pharmacies, and paraclinical services, which leads to an increase in marginal costs and discrimination in providing services with different prices. Furthermore, the physician‐to‐population ratio is one of the most crucial factors in increasing healthcare supply. Since the tariff for services in the public sector is fixed, and the volume of service provision is limited, physicians forge inappropriate and unnecessary relationships with their patients to maintain a competitive environment and enhance their benefits [23].

Due to the acceptance of the physician's recommendations, and the lack of comprehensive consumer information about treatment options, unnecessary prescriptions have become an increasingly attractive option for physicians. These incentives are especially pronounced in private organizations with fewer constraints on physicians [18]. In reality, on the one hand, due to the uncertain income level of physicians at the start of their careers and, on the other hand, the freedom of physicians in providing services and the freedom of patients to choose services, especially in private centers, an overload of patients beyond the capacity of physicians is expected to be observed, leading to an anticipated insufficiency of time for effective diagnosis. These factors significantly influence inappropriate prescriptions [24, 25].

Therefore, there is a need for solutions to control unnecessary services by adopting evidence‐based scientific methods, developing and issuing standards and regulations, designing frameworks for reasonable, efficient, and scientific treatment, and allocating resources efficiently [26].

Given the importance of providing appropriate services for the treatment of cardiovascular disorders to reduce the burden on the healthcare system and the existence of uncertainty in medicine despite the presence of clinical guidelines and the non‐enforcement of medical laws, this study examined the dimensions related to appropriate angiography prescriptions and determined the variables affecting their supply and demand in public and private hospitals. By using the results of this study, it is possible to determine the factors and appropriate rates of angiography prescriptions in public and private hospitals in Iran based on non‐clinical (patient preferences and tolerance of uncertainty) and clinical (clinical guidelines) characteristics. In this way, it is possible to attract the attention of policymakers and insurance organization officials to the provision of inappropriate services and help them control the costs arising from this problem.

Considering that this study is a original article, in order to investigate and study more precisely to determine the variables affecting the appropriate prescriptions and design its model, Iran and the countries of United States, Switzerland, Brazil, Italy, Japan, Spain, Australia, Netherlands, Canada, Sweden, and China were chosen as a comparative paradigm. Because according to the World Health Organization, these countries were introduced as 11 advanced countries in medicine with the best health care in the world. in which the indicators of quality, efficiency, access to care and health index score were considered. Since these indicators affect the provision of appropriate prescriptions and quality services, these countries were chosen as comparative paradigms.

Methods

Evidence Acquisition

In this study, the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews statement was used as a guideline.

Data Sources

To find studies published between 1998 and 2023, we retrieved articles published in foreign and domestic journals, as well as those available in Persian databases including IranMedex, MagIran, Iran Doc, SID (Scientific Information Database), Medlib, and English databases including PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, Web of Knowledge, Cochran database, and search engines of Google and Google Scholar. The search strategy included the use of Persian and English keywords and a combination of them. Also, the reference lists of published studies were examined to choose further studies.

Study Selection

The keywords used in this search include Medical Subject Headings and common keywords related to the subject, such as appropriate prescribing, uncertainty, factors affecting supply‐side prescriptions, patient preferences, clinical guidelines, factors affecting demand‐side prescriptions, insurance coverage, data asymmetry, prescriptions in healthcare settings, angiography, physician payment system, supply and demand barriers to healthcare services, standard health services, appropriate health services, access to healthcare services, health service tariffs, clinical skill deficiency and medical uncertainty. These terms were organized into two categories: supply and demand components. Subsequently, the complete texts of all located articles and documents relating to these components were extracted. After reviewing the article titles, duplicate and irrelevant articles were eliminated. A thorough examination of study texts ensued, wherein studies were chosen based on supply and demand components and pertinent study objectives.

Entry and Exit Criteria

Entry criteria for inclusion in the study include relevance to the subject in Persian and English from 1998 to 2023. Given that the author of this study is fluent in both languages and that many studies have been conducted to determine the variables affecting appropriate prescriptions during this time period, these limitations were considered for the informant. Then, article titles and abstracts were screened based on entry and exit criteria. Thus, 250 studies in the demand component and 178 studies in the supply component were selected after reviewing for duplicates, compliance with entry and exit criteria, and initial screening of all searched studies separately, including information such as year, country, and subject. Finally, after reviewing the study year, subject, and entry and exit criteria, 64 studies in the demand component and 35 studies in the supply component were selected. These selected studies were further organized based on year, country, author names, methodology, and conclusions (Figure 1).

Risk of Bias in Individual Studies

The author and supervisor independently analyzed the methodological quality of the selected studies. In cases of disagreement, consultations were held with two advisors in the clinical (Hejazi) and health economics (Nikravan) domains for decision‐making and necessary guidance. For qualitative studies, we used Spencer's tool and for quantitative studies, Mitton's checklist.

Results

Between 1998 and 2023, 35 articles on the supply side and 64 on the demand side were published to identify influential factors in appropriate angiography prescriptions in Iran and other countries based on the mentioned applied paradigm (Figure 1). Of this, on the supply side, 4 were qualitative studies, 5 were quantitative studies, 3 were quantitative‐qualitative studies, 14 were descriptive‐analytical studies, and 9 were descriptive studies. The largest number of articles in the presentation section belonged to descriptive‐analytical and descriptive articles, respectively and the smallest belonged to qualitative‐quantitative articles (Figure 2). On the demand side, 5 were qualitative studies, 8 were quantitative‐qualitative studies, 20 descriptive‐analytical studies, and 31 descriptive studies. The largest number of articles in the demand section belonged to descriptive and descriptive‐analytical articles, respectively, and the smallest belonged to quantitative articles with 0%. Since demand factors are mostly extracted qualitatively and descriptively from the clinical and non‐clinical conditions of the patient in various studies, therefore, no quantitative study was found in this regard (Figure 3).

According to these studies, various factors in both the supply and demand aspects can influence appropriate angiography prescriptions (Figure 4). The most significant factors in the supply aspect include inadequate skills in diagnosis, increased legal pursuits under the Patients' Rights Charter, physician employment type, insurance type (supplementary insurance), data asymmetry, physician demographic information, clinical uncertainty, emotional intelligence, and risk‐taking, physician payment type, visit duration, service prices, physician density, and competition among physicians, varied pricing across different demographic groups, and patient insistence. The most significant factors in the demand aspect (patients) are divided into clinical and non‐clinical characteristics. In the clinical characteristics, clinical guidelines, and non‐clinical characteristics, patient preferences, tolerance of patient uncertainty, supplementary insurance coverage, data asymmetry, and quality of life related to the health of heart patients have been considered. This study used an expert consensus method to prioritize the factors affecting appropriate prescriptions. In the supply dimension, in the public sector, the factors of information asymmetry, physician density, and insurance coverage were more important, and in the private sector, physician income and patient insistence were more important, and in the demand dimension, uncertainty tolerance, and patient preferences were more important in both sectors.

Discussion

The present study examined the factors influencing appropriate angiography prescriptions within the Iranian healthcare system. Sixty‐four articles related to the demand component and 35 published articles related to the supply component were identified about the factors affecting appropriate prescription in Iran. The results of these studies demonstrate that various factors in supply and demand dimensions, considering patients' clinical and non‐clinical characteristics, impact appropriate prescriptions. Other studies also support the existence of certain factors that affect unnecessary prescriptions [19, 23, 27, 28]. Based on the results obtained from the summary table of the reviewed articles, there were differences and similarities between the selected countries and Iran in determining the factors affecting appropriate prescriptions, separated into two parts: supply and demand. On the supply side, except for risk‐taking and physician emotional intelligence, and visit duration, most of the factors affecting appropriate prescriptions were common in the various studies. However, more differences were observed on the demand side. In addition, paying attention to patient preference factors and patient intolerance of uncertainty were fundamental factors that have not been addressed in Iran.

In previous studies conducted in Iran, the factors affecting the supply side were categorized into three main components: situational, institutional, structural, and organizational factors. On the demand side, ethical considerations and insurance coverage emerged as key influencers. Due to the objectives of these studies, authors have explored the factors affecting prescriptions differently or conducted interviews with physicians. They have also investigated issues arising from unnecessary prescriptions in the Iranian healthcare system [10, 11, 29, 30]. In the study by Sedghi et al., more than one‐third of patients with coronary artery disease had no significant illness, and more than half of the patients had not undergone non‐invasive tests before angiography, receiving inappropriate angiography services [31]. Therefore, attention should be paid to information asymmetry and increasing the patient's share of insurance coverage relative to other factors in developed countries.

The issue of inappropriate prescriptions poses a challenge for other developing countries [32, 33] as well as developed countries [34, 35]. For example, a study by Seyedin classified the factors affecting unnecessary prescriptions into four levels: the high level (Ministry of Health), the macro level (universities), the intermediate level (service providers) and the micro level (patients). Therefore, health managers should design appropriate strategies, such as evidence‐based approaches to purchasing services from insurers and the approval of clinical guidelines and laws to reduce unnecessary prescriptions [30].

The present study strongly emphasizes the need to enforce laws and promote a cultural shift regarding clinical guidelines. This is based on evidence from various studies [15, 29]. The study conducted by Keyvanara et al. suggests that a lack of adequate supervision of physicians could facilitate the issuance of unnecessary prescriptions [11]. This situation arises because physicians serve in dual roles as supervisors and decision‐makers, thereby causing their interests to be prioritized over those of the patients [11, 30]. Since weak regulations and improper implementation of healthcare policies are effective factors in inappropriate and unnecessary prescriptions, the mandatory implementation of clinical guidelines by the Ministry of Health not only reduces physician confusion in prescriptions but also decreases the prevalence of inappropriate prescriptions and the financial burden they impose on healthcare services [29]. Therefore, according to the evidence of various studies between Iran and developed countries, the use, design, and localization of clinical guidelines have received less attention and should be prioritized in health system planning.

Based on this study, the factors influencing appropriate prescriptions encompass supply and demand components. In the demand dimension, attention should be paid to patient preferences, patient uncertainty in decision‐making, clinical guidelines, and supplementary insurance. In the supply dimension, demographic information of the physician, patient insistence, information asymmetry, service tariffs, and insufficient clinical certainty should be considered. The contribution of these factors to appropriate prescriptions in government and private healthcare centers will vary depending on changes in tariff rates, patient access, and physician autonomy [23, 36, 37, 38]. Therefore, paying attention to service tariffs, financial incentives for doctors, and the type of payment to them is very important to control prescriptions in Iran. For example, the type of payment based on the number of services provided to the patient should be changed to a quality‐oriented or Diagnosis‐Related Group method, and this issue has been addressed less in the Iranian health system compared to developed countries.

In the context of health promotion and the provision of personalized services, Rafieifar expressed that patient education on access, improved interpersonal and group interactions in clinical situations, and the acquisition of necessary skills, as well as quantitative and qualitative assessments in the treatment and prevention process, evidence‐based decision‐making, and the use of clinical guidelines in the treatment process, can all be effective in making decisions in complex situations. These findings align with the objectives of the present study [39, 40].

This study's evidence confirms a relationship between the type of insurance contract with medical‐diagnostic centers in the private and public sectors and inappropriate prescriptions. The study by Jahanmehr reveals that most inappropriate prescriptions in the public sector are associated with the social security insurance group. In contrast, in the private sector they are linked to other insurance providers. Due to the significance of supplementary insurance in patient selection and physician prescriptions, this aspect was also investigated. The study's evidence indicated that most inappropriate prescriptions across both public and private sectors were associated with supplementary insurance [29]. This highlights the need for policymakers to pay greater attention to the ethical risks arising from supplementary insurance [36, 41]. Furthermore, creating necessary educational conditions and establishing monitoring units for physicians' prescriptions using clinical guidelines by insurance organizations can reduce costs incurred by the insurance organization and the patients [20].

Multiple studies indicate that there is a connection between a patient's tolerance level and the appropriate prescriptions for angiography [18, 23, 42, 43, 44, 45]. In the study by Johan, the objective was to more comprehensively identify variables that influence the patient's tolerance of uncertainty. The following key factors were recognized: utilization of services, different therapeutic outcomes, alternative treatments, and inappropriate patient decisions, varying costs of services insurance premiums, types of reimbursements, and consumer participation, and physician's predictive factors in decision‐making [46, 47, 48].

In this study, the importance of addressing patient preferences in the context of the demand for appropriate prescriptions is emphasized, supported by evidence from various studies [49, 50, 51]. A study conducted by Tanya found that the patient's risk attitude and the physician's perspective are essential in assessing patient preferences. In assessing the patient's risk attitude, components related to uncertainty tolerance are evaluated through cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions. Therefore, considering patient preferences should be a priority for physicians to enhance patient quality and satisfaction when prescribing appropriate treatments [52].

Multiple studies point to the relationship between patient demographic information, physicians, and physician reimbursement for unnecessary prescriptions [15, 25, 29, 30]. Ali Akbari Sari et al. also stated in their book on healthcare economics and healthcare services that physician density, the type of physician liability insurance, the type of patient insurance, the location of physician education, the place of residence (urban‐rural), the type of physician payments, the type and amount of insurance organization payments to patients, and franchise as the patient's share in service provision are among the factors that affect the provision of unnecessary services [9, 46, 47]. In the research conducted by Mohammad Shahi et al., factors that influence physicians' prescription of unnecessary angiographies were identified to include the age and health status of the patient within the supply‐side components. The physician's age, contract type, physician‐to‐population ratio, financial incentives, and ease of patient access were also highlighted as contributing factors. Therefore, these factors should be considered in healthcare service policy, aligning with our study results [10].

Keyvanara et al. contend that one of the reasons for providing non‐essential medical services is ineffective educational patterns, leading to a lack of sufficient skill and capacity for diagnosing patient issues and diseases [11]. Therefore, in this study, considering the existence of ample evidence in various studies [10, 11, 15] the insufficient skill of the physician has been placed as one of the effective factors in inappropriate prescriptions on the supply side.

Patients' ignorance and excessive trust in their physicians can lead them to follow their instructions carefully, even if the recommended services are unnecessary. As a result, this can lead to them receiving services that may not be helpful for their condition. Becker et al. have shown that when decision‐making is entrusted to physicians by patients, it may lead to supplier‐induced demand [33]. On the other hand, supplier‐induced demand can lead to the misallocation of resources, ultimately leading to the consumption of these resources by patients who do not really need them, instead of allocating them to those who are in actual need. Induced demand of physician can also disrupt the efficient allocation of national resources, even if patients bear the service cost. Fabbri et al. demonstrated that induced demand of physician affects competitive services and resource allocation [52].

Conclusions

This study develops determining the factors influencing for appropriate angiography prescriptions in private and public centers in Iran. The healthcare system should minimize the amount and proportion of inappropriate and unnecessary care in both the public and private sectors. In addition, it should emphasize the use of appropriate and high‐quality care. This study examines the effective factors in appropriate angiography prescriptions in two dimensions: supply and demand. Factors such as insufficient confidence, physician demographic information, information asymmetry, physician reimbursements, service tariffs, physician density, insurance coverage, patient insistence, and physician risk attitude are considered in the supply dimension. On the demand side, considerations focus on the clinical characteristics of the patient, clinical guidelines, and for non‐clinical characteristics of the patient, factors such as the patient's demographic information, disease conditions, tolerance for uncertainty, patient preferences, information asymmetry between patient and physician, and health‐related quality of life are considered. One of the strengths of this study is the investigation of the factors affecting the appropriate prescriptions by considering the clinical and non‐clinical characteristics of the patient compared to other studies. It distinguishes the components affecting appropriate prescriptions, examines the factors influencing suitable prescriptions while considering the patient's clinical and non‐clinical characteristics, compared to other studies. Determining the effective factors for appropriate angiography prescriptions can control unnecessary costs.

Consequently, the findings of this study can attract the attention of policymakers, administrators of both private and public healthcare centers, and insurance organizations within the Iranian healthcare system, specifically concerning angiography services. Indeed, promoting a culture for designing and using clinical guidelines to standardize patient clinical characteristics in prescriptions can be beneficial. Additionally, informing patients about payment options and involving them in treatment choices can significantly impact them. This is especially true when considering non‐clinical characteristics such as patient preferences and tolerance for uncertainty that these aspects have not been adequately addressed in previous studies.
