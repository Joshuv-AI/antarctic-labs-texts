Design and blast mitigation of solid pyramid structure for personnel
Authors: Shufan Lu, Yuan Li, Tao Suo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Blasting shock wave poses significant threats to human Lives, making blast mitigation an important research subject. This study introduces a solid pyramid structure, Derived from a V-shaped Design, aimed at protecting personnel from shock waves. Various pyramid specimens with different heights and surface configurations were designed and fabricated using 3D printing. The explosion mitigation capabilities of these structures were assessed through shock tube testing. Results demonstrate that the solid pyramid structure effectively protects the human body under blast loading. The mitigation effect increases with pyramid height, and concave pyramids outperform convex and flat designs. Numerical simulations further explored the contributions of various factors to blast mitigation, elucidating the underlying mechanisms. These findings provide essential references for advancing personnel blast mitigation research and the development of related protective equipment.

Introduction

An explosion is characterized by the rapid release of substantial physical and chemical energy within a brief period. During an explosion, the pressure, density, velocity, and other properties of the surrounding medium change rapidly. Rapid state changes, especially abrupt pressure variations, are highly destructive and pose significant threats to surrounding structures and personnel1,2. When personnel are near the explosion center, the increased loads on the human body typically result in immediate fatality. Conversely, at greater distances, shock waves can cause blood vessel rupture, muscle fiber tearing, eardrum rupture3, pulmonary hemorrhage, and edema. Consequently, effectively protecting personnel from explosion threats remains a critical research focus.

Compared to structural explosion protection, research on personnel protection is relatively insufficient, primarily due to the harsh conditions inherent in explosion protection studies. Current explosion research primarily employs three methods: theoretical analysis, experimental testing, and numerical simulation. The explosion process involves material reactions, phase changes, significant deformations, fractures, and scattering, complicating the construction of mathematical models. Among these, experimental studies are the most convincing and widely accepted. However, explosion tests are extremely short in duration, with normal shock wave positive pressure lasting only tenths to a few milliseconds. Additionally, the explosion field exhibits high temperatures and pressures, leading to easy damage of surrounding objects. For instance, a citywide explosion can obliterate a building instantaneously4. This presents significant challenges for testing methodologies and leads to elevated costs. Therefore, there is a critical need to perform explosion shock wave simulations utilizing shock tubes alongside corresponding numerical models.

Arumugam and Mu employed shock tubes to simulate blast shock waves, successfully achieving the intended effect5,6. Secondly, regarding personnel protection, human skin and other organs are relatively soft and have low tolerance to shock waves. Defensive structures are unable to adequately support the necessary deformation and energy absorption, and individuals are vulnerable to force transmission from the rear of the protective structure. While structural protection generally requires controlling substantial back plate deformations within specific limits, personnel protection demands not only minimal back plate deformation but also the elimination of significant force transmission. Furthermore, considering wearability for personnel, protective structures must be both lightweight and flexible. For example, Liu’s liquid-filled tube design offers flexible protection against shock waves7. Current personnel protective equipment frequently incorporates flexible polyurethane foam, such as foam linings in helmets, as energy-absorbing elements8. However, numerous studies have demonstrated that foam liners amplify pressure rather than cause its decay when subjected to specific blast shock wave loadings9,10. Consequently, it is essential to address personnel anti-explosion protection concerns and perform adaptable shock wave protection structure design and performance evaluations using a combination of shock tube testing and numerical simulations.

The surface geometry of the shock wave protection structure directly affects airflow following shock wave disturbance and the characteristics of the reflected wave. Specialized shape designs can significantly modify the airflow velocity and pressure on the protective structure’s surface. Currently, many researchers have proposed various shock wave protection structures by altering surface shapes for enhanced protection. Among these, the V-shaped structure is exemplary and has yielded substantial research findings. Yuen11 investigated V-shaped plate shock wave protection using experimental and numerical simulation methods. The results indicate that reducing the V-shaped angle lessens shock wave reflection and decreases the maximum deflection at the midpoint of the V-shaped plate. Trajkovski12 and Konarzewski13 examined the dynamic response of V-shaped and curved plates under shock wave loading using numerical simulations. The findings reveal that the conclusions for V-shaped plates align with Yuen’s, and for curved plates, reduced curvature results in smaller deflections. V-shaped plates demonstrate significantly superior shock wave protection compared to curved plates. Gurumurthy14 examined the shock wave loading conditions of structures with identical projected loaded areas but differing surface geometries using numerical simulations. The results indicate that V-shaped structures sustain minimal load along the direction of shock wave propagation, whereas concave arc surfaces bear the least load perpendicular to the shock wave’s path. Yuan15 developed a double-V-shaped sandwich panel, achieving enhanced blast protection through the combined effects of the V-shaped structure’s energy dispersion and the sandwich core’s energy absorption. In addition to single V structures, multiple V configurations were also investigated.

Hajek16 conducted explosion tests to evaluate the efficacy of pyramid-shaped sheeting panels attached to barriers for shock wave protection. The test outcomes reveal that pyramid protective layers made from both concrete and sound-absorbing materials offer protection compared to smooth barriers. Stanisławek17 employed numerical modeling to study the loading conditions of V-shaped walls under shock wave impact. The findings show that, given the same total base area, slope inclination angle, and loading intensity, both single and multiple V structures attenuate shock waves more effectively than flat walls. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that V-shaped structures provide enhanced shock wave protection and present a promising design for personnel safety by effectively reducing the intensity of reflected shock waves. Currently, few studies explore the use of V-shaped structures for personnel protection, and their blast performance in explosion scenarios remains inadequately investigated.

This study assesses the potential application of V-shaped structures in personal explosion protection by fabricating them with 3D-printed polylactic acid (PLA) and evaluating their shock wave resistance using shock tubes and biological simulation targets. The effects of V-shaped structural parameters, including angle size and face shape, on protective efficacy are examined, and the underlying protection mechanisms are delineated. The findings provide significant insights for enhancing personnel anti-explosion protection measures.

Research methods

Specimen design and manufacturing

This study Designed various triangular pyramid structures, 3D printed using PLA. The solid pyramid design is depicted in Fig. 1, consisting of a cylindrical base with a 95 mm Diameter and 15mm height, and six triangular pyramids arranged around the base. The upper triangular pyramids have equilateral triangle bases with side lengths equal to half the base Diameter, with minor Differences in their upper parts. The upper parts of the planar pyramid structures are all triangular pyramids. Three planar pyramid structures with heights of 10, 20, and 30 mm are presented. The outer surfaces of the 30-mm-high concave and convex pyramids feature a curved radius of 43 mm.

Fig. 1Designed pyramid structures.

Designed pyramid structures.

Upon completing the design, pyramid structures were fabricated. Research indicates that increased stiffness in V-shaped structures enhances their effectiveness in deflecting shock waves18. To minimize deformation of the solid pyramid structures under load while addressing the requirements for lightweight construction, human body support strength, and comfort in personnel protection, fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing technology was employed to fabricate specimens using PLA material with moderate stiffness. PLA is a thermoplastic polymer commonly utilized in FDM 3D printing. It offers advantages such as reduced processing complexity and superior finished product quality. FDM technology melts polymer filament at elevated temperatures before cooling it to a solid state, enabling filament Deposition on the printing platform for additive manufacturing. When fabricating solid pyramid specimens, the model is first imported into the 3D printing slicing software ideaMaker. After cleaning and adjusting the model geometry, the model was sliced, as illustrated in Fig. 2, and subsequently imported into the Raise3D Plus3 printer for processing. During the 3D printing of a solid pyramid sample, appropriate settings for the number of upper and lower surface layers, shell layers, and temperature are configured to ensure that the interior of the sample is completely filled. All solid pyramid structures have identical internal fillings and densities, differing only in geometric shape and quality.

Fig. 2Model slicing before the 3D printing.

Model slicing before the 3D printing.

After completing 3D printing and removing the solid pyramid blank, the sample was polished with files and sandpaper to remove surface burrs and enhance smoothness. The polished specimen was then coated multiple times with a polyurethane topcoat to mask and eliminate surface defects, thereby reducing air friction resistance under shock wave loading. Shock tube experiment

The shock tube test system consists of four components: shock tube, test section, control system, and data acquisition system, as illustrated in Fig. 4. The primary component of the shock tube used in the test is a standard equal-section pipe, as shown in Fig. 5. One end serves as the driving section with a closed terminal, while the opposite end functions as the driven section with an open terminal. The sections are connected and separated by a diaphragm. The test section is positioned at the opening of the driven section, and system sensors are connected externally. The control system regulates the filling and discharge of gas into the driving section of the shock tube.

Fig. 4Schematic diagram of shock tube test system.

Schematic diagram of shock tube test system.

Fig. 5Photograph of shock tube test system.

Photograph of shock tube test system.

During testing, a diaphragm was installed between the driving and driven sections and secured with bolts to ensure airtightness. Subsequently, the valve connecting the high-pressure gas tank to the driving section was opened. When the pressure differential between the driving and driven sections reached the critical threshold for diaphragm rupture, the diaphragm failed, enabling flow within the tube and thereby generating a shock wave that propagated into the driven section. As the shock wave moved forward, it reached the sample’s surface, reflected, and completed the sample’s loading. To simulate the human body’s exposure to shock waves, a silicon rubber barrel was employed as a protective target, as illustrated in Fig. 6. The silicon rubber barrel consists of an internal cylindrical silicon rubber block and a nylon outer barrel. It was secured to the shock tube muzzle and the test section platform via front and rear flanges.

Fig. 6Silicon rubber target.

Silicon rubber target.

Bolts were used during the test to maintain the relative positions between the shock tube Muzzle and the silicon rubber target body. The Distance from the shock tube Muzzle to the silicon rubber target flange is 55mm. The silicon rubber block within the target body is a cylinder with a Diameter of 100mm and an overall length of approximately 300 mm. The protruding flange section measures about 10 mm in length. A sensor is installed on the nylon barrel wall approximately 25 mm from the flange. During the experiments, specimen arrangement is shown in Fig. 7. A solid pyramid structure was placed in front of the protected silicon rubber target, oriented with its apex facing the shock tube muzzle. The pyramid’s base was securely attached to the target’s front surface using double-sided tape over a substantial area. Shock tube tests were conducted at two loading intensities: low and high.

For the low intensity, a 0.2 mm Mylar film was positioned in the clamping section, and the Driving section was pressurized until the film ruptured, resulting in approximately 100kPa incident overpressure. For the high intensity, three 0.2 mm Diaphragms were installed in the clamping section, producing an incident overpressure of around 200kPa. Throughout the tests, two pressure-time history signals were recorded from the shock tube wall pressure sensor (L1 in Fig. 4) and the silicon rubber target side wall pressure sensor (L5 in Fig. 4). The shock tube wall sensor primarily tracked the incident pressure to ensure uniform load input, while the silicon rubber target side wall sensor captured internal pressure signals to evaluate the shock wave attenuation provided by different protective structures. In the experiment, two loading intensities and five solid pyramid specimens were utilized, creating ten distinct working condition groups. Each condition was tested multiple times to ensure reliability and repeatability.

A comparative analysis was conducted between loading scenarios with and without the protective structure applied to the target body to assess the protective efficacy. The protective effect was evaluated using two indices: peak overpressure and specific impulse. Peak overpressure denotes the maximum pressure observed on the pressure-time history curve, indicating the highest load recorded by the sensor during loading. Specific impulse, defined as the integral of overpressure over time on the pressure-time history curve, was used to analyze the momentum transfer of the shock wave during the loading of a specimen and target.

Numerical modeling was initially conducted under unprotected working conditions. The flow field, silicon rubber block, and nylon outer wall were modeled using a quarter model to reduce computational cost, as depicted in Fig. 9. To reduce grid quantity and mitigate boundary reflection effects on calculation results, the flow field was extended 100 mm forward to the incident wave sensor on the shock tube wall and 100 mm outward to the flange’s outer edge, accommodating a specimen installation area of 55 mm in length and 50 mm in radius. A mesh convergence study Determined the modeling grid size to be 1mm. A fixed boundary condition was applied to the shock tube wall due to the absence of flow. The symmetry plane was assigned a symmetry boundary condition. Fixed boundary conditions were also applied to the nylon barrel at the mounting flange. Shock wave loading was simulated by applying PIF flow boundary conditions to the air domain’s front surface. In addition, non-reflective boundary conditions (NRB) were implemented on other regions These NRB conditions are designed to absorb outgoing pressure waves, minimizing non-physical reflections that could corrupt the solution and ensuring that the wave propagation behavior within the domain of interest remains accurate.

Utilizing the ALE unit, the flow field was defined as air material, with the ideal gas equation of state characterizing the mechanical behavior of air at the PIF flow boundary. Relevant material parameters were sourced from reference19 and detailed in Tables 1 and 2. The remaining air material was modeled using a Linear Polynomial equation of state, with corresponding material parameters obtained from20 and listed in Table 3. The adopted air EOS parameters are consistent with those commonly used in various studies. Both the silicon rubber block and nylon barrel were modeled using Lagrange elements.

Silicon rubber demonstrates superelastic behavior under loading, modeled using the Mooney-Rivlin constitutive model21. The experimentally obtained material parameters are listed in Table 4. Since the shock tube test primarily involves low strain rate compression, static compression tests were conducted on the silicon rubber specimens to fit these parameters. Figure 10 displays the compression specimen and its resulting stress–strain curve. The load-bearing behavior of the nylon barrel was characterized with the Piecewise Linear Plasticity constitutive model, using material parameters from references22,23 and detailed in Table 5. Identical nylon material and dynamic impact conditions as in references22,23 were employed. The constrained-Lagrange-in-solid keyword established fluid–solid coupling between the air domain and the silicon rubber barrel, incorporating contact and friction between the nylon barrel and the side surface of the silicon rubber block.

Compared with the experimental specimens, there is an additional pyramid specimen with a height of 15 mm, recorded as a working condition PLA15_0.5, which is based only on numerical simulation results. All solid pyramid structures exhibit a degree of shock wave protection capabilities. Under consistent pyramid angles, factors such as pyramid height, surface shape, and base length impact the effectiveness of shock wave mitigation. Taking PLA30 as an example, Fig. 14 presents the overpressure time-history curve measured within the silicone rubber target when protected by the PLA30 specimen. Test results confirm that the pyramid structure attenuates shock waves under both low- and high-intensity conditions (PLA30-L and PLA30-H). Notably, the pressure curve for PLA30-H remains consistently below that of the unprotected reference group (REF-H). Moreover, both the peak overpressure and specific impulse attenuation rates under PLA30-H exceed those under PLA30-L, with a particularly significant improvement in specific impulse reduction.

Practical implications for personnel safety

The practical value of the pyramid structures lies in its ability to mitigate blast threats to survivable levels. Herein, we contextualize our experimental results using well-established injury thresholds for blast exposure from Bowen26 and Greer27. As shown in Table 10, the threshold for eardrum rupture is 34 kPa, while the onset of severe lung Damage occurs at approximately 240kPa. The threshold for 50% mortality is reached at about 304 ~ 372 kPa. Under high-loading conditions (Table 9), the unprotected reference case (REF-H) experienced a overpressure of 309.5 kPa. This value far exceeds the threshold for severe lung damage (240 kPa) and approaches the regime of high mortality risk, indicating an unsurvivable scenario for personnel. Crucially, all tested pyramid structures provided significant mitigation. The best-performing design, PLA30CONCAVE-H, reduced the transmitted overpressure to 198.2 kPa. This value falls below the critical threshold for severe lung damage (206.92 kPa). This demonstrates the structure’s potential to transform a lethal scenario into a survivable one, primarily by preventing catastrophic internal organ injury. It is important to note that this pressure, while non-lethal, still carries a significant risk of eardrum rupture and may cause less severe lung contusions.

The specific impulse measured in the unprotected reference condition (REF-H) was 1097 Pa·s (Table 9). This value far exceeds the recognized for a high probability of severe lung injury (600 Pa·s)26, indicating a scenario that would almost certainly be fatal due to pervasive pulmonary contusion and hemorrhage. The protective structure (PLA20-H) successfully reduced the specific impulse to 841 Pa·s. This suggests that the design significantly improves survivability compared to the unprotected case.

These findings highlight the efficacy of pyramid structures as a promising strategy for enhancing personnel safety against blast threats. Future work will focus on further optimizing the design to also mitigate the risk of eardrum rupture and on validating these results under more complex blast conditions.

Protection mechanism

This section explores the protective mechanisms of various pyramid structures concerning flow velocity and muzzle distance and elucidates the reasons for impulse attenuation.

Flow velocity

Low loading condition

Under low loading conditions, overpressure peak attenuation ranks as follows: PLA30 > PLA30CONCAVE > PLA30CONVEX > PLA20 > PLA15_0.5 > PLA10 > REF. This trend is attributed to the flow velocity on the pyramid surfaces. Figure 27 illustrates the flow velocity distribution on each solid pyramid surface under low loading intensity. Compared to the reference group, all solid pyramid structures show a moderate increase in flow velocity on their outer surfaces. Under the PLA30-L condition, flow velocity increases on the outer pyramid surface, with the high-velocity zone extending from the expansion wave at the shock tube muzzle toward the outer surface. In contrast, flow remains slower between the pyramids. Compared to the reference group, the low-velocity region broadened and extended toward the shock tube muzzle. PLA10-L and PLA15_0.5-L exhibit lower outer surface flow velocities than PLA30-L. For PLA10-L, the smaller pyramid angle causes stronger shock reflection, reducing flow velocity. For PLA15_0.5-L, the smaller outer surface area similarly leads to lower flow speeds. The axial range of the low-speed zone for these two conditions was significantly smaller than that under the PLA30-L condition, correlating with the pyramid height of the structure.

High loading condition

Under high loading conditions, the order of PLA30CONCAVE and PLA30 reversed. This reversal may result from complex flow field variations and can be explained by the surface flow velocity, with PLA30CONCAVE exhibiting higher surface flow velocity than PLA30. Overall, the increase in surface flow velocity is crucial for shock wave attenuation. When a protective structure is subjected to shock waves, flow is impeded, and velocity decreases, leading to increased pressure. Effective shock wave attenuation is only achievable by maintaining high flow velocity around the structure.

Contrast between concave and convex pyramids

The flow velocity over the pyramid surface shows clear differences under PLA30CONCAVE-L and PLA30CONVEX-L conditions when compared to PLA30-L. Under PLA30CONCAVE-L, the flow along the outer pyramid surface is slower than in PLA30-L, with reduced velocity near the wall. Conversely, under PLA30CONVEX-L, the outer pyramid surface flow velocity is enhanced relative to PLA30-L, and the flow velocity also increases significantly near the wall. These observations can be explained in two ways:

(1) The surface angle at the bottom of the cone affects the flow dynamics. In the CONCAVE specimen, the cone angle from the vertex to the bottom surface decreases, thereby obstructing flow. The continuous change in surface normal direction alters flow paths, reducing surface velocity and increasing load. This ultimately weakens overpressure peak attenuation. In contrast, the angle of the CONVEX specimen from the apex to the bottom of the cone increases, and the surface flow is continuously deflected to the cone. As a result, the outer cone flow attains a higher velocity, effectively mitigating overpressure peaks.

(2) The area between pyramids influences the convergence of shock wave reflections. The CONVEX specimen possesses a narrower intercone area, which significantly obstructs flow between pyramids during shock loading. This intensifies shock wave convergence and reduces attenuation. In contrast, the CONCAVE specimen has a larger intercone area, permitting less restricted flow and weaker shock wave reflection convergence in the intervertebral region, thereby enhancing attenuation. Overall, geometry governs flow field evolution through pressure gradients and shock interference. Moreover, flow field loads modify the energy transfer pathway via structural dynamic responses, which is a decisive factor of the efficiency of shock wave attenuation.

Impulse Attenuation

Clear differences in overpressure under varying loading conditions can be explained by changes in flow velocity. Conversely, in terms of impulse, significant differences among pyramid structures are not observed, and no consistent patterns emerge under low and high loading conditions. Under low loading, impulse attenuation ranks as follows: PLA30CONCAVE > PLA20 > PLA10 > PLA30CONVEX > PLA30 > REF. In high loading, the order becomes: PLA20 > PLA30CONCAVE > PLA30CONVEX > PLA10 > PLA30 > REF. Although the overall rankings vary, trends based on pyramid height and surface shape are evident. In terms of height, impulse attenuation ranks: PLA20 > PLA10 > PLA30. Regarding pyramid surface shape, the ranking is PLA30CONCAVE > PLA30CONVEX > PLA30. Impulse depends on both overpressure and duration. A structure that lowers overpressure while also shortening the loading time will significantly reduce impulse. However, the overpressure in PLA30 does not consistently remain lower than that of PLA20 and PLA10 throughout the process. For example, as shown in Fig. 16, the overpressure of PLA30 surpasses that of PLA20 at approximately 0.006 s, and the acting time is prolonged. As a result, increased pyramid height does not always lead to reduced specific impulse. We should analyze the impulse attenuation mode from two aspects: overpressure history and temporal characteristics.

Explanation of the rule of impulse Attenuation in terms of height

First, regarding impulse attenuation related to pyramid height, the order is PLA20 > PLA10 > PLA30. As mentioned above, the pulse attenuation law should be explained in terms of overpressure peak and action time. In terms of overpressure peaks, PLA30 has the lowest peak, while PLA10 has the highest. However, regarding the duration of pressure application, PLA30 has the largest inter-pyramid volume and a longer application time, whereas PLA10 has the smallest inter-pyramid volume with a shorter application time, as shown in Fig. 16. The combined effects of overpressure and application time result in PLA20 exhibiting the highest pulse attenuation effect.

Explanation of the rule of impulse Attenuation in terms of surface shape

For different pyramid surface shapes, impulse attenuation performance ranks as follows: PLA30CONCAVE > PLA30CONVEX > PLA30. The PLA30CONCAVE not only has the smallest overpressure but also provides the largest channel between pyramids, resulting in the shortest acting time, as shown in Fig. 17. These combined properties make it the most effective design for reducing impulse. PLA30CONVEX exhibits the highest overpressure and the narrowest inter-pyramid flow channels, resulting in the longest pressure acting time. It is therefore expected to perform poorly in impulse attenuation.

Explanation of the rule of impulse Attenuation in terms of base length

The impulse attenuation mechanisms of PLA30 and PLA15_0.5 are similarly explainable. Although PLA15_0.5 displays higher overpressure, it presents fewer flow obstacles compared to PLA30. Therefore, the pressure acting time is shorter for PLA15_0.5, as illustrated in Fig. 18. The interplay between overpressure and acting time ultimately determines performance characteristics. PLA15_0.5 offers significantly reduced height, space savings, slightly lower overpressure peak attenuation compared to PLA30, and enhanced specific impulse attenuation, rendering it suitable for space-constrained protection device designs.

Conclusion

Focusing on personnel blast protection, various pyramid structures were Designed and 3D printed. The shock wave protection performances of the different solid pyramid structures were evaluated through shock tube testing and numerical modeling, assessing transmitted pressure in the silicon rubber target. The following conclusions are reached:

Increasing pyramid height and decreasing pyramid angle enhance overpressure mitigation performance. The ranking for overpressure mitigation performance is as follows: PLA30CONCAVE > PLA30 > PLA30CONVEX > PLA20 > PLA15_0.5 > PLA10.

Overpressure attenuation is attributed to the flow velocity on the pyramid surface. Additionally, the pyramid structure exhibits a coupling effect with the incident shock tube flow, thereby influencing the applied pressure load.

In terms of pyramid height and shape, impulse attenuation follows the order of PLA20 > PLA10 > PLA30 and PLA30CONCAVE > PLA30CONVEX > PLA30, respectively.

The impulse attenuation effect results from both overpressure and acting time. The inter-pyramid volume and the channels between pyramids affect the duration of pressure acting time.

Considering both overpressure and impulse protection criteria, PLA30CONCAVE may offer the most effective blast wave protection.

These results serve as significant references for enhancing personnel anti-explosion protection levels.

Limitations and future work

While this study provides meaningful insights into the shock wave attenuation behavior of pyramid structures through a combined experimental and numerical approach, several limitations should be acknowledged to properly contextualize the findings and suggest directions for future research.

Simplified Material Constitutive Model: The silicone rubber material was modeled using the Mooney-Rivlin hyperelastic model, with parameters fitted from static compression tests. Although this approach is computationally efficient and appropriate for low-strain-rate loading conditions such as those in shock tube experiments, it does not account for potential strain rate effects that may occur under high-rate deformation. This simplification could affect the accuracy of predictions related to energy absorption and large-strain response under more extreme blast-like conditions.

Idealized Loading and Boundary Conditions: The use of a shock tube enables well-controlled and repeatable planar shock wave loading. However, it does not fully represent the multi-dimensional, transient nature of real explosion fields, which typically involve spherical shock waves, complex refraction and diffraction phenomena, and varying incident angles.

Assumptions in Numerical Simulation: The numerical model employed an ideal gas equation of state to describe the behavior of the detonation products. While practical for reducing computational cost, this model may not accurately capture the complex thermodynamics and high-pressure properties of real explosive gases, potentially introducing errors in the prediction of peak pressures and impulse transfer in near-field simulations.

Limited Geometric and Loading Variability: The study focused on a specific set of pyramid geometries, sizes, and two intensity levels. The findings may not be fully generalizable to other structural configurations, scales, or loading conditions outside the tested range.

Despite these Limitations, the present study establishes a valuable foundation for understanding the fundamental mechanisms of shock wave attenuation using pyramid structures. The proposed modeling methodology offers acceptable accuracy for comparative performance analysis under the specified conditions. Future work will involve incorporating strain-rate-sensitive material models, conducting field explosion tests, extending the analysis to 3D complex blast waves, and investigating a broader range of designs.
