Adsorption performance and mechanism of MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite for multiple heavy metal ions in the aqueous extract of Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort
Authors: Jinxin Fang, Hongli Guo, Chenling Xu, Shiyu Wang, Daoxiang Jiang, Qin Huang, Bao Yu, Weiguo Cao, Haijun Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heavy metal contamination in traditional Chinese medicines (TCMs) and their preparations is an urgent quality and safety issue to be addressed in the industrialization of TCMs. Herein, a novel magnetic composite of MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 was prepared as the adsorbent, which was fabricated by using the leaves (Isatidis Folium) and roots (Isatidis Radix) of Isatis indigotica as the carbon source for biochar (FRBCs), followed by in situ growth of MgAl-layered double hydroxides (MgAl-LDHs). In the aqueous extract of Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort. (CX), the composite exhibited high simultaneous removal efficiencies for multiple heavy metal ions: 96.53% for Pb2+, 78.49% for Cu2+, 83.82% for Cd2+, and 35.48% for Ni2+. The adsorption process fitted better with the pseudo-second-order (PSO) kinetic model and Langmuir isotherm, indicating that the adsorption was dominated by monolayer chemisorption. Meanwhile, a certain correlation with the pseudo-first-order (PFO) kinetic model was also observed, reflecting the participation of the physisorption. Thermodynamic parameters (ΔH = 25.84–105.42 kJ mol−1, ΔS = 122.15–386.15 J mol−1 K−1, ΔG = −17.37 to −9.65 kJ mol−1) confirmed a spontaneous and endothermic process with increased disorder. Further mechanism investigation by pH-dependent experiments, zeta potential measurements, and XPS revealed that the adsorption occurred mainly through electrostatic interactions and chemical complexation. Importantly, HPLC analysis revealed no significant alteration of the major bioactive components in the CX extract after adsorption. The composite also exhibited fast adsorption kinetics, high removal efficiency under competitive conditions, good stability and reusability, and easy magnetic separation. The current work provides a high-performance adsorbent for heavy metal removal from TCM aqueous extracts, which also offers a sustainable strategy for valorizing TCM residues.

Introduction

Due to the obvious bioaccumulation and toxicological characteristics of heavy metals, the long-term intake of them may lead to liver and kidney damage, nervous system disorders, endocrine disorders and immune dysfunction, thus posing potential health risks.1 In recent years, the application scale of TCMs and their preparations has been continuously expanding worldwide, and the attention to the quality and safety of TCMs has been growing.2 Heavy metal contamination is considered as a common concern in the quality control of TCMs. Multiple studies have shown that heavy metal ions can be introduced during the planting, harvesting, processing and storage of Chinese medicinal materials.3 Therefore, the effective removal of heavy metals from TCM extracts and preparations is important for the modernization development of TCMs.

At present, the common removal methods for heavy metal contamination include adsorption, ion exchange, precipitation and membrane separation.4,5 Among them, the adsorption method is considered as a promising approach due to its simple operation, economic feasibility, mild conditions and potential for recycling.6–8 However, the existing commonly used adsorbents, such as activated carbon, resins and metal oxides, still have drawbacks such as limited specific surface area, insufficient active sites and difficulty in recovery, which make them difficult to meet the treatment requirements of multi-metal and multi-component systems.9

Traditional Chinese medicine residue, a waste generated during the extraction process of TCM industrial production, is a kind of underutilized biomass resource with the characteristics of stable source, abundant output and sufficient carbon content.10,11Isatidis Folium and Isatidis Radix are the main raw materials of Banlangen Granules, a commonly used TCM preparation. The active ingredients in Isatidis Radix mainly include indole alkaloid glycosides, polysaccharides, isothiocyanates and thiocyanates, while Isatidis Folium is rich in numerous bio-active compounds such as indican, indigo, indirubin, tryptanthrin and flavonoids.12,13 Carbonization of the above herb residues can not only retain their porous structure, but also form abundant functional groups including hydroxyl, carboxyl and amino groups on the surface of biochar, providing a variety of complexation and adsorption sites for metal ions.14,15 Therefore, as compared with other conventional biomass such as corn stalks and waste wood, biochar from TCM residue has unique advantages in terms of the types of functional groups, the number of active sites and adsorption potential.16,17

Layered double hydroxides (LDHs) are formed by metal cations and feature adjustable interlayer structure, strong anion exchange ability and abundant surface hydroxyl groups, and thus exhibit good performance in the adsorption of heavy metal ions.18 In addition, they have good chemical and thermal stability and can be recycled in aqueous systems, making them important candidates for constructing high-efficiency multi-metal adsorbents.19 Magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles can endow materials with magnetic response characteristics when used as adsorbents, facilitating rapid separation and recovery after adsorption.20 The construction of multi-component composites using TCM residue biochar, LDHs and Fe3O4 is expected to realize increased specific surface area, abundant active sites, synergistic adsorption mechanisms and magnetic separation advantages.

In this study, biochar (FRBCs) was prepared from the residues of Isatidis Folium and Isatidis Radix, which was then doped with Fe3O4 magnetic nanoparticles, followed by the in situ growth of MgAl-LDHs to form the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite (Fig. 1). The composite was expected to achieve efficient adsorption of multiple heavy metal ions through the synergy of multiple components, providing an effective method for the adsorption of heavy metals in aqueous extract of TCM.

Experimental section

Materials and reagents

All the used reagents were of analytical grade (AR) or chromatographic grade (GR). Anhydrous MgCl2, AlCl3·6H2O, FeCl3·6H2O, anhydrous sodium acetate, phosphoric acid, ethylene glycol and NaOH were purchased from Aladdin (Beijing, China). The mixed herb residues of Isatidis Folium and Isatidis Radix were provided by a Chinese medicinal material production enterprise (Chongqing, China), the medicinal material of Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort. was purchased from the local Chinese medicinal material market (Chongqing, China). Concentrated nitric acid (HNO3) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) were purchased from Chuandong Chemical Co., Ltd (Chongqing, China). Standard solutions of metal ions including Pb2+, Cd2+, Ni2+, Cu2+, Fe3+, Mg2+ and Al3+ were all purchased from NCS Testing Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Acetonitrile (GR) and methanol (GR) were purchased from Honeywell (Charlotte, USA). In addition, ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm−1) was used for the preparation of the solutions.

Instruments and conditions

The crystal structure of the materials was analyzed by X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Rigaku Ultima IV, Japan), and the surface functional groups were determined by Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR, Thermo Fisher Nicolet iS50, USA). The microscopic morphology and lattice structure were observed by transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100F, Japan). The element composition and chemical states of the materials were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA), and the specific surface area and pore structure characteristics were analyzed by Automatic specific surface area and porosity analyzer (Micromeritics ASAP 2460, USA).

The concentration of metal ions in the solution was determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, PerkinElmer NexION 1100 G, USA). The content determination of active ingredients and fingerprint analysis of Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort. were conducted by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, waters HPLC-2998 PDA, USA).

Synthesis of adsorbents

Firstly, the Isatidis Folium and Isatidis Radix residues were cleaned and dried. Then the residues were crushed with a grinder and sieved through a 100-mesh sieve. The obtained powder was pyrolyzed at 600 °C for 2 h in a tube furnace under nitrogen atmosphere to form the carbonized material. After that, the carbonized material was washed with 10 wt% HCl for 3–5 times, then washed with ultrapure water until pH = 7, and then dried at 80 °C for 24 h, which was designated as FRBCs.

Secondly, magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles were prepared by a solvothermal method. Specifically, FeCl3·6H2O (16.2 g) and CH3COONa (32.4 g) were dissolved in 240 mL of ethylene glycol, stirred at 25 °C for 1 h, then transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and kept at 200 °C for 10 h. After natural cooling to room temperature, the mixture was thoroughly washed with water and ethanol. Subsequently, it was dried at 55 °C for 15 h.21

Finally, MgAl-LDHs were in situ grown on the FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite by a hydrothermal method. The FRBCs (1 g) and Fe3O4 (1 g) were ultrasonically dispersed in 100 mL of deionized water. Then, anhydrous MgCl2 (0.03 mol) and AlCl3·6H2O (0.01 mol) were added to the suspension, followed by vigorous stirring and ultrasonic treatment at room temperature. Subsequently, the mixture was adjusted to pH = 10 with NaOH aqueous solution (1 M), and then subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 24 h.22 The resulting MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite was collected by centrifugation, washed repeatedly, and then freeze-dried. MgAl-LDHs were prepared in the same way without adding biochar and Fe3O4.

Preparation of aqueous extract of Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort

The dried medicinal material of Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort. (80 g) was crushed and sieved (100-mesh), transferred into a 2000 mL round-bottom flask with 10 times the amount of water, then refluxed for 2 h, and filtered while hot. Suspended particles were separated by centrifugation (25 °C, 8000 rpm, 5 min), and the resulting supernatant was passed through a membrane filter (0.22 µm) and kept at 4 °C until use.

Adsorption behavior study

To carry out the adsorption behavior study, the optimization of adsorption condition, adsorption isothermal experiments and adsorption kinetics experiments were performed. A control group without spiked standard ions (non-spiked) and experimental groups with spiked metal ion standard solutions (Pb2+, Cu2+, Ni2+, Cd2+) were involved, and all the experiments were performed in triplicate. Following the adsorption experiments, the samples were centrifuged (8000 rpm, 10 min) for collecting the supernatant, then treated by a 0.22 µm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) filter membrane. The obtained solutions were transferred to a PTFE microwave digestion tank, and the mixture of HNO3 and H2O2 (1 : 1, v/v) was added for digestion to ensure the complete release of metal ions. After being diluted to an appropriate concentration, the digested solution was determined by ICP-MS.

To achieve the optimal adsorption conditions, the initial pH, the adsorption time and the dosage of adsorbent were optimized. The effect of initial pH on adsorption was further evaluated at pH value of 2.3, 3.3, 4.3, and 5.3, and the pH was adjusted using dilute HNO3 or NaOH before adsorption. The adsorption time was optimized with a fixed adsorbent dosage, with a contact time varying from 0 to 720 min. The dosage of adsorbent was optimized in the range of 0 to 100 mg, with a fixed contact time.

Stability and reusability studies

To evaluate the stability of the adsorbent, the XRD determinations were carried out on the adsorbent before adsorption, after adsorption and desorption. Specifically, after the adsorption of metal ion on adsorbent, the desorption was performed by 1 wt% HNO3, and the material was washed with deionized water several times and then freeze-dried. To further investigate the reusability of the adsorbents, the adsorption–desorption cycle was repeated five times to determine the change of adsorption efficiency.

Metal leaching rate studies

To investigate the metal leaching rate of the adsorbent, 80 mg of the adsorbent was added to 40 mL of Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort. extract (unspiked) and shaken under 298 K for 12 h. Then, the mixture was treated as described in Section 2.5 to determine the content of Mg2+, Al3+ and Fe3+ by ICP-MS.

Content determination of active ingredients and similarity evaluation of fingerprints of CX extract

Ferulic acid, senkyunolide A and senkyunolide I were used as the representative active ingredients of the Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort. The content of these compounds and the fingerprint of CX extract before and after the removal of heavy metal ions were measured by HPLC.

The HPLC equipped with a 2998 photodiode array (PDA) detector was employed to measure the active ingredients and fingerprint of CX extract. The chromatographic column used was Waters SunFire C18 Column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm), the mobile phase was methanol (organic phase) – 0.1% phosphoric acid aqueous solution (aqueous phase), the detection wavelength was 276 nm, the flow rate was 0.8 mL min−1, and the injection volume was 10 µL. The content of each active ingredient was calculated according to the standard curve from the chromatographic peak area, and the fingerprint similarity of each group was calculated by the relative peak area normalization method.

Results

Characterization of adsorbents

SEM characterization

To analyze the microscopic morphology of each material, SEM characterization was performed on MgAl-LDHs, Fe3O4, FRBCs and the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite. As shown in Fig. 2A, MgAl-LDHs exhibited a typical layered structure. Fe3O4 presented a nearly spherical structure with a diameter of about 100–800 nm (Fig. 2B). As presented in Fig. 2C, the FRBCs featured complex pore structures on the surface and inside, which provided good support for the subsequent loading.

SEM images of (A) MgAl-LDHs, (B) Fe3O4, (C) FRBCs, and (D) MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4.

As shown in Fig. 2D, the MgAl-LDHs nanosheets were densely grown on the surface of FRBCs/Fe3O4. As Compared to FRBCs, MgAl-LDHs and Fe3O4, the surface of the composite was more complex, which may provide more active sites, thus laying a foundation for its better adsorption performance. On the whole, the SEM results clearly confirmed that both Fe3O4 and MgAl-LDHs were successfully anchored on FRBCs.

TEM characterization

The TEM images (Fig. 3A) showed that MgAl-LDHs presented a typical layered structure with a certain degree of stacking between the nanosheets. The lattice fringes with an average spacing of 0.265 nm were observed, which were ascribed to the (012) crystal plane of MgAl-LDHs (PDF card #35-0965). The TEM image of FRBCs (Fig. 3B) showed an irregular block structure. Lattice fringes with an average lattice spacing of 0.236 nm could be observed in its HRTEM image, which could be attributed to the in-plane lattice of the graphitic carbon-like structure, corresponding to the (100) crystal plane.26 At the same time, there were areas in the HRTEM image where no obvious lattice fringes were observed, indicating that the FRBCs contained both amorphous carbon and carbon structural units with short-range order characteristics, which was consistent with the typical structural characteristics of biomass char.26 Fe3O4 nanoparticles (Fig. 3C) presented an approximately spherical morphology. Lattice fringes with a spacing of about 0.248 nm were observed in the HRTEM image, which could be ascribed to the (311) crystal plane of Fe3O4 (PDF card #65-3107), demonstrating that the prepared Fe3O4 nanoparticles had good crystallinity.

TEM images of (A) MgAl-LDHs, (B) FRBCs, (C) Fe3O4, (D) MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 and (E–L) EDS mapping images of the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4.

Obvious sheet-like structures, spherical nanoparticles and irregular block structures were presented in the TEM images of MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite (Fig. 3D). Lattice fringes with spacings of about 0.200 nm and 0.255 nm were displayed simultaneously in the HRTEM image, corresponding to the (018) plane of MgAl-LDHs (PDF card #35-0965) and the (311) plane of Fe3O4 (PDF card #65-3107), respectively. The EDS mapping images (Fig. 3E–L) revealed that the C element was mainly distributed in the FRBCs region. Mg and Al elements were uniformly distributed, consistent with the distribution characteristics of MgAl-LDHs. The Fe element signal was concentrated in the spherical particle region, corresponding to Fe3O4 nanoparticles. The O element was widely present in the entire field of view, which was contributed by the oxygen-containing groups in each component. The above results indicated that the components and the main elements (C, N, O, Mg, Al, Cl, Fe) coexisted in the prepared composite.

XRD characterization

Fig. 4C shows the XRD patterns of MgAl-LDHs, Fe3O4 and FRBCs. Two remarkable diffraction peaks of MgAl-LDHs appeared at 11.040° and 22.220°, which were attributed to the (003) and (006) planes, respectively. In addition, several weaker diffraction peaks were also observed at around 34.600°, 39.010°, 45.500° and 60.420°, corresponding to the (012), (015), (018) and (110) planes, respectively. The XRD pattern is consistent with the standard card (#35-0965), indicating the formation of MgAl-LDHs. In the XRD pattern of Fe3O4, the diffraction peaks appeared at 30.060°, 35.420°, 43.020°, 53.360°, 56.940° and 62.500°, which were attributed to the (220), (311) (400), (422), (511) and (440) crystal planes, respectively. The above peaks are in accordance with the PDF standard card (#65-3107), indicating the successful synthesis of Fe3O4. The diffraction peaks of FRBCs appeared at 26.62°, 40.480° and 50.140°, attributed to the ordered graphitic carbon, corresponding to the crystal planes of (002), (100) and (102).32

The main characteristic diffraction peaks of MgAl-LDHs, Fe3O4 and FRBCs were retained in the XRD pattern of the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite, and the positions of the diffraction peaks showed no obvious shift, indicating that the preparation process of the composite did not damage the original crystal structures of each component. Compared to the single component, the intensity of the characteristic diffraction peaks of some components in the composite was weakened, which may be due to their high dispersion in the composite or to being covered by other components. In summary, XRD patterns confirmed the successful construction of the composite.

XPS characterization

The XPS measurement was employed to analyze the element composition and chemical states of the prepared materials, and the survey spectra were presented in Fig. 4D. The MgAl-LDHs were mainly composed of Mg, Al and O elements, Fe3O4 was composed of Fe and O elements, and FRBCs mainly contained C, O and N elements. The MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite contained C, O, N, Mg, Al and Fe elements, indicating that MgAl-LDHs and Fe3O4 had been successfully loaded on the FRBCs surface. Furthermore, high-resolution XPS spectra were collected on MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 (Fig. 4E–J), Fe3O4 (Fig. S1), MgAl-LDHs (Fig. S2) and FRBCs (S3), to confirm the successful preparation of the materials.

The C 1s spectrum of the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite is shown in Fig. 4E, which can be deconvoluted into three peaks at 284.7 eV (C–C), 286.1 eV (C–O–C) and 289.0 eV (O–CO). The Fe3O4 (Fig. S1A), MgAl-LDHs (Fig. S2A) and FRBCs (Fig. S3A) presented similar C 1s spectra, indicating the close chemical environment of carbon. However, the carbon-containing groups of Fe3O4 and MgAl-LDHs mainly come from the carbon contamination of air.32 Therefore, the C–C, C–O–C and O–CO bonds in the composite mainly originate from FRBCs. As presented in Fig. 4F, the O 1s spectrum of the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite presented four peaks attributed to Fe3O4 (529.9 eV),33 C–O/O–H (531.7 eV),34 CO (532.5 eV) and OC–O (533.8 eV).35 The O 1 s spectrum of Fe3O4, MgAl-LDHs, FRBCs were presented in Fig. S1C, Fig. S2E and S3C, respectively. The results indicate that the O–H of MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 mainly contributed by MgAl-LDHs, the metal–oxygen bonds (M–O) of MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 mainly contributed by Fe3O4. The oxygen-containing groups in MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite may serve as potential hydrogen bond donors/acceptors and metal ion coordination sites, which provide a variety of chemical interaction sites for the subsequent adsorption process.36,37

The N 1s spectrum (Fig. 4G) shows the presence of pyridinic N (398.3 eV), pyrrolic N (400.0 eV), graphitic N (401.3 eV)38 and N-oxide in MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4. FRBCs present similar N 1s spectrum (Fig. S3B), indicating that the nitrogen-containing functional groups were retained in the composite. Pyridinic N, pyrrolic N and graphitic N mainly originate from nitrogen-containing alkaloids, indole compounds and protein residues generated during the carbonization process. These nitrogen-containing functional groups provided active sites for the adsorption of metal ions, and graphitic N could adjust the electron density to synergistically enhance the adsorption performance.37,39 The peak with high binding energy was nitrogen oxide, which might be introduced by the adsorption of a small amount of nitrogen oxide in the air.

The Fe 2p spectrum (Fig. 4H) of MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite confirmed the simultaneous presence of Fe2+ and Fe3+, including Fe2+ 2p3/2 (710.2 eV), Fe3+ 2p3/2 (713.2 eV), satellite peak (718.0 eV), Fe2+ 2p1/2 (723.2 eV), Fe3+ 2p1/2 (726.0 eV) and satellite peak (732.0 eV).40,41 Compared with Fe 2p spectrum of Fe3O4 (Fig. S1B), the peak positions have no obvious shift, indicating the chemical state of Fe3O4 remained stable during the formation process of composite. Thus providing stable metal active sites in composite, which are favorable for the adsorption of metal ions.42 Mg 1s spectrum of MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite including one peaks at 1304.2 eV, ascribed to Mg–OH (Fig. 4I).43,44 Al 2p spectrum of the composite can be fitted into two peaks at 75.0 eV and 75.5 eV, which correspond to Al 2p3/2 and Al 2p1/2 (Fig. 4J).45 The Mg 1 s and Al 2p spectra are similar to those of MgAl-LDHs.

BET characterization

The pore structure characteristics of MgAl-LDHs, Fe3O4, FRBCs and their composite were characterized by N2 adsorption–desorption method (Fig. 5A–B). According to the IUPAC classification standard,46 all samples exhibited typical Type IV isotherms, indicating that the materials were mainly mesoporous. In the region of relatively low pressure (P/P0 < 0.1), the adsorption capacity of all samples increased slowly with the increase of pressure, indicating the presence of a certain number of micropores in the materials. An obvious hysteresis loop (H3 type) appeared in the medium and high relative pressure range, which was usually related to capillary condensation occurring in mesopores. The specific surface areas of MgAl-LDHs and Fe3O4 were 48.42 m2 g−1 and 22.48 m2 g−1, respectively, and their pore structures mainly originated from the mutual stacking of flake MgAl-LDHs or spherical Fe3O4 nanoparticles. The specific surface area of FRBCs was low (11.60 m2 g−1), which might be related to the collapse of some pore channels and the formation of disordered structures during the carbonization of TCM residues. Compared with FRBCs, the specific surface area of the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite increased to 31.35 m2 g−1, which was not exceed that of MgAl-LDHs, indicating that the growth of MgAl-LDHs and the loading of Fe3O4 occupied part of the pore channels of FRBCs.

N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms (A and B) and BJH pore size distribution curves (C–F) of MgAl-LDHs, Fe3O4, FRBCs, and MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4.

To further analyze the pore structure characteristics of the materials, the BJH method was employed to confirm the mesoporous and macroporous distribution of the materials, and the HK method was used to calculate the microporous distribution (Fig. 5C–F). Among them, MgAl-LDHs showed a wide range of mesopores and macropores (2–200 nm), relatively concentrated at about 120 nm, and a small number of micropores were relatively concentrated at 0.7 nm and 1.2 nm. The pore size of FRBCs was widely distributed in the range of 2–224 nm, relatively concentrated at 22 nm, 34 nm and 53 nm, and the micropores were distributed in the range of 0.8–1.9 nm. The mesopores and macropores of Fe3O4 were widely distributed in the range of 2–208 nm, the mesopores were relatively concentrated at about 15 nm and 19 nm, and the micropores were distributed in the range of 0.8–1.9 nm. Finally, the pore size of MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 was widely distributed in the range of 2–188 nm, with macropores relatively concentrated (about 104 nm). The micropores were distributed in the range of 0.8–1.9 nm, relatively concentrated at about 0.9 nm.

VSM characterization

To investigate the magnetic recovery performance of the composite material, the magnetic properties of equal masses of MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 and Fe3O4 were characterized using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) over an applied magnetic field range of −25 kOe to 25 kOe. The results are presented in Fig. S4. The saturation magnetization of Fe3O4 was determined to be 78.7 emu per g, whereas that of the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite was 17.8 emu per g. This reduction in magnetization is primarily attributed to the introduction of non-magnetic components (MgAl-LDHs and FRBCs). The results demonstrate that the composite material retains a significant magnetic response, indicating its suitability for magnetic separation under an applied magnetic field.47

Effect of initial pH on heavy metal adsorption

To investigate the effect of initial pH on the adsorption performance of heavy metals, adsorption experiments were conducted at pH values of 2.3, 3.3, 4.3, and 5.3. As shown in Fig. 6B, the removal efficiency of most metal ions exhibited an increasing trend with increasing pH. Under strongly acidic conditions, the presence of a large number of H+ ions may lead to excessive protonation of functional groups on the material surface, thereby reducing the affinity of the adsorbent for metal ions.48 As the pH increases, partial deprotonation of the functional groups occurs, reducing the occupancy of negatively charged sites and thus promoting electrostatic interactions with metal cations.49 As the pH increased from 4.3 to 5.3, the resulting adsorption efficiency of Pb2+, Cd2+, and Ni2+ exhibited a slight increase, while the adsorption efficiency of Cu2+ decreased slightly. Considering that pH = 4.3 is closer to the natural state of the CX extract, and taking into account the simplicity and operability of subsequent procedures, subsequent adsorption experiments were mainly conducted at pH = 4.3.

Effect of adsorption time and adsorbent dosage on heavy metal adsorption

As presented in Fig. 6C, the adsorption efficiency of the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite for ions improved with increasing time. It displayed a rapid growth trend in the range of 0–120 min and slowed down after 120 min, thus the optimal adsorption time was confirmed to be 120 min. The possible reason for this phenomenon might be that there were a large number of adsorption sites on the material surface at the initial stage of adsorption, and the adsorption sites were gradually occupied with the passage of time, leading to a slow change in adsorption efficiency until it remained unchanged.50

As shown in Fig. 6D, the effect of the adsorbent dosage on adsorption efficiency was investigated. When the dosage increased from 10 mg to 80 mg, the number of available adsorption sites in the system added, thus promoting the removal of ions in the solution and significantly increasing the adsorption efficiency. However, when the dosage continuously increased from 80 mg to 120 mg, the adsorption efficiency showed no obvious change, which might be attributed to the saturation of adsorption sites.51 In addition, excessive dosage might lead to poor dispersion, reducing the specific surface area, thus inhibiting further removal of metal ions.52

Adsorption kinetics

As shown in Fig. 6E, the adsorption capacity (qe) increased rapidly at the initial stage of adsorption, then the growth rate slowed down gradually and reached adsorption equilibrium after about 120 min. To further explore the kinetic characteristics of the adsorption, PFO, PSO and Elovich models were used to fit the experimental data. The fitting results from the three models are presented in Fig. 6F–H and Table 1. The linear relationship between t/qt and t indicates that, the adsorption kinetics of Pb2+, Cu2+, Cd2+, and Ni2+ on MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 are mostly consistent with PSO model (R2 = 0.9803–0.9997), which reflects that the adsorption process may be mainly ascribed to chemisorption (Fig. 6G).53 In addition, the fitting results also exhibit a certain correlation with PFO model (Fig. 6F, R2 = 0.9270–0.9968), implying that the adsorption process is also accompanied by physical adsorption.54 In contrast, the Elovich model showed a relatively poor fit, with R2 range from 0.5952 to 0.8602 (Fig. 6H).

Adsorption isotherms

The adsorption isotherms of Cd2+, Ni2+, Cu2+ and Pb2+ on the composite were displayed in Fig. 7A–D. The qe of ions on the composite increased rapidly with the increasing initial concentration (C0) and gradually reached equilibrium in the high-concentration region, indicating that the adsorption process reached equilibrium within the experimental concentration range. The adsorption isotherm data were fitted by the Freundlich, Langmuir and Temkin models (Fig. 7E–H, S5A–H). The adsorption of all the four metal ions on MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite fitted well with the Langmuir model (R2 = 0.9968–0.9993), which was significantly better than that with the Freundlich (R2 = 0.3178–0.8224) and Temkin (R2 = 0.2975–0.9552) models.

At the investigated temperatures, the changes of Gibbs free energy (ΔG) for metal ions were all negative, indicating the adsorption process could proceed spontaneously under the experimental conditions. The absolute value of ΔG increased gradually with the rise of temperature, reflecting that the increase in temperature favors the adsorption process.

The enthalpy change (ΔH) of all metal ions was positive, revealing that the adsorption process was endothermic, which was in accordance with that the qm increased with the rise of temperature. Meanwhile, the entropy changes (ΔS) were all positive, indicating that the adsorption process increased the degree of disorder, which may be due to the release of water molecules accompanied by the adsorption of metal ions and the rearrangement of the interface structure.67

Different metal ions exhibited differentiated adsorption capacities in the single-ion system, and the order of the qm was Cu2+ > Pb2+ > Cd2+ > Ni2+. Among them, Cu2+ showed a relatively high qm, indicating that the material had a strong adsorption capacity for Cu2+ under ideal conditions. However, in the aqueous extract of CX with multiple ions coexisting, the adsorption efficiency of the composite for Cu2+ (78.49%) was obviously lower than that for Pb2+ (96.53%), indicating that the coexistence of multiple ions significantly altered their adsorption behaviors. This phenomenon might be due to the fact that Pb2+ could occupy the limited adsorption sites preferentially, thus producing an obvious inhibitory effect on the adsorption of Cu2+.68 In addition, Ni2+ had relatively high ΔH and ΔS, which might be because Ni2+ formed a stable hydration layer in the aqueous solution. Energy was required to break these hydration interactions during the adsorption process, which resulted in high ΔH. Moreover, the release of water molecules increased the disorder of the system, which led to high ΔS. Therefore, increasing the temperature was helpful to break the hydration interaction and promote the combination of Ni2+ with the composite material, thus enhancing the adsorption effect.69

Adsorption mechanism

To obtain the surface charge of the heavy metal adsorption environment and to reveal the electrostatic interactions between the adsorbent and heavy metal ions, zeta potential measurements were performed on the CX extract containing MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 (MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 + CX extract), the CX extract containing MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 after heavy metal adsorption (MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4-M + CX extract), and the blank CX extract. The results are presented in Fig. 8A. In the pH range of 2.3–5.3, both the extract containing MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 and the blank extract exhibited negative zeta potential values, indicating that the above solution presented a negative charge state. At the same pH, the absolute zeta potential value of the extract containing MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 was larger than that of the blank extract, suggesting that MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 provides a favorable negative charge environment for the adsorption of metal cations in the extract. After the adsorption of heavy metal, the absolute zeta potential value of the material decreased, indicating that the adsorbed metal cations bound to part of the negative charges on the composite, further demonstrating the existence of electrostatic interactions between the heavy metal ions and the material surface.

As shown in Fig. 8B, a distinct Pb 4f peak was observed in the XPS survey spectrum of the composite after heavy metal adsorption, whereas no obvious characteristic peaks for Cd, Cu, or Ni were detected. However, the quantitative results have already demonstrated that the composite exhibited considerable adsorption capacity for Cd2+, Cu2+, and Ni2+. Therefore, the absence of these metal elements in the XPS spectra may be attributed to the amount of adsorbed Cu2+, Cd2+, and Ni2+ being below the detection limit of XPS, or to the low sensitivity of XPS for these ions. Further analysis was performed on the high-resolution XPS spectra of Pb 4f and O 1s to obtain the bonding information of Pb ions on the adsorbent surface. The Pb 4f spectrum, shown in Fig. 8C, can be deconvoluted into two spin–orbit doublets. The peaks at 138.50 eV and 143.40 eV are assigned to Pb 4f7/2 and Pb 4f5/2 of Pb–O, respectively; the peaks at 139.20 eV and 144.10 eV are assigned to Pb 4f7/2 and Pb 4f5/2 of Pb–O–C, respectively.70,71 These results further confirm that Pb adsorption is associated with the formation of Pb–O bonds.

As shown in Fig. 8D, the high-resolution XPS spectra of O 1s before and after the adsorption of heavy metal ions on MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite were measured and deconvoluted, and Fig. 8E presents the corresponding peak area percentages of each component. The high-resolution O 1s spectrum of MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4-M can be fitted into five peaks, corresponding to M–O (530 eV),72 M–OH (531.0 eV),73 C–O/O–H (531.7 eV),34 CO (532.6 eV), 74 and OC–O (533.8 eV).35 In terms of binding energy, slight decreases were observed for the C–O/O–H, CO, and OC–O components after the adsorption of heavy metal ions, which may be attributed to the increased electron cloud density around these oxygen-containing functional groups following the integration of metal ions.75 Regarding the peak areas, compared with those before adsorption, M–O increased by 1.58%, M–OH increased by 9.75%, while C–O/O–H decreased by 9.71%, CO decreased by 1.07%, and OC–O decreased by 0.55%. The results further indicate the adsorption of metal ions on composite mainly through the chemical complexation with oxygen-containing functional groups (C–O/O–H, CO, and OC–O).76 Based on the magnitude of the changes in peak areas, metal ions primarily undergo chemical complexation with C–O and O–H groups.

From the zeta potential and XPS characterization results, it can be concluded that the adsorption mechanism of heavy metal ions onto MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 mainly involves electrostatic adsorption and chemical complexation.

Stability and reusability of adsorbent

The stability of the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite was investigated by XRD analysis. The XRD patterns of the composite before adsorption, after adsorption and after elution with 1% HNO3 are shown in Fig. 9H, respectively. Compared with that before adsorption, the composite after the removal of metal ions presented a similar XRD pattern, indicating that the adsorption process had no obvious effect on the crystal structure of the composite material, and the layered structure of MgAl-LDHs and the crystal phase of Fe3O4 remained stable. In addition, some diffraction peaks attributed to FRBCs were weakened to a certain extent, which might be due to the adsorption of heavy metal ions resulted in a reduction in the XRD peak intensity.82 After elution treatment with HNO3, the XRD pattern of the composite was similar to that of the sample before adsorption, indicating that the adsorbed metal ions could be effectively removed by acid washing, and the main crystal structure of the material was not significantly damaged during the elution process, showing good structural stability. In summary, the XRD results indicated that the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite material had good crystal structure stability during the adsorption and regeneration processes, and the adsorption process mainly occurred on the surface or in the interlayer structure of the material, which provided a structural basis for its practical application.

To evaluate the regeneration and reusability of the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite material, continuous adsorption–desorption cycle experiments were carried out. After adsorption, 1 wt% HNO3 was used for elution for the next cycle. As shown in Fig. 9I, the removal rates of the composite material for Pb2+, Cd2+, Ni2+ and Cu2+ in the first cycle were 96.53%, 83.82%, 35.48% and 78.49% respectively, and remained basically stable within five cycles. The corresponding removal rates in the fifth cycle were 92.60%, 81.28%, 32.62% and 76.89% respectively, with no obvious performance attenuation, indicating that the material had good cyclic utilization capacity. The ability to maintain cyclic performance in a complex competitive system further reflected the strong structural stability and the practical application value of the material.

Study on metal leaching rate

To further evaluate the structural stability of the composite material during the adsorption process, the leaching behavior of Mg2+, Al3+ and Fe3+ in the adsorption system was measured. The detection results showed that the leaching rates of Mg2+, Al3+ and Fe3+ were 0.00032%, 0.00033% and 0.0043%, respectively, all at an extremely low level, indicating that the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 had good structural stability in the adsorption system. The leaching behavior had no significant effect on the system and did not cause potential secondary pollution, proving that the material had good application safety.

Discussion

The published adsorbents such as layered double hydroxides (LDHs),68 biochar, 83 and magnetic composites84 have been applied to aqueous solutions, wastewater, or soil with simulated specific matrices, focusing on single or a few metal ions under relatively simple conditions. In contrast, this study prepared a MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 composite using Isatidis Folium and Isatidis Radix residues and applied it to the removal of multiple heavy metal ions from the aqueous extract of CX, which contains a complex mixture of bioactive compounds. This complex matrix can significantly influence adsorption behavior, which considerably differs from that in general aqueous solution systems.

The prepared composite exhibited satisfactory adsorption performance for heavy metal ions under both non-competitive conditions and competitive conditions. Mechanistic investigations by pH-dependent experiments, zeta potential measurements, and XPS indicated that chemisorption involving oxygen-containing functional groups (C–O/O–H, CO, and OC–O) played a major role, with the C–O/O–H bond contributing most significantly. Meanwhile, electrostatic interactions also facilitated the removal of metal ions, because the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 provides a favorable negative charge environment for the adsorption of metal cations in the extract. Most importantly, HPLC analysis revealed no remarkable change in major chemical components of CX extract after adsorption. The negligible effect on active compounds may be attributed to the mild adsorption conditions and the specific surface chemistry of the composite, which preferentially targets metal ions over organic molecules under the experimental pH.

Overall, this work distinguishes itself from prior studies by demonstrating effective multi-metal removal in a real aqueous extract of TCM, while preserving the active components. The use of TCM residues as the carbon source further aligns with circular economy principles, offering a sustainable and cost-effective approach for heavy metal control in TCM preparations.

Conclusions

In this study, the MgAl-LDHs@FRBCs/Fe3O4 magnetic composite was successfully prepared using residues of Isatidis Folium and Isatidis Radix as the biochar carbon source. This composite exhibited a porous structure, high specific surface area, abundant functional groups, and good magnetic separability. It demonstrated efficient simultaneous removal of multiple heavy metal ions (Pb2+, Cu2+, Cd2+, Ni2+) from the aqueous extract of Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort., with fast adsorption kinetics, high capacity, and excellent reusability. The adsorption process fitted better with the pseudo-second-order (PSO) kinetic model and Langmuir isotherm, indicating that the adsorption was dominated by monolayer chemisorption. Meanwhile, a certain correlation with the pseudo-first-order (PFO) kinetic model was also observed, reflecting the participation of the physisorption. Thermodynamic analysis confirmed a spontaneous, endothermic process with increased entropy. Mechanistically, the removal was mainly governed by chemical complexation with oxygen-containing functional groups and electrostatic interactions. Importantly, the adsorption did not alter the major active components of the CX extract. This work not only provides a practical adsorbent for heavy metal decontamination in TCM aqueous preparations but also establishes a sustainable strategy for valorizing TCM residues, offering both theoretical support and application potential for quality control in TCM industrialization.
