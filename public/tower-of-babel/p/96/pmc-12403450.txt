Pan-cancer analysis of tumor suppressor ZNF132 reveals its diagnostic and prognostic significance with immunomodulatory implications in colorectal cancer
Authors: Yuanjun Li, Hongtao Sun, Lili Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundZinc finger protein 132 (ZNF132) has emerged as a potential tumor suppressor, with its dysregulation closely associated with the initiation and progression of various malignancies. However, a comprehensive assessment of ZNF132’s expression patterns across diverse cancer types, its clinical prognostic implications, and its immunoregulatory role in colorectal cancer remains insufficiently characterized. This study aims to elucidate the biological functions of ZNF132 within the context of colorectal cancer.MethodsWe utilized transcriptomic data from 33 cancer types obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the TCGA-COADREAD dataset to systematically analyze the expression characteristics of ZNF132 and its differential expression in colorectal cancer. Validation of protein expression was conducted using data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA). The relationship between ZNF132 and immune infiltration was examined through single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and Spearman correlation analysis. We evaluated the diagnostic, prognostic, and independent risk prediction capabilities of ZNF132 using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, and multivariable Cox regression models. Additionally, we explored the biological pathways associated with ZNF132-related differentially expressed genes through Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), supplemented by in vitro experiments to investigate the functional roles of ZNF132 in colorectal cancer.ResultsPan-cancer analysis demonstrated a significant downregulation of ZNF132 in colorectal and rectal cancers, accompanied by reduced protein levels in colorectal cancer tissues. Immune infiltration analysis revealed significant correlations between ZNF132 and various immune cell subtypes in colorectal cancer, notably a negative correlation with Th17 cells and NK CD56bright cells, while exhibiting positive correlations with T helper cells, central memory T cells, macrophages, and Th2 cells. The expression of ZNF132 was significantly associated with clinical pathological features in colorectal cancer patients, including age, M stage, and pathological grade. ROC curve analysis indicated that ZNF132 possesses high sensitivity and specificity for colorectal cancer diagnosis (AUC = 0.845). Kaplan-Meier analysis further confirmed that elevated ZNF132 expression correlates with extended survival in colorectal cancer patients. Multivariable Cox regression analysis identified ZNF132 as an independent risk factor for overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) in these patients. Enrichment analysis suggested that ZNF132 modulates tumor progression by influencing calcium signaling, PPAR pathways, and apoptosis-related genes. Functional assays demonstrated that ZNF132 significantly inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of colorectal cancer cells.ConclusionZNF132 functions as a novel tumor suppressor, influencing colorectal cancer progression through the regulation of the immune microenvironment and malignant cellular phenotypes. Its diagnostic and prognostic potential offers new therapeutic targets for clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14810-9.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, ZNF132, Tumor suppressor, Immune infiltration, Prognostic biomarker

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the malignancies with the highest incidence and mortality rates worldwide, characterized by a multifaceted pathogenesis that includes genetic mutations, environmental factors, and immune dysfunction [1–3]. Early detection and accurate diagnosis are vital for improving patient survival rates [4, 5]. Despite advancements in screening technologies and targeted therapies in recent years, the five-year survival rate for patients with advanced disease remains below 15%, underscoring the urgent need for highly sensitive diagnostic biomarkers and precise prognostic evaluation systems [6, 7]. Consequently, there is an immediate necessity to develop novel biomarkers that are sensitive, non-invasive, and cost-effective, aimed at optimizing early diagnostic strategies and enhancing prognostic stratification.

Zinc finger protein 132 (ZNF132), a member of the KRAB domain-associated zinc finger protein (KRAB-ZFP) family, has recently attracted considerable attention for its biological functions [8, 9]. Proteins within this family recruit the KAP1/TRIM28 complex via the KRAB domain, facilitating the targeted localization of histone-modifying enzymes such as SETDB1, which are crucial for chromatin remodeling and transcriptional silencing [10–12]. Recently, the role of ZNF132 in various tumors has gained prominence. In breast cancer, ZNF132 expression levels are significantly diminished, and its low expression correlates with reduced relapse-free survival (RFS) in patients, suggesting that ZNF132 may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker [13, 14]. Moreover, the methylation status of ZNF132 in breast cancer cell lines has been shown to negatively correlate with its expression levels, indicating that it may be regulated epigenetically [13]. In colorectal cancer, the methylation status of ZNF132 also shows promise as an early diagnostic biomarker. Studies suggest that the methylation levels of ZNF132 in plasma samples from CRC patients exhibit robust diagnostic performance, particularly in those with KRAS mutations, where its diagnostic capability is significantly enhanced [15]. In head and neck cancer, ZNF132 expression has similarly garnered attention. Research indicates that ZNF132 expression is reduced in tumor tissues of patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), and its low expression is associated with overall survival (OS), suggesting that ZNF132 may function as a tumor suppressor in HNSCC, with its expression level serving as an important prognostic indicator [16]. Despite the increasing recognition of ZNF132’s potential in tumor suppression, its expression patterns across various cancer types remain poorly understood, particularly regarding its role in colorectal cancer.

This study integrates pan-cancer genomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) with a clinical cohort of colorectal cancer samples to systematically analyze the expression characteristics of ZNF132 across 33 malignancies and its correlation with the immune microenvironment. We employed single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and Spearman correlation models to investigate the association between ZNF132 and immune cell infiltration, complemented by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and multivariable Cox regression to assess its diagnostic and prognostic value. Furthermore, we elucidated the potential molecular mechanisms of ZNF132 through differential gene expression screening, Gene Ontology (GO)/Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), while constructing a multiparametric prognostic prediction model. Additionally, we conducted in vitro functional assays to explore the regulatory effects of ZNF132 on the proliferation, migration, and invasion of colorectal cancer cells, alongside immunohistochemical validation utilizing the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database.

Methods and materials

Multi-Omics data collection and preprocessing

We utilized the TCGA data platform (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) [17, 18] to integrate RNA-seq transcriptomic data (TPM normalized) and clinical pathological feature data across 33 cancer types, encompassing 647 CRC tissues and 51 normal control samples. Data for colon cancer (480 tumor samples, 41 normal) and rectal cancer (167 tumor samples, 10 normal) were independently extracted and subsequently merged for analysis. Paired samples were selected based on the requirement that cancerous tissues be matched with adjacent non-cancerous tissues from the same patient (n = 51). Protein expression data were sourced from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/) through immunohistochemical staining results (antibody HPA008726) [19]. RNA-seq data underwent quality control to exclude low-expression genes (mean TPM < 0.1) and were batch-corrected using the ComBat-seq algorithm for subsequent analyses.

Immune microenvironment analysis

Using the TCGA pan-cancer RNA sequencing data, we employed the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm (GSVA v1.42.0) to quantify the infiltration levels of 24 immune cell types [20]. Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationship between ZNF132 expression and immune subpopulations across various cancer types. For the colorectal cancer dataset, immune infiltration characteristics were further analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare immune cell differences between high and low ZNF132 expression groups, with data visualization conducted using the “ggplot2” package (v3.3.6).

Association analysis of ZNF132 expression profile with clinical pathological features

We utilized RNA sequencing data and clinical pathological parameters from the TCGA colorectal cancer cohort to perform chi-squared tests, evaluating the association between ZNF132 expression levels (grouped by median value) and patient age, TNM staging, and pathological grading. A univariate logistic regression model was employed to quantify the independent correlation of ZNF132 expression levels with clinical pathological features, calculating odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The R package “pROC” (v1.18.0) was used to construct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to assess the diagnostic efficacy of ZNF132 in colorectal cancer and across different pathological stages.

Survival analysis and prognostic model construction

Kaplan-Meier analysis was utilized to evaluate the impact of ZNF132 expression on overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) in colorectal cancer patients, with intergroup differences assessed using the log-rank test. Both univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to determine the independent prognostic value of ZNF132, calculating hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CI. Based on the independent prognostic factors identified through multivariate Cox analysis, we constructed a nomogram model incorporating ZNF132 using the “rms” package (v6.3.0) in R. The model’s predictive accuracy for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates was validated through bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) and calibration curves. The discriminative performance of the model was quantified using the concordance index (C-index) from the “survival” package (v3.4.0) [21].

Differential expression genes (DEGs) and functional enrichment analysis

Utilizing RNA sequencing data from the TCGA colorectal cancer cohort, we divided 698 patients into high and low expression groups based on the median ZNF132 expression level. DEGs between the groups were analyzed using the edgeR package (v3.38.4), with screening criteria set at |log2 FC| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-values < 0.05. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.0) [22], covering biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. Additionally, GSEA was conducted to further evaluate ZNF132-related pathways, utilizing REACTOME and KEGG gene sets from the MSigDB database, with a significance threshold set at FDR < 0.25. All visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package (v3.4.0).

Cell culture and transfection

Human colorectal cancer cell lines HCT116 and SW480 were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank. Cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A and RPMI-1640 media, respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, under conditions of 37 °C and 5% CO₂. For the establishment of ZNF132 overexpression and knockdown models, the following protocols were employed: In the overexpression group, the full-length cDNA of ZNF132 was cloned into the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro lentiviral vector. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with this vector and the packaging plasmids psPAX2/pMD2.G using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h, the viral supernatant was harvested to infect HCT116 cells, which were subsequently selected with 2 µg/ml puromycin to establish stable cell lines. In the knockdown group, sh-ZNF132 (5’-CCCAAGCAGAATGTTTCTGTA-3’) was designed and cloned into the pLKO.1-puro vector, and SW480 cells were transfected following the same procedure. Control groups were treated with empty vectors pCDH and pLKO.1, respectively.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer, following the protocol outlined by Li et al. [23]. After quantification via the BCA method, proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to membranes. The primary antibodies employed included rabbit anti-ZNF132 (Proteintech, 21681-1-AP, 1:1000) and rabbit anti-GAPDH (CST, 5174 S, 1:1000).

CCK-8 assay and colony formation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using a CCK-8 kit (Yeasen Biotechnology). Stable HCT116 (overexpression group) and SW480 (knockdown group) cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well in 96-well plates. CCK-8 reagent (10 µL) was added at 24, 48, and 72 h post-transfection, and after a 2-hour incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek). For the colony formation assay, stable transfected cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells per well in 6-well plates. After 14 days of culture, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted under a microscope (Nikon) for colonies with a diameter of ≥ 50 μm.

Transwell and scratch assays

For the migration assay, 1 × 10⁵ cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell (Corning), while the lower chamber contained medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, cells were fixed and stained, and the number of cells that migrated through the membrane was quantified. For the invasion assay, the upper chamber was pre-coated with Matrigel matrix (BD Biosciences), and the remaining steps were identical to those of the migration assay.

In the scratch assay, 5 × 10⁵ cells were seeded in 6-well plates, and when they reached 90% confluence, a scratch was created using a 200 µL pipette tip. Images were captured at 0 h and 24 h (Nikon), and the scratch healing rate was calculated using ImageJ software. All experiments were independently repeated three times, and data were analyzed as means.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 (IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, USA), with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. Quantitative data comparisons were conducted using t-tests or one-way ANOVA, while differences in categorical variables were assessed using chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact test.

Results

Expression profile of ZNF132 in various cancers and differential expression analysis in colorectal cancer

We performed a comprehensive analysis of ZNF132 expression across 33 cancer datasets from the TCGA database. Our findings indicate that ZNF132 is significantly downregulated in several cancer types, including colon cancer, rectal cancer, invasive breast cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, and lung squamous cell carcinoma (Fig. 1A). Further analysis of paired samples confirmed a significant decrease in ZNF132 expression in these malignancies (Fig. 1B). To validate the significance of ZNF132’s specific expression in colorectal cancer, we meticulously examined a cohort consisting of 647 colorectal cancer tissues and 51 normal tissues, all sourced from the TCGA database. Our analysis clearly demonstrated a significant reduction in ZNF132 expression within colorectal cancer tissues (Fig. 1C). Additionally, paired difference analysis corroborated these findings, revealing that ZNF132 levels in tumor samples were significantly lower than those in normal samples (Fig. 1D). We also analyzed cohorts comprising 480 colon cancer tissues and 41 normal tissues, as well as 167 rectal cancer tissues and 10 normal tissues, all derived from the TCGA database. The results indicated a significant decrease in ZNF132 expression in both colon and rectal cancer tissues (Fig. 1E-F). To complement our transcriptomic analysis, we utilized immunohistochemical staining data from the HPA database to assess ZNF132 protein expression levels in colorectal cancer (Atlas antibody, HPA008726). Notably, our analysis revealed a significant downregulation of ZNF132 protein expression in colorectal cancer tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these findings provide compelling evidence for the downregulation of ZNF132 in colorectal cancer.

Immune infiltration analysis of ZNF132 in colorectal cancer

Using ssGSEA, we evaluated the infiltration levels of 24 immune cell types across 33 cancers (Fig. 2A) and explored the correlation between ZNF132 expression and immune cell infiltration in various cancer types through Spearman correlation analysis. The results indicated a significant association between ZNF132 expression and multiple immune cell subtypes in colon and rectal cancers. Specifically, ZNF132 exhibited a negative correlation with Th17 cells and NK CD56bright cells, while showing a positive correlation with T helper cells, central memory T cells, macrophages, and Th2 cells (Supplementary Tables 1–2). To further validate these results, we assessed the infiltration levels of 24 immune cell types in colorectal cancer tissues and analyzed the correlation between ZNF132 expression and immune cell infiltration. The results confirmed significant associations between ZNF132 expression and various immune cell subtypes in colorectal cancer. In particular, ZNF132 was negatively correlated with Th17 cells (R=−0.231, p < 0.001) and NK CD56bright cells (R=−0.121, p < 0.01), while positively correlated with T helper cells (R = 0.322, p < 0.001), central memory T cells (R = 0.304, p < 0.001), macrophages (R = 0.252, p < 0.001), and Th2 cells (R = 0.236, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, we conducted a detailed assessment of the infiltration levels of the six immune cell types most strongly correlated with ZNF132, validating the consistency of our preliminary analysis (Fig. 3A-F).

ZNF132 as a promising prognostic and diagnostic biomarker in colorectal cancer

To assess the clinical relevance of ZNF132, we utilized ROC curves to evaluate its diagnostic value in colorectal cancer. The AUC for ZNF132 was determined to be 0.845, indicating substantial sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of colorectal cancer (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, we explored the diagnostic potential of ZNF132 across various pathological stages, revealing AUC values of 0.856, 0.830, 0.858, and 0.847 for stages I, II, III, and IV, respectively (Fig. 4B-E).

Predictive potential of ZNF132 in colorectal cancer diagnosis. A ROC curve evaluation of ZNF132’s diagnostic value in colorectal cancer. B-C Subgroup analysis based on pathological stages I, II, III, and IV

Subsequently, we conducted a Kaplan-Meier analysis across multiple cancer types to validate the prognostic capability of ZNF132 in clinical outcomes. As depicted in Fig. 5A-C, patients exhibiting high ZNF132 expression in colon cancer, rectal cancer, and kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma demonstrated significantly improved OS (p < 0.05), DSS (p < 0.05), and PFI (p < 0.05) compared to those with low expression levels. We then performed a specific Kaplan-Meier analysis for colorectal cancer to further confirm the prognostic significance of ZNF132. As shown in Fig. 6A-C, colorectal cancer patients with high ZNF132 expression exhibited significantly enhanced OS (p < 0.05), DSS (p < 0.05), and PFI (p < 0.05) compared to their low-expression counterparts. Multivariable Cox regression analysis indicated that ZNF132 functions as an independent risk factor for predicting OS (HR: 0.533, p = 0.002), DSS (HR: 0.346, p < 0.001), and PFI (HR: 0.659, p = 0.015) (Table 3). Additionally, M stage was identified as an independent predictor of OS, DSS, and PFI, while N stage was found to be an independent predictor of OS and PFI.

By stratifying 698 colorectal cancer patients based on the median ZNF132 expression level into high and low expression groups, we conducted a detailed analysis of the DEGs between these groups. In the high ZNF132 expression group, we identified 700 significantly differentially expressed genes (P < 0.05, |log2 FC| ≥ 1), including 610 upregulated genes and 90 downregulated genes (Fig. 9A-B). Functional annotation of ZNF132-Related differentially expressed genes

Comprehensive GO and KEGG analyses, conducted using the “clusterProfiler” R package, revealed that 700 differentially expressed genes are implicated in various biological processes. These processes include the phospholipase C-activating G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway, positive regulation of cell junction assembly, regulation of vasoconstriction, extracellular matrix organization, regulation of megakaryocyte differentiation, and negative regulation of myeloid cell differentiation (Fig. 10A). At the molecular function level, these genes are associated with potassium ion transmembrane transporter activity, voltage-gated potassium channel activity, voltage-gated ion channel activity, voltage-gated cation channel activity, G protein-coupled peptide receptor activity, and transmembrane transporter binding (Fig. 10B). Additionally, their distribution across cellular components includes nucleosomes, postsynaptic membranes, synaptic membranes, intrinsic components of the postsynaptic membrane, protein-DNA complexes, and DNA packaging complexes (Fig. 10C). Notably, KEGG analysis underscored their involvement in various pathways, including systemic lupus erythematosus, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, calcium signaling pathway, PPAR signaling pathway, and the renin-angiotensin system (Fig. 10D).

To further investigate the biological implications of ZNF132 expression patterns, GSEA was performed utilizing Reactome and KEGG pathway databases. The analysis revealed significant enrichment of twelve critical pathways linked to ZNF132 overexpression, including DNA damage response (reactome: DNA damage/telomere stress-induced senescence), cell cycle regulation (reactome: Cyclin A/CDK2-associated events at S-phase entry), and chromatin remodeling (reactome: Regulation of RUNX3 expression and activity). Importantly, molecular pathways related to programmed cell death regulation were prominently represented, encompassing both apoptotic execution phase (reactome: Apoptotic execution phase) and pathological cell death processes (reactome: Diseases associated with apoptotic dysfunction). Cellular communication pathways demonstrated particular enrichment, with notable findings in interleukin-7 signaling (reactome: Interleukin-7 signaling) and lymphocyte interaction mechanisms (reactome: Immunoregulatory interactions between lymphoid/non-lymphoid cells). The identified pathways also implicated ZNF132 in extracellular matrix modulation through focal adhesion signaling (kegg: Focal adhesion) and complement cascade activation (wp: Complement system), while maintaining involvement in DNA repair mechanisms (reactome: Nucleotide excision repair). All enriched pathways reached statistical significance thresholds (Fig. 10E-P). These findings provide critical insights into the molecular mechanisms through which ZNF132 may influence the initiation and progression of colorectal cancer.

ZNF132 inhibits proliferation, migration, and invasion of colorectal cancer cells

To elucidate the functional role of ZNF132 in colorectal cancer progression, we overexpressed ZNF132 in HCT116 cells and knocked down the gene in SW480 cells, using empty vector transfection as a control (Fig. 11A and B). We employed CCK-8 and colony formation assays to evaluate the impact of ZNF132 on the proliferative capacity of colorectal cancer cells. The results demonstrated that ZNF132 overexpression significantly inhibited the proliferation of HCT116 cells (p < 0.01, Fig. 11C), while ZNF132 knockdown markedly enhanced the proliferation of SW480 cells (p < 0.05, Fig. 11D). Consistent results were obtained in the colony formation assays (Fig. 11E-F).

Furthermore, we assessed the effects of ZNF132 on the migration and invasion capabilities of colorectal cancer cells using scratch assays and Transwell migration and invasion assays. The findings indicated that ZNF132 knockdown significantly increased the migration and invasion abilities of the cells, whereas ZNF132 overexpression effectively suppressed these capabilities (Fig. 12A-D). Discussion

ZNF132, a member of the zinc finger protein family, has attracted considerable attention due to its potential involvement in transcriptional regulation and epigenetic modifications. Previous studies have shown that zinc finger proteins play pivotal roles in cell cycle control, apoptosis, and tumor suppression by mediating DNA binding activity [24–26]. However, the expression pattern of ZNF132 across various cancers and its specific mechanistic role in CRC progression remain insufficiently characterized. In recent years, the dysregulation of tumor suppressor proteins and their impact on immune microenvironment reprogramming have emerged as key areas of research. For example, well-established tumor suppressors, such as TP53 and PTEN, regulate immune evasion by modulating immune checkpoint molecules and inflammatory cytokine secretion [27, 28]. However, whether ZNF132 contributes to CRC progression through analogous mechanisms remains unclear. In this study, by integrating multi-omics data and functional assays, we present the first comprehensive evidence of ZNF132 downregulation in CRC, its prognostic relevance, and its role in immune regulation, thereby shedding light on its multifaceted function as a potential novel tumor suppressor.

As a member of the zinc finger protein family, ZNF132 is a transcriptional regulator characterized by its zinc finger domain. In the present study, we observed a significant downregulation of ZNF132 in CRC. Furthermore, its expression level was found to negatively correlate with patient age, M stage, and pathological grade, suggesting that ZNF132 may function as a tumor suppressor and be involved in the malignant progression of CRC. This finding aligns with previous reports from studies in breast cancer and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, where low ZNF132 expression was significantly associated with aggressive tumor traits and poor prognosis [13, 16]. Importantly, through multivariate Cox regression analysis, we provide the first evidence that ZNF132 serves as an independent prognostic factor for survival in CRC patients, independent of the traditional TNM staging system. These findings introduce a novel molecular biomarker for clinical prognosis evaluation [29, 30]. Regarding diagnostic value, ZNF132 exhibited a high AUC in ROC analysis, particularly in advanced-stage patients. This result mirrors the clinical validation trends of established methylation markers, such as SEPT9 and NDRG4, in CRC [31, 32], suggesting that ZNF132 may participate in early tumor evolution via DNA methylation and other epigenetic mechanisms [15]. Further subgroup analyses revealed that the prognostic predictive power of ZNF132 remained consistent across various T stages, N stages, and pathological grades. This finding is in agreement with studies in breast cancer, where ZNF132 expression was correlated with tumor size and lymph node metastasis [14]. Notably, we identified a significant association between low ZNF132 expression and M1 stage, likely due to its regulation of target genes involved in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and extracellular matrix remodeling. For instance, in breast cancer, ZNF132 has been shown to restrict metastasis by inhibiting miR-21-3p-mediated EMT [33]. Additionally, GSEA from this study suggests that ZNF132 may influence CRC metastasis through modulation of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway and cell adhesion-related processes [34, 35]. Moreover, the relationship between ZNF132 expression and pathological grade indicates its potential role in regulating cellular differentiation, akin to other zinc finger family members, such as ZEB1/ZEB2, which regulate tumor differentiation by modulating stem cell characteristics [36, 37].

This study combines DEG analysis and functional annotation to elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms of ZNF132 in colorectal cancer [38]. Among the 700 differentially expressed genes identified in the ZNF132 high-expression group, significant enrichment was observed in critical biological processes, including cell cycle regulation (such as DNA damage response and telomere stress-induced senescence), cell junction assembly, and Wnt signaling pathways. GSEA further indicates that ZNF132 may influence tumor progression through the regulation of DNA damage repair, programmed cell death, and the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade. These results are consistent with prior studies, which show that ZNF132 promotes tumor invasion in breast cancer through epigenetic silencing, leading to cell cycle dysregulation and activation of EMT [13], suggesting a similar mechanism may suppress malignant phenotypes in colorectal cancer. Bioinformatics analysis reveals that ZNF132 plays a crucial role in the regulation of apoptosis-related genes. Research has demonstrated that the KRAB-ZFP family member ZNF471, upon epigenetic silencing and inactivation in renal cancer, significantly inhibits apoptosis while promoting cell proliferation; restoration of its expression can activate apoptotic effects by blocking the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [39]. This finding provides a mechanistic example of how zinc finger proteins can influence tumor progression through direct regulation of apoptotic pathways, further supporting the notion that the tumor-suppressive function of ZNF132 may be closely linked to its modulation of apoptosis pathways.

Additionally, the differential gene enrichment in neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions and calcium signaling pathways suggests that ZNF132 may regulate intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment. This observation aligns with findings in head and neck cancer, where ZNF132 modulates the tumor immune microenvironment by influencing inflammation-related pathways [16]. Experimental validation further confirmed the tumor-suppressive role of ZNF132: overexpression of ZNF132 significantly inhibited colorectal cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while silencing ZNF132 yielded the opposite effects. This is in agreement with previous studies in breast cancer, where ZNF132 suppresses tumorigenesis by inhibiting EMT and cell cycle progression [33]. Importantly, ZNF132’s regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway appears to be central to its functional mechanism. Previous studies have highlighted that aberrant activation of Wnt signaling is a key driver of colorectal cancer [40, 41]. In the present study, Wnt pathway-related genes (e.g., TCF-dependent signals) were significantly enriched in the ZNF132 high-expression group, suggesting that ZNF132 may suppress tumor progression by antagonizing Wnt signaling. Additionally, the activation of DNA damage repair pathways may be linked to the senescence phenotype induced by ZNF132, which aligns with the classical mechanism in which tumor suppressor genes restrict malignant proliferation by inducing replicative senescence in colorectal cancer [42, 43].

Moreover, this study establishes an association between ZNF132 and the PPAR signaling pathway, as well as extracellular matrix remodeling, offering new insights into its multifaceted tumor-suppressive mechanisms. Previous research has demonstrated that PPARγ agonists inhibit colorectal cancer progression by promoting tumor cell differentiation [44], and ZNF132 may enhance cellular differentiation potential through regulation of this pathway. In the context of the extracellular matrix, ZNF132’s modulation of collagen deposition and matrix stiffness may influence tumor cell mechanosensation. Although the current experiments primarily focus on the intrinsic effects of ZNF132 on cells, the combined results from immune infiltration analysis suggest that ZNF132 may exert its tumor-suppressive effects via a dual mechanism: shaping the tumor immune microenvironment while directly modulating the malignant phenotype of cancer cells. These findings provide valuable directions for future research [45, 46].

This study identifies a significant correlation between ZNF132 expression and immune cell infiltration in colorectal cancer, highlighting its potential critical role in regulating the TME [47]. Specifically, ZNF132 is negatively correlated with Th17 cells and NK CD56bright cells, while positively correlated with T helper cells, central memory T cells (Tcm), macrophages, and Th2 cells. These findings are consistent with previous studies on tumor immune regulation. For example, Th17 cells exhibit dual roles in colorectal cancer: on one hand, they promote inflammation and tumor angiogenesis by secreting IL-17, while on the other hand, they may suppress CD8 + T cell function, contributing to the development of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [48]. The observed negative correlation between ZNF132 and Th17 cells suggests that ZNF132 may indirectly influence tumor progression by suppressing Th17-mediated pro-inflammatory or immunosuppressive signals. In addition, NK CD56bright cells, a regulatory NK cell subset, typically exhibit low cytotoxicity but high cytokine secretion. The negative correlation between ZNF132 and this cell population suggests that ZNF132 may modulate NK cell function, thereby influencing the anti-tumor immune response [49]. The positive correlation between ZNF132 and T helper cells, Tcm, and macrophages further underscores its potential role in immune regulation [50]. T helper cells are pivotal in coordinating adaptive immunity, and the increased infiltration of Tcm, as key maintainers of immune memory, is associated with enhanced anti-tumor immune responses [51]. Macrophages display high plasticity within the TME; M1 macrophages exert anti-tumor effects through pro-inflammatory and antigen-presenting functions, while M2 macrophages promote tumor progression by secreting immunosuppressive factors [52, 53]. Although this study did not differentiate macrophage subtypes, the positive correlation between ZNF132 and macrophages suggests that ZNF132 may influence the TME by modulating macrophage polarization, possibly through pathways such as NF-κB or STAT signaling. For example, in breast cancer, ZNF132 methylation has been shown to modulate the recruitment and function of tumor-associated macrophages via epigenetic mechanisms [13]. Similarly, in colorectal cancer, ZNF132 may alter the immune cell infiltration pattern by regulating the expression of chemokines or cell adhesion molecules. The positive correlation between ZNF132 and Th2 cells should be interpreted in the context of its pro-tumor effects. Cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-5, secreted by Th2 cells, promote M2 macrophage polarization and the development of a fibrotic microenvironment, supporting tumor growth and metastasis [54]. This paradoxical relationship likely reflects the multifaceted role of ZNF132 within the TME: while ZNF132 may inhibit tumor progression by enhancing immune surveillance through T helper and Tcm cells, it may also promote tumor progression by facilitating Th2-mediated immunosuppressive pathways. Similar dual roles have been observed for other tumor suppressors, such as p53, which can induce apoptosis while also promoting tumor survival under certain conditions via metabolic reprogramming [55, 56]. Mechanistically, ZNF132 may influence immune cell chemotaxis or function by regulating differentially expressed genes, including those involved in calcium signaling, PPAR pathways, and extracellular matrix-related genes, as revealed by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. For instance, the calcium signaling pathway modulates T cell activation by regulating T cell receptor (TCR) signaling [57], while the PPAR pathway plays a key role in macrophage polarization and lipid metabolism [58]. These findings are consistent with previous research, such as studies in head and neck cancer, where low ZNF132 expression was linked to an immunosuppressive TME and potential immune evasion through modulation of the EMT pathway [16].

Although this study provides a comprehensive analysis of the tumor-suppressive function of ZNF132 in colorectal cancer and its potential role in immune regulation, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the data primarily derive from a retrospective cohort analysis based on the TCGA database, with samples predominantly sourced from a single public repository. This limitation introduces potential biases related to population selection and clinical heterogeneity, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Second, while functional assays have confirmed the inhibitory effects of ZNF132 on colorectal cancer cell proliferation and invasion, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these effects remain incompletely understood. Finally, although a significant correlation between ZNF132 expression and immune cell infiltration has been identified, the specific mechanisms through which ZNF132 modulates the tumor immune microenvironment—whether via secreted factors or direct cell-cell interactions—require further investigation using advanced techniques such as single-cell RNA sequencing or spatial transcriptomics. In summary, future research should adopt a multidisciplinary approach to further delineate the tumor-suppressive network involving ZNF132 and assess its potential as a therapeutic target in clinical settings.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of ZNF132 as a novel tumor suppressor in colorectal cancer. ZNF132 is significantly downregulated at both the tissue and protein levels in colorectal cancer, with its reduced expression strongly correlated with advanced tumor stages, poor prognosis, and immune microenvironment remodeling. Importantly, ZNF132 plays a key role in immune regulation by modulating the infiltration of Th17 cells, T helper cells, and macrophages. Functional assays further demonstrate that ZNF132 significantly suppresses colorectal cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Clinical analyses indicate that ZNF132 possesses high diagnostic accuracy and independent prognostic value, with exceptional performance in multivariate prognostic models, highlighting its robust predictive capacity. This study identifies ZNF132 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for colorectal cancer diagnosis and treatment. ZNF132 may synergistically suppress tumor progression by modulating both malignant phenotypes and immune microenvironments. However, the precise regulatory network and immune modulatory mechanisms underlying ZNF132’s function require further investigation.
