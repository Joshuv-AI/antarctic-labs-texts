Clinical Validation, Bias, and Ethical Deployment of Artificial Intelligence in Imaging
Authors: Arjun Kalyanpur, Neetika Mathur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into radiology practice has the potential to transform the health care sector in the setting of ongoing radiologist shortages. AI extends powerful tools to radiologists that enhance both diagnostic precision and reporting efficiency. A key requirement is that an AI model must display reliable performance across a diversity of radiologic data differing broadly across institutions, with varying imaging equipment and patient demographics. Thus, clinical validation by the radiologist is critical. Another important consideration is the smooth and seamless integration of AI into the radiology workflow without impeding clinical flow. It is also important, when deploying AI, to pay attention to its regulatory and ethical aspects. AI algorithms utilized for image analysis fall within the regulatory umbrella of medical devices and need to address corresponding safety and quality standards. Data privacy protections are paramount to ensure patient confidentiality. Decisions for the deployment of AI should be governed by ethical principles. Overall, for all entities, a governance framework is necessary, providing protocols underlining roles and responsibilities in AI deployment and utilization. Governance committees and regular performance audits are crucial. Initial training followed by periodic follow-up training of radiologists and administrators on functions and pitfalls of AI algorithms should be the norm. By emphasizing regulatory compliance, combined with initial and periodic clinical validation, radiologists and health care institutions as well as teleradiology centers can efficiently utilize AI to enhance diagnostic quality and proficiency, while maintaining the critical element of trust with clinical departments and patients.

Introduction

The digital transformation of imaging has brought about a significant shift in radiology, allowing scalable storage, rapid transmission, and precision post-processing. Technological advancement has brought about the emergence of high-end imaging technologies such as multi-detector computed tomography (CT), digital radiography, ultrasonography, and high-field magnetic resonance imaging, all ultimately generating a prodigious amount of data for radiologists to analyze. 1 In a setting of ongoing radiologist shortages coupled with a continuous increase in imaging volumes, artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to bridge the supply–demand gap by simultaneously enhancing radiologist accuracy and productivity. Owing to its digital architecture, AI today has demonstrated the potential to transform the field of digital imaging by assisting radiologists and ensuring accurate diagnosis, predictive analytics, and tailored treatment plans. 2 3 AI tools have been integrated into teleradiology practice as a result of synergy with its data-rich environment and structured workflows. 4 5 6 At this time, more than 900 AI/machine learning devices have received regulatory clearance from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), with radiology accounting for the largest fraction (76%). 7 AI is increasingly operating as a clinical decision-support tool in India and worldwide, improving diagnostic accuracy, enhancing workflow efficiency, and lowering reporting turnaround times, validated through a number of studies. 8 9 10 11 12 13 In 2026, the India AI Summit, organized in collaboration with World Health Organization, exhibited AI health applications, ranging from AI-enabled medical devices to innovations in early detection and classification of neurological and cardiological diseases. AI-supported diagnostic technology is being used by hospitals and governmental projects, establishing that AI, when developed and deployed responsibly, ethically, and inclusively, can function as a robust tool for clinical decision support, thereby benefitting the health care system overall. 14

In a nationwide survey study conducted by Chen et al among radiology residents on their perception of AI, it was found that 72% of the participants felt AI enhances disease diagnosis and 78% believed radiologists should adopt AI. The study also emphasized the importance of medical training and continuous medical education to support both trainees and practicing radiologists and encourage them to embrace AI. 15 Another online survey among members from the European Society of Radiology/European Network for the Assessment of Imaging in Medicine specified that AI adoption in radiology practices continues to rise, with notable advancement in workflow efficiency, while also requiring human oversight. 16 This technological advancement brings forth a number of clinical, ethical, and regulatory dimensions that must be thoroughly examined to ensure responsible implementation.

Recent publications in the Western literature emphasize the need for establishing a clear governance structure/framework when implementing AI within a radiology department, whether it be a large tertiary care academic center, a diagnostic chain, or a teleradiology service provider. Such a framework allows for planned and efficient implementation and deployment, while ensuring that safeguards are maintained and that patient safety and confidentiality are preserved throughout the process. For example, it is recommended that an AI algorithm be first deployed and tested in a nonclinical setting, followed by shadow mode (wherein performance is assessed in the form of retrospective analysis), then limited clinical deployment with a few test users, and ultimately full clinical utilization by all users. 17

This article seeks to provide insights into the need for AI integration in radiology, exemplifying key use cases, highlighting operational and regulatory impediments, and proposing a governance and compliance framework focusing on the Indian regulatory system. A structured implementation plan with human-in-the-loop oversight, interoperability standards, and constant validation is paramount for secure and functional AI espousal.

Methodology

A literature search was conducted using the PubMed database, focusing on articles published in English-language journals between 2016 and 2025. Keywords such as “Ethics of AI in Imaging,” “Ethical Deployment of AI in Imaging,” “Clinical Validation for AI in healthcare,” “AI Model generalizability,” “bias,” “explainability,” “data privacy,” “patient safety,” “ethical considerations,” and “Indian healthcare” were used in different combinations in the search. The selection criteria focused on identifying peer-reviewed articles and publications relevant to the ethical consideration of AI in imaging. Inclusion criteria targeted only original research articles, reviews, and systematic reviews written in English and with full-text availability. Exclusion criteria targeted studies that were outside the specified date range, non-English articles, not peer-reviewed, duplicate studies, editorials, conference proceedings, commentaries, abstracts without full-text access, and other irrelevant studies.

Twenty-three duplicate studies were removed, 568 studies were screened for title and abstract, and 404 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The Need for AI Integration in Radiology

There has been a significant rise in imaging utilization in India and globally. The Indian diagnostic imaging market is growing rapidly due to an increase in chronic illness, rising population, increasing accidents, and emergency cases that utilize medical imaging. Furthermore, the advancement of technologies, increased health care awareness among people, supportive governmental plans, oncology imaging, population screening programs, strategic partnerships, and improvement in reimbursement policies elevate the demand for imaging. Unfortunately, there has been a shortage of correspondingly trained radiologists to interpret them. 18 In India, there are approximately 20,000 radiologists for a 1.4 billion population, i.e., 1 radiologist per 70,000 population. Further, subspecialty radiologists are few and far between and are mainly concentrated in large tertiary care hospitals and academic institutions, significantly contributing to workload pressures and burnout issues. 19

Teleradiology and AI, both being information technology-powered systems, synergistically revolutionize health care delivery. 4 20 While teleradiology services promise health care access to remote locations, subspecialty opinions, and combat radiologist shortages, 21 AI assists in triaging, prioritization, and structured reporting to reduce cognitive load. By integrating with teleradiology practice, AI's promise is to transform radiologic diagnostics by improving detection, reducing errors, enhancing accuracy, and lowering the time required to interpret images ( Fig. 1 ). 2 14 22 23 AI offers potential to reduce interobserver variability, standardize measurements, and support reproducible reporting. Successful AI deployment requires collaboration between AI engineers, physicians, regulators, and administrators. 24

Some Clinical Use Cases of AI in Radiology

In general, AI improves image analysis by allowing precise lesion identification and characterization, automatic volumetric quantification, longitudinal growth tracking in oncology, and assessment of volume of disease burden, thus enhancing diagnostic consistency and efficiency in the teleradiology practice setting. 25 26

In emergency non-contrast CT (NCCT) brain imaging, AI identifies intracranial hemorrhage, midline shift, large vessel occlusion, and triages cases in the radiologist's worklist, prioritizing interpretation of high-risk studies quickly, particularly in the teleradiology setting. By lowering the door-to-report interval, AI enables prompt diagnosis and expedites decision-making in acute stroke, including thrombolysis or thrombectomy, eventually enhancing patient outcomes. 2 14 27 28 In a study, an AI algorithm classified (accuracy of 85%), localized, and quantified hemorrhages with 92% sensitivity and 83% specificity. 2 A study by Endler et al on 197 NCCT scans reported 95.9% sensitivity and 84.6% specificity of an AI tool in identifying intracranial hemorrhages, but also displayed an elevated false-positive rate as compared to the radiologist's report, indicating the need for human supervision in diagnosis. 29

AI algorithms perform critical tasks in chest imaging by aiding in the detection of pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, pneumonia, tuberculosis, and lung nodules. Under high-volume conditions, such as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) or tuberculosis screening, AI systems can quickly underscore suspicious findings, measure disease progression, and enhance diagnostic sensitivity, while allowing effective caseload prioritization for radiologists, as validated in several studies. 30 31 32 33 34 In a previous study, AI detected pneumonia with a sensitivity of 92% and a specificity of 77%. 8 Another study reported a deep learning algorithm that detected pneumonia in chest radiographs with a sensitivity of 95.4%, a specificity of 66.0%, and reduced the turnaround time for reporting the relevant findings and assisted early triaging. 32

AI identifies suspicious calcifications and asymmetric densities, particularly in dense breasts. Acting as a triage tool, AI enhances cancer detection rates while maintaining controlled recall rates. For example, AI can analyze mammograms for triaging cases for early-stage breast cancer detection. It can evaluate breast parenchyma composition, focal asymmetry, calcification, architectural distortion, lesion, lymph node, shape, size, location, density, and BI-RADS categorization. Workflow Optimization, Integration, and Interoperability

AI streamlines radiology workflows through case prioritization (AI-based triage), report generation assistance, and structured reporting prompts, leading to reduced turnaround time and improved reporting quality. 36 Successful AI systems work seamlessly within existing radiology information systems, automating tasks without impeding clinical flow. 36 Effective AI integration into clinical practice requires seamless connectivity with PACS (Picture Archiving and Communication Systems), RIS (Radiology Information Systems), EHR (Electronic Health Records), DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine), HL7 (Health Level-7) required for clinical data exchange between health care professionals located at geographically diverse places, and FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) for modern web-based interoperability. 22 AI orchestration platforms act as “conductors,” coordinating multiple AI models, large language models, and workflows to function cohesively within clinical systems. AI interfaces must remain intuitive and embedded within existing radiologist workflows, allowing radiologists to easily recognize AI-generated findings within their familiar viewing environment. 13 23

AI Governance Framework

For the responsible development, deployment, and operation of AI in radiology, a robust AI governance framework consisting of structured ethical guidelines, legal principles, and regulatory requirements should be constructed. It should establish guidelines for safety, privacy, and fairness to manage risks like bias and misuse. Multidisciplinary collaboration among clinicians, AI developers, ethicists, legal experts, and policymakers is crucial to achieve such integration.

Phased Approach to AI Implementation

AI clinical validation and deployment in the radiology workflow can be conceptualized in different phases. Phase 1 involves internal evaluation and training of the model in nonclinical settings, where the model's performance is monitored on retrospective data. Based on this “internal validation” by an internal reviewers' team comprising radiologists and technologists, the model is further refined and trained. An approval from the institutional review and ethical committee should be obtained for an AI research project. Phase 2 comprises restricted deployment of the AI model under the clinical workflow setting but with limited test user access and is trained on both retrospective and prospective datasets. Phase 3 is the final full-scale deployment of AI into the clinical radiology workflow, where the AI algorithm's impact on workflow, decisions, or patient outcomes is measured. The AI outputs are utilized as a triage tool within a human-in-the-loop model, where a radiologist provides the final confirmation/validation. 37 It is followed by post-deployment surveillance, where continued monitoring and tracking of model performance after integration into clinical practice is performed. Clinical Validation and Quality Assurance

Radiologist validation of generalizability, reliability, and impact of AI output on prospective and multicenter data is essential to evaluate the performance of AI algorithms in clinical radiology practice. At the time of training the algorithm, the radiologist must analyze the images and define regions of interest to establish the ground truth, which is then used to train the algorithm. Subsequently, during validation, the AI output is compared with the ground truth for each case, and a true class is established. Continuous monitoring and validation of false positives/negatives by radiologists, preparation of performance dashboards and regular audits, and repeatedly evaluating newer test cases against the metric are essential for training, fine-tuning, and refining of AI. 38 39 The goal of such a process is to investigate and attempt to eliminate false negatives, while at the same time minimizing the number of false positives, as the latter can result in delays in interpretation in a real-time clinical environment.

Data Bias

Bias is a factor or prejudice that causes an AI algorithm to generate unequal outcomes. The performance of AI may vary in real-world clinical practice as compared to that under controlled research settings or a sandbox (i.e., isolated and secure) environment, due to variations in equipment, patient demographics, and clinical protocols. Data bias can emerge from data imbalance, differences across subgroups, institutional variability, and algorithm design. As a result of bias, AI models may not generalize, leading to disparities in care or unfair or even potentially harmful outcomes. In clinical imaging, bias can appear from marginalization or decreased representation of certain demographic cohorts (e.g., age, ethnicity, sex), which would in turn result in underperformance of the algorithm for those groups. Radiologist annotation errors or subjective interpretations may introduce noise that also biases the model. 40 41 Presence of artifacts and confounders (such as any medical implant) in scans may further lead to spurious correlations by AI, and not actual pathological features. AI algorithms may also manifest the outcome of overfitting, wherein the algorithm over-interprets/overcalls the finding, when the data utilized for training did not optimally represent the population on which the algorithm is being used. 42 Thus, prospective, multicenter studies, multi-imaging, demographic variance, and real-world performance monitoring and validation are essential. 43 Moreover, quality assurance must be in place to detect model drift or performance degradation over time. Model drift occurs when changes in input data that occur over time impact model performance (an example would be the COVID-19 pandemic, where the prevalence of a single entity dominated the clinical spectrum). 44 45

“Black Box” or unexplainable AI output is another pitfall. 46 Understanding how an AI arrives at its conclusions is crucial for developing trust and ensuring accountability. Explainable AI is important to allow clinicians to verify system reasoning. 47 48 49

Importantly, several AI tools are categorized as Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) and must fulfill precise standards for security, effectiveness, and transparency, including Good Machine Learning Practices. 50 Randomized controlled trials and post-market surveillance are often required to monitor real-world performance, while labelling must clearly define intended use, performance metrics, and limitations. It cannot be overemphasized that AI outputs should always function as advisory tools within a human-in-the-loop model. 51 52 Continuous education and training of radiology staff, physicians, and technologists on AI limitations should be done in this regard to ensure that overdependence on AI output does not occur over time. 53

Risk Assessment

Risk is the assessment of the ability of AI to cause harm, and the assessment of risk is an important part of the governance framework for AI implementation. 54 It is crucial to classify AI applications in radiology as high- or low-risk applications, necessitating compliance with specific requirements and obligations. 55 Ethical risk relates to the violation of standards or governance policies. 56 Operational risk refers to data drift, hallucinations, and AI errors, which can impact a company's day-to-day operations. The result can be increased downtime, communication disruptions, and inaccurate predictions, causing businesses to make wrong decisions. Security risk is related to data corruption or a security breach. Moreover, risk related to incorrect model selection, poor design, and inadequate validation leads to unreliable outputs. 57

Data Protection, Patient Consent, and Confidentiality

AI development and training leverage huge datasets, which prompt concerns regarding the potential risk of data breaches or unauthorized access, which may compromise patient data confidentiality and data security. Patient consent maintains autonomy by offering patients control over their health data and understanding of the role of AI in their diagnoses, while confidentiality checks unauthorized access, fostering trust. 55 58 59 60 This requires a robust cybersecurity system, stringent implementation of data protection laws, and training of health care professionals on data security best practices.

For AI development, anonymization and encryption of the patient data should be enforced. Further, compliance with regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act assures that patient-identifiable health information is guarded and enacts strict rules for patient data sharing.

In India, governmental programs such as the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission have expedited the creation, interchange, and interoperability of digital health data at an unparalleled level. The India AI Datasets (AlKosh) program aims to support AI-powered innovation by authorizing Indian startups, researchers, and AI engineers access to quality and nonpersonal data. 61

NITI (National Institution for Transforming India) Aayog started the National Data and Analytics Platform to democratize access to public government data by making foundational datasets from various government agencies accessible, interoperable, interactive, and available on a user-friendly platform. 62

Regulatory Landscape in India

Several government regulatory bodies have enacted laws/acts for ethical and legal considerations, such as data privacy, responsibility, accountability, transparency, and explainability in AI, and implementation of AI in imaging. In India, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) aims for the empowerment of patients with data fiduciary obligations regarding the usage of individual privacy rights (consent, access, correction, and deletion) with lawful processing needs, not only to digital data within India but also overseas processing related to Indian services. Under DPDA, Data Protection Officers are required to be appointed by an institution to check for potential data security breaches and ensure appropriate notification in such cases, while the Act also makes reference to significant penalties in cases of data breach. 63

On October 15, 2025, the Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO) categorized radiology AI algorithms as SaMDs. It has provided a draft guidance document to elucidate the particular regulatory requirements under the Medical Device Rules (MDR), 2017, necessitating approvals for safety and efficacy. The key elements of the draft of the intended use of medical device software involve the description of clinical application (whether interpretation, diagnosis, treatment, or monitoring of disease), concerned disease or finding, end users, patient population, and AI algorithm input, output, and workflow description. It ensures risk-based classification (Class A–D) of AI algorithms. AI algorithms related to oncologic disease detection have recently been classified as category C, while for other disease entities, the corresponding category is B. 64 Post-approval changes under an approved algorithm change protocol regarding any bug fixes and security patches should be notified prior to deployment. Furthermore, the post-market surveillance to identify performance troubles and corrective actions is mandatory to be performed on a regular basis. 65

Indian Council of Medical Research, in collaboration with National Medical Commission, has enacted important ethical guidelines for the development of AI tools in health care (2023), underscoring data privacy, transparency, and demarcation of accountability between AI developers and health care providers. 66 The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare launched the Strategy for Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare for India (SAHI) to guide the ethical, transparent, and people-centric use of AI. It provides a governance framework for both the public and private sectors. Through the National Health Authority, the Ministry developed BODH (Benchmarking Open Data Platform for Health AI) to test and validate AI solutions against real-world data, ensuring performance, reliability, and fairness before they are deployed at scale. The ministry also works with CDSCO to regulate AI-based medical devices and software. 67

The regulatory frameworks for AI in radiology are also developing rapidly at the international level to ensure patient safety, transparency, and ethical use. The FDA regulates AI-enabled medical devices through the existing SaMD approval pathway, with a focus on regular performance monitoring, validation, and adaptive learning systems. 68 The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act, adopted by 27 member countries in March 2024, classifies health care AI applications, such as radiology tools, as “high-risk” systems that need to fulfill precise requirements for risk management, human oversight, data governance, and explainability. 69 Meanwhile, the World Health Organization has released guidelines and ethical principles for AI development and adoption in health care, such as transparency, accountability, patient privacy, fairness, and equitable access to health care technologies. 70 The United Nations Educational Scientific and Cultural Organization emphasizes international AI governance and ethical considerations, focusing on the value of human rights and transparency in AI deployment. 71 72 Conjointly, these regulatory frameworks are created to ensure secure, sound, and responsible integration of AI in clinical radiology practice.

Conclusion

The integration of AI into radiology practice is not simply a technological advancement but rather a strategic tool that serves to leapfrog the challenges of conventional diagnostic approaches and allows for expanded access to health care services, especially in the current setting of radiologist shortages. The introduction of AI into radiology brings in its wake ethical and regulatory issues that need to be understood and addressed. Data privacy, security, and informed consent are areas that require focused attention. Simultaneously, risk and algorithmic bias are all areas that require focus, since the performance of AI algorithms is dependent on the training dataset on which the algorithm has been trained. At an institutional level, approval from an institutional review board should be mandatory for all AI research projects. A governance framework and structured implementation plan with radiologist-in-the-loop supervision and regular validation are paramount for secure and functional AI deployment. Interdisciplinary collaboration among clinicians, AI developers, ethicists, legal experts, and policymakers is necessary to achieve such integration. For pragmatic solutions, radiologists, technologists, and other clinical and nonclinical staff should engage in continuous education and training on AI advancements as well as limitations, communicate with AI developers, transparently interact with patients about AI utilization, and ensure ethical and legal regulatory compliance. Radiologists utilizing AI tools must be familiar with AI limitations, curb overdependence on AI, and own the clinical outcomes. Approvals from government regulatory bodies for considerations such as data privacy, accountability, transparency, and quality in the implementation of AI in imaging are paramount. These measures will ensure the integration of AI effectively and ethically into radiological practice, so that the maximum benefits of AI may be derived.
