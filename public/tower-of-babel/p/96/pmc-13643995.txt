Plasma proteomic profiling deciphers molecular dynamics linking insulin resistance to multiple chronic diseases and mortality
Authors: Zong-Lei Zhou, Li-Gang Zhou, Chun-Hua Qu, Chun-Xiang Fan, Hai-Hong Qu, Jun Li, Cheng-Xia Wei, Wu Wang, Xu-Hui Bao, Ji-Yu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsulin resistance (IR) correlates with a wide spectrum of diseases and death. However, the specific proteomic signatures of IR and their associations with health outcomes remain incompletely understood.MethodsLeveraging data of 2,920 plasma proteins from 19,556 individuals in UK biobank study, we employed the elastic net model to dissect IR-related proteins and construct proteomic signature scores. Cox proportional hazards model was fitted to examine the longitudinal associations of distinct IR indicators and their proteomic signatures with multiple chronic disease and mortality. Mediation analyses were conducted to explore the role of individual proteins and proteomic signatures in IR-disease associations.ResultsOver a mean follow-up of over 12 years, higher levels of IR surrogate makers were linked to cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic events and mortality, with eGDR outperforming other metrics in predictive discrimination. Furthermore, most IR proteomic signatures were prospectively associated with the incidence of type 2 diabetes mellitus, ischemic heart diseases, stroke, chronic kidney diseases, and mortality. Proteins associated with IR were primarily enriched in inflammation, immune response, and lipid metabolism, with immune-related proteins holding crucial roles. Multiple IR-disease associations were significantly mediated by proteomic signatures and specific proteins, like HAVCR1, CXCL17, and GDF15.ConclusionThis study probed into the plasma proteomic profiles in IR settings and the associations of relevant protein signatures with multiple chronic diseases and mortality, providing additional guidelines on targeted intervention strategies for cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12933-026-03273-2.

Background

As an essential component of metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance (IR) refers to a diminished reaction of tissues or organs to insulin [1]. Currently, growing evidence has suggested the associations between IR indices and adverse outcomes in cardiovascular and metabolic disturbance [2–5]. Besides, IR status is connected with the incidence of multiple cancers and mortality, possibly attributed to secondary endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress [6–9]. However, the exact biological responses in IR scenarios and their influences on health risks remain far from understood.

As a blood-based biomarkers, circulating proteins could function as the direct biological effectors of risk factors from genetic and environmental aspects, as well as the targets for medical intervention [10, 11]. Previous investigations have documented that protein dynamics correlate with disease progression, including myocardial infarction [12], dementia [13], asthma [14], and even death [15]. Despite these findings, prior research rarely gained insights into IR-related proteomic signatures and focused exclusively on single or limited chronic diseases, compromising the interpretation of proteomic links between IR and health outcomes. Given the tedious operability of hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique in IR assessments, a range of reliable IR surrogates have been proposed, like metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR), triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TG/HDL-C), estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), and TyG-derived metrics [16–19]. Notably, each surrogate captures varied aspects of IR phenotype, and the commonalities and differences in proteomic profiles across distinct IR surrogates have yet to be elucidated [20, 21].

To address the aforementioned knowledge gaps, current works further probed into the proteomic signatures for eight IR indicators and their relationships with major chronic diseases and mortality, based on the large-scale longitudinal UK Biobank cohort. Moreover, we examined whether and to what extent circulating proteins mediated the effects of IR on health outcomes in a comprehensive manner. In current study, the designed framework integrating population-based cohorts and high-throughput plasma proteomics offers unique and systematic insights into disease development from different perspectives, thereby allowing to provide stronger evidence beyond those afforded by each alone.

Methods

Study design and population

As an ongoing prospective cohort, UK Biobank has recruited over 0.5 million participants from England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010. All participants were required to complete touch-screen questionnaires to collect socio-demographic information and lifestyle-related data. Further, anthropometric measures and biological sample collection were implemented following standard procedures. Further details of UK Biobank have been reported previously [22].

In this study, the following conditions led to the exclusion of participants: (i) lack of socio-demographic data, like age, gender, ethnicity, education, and household income; (ii) missing information on smoking status, alcohol intake, physical activity, diet, and sleep time; (iii) no information to derive IR surrogates; (iv) lacking proteomic data and laboratory assay; (v) clinical diagnoses of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), ischemic heart diseases (IHD), stroke, chronic kidney diseases (CKD), cancer, and those with acute inflammation at baseline [23]. Accordingly, we excluded individuals with missing data on socio-demography (n = 80,544), no information on lifestyles (n = 119,970), missing IR indicators (n = 14,266), lack of plasma protein quantification (n = 217,698), missing laboratory information (n = 43,617), and participants with a baseline diagnosis of studied diseases or suffering acute inflammation (n = 6,285). Ultimately, a total of 19,556 participants constituted the final study population (Fig. S1). The included participants were comparable to those not included with respect to most baseline characteristics (Table S1).

Plasma proteomic profiling

Baseline blood samples from randomly selected participants were centrifuged and transported to Olink Analysis Service in Sweden for the measurement of protein concentration. Briefly, a total of 2,923 unique proteins involving cardiometabolic analytes, inflammation, neurology, and oncology were quantified using antibody-based Olink Explore 3072 Proximity Extension Assay with eight protein panels. Protein abundance were presented as normalized protein expression (NPX) values. No violate batch effects or plate effects were observed in the dataset (Fig. S2), and further details on protein processing and quality control are available elsewhere [24]. Of the initial 2,923 plasma proteins, we removed those with a missing rate of > 30%, and 2,920 proteins were remained for downstream analyses [25]. The mean substitution method was used to impute missing values [24, 26, 27]. Detailed information on included plasma proteins is documented in Supplementary Table S2.

IR assessment

Prior to the evaluation of IR status, physical examination and blood biochemical analyses were performed to obtain the information on height, weight, waist circumference (WC), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), triglyceride (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). Body mass index (BMI) was computed as weight divided by height squared. Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) was computed as WC divided by height. A body shape index (ABSI) was calculated as described in early literature [28]. Hypertension was defined if fulfilling any of the following conditions: systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or the diagnosis confirmed by physicians. Afterwards, we calculated eight IR indicators to comprehensively assess IR status based on aforementioned indices, including TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, TyG-ABSI, TG/HDL-C, METS-IR, and eGDR [16, 18, 19, 29–31]. Ascertainment of health outcomes

The outcomes in this study were ascertained based on International Classification of Diseases version 10 (ICD-10), including five common chronic diseases (T2DM, IHD, stroke, CKD, cancer) and mortality. The criteria to define the above health outcomes are presented in Supplementary Table S3. Multi-sourced medical records from primary care, hospital admission, death register, and self-reports were employed for the diagnosis of incident diseases and death. Data on cancer and death for all participants were retrieved up to June 1, 2022 and July 8, 2024, respectively. The information for other diseases were available up to July 31, 2024 for England, January 31, 2024 for Scotland, and August 31, 2024 for Wales, respectively. The follow-up duration in person-years was set as the time from the completion of baseline questionnaires to the earliest occurrence of following events: first diagnosis of health outcomes, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up.

Measurement of covariates

To reduce estimation bias, we adjusted for a range of crucial covariates with reference to prior literature and data availability [32, 33]. UK Biobank staff administrated in-person interviews to collect socio-demographic data, including age (< 60 years, ≥ 60 years), gender (male, female), ethnicity (white, non-white), region (England, Scotland, Wales), education (lower secondary, upper secondary, higher, others), annual household income (< €31,000, ≥ €31,000), and Townsend deprivation index (TDI, continuous). For educational background, we designated college or university degree as higher attainment, A/AS levels and O levels/GCSEs as upper and lower secondary group, respectively. Additionally, smoking status (never, previous, current) and alcohol consumption (never, previous, current) were recorded based on their self-reported information. Physical activities were measured using International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and further categorized into three levels: low, moderate, and high. Healthy diet score was computed based on the intake of the following seven items: whole grain, refined grain, vegetable, fruit, fish, processed meat, and unprocessed red meat [34]. For each item, one point was given if fulfilling the corresponding criteria. The total score of healthy diet ranges from 0 to 7, with a higher score suggesting a healthier dietary pattern. Detailed information about the healthy diet score can be found elsewhere [34]. Sleep duration was determined by self-reports, which was grouped into < 6 h, 6–8 h, and ≥ 8 h. Laboratory assays were carried out to ascertain the concentration of C-reactive protein (CRP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and creatinine in peripheral blood. Also, we calculated the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) according to the CKD-EPI equation [35].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics by various outcomes were provided as mean ± SD for continuous variables, and counts and percentages for categorical variables. For between-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Kruskal-Wallis test and Chi-square test were implemented.

This study utilized elastic net (ENET) model to ascertain proteomic biomarkers underlying IR status, with full protein dataset as input. Combining the features of lasso regression and ridge regression, ENET model offers a machine learning algorithm to address variable selection and overfitting [36]. The model performance was optimized through the adjustment of a regularization parameter lambda (λ). Before formal data analyses, the abundance of all proteins was standardized to the same scale. Afterwards, the optimal lambda parameter was determined through 10-fold cross-validation method, with 70% of samples for training and 30% for validation. The lambda with a minimum mean squared error was chosen for further iteration. Then, we fitted ENET regression to estimate the coefficient of each protein using the glmnet function in “glmnet” R package with retrieved tunning parameters. The model performance and penalty intensity parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Then, proteomic signatures were calculated as the weighted sum of selected proteins (coefficients ≠ 0), where the weight referred to the coefficients derived from ENET model. Correlation analyses on IR indicators and protein signatures were carried out using Spearman method. ENET model was fitted by adjusting for age, gender, ethnicity, region, education, household income, TDI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, heathy diet, sleep duration, CRP, eGFR, ALT, and AST. Besides, for IR indices not derived from BMI, further adjustments for BMI were performed.

To unravel potential signaling and pathways involved in disease progression associated with IR, functional annotation on IR-related proteins was carried out using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) database, with “enrichR” R package [37]. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for selected proteins was constructed by the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes database (STRING, version 12.0) with high interaction strength (≥ 0.700) [38], followed by visualization with Cytoscape software (version 3.10.0). Core proteins were discerned using the maximum clique centrality (MCC) algorithm embedded in the Cytohubba plugin of Cytoscape software [39].

Cox proportional hazards model was employed to examine the associations of IR indicators and corresponding proteomic signatures with major chronic diseases and mortality. Proportional hazards assumptions were tested according to scaled Schoenfeld residuals, with no obvious violations observed. Effects sizes were denoted using hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidential interval (CI) for per interquartile range (IQR) increase in exposures. Adjusted P-values were calculated using false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Moreover, we carried out receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis to measure the predictive performance of IR indices for various health outcomes by calculating the area under the curve (AUC), the optimal cut-off values, sensitivity, and specificity. Also, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement index (IDI) were computed to assess the incremental predictive value of IR indictors and proteomic signatures beyond the basic model [40].

Furthermore, we evaluated the mediating roles of individual proteins and proteomic signatures in the associations between IR and major chronic diseases and mortality using “CMAverse” package in R software [41]. Specifically, Cox proportional hazards model was used to predict health outcomes by the IR index and each mediator, adjusting for the same covariates in ENET model. The mediator was then regressed by IR index and covariates in a linear regression model. Afterwards, the fitted outcome and mediator models were employed to decompose the total effect into direct effect and indirect effect, and mediating proportion was estimated via a nonparametric bootstrap procedure with 1,000 iterations.

Sensitivity analyses were carried out through the following approaches: (1) Participants progressing to outcome events within the initial 2 years after baseline were excluded to handle the potential of reverse causality; (2) Participants with extreme obesity defined by BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 were removed from the analyses; (3) We repeated the analyses by further adjusting for total serum proteins, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and serum cystatin C; (4) We excluded participants undergoing specific medical treatments, like aspirin, antihypertensive drug, insulin, and cholesterol-lowering medications; (5) Study population were restricted to Caucasians to address the bias resulting from racial heterogeneity.

All statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.3.1). The statistical significance level was set as a two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 if not otherwise specified.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study population

This study incorporated 19,556 participants in downstream analyses, with average age of 55.16 ± 8.17 years and female accounting for 51.96%. Baseline characteristics by incident chronic diseases and mortality are provided in Table 1. During a mean follow-up spanning 12.51–15.34 years, 1,076 (5.50%) participants progressed to T2DM, 1,479 (7.56%) progressed to IHD, 453 (2.32%) progressed to stroke, 722 (3.69%) progressed to CKD, 2,352 (12.03%) progressed to cancer, and 1,721 (8.80%) deaths were recorded. Compared to females, males showed higher vulnerability to cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic outcomes and mortality. The average estimates of eight IR indicators were 8.67 for TyG, 234.80 for TyG-BMI, 776.85 for TyG-WC, 4.59 for TyG-WHtR, 6.63 for TyG-ABSI, 3.04 for TG/HDL-C, 39.30 for METS-IR, and 9.44 for eGDR, respectively (Table S5). The correlation coefficients between IR indicators ranged from − 0.720 to 0.964, and all other indicators were in a negative association with eGDR, a lower value of which denotes a higher IR level (Fig. S3A).

Pathway enrichment and PPI

Through ENET model, we obtained the proteins linked to different IR indicators, with the counts ranging from 613 to 1,315 (Fig. S4 and S5 and Table S6). Thereafter, we implemented enrichment analysis on KEGG pathways and GO biological processes for these resulting IR-related proteins. As indicated in Fig. 1, KEGG analysis demonstrated significant enrichments in immune function (e.g., cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, complement and coagulation cascades, viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor), inflammation (e.g., IL-17 signaling pathway, NF-kappa B signaling pathway), cell growth and survival (e.g., PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, hematopoietic cell lineage, MAPK signaling pathway, Ras signaling pathway), cell adhesion and migration (e.g., cell adhesion molecules, focal adhesion, ECM-receptor interaction), and other pathways (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis and pathways in cancer). Comparably, GO biological processes were primarily enriched in cytokine-mediated signaling pathway, extracellular matrix organization, cellular response to cytokine stimulus, immune and inflammatory response, and cell proliferation (Fig. S6).

For 331 proteins in consistent and significant associations with at least six IR indicators, similar enriched KEGG pathways were revealed, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (PFDR= 3.15E-12), cholesterol metabolism (PFDR= 8.41E-06), PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (PFDR= 2.83E-05), focal adhesion (PFDR= 0.0003), and ECM-receptor interaction (PFDR= 0.0005) (Fig. S7A). Further, these proteins were employed to constructed a PPI network to unravel the molecular interactions bridging IR and adverse health outcomes. Accordingly, a PPI network consisting of 186 proteins and 352 connected edges was revealed according to STRING database [38]. Proteins with the top rankings based on their MCC scores were CD8A, IFNG, CD86, FASLG, and IDO1 (Table S7 and Figure S7B). Associations of IR status and protein signatures with health outcomes

Proteins surviving ENET model were used to derive proteomic signatures for eight IR indices, among which significant correlations were observed (r = − 0.667 to 0.980, all P-value < 0.001; Figure S3B). Among these IR-related proteins, LDLR, MFGE8, and LEP consistently contributed great weights to the proteomic signature of most IR indicators. Besides, FABP4, TGFBR2, THY1, and ADM were also the predominant contributors to the proteomic signatures related to TyG-BMI and METS-IR. PTPRB, ACTA2, HAVCR1, and ACE played a substantial role in eGDR proteomic signature (Table S6). The pairwise correlations ranged from 0.710 to 0.911 for the associations between proteomic signatures and corresponding IR indicators, suggesting high strength in the relationship (Fig. S8).

As presented in Fig. 2, longitudinal analyses after multivariable adjustment captured generally positive relationships of TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, TyG-ABSI, TG/HDL-C, METS-IR, and their proteomic signatures with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic outcome, whereas eGDR and corresponding proteomic signatures were significantly linked to reduced risks. Detailly, for each IQR increment in exposures, the HRs of major chronic diseases and mortality ranged from 0.97 to 2.19 for TyG and 0.98 to 1.98 for TyG proteomic signatures, 0.95 to 1.25 for TG/HDL-C and 0.96 to 1.51 for TG/HDL-C proteomic signatures, 0.99 to 2.49 for METS-IR and 0.99 to 3.39 for METS-IR proteomic signatures, 0.21 to 0.86 for eGDR and 0.23 to 0.81 for eGDR proteomic signatures. Elevated outcome risks were also observed for those with a high level of TyG-derived IR metrics and their proteomic signatures. Intriguingly, certain heterogeneity was detected in the effects of distinct IR indicators on mortality, where TyG-WHtR, TyG-ABSI, TyG-WC, and eGDR suggested a stronger association compared to other metrics, potentially suggesting differential pathogeneses triggered by various IR indicators. Individual associations of IR-related proteins with major chronic diseases and mortality are illustrated in Figs. S9-S14.

Mediating effects of proteins on the associations between IR and health outcomes

As indicated by mediation analysis with FDR correction, there were 893, 94, 5, 972, and 415 proteins significantly mediating the relationships between various IR measures and T2DM (Table S12), IHD (Table S13), stroke (Table S14), CKD (Table S15), and mortality (Table S16).

Specifically, IGFBP2, LPL, CPM, and GDF15 were revealed as the prominent mediators for IR-T2DM associations, with mediated proportions of 2.82-34.27% (Fig. 4A). CXCL17, HAVCR1, DSG2, and PLTP dominated the mediation effects on IHD, with mediated proportions ranging from 2.78% to 33.32% (Fig. 4B). The association of IR with CKD was primarily mediated by HAVCR1, ASGR1, CLMP, and ADM, with proportions ranging from 5.15% to 58.61% (Fig. S17). For stroke, HAVCR1, NCAN, and BCAN showed a key mediating role in the effects of IR, contributing mediated proportions of 2.53%-12.27% (Fig. S17). In terms of mortality, the association was largely governed by GDF15, HGF, and HAVCR1, with proportions of mediated effects varying from 13.96% to 58.27% (Fig. S17). Sensitivity analyses

In this study, we probed the robustness of our primary findings in a variety of ways. The correlations of IR and related proteomic signatures with outcome risks persisted regardless of various approaches, including removal of the cases occurring outcomes within two years (Table S18), exclusion of participants with BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 (Table S19), additional adjustments of other related biochemical covariates (Table S20), exclusion of individuals using medications (Table S21), and limitation to Caucasian participants (Table S22). Collectively, the observations from sensitivity analyses confirm the reliability and robustness of our main findings.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

Using representative data from nearly 20,000 participants, this study demonstrated the associations of major chronic diseases and mortality with eight IR metrics, among which eGDR exhibited superior predictive accuracy. Extending these findings, our work provided comprehensive and exhaustive insights into the proteins linking IR to these health outcomes. Several enriched biological signals were identified for IR-associated proteins regardless of distinct surrogates, including immune response, inflammation, lipid metabolism, and pathways related to cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival. IR-associated proteins were highly interactive, and the proteomic signatures for different IR surrogates were connected to most adverse outcomes. Furthermore, certain proteins (e.g., HAVCR1, CXCL17, GDF15) significantly mediated the relationships between multiple IR surrogates and chronic diseases and mortality.

IR surrogates and proteomic signatures as predictors for multiple chronic diseases and mortality

Aligning with our findings, the positive relationships of IR surrogates with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic events (e.g., stroke. nephropathy, myocardial infarction, and CKD) and mortality were also corroborated by multiple meta-analyses [42–44], as well as early analyses based on large nation-wide surveys [21, 45, 46]. Alongside the baseline IR-disease associations, Feng et al. further confirmed the interplay between cardiovascular disease risks and the dynamic trajectories of TyG and TyG-BMI [47]. Additionally, of eight IR surrogates, we found eGDR exhibited superior discriminative performance for the chronic diseases of interest and mortality. In agreement with our results, Liu et al. indicated that eGDR yielded higher predictive accuracy for CKD compared to TyG, TyG-BMI, and TyG-WC [48]. With respect to IHD and stroke prediction, two large studies by Huang et al. [45] and Xuan et al. [49] consistently showed that incorporating eGDR into traditional models substantially enhanced risk reclassification and discrimination. Likewise, a recent study detected a reverse relationship between eGDR and stroke in Chinese individuals aged over 45 years, with HR of 0.84 for each unit increase in exposure [50]. The above evidence supports the predictive value of eGDR in capturing those with high risks of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic phenotypes driven by IR.

Intriguingly, our study indicated the effects of IR on health outcomes varied by IR surrogates, where individuals with a greater level of TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, TyG-ABSI, or a lower level of eGDR, experienced higher mortality risks, while such observations were not detected for other metrics, like TyG and TG/HDL-C. Consistently, a meta-analysis of 12 cohort studies with over 6 million participants underscored that TyG significantly correlated with a range of cardiovascular diseases, but not with cardiovascular or all-cause mortality [44]. Another large study involving 4,207 US participants concluded that TyG, TyG-BMI, and TyG-WC were not significantly correlated with all-cause mortality [51]. Vega et al. suggested the association between TyG and mortality was attenuated to non-significance following adjustment for age, BMI, blood pressure, and smoking status [52]. Similar observations have also been reported by Kim et al. [53]. By contrast, other evidence established a significant association of TyG and TG/HDL-C with fatality [54–56]. Discrepancies with prior studies may stem from the threshold effects for TyG-mortality associations, below which no significant relationships were observed [57]. Furthermore, differences in population heterogeneity, pathophysiological complexity, follow-up duration, and covariate adjustment also contribute to the differential findings across studies. Nevertheless, these results be interpreted with caution, and additional prospective studies are necessary for further validation.

Currently, most prior studies have exclusively focused on examining the epidemiological association between IR and few or limited chronic diseases, while the role of the proteome in linking these phenotypes is yet to be elaborated [2, 4, 21, 46]. Our work further substantiated and extended existing evidence at molecular levels by profiling the abundance of 2,920 plasma proteins across eight IR indicators and determining their relationships with five prevalent chronic conditions and all-cause mortality. We established IR-related proteomic signatures by regressing proteins with each IR indicator through ENET model, thereby quantifying the global alterations in the proteome linked to IR status. In line with current study, early flow cytometry analysis showed an elevated expression of LDLR in the surface of CD14+ monocytes from T2DM patients compared to healthy controls, and inhibiting LDLR degradation could lead to the release of proinflammatory cytokines [58]. Prior reports also suggested the associations of MFGE8, LEP, and FABP4 with insulin sensitivity, supporting the findings of our study [59–61]. Generally, these proteomic signatures were in good consistency with IR surrogates, as evidenced by Spearman correlation coefficients of 0.710–0.911. Accumulating evidence has suggested that the pathophysiological impact of IR extends beyond impaired glucose metabolism, affecting diverse organ systems. Such systemic dysfunction manifests, in part, as aberrant protein expression profiles in affected tissues [62]. In contrast to conventional IR surrogates, the plasma proteome offers an overall view of metabolic state across multiple tissues. In current work, we identified extensive perturbations in circulating proteome linked to IR, possibly attributed to associated multi‑tissue dysfunction [63]. Comparable observation was documented by a recent large-scale proteome study [64]. Moreover, this study underlined the potentials of IR-associated proteomic signatures in stratifying risk for diverse chronic diseases and mortality. In particular, the eGDR signature provided incremental values to clinical variables in predictive accuracy. Taken together, these findings offer an additional perspective on strategies targeting proteomic biomarkers to avert IR-associated health outcomes.

Potential roles of IR-related plasma proteins in health outcomes of interest

In this study, we identified significant enrichments of IR-associated proteins in lipid metabolism, immune function, and inflammation. IR is widely recognized to fluctuate glucose and lipid metabolism of multiple body tissues, and such metabolic disturbances predispose individuals to obesity and fat accumulation. In the context of obesity, free fatty acids are accumulated due to excessive adipose tissue lipolysis, functioning as a source of proinflammatory cytokines [65]. Moreover, some adipokines, like LEP discerned by our ENET analysis, mediate pro-inflammatory processes by potentiating the release of cytokines in addition to regulating metabolism homeostasis [66, 67]. Animal experiments also suggested high-fat diet positively regulated inflammatory pathways, like NF-κB, TLR4, and TNF-α [68]. Conversely, chronic inflammation can also impair the oxidative status, as well as carbohydrate and lipid metabolism [69]. As suggested by our study, the role of inflammation as a contributor to cardiovascular diseases and mortality has been broadly acknowledged in prior studies [70, 71]. A prospective study documented that CRP in combination with other inflammation markers yielded good predictive performance for cardiovascular events in females [72]. Another research showed a positive correlation of inflammatory status index (AISI) with CKD risk and low eGFR [73]. Furthermore, functional annotation indicated IR may influence the migration and activation of immune cells, leading to pro-tumorigenic environments [74]. Likewise, PPI analysis in current work also bolstered the core roles of immune-related markers in IR-related pathophysiological response, such as CD8A, IFNG, and FASLG. These proteins contribute to immune cell infiltration, vascular endothelial cell injury, and cell apoptosis, thereby eliciting the onset of various cardiometabolic events [75–77]. In addition, we found PI3K-Akt pathway and MAPK pathway were strongly implicated in IR status, and their dysfunctions are frequently observed in human malignancies [78, 79].

As revealed by mediation analysis, numerous plasma proteins modulated the relationships between IR and major chronic diseases and mortality. In particular, we identified HAVCR1, CXCL17, and GDF15 as the primary mediators for most IR-outcome associations regardless of distinct surrogates. HAVCR1, also termed as TIM1 and KIM1, has been previously established as the molecular biomarker of impaired kidney function [80]. Further, current study uncovered the adverse roles of HAVCR1 in cardiovascular events. Aligning to our observations, a recent cohort involving nearly 10,000 individuals concluded that the increase of urinary HAVCR1 served as an indication of coronary atherosclerosis [81]. Likewise, Zhang et al. underscored that TIMD4-HAVCR1 variants and their haplotypes were associated with the risk of coronary heart disease and ischemic stroke, possibly by modulating the serum lipid metabolism [82]. Besides, we found HAVCR1 also significantly mediated IR-cancer association, which was in agreement with early evidence [83, 84]. Widely expressed in mucosal tissues, CXCL17 acts as a chemokine for monocytes and dendritic cells [85]. In our study, CXCL17 emerged as the top mediator for the relationship between IR and IHD, reinforcing prior findings that circulating CXCL17 elevated among patients with unstable angina [86]. CXCL17 also promotes cardiac fibroblast proliferation by activating AKT signaling and facilitating the production of bFGF protein, thereby contributing to abnormal myocardial cell function [87]. As a component of TGF-β superfamily, GDF15 gets involved in multiple physiological and pathological responses, such as inflammation, reactions to cellular stress, and tissue injury [88, 89]. In line with our work, a prospective study consistently revealed increased risks of all-cause mortality in participants with a higher level of GDF15 [90]. Similar discoveries were also unraveled by Bao et al. [91] and Oppong et al. [92]. Moreover, increasing reports have documented strong associations between GDF15 and an array of health outcomes, like T2DM, cardiovascular diseases, and stroke [93–95]. Collectively, the impacts of IR on a variety of organs and systems can be mediated by circulating proteins, and further biological explorations are needed to gain a deeper understanding of black-box connection between IR and diseases.

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several strengths. First, our work initially adopted a framework that integrated population-based cohorts with high-throughput proteomics to offer systematic evidence over IR-disease associations. Second, the protein-wide association analysis allows to comprehensively ascertain protein markers related to IR, and we for the first time quantified the proteomic signals and their associations with multiple cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic events. Third, leveraging a nationally representative cohort with a follow-up of over 12 years, this longitudinal study enables to address the major limitations inherent in cross-sectional design and yielded robust evidence.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, on account of limited data accessibility, we utilized eight common surrogates to reflect IR status instead of the gold standard, which might induce potential bias in measurements. Nevertheless, various IR surrogates consistently demonstrated similar findings, and the results remained largely stable in sensitivity analysis. Second, the proteomic signatures identified in this study remain at the level of statistical association, and functional experiments are imperative to corroborate these observations and determine the specificity and biological relevance between these signatures and IR. Third, despite employing longitudinal data and formal mediation analyses, our study design remains observational, and causal relationships between IR and plasma proteome cannot be established. While biologically plausible, the associations we reported should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, necessitating further confirmations. Fourth, given that many inflammatory proteins have short half-lives and are sensitive to transient physiological states, a single measurement may not fully capture long-term proteomic patterns. Future studies with repeated protein measurements over time are warranted to validate the stability of these proteomic mediators and to better characterize their relationships with disease risk. Additionally, proteome data used in this study are mainly from individuals of European ancestry, and the extrapolation of our findings to other ethnicities requires additional validation. Lastly, residual confounding might remain notwithstanding extensive adjustments for covariates.

Conclusion

This large-scale cohort study corroborated the associations of eight IR surrogates with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic diseases and mortality, particularly highlighting the superior predictive capacity of eGDR. Additionally, our work first delineated the proteomic signatures measuring proteomic responses in IR scenarios, which significantly correlated with multiple chronic diseases and mortality. These findings provide extensive insights into the biological links with IR and underscore the potential of peripheral protein biomarkers in targeted interventions preceding diseases. Future longitudinal studies are required to substantiate these findings and delve into causal mechanisms.
