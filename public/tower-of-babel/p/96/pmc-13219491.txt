First evaluation framework based on the one welfare concept in extensive broiler rearing systems
Authors: Lucia Rocchi, S Mattioli, A Collin, C Bonnefous, L Warin, F Tuyttens, P Thobe, M Re, C Castellini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe One Welfare concept has emerged to counter the traditional separation of human well-being, environmental sustainability, and animal welfare, emphasizing their interdependence within specific ecological and socio-technical contexts. Despite its promise, the practical operationalization of One Welfare as an evaluation framework remains limited. This study proposes and tests an operational One Welfare approach (OWA) for extensive broiler rearing systems (ERS), explicitly promoting direct and indirect links between animal and human welfare and situating them within the environmental performance. Although ERS constitute a minor share of EU broiler output (≈ 5%), interest in and adoption of ERS have grown markedly over the past two decades, underscoring the need for assessment tools that capture their broader contributions. Conventional evaluation methodologies, designed around intensive systems, tend to prioritize yield, short-term efficiency, and market indicators, and may therefore underrepresent the ecological, social, and welfare advantages achievable in diversified, outdoor systems. We implemented a multicriteria decision analysis (MCDA) using the One Welfare Approach to synthesize indicators across the animal welfare, environmental, economic, and social pillars in a broiler case study. The framework proved feasible despite data constraints (limited availability and some invariant parameters typical of ERS, and its outputs aligned with published evidence on slow-growing genotypes in outdoor systems, supporting construct validity. The Key results showed that: (1) Environmental enrichment with olive trees improved OWA performance: all enriched options outperformed their non-enriched counterparts, showing that tree/shrub cover increases outdoor use, reduces predation and fear, and improves integument condition in free-range birds. (2) Under enrichment, RJ_E ranked first overall. Flow decomposition indicated that CB_E exhibited higher positive flow (Phi+), but RJ_E had a lower negative flow (Phi⁻); given the non-compensatory OWA stance, suppressing negatives proved more decisive than marginal gains in positives. A weight-sensitivity check showed ranking stability across pillars, with limited shifts confined to the Economic pillar, where criterion values were tightly clustered. Taken together, these findings indicate that One Welfare assessment can capture trade-offs and synergies that conventional metrics overlook, offering actionable guidance for extensive organic broiler systems. The approach is compatible with prevailing outcome-based welfare assessment practice. It can be simplified into a transparent index for on-farm self-assessment and external communication, provided that its governance and evidence base meet current best practice expectations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46959-z.Keywords: Extensive rearing system, Chicken, One welfare approach, Multicriteria analysisSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

From the sustainability to the one welfare assessment

Sustainability is commonly conceptualized in the literature as a balance among three core pillars: the environment, the economy, and society1. Over time, a growing body of research has evaluated these dimensions across multiple sectors, including agriculture2. As a multidimensional construct3, sustainability in animal-based farming systems is typically assessed through indicators that capture economic viability, social acceptability, and environmental impact3,4. However, relatively few studies have comprehensively addressed all three dimensions in animal-based systems, and limited attention has been devoted to the social pillar and interactions among the different sustainability components2,5.

Furthermore, improving sustainability requires that a system be ethically acceptable both now and in the future: if the public perceives any of its effects as unacceptable, the system becomes unsustainable6. Animal production systems therefore face substantial challenges in reducing their environmental footprint while maintaining food production and ensuring high levels of both animal and human welfare7–9. Addressing these challenges necessitates integrating animal welfare as a fully recognized component of sustainability7.

Integrating animal welfare into the broader sustainability framework is not only a societal demand2 but also a scientific need for robust, comprehensive evaluation tools. Traditional sustainability assessments often neglect animal welfare, treating it as separate from the environmental dimension, understood as ecosystem integrity, and from the social dimension, where it is reduced to a matter of human preference rather than a value in its own right10. In contrast, the One Welfare approach (OWA) offers a more inclusive perspective by recognizing and valuing the direct and indirect benefits that improvements in animal welfare can generate for humans and the environment11. Understanding the interconnections among human, animal, and environmental factors is, therefore, essential for developing a holistic assessment tool capable of capturing the full range of system impacts.

Recent literature includes attempts to evaluate the sustainability of rearing systems beyond the traditional three-pillar model. For instance, Bonneau et al.12 proposed a tool for pig production encompassing seven dimensions (Animal Welfare, Animal Health, Breeding Programmes, Environmental Sustainability, Meat Safety, Market Conformity, Economy, and Working Conditions) without assigning them to specific pillars. In a complementary contribution, Bonneau et al.13 distinguished between animal welfare and animal health, defining welfare in terms of the Five Freedoms established by FAWC14. Their results indicated a negative correlation between economic performance and animal welfare, and a positive correlation between animal welfare and meat quality, findings based on evaluations of pig production systems within the EU Q‑PorkChains project.

Elzen et al.15 applied Reflexive Interactive Design to promote sustainability transitions across various animal production sectors, each characterized by specific challenges, including environmental load, animal welfare, public acceptance, and economic profitability. To our knowledge, the only attempt to apply a simplified OWA to the evaluation of farming systems is our previous work16, which compared conventional and organic commercial broiler farms.

The present paper represents the first attempt to propose a comprehensive One Welfare Assessment framework, developed with a whole‑supply‑chain perspective and specifically focused on Extensive Rearing Systems (ERS). The framework was developed within the European project PPILOW through a participatory process involving key stakeholders from the broiler supply chain, organized into nine National Practitioner Groups across six EU countries. As a result, the framework is both species‑specific and production‑context‑specific. This paper applies the framework to extensive broiler systems through a case study examining the effect of outdoor enrichment (presence vs. absence of trees) on system performance.

Extensive rearing systems

Extensive rearing systems (ERS) for broilers are characterized by the use of relatively slow‑growing genotypes, low stocking densities with access to outdoor areas, and shortened feed supply chains that prioritize locally sourced inputs17. This definition includes a variety of production models, such as organic, free‑range, and low‑input systems, which display considerable variability across countries, even within the European Union18.

Interest in ERS has increased markedly over the past two decades, partly driven by growing public concern about ethical issues such as animal welfare, environmental impact, and food safety19. This trend has supported sustained sectoral growth: annual ERS expansion in the EU has exceeded 12%20, and organic broiler production grew by 11% per year between 2014 and 202021. Despite this growth, most poultry meat in the EU still comes from intensive systems; ERS represent only a small amount of total poultry production20, and organic production remains a niche, accounting for just 3.6% of the EU total21.

Intensive poultry production systems are often considered the most efficient form of broiler rearing, particularly in terms of feed conversion ratio22. Several studies have reported lower environmental impacts for intensive systems when assessed solely through Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)23. However, despite their efficiency, intensive systems have notable environmental consequences. Their reliance on feed ingredients such as cereals and soybean meal contributes to agricultural intensification, nutrient pollution of sensitive ecosystems, biodiversity loss, and the encroachment of cultivated land into natural habitats22.

Assessment methods that focus primarily on production efficiency (such as LCA) tend to disadvantage extensive systems due to their longer production cycles, lower stocking densities, and reduced feed conversion efficiency24. To overcome the limitations of such narrow evaluation approaches, efforts have been made to develop broader, multidisciplinary frameworks that capture the complexity of rearing systems in a sustainability context16.

In this perspective, applying the OWA11 offers a more comprehensive and robust evaluation framework. By overcoming the artificial separation between human, social, and animal welfare, the OWA recognizes their interdependence within specific ecological contexts25, such as those characteristic of extensive farming systems.

Materials and methods

The framework construction

The framework specifically developed for ERS within the PPILOW project was designed to account for the peculiarities of different species (pigs and poultry) and their production supply chain. It aims to be applicable to real farms, addressing a gap in the literature that rarely includes real-life case studies16. In this study, we reported the framework specifically developed for the broiler system.

Figure 1 presents the theoretical framework developed as the foundation for the assessment model. The central aim was to move beyond the traditional sustainability model commonly applied in farming systems by adopting the OWA. Although the two frameworks differ conceptually, they overlap in several respects, and their respective dimensions remain interconnected. For example, within the OWA, the concept of human well‑being encompasses elements aligned with both the economic and social pillars of the broader sustainability paradigm.

General assessment framework.

Accordingly, the PPILOW project sought to integrate components of the sustainability framework into the OWA by disaggregating the “Human Welfare” component into two distinct pillars: Economy and Society. The final framework, therefore, comprised four pillars, each subdivided into several dimensions, yielding a total of sixteen dimensions. Each dimension was defined by one or more criteria, understood as the conditions that must be met or described to comply with the corresponding OWA dimension26. Each criterion was then linked to one or more indicators, which are measurable variables used to determine whether the criterion is being met.

In this study, and throughout the PPILOW project, the dimensions and criteria within the Animal Welfare and Economy pillars were tailored to each species (in this manuscript, broiler) and supply chain to reflect their specific characteristics. In contrast, the Society and Environment pillars were assessed using a common set of criteria applied consistently across all species included in the project (Fig. 2).

The identification of criteria was initially informed by a selection of European Union–funded projects (e.g., ClimAgri, DEXEL, DIAMOND, EBIOTEP, Engele, GEEP, GTE, GTTT, IDEA, MOLDAVI, QPorkChain, Welfare Quality) and by a comprehensive literature review (see Annex 2). This preliminary list of criteria was subsequently discussed and prioritized through consultations with PPILOW project members and the National Practitioner Groups (NPGs) from seven partner countries (Denmark, Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, and Romania). A distinctive characteristic of the PPILOW project was its strong emphasis on a participatory, co‑creation approach. The NPGs were composed by key stakeholders from across the supply chain, including farmers, practitioners, citizens, breeders, researchers, feed producers, and buyers, as well as representatives of civil society such as animal welfare organizations and policymakers. The resulting framework was therefore grounded in a robust, transnational, participatory process.

At the same time, the selection and prioritization of criteria and indicators were conducted in accordance with FAO (2018) and UN (2019) guidelines, which state that such elements should be relevant and results‑oriented, clearly indicate the direction of the contribution (positive or negative), and be coherent, robust, accurate, and accessible.

The aggregation approach

To aggregate the different criteria into a comprehensive assessment of the OWA, we applied the PROMETHEE methods (Preference Ranking Organization Method for Enrichment Evaluations), a family of algorithms within multicriteria decision analysis (MCDA) developed by Brans27 and Brans and Vincke28. MCDA encompasses a set of mathematical methods designed to support decision‑making processes. It can be used to identify a single most‑preferred option, rank multiple alternatives, generate a shortlist of viable choices for further evaluation, or distinguish between acceptable and unacceptable options. It establishes preferences among alternatives using an explicit set of objectives defined by the decision‑maker, each associated with measurable criteria indicating the extent to which the objective is achieved. In some cases, identifying objectives and criteria alone may be sufficient to guide decision‑makers. However, when multiple, potentially divergent criteria must be addressed simultaneously, MCDA facilitates the assessment of complex issues and has proven a successful approach for operationalizing the One Welfare concept16,24.

PROMETHEE methods have been widely applied across various fields to support decision‑making processes29–33. As outranking methods based on pairwise comparisons of alternatives across each recognized criterion, PROMETHEE approaches prevent compensation among criteria, which is essential for evaluating performance within the OWA framework. For each criterion, pairwise comparison quantifies the degree of preference for one alternative over another using a preference function that assigns values from 0 (indifference) to 1 (strict preference). In total, PROMETHEE offers six different types of preference function (Usual, U-shape, V-shape, Level, Linear, Gaussian), all ranging from 0 to 1. Applying one or another depends on the nature of the criterion (quantitative or qualitative), the decision-maker’s (DM) behavior regarding indifference and preference, and the tolerance for minor differences between alternatives. In this study, the linear preference function was the most used, although in some cases, the usual and level were also applied.

The method computes two main scores for each alternative: a positive preference flow (Phi⁺), indicating the extent to which an alternative is preferred over others, and a negative preference flow (Phi⁻), indicating the extent to which it is less preferred than others. These two flows produce separate rankings, which may not always coincide. An ideal option would display a Phi⁺ close to 1 and a Phi⁻ close to 0. PROMETHEE I provides partial rankings based on these flows, allowing for possible incomparabilities among alternatives. PROMETHEE II, in contrast, generates a complete ranking by calculating the net preference flow, defined as the difference between Phi⁺ and Phi⁻.

In this study, both PROMETHEE I and II were applied, given the nature of the dataset and the specific research objective (comparing the experimental outcomes of the innovative approach against the control). The analyses were conducted using the Visual PROMETHEE software (Academic version available here: https://bertrand.mareschal.web.ulb.be/PG2024/academic-edition.html) v1.4, with the default settings applied to additional parameters required by the method, such as preference thresholds.

Weighting phase

The multicriteria decision-making method requires a weighting phase, in which each criterion is assigned a relative level of importance or preference. Weighting can be conducted through two primary approaches: an objective method, based on information theory, statistical techniques, or the use of random weights16, or a subjective method, which involves eliciting in put from experts, decision-makers, or stakeholders, who assign weights based on their experience, knowledge, and perception of the issue34. To simplify the analysis in this study, we initially adopted an equal weighting approach. Using a uniform weight means not giving greater importance to one dimension or pillar than to the others. Therefore, we can consider this as a benchmark valuation. Subsequently, a sensitivity analysis was performed using weights derived from stakeholder input to assess the robustness of the results and to understand how different preferences may influence them.

Sensitivity analysis and card method

In multicriteria decision analysis, sensitivity analysis is applied to verify the robustness of the results. It can be performed at several levels: criteria weights, performance scores, normalization methods, aggregation functions, or model structure. In this work, we decided to analyze the criteria weights because assumptions about them are subjective.

Specifically, for modifying the weights, we applied the revised Simon’s procedure, also known as the SFR decision card method35. This method enabled the computation of the relative importance of the OWA dimensions for a given group of stakeholders and facilitates the derivation of the criteria weights. Since the procedure needs to involve stakeholders, it was applied involving the NPGs.

The SFR method provides a simple and intuitive way to derive numerical weights. The procedure offers a straightforward approach to indirectly determining numerical weights by using a set of cards. Unlike other weighting methods, it does not require stakeholders to express numerical judgments about the criteria. Instead, each criterion is represented by a “playing card”, and participants are asked to rank them. After ranking all the dimension cards, white cards can be added if needed to indicate perceived gaps in importance between two consecutive criteria (or groups of criteria). The number of white cards placed between two criteria indicates the magnitude of the difference in their importance: if there are no white cards, the two consecutive criteria have closely related weights, whereas one or more white cards suggest a larger interval.

The obtained ranking is then used to calculate the weights. Because the number of criteria in the framework is substantial, asking stakeholders to handle them directly would impose an excessive cognitive burden and could compromise the reliability of the results. For this reason, in our study, the cards were associated with the OWA dimensions, thereby simplifying the task for stakeholders and experts involved. Then, the resulting dimension weights were disaggregated to the criteria within each dimension—via local criterion weights—so that the final MCDA model operates on criterion level weights.

The procedure was implemented within the NPGs of seven EU countries (see Sect. 3.1), involving 30 stakeholders. Facilitators in each NPG were briefed on the methodology and provided with a card set, one for each OWA dimension. The following steps were then carried out within each group:

Each participant received four card sets (one for each pillar). Each card was labeled with the name of one dimension. The facilitator provided any clarification needed regarding their meaning. Following the SFR procedure, the cards contained no numerical values, thereby avoiding any bias. Participants also received blank white cards of the same size, with the number provided depending on their needs.

Case study

One of the innovative levers examined in the PPILOW project formed the basis of this exploratory case study. Specifically, we analyzed an Italian case study of a commercial farm in central Italy that typically raises naked‑neck chickens in a free‑range system. Each pen housed between 2000 and 4000 broilers, with an indoor stocking density of approximately 10 chickens/m² and an outdoor space availability of 3 hectares per paddock (equivalent to about 10 m2 per chicken). The farm operates its own feed mill, producing approximately 40% of its feed on-site, and manages an in-house slaughterhouse.

The MCDA was conducted using data from 1440 broilers reared in three outdoor pens, with four replicates per genotype. The genotypes evaluated were: a Cross Breed (CB), obtained by crossing an Italian local breed (Robusta Maculata) with a commercial Sasso strain; a RED JA757 Hubbard (RJ); and a Naked Neck Hubbard (NN). Each genotype was reared under two conditions: with and without outdoor enrichment consisting of 270 olive trees per hectare, while maintaining the farm’s standard operational activities (Table 2). All birds were reared in accordance with company management practices (indoor and outdoor stocking densities, feed requirements).

Results and discussion

Methodological consideration

Once the framework had been assessed, we applied it to the case study. Most of the indicators were easy to assess. However, in some cases, quantification was either partial or not possible. Consequently, an unexpected outcome was identifying areas where information was difficult to obtain, thereby providing valuable guidance for improving future data collection efforts.

Within the Environmental Pillar (Table 3), the proposed criteria and indicators were clearly quantified and assessed using primary farm data, primarily through LCA. The only criterion that could not be evaluated was ‘Promotion of local farms’, measured by the indicator ‘Use of raw materials coming from local industries’, due to a lack of data. Overall, the Environmental Pillar appeared relatively straightforward to quantify when information about feed production and rearing systems is available. This aligns with the well-established importance of diet and energy consumption in environmental assessment39.

The assessment of the Social pillar (Table 4) proved to be more complex. For the Social Acceptability dimension, data on the criteria ‘Perception of people’ and ‘Willingness to pay’ required specifically tailored studies, which were not yet available. Furthermore, in multicriteria analysis, including criteria with identical values across all the different alternatives is not meaningful, as they do not contribute to differentiation. Consequently, these sub-criteria were excluded, reducing the number of indicators within the pillar. Specifically, all criteria in the ‘Job perception and motivation’ dimension showed no variation across the three alternatives. The criteria for these dimensions were level of confidence, level of pride, and level of satisfaction with the payout level, all measured on a 5-point Likert scale.

For criterion ‘Neighbourhood’, the indicator ‘Social farming’, and for ‘Participation active/communication’, the indicator ‘Communication towards public’ showed uniform values. The same issue arose for the criterion ‘Workforce Composition’, where no differences were observed. Thus, despite the introduction of various indicators to assess the social aspects of rearing systems, their measurement remains challenging, underscoring the urgent need to develop new paradigms and models that can be readily applied in real-world farm settings.

The Economic Pillar (Table 5) faced similar challenges, particularly with constant values across alternatives within the Cost dimension (fixed costs). Within the ‘Performance-quality’ dimension, the two only criteria with invariant values were the ‘White striping’ and ‘Wooden breast’, both key indicators of breast muscle abnormalities. While muscle abnormalities are significant for assessing meat quality in poultry production40, their incidence in slow-growing genotypes is considered rare or absent41.

The fourth pillar, Animal Welfare (Table 6), was largely fulfilled, with only a few criteria deemed inapplicable, specifically for ‘Perches’ and both indicators of Good intervention practices and Crating. However, as in the previous pillars, some criteria were excluded due to their invariance across alternatives These included: ‘Appropriate Drinking’, ‘Litter Quality’, ‘Bird Distribution’, ‘Farmer Practices’, ‘Mortality’, ‘Crowding/Panic Movements During Assessment’, ‘Catching/Handling’, ‘Duration of Non-Feeding’, and ‘Water Access’. Notably, the criterion ‘Good Intervention Practices’ lacked any indicators for the case study, whereas all other criteria had at least one.

The absence of certain criteria or indicators led to an unequal distribution across the dimensions. Although a more uniform distribution is preferable (i.e., the same number of criteria within each dimension), this is not a mandatory requirement in MCDA.

Environmental results

The results of the Environmental pillar indicated that the enrichment options outperformed the non-enriched options: the net flow was only slightly positive for the non-enriched options (Fig. 3). However, the absolute values remained only slightly above zero, reflecting generally modest performance levels (Table 7). This outcome may be attributed to the use of the LCA approach for several criteria. While LCA is a widely adopted method for environmental evaluation, it may not be ideally suited to capturing the complexity of agricultural systems, particularly extensive ones, where the ecological contributions of pasture and all vegetative phases are often unrecognized. Several authors highlighted this aspect in the literature39,24. It is important to note that this study focused exclusively on the rearing phase, rather than the integrated broiler-olive tree system.

As highlighted by Paolotti et al.42, when the analysis encompasses an agroforestry system (e.g., outdoor chickens reared in an olive orchard), its environmental impact tends to be lower than that of the two systems evaluated separately (outdoor chickens vs. olive orchard). When considering the genetic strains, RJ and NN exhibited similar performance across both enriched and non-enriched conditions, in contrast to the distinct performance observed for the CB strain (see Table 6). Although CJ and NN are the more productive genotypes in absolute terms, CB exhibited a genotype × environment interaction: CB, being more active, greatly benefits from shaded microclimates and additional foraging opportunities, whereas medium-growing genotypes (CJ, NN) gain less from the enriched outdoor resources.

Social Pillar findings (Fig. 4) indicate that the majority of criteria were evenly distributed across enriched and non‑enriched conditions. Overall, enrichment produced modest positive effects on the work environment. Variation among the three genetic strains was driven primarily by a single LCA‑based criterion related to potential workplace environmental risks for human health: CB and RJ showed comparable scores, whereas NN exhibited a slightly lower value. Estimating social indicators on real farms presents substantial methodological challenges due to the lack of robust theoretical frameworks and standardized measurement tools43. While research explicitly investigating work satisfaction and well‑being of farmers remains limited44, the situation becomes even more complex when attempting to link these outcomes to specific innovations such as enrichment45. Consequently, the difficulties encountered in distinguishing social performance between enriched and non‑enriched systems in this case study are not unexpected. Notably, Schanz et al.44 identify several key factors influencing work satisfaction in similar rearing contexts, such as workload, number of livestock units, mental complexity, and the distribution of work peaks. None of these differed meaningfully between the two management conditions evaluated here, which helps explain the limited variation observed in our results.

Although the literature on social aspects of livestock farming is less extensive than that on environmental or economic performance, several studies provide important insights into the interconnections between farmer well‑being, work satisfaction, and animal welfare, which are highly relevant within a One Welfare perspective. For instance, farmers’ psychological and physical well‑being is often closely linked to the health, behaviour, and management of their animals, as documented in dairy and sheep systems46,47. Qualitative research indicates that positive farmer/animal interactions, intrinsic job motivation, and farmers’ perceptions of their own working conditions can directly influence both human and animal outcomes48,49.

Sociological studies further highlight how farm culture, stockmanship values, and the ethical tensions experienced by workers shape not only work satisfaction but also the quality of animal care50,51. These insights reinforce the rationale for incorporating a structured social dimension into the OWA framework. They also contextualise why, in the present case study, differences were minimal: many of the key mechanisms through which social conditions influence welfare (e.g., perceived workload, emotional labour, farmer/animal relationships) were similar across all evaluated options.

Taken together, the evidence suggests that while the social pillar remains challenging to quantify, it is nonetheless essential to a holistic One Welfare assessment. The findings also underscore the need for improved and harmonised data collection on farmer well‑being, job perception, workload, and the relational aspects of stockmanship in future applications of the framework.

Animal welfare results

Environmental enrichment was consistently associated with better outcomes, particularly for Animal Welfare (Table 10; Fig. 6). Chickens ranged more in the outdoor area when olive trees were present; this aligns with evidence that vegetative overhead cover (trees/shrubs or shelter structures) encourages hens to use the range more extensively52. Hence, it is reasonable to infer that the overhead protection provided by the tree canopy increased birds’ perceived security, especially from aerial predators, and improved microclimatic conditions (shade), thereby promoting outdoor use. Consistent with this, all three strains exhibited positive net flow. CB achieved the best rank, likely reflecting its higher kinetic activity and broader range of use; its normalized flow values were closest to 1 across the three flows considered.

Regarding genetic strains, however, the ranking did not fully align between enriched and non‑enriched options, although NN consistently ranked lowest. Under enrichment, RJ (RJ_E) ranked first. A plausible explanation is a genotype × environment interaction: olive trees provide overhead cover and microclimatic buffering, increasing outdoor use and reducing fear and heat load. Strains that exploit cover more effectively can gain welfare indicators; this pattern is well documented when trees/shrubs are present on the range52. The flow analysis supports this interpretation. Although CB_E showed a higher positive flow (Phi⁺) than RJ_E, RJ_E exhibited a lower negative flow (Phi⁻). Because the applied OWA configuration is non‑compensatory, a small set of weaker indicators penalized CB_E more than RJ_E, resulting in RJ_E taking the top position on net flow. In other words, minimizing Phi⁻ was more decisive than marginal gains in Phi⁺ for the final ranking in this setting.

These MCDA outcomes are consistent with prior evidence on behavioral and welfare differences among slow‑growing genotypes reared with outdoor access: strains with greater adaptability and outdoor activity tend to achieve better welfare profiles, whereas the highest growth potential does not necessarily coincide with the best welfare status in extensive systems. Moreover, genotype‑dependent growth dynamics and muscle composition are known to modulate carcass yield and product‑quality traits, which helps explain why product‑quality rankings do not always mirror welfare‑based rankings53–55,36. Indeed, it is widely reported that genotype-dependent growth dynamics and muscle composition modulate carcass yield and quality parameters.

Comparing rankings obtained with stakeholder-specific weights and the aggregated geometric-mean weights showed that changes in the final ranking occurred only in the Economic Pillar. In all other pillars, while the final scores sometimes varied, substantially so in the Environmental Pillar, the ordering of the alternatives remained unchanged.

In the Economic Pillar, applying aggregated weights improved the positions of NN_NE (from 3rd → 2nd) and RJ_NE (from 5th → 4th), while CB_NE (from 2nd → 3rd) and NN_E (from 3rd → 5th) lost ground. This pattern is consistent with classic MCDA sensitivity results: when alternatives are tightly clustered (i.e., there is a narrow range of criterion values), even small weight shifts can reorder near-ties. Formal treatments of weight-stability intervals and rank sensitivity in outranking- and flow-based MCDA show that such local instabilities are expected near indifference zones56.

Overall, however, the ranking proved robust, with only the Economic Pillar exhibiting shifts. To note that no significant ranking reversal (i.e., switch between best and worst alternatives) happened. This degree of stability, given the observed dispersion in stakeholder weights, supports the reliability of the main conclusions.

Conclusions

Animal husbandry has not been solely a technical or economic endeavor for at least two decades. Societal expectations have progressively shifted toward production methods that are not only economically viable but also socially and environmentally sustainable, an evolution reflected in recent EU consumer‑protection measures targeting misleading “green” claims and non‑credible sustainability labels. Within this transition, animal welfare is a central concern that warrants explicit, transparent assessment.

Applying this OWA framework within a multi‑criteria decision analysis to extensive broiler production yielded several notable insights. OWA is a well‑established aggregation family that parameterizes the decision maker’s attitude toward compensation and risk, and is widely used in MCDA for sustainability decisions.

External validity. The MCDA outcomes aligned with published evidence on the same (or comparable) slow‑growing genotypes regarding welfare and product/meat quality, supporting the reliability of the framework’s conclusions across extensive systems. This concordance indicates that OWA can synthesize diverse welfare, environmental, social, and economic metrics into a coherent ranking while remaining consistent with genotype‑specific findings in the literature.

Evidence and limitations. The analysis highlighted two practical challenges in data collection:

Operational simplifications. For practical deployment, simplified, more concise framework variants can offer value, especially when data collection is resource‑constrained, while retaining a transparent link to the full model. In particular, for farmers, the index can function as a self‑assessment and benchmarking tool, helping practitioners identify which pillar (welfare, environmental, social, economic) most constrains overall sustainability and where targeted improvements (e.g., housing, shade/cover outdoors, work conditions, or cost/returns balance) would move the needle.

For program managers and funders, the index can support project appraisal and funding selection, provided that weightings are documented and basic robustness checks (e.g., one‑way weight swings or SMAA rank‑acceptability analysis) are reported to demonstrate stability of the rankings under plausible preference variation.

In addition to the limitations in applying the method highlighted above, the present study also presents certain limitations of its own. The main limitation lies in the use of a single case study. This choice was primarily motivated by the need to provide an initial attempt to describe and apply the proposed reference framework. Future applications should investigate how the model performs under greater input-data variability and, consequently, across a broader range of companies.
