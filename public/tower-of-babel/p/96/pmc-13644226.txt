Bispecific antibody AP402 targets p95HER2 and induces costimulation through CD137 to improve efficacy for HER2-positive tumors
Authors: Po-Lin Huang, Hsin-Ta Hsieh, Hung-Tsai Kan, Ching-Hsuan Hsu, Kuan-Ming Huang, Hsin-Yu Chen, Tzu-Ning Chen, Jhong-Jhe You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOutcomes for HER2-positive cancers have been greatly improved by trastuzumab and other HER2-targeted therapies. However, many tumors express a truncated p95HER2 variant that lacks the extracellular trastuzumab binding site. Since these tumors exhibit resistance and more aggressive disease phenotypes, the AP402 bispecific antibody was developed to target p95HER2 and engage activated T cells toward tumor cells via CD137 engagement.MethodsAP402 was characterized in terms of binding, anti-tumor activity and safety using in vitro and in vivo models. Using p95HER2- and full-length HER2-expressing tumor cells, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) assays were performed. AP402-mediated T cell activation was evaluated by measuring IFN-γ production. In vivo anti-tumor efficacy was tested in humanized p95HER2-transgenic tumor models and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. The safety profile was assessed with cytokine release assays and a toxicology study in cynomolgus monkeys.ResultsAP402 induced ADCC in p95HER2-expressing tumor cells and also showed activity against full-length HER2-positive cells. In addition, the CD137-binding arm robustly activated T cells in a p95HER2-binding-dependent manner. In humanized p95HER2-transgenic breast and lung tumor mouse models and in PDX models of p95HER2-positive breast cancer, AP402 significantly reduced tumor weights and volumes. Cytokine release assays and in vivo toxicology studies suggested the molecule has low risk of adverse effects.ConclusionAP402 is a promising therapeutic agent for p95HER2-positive cancers with dual functions of targeting p95HER2 and enhancing p95HER2-dependent T cell activation. These functions may overcome resistance to current HER2-targeted therapies, and the favorable safety profile of AP402 suggests excellent potential for clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10020-026-01564-8.Keywords: Bispecific antibody, P95HER2, CD137, HER2, Breast cancer, Lung cancer

Introduction

Over the past decade, the clinical use of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-targeted therapies has significantly improved the prognosis of patients with HER2-positive breast cancer (Xu et al. 2024; Bhagyalalitha et al. 2024). In particular, the humanized anti-HER monoclonal antibody trastuzumab has markedly improved survival rates (Avelino et al. 2024; Raghav et al. 2024; Iwata et al. 2024; Mehta et al. 2024). Since overexpression or amplification of HER2 is found in approximately 15–20% of all breast cancer cases (Krishnamurti and Silverman 2014), HER2-targeted therapies have revolutionized breast cancer treatment. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of patients with metastatic HER2-positive tumors still experience disease recurrence or relapse, even after adjuvant trastuzumab-based chemotherapy (Cameron et al. 2017; Perez et al. 2014; Vici et al. 2014).

One key factor contributing to poor outcomes in HER2-positive cancers is the presence of p95HER2, a heterogeneous group of truncated HER2 carboxy-terminal fragments (CTFs) reportedly expressed in approximately 20–40% of HER2-positive breast cancers (Chumsri et al. 2018; Scaltriti et al. 2007; Ozkavruk Eliyatkin et al. 2016). Reported p95HER2 isoforms include the 611-, 648-, and 678-CTFs, which retain variable lengths of the extracellular domain together with the transmembrane and intracellular kinase domains. Among these variants, 648-CTF is generated through metalloproteinase-mediated shedding of membrane HER2 (Codony-Servat et al. 1999), whereas 611- and 678-CTFs arise from alternative translation initiation at internal start codons within HER2 mRNA (Anido et al. 2006). These truncated isoforms retain the transmembrane and intracellular kinase domains but lack most of the extracellular trastuzumab-binding region (Rigakos et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2024). Positive p95HER2 expression is frequently associated with aggressive tumor phenotypes (Swain et al. 2023), increased risk of recurrence and metastasis, and intrinsic resistance to HER2-targeted therapies (Chumsri et al. 2018; Ozkavruk Eliyatkin et al. 2016). Functionally, HER2 is a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor involved in regulating cellular growth, survival, and differentiation (Gutierrez and Schiff 2011; Iqbal 2014; Moasser 2007). Despite the loss of most extracellular domains, p95HER2 variants retain constitutive kinase activity and can drive potent downstream oncogenic signaling. In addition, p95HER2 has been shown to promote an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through induction of PD-L1 and IL-6 expression by cancer cells (Hu et al. 2025). Thus, currently available HER2-targeted therapies, including trastuzumab, are largely ineffective against p95HER2-expressing tumors, highlighting a substantial unmet clinical need.

p95HER2 is a tumor-enriched truncated HER2 isoform, meaning it is exclusively expressed on cancer cells and absent from normal tissues (Rius Ruiz et al. 2018). Thus, p95HER2 may be utilized for selective targeting of tumor cells, which may provide an opportunity to reduce potential effects on non-cancerous cells. For example, antibodies targeting p95HER2 are expected to stimulate tumor-specific antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) with minimal systemic toxicity. In addition to its utility as a tumor-specific target, p95HER2 has been implicated as a major driver of therapeutic resistance in HER2-positive breast cancers (Goh et al. 2024), and its expression is associated with more aggressive clinical progression, resistance to trastuzumab and poorer patient prognosis (Chumsri et al. 2018; Scaltriti et al. 2007; Rigakos et al. 2021; Saez et al. 2006). As such, therapeutics that can effectively target p95HER2 could be highly useful as safe treatments for a broad range of HER2-expressing cancers (Rius Ruiz et al. 2018; Dorraji et al. 2022).

In addition to stimulating ADCC, antibodies targeting p95HER2 can be leveraged to enhance tumor-localized immune activation. This function may be achieved by linking the anti-p95HER2 antibody with a moiety that binds CD137 (also known as 4-1BB), a costimulatory receptor expressed on activated T cells, natural killer (NK) cells and other immune cells (Cabo et al. 2021; Pichler et al. 2023; Etxeberria et al. 2020). Ligand binding to CD137 enhances T cell proliferation, survival and effector function, making it an attractive target for T cell-redirecting immunotherapies (Vinay and Kwon 2014; Shen et al. 2023). Moreover, it has been shown that monoclonal antibodies targeting CD137 can enhance immune responses in the tumor microenvironment (Liu and Luo 2023; Melero et al. 2023). Recent work has also shown that agonistic anti-CD137 antibodies may exhibit desirable therapeutic windows for solid tumors and excellent safety profiles due to their crosslinking-dependent activity (Tolcher et al. 2017; Qi et al. 2019). Therefore, a bispecific antibody that concurrently targets p95HER2 and CD137 has the potential to inhibit tumor progression by engaging both innate and adaptive immune responses, leading to a robust and durable therapeutic effect.

AP402 was designed to target p95HER2 on cancer cells and stimulate localized activation of immune cells via CD137 binding. This design not only addresses the limitations of traditional HER2-directed therapies but also exploits the immunostimulatory potential of CD137 to minimize systemic toxicity. The current study was conducted as a preclinical characterization of AP402. The results highlight its potential to overcome p95HER2-driven resistance and enhance anti-tumor immunity for HER2-positive cancers.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

NCI-H292, MDA-MB-231, BT474 and HEK293 cell lines were obtained from the Bioresource Collection and Research Center (BCRC, Hsinchu, Taiwan), and SKBR-3 and NK-92MI cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). All cell lines were cultured following the instructions of suppliers. NK-92MI cells were maintained in MEMα medium (Gibco, # 12,561) supplemented with 0.2 mM inositol, 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 0.02 mM folic acid, 12.5% FBS, 12.5% horse serum. The CD16-expressing NK-92MI (CD16-NK-92MI) stable cell line was generated by transducing the cells with CD16-expressing lentivirus (BPS Bioscience) and applying G418 selection.

Flow cytometry analysis

Adherent cells were detached using Accutase (BioLegend, Cat. #423,201). Detached cells were first stained with primary antibodies in PBS containing 2% FBS (PBS2) for 30 min on ice, followed by staining with an A488-conjugated goat anti-human IgG secondary antibody (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #A-11013) in PBS2 for 15 min on ice. Stained cells were then resuspended in PBS2 containing propidium iodide (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #P3566) to exclude dead cells during analysis. Data were acquired using an Attune flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Evaluation of AP402 dual binding using biolayer interferometry (BLI)

The Octet RED96e system (FortéBio, USA) was employed to evaluate whether AP402 can simultaneously bind to p95HER2 and CD137. The assay began with immobilization of a human p95HER2-derived peptide corresponding to the membrane-proximal extracellular domain of full-length HER2/ERBB2 (50 μg/mL; UniProt accession: P04626-1), beginning at Met611, on a SA biosensor (Streptavidin, ForteBio, Cat. #18–5019). Subsequently, AP402 (100 nM) was introduced to interact with the p95HER2-loaded sensor for a 5-min binding phase. Following this step, the biosensor was exposed to a solution containing mouse IgG2a Fc-tagged human CD137 antigen (100 nM) for an additional 5 min. This setup allowed for observation of increased signal upon dual binding, confirming the simultaneous engagement of both targets by AP402.

Analysis of p95HER2 expression by immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC staining of p95HER2 was conducted according to the HER2 protocol (#338) from the Ventana system. To reduce the background of human IgG1 in cancerous tissue arrays, the variable regions of p95HER2 human IgG1 antibodies were cloned into the constant region of mouse IgG2a, yielding chimeric antibodies with binding activities comparable to parental human IgG1 clones (Supplementary Fig. 1). The p95HER2-transgenic NCI-H292 cell line (expressing p95HER2) was generated and used to create xenografted tumors (positive control). These tumors were utilized together with a parental cell-derived xenograft (lacking p95HER2; negative control) to validate IHC staining conditions. In addition, the HER2 Analyte Control (HistoCyte Laboratories, Ltd.) was used to confirm the specificity of IHC staining conditions. The chimeric antibody clone #R4-5 (mouse IgG2a) was selected for IHC staining due to its specificity for p95HER2. Breast cancerous tissue arrays were purchased from TissueArray.Com LLC (Supplementary Table 1). After IHC staining, positive signals for p95HER2 were visualized using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB). The colorimetric intensity of DAB was quantitatively analyzed using the TissueFAXS imaging system (TissueGnostics GmbH).

Measurement of CDC (Complement-Dependent Cytotoxicity)

Complement-active human serum was collected from healthy volunteers using blood collection tubes (BD, Cat. No. 367955). After blood clotting, serum was isolated by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 10 min, followed by an additional centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 2 min to remove residual debris while preserving complement activity. p95HER2-NCI-H292 cells, HER2-expressing BT-474 cells, or CD137-expressing Raji-41BB cells were incubated with complement-active human serum (12.5% v/v) and the indicated concentrations of antibodies for 6 h. Cell death was evaluated by propidium iodide (PI) staining and quantified using flow cytometry (Attune NxT FlowCytometer, ThermoFisher).

Measurement of ADCC (Antibody-Dependent Cellular Cytotoxicity)

CD16-NK-92MI cells were used as effector cells and cocultured with target cells under two experimental conditions. In the first condition, CD16-NK-92MI cells were cocultured with p95HER2-MDA-MB-231 or BT474 target cells. The effector-to-target (E:T) ratio was maintained at 1:1, with 1 × 105 cells/well for both effector and target cells. Serially diluted antibodies (8 × and 4 × dilution from the top concentration of 66.67 nM in p95HER2-MDA-MB-231 and BT-474, respectively) were added to different wells. An IgG1 isotype control antibody was included at a fixed concentration of 66.67 nM as a negative control. In the second condition, CD16-NK-92MI cells were cocultured with CD137-HEK293 or SKBR-3 target cells (1 × 105 cells/well) at different E:T ratios (10:1, 3:1 and 1:1). Antibodies were used at a fixed concentration (66.67 nM) to assess E:T ratio-dependent ADCC effects. Cytotoxicity was evaluated using a CytoTox 96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (#G1780, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Coculture of human T cells and cancer cells

Healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were purchased from StemCell Technologies. T cells were isolated from PBMCs using the EasySep™ Human T Cell Isolation Kit (StemCell Technologies, #100–0695). Isolated T cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were cocultured with p95HER2-transgenic cells (2 × 104 cells/well) in the presence of in-house-prepared OKT3-coated beads (PolySciences, Cat. #17,135–5) at a cell-to-bead ratio of 1:1 for 3 days. OKT3-coated beads were maintained throughout the coculture period to provide baseline T cell activation. Cocultures were treated with antibodies, and AP402-enhanced T cell activation was assessed by measuring IFN-γ levels in the coculture medium using ELISA.

CD137-Jurkat reporter assay

CD137 signaling induced by AP402 was evaluated using a CD137-Jurkat reporter assay based on the 4-1BB Bioassay (Promega, Cat. #JA2351). CD137-Jurkat reporter cells were cocultured with Wild-type, p95HER2high, p95HER2medium, or p95HER2low MDA-MB-231 target cells (10,000 cells/well) were cocultured with CD137-Jurkat reporter cells (4-1BB effector cells; 50,000 cells/well) in the presence of serial threefold dilutions of AP402. An isotype control antibody was added to cocultures of p95HER2high MDA-MB-231 target cells and CD137-Jurkat reporter cells as a negative control. After 6 h of incubation, luminescence was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions and expressed as relative luminescence units (RLU) to assess AP402-mediated CD137 signaling.

Cytokine release assay

PBMCs were isolated from four healthy volunteers and incubated in antibody-coated Maxisorp plates to evaluate cytokine release under immobilized antibody conditions. Antibodies were coated onto Maxisorp plates at 4 °C overnight at concentrations of 40, 200, 1000, and 5000 ng/well for AP402, 5000 ng/well for the isotype control, and 100 ng/well for OKT3 and TGN1412. PBMCs were then incubated in the antibody-coated plates for 16 h. Cytokines released into the culture medium, including IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α and IFN-γ, were quantified using ProcartaPlex Immunoassays (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

In vivoassessment of tumor growth inhibition using orthotopic xenograft and patient-derived xenograft mouse models

Anti-tumor activity was assessed in three distinct mouse models: an orthotopic xenograft breast cancer model, a subcutaneous xenograft lung cancer model, and a PDX model. All animal experiments performed in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC) in compliance with ethical guidelines for care and management of experimental animals. The maximal tumor size/burden permitted by the IACUC was not exceeded; tumor size was monitored regularly and did not surpass the approved limits. For the orthotopic xenograft breast cancer model, p95HER2-expressing MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells (4 × 106 cells) were mixed with human PBMCs (1.33 × 106 cells) and orthotopically xenografted into the bilateral 4th mammary fat pads of female SCID-beige mice (BioLASCO Taiwan Co., Ltd). Following a 6-day inoculation period, mice (n = 7 or 8 per group) were randomized into four groups: a control group receiving PBS and three treatment groups receiving AP402 at 0.4, 2, or 10 mg/kg. Mice were treated by intraperitoneal injection twice weekly (BIW) for a total of eight doses over four weeks. In the subcutaneous xenograft lung cancer model, p95HER2-expressing NCI-H292 lung cancer cells (1 × 106 cells) were mixed with human PBMCs (5 × 105 cells) and subcutaneously inoculated into the bilateral flanks of immuno-deficient mice. Mice (n = 7 or 8 per group) were randomized and treated with either vehicle (PBS), AP402 (0.4, 2, or 10 mg/kg), or a combination of parental anti-p95HER2 #R4-11 (7.7 mg/kg) and anti-CD137 #54 (7.7 mg/kg) monoclonal antibodies (referred to as the “combo” treatment) weekly (QW).

The efficacy of AP402 was assessed in a PDX model of human breast cancer derived from BR3058 breast cancer tumor fragments. Female NPG mice were subcutaneously inoculated with tumor fragments in the right flank. On the day of randomization, human PBMCs (5 × 106 cells) were intravenously administered to the mice in order to allow for drug interaction with human immune cells. Following randomization, mice (n = 9 per group) were treated with PBS or AP402 twice weekly via intraperitoneal injection for two weeks (total 4 doses). Tumor volumes were measured twice per week using calipers; dimensions were recorded along two directions, and tumor volume was expressed in cubic millimeters (mm3). Tumor measurements were performed in a blinded manner. Tumor growth curves were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by multiple-comparison tests. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Good laboratory practices (GLP)-compliant toxicology study in cynomolgus monkeys

Since the binding moieties of AP402 cross-react with cynomolgus monkey but not mouse proteins, cynomolgus monkeys were used for a GLP-compliant toxicology study conducted by Labcorp, Inc. (Shanghai). A total of 32 cynomolgus monkeys, including 16 males and 16 females, were assigned to four groups receiving vehicle control or AP402 at 25, 50, or 100 mg/kg/dose. The vehicle control and 100 mg/kg/dose groups each included 5 males and 5 females, whereas the 25 and 50 mg/kg/dose groups each included 3 males and 3 females. In the vehicle control and 100 mg/kg/dose groups, 2 males and 2 females per group were designated as recovery animals.

AP402 was administered by intravenous infusion once weekly on Days 1, 8, 15, 22, and 29, for a total of 5 doses, followed by a 6-week recovery phase. The GLP-compliant toxicology study was performed to evaluate potential toxicity, including mortality, clinical observations, safety pharmacology, clinical pathology, anatomic pathology, immunotoxicology, toxicokinetics, and immunogenicity. Safety pharmacology assessments were performed during the predose phase, during the dosing phase at 1–3 h after the first and fourth doses, and during the recovery phase. Clinical pathology, including clinical chemistry, hematology, coagulation, and urinalysis, was assessed during the predose phase and at the end of the dosing and recovery phases.

Blood samples were collected for toxicokinetic evaluation, anti-drug antibody analysis, and immunotoxicology assessment. For toxicokinetic analysis, samples were collected before dosing and at 0.167, 1, 2, 4, 8, 24, 48, 72, and 168 h after the first and fourth doses. Samples for anti-drug antibody analysis were collected during the predose phase, immediately before the third, fourth, and fifth doses, and at the end of the recovery phase. Serum AP402 concentrations and anti-drug antibodies were measured using ELISA methods developed by Labcorp, Inc. (Shanghai). Immunotoxicology was assessed by plasma cytokines, including IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1, and TNF-α, collected at predose and postdose (4 and 24 h) of the first and fourth doses using multiplex immunoassays. Anatomic pathology assessments included organ weight measurement, macroscopic examination, and microscopic histopathology at terminal and recovery necropsy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons are performed using one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA, followed by with post-hoc multiple comparisons tests. The number of experimental replicates (n) and statistically significance levels are indicated in the figure legends. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Generation of p95HER2 monoclonal antibodies and AP402 bispecific antibody

Anti-p95HER2 antibody clones were identified from the fully human-derived OmniMab phagemid library (AP Biosciences, Inc.). To test the binding specificity of these clones, p95HER2-expressing MDA-MB-231 (p95HER2-MDA-MB-231) cells were generated, and it was confirmed that the cells express p95HER2 and minimal HER2 (Fig. 1A, left panel). SKBR-3 cells were further confirmed to express abundant HER2 but no p95HER2 (Fig. 1B, left panel). Compared to trastuzumab, the p95HER2 antibody clones exhibited superior binding activity to p95HER2-MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 1A, right panel). The only p95HER2 antibody clone to bind SKBR-3 cells was clone #R4-11 (Fig. 1B). These results demonstrated that the p95HER2 antibody clones were largely specific for the truncated protein. Since clone #R4-11 showed the best binding affinity to p95HER2, this clone was selected to convert AP402 bispecific antibody.

AP402 was engineered as a human IgG1-based bispecific antibody that simultaneously targets p95HER2 and CD137 (Fig. 1C). The binding affinity and species cross-reactivity of AP402 were evaluated to examine its specificity and potential binding activity for different antigenic forms and animal models. We first examined the interactions of AP402 with its target antigens p95HER2 (truncated variant of HER2) and CD137 (costimulatory receptor involved in immune cell activation). To measure its broader binding ability, the affinity of AP402 for full-length HER2 was also evaluated. As shown in Table 1, AP402 exhibited a strong binding affinity for the p95HER2 peptide (KD 0.336 nM). Since the parental clone #R4-11 showed low binding activity for full-length HER2, the affinity of AP402 to the full-length HER2 protein was also measured. AP402 displayed a higher binding affinity to the Fc-fused form of HER2 (KD 1.13 nM) than to the His-tagged form (KD 38.4 nM), but both affinities were lower than the measured affinity for p95HER2 (KD 0.336 nM). We then examined the specificity of AP402 binding to antigens from different species. The results showed that AP402 has comparable binding affinity to full-length HER2 from human and monkey (Table 1). However, the bispecific antibody showed lower affinity for monkey CD137 than the human protein (Fc- or His-tagged forms) (Table 1). No detectable binding was observed with mouse HER2 or CD137, suggesting that AP402 has limited cross-reactivity outside of primate species.

Binding affinity and species cross-reactivity of bispecific antibody AP402

To further assess the dual binding functions of AP402, simultaneous binding to the p95HER2 and CD137 antigens was evaluated using BLI (Fig. 1D). The results demonstrated that AP402 exhibited potent simultaneous binding affinity to human p95HER2 and CD137 at sub-nanomolar levels, confirming its capability to concurrently engage both targets.

Expression of p95HER2 in HER2-positive breast cancers

Although p95HER2 expression has been reported in HER2-positive breast cancers (Chumsri et al. 2018; Scaltriti et al. 2007), its precise prevalence and tissue expression patterns remain incompletely characterized. We next sought to examine the tissue expression patterns of p95HER2 in patient-derived samples. For this purpose, we first screened our p95HER2 antibody clones for utility in IHC assays. Clone #R4-5 could specifically generate IHC signal in sections of p95HER2-overexpressing but not parental (p95HER2-negative) NCI-H292 cell-derived xenografts or HER2-positive (3 +) tumor control slides (Supplementary Fig. 1). Since clone #R4-5 demonstrated high specificity for p95HER2, this clone was utilized for IHC assays. The breast cancerous tissue arrays with archived HER2 scoring were used to study the percentage of p95HER2 expression in breast cancer patient population (Supplementary Table 1).

The representative images of IHC staining and p95HER2-quantified results by TissueFAXS software were shown, and the DAB intensity greater than or equal to 14 were considered p95HER2-positive (Fig. 2A). In line with previous studies (Chumsri et al. 2018; Scaltriti et al. 2007), p95HER2 expression was detected in about 32% (56/171) HER2 score 3 + patients (Fig. 2B). Based on these results, we concluded that a substantial proportion of HER2-positive tumors (score 3 +) express p95HER2, which suggests that a subset of these tumors may have distinct biological characteristics, and p95HER2 expression has been reported to be associated with altered responses to HER2-targeted therapies in certain contexts (Ozkavruk Eliyatkin et al. 2016; Sperinde et al. 2018).

To determine whether AP402 effector functions are due to p95HER2 binding rather than HER2 binding, breast, gastric, and ovarian cancer cell lines were screened for p95HER2 expression. Although endogenous p95HER2 expression was detectable in some cancer cell lines, such as ovarian cancer cell (SKOV-3), its expression level was relatively low compared with HER2 expression (Fig. 2C). Therefore, three p95HER2-overexpressing MDA-MB-231 clones with distinct p95HER2 expression levels were established by single-cell sorting and designated as p95HER2low, p95HER2medium, and p95HER2high cells according to flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 2D). These clones were used in subsequent experiments to evaluate whether AP402 activity was associated with the level of p95HER2 expression.

AP402 induces antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity but not complement dependent cytotoxicity

To evaluate the ADCC-inducing activity of AP402, we utilized wild-type (WT) and p95HER2-overexpressing MDA-MB-231 cells expressing different levels of p95HER2 (p95HER2high, p95HER2medium and p95HER2low MD-MB-231 cells), as well as CD137-transgenic HEK293 cells (CD137-HEK293). CD16-NK-92MI cells, which express the Fc receptor CD16, served as effector cells to model the ADCC response. The results showed that AP402 potently induced ADCC in p95HER2-expressing MDA-MB-231 cells in a p95HER2 level-dependent manner, but not in WT MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, induction of ADCC by AP402 was assessed with CD137-HEK293 cells to evaluate the potential ADCC-inducing function of the anti-CD137 arm. Importantly, AP402 did not induce ADCC in CD137-HEK293 cells, with no significant difference between cells treated with the isotype control (Fig. 3B). This selective induction of ADCC by the p95HER2-targeting arm corresponds with the design of AP402, wherein the anti-CD137 arm is intended to enhance T cell activation rather than mediate direct cytotoxic effector functions. Importantly, this selectivity minimizes the risk of unwanted cytotoxicity in CD137-expressing cells, enhancing the safety profile of AP402 as a therapeutic agent.

To investigate whether AP402 can still induce ADCC in full-length HER2-positive cancer cells, HER2-expressing cancer cell lines BT474 and SKBR-3 (both without p95HER2 expression) were used for experiments. Trastuzumab was included as a positive control for induction of ADCC in HER2-positive cells. The results showed that treatment with AP402 or its parental anti-p95HER2 monoclonal antibody (#R4-11) both induced ADCC in BT474 cells (Fig. 3C). Of note, the ADCC induced by AP402 and #R4-11 was much less than that induced by trastuzumab. Similar results were observed with SKBR-3 cells at different effector-to-target (E:T) ratios (Fig. 3D). Overall, the experiments demonstrated that AP402 possesses ADCC activity against not only p95HER2-positive, but also HER2-positive (though to a less extant than trastuzumab), cancer cells due to its cross-reactivity with full-length HER2.

Next, induction of CDC by AP402 was assessed. The results showed that neither AP402 nor its parental anti-p95HER2 antibody clone #R4-11 induced significant CDC in p95HER2-NCI-H292 cells or HER2-expressing BT-474 cells at concentrations up to 200 nM (Supplementary Fig. 2 A and 2B). Furthermore, in CD137-expressing Raji-41BB cells, AP402 did not induce a significant CDC response. In contrast, the positive control treatment (rituximab) effectively triggered CDC (Supplementary Fig. 2 C). Taken together, these findings suggest that AP402 robustly induces ADCC but does not induce CDC.

AP402 induces p95HER2-specific T cell activation via the CD137 arm

To evaluate whether the CD137 arm of AP402 can effectively induce T cell activation and whether the degree of this activation is associated with binding of p95HER2, coculture experiments were conducted using p95HER2-transgenic cancer cells and human T cells. Human T cells were cocultured with p95HER2-transgenic MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells or NCI-H292 lung cancer cells in the presence of test antibodies. AP402 induced the highest levels of IFN-γ production in cocultures at all tested concentrations compared to single or combined treatments of parental monoclonal antibodies (Fig. 4A). A similar pattern was observed for p95HER2-expressing NCI-H292 cells (Fig. 4B). To further investigate whether the level of p95HER2 expression influenced T-cell activation, additional experiments were conducted using p95HER2-MDA-MB-231 subclones with different levels of p95HER2 expression (Fig. 2D). As shown in Fig. 4C, AP402 induced the highest level of T cell activation with p95HER2high target cells, followed by p95HER2medium and p95HER2low clones. These results indicate that higher p95HER2 expression levels on cancer cells were associated with stronger AP402-mediated T cell activation. This suggests that clustering of AP402 through its anti-p95HER2 arm is critical for T-cell activation mediated by its anti-CD137 arm. Because AP402 contains two CD137-binding arms, we further examined whether this bispecific format could induce p95HER2-independent CD137 clustering and signaling. A CD137-Jurkat reporter assay was further performed using wild-type and p95HER2-transgenic NCI-H292 target cells. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, AP402 induced minimal CD137 reporter activity in the presence of wild-type NCI-H292 cells lacking p95HER2 expression, whereas strong reporter activation was observed when CD137-Jurkat reporter cells were cocultured with p95HER2-transgenic NCI-H292 cells. Furthermore, the reporter signal increased in association with p95HER2 expression levels on target cells, supporting that AP402-mediated CD137 activation is predominantly dependent on p95HER2-mediated target engagement rather than autonomous CD137 clustering mediated by its bispecific format.

In p95HER2-MDA-MB-231-inoculated animals, tumor volumes and terminal tumor weights were significantly reduced by AP402 at 0.4, 2, and 10 mg/kg (P < 0.001; Fig. 5A). To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of AP402 in a clinically relevant model, the anti-tumor effects were assessed in the breast cancer PDX model screened positive for p95HER2 expression. Although the PDXs grew vigorously from 173 mm3 to approximately 2000 mm3 within 14 days in the PBS group, animals administered with AP402 at doses of 1, 5, and 25 mg/kg exhibited the dose-dependent decrease in tumor volumes with tumor growth inhibition (TGI) of 37.8%, 59.2%, and 61.7%, respectively (Fig. 5C, all p < 0.05). The anti-tumor efficacy in the PDX model further supports its potential as a therapeutic candidate for patients refractory to HER2-targeted therapy.

Safety profile of AP402

To evaluate the safety profile of AP402, a cytokine release assay was performed in vitro using human PBMCs, and a GLP-compliant toxicology study was conducted in cynomolgus monkeys. In the cytokine release assay, human PBMCs were exposed to plate-bound antibodies to mimic in vivo conditions in which antibodies are bound or clustered on tissue or cell surfaces. Then, the levels of cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α and IFN-γ, were analyzed. The results showed that two positive control antibodies, OKT3 and TGN1412, dramatically elevated the secretion of all measured cytokines (Fig. 6A). In contrast, AP402 only induced minimal increases in IL-1β and TNF-α. These minor increases could be attributed to the stimulation of Fc receptors on monocytes by the human IgG1 backbone, as reported in a previous study (Polat et al. 1993). Evidence for this conclusion came from the observation that no TNF-α production was induced by an Fc-mutant (N297A/K322A) isoform of AP402 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

In addition to the in vitro studies, a repeated-dose toxicology study was conducted in cynomolgus monkeys receiving weekly intravenous administration of AP402 at 25, 50, or 100 mg/kg. Male and female monkeys (n = 3/sex/group) were included in each dose group. Serum AP402 concentration–time profiles were monitored over 168 h after the first dose (Day 1) and the fourth dose (Day 22) (Fig. 6B). After the first administration, AP402 exhibited dose-dependent increases in serum concentrations in both male and female monkeys, followed by gradual declines over time during the 168-h observation period (Upper panels of Fig. 6B). Following repeated weekly dosing, serum AP402 concentrations were re-evaluated after the fourth administration on Day 22 (Lower panels of Fig. 6B). Compared with the first dosing interval, reduced exposure and accelerated declines in serum AP402 concentrations were observed, particularly in the 25 mg/kg group. Toxicokinetic parameters derived from these concentration–time profiles, including maximum serum concentration (Cmax), half-life (T1/2), area under the concentration–time curve from 0 to 168 h (AUC0–168), and clearance (CL), are summarized in Fig. 6C. During the first dosing interval, AP402 exposure (AUC0–168: 42,100, 88,400, 156,000 h∙μg/mL for the 25, 50, and 100 mg/kg groups, respectively) increased proportionally with dose level (Fig. 6C). A decreased T1/2 (from 107 to 25.1 h) and decreased AUC0–168 (from 42,100 to 17,400 h∙μg/mL) were observed in the 25 mg/kg group during the fourth dosing interval, likely due to the development of anti-drug antibodies following repeated dosing.

No AP402-related abnormalities were observed in safety pharmacology assessments, including neurobehavioral evaluation, blood pressure, vital signs, or electrocardiography. In addition, no clinically significant AP402-related adverse changes were identified in clinical chemistry, hematology, coagulation, or urinalysis parameters. No definitive AP402-related organ weight changes or macroscopic or microscopic pathological findings were observed at either terminal necropsy or recovery necropsy. Supplementary Table 2 summarizes the safety findings from the GLP-compliant toxicology study. Only mild and reversible findings were observed without significant adverse health effects across all dose groups. Therefore, the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of AP402 was considered to be 100 mg/kg per dose.

Discussion

Targeted therapies have led to better outcomes for HER2-positive cancers, but tumors expressing the truncated p95HER2 isoform exhibit limited responsiveness to existing monoclonal antibody treatments, such as trastuzumab. Since p95HER2 lacks the trastuzumab binding site in the extracellular domain, tumors expressing this protein can evade treatment and also exhibit more aggressive cancer phenotypes. To address these clinical challenges, the AP402 bispecific antibody was designed to simultaneously target p95HER2 and the costimulatory immune receptor CD137. Functional analyses revealed that AP402 stimulates robust ADCC and enhances p95HER2-binding-dependent T cell activation, as indicated by increased IFN-γ production. Consistently, stronger T cell activation was observed in cancer cells with higher p95HER2 expression, in line with the design of the dual targeting arms in AP402. Moreover, AP402 elicited minimal or no cytokine secretion in human PBMCs, suggesting the treatment should carry low risk of causing cytokine release syndrome, a common adverse effect of antibody-based immunotherapies. Furthermore, AP402 exhibited relatively lower affinity for full-length HER2 than for p95HER2, which may reduce the likelihood of T cell activation by normal HER2-expressing cells. Thus, AP402 may have a lower potential for HER2-related adverse effects, although further studies using normal or low-HER2-expressing cells are needed. A GLP-compliant toxicology study in cynomolgus monkeys further showed that AP402 exhibits a favorable safety profile. No AP402-related adverse effects were observed in the toxicology study, including no unscheduled deaths or abnormalities in hematology, clinical pathology or organ systems. In vivo studies in humanized p95HER2-transgenic breast and lung tumor models further confirmed the potent, dose-dependent anti-tumor efficacy of AP402, with robust tumor growth inhibition also observed in PDX models of p95HER2-positive breast cancer. Taken together, the findings suggest that AP402 is a promising therapeutic candidate, which may be able to overcome resistance in p95HER2-positive cancers while maintaining a favorable safety profile.

Although previous studies have shown that roughly 20–40% of HER2-positive cancers have p95HER2 expression (Chumsri et al. 2018; Scaltriti et al. 2007), the precise patterns and frequency of p95HER2 expression remain uncertain. Our antibody clone #R4-5 demonstrates excellent specificity for p95HER2 IHC staining, allowing it to serve as a valuable tool for identification of p95HER2-positive tumors. While we showed that more than 30% of HER2-positive (score 3 +) tumor samples (n = 180) had p95HER2 expression according to TissueFAXS (Fig. 2B), the data were derived from IHC results using a protocol developed for HER2, which has not been optimized for p95HER2. For a more accurate estimation of the patient population with p95HER2 expression and for clinical diagnosis of p95HER2-positive tumors, the IHC method for clone #R4-5 will require further optimization.

AP402 retains binding to full-length HER2 and the capacity to induce ADCC. However, a major therapeutic advantage appears to be its specificity for the truncated p95HER2 variant rather than full-length HER2. The p95HER2 isoform is frequently expressed in more aggressive cancer subtypes, which have demonstrated resistance to standard HER2-targeted therapies (Chumsri et al. 2018; Ozkavruk Eliyatkin et al. 2016). This unique targeting capacity positions AP402 as a promising therapeutic for p95HER2-overexpressing tumors. In contrast, trastuzumab readily binds full-length HER2 and has superior ADCC-inducing effects in HER2-positive BT474 and SKBR-3 cells (Fig. 3C-3D). A plausible explanation for the lower ADCC-inducing potency of AP402 and #R4-11 in these cells is that the test antibodies were selected for affinity to p95HER2, possibly at the expense of binding full-length HER2. One factor influencing these results could be structural differences between full-length and truncated HER2. The p95HER2 epitope is located near the C-terminus of the extracellular domain, near the cell membrane. This proximity may limit epitope exposure, hindering the ability of AP402 and #R4-11 to effectively engage effector cells, which could explain the weaker ADCC response in full-length HER2-expressing cells. In addition to the different structural characteristics, conformational differences between p95HER2 and full-length HER2 may influence how AP402 binds, impacting its ADCC activity toward full-length HER2-positive cancer cells. Importantly, the specificity of AP402 for p95HER2 has significant clinical implications, as AP402 could exhibit minimal off-target effects on normal cells expressing full-length HER2 while effectively stimulating immune effector mechanisms against cancer cells that overexpress p95HER2. Collectively, these results suggest that while AP402 may not surpass trastuzumab in terms of ADCC efficacy against full-length HER2-expressing cancers, it holds therapeutic promise for selective targeting of cancers that either predominantly express p95HER2 or co-express both p95HER2 and full-length HER2.

ADCC is a critical mechanism by which antibody-based therapies exert anti-tumor effects (Lu et al. 2020). As a first-line treatment for patients with HER2-positive breast cancer, the ability of trastuzumab to induce robust ADCC is essential for its clinical efficacy (Li and Liu 2022; Hong et al. 2024). Despite the clinical success of trastuzumab, a significant subset of patients eventually develop resistance or fail to adequately respond to trastuzumab-based therapy. One widely accepted reason for this resistance is the expression of p95HER2 (Ozkavruk Eliyatkin et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2022), which lacks the extracellular domain required for trastuzumab binding. In this study, AP402 not only exhibited potent tumoricidal activity against p95HER2-expressing tumor cells, but it also showed cytotoxic effects on full-length HER2-expressing cells, albeit with slightly reduced efficacy compared to trastuzumab (Fig. 3). This dual functionality underscores the potential utility of AP402 as a therapeutic for p95HER2-dominant cancers, which are largely unresponsive to HER2-targeted therapies (Wang et al. 2022). Furthermore, the ability of AP402 to retain activity against full-length HER2, even at a slightly lower efficacy than trastuzumab, suggests its broader applicability as a potential therapeutic option for patients with resistance or refractory responses to trastuzumab-based therapies. Further studies are warranted to validate these findings and fully elucidate the clinical implications of AP402 in trastuzumab-resistant cancers.

In addition to ADCC, antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) is a potential mechanism of cancer immunotherapy (Van Wagoner et al. 2023). Unlike ADCC, which relies heavily on NK cells, ADCP involves recognition of the Fc region of tumor-bound antibodies by Fcγ receptors on macrophages, leading to the internalization and degradation of tumor cells (Weiskopf and Weissman 2015). This process not only enhances tumor clearance but also helps to stimulate downstream immune responses, further amplifying anti-tumor effects. It is well documented that trastuzumab triggers ADCP by engaging Fcγ receptors on macrophages, thus promoting the phagocytosis of HER2-positive tumor cells (Petricevic et al. 2013; Shi et al. 2015). Notably, the anti-p95HER2-specific arm of AP402 may induce macrophage-mediated phagocytosis of tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. 5). This observation suggests that AP402 might induce ADCP as an additional mechanism of its anti-tumor effects, particularly in contexts where NK cell activity is impaired or insufficient. Nevertheless, further studies will be necessary to validate the full extent of ADCP induction by AP402 and to explore the implications of this activity in trastuzumab-resistant cancers. It is likely that compared to ADCC and T cell activation, ADCP may serve as a more minor mechanism of action for AP402.

An important translational aspect of this study is the inclusion of a GLP-compliant toxicology assessment in cynomolgus monkeys, which provided nonclinical safety information for AP402 under systemic exposure. Under repeated dosing at up to 100 mg/kg per dose, AP402 achieved substantial systemic exposure and did not induce overt toxicity, clinically significant clinical pathology changes, or macroscopic or microscopic pathological findings (Fig. 6B-C and Supplementary Table 2). Nevertheless, the interpretation of these findings requires caution because AP402 exhibited lower binding affinity for cynomolgus CD137 than for human CD137 (Table 1). Moreover, CD137-mediated costimulation is not determined solely by binding affinity, but is influenced by receptor clustering or crosslinking, cellular context, and the activation state of immune cells (Liu and Luo 2023; Qi et al. 2019). In the present study, functional CD137 costimulation in cynomolgus immune cells was not directly demonstrated. Therefore, although high systemic exposures were achieved in cynomolgus monkeys and no overt toxicity was observed, the negative toxicology findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Future cross-species functional bridging studies using primary human and cynomolgus immune cells may include comparisons of EC50 and Emax for p95HER2-dependent CD137 activation and effector-cell functions, which would help further clarify the pharmacological relevance of the cynomolgus model for AP402.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the potential of bispecific antibody AP402, which uniquely targets both p95HER2 and CD137, to offer a novel therapeutic strategy for p95HER2-overexpressing cancers. These cancers are often less responsive or resistant to standard trastuzumab-based therapies and antibody–drug conjugates such as trastuzumab deruxtecan (Guidi et al. 2023). Our results show that AP402 exhibits robust tumoricidal activity against p95HER2-expressing tumor cells, suggesting that it may address a critical unmet medical need in trastuzumab-resistant cancers. Furthermore, the CD137-binding arm of AP402 enhanced anti-tumor immunity through T cell activation, highlighting the dual mechanism of action of this bispecific antibody. Importantly, AP402 induced minimal cytokine release in assays using human PBMCs, supporting a favorable preliminary safety profile with low potential risk of cytokine release syndrome. Taken together, these findings support the therapeutic and translational potential of AP402 for p95HER2-positive cancers. Additional studies using primary human or cynomolgus monkey immune-cell systems may further strengthen the pharmacological and translational validation of AP402. Therefore, further clinical studies are warranted to validate its efficacy and safety.
