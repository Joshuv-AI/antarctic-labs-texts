Alteration of bone architecture following androgen deprivation therapy in patients with prostate cancer using 3D-modeling from hip DXA
Authors: Ji Yong Park, A Ram Hong, Jee Hee Yoon, Hee Kyung Kim, Ho-Cheol Kang, Seung Il Jung, Dongdeuk Kwon, Eu Chang Hwang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This is the first study to evaluate one-year longitudinal changes in volumetric BMD (vBMD) and hip geometry using 3D-DXA in patients with prostate cancer undergoing androgen deprivation therapy (ADT).•One year of ADT exerts a substantial detrimental effect on bone strength, leading to reductions in areal BMD, vBMD, and adverse changes in hip geometry•3D-DXA may serve as a valuable and accessible tool for detecting structural bone changes in prostate cancer patients receiving ADT.Keywords: Prostate cancer, Androgen deprivation therapy, Areal BMD, 3D-DXA, Hip structural analysis

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality among men [1]. While advances in diagnosis and treatment have improved survival rates, they have also brought increased attention to long-term complications, particularly those affecting bone health [2]. Both prostate cancer itself and its treatments contribute to substantial bone loss and a heightened risk of fractures.

Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), a cornerstone treatment for prostate cancer, is well known to accelerate bone loss by significantly reducing sex steroid levels [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Patients undergoing ADT have a 1.6-fold increased risk of fractures compared to those not receiving this therapy [3]. Additionally, ADT induces sarcopenic obesity, which adversely affects muscle function and physical performance, thereby increasing the risk of falls [7]. To monitor and manage ADT-induced bone loss, current guidelines recommend assessing fracture risk using the Fracture Risk Assessment Tool (FRAX®) and measuring areal bone mineral density (aBMD) through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) [2,6,[8], [9], [10]].

Previous studies have consistently reported the detrimental effects of ADT on aBMD. However, bone strength is determined not only by aBMD but also by other key factors, including microarchitecture, volumetric BMD (vBMD), geometry, and bone turnover. While DXA is the most widely used clinical tool for assessing bone strength via aBMD measurements, it does not capture bone quality or distinguish between trabecular and cortical bone compartments [11].

vBMD can be assessed using quantitative computed tomography (QCT) or high-resolution peripheral QCT (HR-pQCT); however, their clinical utility is limited by high cost and radiation exposure. The 3D-Shaper software was recently developed to overcome the limitations of DXA by enabling the reconstruction of three-dimensional bone shape and density distribution from standard 2D-DXA scans. This technique also enables the differentiation between trabecular and cortical compartments at the proximal femur. Notably, 3D-DXA parameters have shown strong correlations with corresponding measurements obtained using QCT, including vBMD [12]. Moreover, this advanced technique allows for a detailed assessment of hip structural analysis (HSA). HSA is a method that evaluates proximal hip geometry using existing hip DXA scans. Among the HSA parameters, cross-sectional area (CSA), cross-sectional moment of inertia (CSMI), section modulus (Z), and buckling ratio (BR) have been reported to be associated with hip fracture risk [[13], [14], [15], [16]].

To date, few studies have examined the effects of ADT on vBMD and bone geometry in patients with prostate cancer. The present study is the first to evaluate one-year longitudinal changes in vBMD and HSA using 3D-DXA in this patient population.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective observational study initially identified all patients with prostate cancer who visited the Department of Urology at Chonnam National University Hwasun Hospital between January 2019 and July 2022. From this cohort, we included only those who received ADT for at least one year and underwent DXA both before and one year after initiating ADT. We tried to minimize potential confounding factors for evaluating the effect of ADT on bone metabolism. Therefore, patients were excluded if they had bone metastases, concurrent active malignancies, were receiving anti-osteoporosis treatment, or had undergone other anti-cancer therapies for prostate cancer aside from ADT—including denosumab 120 mg (Xgeva®) or glucocorticoids—either prior to or during ADT. We also excluded patients with inadequate 2D-DXA results (e.g., due to spinal stenosis or prior spinal/hip surgeries such as internal fixation) or unavailable 3D-DXA data. Ultimately, 41 patients who had received ADT for at least one year and had valid 2D- and 3D-DXA results at both baseline and one year post-ADT initiation were included in the analysis. Comorbidity data were collected, including the presence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and a history of fragility fractures.

Anthropometric and biochemical parameters

Patients were assessed while wearing light clothing and without shoes. Body weight (kg) and height (cm) were measured according to standard protocols, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2).

We collected data on biochemical parameters, including parathyroid hormone (PTH), 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25[OH]D), serum creatinine, total calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, alkaline phosphatase, prostate-specific antigen (PSA), and testosterone levels. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation (mL/min/1.73 m2) [17].

Areal BMD measurement

aBMD of the lumbar spine (L1-4), femoral neck, and total hip was measured using DXA (Hologic Horizon Wi bone densitometer with software version 13.6.0.5, Hologic, Inc., Bedfor, MA). The aBMD of the lumbar spine was assessed at the L1–4 levels after excluding any deformed or fractured vertebrae. According to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, aBMD T-scores for the lumbar spine and hip were classified as follows: a T-score greater than –1 is considered normal, a T-score between –1 and –2.5 indicates osteopenia, and a T-score below –2.5 is classified as osteoporosis [18]. The equipment was automatically calibrated daily using phantoms, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Based on our center’s precision study, in which 30 patients were scanned twice with repositioning between scans, the coefficient of variation (CV) was 0.88 % for the lumbar spine (L1-4), 1.95 % for the femoral neck, and 2.06 % for the total hip. The institutional least significant change at a 95 % confidence interval (CI) was 0.022 g/cm2.

DXA-based 3D modeling at the proximal femur

DXA-based 3D modeling was performed on images from hip DXA scans using the 3D-Shaper® software (version 2.14.0, 3D-SHAPER Medical). The 3D-Shaper algorithm relies on a statistical shape and density model of the proximal femur, constructed from a database of QCT images. This 3D statistical model is registered onto the DXA scan to generate a patient-specific three-dimensional representation of the proximal femur’s shape and BMD distribution. This process generates a patient-specific, QCT-like 3D model of the proximal femur, enabling separate characterization of the cortical and trabecular bone compartments. The 3D-DXA measurements included vBMD of the cortical, trabecular, and integral (cortical + trabecular) compartments, as well as cortical thickness. Cortical surface BMD (sBMD) is calculated by multiplying cortical vBMD by cortical thickness at each vertex on the femoral surface of the 3D model. These measurements were obtained for the total proximal femur, femoral neck, trochanter, and shaft (intertrochanteric) regions. The precise anatomical localization of the 3D-DXA regions, along with the accuracy of the models and measurement algorithms, has been previously described in detail [12].

Hip structural parameters were derived from standard DXA-based regions of interest using methods analogous to those employed in HSA [19]. The parameters included: 1) CSA, an index of resistance to axial forces; 2) CSMI, a measure of a bone's resistance to bending; 3) Z, an index of bending strength; and 4) BR, an indicator of cortical stability under compressive loads. The precision of 3D-DXA-derived parameters has been previously reported [20]. The CVs ranged from 1.60 % to 2.90 % for integral vBMDs, 1.64 % to 2.32 % for cortical sBMDs, 2.92 % to 4.78 % for trabecular vBMDs, 1.71 % to 2.98 % for cortical vBMDs, 1.81 % to 3.22 % for cortical thickness, 1.05 % to 3.09 % for CSA, 1.85 % to 3.98 % for Z, and 1.61 % to 3.83 % for BR. Data on the precision of CSMI were not available.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as number and percentage. A paired t-test was used to compare continuous variables between two groups: patients before initiating ADT and the same patients after one year of ADT. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 29, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the patients

The baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 75.5 ± 6.8 years, and the mean BMI was 24.0 ± 3.0 kg/m2. A Gleason score of 4 or 5 was observed in 53.7 % of the patients, and the majority had T3 disease. The median interval between the initiation of ADT and baseline DXA was 0.0 days (interquartile range 0.0–30.5 days), as half of the patients underwent DXA on the same day as ADT initiation. All patients were treated with luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone agonists/antagonists and/or bicalutamide (anti-androgen). The median baseline serum PSA level was 15.6 ng/mL (4.4–46.7), and the mean total testosterone level was 2.48 ± 1.38 ng/mL. Hypertension and diabetes mellitus were present in 14 (34.1 %) and 7 (17.1 %) patients, respectively. Based on medical records, a history of prior clinical fracture was identified in one patient.

Changes in aBMD

After one year of ADT, aBMD significantly decreased at the lumbar spine (–4.5 ± 4.0 %, P < 0.001) and total hip (–3.7 ± 5.1 %, P < 0.001) (Table 2 and Fig. 1). However, femoral neck aBMD did not show a statistically significant change from baseline (–2.1 ± 7.7 %, P = 0.066). At baseline, the prevalence of osteoporosis and osteopenia was 2 patients (4.9 %) and 20 patients (48.8 %), respectively, which changed to 4 patients (9.8 %) and 20 patients (48.8 %) after one year of ADT.

Changes in 3D-DXA parameters at the proximal femur

After one year of ADT, 3D-DXA analysis at the total hip revealed a significant decrease in integral vBMD by –3.8 ± 3.9 % (P < 0.001), primarily driven by a reduction in the trabecular rather than the cortical component (Table 3 and Fig. 2). At the total hip, trabecular vBMD and cortical sBMD significantly declined by –4.9 ± 7.4 % and –2.7 ± 5.2 %, respectively (both P < 0.001), while cortical vBMD showed no significant change (–1.6 ± 6.0 %, P = 0.062). Cortical thickness at the total hip also significantly decreased from baseline by –1.2 ± 3.2 % (P = 0.011). Subregional analyses of the femoral neck, trochanter, and shaft (intertrochanteric region) showed patterns of change in integral vBMD, trabecular vBMD, and cortical sBMD similar to those observed at the total hip. Cortical thickness was significantly reduced in the shaft region (–1.1 ± 3.2 %, P = 0.022), while changes in the femoral neck and trochanter regions were not statistically significant (P = 0.331 and P = 0.071, respectively).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that the first year of ADT for prostate cancer significantly reduced bone density, not only in terms of aBMD but also in hip vBMD, as assessed by 3D-DXA. The decline in integral vBMD at the total proximal femur was primarily attributable to reductions in the trabecular compartment rather than the cortical compartment—a pattern consistently observed across subregional analyses of the hip. Cortical thickness significantly decreased at the total proximal femur and shaft regions; however, cortical sBMD, calculated as the product of cortical vBMD and cortical thickness, showed consistent reductions at the total hip and all subregions. Furthermore, ADT negatively affected hip structural parameters, with consistent decreases in CSA and increases in BR at the femoral neck, trochanter, and shaft. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report changes in hip vBMD and HSA using 3D modeling derived from hip DXA in ADT patients.

ADT-induced bone loss is well established in patients with prostate cancer and is primarily attributed to the suppression of sex hormones, including both testosterone and estradiol, leading to reduced bone strength [3,[21], [22], [23], [24]]. The rate of bone loss associated with ADT is more rapid and severe than that observed in normally aging men. For example, one study reported a 4.6 % annual decrease in lumbar spine aBMD during ADT, a rate even higher than the bone loss caused by aromatase inhibitors in postmenopausal women with breast cancer [25]. In the current study, we also observed significant decreases in aBMD at the lumbar spine and total hip after one year of ADT. The greatest percentage reduction in aBMD was observed at the lumbar spine (4.5 %), which is consistent with findings from previous studies. This was followed by reductions at the total hip and femoral neck, suggesting a relatively greater impact of ADT on the trabecular bone compartment.

Bone strength is determined not only by bone density but also by bone quality factors, including microarchitecture, remodeling rate, and geometry. Although the effects of ADT on bone quality have received relatively limited attention until recently, several important insights have emerged through the use of advanced imaging techniques such as HR-pQCT and high-resolution micro-magnetic resonance imaging (HR-MRI). A cross-sectional study by Dalla Via et al. demonstrated that ADT significantly decreased total vBMD at the distal tibia and radius, as well as trabecular vBMD at the distal radius, as measured by peripheral QCT [22]. Hamilton et al. also reported a decline in total vBMD at the distal radius and tibia after one year of ADT, assessed by HR-pQCT. This reduction in total vBMD was attributed to decreases in both cortical vBMD and trabecular density after adjusting for trabecularization of cortical bone [24]. More recently, Handforth et al. reported changes in vBMD, bone microarchitecture, and strength after one year of ADT using HR-pQCT [26]. Additionally, Greenspan and colleagues demonstrated deteriorations in bone microarchitecture using HR-MRI [27]. However, both HR-pQCT and HR-MRI are primarily used in research settings, limiting their utility in routine clinical practice for assessing vBMD or bone quality. In the present study, we observed significant decreases in integral vBMD at the total hip and its subregions, primarily due to reductions in trabecular vBMD after one year of ADT. Considering the consistency between previous findings using HR-pQCT or MRI and our results obtained via 3D-DXA, 3D-DXA may offer a more convenient and clinically accessible tool for detecting ADT-induced bone alterations.

HSA has been shown to be associated with hip fracture risk. To date, only one study by Watanabe et al. has used HSA derived from 2D-DXA-integrated software in patients with prostate cancer [23]. Their study reported annual decreases in femoral neck CSA, CSMI, and section modulus (Z) of 2.6 %, 3.5 %, and 3.1 %, respectively, with no significant change in BR after one year of ADT in 65 prostate cancer patients. In our study, using 3D-DXA, CSA and section modulus (Z) at the femoral neck were significantly reduced by 2.5 % and 3.8 %, respectively, which is comparable to the previous findings. Additionally, we observed a significant increase in BR by 2.9 %, while CSMI remained unchanged at the femoral neck. Unlike 2D-DXA, 3D-DXA enables assessment of structural parameters at other regions, including the trochanter and shaft. Similar patterns of decreased CSA and increased BR were also observed in these regions. These findings collectively support the notion that ADT reduces bending strength and structural rigidity of the hip.

In the present study, we were unable to directly compare changes in bone architecture between patients undergoing ADT and age-, sex-, and BMI-matched healthy controls. To determine whether the changes in 3D parameters and HSA observed in ADT patients differ significantly from those in the healthy population, we carefully reviewed previously published studies. Regarding vBMD changes, we identified a recent study by Dhaliwal et al. [28], which assessed the efficacy of one-year abaloparatide treatment in men using 3D-Shaper software. In that study, the mean age of the control men without abaloparatide was 67.9 years, younger than our cohort (75.5 years). The one-year mean percentage changes in cortical vBMD, cortical sBMD, trabecular vBMD, and integral vBMD in the proximal femur among control men were all within 1 % of baseline, supporting significant ADT-induced bone loss in 3D parameters. For HSA, we referred to an earlier study by Kaptoge et al. [29], which investigated longitudinal changes in HSA parameters in older men (mean age, 72 years). The reported annual percentage changes in CSA, Z, and BR at the narrow neck ranged from 0.43 % to 1.12 %, further supporting our study findings.

At present, there are no specific cut-off values for vBMD that define significant bone loss, as exist for aBMD. Moreover, it remains uncertain whether 3D-DXA is superior to aBMD for fracture prediction. Therefore, 3D-DXA should currently be regarded as an adjunctive tool to aBMD rather than a routine alternative. Nonetheless, two recent studies by Grassi et al. [30,31] demonstrated that 3D subject-specific finite element models of the proximal femur, derived from 2D hip DXA images using statistical shape and appearance models, outperformed aBMD in predicting incident hip fractures in elderly men and women. Taken together with current clinical evidence, these findings suggest that 3D-DXA may become more widely adopted in clinical practice, depending on future research outcomes.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The sample size was relatively small, and data on bone turnover markers—such as procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide (P1NP) and C-terminal telopeptide of type 1 collagen (CTX)—were unavailable for most patients due to the retrospective study design. Increased bone turnover associated with sex hormone deficiency is known to impair bone health in men [24,[32], [33], [34]]. A 12-month prospective study by Greenspan et al. reported elevated levels of both bone formation and resorption markers at 6 and 12 months of ADT [33]. Similarly, Hamilton et al. observed significant increases in CTX, osteocalcin, and P1NP levels from baseline after 6 and 12 months of ADT [24]. In addition, our study population demonstrated a relatively high baseline T-score, particularly at the spine, which may be attributable to age-related degenerative changes. Moreover, potential confounding factors for osteoporosis (e.g., diabetes) were not adjusted for. Despite these limitations, we believe our study provides valuable insights by demonstrating the significant negative effects of ADT on hip vBMD and geometry over a relatively short period of one year.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings suggest that ADT exerts a substantial impact on bone metabolism, resulting not only in reduced aBMD but also in significant alterations in vBMD and HSA parameters, as assessed using 3D-Shaper software. Thus, 3D-DXA may represent a practical and valuable tool for detecting structural bone changes in prostate cancer patients undergoing ADT. From a clinical perspective, establishing a cut-off value for vBMD that reflects clinically meaningful bone loss requiring timely intervention would further enhance the clinical applicability of 3D-hip analysis. To this end, our results warrant validation in larger, well-controlled studies.
