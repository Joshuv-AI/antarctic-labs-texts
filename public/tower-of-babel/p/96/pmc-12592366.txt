Authors: Lan Wei, Siyang Wen, Tingting Dang, Tao Zeng, Shiyu Yang, Yiqing You, Jiafeng Tang, Haoli Sun, Liang Zhang, Qian Li, Xiaolu Li, Mengxin Sun, Xiran He, Liuyang Zhao, Xiaobing Zhu, Yan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is characterized by poor prognosis and limited targeted treatment options. Efferocytosis, an essential immune mechanism for the clearance of apoptotic cells, is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to tumor immune evasion. This study aimed to identify key efferocytosis-related genes in TNBC, investigate their impact on the tumor microenvironment and immunotherapy responses, and construct a prognostic model to inform and optimize treatment strategies. RNA sequencing data and clinical information for patients with TNBC were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas and the Gene Expression Omnibus databases. Machine learning models were employed to derive efferocytosis-related signatures to predict clinical outcomes and immunotherapy responses. Eight efferocytosis-related genes were identified, considered efferocytosis-related gene signatures herein: P2RX1, IFNG, IL1A, CD93, XKR8, SIAH2, F2RL1, and TLR4. Using the individual risk scores derived from this model, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups, revealing significant differences in immune infiltration and immuno-therapy response. Our study highlights the predictive significance of efferocytosis in assessing chemotherapy sensitivity, emphasizing the pivotal role of the immune microenvironment in mediating drug resistance. Moreover, we identified potential targets for immunotherapeutic strategies in the treatment of TNBC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22816-3.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a subtype of breast cancer characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 21. Accounting for approximately 20% of all breast cancer cases, TNBC is characterized by its aggressive nature, high rates of recurrence, and unfavorable prognosis2. TNBC cells exhibit a mutation rate approximately 13 times higher than that of normal cells, accompanied by frequent copy number variations and pronounced heterogeneity3. Although most primary TNBC cases demonstrate a favorable response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the inherent heterogeneity of TNBC frequently hinders the complete elimination of tumor cells. This, in turn, elevates the risk of recurrence, drug resistance, metastasis, and poor clinical outcomes4,5. The overall survival (OS) of patients with TNBC is generally 18 months or less6. Therefore, identifying effective biomarkers to predict the outcomes of patients with TNBC is critical, particularly regarding their responses to immunotherapy and chemotherapy. Discovering these biomarkers will offer clinicians valuable insights into developing personalized treatment strategies.

Efferocytosis is a critical immune cell function that facilitates the clearance of abnormal cells, pathogens, and cellular debris through phagocytosis7–9. When efferocytosis is impaired, apoptotic cells that are not efficiently removed can accumulate, resulting in necrosis and the release of pro-inflammatory factors10. Defective efferocytosis also disrupts intracellular cholesterol transport, which may contribute to the development of various pathological conditions, including atherosclerosis, lung injury, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and neurodegenerative disorders11–14. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing efferocytosis holds significant potential for informing the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

Efferocytosis not only resolves inflammation but also exerts immunosuppressive effects, creating a microenvironment conducive to tumor cell survival and progression. Studies have shown that efferocytosis-induced M2 macrophages secrete chitinase 3-like protein 1, which contributes to transformation of the tumor microenvironment (TME) into an immunosuppressive state, thereby promoting tumor metastasis15–17. Additionally, efferocytosis enhances the expression of cytokines associated with tumor invasiveness, such as MFG-E8 and NOX218,19. A deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing efferocytosis could open new avenues for cancer therapy. Targeting apoptotic cell debris, cytokines produced during efferocytosis, and surface signals may offer a promising strategy for anti-tumor treatment. However, the specific roles of efferocytosis-related genes in the progression and prognosis of TNBC remain poorly understood.

In this study, we extensively analyzed TNBC transcriptome datasets from various databases. Eight efferocytosis-related signatures (EFRSs) were identified with machine learning algorithms, which were subsequently used to develop a prognostic model for TNBC. This model demonstrated a strong predictive accuracy for patient outcomes and responses to immunotherapy. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis demonstrated the expression profiles of EFRSs in immune cells, whereas in vitro experiments confirmed that the EFRS model is a predictive marker for TNBC. Overall, this study underscores the critical role of efferocytosis in TNBC progression and its implications for the immune therapy response and prognostic risk assessment. These findings provide valuable insights for clinicians, facilitating the development of more effective treatment strategies.

Results

Study design overview

We reanalyzed several previously published datasets to train and validate our predictive model. The analysis included four bulk RNA sequencing cohorts (TCGA, METABRIC, GSE21653, and GSE58812) and four scRNA-seq datasets (GSE176078, GSE143423, GSE161529 and GSE114725). In total, 739 samples were used for model development, and 29 samples were used for single-cell analysis. A study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Construction and validation of a prognostic gene signature for patients with TNBC using an integrative machine-learning approach

In total, 533 efferocytosis-related genes were identified from the MSigDB and GeneCards databases (Supplementary Table S2). Analysis of differentially expressed genes between TNBC and normal breast tissues revealed 11,127 TNBC-related genes, of which 6,607 were upregulated and 4,520 were downregulated (Supplementary Table S3, Supplementary Fig. S1A–B). By intersecting these differentially expressed genes with efferocytosis-related genes, we identified 41 overlapping genes (Supplementary Table S4, Supplementary Fig. S1C). Among these, 35 genes were consistently expressed across all three test datasets and selected for further modeling. In total, 101 machine-learning algorithms were applied to compute the C-index of each model (Fig. 2A). Notably, the LASSO algorithm yielded the highest C-index (0.654). Consequently, the LASSO algorithm was used for gene selection and model construction, resulting in the identification of eight genes (termed EFRSs): P2RX1, IFNG, IL1A, CD93, XKR8, SIAH2, F2RL1, and TLR4 (Fig. 2B–D).

Subsequently, we used the “SurvivalROC” R package and found that high-risk group exhibited shorter survival times, as shown by Kaplan–Meier analyses (P < 0.05, Fig. 2E). The 5-year areas under the curve AUC were 0.918 for TCGA-TNBC, 0.726 for METABRIC, 0.919 for GSE58812, and 0.874 for GSE21653 (Fig. 2F). Overall, the EFRS model demonstrated robust predictive power across the validation datasets.

Association of EFRSs with clinical significance in patients with TNBC

To evaluate the clinical significance of EFRSs, we calculated the C-index for patients with TNBC by combining the EFRSs risk score with clinical factors such as age and pathology (TNM stage). The C-index values for EFRSs were 0.654 in TCGA, 0.571 in METABRIC, and 0.665 in the GSE21653 datasets, all of which were higher than those for other clinical indicators (Fig. 3A). This suggested that the EFRS model demonstrated good predictive efficiency.

To assess the independent prognostic significance of EFRS, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed. The univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that EFRSs were a significant risk factor (hazard ratio = 3.600, 95% confidence interval: 2.200–6.100, P < 0.001, Fig. 3B). Moreover, multivariate analysis confirmed that EFRSs retained their independent prognostic value in patients with TNBC, even after adjusting for other confounding factors (hazard ratio = 3.000, 95% confidence interval: 1.700–5.100, P < 0.001, Fig. 3C).

Based on the results of multivariable Cox and stepwise regression analyses, a prognostic nomogram model was constructed using the TCGA cohort to predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS of patients with TNBC (Fig. 3D). Calibration curves demonstrated the accurate predictive ability of the nomogram for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Fig. 3E). Decision curve analysis confirmed that the nomogram outperformed other models used in this study (Fig. 3F). Additionally, the C-index of the EFRSs was evaluated and compared with other signatures. Notably, EFRSs exhibited superior performance across all datasets compared to the other models (Fig. 3G).

Single-cell analysis suggests that EFRSs correlate with immune features in patients with TNBC

To investigate the expression and distribution of EFRSs at the single-cell level, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from 29 TNBC tumors. A total of 35,635 high-quality single-pass data points were annotated using canonical lineage markers. Quality control metrics, including gene counts, unique molecular identifier counts, and mitochondrial gene percentages, were evaluated for all cells (Supplementary Fig. S2A). The cells were classified into 13 clusters and 10 cell types (Supplementary Fig. S2B). The heatmap demonstrates the expression profiles of the top five marker genes for each major cell cluster (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Uniform manifold approximation and projection visualizations of all cells were plotted, colored by dataset (Fig. 4A), clinical group (Supplementary Fig. S2C), cell types (Fig. 4B), and efferocytosis scores (Fig. 4C–D). Notably, the distribution pattern of immune cells was heterogeneous between normal and TNBC patients (Fig. 4E). The efferocytosis scores of B cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, cancer epithelial cells, macrophages, natural killer cells, normal epithelial cells, and T cells were significantly different between patients with TNBC and healthy patients (Fig. 4F).

Subsequently, we analyzed the pseudo-time trajectory of Monocle2, with normal cell clusters as root nodes, to trace disease progression. The results showed two differentiation branches, with macrophages present at both early and late stages, potentially contributing to immune surveillance (Fig. 4G). TLR4 and CD93 were expressed during both early and late development (Fig. 4H), primarily in macrophages (Fig. S2E–F). Previous studies suggested that CD93 influences cancer progression by interacting with the extracellular matrix and activating signaling pathways which regulate cell adhesion, migration, and vascular maturation20. TLR4 plays a key role in tumor growth and immune evasion21. Macrophages exhibited frequent and stronger interactions with immune cell populations (Fig. 4I–J), and the macrophage migration inhibitory factor pathway was activated in patients with TNBC (Fig. 4K–L). This suggests that macrophages may play a role in TNBC progression through the regulation of efferocytosis.

Predictive effects of EFRSs in immunotherapy

To explore the relationships among risk groups, the immune microenvironment, and immune response pathways, we analyzed expression data from TNBC samples. Immune cell infiltration scores were evaluated across different risk groups using four methods: CIBERSORT, ESTIMATE, MCPcounter, and ssGSEA (Fig. 5A), and the expression differences of immune-related genes among these risk groups were also analyzed (Fig. 5B). Gene sets related to checkpoints, MHC class I, T-cell co-stimulation, and type I and II interferon (IFN) responses were selected, with other pathways used as the background for gene set variation enrichment analysis. Scores were calculated for each sample and correlated with machine learning-derived risk scores. A significant association was found between the type-II IFN signaling pathway, checkpoints, and cell cycle, particularly in the high-risk group. In the analysis of tumor immune microenvironment signatures, a high efferocytosis signature score was associated with higher levels of macrophages, monocytes, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, memory T cells, type-2 T helper cells, activated CD4 T cells, and activated CD8 T cells (Fig. 5C).

To investigate the role of risk scores in predicting the immunotherapy response, we analyzed their relationship with several immunotherapy-related predictive factors. Six types of immune modulators were examined: T-cell inflammation gene expression profile (GEP), microsatellite instability (MSI), immunophenoscore (IPS), IFN-γ, cytotoxic activity (CYT), and tumor mutational burden (TMB). The enrichment levels of these modulators in TNBC were calculated using, while TMB scores were calculated using the maftools package. The results indicated that significant differences were observed across risk groups in CYT, GEP, MSI, IFN-γ, IPS, and TMB (P < 0.05, Fig. 5D). Notably, the high-risk group exhibited higher IFN-γ stimulation and TMB scores compared to the low-risk group, suggesting these factors are stronger indicators of immunotherapy response.

Additionally, we developed a risk score based on eight efferocytosis-related genes in an immunotherapy dataset to assess their predictive value. Immunotherapy response data from patients with TNBC were collected from the GEO datasets GSE22226, GSE25055, and GSE25065. Risk scores were calculated for each sample based on gene expression profiles. The results showed that, in the GSE22226 dataset, patients with lower risk scores had longer survival times (P < 0.001, Fig. 5E). Patients with high risk scores were more likely to respond to PD-L1 immunotherapy in the GSE22226 and GSE25055 datasets (P < 0.01, Fig. 5F).

Predictive effects of EFRSs on drug sensitivity

The Wilcoxon test was applied to compare drug sensitivity between high- and low-risk groups, revealing significant differences for TNBC treatments such as erlotinib, cisplatin, temsirolimus, and sorafenib (P < 0.05, Fig. 6A). Spearman’s correlation analysis of drug-related genes from the TCGA and GSE58812 TNBC cohorts identified potential therapeutic targets for high-risk patients. Genes strongly correlated with the risk score (r > 0.5) included SBK1, MMP1, CXCR4, EPHB3, GABBR2, RBX1, TUBB4B, GFER, GRIN2D, NUAK2, PRKX, and PPIB (Fig. 6B, C). We then downloaded the whole genome from the DepMap website and calculated the CRISPR-based Essentiality Ranking by Evaluating Short guide RNAs scores for the 12 drug-related genes. The results showed that most drug targets were closely associated with the cell cycle, with TUBB4B playing a role in microtubule formation (Fig. 6D). Targeting microtubules could inhibit tumor cell proliferation. To identify potential therapeutic agents, we used the CMap tool (https://clue.io/query) to compare the differential gene signatures with all perturbation signatures in the CMap database. The results indicated that tubulin inhibitors are the most promising therapeutic candidates (Fig. 6E).

In-vitro experimental validation of signature genes in clinical samples

mRNA expression of eight model genes between TNBC tissues and adjacent paracancerous tissues from clinical samples were conducted with RT-qPCR. The results revealed that SIAH2, IL1A, and IFNG were significantly overexpressed in TNBC tissues, while CD93 and TLR4 exhibited lower expression than those in paracancerous tissues. In contrast, the expression levels of F2RL1, XKR8, and P2RX1 did not differ significantly (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, protein-level validation of these model genes was consistent with the RT-qPCR results (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

TNBC: an aggressive subtype of breast cancer

Breast cancer remains the leading cause of cancer burden among women, with TNBC representing its most heterogeneous subtype. Characterized by high metastasis and recurrence rates and lacking specific therapeutic targets (estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2), TNBC is unresponsive to endocrine or targeted therapies22. Consequently, chemotherapy is the primary treatment, yet clinical outcomes are often suboptimal23. Studies report a 5-year mortality rate of up to 40% and an approximate 25% postoperative recurrence rate even with early diagnosis24. Thus, identifying reliable prognostic biomarkers is crucial for accurately assessing treatment response and optimizing clinical management in patients with TNBC.

Efferocytosis: a dual role in tumor biology

Efferocytosis, the phagocytic clearance of apoptotic cells, exhibits both anti-inflammatory and pro-tumor effects25,26. As a frequent event in the TME, it critically regulates tumor cell proliferation, apoptosis, metastasis, invasion, and anti-tumor immunity27–30. The complex metabolic states during efferocytosis influence tumor development via “find me” and “eat me” signals and the release of degradation products31. Furthermore, efferocytosis promotes M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages, modulates secretion of anti-inflammatory and proinflammatory cytokines, and regulates immune cell activation and maturation (e.g., T cells, natural killer cells)32,33. Consequently, it generates anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive signals that reshape the TME, enabling tumor immune evasion and progression. Research on efferocytosis, particularly its implications for immunotherapy and breast cancer prognosis, remains underexplored. Employing multi-omics approaches, we plan to further elucidate its role in tumor progression and treatment outcomes.

Development, validation, and clinical implications of EFRS prognostic prediction models in TNBC

In this study, we developed eight EFRS prognostic prediction models (P2RX1, IFNG, IL1A, CD93, XKR8, SIAH2, F2RL1, and TLR4), which demonstrated strong prognostic capabilities (Fig. 2F). Survival analysis revealed significant differences in survival rates between high-risk and low-risk patients with TNBC (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2E). Moreover, decision curve analysis indicated that this model has the potential to enhance survival outcomes for patients in clinical practice (Fig. 3F). To validate our bioinformatics findings, we performed RT-qPCR and immunohistochemistry to compare the expression of model genes in TNBC and adjacent precancerous tissues. These results were consistent with most published reports, except for F2RL1, XKR8, and P2RX134–37. These discrepancies may be attributable to differences in sample size, patient populations, experimental methodologies, and breast cancer subtypes. In addition, individual biological heterogeneity and variations in clinical characteristics among patients may also contribute to these inconsistent findings, highlighting the necessity for further investigation of clinical samples.

P2RX1, a member of the purinergic receptor family, is associated with clinical outcomes and immune infiltration in patients with invasive breast cancer37. IFN-γ encodes a member of the type II IFN family. The secretion of IFN-γ by immune effector cells typically exerts potent anticancer effects. However, recent findings by Beziaud et al. indicate that, in specific contexts, IFN-γ could modify the breast cancer microenvironment, enhancing stemness, promoting disease progression, and contributing to resistance to therapies, including immunotherapy38,39. IL1A encodes a member of the interleukin 1 cytokine family. Tissue microarray analysis of human breast tumor specimens revealed a strong positive correlation between IL1α/IL6 expression and the cancer stem cell-positive phenotype. Pharmacological inhibition of IL1α signaling reduced the cancer stem cell population and improved chemotherapy efficacy40. CD93, a cell-surface glycoprotein and type I membrane protein, regulates breast cancer growth and vasculogenic mimicry through the integrin β1-activated PI3K/AKT/SP2 signaling pathway41. SIAH2 encodes a protein in the absentia homolog (SIAH) family. This protein acts as an E3 ligase, involved in the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of specific proteins. A study demonstrated that genetic deficiency of SIAH2 resulted in vascular normalization and inhibited tumor growth in a transgenic model of aggressive breast cancer42. TLR4, a member of the Toll-like receptor family, plays a fundamental role in recognizing pathogens and activating innate immunity. TLR4 expression inhibits the proliferation of human breast cancer cells and the secretion of inflammatory cytokines43. In conclusion, these model genes were significantly correlated with the clinical outcomes of patients with breast cancer.

In the present study we assessed the prognostic value of the EFRS model in patients with TNBC by calculating the C-index and integrating EFRS risk scores with clinical factors such as age and TNM stage. The EFRS model exhibited robust predictive performance, serving as an independent prognostic factor. Patients with low EFRS scores demonstrated significantly better survival outcomes compared to those with high scores, highlighting their potential as a prognostic biomarker. Nevertheless, given the inherent tumor heterogeneity, further studies involving mechanistic exploration, animal models, and larger clinical cohorts are essential to refine and validate the clinical applicability of the EFRS model.

To assess EFRS expression and distribution at the single-cell level, we analyzed scRNA-seq data. Immune cell distribution was heterogeneous between normal and TNBC samples. Monocle2 trajectory analysis identified two major differentiation branches, with macrophages present at both early and late stages, underscoring their key role in immune surveillance during disease progression. CD93 and TLR4 were consistently expressed along the pseudo-time trajectory, primarily in macrophages; CD93 is associated with cancer progression, while TLR4 promotes M1 polarization and immune activation44,45. Frequent interactions between macrophages and other immune cells were observed. Activation of the macrophage migration inhibitory factor pathway in TNBC further suggests a role for macrophages in disease progression. These findings indicate that macrophage-mediated mechanisms may represent potential therapeutic targets in TNBC.

Tumor cells evade immune surveillance and develop therapy resistance, facilitating their survival and progression46. Our study demonstrated distinct immune microenvironment profiles across tumors with different EFRS scores. EFRS scores were positively correlated with immune regulatory factors related to antigen presentation, cell adhesion, and co-stimulation, consistent with previous reports47,48. We also observed significant associations among type-2 IFN signaling, immune checkpoints, and cell cycle regulation. Risk stratification revealed significant differences in CYT, GEP, MSI, IFN-γ, IPS, and TMB between high- and low-risk groups. Notably, the high-risk group exhibited increased IFN-γ activation and TMB, which may better predict immunotherapy response. Patients with lower risk scores had improved survival, while those with higher scores were more likely to benefit from PD-L1 immunotherapy. In summary, our findings suggest that an EFRSs-based prognostic model may enhance understanding of TNBC chemotherapy response and inform clinical drug selection.

Research on the role of efferocytosis in tumor drug resistance is limited. Given its association with immune cells, we assessed whether efferocytosis could predict chemotherapy sensitivity in TNBC. Common treatments (erlotinib, cisplatin, temsirolimus, sorafenib) exhibited significant efficacy differences between risk groups. Twelve drug-related genes were identified, with CRISPR-based Essentiality Ranking by Evaluating Short guide RNAs scores implicating cell cycle regulation, particularly TUBB4B, which is involved in microtubule formation. These results suggest that targeting microtubules may inhibit tumor proliferation. Overall, efferocytosis may be a useful predictor of chemotherapy response in TNBC, underscoring the role of immune cells in drug resistance.

Limitations and future directions

Although our study provides valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. The use of publicly available datasets may have introduced selection bias; therefore, further validation of the EFRSs prognostic model in larger TNBC cohorts is warranted. Additionally, comprehensive characterization of the immune landscape requires in vivo and in vitro experimental validation to elucidate the mechanisms by which efferocytosis influences TNBC progression. Overall, our findings underscore the pivotal role of EFRSs in linking efferocytosis, the immune microenvironment, and TNBC prognosis, and confirm the predictive value of our gene model for prognosis, immunotherapy, and chemotherapy. These results may inform novel strategies for the clinical management of TNBC.

Conclusions

The present study identified an eight‑gene efferocytosis-related signature that can be used to effectively stratify patients with TNBC into prognostic subgroups with distinct immune landscapes and survival outcomes. The high-risk phenotype was associated with increased immune activation, an elevated TMB, and worse survival outcome, but this phenotype also showed greater predicted sensitivity to PD-L1 immunotherapy (Fig. 8). These findings highlight efferocytosis as a potential therapeutic target in the treatment of TNBC and provide a foundation for personalized immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Human breast tumor samples

Breast tumor tissues and paired adjacent normal tissues (at least 5 cm from the tumor margin) were collected from patients with breast cancer undergoing primary surgery at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (People’s Republic of China). In total, samples from 14 patients were included. None of the patients received preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. 2021 − 154).

Data resources

Transcriptome and clinical data (TNM classification, age, sex, and OS) for patients with TNBC were obtained from TCGA, METABRIC, and GEO databases49–51. A total of 739 samples were included for model construction: 228 from TCGA, 320 from METABRIC, 84 from GSE21653, and 107 from GSE58812. scRNA-seq data were retrieved from GEO52, comprising 29 samples: 10 from GSE176078, 1 from GSE143423, 8 from GSE161529, and 10 from GSE114725.

Efferocytosis-related gene acquisition of TNBC

Efferocytosis genes were obtained from the MSigDB (v7.5.1) and GeneCards database53,54. The differentially expressed genes in normal individuals and patients with TNBC were analyzed with the “DESeq2” package55, based on an adjusted P-value of < 0.05 and |logFC| > 0.26356. Intersecting genes from these two gene sets were referred to as the efferocytosis-related genes of TNBC.

Development and validation of the EFRSs prognostic model

To establish a consensus on high-accuracy and stable EFRSs, we applied 10 machine learning algorithms and 101 algorithm combinations, including random survival forest, CoxBoost, LASSO, Ridge, stepwise Cox, elastic net, partial least squares regression for Cox, supervised principal components, generalized boosted regression, and survival support vector machines. The signature was developed in three steps: (a) constructing predictive models with the 101 combinations using differentially expressed genes in the TCGA-TNBC cohort; (b) cross-validating all models in three independent datasets (METABRIC, GSE58812, GSE21653); and (c) calculating Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) for each model across validation datasets. The model with the highest average C-index was selected as optimal.

Subsequently, we constructed an EFRS model using the LASSO regression method and calculated the risk scores for each patient using the following formula: Risk score = Exp.P2RX1*0.174 + Exp.IFNG*−0.037 + Exp.IL1A*0.336 + Exp.CD93*0.348 + Exp.XKR8*−0.121 + Exp.SIAH2*−0.201 + Exp.F2RL1*0.162 + Exp.TLR4*0.572. Samples were then divided into high-risk (risk score ≥ median) and low-risk (risk score < median) groups. The Kaplan–Meier curve method from the R survival package was used to evaluate the association between risk groups and survival outcomes. Distribution curves of risk scores, survival status and heat maps of gene expression levels for both groups were plotted.

Furthermore, we used the R package “survminer” to generate Kaplan–Meier survival curves and compare OS between the two groups. The “timeROC” package was applied to plot time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculate the AUC for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, assessing the model’s prognostic performance. Higher AUC values indicated better predictive capability.

Clinical prognostic value of the EFRSs

Risk scores were analyzed in relation to clinical factors including sex, age, pathological TNM stage, and overall pathological stage. TCGA samples were stratified into risk groups, and differences were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum or Kruskal–Wallis tests. The Cox proportional hazards model identified clinical features with the highest C-index. The chi-squared test was used to evaluate categorical variables (age, sex, pathological stage, TNM stage, radiation therapy), while continuous variables such as age were dichotomized (> 55 vs. ≤55 years) and compared using the Student’s t-test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses identified independent prognostic factors. To assess model independence, univariate Cox regression was performed for age, risk group, sex, and tumor stage; significant variables (P < 0.05) were included in multivariate analysis. A nomogram and calibration curve were constructed to evaluate model accuracy. Finally, the efferocytosis-related prognostic model for TNBC was compared to 32 published models57. Using a standardized method, we calculated risk scores across the TCGA TNBC cohort and computed the C-index to evaluate predictive performance.

Single-cell gene analysis

Single-cell transcriptome data from GSE176078, GSE143423, GSE161529, and GSE114725 were analyzed using the Seurat R package (v4.3.0)52,58. Genes expressed in at least three cells and cells expressing 200–10,000 genes were retained; cells with > 20% mitochondrial gene expression were excluded. Data were normalized with LogNormalize and ScaleData. Highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures, and clustering was performed with FindNeighbors and FindClusters. Cell types were annotated with SingleR and literature-based markers58. Dimensionality reduction and visualization were conducted using RunTSNE and RunUMAP. Efferocytosis scores were calculated by ssGSEA, and group differences were assessed with the Wilcoxon test. Developmental trajectories were analyzed using Monocle 3 (v1.2.7) and Seurat59,60. Intercellular communication was evaluated using CellChat (v1.5.0) and CellPhoneDB (v2.0)61,62.

TME Estimation

The impact of EFRSs on the TME was assessed using the CIBERSORT algorithm and ssGSEA to quantify tumor-infiltrating immune cell proportions63. CIBERSORT estimated immune cell infiltration for each sample, with intergroup comparisons performed subsequently. Gene set variation analysis was used to investigate biological differences related to EFRS. Immune cell infiltration was further evaluated by ssGSEA. Survival analysis was performed using the “survival” and “survminer” R packages.

Drug sensitivity prediction and validation

To identify optimal therapies for TNBC, the “pRRophetic” R package was used to estimate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration of various clinical drugs64. Drug sensitivity differences between high- and low-risk groups were compared using the Wilcoxon test. A total of 1,396 druggable targets were obtained from previous studies and screened for correlation with risk scores (r > 0.3, FDR < 0.05) via Spearman’s analysis65. Associated genes were further analyzed for drug interactions using the CMap online database (https://clue.io/)66.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using the TRIzol method. RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA with a reverse transcription kit (TAKARA, Japan). Real-time qPCR was performed using 2×SYBR Green Master Mix (ABclonal, China). Primers were designed with Primer 5.0 and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China); sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2ΔΔCt method.

Immunohistochemistry staining

Expression of SIAH2, IL1A, IFNG, CD93, and TLR4 in tissue samples was assessed using immunohistochemistry. Tissue sections were deparaffinized, subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (0.01 mol/L, pH 6.0), and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 for 15 min. After blocking with 5% goat serum for 30 min, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against SIAH2, IL1A, IFNG, CD93, and TLR4 (Proteintech, China; 1:500 in 5% BSA). Following PBS washes, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Biyuntian, China) for 1 h. Signal was developed with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine and counterstained with hematoxylin. Sections were dehydrated and mounted with neutral resin.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (v 4.2.3). Continuous variables are presented as the standard error of the mean and were compared using either the Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-squared test. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
