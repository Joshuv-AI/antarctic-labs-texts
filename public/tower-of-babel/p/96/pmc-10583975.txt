Neonatal and Early Infancy Features of Patients With Inactivating PTH/PTHrP Signaling Disorders/Pseudohypoparathyroidism
Authors: Giulia Del Sindaco, Jugurtha Berkenou, Angela Pagnano, Anya Rothenbuhler, Maura Arosio, Giovanna Mantovani, Agnès Linglart
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPseudohypoparathyroidism (PHP) and related disorders newly referred to as inactivating PTH/PTHrP signaling disorders (iPPSD) are rare endocrine diseases. Many clinical features including obesity, neurocognitive impairment, brachydactyly, short stature, parathyroid hormone (PTH) resistance, and resistance to other hormones such as thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) have been well described, yet they refer mainly to the full development of the disease during late childhood and adulthood.ObjectiveA significant delay in diagnosis has been reported; therefore, our objective is to increase awareness on neonatal and early infancy presentation of the diseases. To do so, we analyzed a large cohort of iPPSD/PHP patients.MethodsWe included 136 patients diagnosed with iPPSD/PHP. We retrospectively collected data on birth and investigated the rate of neonatal complications occurring in each iPPSD/PHP category within the first month of life.ResultsOverall 36% of patients presented at least one neonatal complication, far more than the general population; when considering only the patients with iPPSD2/PHP1A, it reached 47% of the patients. Neonatal hypoglycemia and transient respiratory distress appeared significantly frequent in this latter group, ie, 10.5% and 18.4%, respectively. The presence of neonatal features was associated with earlier resistance to TSH (P < 0.001) and with the development of neurocognitive impairment (P = 0.02) or constipation (P = 0.04) later in life.ConclusionOur findings suggest that iPPSD/PHP and especially iPPSD2/PHP1A newborns require specific care at birth because of an increased risk of neonatal complications. These complications may predict a more severe course of the disease; however, they are unspecific which likely explains the diagnostic delay.

The terms pseudohypoparathyroidism (PHP) and Albright hereditary osteodystrophy newly referred to as inactivating PTH/PTHrP signaling disorders (iPPSD) [see (1) for description of the new nomenclature and classification] include rare, heterogeneous, and extremely disabling disorders associated with physical, endocrine, and neurocognitive abnormalities (2).

Since the first description of pseudohypoparathyroidism by Fuller Albright and colleagues (3), a remarkable clinical variability, which translates in several iPPSD/PHP subtypes, was described, and the comprehension of the signal transduction cascade activated by PTH allowed us to improve our understandings in PHP pathophysiology. The most frequent subtype of iPPSD/PHP is caused by mutations or methylation defects within the GNAS complex locus, an imprinted gene encoding the α-subunit of the stimulatory G protein (Gsα) and several other transcripts that are expressed in a parent-of-origin specific manner (4, 5). Moreover, mutations in PRKAR1A and PDE4D, which are crucial for Gsα-cAMP-mediated signaling, have been found in patients affected by acrodysostosis, a disease that is included under the iPPSD/PHP umbrella (6, 7).

iPPSD/PHPs vary in clinical presentation and disease severity among affected individuals, even within those carrying the same genetic background (5, 8). The main clinical features of each iPPSD/PHP subtype are described in Table S1 (9), together with their frequency; these disorders are diagnosed based on major and minor criteria, as suggested by international experts (1, 10). The major criteria have minimum overlap with other conditions; they include resistance to PTH, ectopic ossification, and brachydactyly. Minor criteria are commonly associated with iPPSDs, though they are less specific for the diagnosis; they include thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) resistance, other hormone resistance such as GH releasing hormone, calcitonin, gonadotropins, glucagon, and adrenaline, motor and cognitive retardation or impairment, intrauterine and postnatal growth retardation, early-onset obesity and overweight, or facial dysmorphism (1).

Clinical presentation of iPPSD/PHP usually develops during mid- and late childhood, and it is very common to miss the diagnosis in very young children. For instance, brachydactyly is not present at birth in patients with GNAS inactivation, and it develops over time, especially during puberty, while, in patients with acrodysostosis, the shortening of the bones in the hands and feet is usually observed in early infancy (11). Many children with iPPSD2/PHP1A have a normal stature until they undergo rapid and premature closure of the epiphyses between 10 and 15 years of age (12). Similarly, PTH resistance, the hallmark of this disease, is usually not present at birth but develops over time, and hypocalcemia is present in most patients by the age of 7 to 8 years (10, 13, 14).

Moreover, there are only a few reports and case series in the literature about neonatal and early infancy features of iPPSD/PHP; the main reported descriptions are hypocalcemic seizures (15, 16).

In absence of a current description of the neonatal features and early natural history of iPPSD/PHP and with the goal to improve disease awareness, the aim of our study is to analyze a large cohort of iPPSD/PHP patients and to describe the early onset of the disease, with a special focus on neonatal complications.

Patients and Methods

Patients

We collected data from 136 patients affected with iPPSD/PHP, regularly followed up at the Endocrinology Unit of Fondazione IRCCS Ca’ Granda Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico (Milan, Italy) and at the Department of Endocrinology and Diabetes for Children of Bicêtre Paris Saclay Hospital (Le Kremlin Bicêtre, France). Eighty-three children and 53 adults were included in this study (81 females and 55 males, median age 16.3, range 0.9-60.7 years). The comparability of the 2 cohorts for main demographical features is shown in Table 1. Moreover, the public healthcare systems of the 2 countries, with easy access to primary health care, makes the pregnancy outcomes and the rate of neonatal complications likely to be comparable. Seventy-six patients (55.9%) were diagnosed with iPPSD2 (PHP1A), 24 (17.6%) with iPPSD3 (PHP1B), 11 (8.1%) with iPPSD4 (acrodysostosis type 1, mutation in PRKAR1A), 5 (3.7%) with iPPSD5 (acrodysostosis type 2, mutation in PDE4D), 3 (2.2%) with iPPSD1 (mutation in PTH1R), and 17 (12.5%) with iPPSDx (unknown mutation). Neonatal Features

We retrospectively extracted from each medical record data on birth and perinatal period including gestational age, weight, length, and cranial circumference. APGAR score was available in 39 patients only. We focused on the onset of neonatal complications and the development of symptomatic hypocalcemia at birth. Prematurity refers to the broad category of neonates born before 37 weeks of gestation. Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) defines diminished growth velocity in utero, documented by at least 2 intrauterine growth measurements. The term small for gestational age (SGA) is defined as birth weight and /or birth length being less than −2 SD score according to gestational age and gender, compared to reference data. Although not all IUGR babies are born SGA and not all SGA born babies are IUGR, these terms are sometimes used interchangeably. We have searched for reports of neonatal complications occurring during the first month of life, then we have divided neonatal complications into 8 categories, including respiratory, gastrointestinal, cardiac, neurological, metabolic (ie, jaundice or neonatal hypoglycemia), isolated hypocalcemia, others (ie, fetal dystocia, cephalohaematoma), and multiple systems when more than one category is involved.

Moreover, we have investigated neonatal features such as cow-milk protein allergies during infancy, cryptorchidism in male newborns, craniosynostosis, or constipation.

As for iPPSD/PHP typical features, we have assessed early-onset ectopic ossification and early-onset obesity, ie, before 12 months, defined by a weight 2 SD above the reference. We have also collected data about the development of hormonal resistance, eg, PTH and TSH resistance before 12 months, and the onset of brachydactyly and short stature before 12 months. Short stature was defined as height 2 SD or more below the mean for children of the same sex and age. We respectively used Italian or French growth charts or World Health Organization growth charts for children of different nationalities.

Resistance to PTH was defined as the association of an increased serum level of PTH and high serum phosphate (greater than the normal range for age: birth to 4 years, 1.3 to 2.2 mmol/L; 4 to 10 years, 1.2 to 1.7 mmol/L; 10 to 18 years, 1.1 to 1.8 mmol/L; and >18 years, 0.8 to 1.5 mmol/L) and/or low serum calcium (<2.20 mmol/L) in the absence of vitamin D deficiency. Resistance to TSH was defined as elevated serum levels of TSH with normal or slightly reduced levels of thyroid hormones. In some cases, we considered the hormonal resistance when the replacement therapy was initiated, respectively active vitamin D or levothyroxine.

To explore the possibility of genotype/phenotype relationship for iPPSD2/PHP1A, GNAS variants were divided as follows: variants that severely impact Gsα function (ie, premature stop codons and insertions/deletions leading to a shift in the open reading frame) vs variants predicted to impact Gsα function less profoundly (ie, amino acid substitutions and in-frame indels).

Statistics

Quantitative variables describing patient characteristics, including age (years) and age at diagnosis (years), were expressed as medians with interquartile range or means ± SD according to their distribution. Qualitative data were described in numbers and percentages. The univariable analysis was performed to estimate potential predictors of neonatal complications among all the clinical variables and to explore the correlation between neonatal complications and severity of the disease. Univariable analysis was performed both for the total iPPSD/PHP cohort and for the iPPSD2/PHP1A cohort. We used the crude logistic regression analysis to estimate the odds ratio of the relationships between the presence of neonatal complications and the quantitative [birth weight (gr), birth length (cm), age at diagnosis (years), and gestational age (weeks)] or the qualitative (sex, iPPSD2 category, constipation, obesity, short stature, and other clinical features) variables.

Crude analyses were used to select variables to include in multiple logistic regression models both for the iPPSD/PHP and for the iPPSD2/PHP1A cohort, respectively.

Variables associated with the outcome with a P-value less than 0.15 were added to the multivariable analysis, except anthropometrical variables at birth, which were highly correlated, leading to collinearity issues, and iPPSD2 category, highly related to the other characteristics of the disease (ie, ectopic ossification, constipation, neurocognitive impairment). When gestational age was included in addition to either birth weight, birth length and cranial circumference, associations of each these anthropometrical variables became statistically not significant. By consequence, we retained only gestational age and cranial circumference for final multivariable analysis. Sex was also maintained in the multivariable model, as it was considered a major variable.

We used a Mann-Whitney test and a chi-square test to compare quantitative and qualitative variables, respectively, when analyzing the differences between the Italian and the French cohorts.

Statistical analysis, tables, and figures were done using R software version 4.0.3.

Results

Neonatal Complications

In our cohort, 14.7% (20/136) of patients were born prematurely; the mean gestational age was 38.4 ± 2.3 weeks. The study population's mean birth weight was 2880 ± 778 g, with a mean birth length of 47 ± 4 cm.

Among the entire cohort (n = 136), at least 1 neonatal complication occurred in 36% (49/134) of the patients. If we do not include newborns with IUGR (45/136, 33%), who could be considered at increased risk of neonatal complications, the rate of complications does not change significantly (32/91, 35%).

Among the patients presenting with neonatal complications, 28.6% had respiratory issues, 24.5% presented with complications involving more than one system, and 10.2% experienced neurological or cardiac complications (Fig. 1). We could identify some recurrent complications such as neonatal hypoglycemia and transient respiratory distress, requiring ventilation in some cases. We considered 38.7% (19/49) of these neonatal complications as severe as they required, in most cases, prolonged hospitalization or, eventually, intensive care assistance; this included cardiac rhythm abnormalities, neonatal asphyxia, transient thrombocytopenia, seizures, and even cardiac arrest or septicemia.

We then performed a multivariable analysis for the entire cohort; the neonatal complications in iPPSD/PHP patients correlated only with the early development of TSH resistance and with the gestational age (see Table 4).

In the total cohort of patients, 51 inherited the disease from their parents (maternal or paternal inheritance pattern), 43 patients had a de novo mutation, and for 42 patients we did not have the information about inheritance. Interestingly, there were not significant differences in the rate of neonatal complications between inherited diseases vs de novo patients (43% vs 37% respectively; P = 0.55). As for the main features of iPPSD/PHP, patients who inherited the disease had an earlier diagnosis of TSH and PTH resistances [Table S2 shows the details (9)].

iPPSD2/PHP1A

iPPSD2/PHP1A patients represent the largest group of our study cohort; noteworthy, 47% (36/76) of them experienced neonatal complications at birth, with the iPPSD/PHP category the most affected (P = 0.001).

Importantly, we found no genotype/phenotype relationship in iPPSD2/PHP1A patients nor a relationship between the parental inheritance pattern and the rate of complications at birth. In particular, the presence of a mutation that impacts severely Gsα function is not associated with the occurrence of neonatal complications (P = 0.5). Similarly, the maternal or paternal or de novo origin of the GNAS genetic defect does not correlate with the occurrence of neonatal complications (P = 0.5).

Patients with iPPSD2/PHP1A presented with complications affecting multiple systems in 14.8% of the cases, respiratory issues in 14.8% of the cases; neurological issues in 5.3% of the cases; cardiac symptoms in 5.3% of the cases; and metabolic, gastrointestinal, or other complications in less than 5% of the patients. Moreover, 25% (9/36) of iPPSD2/PHP1A patients developed more than 2 complications at birth (Fig. 2). We found that 14/76 (18.4%) among the iPPSD2/PHP1A patients experienced transient respiratory distress and 8 (10.5%) developed neonatal hypoglycemia.

We performed a univariable analysis [Table S3 (9)], to explore the correlation between neonatal complications in iPPSD2/PHP1A patients and the risk of developing disease features later in life. We found significant correlations overlapping with that of the entire cohort, ie, gestational age, neurocognitive impairment, low birth weight and low birth length, cranial circumference, and early TSH resistance.

We selected these criteria to perform a multivariable analysis [Table S3 (9)], in this cohort of iPPSD2/PHP1A patients; we found a significant correlation only between the neonatal complications and the early development of TSH resistance.

First Year of Life

We extended our analysis beyond the neonatal period to the features presented during the first year of life. As for major criteria, 16% of patients developed subcutaneous ectopic ossifications, 7.3% developed PTH resistance, and 6.6% developed brachydactyly. Moreover, 15.4% of patients developed early-onset obesity.

When considering only the iPPSD4 and iPPSD5 (acrodysostosis type 1 and 2 with mutation in the PRKAR1A and PDE4D genes, respectively), brachydactyly was described in the first year of life, respectively, in 18% and 20%, and early-onset obesity was not detected in any of them, though in a small cohort of 11 and 5 patients, respectively. Importantly, only 28% of the iPPSD/PHP children developed at least 1 major criterion before 12 months of life. Beside these 3 major symptoms, ie, PTH resistance, ectopic ossification, and brachydactyly, we identified TSH resistance in only 22.8% of the cohort, though this feature can be considered quite specific of the iPPSD/PHP disease. Interestingly, when we considered only the iPPSD2/PHP1A patients, 34.2% of the children developed TSH resistance before 12 months of life. Early-onset obesity was also frequent in IPPSD2/PHP1A and iPPSD3/PHP1B patients, found in 20% and 25% of the patients, respectively.

Uncommon Features

It is worth noting that atypical features were well represented in this cohort of patients, eg, neurocognitive impairment in 54.4% of patients with variable degree of presentation and severity, varying between 25% in iPPSD3/PHP1B and 100% in iPPSD5/acrodysostosis type 2, although we describe here a small cohort of only 5 patients. Constipation was described in about 50% of the patients affected with iPPSD2/PHP1A and cryptorchidism in 20% of the cohort and in 40% of iPPSD3/PHP1B.

This suggests that these features may represent early signs of the disease, which should raise suspicion for an early diagnosis of iPPSD/PHP.

Discussion

iPPSD/PHP is a group of rare diseases with an unknown prevalence with an estimate of 1/295 000 in Japan, 1/150 000 in Italy (Orphanet ID#12935), and 1.1/100 000 in Denmark (17); however, the prevalence may be closer to 1/20 000 in the United States (18). The diagnosis of iPPSD/PHP is based on the association of clinical and biochemical features that may vary depending on the age of the patient, family history, and mode of inheritance. The median age at diagnosis in our study cohort was 6 years (mean 8 years), in accordance with the onset of the iPPSD/PHP major features described in the literature, ie, PTH resistance and brachydactyly (10). In patients with iPPSD2/PHP1A, PTH resistance, the hallmark of this disease, develops over time; hence hypocalcemia is present in most patients by the age of 7 to 8 years (5, 8, 13, 19). In this scenario, the early diagnosis of iPPSD/PHP is quite challenging. Obviously, patients who inherited the mutation and had a known family history are regularly screened for main biochemical features associated with iPPSD/PHP and had an earlier diagnosis of TSH and PTH resistances, demonstrating that there is an overall diagnostic delay in this group of patients and we should try to reduce it.

As for neonatal period, there are only very few reports describing infants with pseudohypoparathyroidism who presented in their early infancy with hypocalcemic seizures. Patra et al described a case series with patients in their late neonatal period (aged between 10 days and 40 days) (16), and similarly, Narang et al described a 4-day-old male neonate presenting with a history of multiple episodes of clonic convulsions since the second day of life (15). None of these cases were genetically characterized. Accordingly, we found only 4.4% of patients with neonatal symptomatic hypocalcemia. Yet, we identified a more complex spectrum of neonatal complications in a large cohort of iPPSD/PHP patients. We studied a large cohort of patients affected with iPPSD/PHP, and we could demonstrate that at least one neonatal complication occurred in 36% of our cohort. Among this cohort, patients affected with the GNAS loss of function mutations, ie, iPPSD2/PHP1A patients, were the most affected by the neonatal complications. Indeed, almost half of the patients presented with at least one complication during the neonatal period, suggesting that this group of patients may represent a more severe and developmental disorder that could (and should) be diagnosed earlier in life (2, 5, 8, 12, 20, 21).

A significant subset of patients (38.7%) presented with severe neonatal complications that in most cases required prolonged hospitalization time or eventually intensive care unit assistance. About 14.7% of the patients reported in our study were born prematurely, which is slightly higher than the referent population. However, this does not fully explain the prevalence of severe neonatal complications in this group. Estimates from vital statistics data suggest that globally between 9% and 12% of births occur before 37 weeks’ gestation, resulting in around 14.84 million preterm births a year (22). More specifically, for Italy and France, we have data respectively from 2021, 2016, and 2010 by SIN (Italian Society of Neonatology) and INSERM (National Institute of Health and Medical Research in France) describing the rate of preterm births as about 7% but continuously increasing over the years (23, 24).

However, even if the comparison of neonatal complications with healthy newborns is somehow biased, the incidence of complications at birth is estimated as follows: about 10% of term newborns require stimulation to initiate breathing, 5% require positive pressure ventilation before spontaneous breathing, 2% require intubation for longer respiratory support, and 0.1% require cardiac compressions (25, 26). About 15% to 20% of newborns require admission in neonatal intensive care units (26, 27). Referring to these neonatal health indicators, our study patients group presented with a high incidence of neonatal complications at birth.

Besides respiratory distress, we have identified an increased rate of neonatal hypoglycemia (10.5% considering only iPPSD2/PHP1A), one of the most common diagnoses that require admission to the neonatal intensive care unit in neonates. The true incidence of neonatal hypoglycemia varies significantly because of different levels of blood glucose used to define hypoglycemia, with an incidence between 19% and 51% (28). Both IUGR and hyperinsulinism are common risk factors for neonatal hypoglycemia (29). IUGR, as mentioned before, is one of the minor criteria of iPPSD/PHP, and it might have contributed to the incidence of neonatal hypoglycemia in our patients. Moreover, reduced insulin sensitivity is a feature of iPPSD2/PHP1A (30, 31). We propose that some iPPSD/PHP newborns may display signs of reduced insulin sensitivity and subsequent hyperinsulinism beginning in the postnatal period, thus experiencing neonatal hypoglycemia. Additional studies are obviously necessary to better investigate this aspect.

Importantly, we could not find any predictive factor of neonatal complications including the genotype, as previously reported in the literature for other features of the disease (12). In contrast, we found correlations between the occurrence of the neonatal complications and the development of characteristic features of iPPSD/PHP later in life such as neurocognitive impairment. This finding may imply a causal relationship between neonatal complications and neurological damage, but it may also point out that newborns who had experienced complications within their first month of life will develop a more severe disease later in life.

As for constipation and neonatal complications, there is a correlation in the total cohort (not in iPPSD2/PHP1A). Constipation represents an uncommon feature of iPPSD/PHP, not yet described in the literature but strongly supported by observations from the authors’ day-to-day practice. Also in this case, this association could represent an alert of more severe course of disease in children/adults who had suffered from complications at birth.

Interestingly, patients who had experienced any neonatal complications are more prone to develop an earlier resistance to TSH, ie, before 12 months of age. This correlation stresses the importance of TSH resistance as one of the first iPPSD/PHP features to appear, even if considered a minor criterion (1, 5, 8). It highlights the importance of looking for iPPSD/PHP, especially iPPSD2/PHP1A due to GNAS loss of function mutations in infants with an unexplained elevated TSH, for instance through the neonatal screening. On the other hand, this correlation may also reflect a more severe course of disease for patients who developed neonatal complications.

Patients without any obvious causing mutation detected at the genetic screening, ie, iPPSDx, experienced a similar rate of neonatal complications as the other subtypes. This finding elucidates the need for more accurate genetic investigations in these patients, maybe through exome or even genome sequencing.

Lastly, when extending our analysis to the features developed during the first year of life, we have identified symptoms that should be flags for the diagnosis of iPPSD/PHP, especially in infants with a family history or who already experienced neonatal complications. Ectopic ossifications, early-onset obesity, or TSH resistance develop, respectively, in at least 20% of the patients investigated; in our hands, about 46% of the patients presented at least 1 of these 3 symptoms before the age of 1 year.

Obviously, the retrospective nature of this study has somehow limited the amount of available data about neonatal period and early infancy. However, unless very few missing data on neonatal complications (2/136), we were able to describe the early neonatal period both by collecting data from medical records and through patients’ or parents’ interviews in a few cases when details were not provided in medical reports. Finally, this is one of the largest cohort of iPPSD/PHP patients describing neonatal features.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have analyzed a very large cohort of iPPSD/PHP patients, and we have focused on their neonatal complications and early natural history, yet undescribed aspects until now. Our cohort highlighted that iPPSD/PHP newborns are at risk of developing neonatal complications. It is difficult to compare the prevalence of neonatal complications in iPPSD/PHP patients to the general population; nevertheless, we suspect that these newborns are at higher risk of neonatal complications, quite independently of their gestational age. According to our findings, neonatal complications should become one of the clinical features to be considered in the diagnostic work-up of iPPSD/PHP patients. Interestingly, these complications are far from being specific for iPPSD/PHP disorders, which renders the disease diagnosis even more difficult. Therefore, iPPSD/PHP and especially iPPSD2/PHP1A newborns require special care at birth, but further studies are needed to explore potential predictors of neonatal complications.

Despite the high rate and the accumulation of complications during the neonatal period or in early infancy, the median age at diagnosis, in our cohort, was 6 years. In this scenario, we propose that it is necessary to review the diagnostic criteria for iPPSD/PHP in early childhood based on our findings. In fact, many of the clinical features of iPPSD/PHP, including the 3 major criteria, are age-dependent and, thus, may not be specific or pertinent for young children. For instance, an early diagnosis of iPPSD2/PHP1A could be achieved through the screening for maternal GNAS mutation in the presence of ectopic ossification, TSH resistance, early-onset obesity, or family history, even in the absence of PTH resistance and hypocalcemia (13).

Finally, it is possible that, in iPPSD/PHP patients, neonatal complications may predict a more severe course of the disease. In our hands, patients who had experienced neonatal complications were more likely to develop neurocognitive impairment later in life and earlier TSH resistance. More studies are needed to investigate if there are recurrent neonatal complications that could become specific and raise the suspicion of this group of rare disorders.
