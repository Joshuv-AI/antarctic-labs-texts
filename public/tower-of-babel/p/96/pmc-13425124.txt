Influence of milk and dairy products on wound healing after wisdom teeth removal: a prospective clinical study
Authors: Antigona Aliu, Norbert R Kübler, Rita Depprich, Andre Zimmermann, Julian Lommen, Felix Schrader, Max Wilkat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe oral cavity poses a challenging surgical site due to physiologically high bacterial load and limited disinfection efficacy, attributed to biofilm-promoting surfaces of teeth and saliva. To minimise postoperative wound healing complications, clinicians in German-speaking countries traditionally advise against the consumption of milk and dairy products following dental, oral and maxillofacial (OMF) surgery. Although primarily rooted in historical tradition, the belief that milk impairs wound healing remains prevalent. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the impact of milk and dairy product consumption on intraoral wound healing following elective surgical removal of impacted third molars.This prospective, controlled, single-blinded clinical trial analysed data from 157 healthy patients who underwent surgical removal of impacted third molars. Participants were randomised to either consume milk and dairy products at least three times per day (experimental group; n=77) or completely avoid dairy (control group; n=80) during a 7-day postoperative period. Wound healing was assessed using the adapted ASEPSA (Additional treatment, Serous exudate, Erythema, Purulent exudate, Separation of deep tissue, Admission to the hospital) wound scoring system. The total follow-up period was 3 months to monitor for late wound healing complications.The majority of patients (90,4%; n=142), regardless of group allocation, achieved an ASEPSA score of 0, indicating undisturbed wound healing. The overall postoperative complication rate was 7.0% (n=11), with a higher incidence observed in the control group (n=8) compared with the dairy group (n=3) (p>0.05). Specifically, the rate of infectious complications was 2.5% (n=4), with no evidence of an increased infection risk associated with dairy consumption.These findings do not support the assumption that milk consumption impairs wound healing. Postoperative intake of milk and dairy products was not associated with an increased rate of wound healing disturbances or infectious complications. Moreover, the dairy-containing diet was perceived as less restrictive and contributed to greater patient comfort. Consequently, the recommendation to avoid milk following oral surgery can no longer be justified.Keywords: Dietary patterns, Nutrition assessment

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Patients should no longer be advised to avoid milk or dairy products following intraoral surgery. Instead, the decision to include or exclude dairy in the postoperative diet should be based on individual preference. Outdated recommendations discouraging dairy consumption may warrant revision, and patient information leaflets or relevant online platforms should be updated to reflect current evidence and improve accessibility of this message.

Introduction

Third molar surgery is one of the most common procedures in oral and maxillofacial (OMF) surgery. Preoperative pain and postoperative recovery of third molar surgery are a challenging matter in oral surgery.1 2

Prevention of wound healing complications involves rigorous preoperative perioral and intraoral disinfection. Additionally, postoperative management emphasises dietary recommendations aimed at supporting the healing process. Although adherence to a soft diet is standard postoperative practice, patients are still routinely advised to avoid milk and dairy products. Such information may be found in patient information sheets provided by health insurance companies and likewise on diverse internet pages.3

Despite its historical origins, this advice continues to be embraced by general dentists, though it is notably less approved by OMF surgeons.4 5 According to a survey from 2022 this recommendation appears to be a regional peculiarity of German-speaking countries. In contrast, in Southern Europe (eg, Italy) and overseas regions such as the USA, Thailand and China, the consumption of milk and dairy products following oral surgery is explicitly recommended.5

English-written literature provides ‘proactive diet suggestions’ towards a high caloric liquid diet incorporating cold soft foods such as ice cream, milk shakes and puddings after extensive oral surgery procedures.6 As patients tend to avoid eating after oral or dental surgery because of severe pain or the fear of aggravation through eating, nutritional deficiencies and weight loss may occur. Especially, underweight patients and those suffering from chronic or consuming diseases are considered at special risk for malnutrition due to an already impaired general health status.6

Some literature sources propose hypothetical assumptions supporting the avoidance of milk and dairy after surgery: Milk may increase the risk of wound infection; fermented dairy foods containing lactic acid bacteria may adversely affect wound healing; fibrinolytic plasminogen in milk may inhibit blood clotting and disrupt coagulum development and stability.4 5 Further, milk as a rich source of calcium could interact with certain classes of antibiotics or react with resorbable suture material. It was also presumed that raw milk products may cause tuberculosis infections, which is considered obsolete nowadays, since the cattle population has been declared officially tuberculosis-free in 1997 according to WHO.4 7

The present study aims to examine the impact of milk and dairy products on intraoral wound healing after third molar surgery. The following questions are addressed in this study: does milk cause an increased incidence of wound healing disorders and wound infections after surgical removal of impacted and displaced wisdom teeth? How do patients perceive milk consumption or avoidance in relation to healing and symptoms? What role does milk play in daily nutrition? What other patient-related and procedure-related factors impact wound healing outcomes?

Methods

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Heinrich-Heine-University Düsseldorf (No. 2020–1204; date of approval: 07 December 2020) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This prospective, randomised, single-blinded controlled study included healthy patients who underwent elective surgical removal of fully impacted and displaced third molars at MKG-Praxis Rheintor (Neuss, Germany) between January 2021 and May 2022. Exclusion criteria were removal of less than all four wisdom teeth, partially impacted or fully erupted third molars, a history of coagulopathies or the use of anticoagulant medications, the presence of immunocompromising conditions such as infectious diseases or prior radiation therapy in the head and neck region, regular alcohol consumption or nicotine use, allergy or sensitivity to milk components including lactose intolerance, age under 14 years and pregnancy.

All participants were randomly assigned into either the experimental group or the control group. Randomisation was performed using balanced block randomisation with permuted blocks of the same dimension. The experimental group incorporated milk and dairy products into (at least) three daily meals, while the control group was instructed to completely abstain from milk and dairy consumption for a duration of 7 days. Three surgeons remained blinded to the participants’ dietary group assignments. Both groups underwent a 3-month postoperative follow-up to assess intraoral wound healing complications.

Surgical difficulty was assessed based on dental panoramic radiographs using the Archer and Pederson classification8 9 and compared with the surgeon’s subjective evaluation. As part of the standard operative procedure, primary wound closure was performed using a pseudo-monofilament, non-absorbable suture material (Supramid). Surgical antibiotic prophylaxis (SAP) was administered at the discretion of the operating surgeon.10 For procedures conducted under general anaesthesia, intravenous cefuroxime (1.5 g) was administered 30 min prior to surgery. Prolonged SAP was defined as continued administration of Amoxicillin (750–1000 mg) for 3 days postoperatively. Each participant received group-specific questionnaires and documentation forms to record daily milk consumption in the experimental group and to evaluate subjective parameters in both groups. Additionally, general preferences for milk and dairy products on a daily basis were also collected in both groups.

Wound healing was evaluated using ASEPSA (Additional treatment, Serous exudate, Erythema, Purulent exudate, Separation of deep tissue, Admission to the hospital), a numerical wound scoring method. This modified version of the ASEPSIS (Additional treatment, Serous discharge, Erythema, Purulent exudates, Separation of deep tissues, Isolation of bacteria, Stay in hospital prolonged over 14 days) wound healing score, originally developed in cardiothoracic surgery, was specifically adapted for the assessment of intraoral wounds.11 ASEPSA scoring was conducted twice by the surgeon: during the first postoperative follow-up (day 1–3) and again at the time of suture removal, 7 days after surgery. Each quadrant (ie, operative site of each removed wisdom tooth) was assessed following the criteria of SEPS (Serous exudate, Erythema, Purulent exudate, Separation of deep tissue) as the initial component of ASEPSA (table 1). The mean SEPS score for each patient at each time point was calculated by summing the scores from all assessed quadrants and dividing by the number of surgically removed teeth. The overall ASEPSA score was determined by adding the mean SEPS score to the points assigned for ‘additional treatment’ occurring at any point during the follow-up period.

The statistical analysis of data was carried out using the statistical software SPSS Statistics V.28.0.1.1 (SPSS; IBM Company, Armonk, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics were employed to describe patient characteristics. Pearson χ2 test was used to test normality. Non-parametric tests such as the Mann-Whitney U test or t-test were applied to evaluate significant differences between the two groups. Results with p≤0.05 were considered statistically significant. Further linear regression analysis was used to determine the strength of relationship between two variables.

Results

General statistics

After data cleansing, which involved the exclusion of patients based on predefined criteria or non-compliance with study instructions, data from 157 of the originally enrolled 203 patients were deemed eligible for inclusion and subsequently analysed. Of these, 77 patients were assigned to the experimental group and 80 to the control group. A total of 628 wisdom teeth were removed (experimental n=308, control n=320). Removed wisdom teeth were equally distributed among upper and lower jaw. The average age of treated patients was 19.03 (±4.53) years in the experimental and 18.71 (±4.13) years in the control group. Gender distribution was balanced across both groups, with females comprising 60.5% (n=95) and males 39.5% (n=62) of the total patient cohort.

Surgical procedure parameters

A total of 62.4% (n=98) of the surgical procedures were conducted under general anaesthesia, while the remaining cases (37.6%; n=59) were performed under regional anaesthesia. The mean duration of operative removal was 34.43 (±9.50) minutes in the experimental group and 37.11 (±10.42) minutes in the control group. Surgery duration was significantly shorter under general anaesthesia (32.62±7.59 min) compared with local anaesthesia (41.00±11.47 min) (p=0001).

Preoperative assessment of surgical difficulty, based on the Archer and Pederson classification, showed an overall medium level of difficulty. Both radiological assessment of surgical difficulty for lower molars and the surgeon’s subjective assessment showed significant correlation with surgery duration and were predictive of extended operative times (p<0.05).

The need for intraoperative odontosection (n=166) or reconstruction of maxillary sinus membrane perforation (SMP) (n=22) significantly increased the duration of surgery, with a mean increase in time of 8.34 (± 2.87) minutes for SMP and 8.03 (±3.76) minutes for odontosection, respectively (p=0001). The number of sectioned teeth and SMP was nearly identical in both groups (sectioned teeth: n=83 for each group and SMP: experimental n=10 group, control n=12).

Duration of surgery was significantly linked to increased postoperative pain in the first week (p=0002). Spearman’s analysis showed a stronger correlation between surgery time and pain during days 0–3 in the control group (p=0001) than in the experimental group (p>0.05) (figure 1), suggesting dairy consumption may positively affect pain.

Pain levels increased with longer surgery duration in both groups, but the correlation reached statistical significance only in the control group (p=0001). The relationship was stronger in the control group, as indicated by higher Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ).

Antibiotic prophylaxis

SAP was administered to 43 patients (55.8%) in the experimental group and 48 patients (60.0%) in the control group. Among these patients, 44.2% (n=19) of the experimental group and 22.9% (n=11) of the control group received a single preoperative dose of 1.5 g cefuroxime (figure 2), while the remaining patients were prescribed oral antibiotics for 3 days. The SAP regimen did not significantly influence the overall ASEPSA wound score, yielding comparable wound healing outcomes between the two groups.

Assessment of wound healing

A mean SEPS score of 0 was recorded in 91.7% (n=144) of patients, reflecting intact wound healing across both groups. SEPS scores did not exceed 0.5 out of 5 points in any case (figure 3). Unscheduled medical consultations for wound complications were recorded in both groups during the first follow-up at 7 days and the late phase between 1 week and 12 weeks after surgery (figure 4). Throughout the 3-month aftercare period, the experimental group (n=3) needed notably fewer medical consultations compared with the control group (n=8).

Reasons for unscheduled consultations in the early postoperative phase included wound dehiscence (n=2), abscess formation (n=1), postoperative bleeding (n=2) and haematoma (n=2). Bleeding was likely due to mechanical trauma during tooth brushing and was managed by either pressure application or surgically through suturing or electric coagulation. Both haematomas and abscesses were treated by incision and drainage. Antibiotic therapy was required only for abscess formation. Most complications (71.4%) occurred in the lower jaw, with only one maxillary case each of bleeding and haematoma. None involved SMP or infection.

Late complications mostly occurred 3–4 weeks after surgery, with abscesses documented in one patient of the experimental group and two patients of the control group, unrelated to prior SMP. Additionally, two of these three cases had received antibiotic prophylaxis. Overall postoperative complication rate was 7.0% (n=11), including late complications at 2.5% (n=4). Everyday dairy intake (unrelated to the study)

In the experimental group, most participants reported that they regularly consume milk and dairy products independent of their participation in this study (figure 6) and were able to maintain their usual diet postoperatively. They reported beneficial cooling and pain-relief effects from cold dairy. In contrast, the control group found dietary restrictions burdensome and were dissatisfied with limited soft food options.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the influence of milk and dairy products on intraoral wound healing after surgical removal of wisdom teeth. The overall complication rate of 7.0% was statistically independent of dairy intake. These findings, therefore, provide no evidence that dairy intake increases the risk of wound healing complications after third molar extraction. To the best of our knowledge, no comparable clinical trial has prospectively assessed both the clinical and patient-reported outcomes related to milk consumption following intraoral surgery. However, prospective studies investigating postoperative complications after third molar surgery—without specifically examining dairy intake—report complication rates comparable to those observed in our study.12 13 This supports the conclusion that dairy intake does not affect overall complication rates.

Likewise, a clinical study examined quantitative and qualitative changes in the oral microbiome following consumption of milk and dairy products, comparing healthy adults to patients treated with radiotherapy following oropharyngeal malignancy resection.14

It was hypothesised that increased pathogen levels or altered bacterial composition might worsen radiation-related complications like mucositis. However, no significant changes in bacterial load were observed after dairy consumption in either radiated patients or healthy controls. The authors concluded that patients with severe irradiation-related mucositis did not benefit from avoiding dairy. Rather, they required a nutrient-dense diet tailored to the postsurgery and postradiotherapy conditions affecting the oral region.14

The hypothesis that milk consumption after third molar extraction or other intraoral surgical procedures may lead to impaired wound healing is particularly prevalent in German-speaking countries.5 In their survey, Moghaddam et al found that overall, 56.1% of clinicians involved in either dental or surgical care support this assumption and provide corresponding postoperative dietary recommendations to their patients.4 Several potential pathomechanisms have been proposed to theoretically support this hypothesis, which will be discussed in the following sections in relation to the findings of the present study.

Pathogenic bacteria such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis may potentially be present in milk

The risk for disease transmission of bovine tuberculosis by eating or drinking contaminated, unpasteurised dairy products has been technically outdated in high-income countries since modern milk production technologies as required by law have been established. The intense heat treatment, including pasteurisation/ultra-high temperature treatment, eventually destroys all pathogenic germs.4 15 In Europe, however, infections with Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex primarily affect immigrants or immunocompromised individuals.5 In this study, patients with relevant comorbidities were excluded and tuberculosis-related symptoms were not systematically assessed.

Milk may act as a nutritious growth medium for microorganisms, including facultative pathogenic bacteria found in the oral cavity

Milk contains essential nutrients such as carbohydrates, fat, protein as well as vitamins and minerals.16 Therefore, it is considered an important component of the human diet. However, these nutrients can also support bacterial growth, raising concerns about increased bacterial load—especially since dairy’s mucosa-coating properties may enhance bacterial adherence to mucosal and tooth surfaces.5 This study found no evidence that milk intake increases bacterial growth at surgical sites. Even in patients with reduced saliva production due to irradiation, which impairs mucosal clearance capacity, no increase in bacterial load was observed.14

Common risk factors for impaired wound healing after surgical third molar removal

Common risk factors for wound infections after wisdom tooth removal include excessive bleeding, extensive surgery, coagulopathy or anticoagulant use.5 17 18 Prolonged surgery duration also increases the risk of surgical site infections (SSIs).19–21 In this study, odontosection, SMP repair and surgical difficulty were not associated with increased wound complications. Longer surgical duration, however, correlated significantly with higher ASEPSA wound scores (p=0,03). Since ASEPSA scores ranged from 0 to 2 in nearly all patients, the observed correlation does not suggest that surgical duration predicts severe wound complications for this specific indication. Moreover, the dimension of prolonged surgical duration requires further definition to determine whether complications can be attributed to an average operative time of 60±30 min. Furthermore, there is no evidence that milk consumption interacted with established risk factors for third molar surgery, as these were not more prevalent in the experimental group.

Milk interferes with the uptake of antibiotics

The use of prophylactic antibiotics in OMF surgery remains controversial. Existing guidelines for third molar surgery provide no definitive recommendations on their routine use.10 Several high-quality reviews suggest that prophylactic antibiotic regimens reduce the risk of SSIs and alveolar osteitis.21–23 Conversely, improper antibiotic use, especially over short periods, poses risks such as gastrointestinal disturbances and increased antimicrobial resistance.24 25 Some evidence indicates that milk can interfere with certain antibiotic classes, such as tetracyclines and quinolones. However, amoxicillin—the primary antibiotic used in dentistry and maxillofacial surgery5—is not known to interact with dairy products. In this study, SAP did not significantly affect wound infection rates in either the dairy or control group. This consistency across groups suggests no interaction between antibiotic use and dairy consumption.

Milk resolves surgical sutures leading to suture insufficiency and wound dehiscence

Several studies have investigated the influence of wound closure techniques and suture material on postoperative outcomes after third molar surgery. Primary wound healing by suturing is typically associated with accelerated healing but may increase postoperative symptoms during the early recovery period.26 27

Supramid has been shown to be reliable for intraoral surgeries due to its enhanced ability to maintain knot integrity.28 Synthetic absorbable sutures, however, degrade through hydrolysis, a process that is pronounced in alkaline environments. Since lactic acid bacteria in milk create an acidic environment, hydrolytic degradation of absorbable sutures after dairy consumption appears unlikely.4 As non-resorbable sutures were implemented in this study, no interaction with milk or dairy products was anticipated and could not be observed either.

Fibrinolytic properties of milk pose a higher risk for dry socket syndrome

The development of alveolitis sicca dolorosa, also known as dry socket syndrome, is associated with the loss of the coagulum in the alveolar socket following surgical removal of impacted third molars.29 30 Certain bacteria can produce fibrinolytic plasminogen, which dissolves blood clots; however, it remains unclear whether lactic acid bacteria possess this fibrinolytic ability.28 Milk contains very low concentrations of plasminogen. Given the substantially higher levels of functionally active plasminogen in the bloodstream, the small amounts present in milk are unlikely to have any significant bioactive effect.4 No cases of alveolitis sicca dolorosa were noted in either the experimental or control groups in this study. Interestingly, patients reported less pain after milk consumption, especially after extended surgeries.

Health aspects of dairy consumption

The current literature increasingly highlights health-promoting properties of milk and dairy products, with particular focus on probiotics and whey protein gaining widespread attention.31–33 In the surgical context, dairy provides essential nutrition for wound healing and probiotics in fermented dairy products may enhance recovery.4 Current research investigates the effects of probiotics on oral health and their therapeutic use in managing caries and periodontitis when taken orally.34 35 It is proposed that probiotics may positively influence oral microflora by inhibiting pathogenic bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans, suggesting a potential anticariogenic effect.35 Additionally, the anti-inflammatory, antiviral and immunomodulatory effects reported in the literature may also play a relevant role in this context.36 Moreover, probiotics have been shown to enhance wound healing at surgical sites by stimulating collagen production and tissue granulation. They may also support angiogenesis and contribute to more rapid wound closure. In vitro research indicates that yoghurt drinks interact with gingival epithelial cells, potentially promoting healing of intraoral wounds.5 37 38 In the present study, milk and milk products—without further specification as to whether they were fermented or contained probiotics—did not demonstrate a measurable effect on wound healing, either positive or negative.

Methodological evaluation and study limitation

The sample size should be acknowledged as a limitation of this study. The minimum required number was calculated using a G*Power analysis—based on a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.25 and a statistical power of 0.95.

ASEPSA presents another limitation of this study. As ASEPSA scores remained very low due to largely intact wound healing, the scoring system may lack the sensitivity to detect minor disturbances in the healing process.

Intraoral wound healing scores are rarely applied in clinical settings and typically do not encompass the time-dependent phases of wound healing: inflammation, proliferation and remodelling.39 40 They are more routinely used in periodontal surgery and include evaluation of intraoral scar formation. Since scarring is clinically more significant in aesthetically sensitive areas such as the anterior teeth, it is less relevant in the retromolar region after wisdom tooth removal, where functional issues like limited mouth opening are of greater concern. Severe complications, including abscesses or the necessity for therapeutic changes, are usually not captured by these scoring methods.

However, ASEPSA specifically evaluates additional therapeutic interventions and the need for inpatient treatment, thereby capturing severe complications in the wound healing process. Assessment timing was aimed to reflect the course of the three essential wound healing stages. Together with subjective parameters such as pain or mouth opening and without limiting patients to specific types of dairy products, these findings reflect a realistic and representative model of dairy consumption in the observed population.

Conclusions

In this prospective study, wound healing complications after third molar surgery were infrequent and mainly associated with established risk factors. Milk consumption was not identified as a contributing factor to intraoral wound healing disorders, and postoperative dairy intake showed no adverse impact on surgical outcomes.

Although milk may serve as a practical and nutrient-rich component of a postoperative soft diet, its potential benefits for wound healing remain inconclusive. Therefore, a universal recommendation for the inclusion of milk and dairy products in the postoperative diet cannot be made. Patients should be allowed to decide individually whether to include milk and dairy products after wisdom tooth removal.
