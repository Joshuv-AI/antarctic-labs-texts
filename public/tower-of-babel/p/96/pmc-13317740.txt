Integrating fab phage and yeast display through seamless transfer of paired heavy- and light-chain libraries for efficient antibody discovery
Authors: Wei Zhang, Hao Ye, Xingrun Zhang, Cheng Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground In vitro display technologies, especially phage and yeast display, are revolutionary tools in antibody discovery, enabling high-throughput screening and engineering. Previous efforts to integrate phage and yeast display for synergistic antibody discovery have predominantly focused on scFvs and sdAbs.MethodsHere, we developed an integrated phage-yeast display platform for efficient Fab antibody discovery. We transferred paired Fab libraries from phage output into yeast display vectors while preserving VH-VL pairings with the engineered compatible Fab display vectors that we developed for both phage and yeast. Specifically, we identified restriction enzyme sites that are rare in antibody variable regions and designed novel signal peptides compatible with these sites for Fab display. These signal peptides performed at least as well as commonly used benchmarks (such as PelB or StII for phage display, and AGA2 or MFAL1 for yeast display) in bio-panning experiments. Our modular vector architecture supports straightforward library shuttling across mouse-derived, fully human, and naïve Fab libraries.ResultsUsing a mouse immune Fab library, we applied our integrated platform to isolate antibodies against a soluble protein. Following two rounds of liquid-phase phage display and four rounds of fluorescence-activated cell sorting combined with a yeast-based receptor-blocking screening method developed herein, we successfully isolated antigen-specific Fabs with sub-nanomolar affinities and potent receptor-blocking activity.ConclusionsOur integrated approach leverages the capacity of phage display to screen large Fab libraries while harnessing the strength of yeast display for functional screening, exemplified here by the successful identification of receptor-blocking antibodies, thereby streamlining the discovery of high-quality Fab candidates. Statement of SignificanceWe established a facile and powerful approach integrating phage display and yeast display for the identification of functional antibodies from Fab repertories. This approach leverages the rapid, high-throughput screening manner of phage display and FACS–based functional interrogation of yeast display to enable the efficient discovery of rare functional antibodies. Keywords: phage display, yeast surface display, library transfer, paired VH:VL library, functional antibody identification

Introduction

The discovery and development of high-quality antibodies are critical for advancing therapeutic, diagnostic, and research applications. Beyond hybridoma technology, diverse surface display technologies have emerged to accelerate antibody discovery with unprecedented throughput. For the screening of antibodies through display technologies, physical linkage between genotype (the antibody coding sequence) and phenotype is essential. Phenotype traditionally refers to desirable biophysical properties, such as binding activity, catalytic activity, specificity, and thermostability. Over the past decade, advances in autocrine and paracrine antibody libraries, combined with refined screening systems, have expanded the utility of display technologies for direct functional screening, including agonist activity and even cell fate [1–6].

The first law of directed evolution is that you get what you screen for. Despite the recent emergence of nanobody therapeutics, conventional monoclonal antibodies remain the mainstream of current therapeutic antibody development. Historically, single-chain fragment variable (scFv), comprising the variable regions of the heavy and light chains connected by a flexible linker, has been widely used for monoclonal antibody screening and engineering because of its overall simplicity during library construction. However, it is also well accepted that converting scFv to antigen-binding fragment (Fab) or full IgG may compromise expression and binding activity [7–10], making Fab a more preferable format for display-based antibody discovery and optimization.

With the advent of single B-cell antibody technologies [11, 12], antibody campaign paradigms have shifted from binder discovery to functional lead discovery. Display technologies should also be adapted to this trend with a focus on functional hit identification. Phage display is a natural choice for the initial screening stage to eliminate molecules with improper folding or inadequate binding. Yeast surface display and mammalian display, owing to their compatibility with affinity, epitope, and phenotype screening [1, 13–15], ought to be coupled with phage display in tandem so that functional hit identification can be realized in an ultra-high-throughput manner.

Here, we present an integrated phage–yeast display approach that enables seamless transfer of Fab libraries from phage to yeast vectors, thereby facilitating the identification of functional antibody hits. First, we computationally predicted and experimentally validated novel phage and yeast signal peptides (SPs) compatible with mouse-derived antibody sequences. Restriction enzyme sites that occur infrequently in murine antibody-variable regions were strategically engineered into both the SPs and antibody-constant regions to enable efficient, coordinated transfer of heavy and light chains between display systems. Second, we optimized and validated compatible vector architectures for both phage and yeast display to ensure high-fidelity library shuttling. Third, we demonstrated the successful isolation of rare antigen-specific Fabs, exhibiting high affinity and potent receptor-blocking activity, from an immune Fab library using tandem phage and yeast display screening. Collectively, this synergistic approach enables efficient exploration of immune repertoires and supports the discovery of functional therapeutic antibodies.

Materials and methods

Identification of yeast signal peptide candidates for mouse-derived antibodies

Functional germline IGHV, IGKV, and IGLV genes from mouse strains BALB/c, C57BL/6, DBA/2 J, 129/Sv, A/J, NFS, and MRL/lpr were obtained from IMGT (IMGT/GENE-DB program version: 3.1.42; (25 September 2024) [16]. From these retrieved genes, the first twenty amino acid residues were extracted, deduplicated, and used to generate curated mature N-terminal sequences of the antibody heavy and light chains. Five SPs (AGA2, EXG1, MID2, KISH, and MFAL1), previously identified for their high tolerance to diverse N-terminal sequences [17], were inserted directly upstream of each curated N-terminus. These constructs were then subjected to SP probability and functional prediction using SignalP 4.1 [18].

Optimization of yeast display vector for efficient Fab display

Optimization of the yeast display vector was conducted on the pESC-TRP backbone. Unidirectional expression cassettes, compatible with conventional Fab phage display, were designed and optimized. These cassettes were then benchmarked against the commercial pESC-TRP vector, which uses a bidirectional GAL1/GAL10 promoter for Fab display on the yeast cell surface. For the unidirectional expression of the Fab fragment, various self-cleaving 2A peptides and internal ribosome entry site (IRES) elements were introduced at the 3′ terminus of the Fab’s light chain (LC), right upstream of the heavy chain (HC). In addition, the HC was anchored to the yeast cell wall through fusion to the N-terminus of the Aga2p protein.

Yeast strains, media, and manipulation

For antibody yeast surface display, the Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain EBY100 (MATa AGA1::GAL1-AGA1::URA3 ura3-52 trp1 leu2-Δ200 his3-Δ200 pep4::HIS3 prbd1.6R can1 GAL) was purchased from ATCC (MYA-4941). YPD (yeast peptone dextrose broth), SD-CAA (synthetic dextrose with casamino acids), and SGR-CAA (synthetic galactose, raffinose, and casamino acids) were prepared for yeast cell culture as previously reported [19]. Yeast display plasmids were transformed into the yeast cells using electroporation, following the protocol described by Benatuil et al. [20].

Flow cytometry and fluorescence-activated cell sorting

Plasmid-transformed yeast cells were initially grown overnight in SD-CAA medium with a starting OD600 of 0.3, at 30°C and 220 rpm. Subsequently, the yeast cells were pelleted and resuspended in SGR-CAA medium to a starting OD600 of 1 to induce antibody expression. These cells were then cultured at 20°C and 220 rpm for 24 h. For flow cytometry or fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), the induced yeast cells were spun down and washed twice with FACS buffer. A total of 5 × 106 or more cells were incubated with biotinylated antigen and anti-c-myc antibody (Thermo, A-21281) for 1 h at room temperature with rotation. All subsequent washing and immunolabeling steps were carried out at 4°C to minimize antigen dissociation. The yeast cells were washed twice with prechilled FACS buffer, then incubated with secondary reagents (DyLight 633-conjugated NeutrAvidin (Thermo, 22844) and FITC-conjugated goat anti-chicken IgY (Thermo, PA1-28794)) at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. After two additional washes, the stained yeast cells were analyzed or sorted using the BD FACSAria III.

Bicistronic vector design for phage display

The pComb3 vector was engineered for Fab phage display to be compatible with the udFab-ME-EXG1 construct and was thereafter designated pComb3-DD. The display cassette comprises a Lac promoter followed by an LC expression element and an HC element arranged in tandem. Specifically, the cassette is configured as follows: Lac promoter, LC SP, cloning sites for Vκ, human Cκ, HC SP, cloning sites for VH, human IgG1 CH1, 6 × His tag, c-myc tag, and phage coat protein pIII (gp3). Critically, the restriction sites used for library transfer were selected such that, upon digestion during vector conversion from phage to yeast, the residual amino acid sequences remaining at the N-termini of both the LC and HC, derived from the original bacterial SPs, would seamlessly serve as the C-terminal portions of functional yeast-compatible SPs. In other words, the C-terminal remnants of the phage-display LC and HC SPs were intentionally designed to be incorporated into the mature yeast SPs (e.g. MFAL1 and EXG1), thereby preserving secretion competence across platforms without disrupting VH:VL pairing. Among candidate bacterial SPs evaluated, MalE SP (MKIKTGARILALSALTTMMFSASALA) [21] and MmAp SP (MKKNIIAGCLFSLFSLSALA) [22] were further experimentally assessed for Fab display efficiency and performance in mock selection (see Supplementary Data).

Phage propagation and bio-panning

Phage-infected Escherichia coli (TG1) were cultured in 2 × TY medium (100 μg/ml ampicillin, 2% glucose) at 37°C until OD600 reached 0.4–0.6. M13KO7 helper phage (NEB, N0315S) was added, and incubated at 37°C for 30 min without shaking, followed by 1 h with shaking. Cells were pelleted, resuspended in fresh 2 × TY medium (100 μg/ml ampicillin, 50 μg/ml kanamycin), and incubated overnight at 30°C with shaking for phage propagation. Phage particles were precipitated with PEG solution, resuspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS), and centrifuged to remove bacterial debris.

For antibody phage screening, we performed liquid-phase bio-panning. One milliliter of the prepared phage library was incubated with 50 μl of pre-blocked Dynabeads™ M-280 Streptavidin (Thermo, 11206D) in 4% skim milk in PBS-0.05% Tween 20 (MPBST) for 30 min. After incubation, the supernatant was transferred to a new tube and incubated with 5 μg of biotinylated antigen for 1.5–2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, preblocked Dynabeads were then added and incubated for 30 min, followed by beads separation using a magnetic rack. The beads were then subjected to eight washes with PBST and four washes with PBS. Bound phages were eluted with glycine-HCl buffer (pH 2.2) and then neutralized with Tris-HCl (pH 8.8). The eluted phages were used to infect log-phase TG1 cells and then plated on TYE agar plate (100 μg/ml ampicillin, 2% glucose) for overnight incubation at 30°C. The resulting bacteria colonies were harvested for subsequent phage preparation and a second-round panning with target protein.

Monoclonal phage Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay

Escherichia coli cells, either electroporated with phage vectors or infected with panning output phages, were randomly picked and inoculated into 96-deep-well plates containing 2 × TY medium supplemented with ampicillin. The cultures were grown at 37°C for 2 h before being infected with helper phage at a concentration of 1 × 1010 cfu/well. After an overnight incubation at 30°C, phage supernatants were collected for further analysis. For the monoclonal phage Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA), 96-well plates were coated with target antigen proteins and incubated overnight at 4°C. Phage supernatants were added and incubated for 1 h, followed by eight washes with PBST. Anti-M13-HRP (horseradish peroxidase) antibody (Sinobiological, 11973-MM05T-H), anti-His tag antibody HRP (Sinobiological, 105327-MM02T-H), or goat anti-human kappa LC secondary antibody, HRP (Invitrogen, A18853) were added and incubated for an additional 30 min. After eight more washes, ABTS substrate (ThermoFisher, 002024) was added, and absorbance at 415 nm was measured.

Mouse immunization, immunized library construction, and functional antibody screening

All the animal studies were carried out in accordance with ChemPartner’s guidelines. 6–8-week-old BALB/c or SJL mice were immunized with recombinant antigen A. Three days before immunization, the mice were received an intraperitoneal injection of 100 μg of anti-CD25 antibody (BioXcell, BE0012) to deplete CD25+ Tregs and break immune tolerance. The prime immunization was initiated with 50 μg of recombinant antigen A in complete Freund’s Adjuvant (CFA), followed by boost immunization with 25 μg of antigen in incomplete Freund’s Adjuvant (IFA) at 2-week intervals. Three days after a final intravenous boost of 25 μg of antigen in PBS, the spleens were harvested and homogenized. Splenocyte suspension was prepared, and Pan-B cells were isolated using the EasySep™ Mouse Pan-B Cell Isolation Kit (Stem Cell Technologies, 19844). RNA extraction using Trizol was followed by reverse transcription into cDNA using the SuperScript™ IV First-Strand Synthesis System (ThermoFisher, 18091200). For subsequent library construction, we limited our efforts to SJL mice–derived material only.

For efficient transfer between phage and yeast vectors, ApaLI and SgrDI sites were used for subcloning Vκ:VH libraries, while BsiWI and NheI sites facilitated the switch of the Cκ-SP element from phage to yeast systems. Based on mouse germline sequences, V-region specific forward and reverse primers [23] with introduced restriction sites were designed.

Moreover, nucleotide overhangs at the 3′ end of Vκ and 5′ end of VH were also incorporated to generate stuffer sequences during overlap extension PCR assembly of VH and VL. Using cDNA templates, VH and VL regions were amplified from mouse B cells with KOD One™ PCR Master Mix (Toyobo, KMM-201) under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 98°C for 1 min, 5 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 55°C for 10 s, and 68°C for 15 s, followed by 25 cycles at an increased annealing temperature of 63°C and a final extension step of 68°C for 4 min. Purified VHs and VLs were further randomly paired through overlap extension PCR under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 98°C for 1 min, 10 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 64°C for 10 s, and 68°C for 15 s, followed by 15 cycles at an increased annealing temperature of 55°C and a final extension step of 68°C for 4 min. The overlapping PCR amplicons were digested and ligated into pComb3-DD-MmAp/MalE for electroporation into TG1. Plasmid DNA encoding VH:VL libraries was miniprepped, further digested with BsiWI and NheI, ligated with the Cκ-SP elements compatible with phage, and electro-transformed into TG1 again to generate an immunized antibody library. Bio-panning was performed similarly as shown above.

After phage bio-panning, the VH:VL library from phage output was transferred into the modified yeast display vector via restriction enzyme digestion and ligation, followed by replacement of Cκ-SP elements compatible with yeast display. The resulting yeast library was constructed and subjected to next-generation sequencing (NGS) using PacBio third-generation sequencing services at Genewiz, then sorted for functional antibody hits as described above and in other studies.

ELISA-based receptor blocking assay

The biological activity of antigen A is mediated through its interaction with the corresponding receptor A under physiological conditions. To evaluate the inhibitory effect of antibodies on this interaction, a competitive ELISA was performed. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4°C with His-tagged extracellular domain (ECD) of receptor A (2 μg/ml). After a single wash with PBST, the plates were blocked with 1% BSA in PBST. Separately, serially diluted antibodies were mixed with an equal volume of recombinant human antigen A-Fc fusion protein to yield a final antigen concentration of 2 nM. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min and then transferred to the receptor A-coated plate. Following a 1-h incubation at room temperature, the plate was washed three times with PBST and subsequently incubated with an anti-human Fc-HRP secondary antibody (Bethyl, A80-304P) for 30 min. After three additional washes, ABTS substrate was added, and absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a microplate reader. The percentage inhibition was calculated as follows: Inhibition (%) = [1 − (OD415 sample/OD415 control)] × 100, where the control contained no inhibitory antibody.

Surface Plasmon Resonance analysis

Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) analysis was conducted on a Biacore 8 K instrument (GE Healthcare) using Protein A sensor chip from Cytiva. The Antigen A-Fc dimer protein was diluted in HBS-EP+ buffer (pH 7.4), and captured on the chip surface at a flow rate of 10 μl/min for 30 s. Serially diluted Fab-formatted antibodies were then injected as analytes. Sensorgram data were processed using the Biacore 8K Insight Evaluation Software, and the kinetic parameters were determined by fitting the binding responses to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model. The association rate constant (ka), dissociation rate constant (kd), and equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) were thereby calculated.

Developability assessment

Differential Scanning Calorimetry

Thermal stability was assessed by Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) using a MicroCal PEAQ-DSC Automated system (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). Antibody samples were diluted to 0.5 mg/ml in PBS and heated from 20°C to 100°C at a scan rate of 1.0°C/min. Thermograms were analyzed using MicroCal PEAQ-DSC software according to the manufacturer’s instructions to determine the thermal onset temperature (Tonset) and melting temperature (Tm).

Forced degradation studies

Antibody samples were buffer-exchanged into PBS or acetate buffer and adjusted to final concentrations of 3 and 10 mg/ml, respectively. Samples were then incubated at 40°C for 2 weeks. Following incubation, solution turbidity was assessed by measuring absorbance at 350 nm (OD350) using a microplate reader, and changes in monomeric purity were monitored by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC).

Size-exclusion chromatography

SEC analysis was performed on an ACQUITY Arc UPLC system (Waters Corporation). Antibody samples collected at various time points were injected onto an XBridge Premier Protein SEC column (2.5 μm, 7.8 × 300 mm, 250 Å; Waters) maintained at 30°C with a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 M sodium sulfate and 0.118 M disodium hydrogen phosphate, pH 6.7. Eluted proteins were detected by ultraviolet (UV) absorbance at 280 nm.

Hydrophobic Interaction Chromatography

Hydrophobic Interaction Chromatography (HIC) was carried out on antibody samples at 0.5 mg/ml in PBS using a MAbPac HIC-10 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 100 mm, 1000 Å; Thermo Scientific). A linear 40-min gradient was applied between mobile phase A (1.5 M ammonium sulfate in 50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.0) and mobile phase B (50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.0) at a constant flow rate of 1.0 ml/min. Protein elution was monitored by UV absorbance at 280 nm. Trastuzumab was selected as a reference standard for HIC due to its relatively low hydrophobicity, which facilitates the successful development of antibody–drug conjugates. Ipilimumab was chosen as the upper threshold for HIC based on its distinct hydrophobic characteristics [24].

Heparin affinity chromatography

Heparin affinity chromatography (HAC) was performed as previously described [25]. Briefly, samples were re-buffered into 20 mM sodium acetate, pH 5.0. A TSKgel Heparin-5PW column (5 × 50 mm; Tosoh Biosciences) was equilibrated with buffer A (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4) at a flow rate of 0.8 ml/min and a column temperature of 25°C. After injection of 35 μg of protein, a linear elution gradient was initiated following a 2-min hold, increasing from 0% to 55% buffer B (20 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 1 M NaCl) over 16.5 min. Elution profiles were monitored by UV absorbance at 280 nm. Adalimumab was selected as a reference standard and upper threshold for HAC based on its relatively long retention time compared to other clinical-stage monoclonal antibodies [25]. Additionally, its favorable pharmacokinetic profile, including a half-life exceeding 2 weeks in humans after intravenous administration, establishes it as a benchmark molecule in this experiment [26].

Polyspecificity ELISA

Antibody polyspecificity was evaluated by ELISA using plates coated with common off-target ligands. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4°C with single-stranded DNA (ssDNA; 10 μg/ml), soluble cytosolic proteins from HEK293 (5 μg/ml) [27], or insulin (5 μg/ml). After a single wash with PBST, plates were blocked with 1% (w/v) BSA in PBST for 1 h at room temperature. Serially diluted human IgG antibodies were added and incubated for 1 h. Following five washes with PBST, an anti-human Fc-HRP conjugated secondary antibody was added and incubated for 30 min. After five additional washes, the ABTS substrate was added, and absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a microplate reader. Bevacizumab, which exhibits negligible polyspecificity [28], was employed as a negative control, whereas Lenzilumab, which displays robust polyspecific reactivity, served as the positive control [29].

Results

Signal peptides derived from yeast EXG1, MID2, KISH, and MFAL1 selected for fab yeast display

As shown in Supplementary Table 1, a total of 183 and 77 curated N-terminal sequences were extracted for mouse VH and VL, respectively. For mouse VH chains, the SPs derived from EXG1, MID2, KISH, and AGA2 demonstrated similarly strong performances in Y-scores, with only one curated N-terminal sequence scoring below 0.6, where the Y-scores represent cleavage site estimation. In contrast, MFAL1 was shown to be unsuitable to ensure authentic VH N-termini, with only 6 out of 183 sequences achieving Y-scores above 0.7. For mouse Vκ chains, EXG1, MID2, and KISH were superior in supporting the correct maturation of Vκ chains, followed by MFAL1 and AGA2.

Based on germline IGHV, IGHD, IGHJ, IGKV, IGKJ, IGLV, and IGLJ genes from the aforementioned mouse strains obtained from IMGT [16], type IIS restriction enzymes that exhibit minimal recognition of mouse antibody germline sequences were identified as ApaLI, BsiWI, NheI, SgrDI, and SalI. The number of cleavage sites corresponding to each selected enzyme within the mouse antibody repertoire is summarized in Supplementary Table 2. To facilitate subcloning of antibody Fab libraries from phage to yeast vectors through digestion and ligation, we propose introducing these restriction sites into the SP regions and constant regions in the vectors, spanning the Vκ and VH regions.

Superiority of yeast bicistronic vectors with 2A peptides over those with internal ribosome entry site elements in an antibody display/secretion system

For antibody Fab display, both unidirectional and bidirectional promoters can be utilized to achieve efficient antibody expression. Traditionally, bicistronic vectors have been widely employed in Fab phage display. For S. cerevisiae, various 2A peptides and IRES elements have been tested for simultaneous expression of two genes from a single transcript, with particular success for 2A peptides [30]. Despite these findings, the influence of bicistronic translational elements on antibody expression for efficient Fab yeast display remains ambiguous. In this study, we explored the use of 2A peptides and IRES sequences to express yeast cell wall–anchored Fab under the control of a unidirectional promoter.

The GAL1/GAL10 bidirectional promoter enables simultaneous expression of two or more different protein components in S. cerevisiae. In the antibody display/secretion system [31], the GAL1 promoter was placed upstream of the antibody LC, followed by a C-terminal c-myc tag for expression detection. Meanwhile, the GAL10 promoter was utilized to drive the expression of the cell wall-anchored antibody HC in the antibody display/secretion system. Downstream from the GAL10 promoter, an intact heavy chain with a “Hole” followed by a CH3-Aga2p fusion containing a “Knob” for cell wall anchoring was organized in tandem [32] (Fig. 1a). Using trastuzumab as a model antibody in yeast surface display, four commonly used 2A peptides (P2A, O2A, E2A, and ERBV-1) [33] and three IRES elements (PVY, TEV, and TMV Ω) [34] were employed to enable simultaneous expression of the two HC components (Supplementary Table 3). This setup allows IgG half-antibodies (with a hole) to be displayed on the yeast cell wall through heterodimerization with the knob CH3-Aga2p fusion (Fig. 1b). Additionally, the IgG half-antibodies can automatically assemble into hole–hole homodimers and be secreted into the medium, facilitating potential downstream characterization.

Optimization of regulatory elements for bicistronic antibody display (e.g. trastuzumab) in yeast. (a) Schematic architecture of the engineered yeast-based antibody display/secretion system. The GAL1/GAL10 bidirectional promoter drives simultaneous expression of multiple protein components, including antibody LC, HC, and the Fc-Aga2p anchor fusion protein. (b) Cartoon representation of the antibody display/secretion system depicted in (a). The secreted IgG form is illustrated in the top right. (c) Flow cytometry profiles of yeast displaying the bicistronic antibody constructs depicted in (a). Events are plotted as a scatter plot (FITC vs. PE). The diagonal shift along the antigen-binding/antibody-expression axis reflects relative binding affinity. Yeast cells expressing functional trastuzumab localize to the upper right of the cross-shaped gating boundary (quadrant Q2). Notably, Fc-Aga2p is efficiently expressed only when linked via a 2A peptide but not when expressed from an IRES-mediated bicistronic construct.

When IRES elements were employed for bicistronic expression, there was barely any expression of the LC on the yeast surface, resulting in a significant loss of antigen-binding activity in the corresponding constructs (Fig. 1c). In contrast, constructs containing any of the tested 2A peptides demonstrated expression levels and antigen-binding abilities comparable to those of a conventional Fab display vector (bdFab) (Fig. 1c). As noted in other studies [35], 2A elements displayed clear advantages over IRES elements for Fab expression and display, due to the typically low expression level of the protein located downstream of the IRES.

Unidirectional expression cassettes for Fab yeast display

For yeast display of trastuzumab using the bidirectional GAL1/GAL10 promoter, the MFAL1 SP was employed for LC expression. When used for HC expression, the KISH and EXG1 SPs outperformed the canonical AGA2-derived SP in terms of both antibody expression levels and antigen-binding activity (Fig. 2a). After overnight induction, only 20.4% of yeast cells employing the AGA2 SP were determined to be antigen positive, defined as exhibiting both surface antibody expression and specific antigen binding (quadrant Q2). In contrast, 40.1% and 37.4% of yeast cells using the KISH and EXG1 SPs for HC secretion, respectively, displayed measurable antigen binding via flow cytometry (Fig. 2c). Consequently, KISH and EXG1 SPs were further utilized for HC expression in the unidirectional Fab display (udFab).

2A peptide–driven unidirectional Fab display (e.g. trastuzumab) in yeast. (a) Schematic architecture of unidirectional (udFab) and bidirectional (bdFab) Fab display systems in yeast. Restriction enzyme cleavage sites are indicated by dashed lines, and all protein domains and regulatory elements are color-coded for distinction. (b) Cartoon representation of Fab displayed on the yeast cell surface, as described in (a). (c) Flow cytometry profiles of yeast displaying the Fab constructs shown in (a). Antigen-positive events following incubation with 1 nM fluorescent antigen are quantified in quadrant Q2 to evaluate the performance of optimized signal peptides for heavy chain (HC) secretion. Both KISH and EXG1 signal peptides exhibit efficacy at least comparable to that of the conventionally used AGA2 signal peptide. Constructs containing only the MFα1R element (udFab-M) fail to support detectable HC secretion. Efficient HC secretion and consequently robust surface Fab display are achieved only when a 2A peptide element is incorporated (udFab-MP or udFab-ME), resulting in antigen-positive events localized to Q2.

Kex1 protease (Kex1p), Kex2 protease (Kex2p), and Ste13 proteases (Ste13p) work together in the late Golgi network to cleave the tandem repeated units in the precursor of the budding yeast mating factor α, facilitating its maturation [36]. To harness this mechanism, the mating factor alpha-1 repeat motif (MFα1R) was placed between the LC and the anchored HC, either alone or in combination with 2A peptides. This arrangement allows for the processing of HC-Aga2p either by proteases in the late Golgi compartment or through ribosomal skipping, or both of them.

As depicted in Fig. 2a, the udFab system is designed for Fab display under the control of the GAL1 promoter. To achieve high tolerance to a range of N-terminal amino acids in the VHs (Supplementary Table 1) and to enhance the display level of Fab fragments, KISH and EXG1 SPs were incorporated upstream of the VH. Meanwhile, the MFAL1 SP was used for VL expression.

For seamless transfer of paired VH:VL sequences between phage and yeast libraries, we introduced a set of strategically selected restriction enzyme–cutting sites into the coding sequence of the chosen SPs and the constant regions of antibodies via silent mutations (Fig. 2a). Specifically, NheI or ApaLI sites were added at the 3′ end of the MFAL1 SP when paired with either KISH or EXG1 SPs; BsiWI was introduced at the 5′ end of Cκ region; NcoI or NheI sites were placed at the 3′ end of the KISH or EXG1 SPs; and SgrDI was inserted at the 5′ end of the CH1 region. All designed unidirectional Fab (udFab) constructs are configured to be processed as Fabs displayed on the yeast surface via covalent attachment to α-agglutinin, as illustrated in Fig. 2b.

Flow cytometry revealed that the udFab-M (unidirectional Fab display controlled by MFα1R) series demonstrated almost no light chain expression, resulting in poor antigen-binding activity (Fig. 2c). This suggested that Kex1p, Kex2p, and Ste13p were insufficient to cleave the mating factor alpha-1 repeat motif between the LC and HC for the maturation of Fab antibody fragments. However, after introducing the ERBV-1 2A peptide downstream of MFα1R, a substantial proportion of yeast cells became antigen-reactive (26.8% for KISH SP and 42.8% for EXG1 SP) (Fig. 2c). The udFab-ME-EXG1 construct displayed expression levels and antigen-binding reactivity comparable to those of bdFab-EXG1, highlighting the high-performance potential of this unidirectional architecture. Furthermore, the specificity of all the designed Fab display constructs was confirmed by incubation with an unrelated protein (Supplementary Fig. 1).

In conclusion, the udFab series employing the ERBV-1 2A peptide demonstrated strong antibody display efficiency and specific antigen specific binding activity. The engineered vector udFab-ME-EXG1 was named pESC-TRP-DD and further used for yeast Fab library construction and screening following phage display.

Mock selections using modified phage vector pComb3-DD

Following confirmation of display efficiency and specific antigen binding activity for pComb3-DD-Trastuzumab, a fully human naïve Fab phage library (1 × 1012) was spiked with small amounts of trastuzumab Fab phage carrying distinct signal sequences at an initial ratio of 0.01% for the mock selection. The spiked library underwent two rounds of enrichment using HER2-Fc. 73 clones were randomly picked from the second-round output and sequenced. The frequency of trastuzumab in the enriched output population reached 100% after two rounds of screening (data not shown). Sequencing analysis revealed that, the phagemids with ​MmAp/MalE​ and ​MalE/MalE​ SP pairs accounted for 40% and 38% of the population, respectively, both significantly higher than the ​MalE/MmAp​ pair. These results validated the optimized phage vector for high-fidelity target enrichment, with pComb3-DD-MmAp/MalE chosen for Fab phage display (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discovery of rare functional antibody achieved by Fab phage and yeast display in tandem

The mouse immune antibody phage library against antigen A, comprising 5 × 108 independent clones, was constructed and subjected to two rounds of antigen-based enrichment. Phage output titers reached 5 × 105 and 8 × 106 cfu in the first and second rounds, respectively, with a positive binding rate exceeding 60% (as determined by phage ELISA after the second round; data not shown).

To identify clones exhibiting high affinity and neutralizing activity, the enriched library was converted for yeast display. As outlined in Fig. 3a, phagemid DNA was first extracted from the enriched pool. The DNA fragment encoding both the VL and VH domains was released by double digestion with ApaLI and SgrDI, gel-purified, and ligated into the yeast display vector pESC-TRP-DD. The resulting plasmid library was electroporated into TG1 competent E. coli for amplification, re-isolated, and subjected to a second double digestion using BsiWI and NheI. The linearized vector was then ligated with a Cκ-2A-EXG1 SP cassette and electroporated into TG1, yielding a final yeast display library of 6 × 106 clones. Sanger sequencing of 60 randomly selected clones revealed that 92% (55/60) contained the correct construct. The remaining 8% (5/60) failed to incorporate the Cκ-2A- EXG1 SP cassette properly, likely due to suboptimal restriction enzyme digestion efficiency.

To enrich for high-affinity binders, the yeast display library was subjected to successive rounds of screening with sequentially reduced concentrations of antigen (from 100 to 1 nM) (Fig. 3b). Each individual yeast cell displaying a unique Fab antibody was simultaneously analyzed by 2D flow cytometry, with antibody surface expression level [detected via c-myc tag through Fluorescein (FITC) channel] plotted on the x-axis and antigen binding [detected using biotinylated or Alexa Fluor 546 labeled antigen through R-phycoerythrin (PE) channel] on the y-axis. Yeast cells exhibiting fluorescence signals above predefined thresholds in both channels were sorted, cultured, and used as input for the subsequent round of selection. Initial screening revealed that the majority of phage-enriched antibody clones were weak binders, showing minimal antigen-binding signal even at an antigen concentration of 100 nM. Following two rounds of FACS, a distinct population of clones (~2% of the total) emerged with clear, specific binding to the target antigen. After the fourth round of FACS, in which the top 15% of the yeast population was collected, ~90% of the yeast clones demonstrated robust antigen binding at a low antigen concentration of 1 nM, confirming the effectiveness of the FACS-based enrichment strategy.

For receptor blocker discovery, the yeast round 4 (R4) output library was first incubated with 1 nM antigen A-Biotin. Following wash steps, 10 nM Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated receptor was added to the yeast library to identify receptor-blocking antibodies. Yeast cells displaying blocking antibodies, which prevent receptor binding, showed fluorescence only in the FITC channel (reporting Fab surface expression) and the PE channel (reporting antigen A binding) but not in the APC channel (reporting receptor A binding). In contrast, yeast cells displaying nonblocking antibodies bound to the antigen–receptor complex also exhibited fluorescence in the APC channel, in addition to the PE and FITC signals. To our surprise, affinity-matured binders isolated from the R4 output of the tandem phage-yeast display approach were almost exclusively receptor blockers, showing no detectable APC signal in the second gating (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 3).

Therefore, 146 clones from the R4 output were randomly selected for Sanger sequencing, yielding 30 unique yeast-displayed Fab variants for monoclonal flow cytometry analysis of antigen binding and receptor-blocking activity. Six of these clones were reformatted as human IgG1 chimeras and validated by SPR and ELISA-based receptor-blocking assays. Among the characterized variants, YSD05, YSD06, YSD09, and YSD23 exhibited sub-nanomolar binding affinities (Fig. 4a–e and Table 1) and potent receptor-blocking activities, with IC50 values of 1.18, 0.74, 2.69, and 1.11 nM, respectively (Fig. 4f and Table 1), comparable to those of the benchmark (BMK). Retrospective NGS analysis revealed that YSD05, YSD06, YSD09, and YSD23 were present at extremely low frequencies in the original yeast input library, with only 1, 0, 8, and 0 sequence reads, respectively, out of 11 773 full-length VH:VL reads recovered (Table 1). In order to visualize the overall diversity of yeast input library, we also recovered 34 130 full-length VH sequences for CDRH3 (Kabat numbering) abundance analysis. Bioinformatic characterization indicated that each functional antibody, defined by exact CDRH3 sequence identity to the selected clone, accounted for ≤0.1% of the original library (Fig. 3d). Nevertheless, these four variants, particularly YSD06, underwent substantial enrichment over the course of four selection rounds.

All four selected chimeric IgG1 antibodies exhibited drug-like properties, with isoelectric points (pI) ranging from 6.44 to 8.24 and thermal onset temperatures (Tonset) between 55.87°C and 65.16°C (Table 2). The aggregation propensity of monoclonal antibodies is often driven by hydrophobic interactions, particularly the hydrophobicity of CDR residues. Moreover, extremes in Fv charge and high hydrophobicity are also associated with a short pharmacokinetic (PK) half-life [37]. Therefore, we performed developability profiling of the four lead candidates using HIC and HAC. All the chimeric antibodies displayed shorter retention times on the heparin column compared to adalimumab. Similarly, their HIC retention times were comparable to that of trastuzumab. And for reference, ipilimumab exhibited a relative HIC retention time of 1.24 (normalized to trastuzumab = 1.00, data not shown). Notably, YSD05, YSD06, and YSD23 showed even shorter HIC retention times than trastuzumab, indicating greater surface hydrophilicity. Consistent with these biophysical profiles, all four lead candidates demonstrated favorable preliminary stability under accelerated stress conditions (40°C for 2 weeks) in both PBS and acetate buffer, with <2% loss of monomeric purity as assessed by SEC. Polyspecificity was further evaluated by ELISA using plates coated with soluble cytosolic proteins from HEK293, ssDNA, and insulin. All four candidates exhibited low polyspecificity, with specificity profiles comparable to that of bevacizumab (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Collectively, the integration of Fab phage and yeast display via our library transfer system enabled the isolation of rare antigen-specific Fabs with high affinity and potent functionality, which would likely have been missed using either platform alone.

Discussions

Phage display and yeast surface display are two of the most widely used techniques for high-throughput antibody discovery and engineering. The ease of cloning facilitates the construction of large antibody phage libraries, with sizes reaching up to 1011. However, phage display often encounters challenges, such as amplification bias due to codon preferences and concerns related to developability stemming from the prokaryotic nature of the screening system [28]. In contrast, yeast surface display and mammalian display benefit from eukaryotic protein expression machinery and FACS-based quantitative measurements of antibody biophysical properties, making them naturally compatible with phage display.

Previous research has demonstrated the advantages of integrating phage and yeast/mammalian display techniques for antibody discovery and engineering [1, 5, 38, 39]. Thus far, most integrated approaches have focused on scFv or sdAb libraries, where the pairing of VH and VL chains is not a significant issue when transferring from phage to yeast/mammalian vectors. However, there are reports showing a loss of affinity and activity when scFv is converted into Fab or IgG [8, 40, 41], limiting their general applicability in real-world antibody discovery and optimization tasks. While randomizing selected VHs and VLs from phage outputs to construct yeast/mammalian antibody libraries is technically feasible, it can compromise the outcomes achieved through phage bio-panning. Moreover, the development of single B-cell technologies has enabled the construction of natively paired antibody libraries, which display higher sensitivity and specificity compared to conventional randomized libraries [42, 43]. These advancements underscore the necessity for library transfers between different display vectors while preserving the original pairing of heavy and light chains.

The conventional shuttle strategy between two display systems relied on PCR-based methods [38, 44], which failed to preserve native antibody pairing and achieve seamless system interoperability. To address these limitations, three critical considerations were implemented: (i) incorporation of restriction sites for inter-system transfer; (ii) maintenance of intact amino acid sequences without deletions or insertions during restriction–ligation processes; and (iii) preservation of identical transcriptional orientations for VH and VL across both systems. In antibody display platforms, yeast systems predominantly employ bidirectional promoter-based vectors, whereas phage systems typically utilize bicistronic expression vectors. Previously, Karuppiah et al. [45] designed a bidirectional Fab-displaying phagemid vector, utilizing a head-to-head configuration of the tac promoter and tacII promoter, which allows for independent transcription initiation of antibody heavy and light chains. Using this vector, they constructed an antibody phage library and successfully isolated CD20-specific antibodies through cell-based bio-panning. We adopted this strategy and experimentally confirmed the efficient display of the trastuzumab Fab. However, during iterative mock screening, phage clones with deletions in the bidirectional promoter element were progressively enriched. We hypothesized that long palindromic sequences adjacent to the promoter regions induced phagemid instability during bacterial replication, leading to the preferential elimination of these elements. Therefore, bicistronic phage vectors compatible with yeast display were developed and employed for Fab display throughout our phage display campaigns in this study.

The integration strategy of phage and yeast display we presented was employed for screening neutralizing antibodies, which are typically present at low abundance, as this class of antibodies generally requires both high affinity and potent blocking activity. Following multiple rounds of phage enrichment, antibody-coding fragments were extracted and seamlessly transferred into yeast display vectors for functional antibody screening. Although we employed restriction enzymes that exhibit minimal recognition of mouse antibody germline sequences, some degree of diversity loss during library transfer from phage to yeast vectors is likely unavoidable. In this study, a direct quantitative comparison of diversity between the pretransfer phage output and the post-transfer yeast input could not be performed due to the loss of the original phage output sample. Nevertheless, despite this inevitable loss of diversity during library transfer, the tandem screening strategy remains a powerful approach for rescuing low-abundance, high-value Fab antibodies from immune repertoires. It should be noted that, although the examples presented in this study focus on antibody discovery from immunized mouse strains, we have also successfully applied this approach to antibody discovery from immunized libraries derived from fully human antibody mice, as well as naïve and semi-synthetic phage libraries, with only minor modifications to the choice of restriction enzymes and SPs to minimize cleavage within germline-encoded regions.

Membrane proteins are sometimes conformationally plastic; for example, integrin heterodimers exist in multiple structural states that are notoriously hard to recapitulate using purified recombinant proteins [46, 47]. Historically, antibody discovery campaigns targeting such conformationally dynamic membrane proteins have been challenging. Panning phage or yeast display libraries against native membrane proteins presented on the surface of mammalian cells has thus represented one of the few viable strategies. However, recent advances in the facile production of nanodisks and virus-like particles, which preserve native protein conformations, now enable more physiologically relevant antigen presentation. In this context, the integration of phage and yeast display is well positioned to enrich for rare, high-potency antibodies with desired functional properties.
