Serial mediation of perceived stress and depression in the relationship between childhood trauma and mobile phone addiction among undergraduate nursing students in China: a cross-sectional questionnaire survey
Authors: Hongna Li, Xiuhuan Wang, Wenjin Chen, Na Li, Jinghui Tong, Jiao Fang, Haitao Wen, Jue Wang, Chunni Heng, Xiujuan Feng, Meifang Wang, Qing Zhao, Wenkai Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses are critical front-line members of the healthcare workforce in China. Investigating mobile phone addiction (MPA) among nursing undergraduates is essential, as it may hinder their professional development and exacerbate the current nursing shortage. Understanding this issue is crucial for safeguarding future healthcare workforce sustainability. Therefore, it is necessary to examine the contributing factors of MPA and clarify their interrelationships.ObjectivesThis study aims to investigate the serial multiple mediating effect of perceived stress and depression in the relationship between childhood trauma to MPA among undergraduate nursing students.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 1,249 undergraduate nursing students in Xi’an, Shaanxi Province, China, between November 2023 and April 2024, using a multistage sampling approach. Validated scales were used: the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ), Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), and Mobile Phone Addiction Tendency Scale (MPATS). The serial mediation was analyzed using the SPSS PROCESS macro by Hayes. The statistical analysis included descriptive analysis, independent samples t-tests, Pearson correlation analysis, and bootstrap methodology.ResultsMPA was positively correlated with childhood trauma (r = 0.237, p < 0.001), and was positively correlated with perceived stress (r = 0.391, p < 0.001) and depression (r = 0.337, p < 0.001). In the model of childhood trauma → perceived stress → depression → MPA, the overall mediated effect was 0.106 (95%Cl: 0.082– 0.131), and the mediating effect of perceived stress was 0.065 (95%Cl: 0.047–0.083), accounting for 38.92% of total effect. The mediating effect of depression was 0.028 (95%Cl: 0.011–0.046), accounting for 16.77% of total effect. The serial mediating effect of perceived stress and depression was 0.013 (95%Cl: 0.005–0.023), accounting for 7.78% of total effect.ConclusionsChildhood trauma influences MPA through the chain-mediating effects of perceived stress and depression in undergraduate nursing students. These findings demonstrate that early detection of trauma-exposed nursing students, combined with evidence-based stress reduction and emotion regulation strategies, could reduce MPA risk in this population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Mobile phone addiction, Childhood trauma, Perceived stress, Depression, Serial mediation, Nursing students

Introduction

Nurses are critical front-line members of the healthcare workforce in China, with their contributions recognized as essential for addressing public health needs and improving health outcomes. They play significant roles in various domains, including clinical nursing practice, health education, and global health governance, contributing to disease prevention and the facilitation of patient care. As of 2023, there are over 5.63 million registered nurses in the country, over 80% of whom held college diplomas or higher qualifications [1]. With the accelerating aging population, the shortage of nursing professionals has become increasingly prominent, emerging as a critical challenge in healthcare in China [2]. Undergraduate nursing students, as the future backbone of the nursing workforce, exhibit mental health conditions that critically impact both their clinical skill development and long-term career sustainability [3–5].

Recent studies have identified mobile phone addiction (MPA) as a significant risk factor among various mental health determinants for nursing students. Studies indicate that the prevalence of MPA among nursing students ranges from 19.0% to 72.0%, with higher rates reported in China compared to other countries [6–8]. Key characteristics of MPA include excessive device usage, impaired self-control, and significant psychosocial dysfunction. While smartphones can facilitate learning and enhance clinical skills among nursing students, excessive use leading to MPA imposes multifaceted adverse effects. MPA has been shown to significantly impair academic performance and professional condct [9], and is associated with psychological issues including anxiety, depression, and reduced self-esteem [10, 11]. At the physiological level, MPA can contribute to sleep deprivation, poor concentration, headaches, and mental fatigue [12, 13]. These psychophysiological impairments may ultimately exert sustained negative impacts on academic achievement. Notably, nursing interns are particularly vulnerable to MPA due to the high-intensity academic pressures inherent in medical education, including heavy coursework and rigorous professional standards [14]. Therefore, investigating MPA’s impact on this population is crucial for fostering healthier educational environments and promoting essential clinical competencies. Research by Tang et al. [15] further demonstrates that childhood trauma is a significant risk factor for the development of MPA.

Childhood trauma, including emotional, physical, or sexual abuse and neglect, persists as a global public health priority, particularly within low- and middle-income countries [16]. Exposure to adverse childhood experiences elevates vulnerability to multiple psychopathologies, particularly anxiety disorders, depressive symptoms, and externalizing behaviors such as aggression and impulsivity [17, 18]. Growing evidence highlights the role of childhood trauma in predisposing individuals to MPA, supported by meta-analytic findings on dose-response relationships between childhood adversities and digital overuse behaviors [13, 15, 19]. Specifically, emotional abuse and neglect, which represent two prevalent forms of childhood trauma, disrupt the maturation of prefrontal-limbic circuitry. This impairment subsequently compromises emotion regulation capacity and promotes reliance on maladaptive coping mechanisms, such as compulsive smartphone use [19]. A synthesis of 19 cross-cultural studies (N = 21,398 adolescents) further substantiates this association, revealing a strong positive association between childhood trauma exposure and internet addiction phenotypes [15]. The findings demonstrate that childhood trauma significantly increases individuals’ susceptibility to MPA. Consequently, early intervention targeting childhood trauma should be considered a crucial strategy for preventing MPA and related behavioral issues [20–22].

Beyond direct associations, childhood trauma may also contribute to MPA through various psychosocial mechanisms. One critical mediator is perceived stress [23], which refers to the psychological and physiological strain individuals experience when facing demands that exceed their coping resources or threaten well-being [24]. A study of 554 Chinese nursing students revealed significantly higher psychological distress than the national average, primarily due to discipline-specific pressures including heavy academic workloads, examination demands, familial expectations, and professional identity development, which surpass those experienced by non-nursing peers [25]. To cope with life stressors, some students may resort to passive coping strategies, such as excessive internet use, as a form of escapism from their problems [26]. Numerous studies have demonstrated a significant association between perceived stress and various internet-related problematic behaviors [26, 27]. Individuals exposed to childhood trauma exhibit heightened sensitivity to stress which amplifies their perception of stressors as threats [20, 28, 29]. This altered stress appraisal further impairs their coping strategies and ultimately compromises behavioral and emotional regulation.

Furthermore, depressive symptoms may also constitute a significant mediating pathway linking childhood trauma to MPA. Specifically, undergraduate nursing students are at an elevated risk of depression due to their high-stress academic environment [14, 30]. Evidence suggests a high prevalence of depression among nursing students, with rates approximating 30% in certain populations [31], adversely affecting academic performance, quality of life, and readiness for professional practice [32]. Cross-cultural studies conducted in diverse geographical contexts, including the United States, South Korea and China, have consistently demonstrated a strong positive correlation between depressive symptoms and MPA [33–35]. Childhood trauma constitutes a significant risk factor for depressive symptoms. It impairs an individual’s emotional regulation capacity, diminishes coping ability under stressful situations among university students, and contributes to the accumulation of emotional distress, thereby significantly increasing the risk of developing depressive symptoms [36]. Within the high-pressure context of nursing education, depressive symptoms can promote maladaptive coping strategies such as emotional avoidance and social withdrawal, thereby increasing reliance on mobile phones [8, 30].

In summary, childhood trauma heightens an individual’s sensitivity to stress [37]. Nursing students frequently encounter multiple stressors that can activate the Hypothalamic Pituitary Adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to increased cortisol secretion, chronic accumulation of such responses may exacerbate the risk of depression [38]. Moreover, individuals with a history of childhood trauma are more susceptible to developing depression and anxiety disorders compared to the general population [39] Those experiencing depressive states often turn to excessive smartphone use as a means of escape and temporary emotional relief. However, such maladaptive coping strategies tend to amplify psychological distress and reinforce mobile phone addiction patterns [40]. Although existing studies have separately demonstrated interrelationships among childhood trauma, perceived stress, depressive symptoms, and MPA, the specific psychological mechanisms and pathways connecting these variables within undergraduate nursing students remain underexplored and require further systematic empirical investigation.

Theoretical framework

To address this gap, the present study is guided by the Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model [23, 41]. This comprehensive theoretical framework posits that the development of specific Internet-use disorders is driven by the interaction between predisposing vulnerabilities (e.g., childhood trauma) and mediating affective-cognitive components (e.g., perceived stress and depression). Within this conceptual framework, childhood trauma is positioned as a critical distal vulnerability factor that amplifies an individual’s sensitivity to stress [20, 42]. This heightened perceived stress, in turn, can trigger and exacerbate depressive symptoms [43]. Both heightened stress and depression are proximal mechanisms that may lead to maladaptive coping behaviors, such as excessive smartphone use, to regulate negative emotions, a core tenet of the compensatory internet use theory [44]. Mitigating mobile phone dependency risk among undergraduate nursing students, a population facing uniquely intense academic and clinical pressures, is crucial for safeguarding their academic performance and future professional well-being.

Based on the above finding, this study focuses on undergraduate nursing students, examining childhood trauma as the independent variable, perceived stress and depression as sequential mediators, and MPA as the dependent variable. The research objectives include: (1) elucidating the childhood trauma-MPA association, and (2) determining the independent and serial mediating effects of perceived stress and depression, thereby establishing a theoretical and empirical basis for developing targeted interventions. Based on the I-PACE model and existing literature, we propose the following hypotheses: H1): Childhood trauma is positively correlated with MPA. H2): Perceived stress mediates the association between childhood trauma and MPA. H3): Depression mediates the association between childhood trauma and MPA. H4): Perceived stress and depression sequentially mediate the relationship between childhood trauma and MPA. The theoretical model is presented in Fig. 1.

Method

Study design and participants

This study adopted a descriptive-correlational research design with a cross-sectional approach. The survey was conducted among undergraduate nursing students in Xi’an, Shaanxi Province, China, between November 2023 and April 2024. A multistage sampling method was applied: all ten accredited undergraduate nursing institutions in the region were numerically coded, and five institutions were randomly selected using a computer-generated random number sequence. To examine the hypothesized serial mediation model, path analysis was performed using PROCESS macro (Model 6) for SPSS.

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) full-time undergraduate nursing students currently enrolled in their 1st to 4th academic year of a standard four-year Bachelor of Nursing program; (2) able to understand and complete the questionnaire independently; (3) voluntarily agreed to participate and provided written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were: (1) students on a leave of absence (e.g., for medical, personal, or military reasons) during the data collection period; (2) students diagnosed with severe medical conditions or psychiatric disorders (including schizophrenia and bipolar disorder) that might compromise the validity of psychological measurements; (3) students who were participating in other psychological intervention studies concurrently, which could potentially confound the results; (4) students with incomplete questionnaires or invalid responses (e.g., excessively short completion time, patterned answering).

The sample size for this study was calculated using the formula N = Z2 × [P × (1-P)] / E2 [45] where N represents the required sample size, Z denotes the Z-statistic corresponding to the desired confidence level, P signifies the estimated proportion of the outcome of interest in the population, and E indicates the acceptable margin of error. In this study, a 95% confidence interval was adopted, resulting in Z = 1.96, while a margin of error of 4.0% was deemed acceptable. Based on these parameters, the minimum sample size required for this study was estimated. A previous study reported a MPA prevalence rate of 53.84% among nursing students [46], which was utilized as the value for P. Applying the formula, the calculated sample size was 597 participants. To account for potential invalid responses or missing data, an additional 20% was added to the sample size, resulting in a final sample size of 720 participants.

Following standardized training, the survey was administered to eligible undergraduate nursing students via the Questionnaire Star platform. To ensure data integrity, stringent quality control measures were implemented: (1) each IP address was limited to one submission to eliminate duplicate entries; (2) the survey completion duration was set at approximately 15–20 min; and (3) invalid responses, such as those with incomplete answers, uniform responses across all items, or completion times below 5 min, were systematically excluded. from the initial pool of 1325 collected questionnaires, 1249 met the quality criteria, yielding a valid response rate of 94.26%.

Measurements

Demographic characteristics

A demographics questionnaire was used to collect participants’ characteristics, including age, gender, grade, family structure (e.g., only child, single-parent family), and residence type.

Measurements of childhood trauma

The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ), originally developed by Bernstein and colleagues (1998) [47], is a psychometrically validated 28-item self-report instrument for retrospective assessment of childhood maltreatment. The Chinese adaptation has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties in prior validation studies [48]. This multidimensional measure comprises 25 clinically scored items organized into five subscales: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect, supplemented by three validity screening items. Utilizing a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = never to 5 = always), aggregate scores range from 25 to 125, with elevated scores indicating greater trauma severity. Internal consistency analysis revealed acceptable scale-level reliability (α = 0.73) and strong subscale reliability coefficients ranging from 0.78 to 0.87. Split-half reliability coefficients (0.81–0.89) further confirmed measurement stability. In the current study, the calculated α was 0.91, indicating satisfactory reliability.

Measurement of perceived stress

Perceived stress was operationalized using the 14-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), originally developed by Cohen and colleagues [24] and subsequently validated in Chinese populations through Yang’s cultural adaptation [49]. This multidimensional instrument comprises two theoretically derived subscales: (1) perceived loss of control (7 items) and (2) chronic tension (7 items), each employing a 5-point Likert-type response format (1 = never to 5 = always). Total scores range from 14 to 70, with elevated scores reflecting heightened subjective stress appraisal. The Chinese adaptation has demonstrated robust psychometric properties in previous psychometric evaluations [50]. In the current sample, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.75), with subscale coefficients ranging from 0.72 to 0.78. In the current study, the calculated α was 0.73, indicating satisfactory reliability.

Measurement of depression

Depressive symptomatology was evaluated using the 20-item Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), originally developed by Zung (1965) [51]. This multidimensional instrument systematically assesses four core domains of depression: affective (e.g., hopelessness), cognitive (e.g., indecisiveness), behavioral (e.g., social withdrawal), and psychophysiological manifestations (e.g., fatigue). Each item employs a 4-point Likert-type scale (1 = “a little of the time” to 4 = “most of the time”), with raw total scores (range: 20–80) converted to standardized scores via linear transformation (raw score × 1.25) to facilitate cross-study comparisons and clinical interpretation. Extensive validation studies in Chinese populations have established robust psychometric properties [52, 53]. In the current sample, the scale demonstrated excellent reliability (α = 0.84; test-retest reliability r = 0.81). In the current study, the calculated α was 0.86, indicating satisfactory reliability.

Measurement of mobile phone addiction

The Mobile Phone Addiction Tendency Scale (MPATS) [54], a validated 16-item psychometric instrument, was employed to operationalize addiction propensity. This scale conceptualizes problematic smartphone use through four behavioral domains: (1) psychological salience; (2) time expenditure; (3) social impairment; and (4) escapism. Responses are recorded on a 5-point Likert continuum (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), yielding total scores between 16 and 80. Consistent with diagnostic thresholds established in cross-cultural validations [55], scores ≥ 48 denote clinically significant addiction risk. In the current cohort, measurement reliability exceeded established benchmarks (α = 0.93). In the current study, the calculated α was 0.93, indicating satisfactory reliability.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp) and the PROCESS macro (version 4.0). In this study, common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. The first factor accounted for 22.64% of the total variance, which falls below the recommended threshold of 40% [56]. This result indicates that common method bias is unlikely to be a substantial concern in the present dataset. Demographic characteristics between groups were compared using independent samples t-tests. Bivariate correlations were computed to examine associations among childhood trauma (CTQ total score), perceived stress, depression symptoms, and MPA. A serial mediation analysis was implemented through Model 6 of the PROCESS macro to test the hypothesized chained mediating effects of perceived stress and depression in the CTQ-MPA association, controlling for gender, age, and academic year. The statistical significance of mediation effects was evaluated using 10,000 bootstrap resamples with bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals. All statistical tests employed a two-tailed α level of 0.05 for significance determination.

Discussion

This research aimed to examine the relationship between childhood trauma and MPA among nursing students, with a specific focus on the mediating roles of perceived stress and depressive symptoms. By clarifying these mechanisms, the study provides insights that can inform targeted interventions to help nursing students regulate digital device usage. The findings supported four central hypotheses: (1) Childhood trauma severity exhibited a graded association with MPA risk. (H1); (2) Perceived stress played a mediating role in the relationship between childhood trauma and MPA (H2); (3) Depression served as a significant mediator in the relationship between childhood trauma and MPA (H3); and (4) A sequential mediation pathway was established, in which trauma-induced stress triggers emotion dysregulation, ultimately leading to MPA (H4).

Further analysis using bootstrap mediation methods revealed three significant indirect pathways. The indirect effect through perceived stress alone (childhood trauma → perceived stress → MPA) accounted for 38.92% of the total effect. The indirect effect through depression alone (childhood trauma → depression → MPA) accounted for 16.77%. Finally, the serial indirect pathway through both perceived stress and then depression (childhood trauma → perceived stress → depression → MPA) accounted for 7.78% of the total effect. This result underscores the pivotal role of perceived stress as the most influential mediator between childhood trauma and MPA. In conclusion, our results indicated that childhood trauma indirectly affects MPA via a chain-mediating pathway involving perceived stress and subsequent depression.

Pathway H1 demonstrated that childhood trauma significantly predicts MPA, a finding consistently replicated across multiple studies [15, 19]. A recent meta-analysis of 19 studies (N = 21,398 adolescents) confirmed robust trauma-internet addiction associations [15], extending to MPA through shared neural mechanisms in reward processing [19]. These results were consistent with the theoretical framework of the Interaction of I-PACE model [23, 41]. The I-PACE model conceptualizes addiction as driven by two interrelated components: (a) predispositional vulnerabilities, such as childhood trauma, which reflect enduring risk factors rooted in personal history or biological makeup; and (b) proximal affective-cognitive mechanisms, including attention biases toward mobile devices and the use of phones to escape stress, which represent immediate cognitive-emotional processes that directly initiate addictive behaviors. These two factors reinforce each other: personal vulnerabilities make people more likely to rely on phones for emotional relief, while frequent phone use further weakens their ability to cope with stress, creating a self-sustaining cycle of addiction [19, 28, 41]. Specifically, neglect and abuse weaken family relationships, leading to emotional emptiness that teenagers try to overcome by excessive internet use - a behavior pattern that supports the social compensation theory [57]. In contrast to students in non-clinical disciplines, nursing undergraduates encounter persistent profession-specific stressors including rotational shift work (particularly night shifts) and academically demanding training regimens. This chronic stress exposure precipitates HPA axis dysregulation, thereby amplifying childhood trauma’s etiological contribution to MPA through neuroendocrine-psychological cascades [58].

Supporting H2, perceived stress mediates the relationship between childhood trauma and MPA. Research indicates that individuals who experienced childhood trauma are likely to perceive their environment as more threatening, which exacerbates their stress response and diminishes their coping abilities [59]. In the nursing profession, job-related stressors can interact with an individual’s perception of stress, resulting in adverse effects on both mental and physical health. The high demands of nursing work, particularly in intensive care settings, often amplify perceived stress levels, contributing to burnout and diminished quality of life [60]. The relationship between childhood trauma and perceived stress in nursing can be explained through several psychological frameworks. The stress sensitization framework posits that childhood trauma triggers HPA axis dysregulation [58, 61, 62], leading to chronic hypervigilance and amplified stress perception, such as those prevalent in nursing environments [28]. This heightened stress sensitivity drives compensatory smartphone overuse for mood regulation, consistent with compensatory internet use theory [44]. Additionally, the cumulative advantage/disadvantage theory suggests that individuals who have experienced childhood trauma may face a series of cumulative disadvantages later in life, further exacerbating their perceived stress levels [63]. Furthermore, research demonstrates that the emotional and psychological responses elicited by stressful situations in nursing may be influenced by the trauma experienced in childhood. Nurses with a history of trauma are likely to perceive stress as more overwhelming and may resort to negative coping strategies, leading to a vicious cycle of increased stress and mental health issues over time [63]. A large-scale study indicates that individuals experiencing high stress levels show greater susceptibility to mobile gaming addiction [64]. Therefore, addressing perceived stress through effective coping strategies and supportive work environments is essential for improving nurses’ mental health and overall well-being.

Supporting H3, depression mediated the association between childhood trauma and MPA, consistent with prior research [33–35, 65, 66]. Nursing students are highly susceptible to depression, and those experiencing depressive symptoms may turn to smartphones as a means of escape. However, this dependency can lead to a cycle of isolation and addiction, ultimately impairing academic performance and overall mental health [40]. Mechanistically, depression exacerbate MPA through maladaptive cognitive-emotional pathways, including​ruminative thinking [67], emotional dysregulation [68], and behavioral disinhibition [69]. These changes make it harder to cope in healthy ways, leading people to depend on smartphones for quick (but not lasting) emotional relief. Childhood trauma represents a well-established risk factor for depression. Molecular studies suggest that early adverse experiences disrupt the synthesis and release of key neurotransmitters including serotonin and dopamine, which in turn impairs emotional regulation [70]. Consistent with this mechanism, Felitti et [39] reported a four-fold increase in the prevalence of depression among individuals with childhood trauma compared to those without such experiences. This finding aligns with the I-PACE model, which posits that personal characteristics interact with affective responses to promote MPA [71]. Negative emotions, such as those stemming from stress or academic pressures, can deplete cognitive resources and lead to addictive behaviors as individuals attempt to cope with these feelings through excessive smartphone use.

The study found that perceived stress and depressive symptoms sequentially mediated the relationship between childhood trauma and mobile phone addiction among nursing undergraduates, confirming Hypothesis 4. This suggests that childhood trauma initially leads to increased perceived stress, which is subsequently associated with exacerbated depressive symptoms, ultimately resulting in heightened mobile phone dependence. Childhood trauma adversely affects an individual’s coping mechanisms, leading to a deficit in effective coping strategies and diminished capacity to manage stressful situations, thereby heightening susceptibility to perceived stress [20, 29, 37, 72]. Furthermore, this study identified a positive correlation between perceived stress and depressive symptoms, which aligns with the loss of control theory model [73]. This model posits that the onset of depressive symptoms is associated with perceived and actual loss of control over one’s circumstances. Consistent with this mechanism, higher levels of perceived stress among nursing students were associated with an increased likelihood of developing depressive symptoms. Subsequently, individuals with depression are more likely to engage in problematic smartphone use as a maladaptive coping mechanism [74, 75]. Although previous studies have suggested that mobile phones can offer social support [76]. compulsive use tends to exacerbate psychological outcomes. It reduces face-to-face communication, intensifies social appearance anxiety and feelings of isolation [77, 78], and reinforces negative emotional states and maladaptive coping strategies. Consequently, among nursing students, smartphone addiction appears to function more as a risk factor than a relief mechanism, intensifying pre-existing depression and contributing to poorer educational and health outcomes.

These findings carry significant implications for understanding and addressing MPA among nursing students with childhood trauma histories. Firstly, nursing educators and administrative institutions should implement routine, voluntary, and anonymous psychological screening programs using validated tools (e.g., CTQ, PSS) to identify students with a history of childhood trauma or experiencing high levels of stress. Subsequently, targeted support should be provided, including: (1) facilitating easy access to counseling and psychological services; (2) offering stress management such as mindfulness-based therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy [79, 80] and resilience training workshops; and (3) establishing a supportive academic environment that reduces stigma. These initiatives should equip students with healthy psychological coping strategies, guiding them to objectively appraise stress-inducing events with a positive mindset during their academic pursuits, ultimately reducing mobile phone dependency among nursing students.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was restricted to universities in Xi’an, Shaanxi Province, and the limited sample size may constrain generalizability and introduce regional selection bias. Future research should expand geographic diversity and recruit larger cohorts to improve external validity. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to establish temporal relationships between variables. Third, while we identified psychological resilience as a moderator between perceived stress and depression, other potential moderators (e.g., social support, coping styles) were not examined. Future studies should incorporate these variables to refine the stress-MPA pathway. Finally, reliance on online self-reported data may inflate recall and social desirability biases, potentially compromising measurement accuracy. Triangulating self-reports with objective usage metrics (e.g., smartphone screen time logs) could enhance data reliability.

Conclusion

This study is the first to employ a serial mediation model to examine the chained mediating effects of perceived stress and depression in the relationship between childhood trauma and MPA among Chinese undergraduate nursing students. The findings demonstrate that perceived stress and depression serve as serial mediators between childhood trauma and MPA. Specifically, childhood trauma heightens individuals’ sensitivity to stress, and persistently elevated stress levels tend to trigger depressive symptoms, ultimately increasing the risk of excessive smartphone use among nursing undergraduates. Therefore, trauma screening to identify high-risk groups, reducing perceived stress, and actively managing emotions hold significant potential for mitigating MPA risk in undergraduate nursing students.
