Seasonal and monthly stroke incidence in relation to weather variables: a preliminary single-centre epidemiologic study
Authors: Andrzej Maciejczak, Małgorzata Smutek, Andżelina Wolan-Nieroda, Agnieszka Guzik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis single-centre epidemiologic study investigated seasonal/monthly stroke incidence and stroke-related mortality in relation to weather variables, gender, age, as well as stroke subtype and stroke aetiology. The study involved 638 patients hospitalised due to stroke at the Neurology Ward with a stroke unit at the Regional Hospital in Poland, during one complete calendar year. The data collected included the date of admission to the ward, year of birth, sex, type and subtype of stroke relative to the aetiology and location within different arteries as well as mortality. The data related to wind speed, atmospheric pressure, and temperature were acquired from the archive at the weather station in Tarnów, Poland. Majority of the patients in the study group were aged 66–85 years (57.2%), and there were more women than men. Ischaemic stroke was significantly more common than haemorrhagic stroke (87 vs 13%). Large artery atherosclerosis was the predominant cause of stroke, particularly in June and October, and total anterior circulation stroke (TACS) was the most common type, particularly in January and December. The analysis showed that stroke type and aetiology, as well as weather variables such as temperature and pressure, were associated with stroke incidence and mortality rates. The findings suggest that both high and low temperatures may contribute to an increased stroke incidence, as significant differences in the number of stroke cases are observed during periods of extreme temperatures. Therefore, increased vigilance is necessary during periods of both hot and cold weather. Further research is needed to better understand these relationships and develop effective prevention strategies.Subject terms: Health care, Neurology

Introduction

Being one of the major causes of morbidity, mortality and long-term disability, stroke is a very important problem, not only from medical but also from social viewpoint. Given the need for ongoing care and rehabilitation of the patients, this is a medical condition generating very high costs of treatment1.

It has been known for a long time that there are significant diurnal and seasonal differences in numerous vital functions of the human body2. Research has also documented the associations between environmental factors, including climate change, and human health, particularly with respect to cerebrovascular diseases3. It was shown that in various geographical regions of the world, meteorological variables are associated with the specific stroke subtypes4–9. Some researchers have focused mainly on the relationship between the mean temperature and the incidence of stroke10–15. Others suggest that atmospheric pressure impacts heart rate variability and, ultimately, cardiovascular events16. It has also been reported that humidity and wind speed are associated with vascular incidents17. Furthermore, winter season has been pointed out as a potential factor corresponding to higher stroke incidence4–8. These observations, however, are not consistent, as in the related literature we can also encounter reports where no seasonal changes in stroke incidence were identified, and no significant associations were found between weather variables and type of stroke18.

In Poland this issue has been largely overlooked. Up until now, researchers have only published minimum of data related to seasonal variation in the incidence of various nervous system diseases, including stroke. Notably, the information discussed in Polish articles is mainly of illustrative nature and the authors present general overview of seasonal variation in the incidence of such nervous system disorders as multiple sclerosis, migraines, epilepsy or cerebrovascular diseases19. This observation provided motivation for our research, and the present article is the first scientific report related to this subject matter.

The aim of the research, designed as a single-centre epidemiologic study, was to investigate seasonal and monthly stroke incidence and stroke-related deaths in relation to weather variables, gender, age, as well as stroke subtype and stroke aetiology.

Methods

Study setting and geographic area

The study was conducted in Tarnów, Małopolskie Region in southern Poland, between January 2020 and December 2020. Tarnów occupies an area of 72.38 km2, and has a population of over 100,000. It is one of the warmest places in Poland. This is connected with its specific location—the town experiences warm gusts of mountain wind (halny) and is sheltered from cold draughts by ranges of hills. Tarnów and the nearby area has the longest growing period in Poland, which is approximately 225–230 days per year (this is the number of days with an average 24-h temperature above 5 ℃). The following meteorological seasons in Poland were defined for the study: spring (1 March to 31 May), summer (1 June to 31 August), autumn (1 September to 30 November) and winter (1 December to 28 February).

Participants

The screening examination took into account all individuals with haemorrhagic or ischaemic stroke who were hospitalised at a stroke unit of the Neurology Ward at St. Luke Regional Hospital in Tarnów. The current study involved all the male and female patients who experienced ischaemic stroke or haemorrhagic stroke, and the related evidence acquired using brain computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) matched the neurological deficits. The final diagnosis was confirmed by a neurologist. All the patients diagnosed with transient ischaemic attack (TIA) or traumatic brain haemorrhage were excluded. The study involved 638 patients hospitalised due to stroke at Neurology Ward with a stroke unit at St. Luke Regional Hospital in Tarnów, during one complete calendar year (from 1 Jan. 2020 to 31 Dec. 2020). The study group comprised 352 (55.2%) women and 286 (44.8%) men. The patients’ mean age was 75.3 ± 12.3 years (ranging from 27 to 98 years).

The experimental protocol was approved by the local Bioethics Commission at the University of Rzeszow (approval no. 2018/01/07). The study design complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. All the study participants gave their informed consent in writing.

Procedures

The data collected included the date of admission to the ward, year of birth, sex, as well as type of the stroke (ischaemic/haemorrhagic), subtype of stroke relative to the location and aetiology, as well as death rate. Our research protocol took into account the differences associated with the location of ischaemic strokes within different arteries, as recommended in the literature20. The following subtypes were distinguished relative to the location of the circulation stroke: total anterior circulation stroke (TACS), partial anterior circulation stroke (PACS), lacunar stroke (LACS), posterior circulation stroke (POCS), as well as uncertain classification. The stroke subtypes according to the aetiology include large artery atherosclerosis, small artery occlusion, cardioembolism, stroke of undetermined cause, as well as stroke of other determined cause. The data related to wind speed, atmospheric pressure, and temperature were acquired from the archive at the weather station in Tarnów21. Diurnal temperature variation, diurnal atmospheric pressure variation and wind speed were determined for each day of the relevant year.

Data analyses

The data analysis aimed to determine the relationship between various factors (e.g., wind speed, patient age, temperature, etc.) and mortality as well as stroke type and aetiology. Chi-square tests with Yates’ correction were employed to assess the statistical significance, while Cramer’s V was used to measure the strength of the associations. Also, the associations between stroke type/aetiology and mortality were investigated based on the analysis of survivorship-to-mortality ratios. The significance of variation in the factors between the subgroups of patients with haemorrhagic and ischaemic stroke was assessed using the non-parametric Mann Whitney U test due to the fact that the assumptions of normality of distribution were not met, as shown by the analyses performed using the Shapiro–Wilk W test. The analyses were performed using SPSS software and SciPy Python library, and the probability value was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Majority of the patients in this study were senior citizens aged either 66–85 years (57.2%), or over 85 years (21.8%); those in the younger age groups of 46–65 years and 25–45 years accounted respectively for 19% and 2% of the total. Women were on average older than men. Far more patients suffered an ischaemic (N = 555, 87%) than a haemorrhagic stroke (N = 83, 13%). Stroke subtypes according to aetiology were identified at the following rates: large artery atherosclerosis (N = 155, 24.3%), stroke of other determined cause (N = 128, 20.1%), stroke of undetermined cause (N = 153, 24%), small artery occlusion (N = 96, 15%), cardioembolism (N = 87, 13.6%), aneurism in the anterior part of the circle of Willis (N = 19, 3%). Ischaemic stroke subtypes relative to the location of circulation stroke were identified at the following rates: TACS (N = 174, 27.3%), PACS (N = 160, 25.1%), LACS (N = 91, 14.2%), POCS (N = 74, 11.6%), and uncertain classification (N = 56, 8.8%).

The findings related to aetiology and types of stroke in the specific months during the year in question show that large artery atherosclerosis was the predominant cause of stroke most commonly occurring in June (31.4%) and October (28.6%). Cardioembolism most frequently occurred in November (26.5%) and August (23.3%), whereas TACS was most often observed in January (41.8%) and December (32.8%), and PACS was most common in August (37.2%) and May (31.5%). Most cases of aneurysm-related haemorrhagic stroke were identified in September (6.0%) and December (6.6%), while stroke of undetermined cause was most common in May (35.2%) and August (30.2%).

Greater diurnal variation in atmospheric pressure and ambient temperature was observed in the case of haemorrhagic strokes compared with ischaemic strokes. Higher mean maximum wind speed was observed in the case of ischaemic strokes (Table 1).

We also conducted analyses related to the meteorological seasons in Poland: spring (1 March to 31 May), summer (1 June to 31 August), autumn (1 September to 30 November) and winter (1 December to 28 February). Stroke incidents were most frequent in winter 28.8% (184), and least frequent in summer 22.7% (145). In spring and autumn stroke rate amounted to 23.8% (152), and 24.6% (157), respectively.

The most significant association between mortality and aetiology is shown by a very low p-value from the Chi-square test (3.8e−5) and high Cramer’s V (1.0). This suggests a direct and robust relationship between these two parameters. The type of stroke is another crucial parameter as regards mortality rates, with a p-value of 0.0081 (Table 3) and a correlation coefficient of 0.306 (Table 4). A strong and significant association was also found between month and mortality (p-value = 0.0001, Cramer’s V = 0.237—Tables 3 and 4). Both minimum and average temperatures showed significant associations (p-values < 0.003, Cramer’s V = 0.172 and 0.160, respectively) with mortality.

A significant association was also found between month and stroke type (Chi-square test p-value = 0.0059—Table 3). Cramer’s V of 0.166 indicates a moderate to strong relationship. All other factors (maximum wind speed, patient age, average temperature, sex, and pressure difference) showed non-significant associations with stroke type. Cramer’s V values for these factors ranged from 0.080 to 0.104 (Table 4), suggesting weak to moderate associations.

There is also a strong association between temperatures (average, min., max.) and stroke aetiology (the Chi-square test p-value < 0.05—Table 3). The latter relationship is also shown by Cramer’s V coefficient (values above 0.11—Table 4). There is also a moderately strong relationship between stroke aetiology and the factors of min/max temperature, month and pressure difference for which Cramer’s V coefficients range from 0.11 to 0.13 (Table 4). Maximum wind speed, patient age, pressure difference, and sex showed non-significant associations with aetiology. Cramer’s V values for these factors ranged from 0.071 to 0.101, suggesting weak to moderate associations.

Overall mortality was at a level of 11.5%. TACS was found with the highest mortality rate of 41.9%. Cardiac embolism and large artery thrombosis were identified as the leading cause of death in 31% and 24.3% of the cases, respectively. These two aetiologies were also associated with a high mortality rate in PACS, making PACS the third most frequent type of stroke resulting in death. Mortality ratios, calculated as the number of deaths divided by the total number of cases, further confirmed these findings. The analyses disregarding the results with very low total numbers, like “Other” types of stroke or brain aneurysms with POCS and PACS, show that TACS with cardiac embolism was associated with the highest mortality ratio at 0.35. Furthermore, stroke types with notable mortality rates also included POCS with cardiac embolism (0.31), TACS with “Other” aetiologies (0.22), and PACS with cardiac embolism (0.19). Conversely, the highest survivorship ratios, calculated as the number of patients who survived the stroke to the total number of cases, were identified for LACS, ranging from 0.98 to 1, and “Other” types of strokes, ranging from 0.86 to 0.91.

Discussion

Based on a review of literature we can conclude that in Poland there are no data related to seasonal variations in stroke. Likewise, there are no studies investigating (monthly and seasonal) stroke incidence rates and stroke-related deaths as well as associations between such factors as weather variables, sex, age, subtype and stroke aetiology. This observation provided a motivation for the current study.

In our study group stroke was most frequently observed in winter and least frequently in summer. Similarly, Bwangahonar et al. in a study carried out in Iran and focusing on seasonal and monthly variations in stroke identified months in which the incidence of the condition was higher. According to those authors the highest morbidity rates were observed in winter, in February. Many studies present evidence that the lowest morbidity rates are observed during summer months, i.e. in May, June and July6. Likewise, researchers from Sweden7 and Australia4 suggest that winter time potentially is a risk factor for greater morbidity rates.

According to the present findings, month and season appear to be significant factors associated with stroke type, aetiology, and mortality. Specifically, higher stroke incidence was identified in the cold months of January and December, which suggests that lower temperatures may increase the risk of stroke. Temperature-related factors (minimum, maximum, and average) are significantly associated with stroke aetiology, indicating a potential environmental influence, where lower temperatures could be a contributing factor. Although weak to moderate associations were observed between other factors (e.g., wind speed, patient age, sex) and stroke outcomes, these were not statistically significant. Further investigation is required to identify specific months and environmental factors that pose the highest risk.

The results suggest that the different stroke types may present different pathophysiological mechanisms associated with the seasons/weather conditions. Hence, there may be a need to match prevention and treatment strategies to specific types of stroke in different months of the year.

Increased number of strokes due to cardioembolism in February may be related to the seasonal increase in respiratory infections that can lead to embolism. Shigematsu et al. investigated seasonal variation in haemorrhagic stroke and ischaemic stroke. They calculated odds ratio for the incidence of stroke in general, and for the three subtypes, i.e. ischaemic stroke, as well as cerebral haemorrhage and subarachnoid haemorrhage in the specific seasons, i.e. in spring, summer, autumn and winter. The authors showed there was seasonal variation in the incidence of the specific stroke subtypes. Ischaemic stroke was less frequent in autumn than in summer, while haemorrhagic stroke was more frequent in spring, autumn and winter compared to summer. Incidence of subarachnoid haemorrhage was higher in spring and winter than in summer22. On the other hand, Oberg et al. suggest that we can only talk about seasonality in the case of ischaemic stroke. Haemorrhagic stroke follows a different pattern. Associations between the specific types of stroke and seasonality may differ because haemorrhagic and ischaemic stroke have different pathophysiologic mechanisms, and that may incorrectly present impact of seasonality on certain types of stroke23. It has also been suggested that the differences in the results reported by various studies may be linked to air pollution with sulphur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide during the specific months and seasons of the year24.

In our study female patients slightly more often experienced ischaemic stroke than men. Higher risk of stroke in women may be associated with pregnancy and postpartum period, as well as hormonal factors, including the use of contraceptives25. On the other hand, no statistically significant associations were found as regards the patients’ sex and age relative to the season of the year in which stroke was experienced. It was only in the case of the patients over 85 years of age that the findings show lower likelihood of ischaemic stroke in winter and spring. Likewise, Shigematsu et al. showed that seasonality in stroke rates is not related to the patients’ age or sex21. On the other hand, Wang et al. observed a significant tendency for increased incidence of stroke from February to July, mainly in individuals aged > 65 years4. A very interesting study carried out in Japan investigated weekly and seasonal variation of stroke8. Incidence of stroke was far greater in winter and spring, compared to summer, and on weekdays compared to weekends. The researchers also reported a visibly negative relationship between seasonal variation in stroke rates and age. It can be speculated that young people and older citizens differ significantly in terms of lifestyles and physiological status8.

The current findings suggest an association between extreme temperatures—both low and high—and stroke incidence. Our research indicates that colder temperatures, in particular, may affect the mechanisms leading to stroke, elevating the risk during colder months. However, high temperatures also contribute to stroke risk, which may reflect a broader impact of environmental factors on stroke outcomes. These findings suggest that both cold and hot temperatures should be considered in stroke prevention strategies, emphasizing the need for increased vigilance and protective measures during extreme weather conditions. Further research is required to fully understand the mechanisms driving this association26.

Our conclusions are consistent with prior studies that explored the relationship between ambient temperature and stroke risk. For instance, Lian et al. demonstrated that short-term fluctuations in both low and high temperatures significantly impacted the incidence of major cerebrovascular events, such as stroke. Their meta-analysis showed that cold temperatures in particular were linked to increased stroke morbidity and mortality27. Similarly, Wen et al. confirmed that extreme temperatures, hot or cold, were positively correlated with risk of stroke; the authors emphasised the importance of targeted public health interventions to mitigate this risk28.

Researchers have demonstrated that both low and high temperatures are associated with an increased risk of stroke-related death. Notably, stroke incidence has also been linked not only to long-term exposure but also to short-term fluctuations in temperature29. For example, one study showed that an increase in temperature during the 96 h preceding the first stroke symptoms significantly increased the likelihood of stroke onset in male populations30. Moreover, Fustinoni et al. reported that atrial fibrillation, a common risk factor for ischemic stroke, is linked to seasonal temperature fluctuations, with the highest stroke incidence occurring in cold seasons31. This body of evidence, including the present findings, supports the notion that temperature fluctuations significantly contribute to stroke risk. Therefore, it is important to raise awareness, particularly in vulnerable populations, about stroke-related hazards resulting from short-term environmental changes.

Our analysis of seasonal trends revealed that both high and low temperatures are significantly associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke. This is consistent with previous studies linking extreme temperatures to a heightened risk of stroke incidence and mortality. These variations suggest that both high and low temperatures may exacerbate existing cardiovascular issues, leading to higher stroke rates during extreme weather conditions. This highlights the importance of adapting stroke prevention strategies to account for these fluctuations.

Regarding mortality rates, the current study found that TACS and embolisms of cardiac origin are associated with significantly increased risk of death, while small artery occlusion is related to lower risk of death. This suggests there is a need for special attention and intensified therapeutic interventions in the case of TACS and cardiac embolisms to reduce mortality rates in these groups of patients.

It would also be worthwhile to discuss potential confounding factors, such as variation in emergency medical service transport patterns, as well as variation in activities possibly predisposing to stroke and correlating with weather, which may have affected the results of the study and were not taken into account. The association between weather-related factors and stroke-related ambulance transportation was investigated by Matsumaru et al.32. The study showed that risk factors for emergency medical transportation due to stroke included a lower temperature, a radical change in humidity and a decrease in temperature. The authors suggest that meteorological data could be applied in medical economics to ensure more effective management of the medical resources32. Researchers have also been trying to understand the relationship between stroke incidence and physically demanding activities involving cardiovascular strain and possibly leading to artery rapture or thrombus dislodgement and consequently triggering stroke onset33–35. One of such activities is shovelling, associated with snowfall in winter. According to Polcaro–Pichet et al. snowfall and the associated shovelling activity seem to increase the risk of death due to haemorrhagic, but not ischaemic stroke35. On the other hand, Nocera et al. reported that increased stroke incidence corresponded to higher temperatures, however at the time of their stroke no patients in their study group were performing activities which were more physically demanding than walking. This observation led to a conclusion that physical overexertion in warmer temperatures does not seem to play a role in stroke incidence rates36. In view of the above, we are planning further research taking into account the potential cofounding variables. Furthermore, a future research focusing on this subject matter should also take into account the time of day at the onset of acute stroke in lacunar versus non lacunar infarcts. This will be an important line of investigation because the pathophysiology, prognosis, and clinical features of lacunar strokes are different from other acute cerebrovascular diseases37.

We have limited our analyses of meteorological data in order to compare diurnal variation of ambient temperature, atmospheric pressure and wind speed in relation to the type of stroke. Our decision may have been arbitrary and perhaps we should have included other meteorological parameters in our study. However, the number of independent variables to be considered is limited. Importantly, our research aimed to investigate if weather conditions such as diurnal fluctuations of temperature or pressure may be linked to the risk of stroke onset in time. Because of this we decided to focus on the above parameters rather than investigate a wide range of meteorological factors. What is more the town selected for the study is one of the warmest places in Poland. This is connected with its specific location, the town frequently experiences warm gusts of mountain wind (halny) and is sheltered from cold draughts by ranges of hills.

In summary, our study explicitly shows the complexity of interactions between atmospheric factors, stroke type and aetiology, and their relationship with the risk of ischaemic stroke and stroke-related death. These findings have the potential to improve prevention and treatment strategies, especially in the context of changing climate and seasonal weather patterns. Further research should provide better insight into the mechanisms behind the observed relationships, which will make it possible to develop effective stroke prevention methods in different populations.

Limitations of our study

Limitations of our study include the short time of observation (the analyses took into account only one calendar year), the small sample size, as well as the regional emphasis (the data set was acquired in one hospital only) due to which the findings may reflect regional rather than global trends related to the relevant issue. Since the data acquired are limited to one calendar year, it is impossible to distinguish monthly variation due to seasonal effects from random variation. Furthermore, data from 2020 may not be representative, as the COVID-19 pandemic may have affected stroke admission rates. Notably, the present study did not take into account certain potentially confounding variables such as emergency medical service transport patterns and activities performed at the time of stroke that may pose a risk of stroke and may correlate with weather. Another limitation of this study was the large disparity in the size of the subgroups of patients with ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke. This was associated with the stroke-related admission rates in the facility investigated during the relevant period, and the fact that ischaemic stroke is more common than haemorrhagic stroke. Given the above, there is a need for further research to investigate the problem in a more comprehensive manner, in multiple research centres, in a time period different than the COVID-19 pandemic, with observations continued for at least several years, and consequently taking into account a larger sample, and additionally investigating the time of day at the onset of acute stroke in lacunar versus non lacunar infarcts, and assessing effects of potential confounding variables.

Conclusions

The present study shows that the highest number of stroke incidents occurred in winter and the lowest in summer, which shows seasonal trends in stroke incidence. Month and season appear to be significant factors linked to both stroke type and aetiology, which suggests there is a need to adapt prevention and treatment strategies, especially in the context of changing climate and seasonal weather patterns. These findings also suggest that temperature fluctuations are an important environmental factor; both increases and decreases in temperature may lead to a greater risk of stroke, so higher vigilance is needed during periods of extreme temperatures, in both warmer and colder months. Regarding mortality rates, we found that TACS and embolisms of cardiac origin are associated with significantly increased risk of death, while small artery occlusion is related to lower risk of death. Further research is needed so that we can better understand these relationships and develop effective prevention methods.
