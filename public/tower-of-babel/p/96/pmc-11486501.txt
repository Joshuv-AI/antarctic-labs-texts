Cardiothoracic surgery and white elephants in the room: medical neoliberalism and its consequences for patient care
Authors: Hendrik Jan Ankersmit
Published: 2024

This reply is a comment on a position paper authored by 22 experts with the backing of 8 national associations related to cardiac surgery and conflicts of interest (COIs) in cardiovascular medicine [1]. The opinions are clear that clinical practice and guidelines have been altered by randomized clinical trials (RCTs), particularly those pertaining to percutaneous coronary intervention versus coronary artery bypass grafting surgery and to surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) versus transfemoral aortic valve intervention (TAVI). Prominent academic journals and academic societies take the lead in promulgating such ‘innovative therapies’ in the realm of cardiovascular therapy. Astute readers will realize that this European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (EACTS) Position Statement discusses, in a most prudent way, how RCTs serve as an argument for the alteration of guidelines in the treatment of disease-ridden patients. A shift of treatment options for patients is the consequence and causes hospital systems to invest in so-called hybrid operating rooms in order to offer these novel therapies to the patients in need. In a nutshell, institutional RCTs driven by a multimillion dollar industry are performed to dissect which procedure serves the patient better in the realm of cardiovascular medicine. In the field of TAVI versus SAVR, a most prominently published report determined that the wire intervention was non-inferior to surgery in low or intermediate risk patients.

Most interestingly to me was that the primary end points were determined after 12 months of patient history [2]. The selection of such a clinical end point ridicules the surgical intervention per se and should be critically discussed in upcoming ESC/EACTS aortic valve replacement guidelines [3].

Cardiologists are the first to see, diagnose and support the patient’s choice by offering catheter-based therapy because of its less invasive nature. However, the workload of cardiac surgeons, as well as revenue, is heavily dependent on the referral policy of their institutional counterparts. An article in The Economist, an international periodical, in 2023 surprised many cardiac surgeons [4]. The title expressed the opinion that cardiothoracic surgery will vanish, as did lung surgery for tuberculosis or surgical treatment for gastric ulcers, because the catheter-based interventions and its expensive infrastructure are present at institutions. The pendulum is swinging and a death knell is being heard for the cardiac surgery profession. Should we be afraid?

Interventional cardiology specialists, the device industry and hospital management are exuberant because of the growing ageing population and bright business models. The documented and proven short-term effectiveness of wire interventions and their rapid execution are responsible for increased diagnosis-related group earnings of the hospital carrier. However, we have to dive into our national outcome data to investigate whether wire interventions are beneficial in patients with aortic valve disease.

In the field of TAVI therapy, ‘lifetime management’ is the propagated ‘new truth’ [5]. In our recent national AUTHEARTVISIT (Austrian observational study) outcome exploration (with data available for 98% of the Austrian population), we found that the American Heart Association/EACTS clinical guidelines in the field of aortic valve replacement (pertaining to age limits, choice between biological vs mechanical prosthesis) are ignored to a greater extent by ‘heart teams’. Consequences for ‘real life’ morbidity and mortality have been demonstrated [6, 7] with all the inherent limitations [8]. Even more alarming were results presented by Auer et al. [9], in which it was obvious that TAVI causes increased mortality and morbidity compared to surgically implanted aortic valve replacement, especially in patients between 65 and 75 years of age. It is evident from our data that medical marketing from stakeholders, contrary to published guidelines, is leading to clinical decisions in our publicly funded hospitals. The documented medical reality is biting back at the executing professionals.

It is intellectually naïve to claim that COIs in medical research are the only reason for the above-described clinical development. In a broader context, we may have to ‘follow the money’ in our dialectic approach. Who are the big benefactors of this ‘new’ clinical reality? Medical neoliberalism has restructured healthcare delivery into a commodity to be purchased rather than the natural-born right of taxpayers living in a socialized welfare system [9]. We have seen that most nations exchanged their ‘non-profit hospital carrier system’ for a ‘for-profit hospital carrier system’ between 1980 and 2000. Increased access, equity, quality, efficacy and a healthier life were the promises of most governing bodies. This observation is, however, a delusion because hospital managers serving a for-profit hospital environment will pressure their employed physicians to opt for treatment options that will increase reimbursement in an ever-shorter time period [10]. A second aspect in this hotchpotch is that executive compensation schemes are linked to the incentive structures described above. Hospital managers and their employed medical doctors have, in their for-profit systems, by law, a fiduciary duty to create a return on investment [11]. Hospital leadership will pursue organizational restructuring with this in mind. Such interventions will include pushing employees to increase patient turnover, influencing employees to increase the number of procedures, prescribing/selling costlier products, using higher imbursement billing codes and influencing employees to refer patients to subspeciality departments at their institution.

Compared to SAVR, TAVI is a short intervention that mandates only a short hospital stay for the morbid patient. However, 12% of all TAVI patients will also need a pacemaker intervention, compared to 4% of SAVR patients. In a for-profit hospital system, all stakeholders (hospital carrier, medical professionals, hospital management, device industry, professional societies) benefit, except the patients who trusted their lives to their treating physicians. In informed consent—the Pandora’s box—the patient is a legal entity: compared with RCTs, outcome research offers, with all its limitations, the consequences of approved therapeutic interventions by the regulatory body (e.g. in the United States, the Federal Drug Administration; in Europe, notified bodies). These approvals have to be revoked when multiple clinical outcome researchers have proven that the endorsed intervention is deleterious to the patients. The threat of litigation against treating physicians utilizing these innovative therapies is looming. When signing the consent papers, were the patients informed correctly by the treating physicians? This issue is, for me, the last identified white elephant in the room when commenting on the EACTS position statement. The COIs will continue. As an intellectual and realist in the medical field, I am convinced that the outcomes of medical therapies deeply impact the soul of our medical profession. The leverage of outcome research will define and refine the best treatment options for our future patients. Cardiac surgery and anaesthesia will not vanish.
