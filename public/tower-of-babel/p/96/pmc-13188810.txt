Comparison of surgical and conservative treatments for distal radius fractures in the elderly: a multicenter, randomized, single-blind (DART study)
Authors: Weilu Gao, Zhengfeng Jia, Cheng Xu, Guiyou Wu, Changsen Yang, Hao Zhang, Daofeng Wang, Jiantao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDistal radius fractures (DRFs) are a global public health issue and represent the second most common type of fracture among the elderly. There remains controversy in the orthopedic community regarding the optimal treatment strategy for DRFs.MethodsWe will conduct a multicenter, randomized, single-blind, controlled trial to assess the efficacy of surgical versus conservative treatment for distal radius fractures in elderly patients. Sample size calculations are based on the PRWE score, aiming to randomize 456 participants in a 1:1 ratio. Once randomized, patients will be followed for 24 months post-treatment. The primary outcome will be wrist function reported by patients within the first year post-injury. Secondary outcomes include QUICKDASH scores, VAS scores, EQ-5D-3L scores, range of wrist motion, grip strength, complications, and patient satisfaction. Additionally, the study will evaluate the economic and social benefits using statistical methods.DiscussionThere is no definitive answer as to whether elderly patients with displaced distal radius fractures universally benefit from surgery. However, current literature suggests that surgery does not provide significant advantages. Through this study, we aim to provide the highest quality evidence to determine the optimal treatment strategy for distal radius fractures in patients aged 60 and above, thereby enhancing post-trauma recovery for the elderly.Trial registrationThis study had been registered in the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Distal radius fractures (DRFs) represent a significant global public health concern [1–6], ranking as the second most common fracture type in the elderly population, surpassed only by hip fractures. In younger individuals, these fractures are predominantly associated with high-energy trauma, whereas in the elderly, they typically result from low-energy incidents such as a fall onto an outstretched hand. The economic and social impacts of DRFs are considerable [7]. Annually, the cost of treating DRFs in the elderly is estimated to range between $385 and $535 million. Beyond the financial burden, the non-economic costs are also substantial. While younger patients often experience a shorter duration of pain and dysfunction, older adults may face prolonged injury effects, significantly impacting their ability to live independently and affecting their mental health [8].

The optimal treatment strategy for DRFs remains a topic of debate within the field of orthopedics [9, 10]. Conservative treatment can necessitate up to 6 to 9 months of rehabilitation, whereas internal fixation, particularly with recent advancements such as the Vertical Lateral Palmar Plate with Locking Screws (VLPS), allows older adults to reduce fixation time and resume normal activities more quickly. The increasing popularity of internal fixation techniques underscores their potential benefits in managing DRFs [7].

In elderly patients, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) are not consistently superior to conservative treatment. Over the past decade, numerous studies have compared the efficacy and safety of conservative versus surgical treatments in terms of imaging outcomes, patient perceptions, and complications [11–13]. However, standardized criteria for the advantages and disadvantages of each approach remain undeveloped. A significant benefit of the palmar locking plate system is its inherent stability, allowing most patients to mobilize their wrists immediately post-surgery without concern for plate failure or fracture collapse [14]. Furthermore, internal fixation techniques require extensive incisional exposure and can be technically challenging. The additional surgical trauma may also increase the risk of complications and elevate medical costs, reducing societal cross-over benefits [15].

Economic analyses are crucial in determining the most cost-effective treatment strategy for distal radius fractures (DRFs) [16]. These analyses provide decision-makers with the necessary information to assess the feasibility of financial support, which holds significant research and societal value. Unfortunately, there is a lack of high-quality evidence comparing the economic burden of different treatment modalities in many countries. Therefore, robust evidence is needed to elucidate the economic advantages and disadvantages associated with various treatment strategies.

Current clinical studies are mainly small-scale trials with insufficient sample sizes and short follow-up periods. Some published trials suffer from patient enrollment and follow-up challenges [17], leading to designs that do not fully adhere to randomized controlled study principles, potentially introducing patient selection bias. Given the large patient population, multicenter studies with rigorous experimental designs could address these limitations and provide more comprehensive data [1].

Distal radius fractures are prevalent in our society, and improper management can result in long-term functional impairments. Patients, healthcare providers, and policymakers demand high-quality research to inform medical decision-making.

Primary objective

To compare functional outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and patient satisfaction between conservatively and surgically treated elderly patients (aged 60 years and older) with displaced intra-articular distal radius fractures over 2 years post-trauma.

Secondary objective

To compare recovery trends in distal radius fractures treated with palmar locking plate internal fixation versus fixation techniques and to identify predictors of clinical outcome.

Objectives {7}

This study aims to comprehensively evaluate the management of distal radius fractures in older adults through five interconnected objectives.

Design objective 1

To compare the clinical outcomes of distal radius fractures in older adults treated with a palmar lateral plate fixation system, percutaneous perforator pinning, external fixation with or without percutaneous perforator pinning, and plaster casting at 12–24 months post-treatment.

Design rationale

Internal fixation allows early wrist movement, avoids complications associated with prolonged immobilization, and may lead to better functional outcomes.

Design objective 2

To compare recovery trends of distal radius fractures in older adults treated with a palmar lateral plate fixation system, percutaneous perforator pin fixation, and external fixation with or without percutaneous perforator pin fixation.

Design rationale

The rate of recovery for these three surgical modalities is unknown. Therefore, this design proposes evaluating patients at 1 week, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and 24 months postoperatively to elucidate the recovery rates for the different treatment methods.

Design objective 3

To identify predictors of outcome after surgical treatment of distal radius fractures in older adults.

Design rationale

Predictors of functional outcomes in older adults have not been thoroughly studied. We will assess predictors of outcome based on demographic characteristics, fracture type, and treatment method.

Design objective 4

To compare recovery trends at 12 and 24 months post-surgery between patients who chose cast immobilization and those in the surgical group.

Design rationale

Elderly patients may have lower functional requirements and are thought to tolerate the deformity resulting from closed reduction and cast fixation alone.

Design objective 5

To perform a cost-utility analysis comparing the cost-effectiveness of the three surgical treatments with conservative treatments.

Design rationale

A formal economic analysis of the different treatment options for distal radius fractures has not been performed. This design will evaluate the costs of different treatments and incorporate patient preferences to determine the optimal strategy.

Trial design {8}

This study is a multicenter, randomized, single-blind clinical trial that combined economic assessment and patient satisfaction assessment. Our trial has been registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry. The protocol was prepared in accordance with the standard program item: Recommended Guidelines for Intervention Tests. An article describing the results will be written in accordance with the guidelines of the Comprehensive Standard for Trial Reporting (CONSORT).

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

The study will be conducted in China by three large tertiary care hospitals as well as orthopedic centers in Beijing. The specific study design is shown in Fig. 1. Patients will be recruited at eight participating hospitals. This is a clinical trial that will recruit older patients who meet the inclusion criteria. After providing written informed consent, participants will be randomly assigned to one of four groups: closed reduction percutaneous needle fixation, external fixation with or without percutaneous needle fixation, internal fixation with palmar locking plate, and plaster fixation (observation group). The trial follows the Standard Program Item: Intervention Trial Recommendations (SPIRIT).

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

A qualified member of the site-specific clinical research team will secure comprehensive written informed consent from all prospective participants prior to pre-enrollment eligibility screening and baseline assessments, which will be conducted through face-to-face interviews. Protocol details, including risks, benefits, and procedural requirements, will be communicated using plain language adapted to participants’ comprehension levels, with multilingual support provided where necessary. Participants will be provided multiple opportunities to seek clarification throughout the consent process, supported by visual aids and standardized educational materials approved by the institutional review board.

All consent administrators will undergo standardized training under the principal investigator’s supervision to ensure protocol adherence and competency in addressing participant concerns. The schedule of study-related procedures, including follow-up assessments and potential burdens, will be thoroughly reviewed during initial consent discussions, with periodic reminders provided post-enrollment. Participants retain the unrestricted right to withdraw from the trial at any stage without prejudice to ongoing clinical care, a provision explicitly emphasized during consent documentation and reinforced through a formal withdrawal process.

To uphold ethical rigor, consent will be exclusively obtained from cognitively competent individuals, as verified by a standardized cognitive evaluation conducted independently by trained assessors unaffiliated with treatment allocation. All consent interactions will be documented verbatim in the trial’s electronic data capture system, with timestamped signatures and witness attestations where required by local regulations.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

This study is a multicenter, randomized, single-blind clinical trial. After providing written informed consent, participants were randomly assigned to one of four groups: closed reduction percutaneous needle fixation, external fixation with or without percutaneous needle fixation, internal fixation with palmar locking plate, and plaster fixation (observation group).

Intervention description {11a}

Interventions will be divided into four categories:

Open reduction and internal fixation

A standard Henry approach is used to expose the distal radius. Fracture reduction is performed, and a volar locking plate is applied according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Closed reduction external fixation with a bridging fixator and, depending on the situation, percutaneous transluminal internal fixation

Under fluoroscopic guidance, fracture reduction is achieved by traction and manipulation. An external fixator (bridging type) is applied; supplementary K-wires may be used at the surgeon’s discretion. Patients are instructed to keep the pin sites clean and dry. The fixator is removed after 6 weeks in the outpatient clinic.

Closed reduction and percutaneous pin internal fixation

Following closed reduction, two to three K-wires are inserted percutaneously to stabilize the fracture. The wires are buried under the skin or left protruding, and a short arm cast is applied for 4–6 weeks.

Closed reduction followed by plaster and splint fixation

After closed reduction, the lower part of the arm will be immobilized using a plaster cast or a splint for 6 weeks. X-rays will be taken every week for the first 3 weeks to ensure there is no loss of reduction.

Participants will be permitted to receive standard medical care for unrelated conditions. All participants will receive standard medical care throughout the trial and after its completion. In the event of any trial-related injury, participants will be provided with appropriate medical treatment at no cost.

Outcomes {12}

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the patient-reported wrist function at 1-year post-trauma, measured using the Patient-Rated Wrist Evaluation (PRWE) score. The PRWE, along with the Arm, Shoulder, and Quick DASH assessments, are valid and reliable tools for patients with distal radius fractures. However, the PRWE is considered a more wrist-specific tool for assessing the prognosis of these patients.

PRWE details

The PRWE is a 15-item questionnaire designed to measure wrist pain and disability in activities of daily living. The minimum clinically important difference (MCID) for the PRWE is 14. This MCID value is used as a threshold for non-inferiority in sample size calculations, with smaller differences considered clinically irrelevant.

Sample size {14}

This non-inferiority trial calculates the sample size based on the primary outcome, the Patient-Rated Wrist Evaluation (PRWE) score at 12 months post-trauma. The non-inferiority margin is set at 7 points, corresponding to the minimal clinically important difference. Assuming a standard deviation of 15 points, a one-sided significance level of α = 0.025, and 80% power, 103 patients per group are required. After adjusting for an anticipated 10% loss to follow-up, the sample size is increased to 114 patients per group. With four parallel groups, the total sample size is 456 participants.

Recruitment {15}

As protocols for emergency and outpatient admissions vary between the same and different hospitals, specialized research assistants need to be trained to assist with research and to develop a standardized process for patient inclusion and exclusion. The process of screening patients will be expedited while ensuring standardization.

Potentially eligible patients will be recruited from inpatient wards, outpatient clinics, and emergency departments. To identify eligible patients, research assistants will screen all patients 60 years of age and older with distal radius fractures. Patient eligibility must be confirmed by a designated surgeon or clinician. Eligible patients will be approached to discuss the study and given a patient information sheet, they will be given time to discuss the study with family and/or friends, and they will have the opportunity to ask questions of the surgeon and the local research team.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Randomization assignment is initiated only after assessment confirms that the patient is ready to enter the trial and signs an informed consent form. An independent statistician will generate an allocation sequence in advance using computer-generated random numbers. Each enrolled subject will be assigned a unique identification code associated with the random code.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

Allocation concealment will be ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The envelopes will be prepared by a research coordinator who is not involved in patient recruitment or assessment. Only after the participant has provided written informed consent and baseline assessments are completed will the next envelope be opened to reveal the treatment allocation.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Given the nature of the intervention, it is impractical for patients and the primary surgeon to remain completely unaware of treatment allocation. However, to minimize assessment bias, the statistician responsible for outcome assessment and data analysis will remain blinded to treatment group assignments.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Unblinding of the outcome assessors or statistician is not anticipated under normal circumstances. If unblinding is deemed necessary for safety reasons, this must be approved by the principal investigator (PI) and the trial steering committee, and will be fully documented.

Acquisition of VAS scores

At each follow-up visit, patients will complete a VAS for wrist pain: hard copy: marking on a visual 0 to 10 scale. Computer: dragging an arrow on a scale of 0 to 10. Telephone: the patient is asked to visualize the 0 to 10 scale and provide a score.

Acquisition of physical measurements

If patients can visit the hospital, measurements will be taken there. If patients are unable or unwilling to visit the hospital, researchers can visit the patients at home. Range of motion (ROM) will be tested on both the injured and uninjured sides using an analog Jamar goniometer to measure the degree of movement in all three planes (as described in secondary outcomes).

Grip strength will be measured three times on each side [13]. The mean of the three measurements on each side will be used as a response variable for the treatment effect. Grip strength will also be used as an explanatory variable, as it is considered a diagnostic biomarker of weakness. For this purpose, grip strength measured on the uninjured side at 6 weeks will be used.

Acquisition of radiological parameters

Radiographs will be obtained in PA and lateral views. At each follow-up visit, radiological parameters will be measured by the coordinating investigator in the picture archiving and communication system of each participating hospital.

All data will be collected online at follow-up visits as well as through the case system and will be automatically recorded by the Epidata application. Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Primary analysis

The primary analysis will assess non-inferiority of surgical treatment compared to conservative treatment regarding the PRWE score at 12 months post-trauma [18, 19]. A linear mixed model will be used to account for the repeated measurements at multiple follow-up time points (1 week, 6 weeks, 3, 6, 12, and 24 months). This approach accommodates within-patient correlation and allows for missing data under the missing-at-random assumption.

The model will include treatment group, time, and their interaction as fixed effects, with age and baseline PRWE score as covariates. Random intercepts and random slopes for time will be included to capture individual variation in baseline levels and recovery trajectories.

Non-inferiority will be declared if the upper limit of the one-sided 97.5% confidence interval (equivalent to a two-sided 95% confidence interval) for the between-group difference in PRWE scores lies below the non-inferiority margin of 7 points. This margin represents the minimal clinically important difference, and a lower PRWE score favors surgical treatment. Both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses will be performed to assess the robustness of the non-inferiority conclusion [20].

Secondary outcome analyses

For continuous secondary outcomes (QuickDASH, EQ-5D-3L, VAS pain, range of motion, grip strength) [21, 22], similar linear mixed models will be used, adjusting for baseline values where applicable. Complications and adverse events will be compared between groups using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. All secondary analyses will be conducted at a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05, with 95% confidence intervals reported. No adjustment for multiple comparisons will be performed, as these analyses are exploratory.

Economic evaluation

The economic evaluation will be conducted from both societal and healthcare perspectives, adhering to the intention-to-treat principle [23]. From a societal perspective, all relevant costs incurred by participants, regardless of the payer or beneficiary, will be considered. The study will analyze the characteristics of the primary clinical outcomes (PRWE, EQ-5D-5L) and hospital days to assess their value as final trial efficacy measures, thereby determining the primary outcomes [24]. QALY data provided by EQ-5D-5L will be utilized for the economic evaluation.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

A preliminary cost-effectiveness analysis will assess the potential benefits of conducting a full trial. Sensitivity analysis will be performed by varying key cost drivers, such as hospital and bed days. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) and incremental cost-utility ratio (ICUR) will be calculated by dividing the cost difference by the effect/utility difference. Linear multilevel analysis will be used to account for potential clustering effects in the data, and bootstrapping techniques will estimate the uncertainty in cost-effectiveness estimates. Uncertainty will be displayed in cost-effectiveness planes and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves. Sensitivity analyses will test the robustness of the results (e.g., according to protocol analysis and potential EQ-5D-3L values).

Discussion

This is a protocol for a randomized controlled trial comparing the difference in clinical efficacy between surgical and conservative treatment of distal radius fractures in the elderly. There are three main categories of surgical treatment: closed reduction with percutaneous pinning, external fixation with or without percutaneous pinning, and internal fixation with a palmar locking plate. Conservative treatment is closed reduction and cast immobilization. In addition to this trial, we will conduct a comprehensive economic evaluation as well as a patient satisfaction assessment.

There is still a lack of high-level studies on the efficacy of surgical versus conservative treatment of distal radius fractures [25, 26]. In clinical work, once physicians have established a practice pattern, it is difficult for them to change and accept alternative treatments [27]. Distal radius fractures are prevalent in our society, and incorrect management of this injury will lead to long-term functional problems. Evidence-based medicine has come to the forefront as patients, providers, and healthcare policymakers demand high-quality research to enhance understanding of medical decision-making [28]. There is a need for systematic research into this controversy, rigorous trial design, and high-level clinical studies to support the highest level of evidence-based clinical trials for the treatment of distal radius fractures in the country [29].

In trials where conservative treatment therapy is shown to be non-inferior to surgical treatment in terms of patient-reported wrist function, the choice of treatment may be based on other factors such as the patient’s propensity or the cost of treatment. In the economic evaluation, we will quantify the healthcare and social costs of both types of treatment regarding patient-reported wrist function quality of life over the years, and work status [25, 30]. Despite our efforts to avoid methodological weaknesses in the experimental design, this study is not without limitations. As with many randomized controlled trials, the main issue is external validity. Based on the literature, there is no evidence to suggest that surgically treated elderly patients have more favorable outcomes than conservatively treated patients. However, based on their own experience, surgeons may feel that it is unethical to refuse surgery to active and healthy elderly patients. On the other hand, surgeons may also refuse to operate on patients who they believe would be better off in a cast. Although the topic at hand is controversial and opinions are widely divergent, our goal was to design this study in such a way that the majority of participating surgeons would agree with the uncertainty or balance of treatment in this study population [31]. By endeavoring to select inclusion criteria such as “living independently” and exclusion criteria such as “having dementia,” we aimed to exclude very frail older people who are rarely suitable for surgery. In addition, we designed the study in two strata: one for patients aged 65 to 75 years and one for patients aged 75 + years [32]. Each stratum had its randomization schedule. This allowed participating centers that did not want to include patients aged 65–75 years to randomly assign only patients aged 75 years or older.

In addition, due to the re-multi-center conduct of the trial, there will be differences in repositioning techniques, duration of plaster treatment, physiotherapy, surgical techniques, and anesthesia. These differences lead to diversity. However, by allowing for this diversity, the study will be more feasible for external validation, making the findings more generalizable.

To date, there is no definitive answer to the question of whether older adults with displaced distal radius fractures generally benefit from surgery, but the current literature suggests no benefit. With this study, we aim to provide the highest-quality evidence on the best treatment options for treating patients over 60 years of age with distal radius fractures.
