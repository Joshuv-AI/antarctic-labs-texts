Visual cues attract adult Tunga penetrans (Siphonaptera: Tungidae)
Authors: Ayako Hyuga, Paul Ouma, Lynne Elson, Kazuchiyo Miyamichi, Satoshi Kaneko, Kensuke Goto, Tullu Bukhari, Jürgen Krücken, Ulrike Fillinger, Michael Rust
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Tunga penetrans (Linnaeus, 1758) (Siphonaptera: Tungidae) is an ectoparasitic flea whose adult females cause tungiasis by embedding into the host’s skin. Environmental, social and financial challenges in tungiasis endemic areas have left this disease neglected and lacking locally available control measures. One possible approach for controlling T. penetrans targets its off-host stages; however, surveillance tools for adult fleas are still lacking. Moreover, host-seeking cues and behaviour of adult T. penetrans remain largely unknown. In this study, we show that visual cues are attractive to adult T. penetrans. Newly emerged T. penetrans responded to light stimuli across a broad spectrum, ranging from 375 nm to 690 nm. Within this range, significantly stronger attraction was observed at 430 nm over 375 nm, at 490 nm over 610 nm, and at 610 nm over 690 nm. Attraction did not differ significantly between 430 nm and 490 nm or between 535 nm and 490 nm. Overall, wavelengths between 430–535 nm induced stronger attraction in both sexes under constant electrical current conditions. In contrast, although host odor and body temperature attracted more than half of the tested adult fleas on average, the observed responses were not statistically significant. These findings suggest that visual cues in this spectral range could serve as promising attractants for capturing adult fleas. Incorporating these insights into trap strategies could support improved monitoring, surveillance, and potentially contribute to control efforts.Keywords: trapping, host seeking, odor cue, thermal cue, visual cue

Introduction

Tunga penetrans (Linnaeus, 1758) (Siphonaptera: Tungidae) is a flea of medical and veterinary importance in sub-Saharan Africa, South America, and the Caribbean (Heukelbach et al. 2001). Female T. penetrans permanently burrow into the epidermis of host skin, particularly on the feet of humans, dogs, cats, and livestock, causing a parasitic skin disease known as tungiasis (Feldmeier et al. 2014). Tunga penetrans fleas, commonly known as sand fleas, undergo complete metamorphosis: eggs laid by embedded females fall onto the ground, and develop through 2 larval stages and pupation before maturing into adults (Calheiros 2007, Nagy et al. 2007). Their lifecycle is primarily maintained indoors, such as in sleeping areas with earthen floors (Linardi et al. 2010, Matharu et al. 2024). Although the circadian rhythm of T. penetrans is not well understood, sleeping areas, where hosts spend extended periods, are considered major sites of infection regardless of the time of day. The ecological needs of the flea for off-host development in loose sand or dust contribute to the disease burden among impoverished populations living in dwellings without sealed floors. Approximately 668 million people in sub-Saharan Africa and 450 million people in Latin America are living in environments suitable for tungiasis transmission (Deka 2020, Deka and Heukelbach 2022). Tungiasis inflicts itching, pain, and walking difficulties as well as potentially life-threatening secondary bacterial infections (Heukelbach et al. 2001). Social stigmatization and immobility impose significant barriers to the most vulnerable populations, including school-age children and elderly people in their social activities, such as attending school or seeking medical care (Feldmeier et al. 2013, Kimotho et al. 2015, McNeilly et al. 2023). Infected children have been shown to have higher school absenteeism, lower school performance and impaired neurocognitive function (Otieno et al. 2023, Elson et al. 2024).

Despite the profound impact on the quality of life (Elson et al. 2024, 2025), the disease remains neglected, lacking established effective and locally available control measures. In tungiasis-endemic areas, while multiple risk factors have been identified, such as inadequate water supply, substandard housing conditions, improper hygiene practices, caregiver mental health and parenting behavior (Muehlen et al. 2006, Ugbomoiko et al. 2007, Wiese et al. 2017, Girma et al. 2018, Elson, Wiese, et al. 2019, Nsanzimana et al. 2019, Nyangacha et al. 2019, Elson et al. 2026), addressing these issues remains a significant financial and environmental challenge. In this context, the current attempts for control are mostly focusing on treatment of the parasitic stage using dimethicone oils as strongly recommended by the Pan American Health Organization guidelines (World Health Organization 2020, McNeilly et al. 2024, Suzuki et al. 2024, Pan American Health Organization 2025), locally available plant based oils (Elson, Randu, et al. 2019, Enwemiwe et al. 2021), or oral insecticide for dogs (dos Santos et al. 2023, 2024, Santana et al. 2023, Tortoriello et al. 2024), which would shorten the life of embedded females and impair egg production. However, such treatment will not prevent off-host development already in the environment, nor the subsequent reinfection. These concerns underscore the need for integrated and multidisciplinary approaches that target both parasitic and off-host stages for effective control. Focusing on off-host stages, a reliable method for detecting T. penetrans larvae from soil has been established, enabling quantitative off-host stage monitoring (Matharu et al. 2024). In contrast, no established surveillance tool currently exists for T. penetrans adults. Flea collection using white socks or flannel dragging (Borchert et al. 2012) has not been systemically evaluated for T. penetrans, and vacuuming may be less suitable for fleas inhabiting earthen floors. The lack of surveillance tools limits our understanding of their ecology and hinders the development of effective control measures.

To trap blood-feeding insects, host-seeking cues and behavior are exploited to attract them. For instance, artificial human scents and carbon dioxide mimicking host breath are used as lure to collect host-seeking female mosquitoes (Akoton et al. 2024). In addition, positive phototaxis of Ctenocephalides felis, cat flea (Bouche, 1835) (Siphonaptera: Pulicidae) has led to several flea light traps (Dryden and Broce 1993, Müller et al. 2011). Similar to other blood-feeding ectoparasites, both emerged female and male T. penetrans as obligate blood feeders must utilize sensitive sensory systems to identify and locate potential host blood sources. In fact, they have been observed to approach the host by walking and, once sufficiently close, ultimately jump onto the host. However, little is known about host-seeking cues and behavior of T. penetrans.

The aim of this study was to systematically investigate host cues that attract adult T. penetrans in a laboratory setup, with the goal of developing a trap as a potential surveillance tool. Light as a cue was initially explored for T. penetrans by installing a commercially available light trap, originally developed for other flea species, in tungiasis-affected households. Subsequently, visual, chemical, and thermal cues were evaluated using a novel I-shaped 2-choice chamber.

Materials and Methods

Study Sites

The experiments were conducted at the Muhaka Field Station (-4.3242317, 39.5243676) of the International Centre of Insect Physiology and Ecology (icipe) in Msambweni Sub-County, Kwale County, Coastal Kenya. The field studies and soil sample collection were conducted in the surrounding areas between July 2022 and March 2024. Kwale has a tropical savanna climate (Kottek et al. 2006) and with rainy seasons from late March to May and from November to December. High prevalence of tungiasis has been observed in this area (Republic of Kenya Ministry of Health 2014, Elson et al. 2023, Mwai et al. 2023).

Ethical Considerations

The study approval and the research permit were obtained from the Kenya Medical Research Institute Scientific Ethics Review Unit (NON-KEMRI 644). Written informed consent was obtained from the head of the household. Household members affected by tungiasis were referred to community health promoters, who had been provided with benzyl benzoate by the study for treatment, as was common practice in the health department.

Field Phototaxis Experiment with Commercially Available Flea Trap

As a proof of principle that T. penetrans also uses phototaxis, a commercially available blue light flea trap, originally developed for the trapping of cat fleas, Ctenocephalides felis, and dog fleas, Ctenocephalides canis (Earth Pet Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), was tested. The flea trap consisted of 2 blue light-emitting diode (LED) lights, sticky sheet (8 × 9 cm), and batteries (Fig. 1A). Fifteen households with at least one tungiasis-infected household member were enrolled to set the traps after written informed consent was provided. In each house, a pair of the flea traps was set in the evening, one with the lights on and one with the lights off. The traps were set one meter apart on the foot side of the sleeping area of an infected person with T. penetrans (Fig. 1B). The traps were collected in the morning, and the fleas on the sticky sheet were identified by species and sex under a stereo microscope (20× magnification). The wavelengths of the blue light LED were measured by the spectrometer (MRT Corp., Osaka, Japan).

A) Commercially available blue light flea trap developed for cat flea and dog flea. B) A pair of blue light flea traps set in a sleeping area; light was turned on for one trap and kept off for the other.

Two-Choice Laboratory Bioassays for Testing of Putative Tunga Penetrans Attractants

Tunga Penetrans Pupae Preparation

A total of 199 tungiasis-affected households with earthen floors were identified and visited with the guidance of community health promoters on a rolling basis. Tunga penetrans off-host stages (eggs and larvae) were collected from the sleeping area of persons with tungiasis by sweeping soil and dust on the floor following established procedures (Amugune et al. 2022, Matharu et al. 2024). The Berlese-Tullgren funnel extraction method (Matharu et al. 2024) was used to isolate off-host stages from the soil. In the larval stage, T. penetrans was separated from other flea larvae using the method developed by Matharu et al. Tunga penetrans larvae were transferred into petri dishes containing a larval medium of sterilized soil and a pinch of ground fish flakes (Supa Aquatic Supplies Ltd., Sheffield, UK), and kept until pupation at ambient conditions: 17.6 to 30.9 °C (mean 25.3 °C), 42.0 to 99.0% (mean 89.7%) relative humidity, natural light conditions (Amugune et al. 2022, Hyuga et al. 2024, Matharu et al. 2024). Once the larvae pupated, they formed a cocoon, which was checked on daily basis. Cocoons were immediately transferred into a petri dish. Every 2 d a different petri dish was used to age match the fleas. Pupae in cocoons were left to mature for 10 d, at which time they were introduced into the choice bioassays.

Experimental Design

A 2-choice bioassay was developed to evaluate and compare the attractiveness of different light wavelengths, host odors, and heat stimuli (Fig. 2A). The bioassay consisted of 3 connected plastic tubes (each 12 cm long and 4.2 cm in diameter), routinely used to evaluate the susceptibility of mosquitoes to insecticides (World Health Organization 2022). A sticky sheet (KAMOI KAKOSHI Co., Ltd., Okayama, Japan) was inserted to line the wall of the outer 2 tubes. The central tube had a cocoon holder fixed centrally at the bottom (1.4 × 1.4 cm wide and 0.3 cm high, made of filter paper). The 2 choices provided were alternately positioned in the 2 tubes left and right from the center. In each system, 4 to 7 mature T. penetrans cocoons were introduced depending on the availability of pupae at the time. After introducing the cocoons, each bioassay system was placed in a separate dark compartment inside a dark room to avoid light exposure and interference with each other. The conditions were otherwise ambient, with an average temperature of 28.3 °C (24.4 to 31.9 °C) and average relative humidity of 89.2% (47.0 to 99.0%). The system was then left for 10 d before recording the results: the number of emerged adults captured on the sticky sheet on either test side, and the number of emerged adults who stayed in the central tube were recorded. See Supplementary Fig. S1 for preliminary data used for designing the protocol. Each choice bioassay was repeated 16 to 20 times to obtain at least 100 emerged adults for each test. Prior to the test, the bioassay was validated by rotating the positions to confirm there is no directional bias in the bioassay with no sample.

Set-up of 2-choice bioassays. A) The basic set-up consisting of 3 connected WHO tubes. B) Six light colors of LEDs in 5 mm bullet-type and wavelengths spectra. Set-ups of single light assays C) and competitive 2-lights assays D) are shown.

Light Assays

Six different LED colors (5 mm bullet-type) were tested for attractiveness for T. penetrans adults. LEDs had peak wavelengths of 375 nm (ultraviolet), 430 nm (dark blue), 490 nm (blue green), 535 nm (yellow green), 610 nm (orange), and 690 nm (red; Fig. 2B, C). The 375 nm LED was supplied by Nitride Semiconductors. Co., Ltd., Tokushima, Japan; the other LEDs were supplied by Ushio Inc. Tokyo, Japan. The peak wavelengths were confirmed with a spectrophotometer (MRT Corp., Osaka, Japan). The LEDs were powered at a constant current of 20 mA provided by rechargeable batteries that were replaced every 3 d. The LED bulbs were placed 1.3 cm above the bottom at each end of the bioassay tubes. Then the tube opening was closed with black manila paper.

Competitive 2-Lights Assay

After the individual evaluation of T. penetrans attraction to light of different wavelengths, the most attractive colors were determined in choice tests. Five combinations were tested: 375 nm versus 430 nm; 430 nm versus 490 nm; 535 nm versus 490 nm; 610 nm versus 490 nm; 690 nm versus 610 nm (Fig. 2D). Absolute light intensity was not controlled between the light colours, as all LED bulbs operated at a standard forward current of 20 mA. Instead, the original optical power output at forward current of 20 mA was used: 11 mW for 375 nm; 17 mW for 430 nm, 12 mW for 490 nm, 4.0 mW for 535 nm, 8.0 mW for 610 nm, and 4.0 mW for 690 nm.

Odor Assay

As embedded sand fleas are primarily located in the feet, foot odor was collected from 4 volunteers of icipe staff members without tungiasis. They wore polyester socks (97% polyester, 2% elastane, MINISO, Guangzhou, China) for about 12 h during the day after showering with soap. Until the usage, worn socks were packed in aluminum foil and stored frozen in a Ziploc bag for up to 2 d. To set up the experiment, a pair of worn socks and a pair of unworn socks were each placed into separate Ziploc bags (14 × 10 cm) with three 1 cm diameter holes at the bottom to enhance airflow. The zipper part of each bag was positioned over the outside of each end of the bioassay tube and secured to the tubes using wire as shown in Fig. 3A. The socks inside the bags were oriented to face a mesh (Nylon, opening 250 μm) at the end of the bioassay tubes. The mesh was positioned at the same location as the black manila paper used to close the tube opening in the light assays, preventing flea escapes and securing airflow. Worn and unworn socks in each Ziploc bag were alternately allocated to the right or left side. To provide a directed airflow from the socks to the central tube in the bioassay, a fan from a mosquito trap with a diameter of 11.5 cm (Biogents, Regensburg, Germany) was connected to the center point of the bioassay with a silicone tube with an inner diameter of 3 mm. The fan ran continuously 24 h a day for 10 d. Power banks supplying DC 5 V for the fan were replaced daily with minimum interference. The socks were not exchanged during the run time of the experiment to maintain undisturbed operation, assuming fleas have a similar ability as mosquitoes to detect host odor from socks for up to 8 d (Njiru et al. 2006). After the tests, worn socks, and unworn socks were separately washed with odor-less soap and rinsed in water, dried, autoclaved, and stored in Ziploc bags until the next use. In every assay, the tubes were washed with odor-less soap, and the mesh and the Ziploc bag were newly replaced.

Set-up of the odor and heat attraction choice assays. A) Odor assay and B) heat assay with thermal images. Etched foil heaters were supported by a container to be attached to the closed edge of the bioassay.

Heat Assay

Body temperature of mammalian hosts is a cue used by all blood-sucking arthropods. A heat source provided by an etched foil heater (10 cm in diameter, Heat Lab. Corporation, Shizuoka, Japan) was used as a heat attractant. The etched foil heater was attached to the outer ends of the bioassay tubes closed with black manila paper, whereby only one was connected to a battery to provide some warmth to the connected test tube, whilst the opposite side had the heater foil but was not connected to electricity (Fig. 3B). The AA rechargeable batteries supplied 3.6 V, and the etched foil heater reached 45.5 ± 1.41 °C, resulting in temperatures of 38.2 ± 0.96 °C in the test tube versus 28.5 ± 0.43 °C in the opposite non-heated tube for 3 h (Supplementary Fig. S2). For operational reasons, the heating was run 3 times daily, each lasting 3 h, by exchanging batteries. The standard schedule was as follows: 8:30 to 11:30, 11:30 to 14:30, and 14:30 to 17:30, resulting in a total of 9 h of heating per day (3 h times 3 runs). The surface temperatures of the etched foil heater and the test tube were measured using an infra-red thermal imaging camera (HT-A1, Dongguan Xintai Instrument Co. Ltd, Dongguan, China).

Data Analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using R4.4.1 (R Core Team 2022). Wilcoxon signed-rank test (non-parametric) was used to compare flea numbers trapped in the field phototaxis experiment using the exactRankTests package (version 0.9.35), which applies the Shift-Algorithm by Streitberg & Roehmel (Hothorn and Hornik 2022) to handle ties. The emergence rate of the 2-choice bioassay was calculated by dividing the total number of emerged adults by the number of cocoons introduced and multiplying the results by 100 to express it as a percentage. Similarly, the response rate was determined by dividing the total number of captured adults by the total number of emerged adults and multiplying by 100. Binomial generalized linear models were implemented using the stats package in R (version 4.4.1) to analyze the 2-choice bioassays comparing the proportions of adult fleas attracted to the test side of the 2-choice bioassays, where a test cue was installed, to the proportions of adult fleas attracted to the test side of the validation bioassay, where the label of test was alternately designated to either side but there was no cue on either the test or control sides (Bokore et al. 2021). Thus, the dispersion of adult fleas to the test side of the validation bioassay was utilized as a reference to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI). Only adults that emerged and were capable of making a choice were included in the analysis while cocoons from which no adults emerged during the assay were excluded from the analysis. Overdispersion in the binomial generalized linear models was assessed using the performance package (version 0.15.1) in R (Lüdecke et al. 2021). When overdispersion was detected, models were refitted using a quasibinomial distribution by specifying family = quasibinomial.

Results

Field Phototaxis Experiment

Whilst traps were set in 15 households, data from 2 households was excluded from analysis as the traps had been moved by residents. Traps with the light switched on recorded significantly higher numbers of female and male T. penetrans than traps without light (Fig. 4A). Traps operated with the light on captured between 0 and 26 female T. penetrans (median 2, IQR 0 to 5), yielding a total of 54 females, and between 0 and 15 male T. penetrans (median 1, IQR 0 to 2), totaling 34 males from 13 households. In contrast, traps operated with the light off captured between 0 and 2 females (median 0, IQR 0 to 1; total 5) and between 0 and 2 males (median 0, IQR 0 to 1; total 5). Other cohabiting adult fleas, 2 C. felis and one Echidnophaga gallinacea, were captured by light-on trap from 3 households as well (Fig. 4B–D). The blue LED of the flea trap was measured using the spectrophotometer and had a peak wavelength of 460 nm (Fig. 4E).

Two Choice Bioassays

The validation of the bioassay (blank versus blank) indicated random dispersion of freshly emerged T. penetrans (Fig. 5). A summary of all 14 bioassays is shown in Table 1. The overall emergence rate was 91.1% (median 100%, IQR 85.7 to 100%) across 14 bioassays with 231 replicates (each with 4 to 7 cocoons): among 110 to 130 matured pupae in cocoons introduced across the replicates for each bioassay in total, 100 to 111 adults emerged. Out of the emerged adults, overall 98.5% (median 100%, IQR 100 to 100%) made a choice on either side and were captured on sticky sheet. The remaining few percent did not make a choice and remained in the central tube. Among the emerged adults, 59.3% were females and 40.7% were males.

Summary of 2-choice bioassays

All 6 tested LED light colors at peaks of 375 nm, 430 nm, 490 nm, 535 nm, 610 nm, and 690 nm exhibited significant attraction to adult T. penetrans compared to light off (Fig. 5). Across 6 single LED light colors, each bioassay demonstrated an average attraction of 94 to 100% to the light-on stimulus, indicating comparable attraction across the tested wavelengths. Both females and males exhibited a similar response, with only the remaining few percent reacting to the light-off control. Subsequently, each of the 2 adjacent wavelengths was tested to compare the attraction of different LED light colors. The results showed that T. penetrans exhibited a significant attraction to 430 nm over 375 nm, to 490 nm over 610 nm, and to 610 nm over 690 nm (Fig. 5). The comparison of attraction to 430 nm versus 490 nm and 535 nm versus 490 nm indicated that T. penetrans preferred 490 nm over both 430 nm and 535 nm, although the difference was not statistically significant for either wavelength. Similarly, both the worn socks and heating-on sides attracted more than half of the emerged T. penetrans on average; however, the attraction was not statistically significant compared to the dispersion observed in the blank validation assay.

Discussion

In this study, a novel 2-choice bioassay was developed to safely and systematically evaluate the attraction of newly emerged T. penetrans adults to host cues by handling cocoons instead of adult fleas. The results demonstrated that emerged T. penetrans adults were attracted to light stimuli across a broad wavelength range between 375 nm and 690 nm. Among the tested LED light colors, wavelengths from dark blue to yellow green (peaks between 430 to 535 nm) elicited better attraction in both sexes. These findings highlight potential visual attractants that could be integrated into future trapping methods or control systems.

In the field trial, the commercially available flea light trap demonstrated significant attraction to adult T. penetrans, indicating their positive phototaxis (movement toward a light source) to blue light with a peak of 460 nm. While the efficacy of trapping was not assessed due to the lack of a method for estimating adult flea population size within individual households, these findings may support the potential use of light trapping in field applications. In addition to capturing T. penetrans, the blue light flea traps with light on also caught cohabiting adults of other flea species, namely C. felis and E. gallinacea. The presence of these fleas corresponds to descriptions of off-host flea larvae recovered from soil samples collected in households affected by tungiasis in the same area (Matharu et al. 2024). The 2 additional species, besides T. penetrans, are likely house fleas commonly collected by light trap in human dwellings across East Africa, as reported in Tanzania (Kilonzo et al. 1992), Uganda (Borchert et al. 2012), and Kenya (Otiang et al. 2021). Accordingly, these species could be co-targets of light trapping alongside T. penetrans in the future monitoring.

For the laboratory experiment, the bioassay apparatus was customized using WHO plastic tubes originally designed for mosquito testing. This adaptation enabled a consistent 2-choice setup in the I-shaped configuration for evaluating responses of T. penetrans. The use of WHO tubes helped overcome challenges commonly encountered in resource-limited settings, where access to specialized materials, equipment, or technical skills may be limited. In the developed bioassay, the overall emergence rate was 91.1% and the loss from pupae to adults was less than 10%. This was comparable to previously reported emergence rate of C. felis from cocoons, which range from 75 to 85% under ambient conditions (Yiguan et al. 2016). Thus, initiating the bioassay from the pupal stage offered a safer approach for testing adult T. penetrans attractants while ensuring a reasonable recovery rate. Furthermore, 98.5% of emerged adults made a choice during the assays, which was a high response rate. In the validation assay using a blank versus blank setup, no directional bias in the apparatus was observed as adults dispersed randomly between the 2 sides. Overall, the developed bioassay proved to be a reliable method for evaluating responses of T. penetrans and enabled systematic assessment of attraction to candidate stimuli.

Tunga penetrans has been shown to have a visual sensing range similar to that of humans, responding to light wavelengths from 375 nm of ultraviolet to 690 nm of red. The finding from T. penetrans is almost equal to that from C. felis (Crum et al. 1974) and supported by a study suggesting that long-wavelength opsins in the order of Siphonaptera (fleas) are sensitive to light from green to red part of the spectrum (Taylor et al. 2005). Opsins are light-sensitive proteins involved in spectrum sensitivity and are typically found in the visual organs of insects, such as compound eyes or ocelli in adults (Guignard et al. 2022). The eyes of adult Siphonaptera consist solely of ocelli located laterally on both sides of the head (Kettle 1995). Therefore, T. penetrans may possess opsins sensitive to a relatively wide spectrum within their ocelli. To be exact, T. penetrans could detect light intensity, but not color vision, as ocelli do not provide detailed visual information like pictorial images due to their function as simple photoreceptors (Krapp 2009, Lazzari 2009).

On the other hand, the results of the competitive 2 lights assay showed varying attraction rates with different LED wavelengths. In the comparisons, the following wavelengths demonstrated an ordinal relationship between attraction and optical power output: LED light with peaks at 490 nm (12 mW) > 610 nm (8 mW) > 690 nm (4 mW); 430 nm (17 mW) > 375 nm (11 mW). In contrast, no significant differences in attraction were observed in 430 nm (17 mW) versus 490 nm (12 mW) and 535 nm (4 mW) versus 490 nm (12 mW). It could not be concluded how the interaction between wavelength and light intensity contributed to the observed differences in attraction, due to the different optical power output across the different light colors. However, given that not all LEDs produce uniform light intensity at a constant current, the evaluation of attraction under these conditions reflects a more practical assessment based on actual electrical usage scenarios. Under constant current conditions, wavelengths in the range of 430 to 535 nm elicited stronger attraction in T. penetrans, suggesting their potential for light-trapping purposes. Furthermore, these laboratory findings were consistent with the results of the field phototaxis trial using the commercially available blue light flea trap, where the 460 nm wavelength proved attractive to T. penetrans.

Like other blood-feeding insects, T. penetrans might use host-derived chemical and thermal cues for host localization. In this study, responses to host odor and body temperature were slightly higher than those to the blank control although no significant attraction to these stimuli was observed. This might be partly explained by the bioassay design. In the host odor assay, the airflow was intended to mimic a Y-shaped olfactometer commonly used in insect behavioral studies, but may not have been fully optimized in the I-shaped design. Positive responses may be detectable under further optimized airflow and air current conditions, and by exploring key parameters such as sock materials, sock storage methods, or socks worn by individuals with tungiasis. In the host body temperature assay, the heat source was active for only 9 h per day due to safety constraints, potentially resulting in random responses and thereby limiting the detection of a potential effect. Given that both host odor and heat are established cues for host-seeking in other hematophagous insects (Lazzari 2009, 2019), further studies under optimized conditions could provide more insight into the role of these cues in T. penetrans. For instance, the odor assay could be further developed to evaluate flea responses to other chemical cues, such as pheromones that are believed to be secreted by embedded virgin female T. penetrans fleas to attract males for successful mating, as well as other females, potentially promoting aggregated infections that increase mating opportunities (Eisele et al. 2003, Witt et al. 2004). Moreover, future studies could focus on more field-relevant environments by comparing the efficacy of LED light with host cues, or by assessing the attractiveness of light-based trapping in the presence of natural hosts as adult T. penetrans flea trapping would primarily be implemented indoors particularly in the sleeping areas of individuals with tungiasis, where off-host stages are likely to occur and adult fleas subsequently emerge (Linardi et al. 2010, Matharu et al. 2024).

In the bioassays, while a high response rate of emerged adults was recorded, a certain percentage of adults (a few percent of females and several percent of males) did not make any choice (Table 1), possibly because they had recently emerged and required more time to become responsive. The higher proportion of nonresponsive males compared with females may be explained by differences in the timing of adult emergence by sex: females emerged between Days 10 and 14 after cocooning, whereas males emerged between Days 12 and 19, leaving a minimum of only one day before the assay concluded on Day 20 (Supplementary Fig. S1). Additionally, female T. penetrans may require a shorter post-emergence acclimation period to be responsive to host cues, as they must embed themselves within the host’s skin to await males for copulation (Nagy et al. 2007, Thielecke and Feldmeier 2013). Furthermore, seasonal environmental factors such as temperature may have influenced their maturation rate and delayed emergence.

In conclusion, the present study investigated visual, chemical and thermal cues involved in host-seeking behavior of adult T. penetrans. It was found that light stimuli across a broad spectrum from ultraviolet to red are attractive, with dark blue to yellow-green wavelengths demonstrating a stronger response. These findings provide a foundation for the development of light-based trapping systems for the flea. While the commercially available blue light flea trap successfully captured T. penetrans in tungiasis-affected households, providing supportive evidence, further investigations are necessary to evaluate the effectiveness of such trapping approaches systematically under field conditions, particularly in the presence of natural hosts. Additionally, future research should explore other potential host cues, such as host odor, body temperature, and carbon dioxide, for comprehensive understanding of their host-seeking behavior.
