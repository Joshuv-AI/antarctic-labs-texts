Curcumin-loaded gelatin ethosomal gel: A novel approach for anti-inflammatory efficacy
Authors: Varsha Rawat, Smriti Dewangan, Khomendra Kumar Sarwa, Moonmun Dhara, Shakti Ketan Prusty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The research focuses on enhancing the delivery of curcumin's potent anti-inflammatory properties through a novel approach using a curcumin-loaded gelatine ethosomal gel. Curcumin, known for its therapeutic potential, faces challenges due to poor solubility and bioavailability. The method involved dissolving curcumin, phospholipids, and gelatin to form ethosomes within the gelatin matrix using sonication. Various parameters were evaluated including vesicle size, morphology, entrapment efficiency, drug permeation, stability, and anti-inflammatory activity. The optimized curcumin gelatine ethosomes effectively encapsulated the drug, achieving an entrapment efficiency of 89.52% ​± ​0.1% and substantial drug loading capacity ranging from 75.75% ​± ​0.3% to 85.64% ​± ​0.1%. The formulation exhibited stability with a small vesicle size of 122.08 ​nm, a zeta potential of −36.2 ​mV indicating good stability, and spherical ethosome morphology. TEM images confirmed uniform drug distribution within the ethosome without precipitation or unwanted aggregates. The gel exhibited suitable characteristics for topical application such as clear texture, neutral odor, appropriate pH (5.1–5.6), and viscosity (26,000–58,000 ​Pa ​s). It also showed excellent spreadability, homogeneity, minimal irritancy, and pseudo plastic behaviour with sustained drug release (up to 94%) over 24 ​h. Stability testing and acute toxicity studies confirmed the safety and efficacy of the gel, while anti-inflammatory activity was demonstrated through carrageenan-induced rat paw edema model. The findings suggest that this curcumin-loaded gelatine ethosomal gel holds promise in revolutionizing inflammation therapy by improving efficacy, patient compliance, stability, controlled release, and bioavailability, thereby paving the way for innovative approaches in inflammation management.

Introduction

Curcumin, a bioactive compound derived from the rhizome of the turmeric plant (Curcuma longa), has garnered significant attention for its potential therapeutic benefits, particularly in managing inflammation. Its anti-inflammatory properties arise from its ability to modulate multiple molecular targets, including the inhibition of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) activation, which reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory genes and mediators such as cytokines, chemokines, and enzymes like cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and lipoxygenase (LOX) [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Despite these benefits, curcumin faces major challenges related to its poor solubility, bioavailability, and stability, which limit its therapeutic effectiveness in reaching target tissues and inflammatory sites [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

Ethosomes, lipid-based vesicular carriers, offer a promising solution to these limitations by enhancing the penetration of active compounds through the skin. They enable higher concentrations of curcumin to reach target tissues, thereby augmenting its anti-inflammatory effects. The gelatin matrix, derived from collagen, further contributes to the formulation by providing a biocompatible, biodegradable, and sustained-release medium. Gelatin contains amino acids such as glycine, proline, and hydroxyproline, which have been reported to possess intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties [13]. These amino acids may interact with inflammatory mediators or pathways, further influencing curcumin's efficacy in inhibiting NF-κB activation.

By combining gelatin with ethosomes, a synergistic drug delivery system can be achieved, capitalizing on the advantages of both carriers. While gelatin provides sustained release and structural stability, ethosomes enhance skin permeation and improve the solubility of hydrophobic drugs like curcumin. This innovative combination is expected to offer superior therapeutic efficacy compared to traditional formulations or single-component systems [[14], [15], [16]]. The approach aims to overcome key formulation challenges associated with curcumin, ethosomal carriers, and gelatin, including stability issues during storage, immunogenicity risks, and curcumin's instability under environmental stressors [[17], [18], [19]].

The curcumin-loaded gelatin ethosomal gel is designed to address these issues synergistically, providing a stable and effective topical system for the management of inflammatory skin disorders. Comparative evaluations against standard therapies such as corticosteroids, NSAIDs, and other curcumin formulations will highlight its advantages in terms of efficacy, safety, and bioavailability. Moreover, the gel's potential for topical delivery makes it suitable for treating inflammatory conditions such as dermatitis, eczema, psoriasis, and acne [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]].

New therapeutic formulations like the curcumin-loaded gelatin ethosomal gel require comprehensive evaluation to establish their safety, stability, and therapeutic performance. Quality control parameters, physicochemical characterization, and in vitro release kinetics are essential to ensure reproducibility and performance optimization. Also, in vivo studies will validate the formulation's anti-inflammatory efficacy and biocompatibility. Overall, this study aims to develop and evaluate a curcumin-loaded gelatin ethosomal gel as a novel, safe, and patient-friendly formulation for effective inflammation management, combining enhanced bioavailability with controlled release and improved patient compliance.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Curcumin, Gelatin (type A), soyalecithin, carbopol 940 (molecular weight ∼3,000,000 ​g/mol) and dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 12–14 kDawere purchased from Himedia Chemicals in Mumbai, India. Other chemicals were purchased from the local vendor. All chemicals employed in the study met the standards of analytical grade.

Physicochemical properties of curcumin

Curcumin (C21H20O6; MW: 368.38 ​g/mol) is a hydrophobic polyphenolic compound with logP ∼3.29, melting point 183 ​°C, and poor aqueous solubility (<0.01 ​mg/mL). It is soluble in ethanol, acetone, and DMSO, and exhibits λmax at 425 ​nm in ethanol. These properties justify the need for vesicular delivery systems like ethosomes to improve its solubility and bioavailability [14].

Development of calibration curve of curcumin

To prepare a stock solution, 100 ​mg of curcumin was dissolved in 10 ​ml of ethanol, resulting in a concentration of 10 ​mg/ml; various aliquots from this stock solution were then transferred to a series of 10 ​ml volumetric flasks and diluted with ethanol to create working standard solutions with concentrations range from 1 to 5 ​mg/ml. The maximum absorbance (λmax) of the drug was determined, and a standard curve was plotted by graphing concentration against absorbance.

Preparation of curcumin loaded gelatine ethosomes

Curcumin was dissolved in ethanol (0.1%–5% w/v). Soya lecithin (0.2%–0.4% w/v) was dispersed in a hydroalcoholic mixture (ethanol:water, 1:1) with continuous magnetic stirring (200–300 ​rpm) and mild probe sonication to ensure uniform dispersion, since lecithin is not freely soluble in water. A 1% w/v gelatin solution was prepared separately in distilled water at 40 ​°C. The curcumin solution, lecithin dispersion, and gelatin solution were combined under continuous stirring to obtain a homogeneous mixture. Distilled water was gradually added to make up the final volume to 50 ​mL, resulting in a colloidal ethosomal suspension (Fig. 1). The suspension was subjected to probe sonication at 20 ​kHz frequency, 50% amplitude for 5 ​min with intermittent cooling to avoid overheating. After sonication, the dispersion was allowed to equilibrate and cool to room temperature. The resulting ethosomes theoretically contained 2 ​mg/mL of curcumin (100 ​mg in 50 ​mL at 100% entrapment). The suspension was stored at 4 ​°C until further use. Free (unentrapped) curcumin was separated by ultracentrifugation at 15,000 ​rpm for 1 ​h at 4 ​°C, and the pellet containing entrapped drug was re-dispersed in phosphate buffer pH 5.5 for characterization. Characterization of curcumin-loaded gelatine ethosomes

UV–vis spectroscopy studies

To characterize curcumin-loaded gelatine ethosomes, UV–Vis spectroscopy is employed using an instrument such as the UV-1800 from Shimadzu. The UV–Vis spectrophotometer was calibrated using a blank solution of pure ethanol to ensure accurate baseline readings. After calibration, the curcumin-loaded gelatin ethosome sample was placed in a quartz cuvette, and the absorbance spectrum was recorded across a wavelength range of 200 ​nm–800 ​nm. Any observed shifts in the spectrum were noted, particularly peaks at 417 ​nm and 432 ​nm, which were adjusted to 425 ​nm and 430 ​nm, respectively, to account for interactions within the ethosomal environment [25]. This spectral analysis provided insights into curcumin's encapsulation within the ethosomes and its stability within the gelatin matrix, thereby confirming successful loading. For additional accuracy, this section should be referenced with appropriate literature sources to validate the absorption peaks [[26], [27]].

Entrapment efficiency (EE)

The entrapment efficiency (EE) of curcumin-loaded gelatine ethosomes was determined after separating the unentrapped (free) drug. The ethosomal suspension was centrifuged at 15,000 ​rpm for 1 ​h at 4 ​°C to obtain the pellet (entrapped curcumin) and the supernatant (free curcumin). The clear supernatant was collected, diluted with ethanol, and analyzed using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu) at λmax 425 ​nm. In this method, the earlier overestimation (where 1 ​mL was assumed to contain 10 ​mg of drug) was corrected. Based on the preparation, the theoretical concentration of curcumin in the ethosomal suspension is 2 ​mg/mL (100 ​mg curcumin in 50 ​mL, assuming 100% entrapment). A calibration curve of curcumin in ethanol was used to quantify the free drug in the supernatant. The EE% was calculated using the equation:(Eq.1)EE(%)=(Amountofcurcuminsupernatant/Totalamountofcurcuminused)×100

Drug loading

To determine the drug loading capacity of the ethosomes, a known quantity (10 ​mg) of freeze-dried ethosomal formulation was accurately weighed and dissolved in 10 ​mL ethanol to completely release the entrapped curcumin, resulting in a final concentration of 1000 ​μg/mL. After appropriate dilution (10 ​μg/mL), the absorbance of the solution was measured at 425 ​nm using a UV spectrophotometer (Shimadzu 1800). The amount of curcumin present was determined from the calibration curve [28]. Drug loading percentage (DL%) was calculated using the following equation:(Eq. 2)Drugcontent(%)=Amountofdrugincurcumin/AmountofcurcuminX100

Vesicle size and zeta potential

Laser light diffractometry was used to assess the vesicle size and zeta potential of the produced ethosomes using the Malvern Zetasizer ZS 90 (Malvern Instrument Inc., UK). Scattered light was detected at a 173° angle, and the hydrodynamic vesicle size was calculated based on intensity distribution. Measurements were performed in buffer (pH 5.5) at a constant temperature of 25.4 ​°C to ensure consistency. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the mean and standard deviation were calculated [29].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The transmission electron microscope (TEM) technique employed for investigating the shape and aggregation of optimized ethosomes was performed with a JEOL-JEM-1011 microscope (JEOL-Tokyo, Japan). A drop of each sample was applied to a copper grid coated with carbon film and the resultant construct was then air-dried. Following this, the films were negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid solution and air-dried under room temperature. After these steps, the samples investigated via TEM [30].

Preparation of ethosomal gel

Curcumin-loaded gelatine ethosomes were incorporated into a gel base to enhance topical applicability. Carbopol 940 (0.5 ​g) was dispersed in 100 ​mL of deionized water and stirred at 400–600 ​rpm for 4–6 ​h to allow complete hydration and swelling of polymer chains, ensuring optimal viscosity. After complete hydration, the optimized curcumin-loaded gelatinethosomes were slowly incorporated into the hydrated Carbopol dispersion under gentle stirring to obtain a homogeneous mixture. The pH was carefully adjusted to 5.0–5.5 using triethanolamine (TEA) added dropwise with constant mixing, thereby avoiding localized precipitation and ensuring uniform gel neutralization. Characterization of ethosomal gel

Appearance

The evaluation of ethosomal gel appearance, including colour and order, as well as its feel after use, was conducted through the assessment of organoleptic characteristics. This involved standardized methods to objectively analyze the visual and sensory properties of the gel, contributing to a comprehensive characterization of its overall appearance and user experience [32].

pH

The pH of the ethosomal gel was measured using a digital pH meter (L1-612 pH Analyzer). The meter was calibrated before each use, and the glass electrode was immersed into the ethosomal gel. Three readings were taken for each sample, and the average value was calculated [33].

Viscosity

Viscosity of the ethosomal gel was determined using a Brookfield digital viscometer (Model LT-730) with spindle no. 3. Measurements were taken at rotational speeds of 0.5, 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 ​rpm under room temperature conditions [34].

Extrudability

Extrudability refers to how easily the gel can be squeezed or dispensed from its container. Ethosomal gel was filled into standard capped collapsible aluminium tubes and securely sealed by crimping at the tube's end. The initial weights of these tubes were recorded to establish a baseline. To assess extrudability, the tubes were placed between two glass slides and secured, with a weight of 500 ​g applied on top. The tube cap was then removed, and the amount of extruded gel was meticulously collected and weighed. The percentage of the extruded gel was then calculated [35].(>90%extrudability:Excellent,>80%extrudability:Good,and>70%extrudability:Fair

Spreadability

Adopting a quantitative approach for tests related to spreadability can significantly improve the robustness of assessments. The spreadability of the ethosomal gel was measured using the parallel plate technique. A sample of 0.5 ​g of the gel was placed within a marked 1 ​cm diameter circle on a glass plate. Another glass plate was positioned on top, and a weight of 500 ​g was applied to the upper plate, allowing it to rest for 5 ​min. The increase in diameter due to the gel's spreading was then observed and recorded [36,37].

In-vitro drug release

The in-vitro release study was carried out using a diffusion cell assembly consisting of a donor compartment, receptor compartment (20 ​mL), and a sampling port. A dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) of 12–14 ​kDa was placed between the donor and receptor compartments. The MWCO indicates the pore size of the membrane; molecules larger than this range are retained, while smaller molecules such as curcumin (molecular weight 368 ​Da) are able to diffuse through. The receptor compartment was filled with 20 ​mL phosphate buffer (pH 5.5) and maintained at 37 ​± ​0.5 ​°C with continuous stirring at 50 ​rpm. A total of 5 ​g of the prepared ethosomal gel (equivalent to ∼2 ​mg of curcumin) was applied uniformly onto the membrane in the donor compartment. At predetermined intervals (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 24 ​h), 2 ​mL samples were withdrawn from the receptor compartment and replaced with fresh buffer to maintain sink conditions. The samples were diluted appropriately and analyzed using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (λmax 425 ​nm) to determine curcumin release. The in-vitro drug release profile of the formulated gel was compared with a marketed Diclofenac sodium BP 1% gel [38].

Ex-vivo diffusion study

The ex-vivo skin permeation study was performed using Franz diffusion cell with an effective diffusion area of 2 ​cm2. Excised porcine ear skin was obtained from a local slaughterhouse and placed between the donor and receptor compartments of the diffusion cell, with the stratum corneum oriented toward the donor compartment. A curcumin-loaded Gelatin based ethosomal gel was applied uniformly to the donor side in direct contact with the skin. The receptor compartment was filled with phosphate buffer (pH 5.5) as the diffusion medium, maintained at 37 ​± ​1 ​°C to simulate physiological conditions, and stirred continuously at 50 ​rpm using a magnetic stirrer. Samples were withdrawn at predetermined intervals and analyzed using a UV spectrophotometer. To maintain sink conditions, fresh buffer was promptly added to the receptor compartment after each sampling. The cumulative percent release of the drug was plotted against time to generate the release profile. This experimental setup allowed for the assessment of curcumin release kinetics from the Gelatin loaded ethosomal gel formulation across the porcine ear skin barrier [39].

Stability

The storage stability of ethosomal gel was assessed by evaluating pH, spreadability, and drug content under three distinct temperature conditions, humidity, and light exposure. Ethosomal gels were stored under ICH-recommended conditions: room temperature (25 ​± ​2 ​°C/60 ​± ​5% RH), accelerated conditions (40 ​± ​2 ​°C/75 ​± ​5% RH), and refrigerated conditions (2–8 ​°C). Evaluations were carried out at initial (0 month), 1 month, 2 months, and 3 months intervals. These time points provided insight into the long-term stability of the gel and ensured that the formulation maintained its physicochemical characteristics and therapeutic potential during storage [40].

Anti-inflammatory activity of ethosomal gel

Experimental animal

Wistar albino rats (male and female; adult) weighing 120–160 ​g were procured in the animal house of the School of Pharmaceutical Sciences (SPS), SOA (Deemed to be) University, Bhubaneswar (Reg. No. IAEC/SPS/SOA/106/2022) and all animals were ethically approved as per the guidelines of the institutional animal ethical committee (IAEC). Animals were maintained in well-controlled conditions, in a polycarbonate cage, with a 12-h light/dark cycle, a temperature of 22 ​± ​2 ​°C, and relative humidity of 45% ​± ​5%.During the experimental duration, the rats were supplied with food that had been approved by the ethical committee of the institute and were allowed to access water on ad libitum basis. The cages had been levelled correctly and all animals were taken care of in compliance with the Principles of Laboratory Animal Care (NIH,1985).

Carrageenan-induced rat paw edema

The anti-inflammatory effect of the ethosomal gel was evaluated in a study using a carrageenan-induced rat paw edema model. Ethosomal gel was topically applied to the rat paws after the induction of inflammation with carrageenan [41,42]. This well-established method is used to assess anti-inflammatory properties of the formulations. Rats weighing between 120 and 130 ​g, both genders, were randomly selected after a 12-h fast. They were divided into five groups, each consisting of five animals (n ​= ​5/group). The treatment groups received ethosomal gel, along with a standard drug, Diclofenac Diphenylamine BP 1.16% w/w equivalent to Diclofenac sodium BP 1% gel (VoveranEmulgel- Novartis India Limited) positive control group [43]. Inflammation was induced by subcutaneously injecting 100 ​μl of carrageenan (1% v/v in saline water) into the sub-planter tissue of the right hind paw, and 100 ​μl of normal saline into the left hind paw of each animal. The negative control group received carrageenan without any drug administration [44]. After 15 ​min from the induction of inflammation, the rats were treated with formulated gels and standard drugs as per the groups.

Readings were recorded at 1-h intervals following the carrageenan injection, and paw diameter measurements were taken before and at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 h post-injection using the cotton thread method [45,46]. The anti-inflammatory activity of the treated groups was quantified by expressing the percentage reduction in edema compared to the positive control, calculated using the provided formula [47,48].Percentage(%)inhibition=Meandifferenceinpawedema.thecontrol−Meandifferenceinpawedema,treatedDifferenceinedema,controlX100

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data were expressed as mean ​± ​standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). The differences between multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test to determine statistical significance between the test and control groups. A value of p ​< ​0.05 was considered statistically significant. For in vivo anti-inflammatory studies, the percentage inhibition of paw edema was calculated and compared among groups to evaluate the efficacy of the formulations.

Curcumin showed the λmaxof 425 ​nm whereas when curcumin is loaded into gelatine ethosomes, the λmaxwas found to be 430 ​nm, a little bathochromic shift which was attributed by the interactions between curcumin, gelatin, and the lipid components of the ethosome.

Entrapment efficiency and drug loading

The optimized formulation exhibited high entrapment efficiency (EE) of 89.52% ​± ​0.33%. This can be attributed to the lipophilic nature of curcumin, which favors partitioning into the phospholipid bilayer of ethosomes, thereby enhancing entrapment within the lipid matrix. Variations in entrapment efficiency across different formulations (Table 3) were observed, with increased ethanol content likely improving the solubilization and interaction of curcumin with phospholipids, resulting in higher drug retention. Optimization of ethanol concentration, drug quantity, and solvent volume contributed to maximizing encapsulation efficiency. The ethosomes also demonstrated a notably high drug loading (DL) capacity, ranging from 75.75% to 85.64%. The maximum DL was observed in formulation EF1 (85.64%). Such high values can be explained by the strong affinity of hydrophobic curcumin for the lipid bilayers of ethosomes, minimizing drug loss during preparation and centrifugation. Moreover, the gelatin matrix surrounding ethosomes provided additional stabilization, preventing drug leakage. Interestingly, process variables such as homogenization and sonication had little impact on DL, whereas the concentration of phospholipids played a more significant role in determining the final drug loading capacity. Vesicle size and zeta potential

The release rate and extent of drug release from lipid vesicles are significantly influenced by their vesicle size, while formulation stability is closely related to the zeta potential. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was employed to determine the vesicle size, a critical parameter in designing an effective drug delivery system [51,52]. As per analysis conducted using a Zetasizer (Fig. 3A), the ethosomes exhibited an average vesicle size of 122.08 ​± ​9.66 ​nm. The reduced vesicle size facilitates drug release through diffusion and surface erosion, and the formation of drug–phospholipid complexes further enhances their ability to cross physiological barriers. Colloidal dispersions with zeta potential values exceeding ±30 ​mV are generally considered physically stable. In the present ethosomal formulation, the zeta potential was measured at −36.2 ​mV (Fig. 3B), confirming good stability. A high absolute zeta potential (either negative or positive) prevents particle aggregation through electrostatic repulsion and indicates consistent dispersion of ethosomes. Our optimized formulation therefore demonstrates a favorable combination of small vesicle size and high negative zeta potential, making it a highly stable and effective carrier for curcumin delivery.Fig. 3(A) Vesicle size distribution of ethosomes; (B) zeta potential measurement of the formulation.Fig. 3

(A) Vesicle size distribution of ethosomes; (B) zeta potential measurement of the formulation.

Stability

Limitations

This study has certain limitations, including a small sample size for animal experiments, short-term stability evaluation, and the absence of clinical studies. Also, only one animal inflammation model was used, which may not fully represent complex human inflammatory pathways. Future studies with larger cohorts, extended stability periods, multiple disease models, and clinical validation are required to strengthen the translational potential of this formulation.

Conclusion

The study successfully developed a curcumin-loaded gelatin ethosomal gel as a novel topical formulation for anti-inflammatory therapy. The optimized system exhibited high entrapment efficiency, small vesicle size, and good stability, leading to sustained drug release and significant anti-inflammatory activity. The combination of gelatin and ethosomes provided a synergistic platform that enhanced curcumin's stability, permeation, and bioavailability, addressing its conventional formulation challenges. This hybrid vesicular gel represents an innovative, patient-friendly approach for managing inflammatory conditions and holds promise for further clinical translation and development in advanced topical drug delivery.
