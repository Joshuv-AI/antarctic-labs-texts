Turbulent isopycnal mixing dominates thermohaline transformations of intermediate ocean waters
Authors: Florian Sévellec, Nicolas Kolodziejczyk, Esther Portela
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The ocean, because of its ability to store heat and as the biggest mediator of water exchanges on Earth, modulates the climate from years to centuries. By combining Water Mass transformation and the Temporal-Residual-Mean frameworks, together with a direct estimation of turbulent diffusivity (computed through an artificial intelligence method), we determine temperature and salinity transformations along density surfaces. Because isopycnal transformations barely alter the density field, they remain hidden from the ocean dynamics. Here we unveil these hidden, isopycnal transformations from in situ observations at 1000-m depth. We show that these isopycnal transformations are 5 times as large as their diapycnal counterpart and are occurring for ~ 67% in the Southern Ocean. Our estimations stress the role of the spatially-varying and anisotropic characteristics of isopycnal diffusion, often not considered in ocean models. This has major consequences for our understanding of the role of the ocean in response to anthropogenic climate changes.

Introduction

Ocean circulation transports a vast amount of water over the globe (up to 150 Sv, where 1 Sv ≡ 106 m3 s−1, in the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, for instance)1. This large-scale ocean circulation is also associated with transport of heat (up to 1.5 PW northward at 20°N)2 and freshwater (around 0.5 Sv southward at 35°N in the North Atlantic)3. The former is a fundamental component of the climate equilibrium and adjustment by transporting the excess of heat from the tropics to the poles. Whereas the latter indirectly reflects the geographical pattern of evaporation, precipitation, river discharge, and ice melting, all taking part in the water cycle. In particular, the ocean is the most important region of precipitation and evaporation (i.e., 75% and 85%, respectively), making it particularly relevant for the Earth water cycle4.

Beyond this equilibrated view, the Earth is experiencing changes since the pre-industrial era, with a significant amount of anthropogenically-induced greenhouse gazes released into the atmosphere5. As a consequence, the surface atmospheric temperature is increasing5, despite a significant mitigation by the ocean, with an ocean absorption of about 90% of the Earth’s heat excess5–7. Together with this warming, the water cycle is also affected8. This is reflected by changes in the evaporation and precipitation patterns and amplitudes5. These changes, either directly (when over the ocean) or indirectly through runoffs (when over land), are imprinted in the ocean freshwater transport9,10 through, for instance, modification of the ocean salinity distribution11. Hence, the ocean heat and freshwater transports are central actors of the Earth equilibrium and climate changes.

Mesoscale eddies are ubiquitous worldwide in the ocean12 and, unlike the mean transport, they contribute to the heat and freshwater transport in two-fold. Firstly, equivalently to the laminar component, they move ocean water properties (i.e., its temperature and salinity) in a conserving manner within the ocean. Secondly, eddies also induce a transformation of these properties through turbulent diffusion13 (e.g., mixing cold and warm waters or salty and fresh ones). Transformations of seawater properties can occur either through interactions with the atmosphere at the ocean surface or through turbulent diffusion in the ocean interior. Whereas the former is easier to etimate8,14, the latter has always been more difficult to assess and is often deduced using inverse methodology15–18. Assessing the role of turbulent eddy diffusion for heat and freshwater transports and for the transformation of seawater properties is a timely question of ocean and climate sciences, as demonstrated by numerous numerical model studies19–21.

There has been a large range of studies on Water Mass Transformations22–28. These studies often assess the transformations in terms of temperature and salinity changes, without specifically separating the respective role of isopycnal and diapycnal transformations. In parallel, the role of diapycnal diffusion on temperature and salinity transformations in the ocean interior has been well established16,29. More recently, studies have suggested the key role of isopycnal processes on heat and freshwater uptakes30,31, opening the possibility of an important role of the isopycnal diffusion. This has been further confirmed as potentially important in the North Atlantic for the maintenance of the mean temperature and salinity structure32. However, these studies rely on either numerical models or inverse methods, both intrinsically depending on a chosen closure scheme (and sometimes imposed parameters). Hence, it is key to use direct observations to assess whether isopycnal or diapycnal diffusion is dominant in the ocean interior. Indeed, in ocean science, where reproducible controlled experiments are not possible, the use of direct observations is the only way to validate or invalidate hypotheses derived from these numerical or inverse modeling approaches.

In this work, we use direct observations to assess the changes and transformations of temperature and salinity induced by mesoscale eddy turbulence, which acts as an equivalent isopycnal turbulent diffusion. We show that, at 1000 m depth, these temperature and salinity changes mainly occurred in the Southern Ocean and along the Gulf Stream. These isopycnal changes induce temperature and salinity transformations significantly more important than their diapycnal equivalents. We also conclude that the spatially variable and anisotropic nature of the isopycnal turbulent diffusion is keys for an accurate estimation of the temperature and salinity transformations.

Results

The major difficulty of obtaining robust, direct estimations of heat and freshwater transformations by eddies comes from the need of knowing turbulent diffusive coefficients independently of temperature and salinity fields (to avoid the dependence of the results to a closure scheme)33,34. Here, to circumvent this difficulty, we have used the recently estimated diffusivity coefficients35 derived from Argo float deep displacements36. This dataset has been obtained using a class of data-driven Artificial Intelligence methods: the Analog Method37,38. There, the spatially varying and anisotropic diffusivity coefficients were estimated through local tracer dispersion induced by a turbulent velocity field reconstructed from deep Argo displacements. This dataset provides the full diffusivity tensor, explicitly acknowledging the anisotropy, rather than a single diffusivity coefficient, as most commonly done33,34,39. These resulting diffusivity coefficients are strong where the flow is strong (Fig. 1d, e), but with a slight spatial offset with the core of the currents40,41, hence with the largest density gradients. Effect of isopycnal turbulent diffusion

In the present study, we assess the global effect of the turbulent diffusion on temperature and salinity fields. However, because of observational constraints (i.e., the typical parking depth of Argo float deep displacements), our study focuses at 1000 m depth. This depth is particularly relevant for estimating heat and freshwater transports and seawater property transformations for two reasons. (1) It is close to the depth of the maximum of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (depth of no-motion for this circulation, as estimated at 26.5°N in the North Atlantic by RAPID)45. (2) It is the depth with the largest contrast in salinity46,47, but also includes signatures of the coldest waters47 (Fig. 1).

The transformation of seawater properties works hand in hand with the concept of Water Masses48. Water Masses are volumes of water with consistent seawater properties (Fig. 1f) that are set through ocean-atmosphere exchange at the ocean surface, where water masses originate. They then spread within the ocean through advection and mixing and are transformed by the latter. At 1000 m depth, we will consider three intermediate water masses: Red Sea–Persian Gulf Intermediate Water (RSPGIW) formed in the Red Sea and Persian Gulf49, the Mediterranean Water50 (MW) reaching the Atlantic through the Strait of Gibraltar, and the AntArctic Intermediate Water51 (AAIW) intersecting the 1000-m depth along the Polar Front52. We will also consider more dense Water Masses, namely the North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW), its transformed southern extension, the Circumpolar Deep Water53 (CDW), and the Antarctic Bottom Water54 (AABW). Despite not being their depth of maximum intrusion, these Water Masses are present at 1000 m depth in the high latitudes of the North Atlantic and of the Southern Ocean (Fig. 1f).

To estimate the impact of the turbulent diffusion on the temperature and salinity of these water masses, we follow the Temporal-Residual-Mean framework (see Methods – Isopycnal Turbulent Transformation Framework)13. This framework disentangles advective and mixing effects when eddy turbulence is averaged. This allows for an accurate attribution of eddy-induced transport and transformation. Also, this framework acknowledges that transformations act within isopycnal layers as epineutral flux (Fig. 2). To this purpose averaging are done along-isopycnal, so that fictitious diapycnal transport, due to incorrect estimates of the isopycnal slopes, can be limited55. This transformation-induced process has almost no signature on the density field (except for the weak cabbeling and thermobaricity components, although they remain crucial for the density transformations56), nor pressure, nor circulation. As a consequence, there is no impact on Potential Energy, and it can act passively, as long as Eddy Kinetic Energy remains to maintain the turbulent diffusion. In this sense, for the ocean dynamics, it remains hidden along the isopycnals (Fig. 1c). However, it still contributes to heat and freshwater transports from cold to warm and from salty to fresh regions, respectively. This process can be described in the form of temperature and salinity rates of change or of water-mass transformation rates (see Methods – Water Mass Transformation Rates). Rates of temperature and salinity change

As mentioned above, the isopycnal diffusion of temperature and salinity can be assessed as the induced rates of change in terms of temperature and salinity [see Methods – (10)]. High magnitudes of the rates of change are due to high diffusivity coefficients, sharp isopycnal temperature or salinity gradients, or a combination of both. At 1000 m depth, we find that they are of the order of kelvins per year or tenth of (g kg−1) per year [or a few 10−8 K s−1 and 10−9 g kg−1 s−1] for temperature and salinity, respectively. These rates of change are mainly located in the Southern Ocean, along the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, and, to a lesser extent, in the North West of the North Atlantic (Figs. 3a and 4a). Indeed, the Southern Ocean accounts for 67% of the global rates of change for temperature and salinity [as measured by the R2 of the transformations described in (11)], despite representing only 31% of the global ocean area. When including the North Atlantic it reaches 95% for temperature and salinity rates of change. A local maximum of these rates of change can be seen in the Zapiola gyre, which corresponds to a local maximum of diffusivity coefficients (Fig. 1d, e). There is also significant rates of change along the Agulhas return current and its merging with the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, which also corresponds to a local maximum of diffusivity coefficients. Indeed, between South Africa and the Kerguelen plateau, there is a warming and salinification of the relatively cold and salty AAIW (Figs. 3a and 4a), a cooling and freshening of the relatively warm and fresh AAIW, and a warming and salinification of the warm and salty residual of RSGPIW (trapped in the Agulhas system57, Fig. 1b).Fig. 3Temperature rates of change at 1000 m depth.Rates of Change in (a) Conservative Temperature at 1000 m depth induced by the isopycnal turbulence. Unlike the geographical map that reveals where the rates of change are located, the TS-diagram (i.e., representation with temperature-salinity axes) reveals which water masses are subjected to the isopycnal diffusion (i.e., change along isopycnal surfaces). This can be represented globally (insets in Figs. 3a and 4a) or displayed for each relevant basin (following the division of Fig. 1f), which allows a simpler relation with local Water Masses (Fig. 3b–e and 4b–e). In the North Atlantic, despite the weak isopycnal turbulent diffusion coefficients in the subtropical gyre interior (Fig. 1d, e), temperature and salinity rates of change within the MW (along the 27.6-27.8 kg m−3 density class) are visible with a slight cooling and a freshening of MW (Fig. 3b). A more important cooling and freshening is also visible at the edge between MW and NADW. In the Southern Ocean, the geographical separation is quite revealing. It suggests different water masses at play for each of the three sectors (Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific). In the Atlantic sector of the Southern Ocean, we observe a cooling and freshening of CDW and a warming and salinification of AAIW and AABW (Figs. 3c and 4c). In the Indian and Pacific sectors, the rates of change correspond to a warming and salinification of the AABW, a cooling and freshening of the CDW, a warming and salinification of the coldest and freshest portion of the AAIW, and a cooling and freshening of the warmest and saltiest portion of the AAIW (Figs. 3d, e and 4d, e). In the Indian sector, there is also a warming and salinification of the warmer portion of the AAIW, which derives from RSPGIW57.

Water mass transformations

To further quantify these effects, the isopycnal turbulent diffusion can also be expressed as Water Masses Transformation (see Methods – Water Mass Transformation Rates). As discussed earlier, since the turbulent eddy-induced diffusion acts along isopycnals13 it cannot modify density (except for weak nonlinear effects, which affect density and hence the circulation)56. Hence, we will focus on transformations induced by temperature and salinity changes. We use the traditional Water Mass Transformation framework48,58, often defined across density layers. Here, we compute transformation rates induced by temperature and salinity changes along the isopcycnals [see Methods – (11)]. They are computed both globally and for each region defined earlier (Fig. 1f). These two transformations are evidence of heat and freshwater transport by isopycnal turbulent diffusion.

We find density transformations of the order of ± 2 Sv (i.e., density-class standard deviation) for density changes induced by either temperature and salinity, respectively (colored – red and blue – bars in Fig. 5a, c, respectively). This is important as these transformations correspond to a 70 m layer only (i.e., the typical density layer thickness due to the stratification at our studied depth, see Methods – Water Mass Transformation Rates and Supplementary Fig. 1). For comparison, the buoyancy transformations are of the order of a few tens of Sverdrup when computed over the full ocean depth56. We rationalize the importance of the temperature and salinity transformations over the density ones because of their strong thermohaline compensating effect on density (i.e., the so called spiciness). The Water Masses located at our studied depth exhibit particularly intense spiciness contrasts18, although it is not necessarily the case at other depths. More broadly, at the ocean surface, warming regions are often regions of net evaporation, whereas cooling regions are often regions of net precipitation. For reference, we compare these isopycnal transformations to the diapycnal ones, which induce a net density transformations56. These diapycnal transformations are computed using reconstructed vertical diffusion coefficients59,60 (see Methods – Alternative Diffusivity Coefficients.), which show the expected typical control by topography (Supplementary Fig. 2). To compare the isopycnal and diapycnal transformations, we measure their amplitude through their standard deviation along the density coordinate. We find that the temperature and salinity isopycnal transformations are 3 and 5 times as large as the diapycnal ones (colored-bars vs cyan line in Fig. 5a, c), respectively. Note that this result contrasts with density transformations for which, as expected, diapycnal transformations is the dominant factor over isopycnal ones, except for a few specific Water Masses (i.e., Subantarctic Mode Water, AAIW, and CDW), for which cabbeling and thermobaricity are important factors56.

These dominant isopycnal transformations reveal a mean density gain and loss of + 1.5 Sv and − 1.9 Sv, respectively. The density transformations correspond to three regimes in density coordinates (Fig. 5): a warming and salinification of the lightest water, a cooling and freshening of density classes between 27.35 and 28 kg m−3, and a warming and salinification of the densest water. As expected within an isopycnal framework, transformations with respect to neutral density classes show that density gain and loss by cooling and warming are associated to density loss and gain by freshening and salinification, respectively, preserving the density field (Fig. 5e). Looking at water classes and regional separation, we find warming and salinification of the lightest class of AAIW and cooling of the densest class of AAIW. This mainly occurs in the Indian sector of the Southern Ocean. On the other hand, transformations associated with CDW and AABW exhibit a warming and salinification, which mainly occurred in the Atlantic sector of the Southern Ocean, and to a second degree in the Pacific sector for CDW. A transformation peak is also visible in the North Atlantic sector. It corresponds to a cooling compensated by a freshening at the edge between MW and NADW. Similarly, in the same sector, the full range of MW is also transformed through cooling and freshening, whereas a slight warming and salinification also occur for the NADW.

To better understand the role and to assess the importance of spatially varying anisotropic diffusivity coefficients36, we have tested a range of less complex diffusivity coefficients (see Methods – Alternative Diffusivity Coefficients). To quantitatively test the mis-estimations of these simplest diffusivity coefficients, we used their relative error in comparison to the most complete version [see Methods – (12)]. The simplest one was a spatially-uniform and isotropic isopycnal diffusivity coefficient (light gray bars in Fig. 5a, b). It mainly preserves the signs of the transformations, but underestimates them by up to 53% for both temperature and salinity. Nonetheless, this approximation warm and salinified AAIW from 27.4 kg m−3 to 27.5 kg m−3, whereas it was cool and freshened with the most complete version of the diffusivity. Using spatially-varying and isotropic isopycnal diffusivity coefficients (dark gray bars in Fig. 5a, b) better conserves the shape of the transformations but overestimates by up to 48% both of these temperature and salinity transformations.

Robustness of the isopcynal diffusivity dataset

To test the robustness of our results, we have recomputed the temperature and salinity transformations using two alternative diffusivity coefficient datasets (see Methods – Alternative Diffusivity Coefficients). The first dataset, that we named Groeskamp, estimates diffusivity using salinity observations and assuming the validity of the mixing length theory34. The second dataset, that we named Roach, uses the same deep Argo float displacements than us, but a different methodology: a pairwise dispersion39.

Groeskamp and Roach diffusivities (Fig. 6a, b, respectively) have the same overall pattern as our reference diffusivities (Fig. 1d, e): intensification in western boundary currents and within the Antarctic Circumpolar Current. Because Groeskamp and Roach diffusivity coefficients correspond solely to the cross-flow diffusion, they are more comparable to our secondary diffusivity coefficients (Fig. 1e), which are by construction, weaker than the primary ones. Groeskamp and Roach diffusivity coefficients range between 2000 m2 s−1 and 5000 m2 s−1 in active regions (as our secondary diffusivity coefficients). Because the Groeskamp and Roach datasets provide only a single local diffusivity coefficient, we restrict the comparison to the spatially varying and isotropic framework. When using our spatially varying and isotropic diffusivity coefficients, we find stronger transformations than those obtained with Groeskamp and Roach diffusivity coefficients. However, the transformations are qualitatively well represented. As expected, the agreement is better when applying the spatially varying and isotropic diffusivity coefficients solely based on the secondary diffusivity coefficients. Here, the agreement is qualitative and quantitative. This stresses the robustness of our described temperature and salinity transformations with respect to the methodology used to infer the diffusivity coefficients.

Discussion

The ocean, through its absorption of 91% of the heat imbalance5 and as the largest region of evaporation and precipitation on Earth4, is a key component of our changing climate. At first glance, the ocean is filled by fast, intense mesoscale eddy features, ubiquitous worldwide12. Hence, the role of these eddies and their associated turbulence in balancing the ocean circulation and its heat and freshwater transports remains a vivid question of current climate and ocean research.

This question is even more intriguing when acknowledging that, since eddy turbulence corresponds to a flow along isopycnals, its induced mixing cannot modify density (except through weak – but potentially locally important56 – nonlinear effects), hence the circulation. However, they interact with temperature and salinity fields, transforming seawater properties along their way13, without a trace on the circulation, but still acting on the heat and freshwater transports. From an energetic point of view, by transporting temperature and salinity properties, without disturbing the density (because of the compensating effect), these transformations do not impact the potential energy, remaining hidden for ocean dynamics and acting passively, as long as Eddy Kinetic Energy exists for maintaining the eddy turbulence.

The first result of the present study is the observational estimation of temperature and salinity transformations at 1000 m depth. By using observations of the ocean temperature and salinity together with a direct observational estimate of anisotropic diffusivity coefficients induced by mesoscale eddy turbulence36, we have unveiled the existence of this intense, hidden isopycnal transformations fundamental for the transport of heat and freshwater in the ocean and, therefore, for setting the global temperature and salinity seawater properties. These isopycnal transformations are 3 and 5 times as large as the more classical diapycnal transformations in terms of temperature and salinity transformations, respectively. In particular, in the North Atlantic we find an important dominance of isopycnal transformations over diapycnal transformations at our studied, 1000 m depth, consistently with an independent recent observational study61. We have shown that these dominant isopycnal transformations occur for ~ 67% in the Southern Ocean (despite representing only 31% of the global ocean area) and is particularly relevant for AAIW, CDW, and AABW.

The second result is the demonstration of the importance of acknowledging the full complexity of the isopycnal diffusivity tensor. We have shown that considering the spatially varying and anisotropic diffusivity coefficients is key for the accurate transformation estimations. Simpler diffusivity coefficients (as often used in parameterization of laminar/eddy-less ocean component used in some current climate models)62 lead to misestimation of the seawater properties transformation (by up to 53%), thus impacting oceanic heat and freshwater transports. Although because our study is limited to a single-depth layer, we could not quantitatively estimate these impacts. Hence, this should be tested in state-of-the-art climate models to further identify the relevance and significance of our finding. In particular, the role of spatially varying and anisotropic diffusivity coefficients in modulating and redistributing ocean heat storage in climate simulations should be evaluated. Given the projected changes of ocean mesoscale turbulence over the 21st century63, hence of the isopycnal diffusivity coefficients36, the future response of our identified intense hidden isopycnal transformations is in question.

Whereas, the role of diathermal and diahaline water-mass transformations has been long established16,29, there exists in the recent literature evidence of the importance of the isopycnal direction over the diapycnal one for these temperature and salinity transformations18,32,61. Our direct estimates suggest the dominance of isopycnal diffusion over diapycnal diffusion at 1000 m depth. We hypothesize that this is because we fully acknowledged the anisotropic nature of the isopycnal diffusion within our framework, unlike in inverse or numerical modeling studies. This dominance is particularly important since isopycnal transformations are key for heat uptake in the context of Global Warming30.

Because of observational constraints36, our analysis is restricted at 1000 m depth. Hence, our results are only representative of a horizontal slice of the ocean, potentially masking geographical variations linked to the local layer thickness (relatively more important in the Southern Ocean and in the subpolar North Atlantic). However, this depth is particularly interesting because it is close to the depth of the maximum meridional overturning transport in the Atlantic (i.e., the depth of no-motion for this circulation), and it is where some of the most saline waters reside and where the coldest waters also exist. This makes this depth particularly relevant for heat and freshwater transports. However, given the intensity of the circulation and transformation identified in our study, it is important to acknowledge it at other depths. In particular, diapycnal mixing could become more prevalent near the seabed, mitigating the key role of isopycnal mixing for temperature and salinity transformations. Hence, building a robust 3-dimensional picture, fully constrained by observations, of the isopycnal turbulent transformations of the global ocean will be a direction for future work.

We have used the two-dimensional gridded direct estimate of the turbulent anisotropic, isopycnal diffusivity coefficients derived from Argo deep displacements36, which are available35 at 10.17882/91335, and the global three-dimensional ISAS17 gridded temperature and salinity data derived from objective analysis of in situ observations43 and available at 10.17882/52367.

The spatially varying and anisotropic diffusivity coefficients were obtained by reconstructing an analog turbulent field from deep Argo displacement. This reconstructed turbulent field was used to determine the local dispersion of a locally-injected spike of passive tracer, the rates of dispersion estimating the local diffusivity coefficients along the zonal, meridional, and cross directions. This allows for the full determination of the diffusivity tensor, rather than a single coefficient as often done33,34,39.

In practice, after conversion of the data to Conservative Temperature and Absolute Salinity, as well as the computation of neutral density, all the computations are done using 3° × 3° cells (i.e., the native cell of the diffusivity coefficients) on 1° × 1° grid (i.e., the native grid of the diffusivity coefficients). Data are vertically averaged over 40 m bins, and vertical levels (needed for the computation of vertical gradients) are separated by 20 m. All conversions to Conservative Temperature and Absolute Salinity, as well as computation of neutral density, are done using the Gibbs SeaWater Oceanographic Toolbox of TEOS-10 code.

For the water masses framework, we have identified six Water Masses located at the depth of the study49. They are defined through their temperature and salinity range. They are the Mediterranean Water (MW), the Red Sea–Persian Gulf Intermediate Water (RSPGIW), the Antarctic Intermediate Water (AAIW), the North Atlantic Water (NADW), the Circumpolar Deep Water (CDW), and the AntArctic Bottom Water (AABW).
