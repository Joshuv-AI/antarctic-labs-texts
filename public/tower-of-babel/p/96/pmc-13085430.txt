Pattern of treatment outcome and safety of prednisolone in the treatment of leprosy reactions among leprosy patients in ALERT hospital: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Gemechis Mikael Mamo, Samrawit Solomon Ethiopia, Tolesa Diriba Biratu, Daniel Legese Achalu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeprosy reaction is inflammation caused by the body’s immune system attacking the leprosy bacteria. If left untreated or improperly managed, it may lead to irreversible nerve damage, causing mortality, disability, and physical deformities. Oral prednisolone is a recommended drug to manage these reactions. Despite this, the treatment outcome and safety of prednisolone in treating leprosy reactions in clinical practices have not studied well. Thus, this study aimed to assess the status and factors of treatment outcomes and safety of prednisolone in the treatment of leprosy reactions.MethodsA hospital-based retrospective cohort study was conducted. Data were collected using a data abstract form, entered into Epi-Data version 4.0, and then exported to SPSS version 25 for further analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed to get summary results, and binary and multinomial logistic regression analyses were used to predict factors that affect the treatment outcome and safety of prednisolone.ResultsIn this study, 262 patients’ record files were reviewed. The median age of patients was 30 (IQR 25–42.5) years. Two-thirds (66.8%) of patients were males. Of 262, only 62.2% of the patients improved from the leprosy reactions with prednisolone treatment. Patients with WHO disability grade-0 showed more improvement than those with higher grades. Furthermore, out of 262 patients, 79.4% experienced at least one adverse event during the course of treatment. The starting dose of prednisolone and treatment duration were significantly associated with the occurrence of the adverse events.ConclusionA considerable proportion of the patients didn’t show improvement in their reaction status with prednisolone for both type one and two reactions. The prevalence of adverse events was higher, and the magnitude was proportional to duration and dose of prednisolone, suggesting the need to optimize treatment strategies.Clinical TrialNot applicable.

Introduction

Leprosy is an infectious disease caused by bacteria called Mycobacterium Leprae and predominantly affects the skin and peripheral nerves, resulting in neuropathy and associated long-term consequences, including deformities and disability [1]. Even though the leprosy case has decreased dramatically to less than 1 case per 10,000 inhabitants in 2000 globally, in 2015 a total of 210 000 new leprosy patients were diagnosed worldwide [2]. Ethiopia is among 14 countries with high leprosy endemic, reporting more than 1,000 new cases annually. A total of 3,970 new leprosy cases (with 85% Multi-Bacilli) were registered in 2015 alone [3].

Even though, multi-drug therapy (MDT) is highly effective in treating leprosy, about 30–50% of patients with leprosy experienced reactions that results in further complications [4]. Leprosy reaction is inflammation caused by the body’s immune system attacking the leprosy bacteria and it can occur before, during, or after treatment with MDT [5]. Leprosy reactions are categorized in two types. Type 1 reaction (T1R), also known as reversal reaction (RR), and Erythema Nodosum Leprosum (ENL) or Type 2 reactions (T2R). T1R is characterized by an increase in inflammation in skin lesions or nerves or both, pain and sensitivity in nerves (neuritis), progressive neurological failure [6, 7]. T2Rs (ENL) are acute inflammatory reactions with multisystem disorder, and other organ involvement includes iritis, arthritis, lymphadenitis, orchitis, and neuritis in addition to the skin [6].

Leprosy reactions cause significant impacts on the health of the patients; if untreated or improperly managed, it can cause irreversible nerve damage, affect the skin and other organs, and expose patients to many social problems like social stigma and discrimination. Most of the deformities and disabilities in patients are primarily caused by the leprosy reactions [5]. T1Rs cause Nerve Function Impairment (NFI) in about 30% of patients with reaction [8]. On the other hand, ENL affects up to 5% to 40% of patients with leprosy [9]. The estimated mortality rate is 1.4% and 8.1% in T1R and T2R, respectively [8].

Corticosteroids have been used in the management of leprosy reactions for over several years, and oral prednisolone is the most common treatment for severe reactions [10]. Prednisolone is more effective in treating T1R in the skin than in the nerve, with recovery rates in skin signs (88%) but less improvement in nerve function; sensory (49%) and motor nerve (74%) [11].

Despite prednisolone is recommended standard treatment regimen, studies showed variable results on the effectiveness of prednisolone in treating leprosy reactions. Results of an observational study showed that prednisolone is not effective enough to restore nerve function in T1R, approximately 40% of individuals do not show clinical improvement [12, 13]. In T2R/ENL, prednisolone does not prevent recurrences, and usually, an ENL flare-up occurs when doses are decreased to 20-30 mg per day [14, 15]. Furthermore, the safety of prednisolone is a significant issue and should be given due consideration. The use of potent immunosuppressants is a potential risk in areas endemic for severe infections. Prolonged and high doses of prednisolone raise a safety concern as it is usually associated with serious adverse events [16–18].

In Ethiopia, leprosy reaction affected approximately 46.8% of patients with leprosy infection. T1R ranges from 28.7% to 47% while T2R accounted for 18.1% to 31.7% [19]. As a management strategy, prednisolone is being used as a first-line drug in both types of reactions. However, prednisolone is not sufficient to control reactions, recurrence, and flare-ups are still challenging in clinical practices and thus, require additional and high doses of prednisolone, which may increase the risk of steroid complications [3]. Despite the high prevalence of reactions and wide use of steroids, the treatment outcome and safety of prednisolone are uncertain, as they have not been systematically studied. Thus, this study aimed to assess the treatment outcome and safety of prednisolone along with predictors associated with them during the treatment of leprosy reactions among leprosy patients.

Methods and materials

Study setting

The study was conducted in the ALERT Centre, which is located 7 km southwest of Addis Ababa, Kolfe Sub-city, on Jimma Road, around Zenebework. ALERT was established in 1934. ALERT Centre has Hospital, Training Centre for leprosy and tuberculosis (TB). ALERT hospital is the national referral center for leprosy-related complications and it is a large facility containing all leprosy-related specialty care, including a rehabilitation program. In addition, the hospital provides specialized and all general health care services for 1.5 million estimated populations.

Study design

A hospital-based retrospective cohort study was conducted using the medical records of leprosy patients managed for leprosy reactions at ALERT Hospital between July 2017 and July 2022. The cohort consisted of all patients who initiated prednisolone therapy for reaction management during the study period (exposure/start point). Each patient was followed from the date prednisolone treatment was started until the documented end of treatment completion. Treatment outcomes and safety data were extracted from the patient’s medical chart to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of prednisolone therapy.

Study participants

All patients who initiated prednisolone therapy for leprosy reactions at ALERT Hospital during the study period (from July 2017 to July 2022) and had a documented treatment outcome were eligible for inclusion. Patients were excluded if their records lacked key variables required for analysis, defined as outcome data (including reaction type, WHO disability grade, prednisolone dose, or treatment outcome). Patients who were transferred out before treatment evaluation or had no documented outcome assessment were also excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated by Open-Epi version 3, using single population proportion formula. For the calculation, 95% confidence level, 5% marginal error and 50% proportion were used. The calculated and a minimum sample size required for this study was 249 Sample size.

Study variables

Treatment outcomes (improved, no change/same, deteriorated) and safety of treatment were outcome variables. While socio-demographic data, clinical data like leprosy reaction types and severity/ grade, leprosy categories/classifications, history, duration, and dose of treatment with prednisolone, co-morbid diseases, and concomitant drug uses were independent variables of this study.

Treatment outcomes

Treatment outcomes with prednisolone were evaluated based on changes in nerve function status during the treatment period. Nerve function was assessed at baseline and at scheduled follow-up visits every two weeks by the treating physicians at ALERT Hospital, all of whom have longstanding clinical experience in the management of leprosy reactions. Follow-up for each reaction episode continued from initiation of prednisolone therapy until completion of the prescribed corticosteroid course, typically 24 weeks, after which the final outcome assessment was recorded.

Treatment outcomes with prednisolone were operationalized using explicit, predefined criteria based on motor and sensory nerve function testing. Improved outcome was defined as an increase in motor strength by at least one VMT grade in two or more muscles or an increase of two grades in a single muscle, and/or an improvement in sensory function by at least two monofilament points per nerve in the hand and/or three or more points per nerve in the foot. No change was defined as the absence of any measurable improvement or deterioration, with the same number of nerves impaired at baseline and follow-up. Deteriorated outcome was defined as any worsening, meaning either a reduction in VMT grade or a decrease in monofilament points that resulted in a net increase in the number or severity of impaired nerves. These operational thresholds served as the definitive rule set for classifying treatment response [20]. Although formal inter-rater reliability testing was not performed, all assessments were conducted by clinicians following standardized hospital protocols, which supports consistency in outcome evaluation. On the other hand, an adverse event (AE) is defined as any untoward occurrence that may present during treatment with a pharmaceutical product, but that does not necessarily have a causal relationship to the treatment [16].

Clinical features of leprosy reactions

Leprosy reactions can be further categorized based on their clinical features. T1R Recurrence or Flare-up is an increase in skin severity score to 4 or more out of 9 AND/OR an increase in NFI defined as worsening of VMT by one point in two or more muscles, or by 2 points in one muscle and/or worsening of ST: decreased sensation in at least two out of 3 points per nerve on the hand and/or 3 or more points on the feet [16]. New ENL means the occurrence of ENL for the first time; recurrent ENL is the appearance of specific ENL symptoms in a patient previously treated with prednisolone and had been free of ENL symptoms for four weeks off prednisolone, while chronic ENL is an episode lasting more than 6 months, as the patient experienced a flare-up of ENL whilst on prednisolone treatment [21].

Consequences of leprosy reactions included: a nerve function impairment (NFI), which was evidenced by a clinically detectable reduction in function in sensory, motor, and/or autonomic fibers [12]. Neuropathy (peripheral) is a functional impairment and/or structural damage to autonomic, sensory, and motor nerve fibers within the peripheral nervous system [22] and Neuritis, a reaction affecting the peripheral nerves, characterized by sudden pain or palpation of peripheral nerves, with or without thickening of nerve trunks [6].

Based on immunity, Ridley-Jopling (1966) categorized leprosy patients into a spectrum with polar tuberculoid (TT) and lepromatous (LL) forms and middle types of borderline tuberculoid (BT), mid borderline (BB), and borderline lepromatous (BL) leprosy. It assists the understanding of the disease and is usually used in research settings. Patients with different disease types exhibit different immunological responses to M. leprae that will result in many complications [23].

Data collection

Data were collected from patients’ medical records using a data abstraction form which is prepared by the principal investigator in English language. The content of the format includes socio-demographic information, Clinical information, Leprosy reaction treatment, Treatment outcome, and adverse events associated with prednisolone use. Data collection was carried out from August to October 2022 by five trained nurses under the close supervision of investigators.

Data analysis procedures

Each completed data collection tool was assigned a unique code. The data were entered and cleaned in a template of variables prepared in Epi-Data version 4.0 and exported to SPSS version 25 for further analysis. Descriptive statistics like frequencies, percentages, mean, and standard deviation were computed to get summary results. The multinomial logistic regression analyses were used to predict the factors that affect outcome variables. For each of the study variables, the association was estimated by the odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI). The association between outcome and safety of treatment with prednisolone and explanatory variables was considered statistically significant at a p-value less than 0.05 in regression analysis.

Results

Baseline socio-demographic characteristics and clinical information of the patients

Among a total of 262 patients, 66.8% were males. The median age of patients was 30 years, with an IQR of 25–42.5, from which the majority (94.7%) were adults. The comorbid condition was minimal; only 5.7% patients had additional diseases (Table 1). Regarding the status of leprosy, a significant number of cases (90.4%) are Multi-bacillary (MB) as per the WHO classification. According to the Ridley-Jopling classification, out of 236 cases, 55.9% and 40.7% were borderline leprosy (BL) and lepromatous leprosy (LL), respectively. As indicated in Table 2, among 240 patients with available data index, 38.3% of bacillary index had a ‘0’ (zero) score, and the remaining showed one to six plus bacillary index (Table 1).

From a total of 262 cases, type one reaction (T1R) accounted for 58.8%, whereas type two reaction (T2R) were 41.2%. Based on the occurrence of reactions, the most common was recurrence reactions (67.9%), followed by new reactions (32.1%). Complications associated with reactions were assessed, and the majority of the cases involved both motor and sensory impairment (41.1%). The remaining led to isolated sensory impairment (28.4%) and isolated motor impairment (1.1%). Based on WHO disability grading, half of the cases had a grade 0 disability score, while the remaining 80 (30.5%) and 51 (19.5%) cases showed grade 1 and 2, respectively (Table 1).

Regarding treatment status, 30.5% were previously treated with prednisolone, while the rest were new to the treatment regimen. Of patients treated, 85 (32.4%) were concomitant drug (clofazimine) users. Poor adherence was common, and 103 (39.3%) patients had a history of non-adherence during the treatment period (Table 1).

Table 2 presents the mean, median, and inter-quartile range (IQR) for key corticosteroid treatment variables, including starting dose, duration of therapy, and cumulative dose. The median starting prednisolone dose was 40.0 mg (IQR: 40.0–40.0), indicating standardized prescribing practices. Treatment duration showed moderate variability, with a median of 14.0 weeks (IQR: 12.0–16.0), likely reflecting individualized tapering schedules based on clinical response. The median cumulative prednisolone dose was 2667.5 mg (IQR: 2030.0–2957.5), (Table 2).

Outcomes of patients treated for leprosy reactions with oral prednisolone

Out of 262, the majority (62.2%) of the patients showed improvement in reaction status with prednisolone treatment. However, 75 (28.6%) patients didn’t show any alteration (no change) in their condition at the end of the treatment course, while the condition of 24 (9.2%) patients was even became worsen (deteriorated) with treatment (Fig. 1).

Table 3 illustrates the shares of each variable in each outcome category. According to sex category, of 99 patients with no improvement outcome, most (70. %) of them were males. Based on reaction type, the majority, 97 (59.5%) of improvement outcomes were observed among T1R. Outcomes were also assessed with frequency of reactions; accordingly, nearly three-fourths of poor outcomes (no improvement), (72.7%) were experienced by patients with recurrence reaction. There is a wide range of complication associated with Leprosy Reaction. The majority, 80.8% of the patients who didn’t show improvement with treatment, were those who encountered with complications. Specifically, 58.6% and 22.2% of poor outcomes were accounted for by patients with both sensory and motor impairment and only sensory impairment, respectively. Similarly, improvement decreased with grade of disability, 64.4%, 23.9%, and 11.7% improvement were achieved in patients with disability grade 0,1 and 2, respectively. The proportion of non-adherent individuals was higher in the improved and deteriorated groups than in the group with no change, suggesting that non-adherence was not limited to those with unfavorable outcomes. Instead, it appeared in both extremes, patients who improved and those who worsened (Table 3).

Adverse event during treatment of leprosy reactions with oral prednisolone

Out of the 262 patients, 208 (79.4%) experienced at least one type of adverse event (AE), while the remaining 20.6% had no recorded AE. Adverse events were captured in patients’ charts using a structured data collection form and analyzed by type at both the patient and event levels. Based on patient-level analysis, 74.8% of patients experienced gastrointestinal (GIT) problems, followed by infection (6.5%), fungal infection (5.3%), and hypertension (5.0%). Overall, a total of 289 AE cases were reported among 208 patients. Of a total of 289 AE cases, gastrointestinal tract (GIT) symptoms were common, accounting for 67.8%, followed by infection (5.9%), fungal skin infection (4.9%), hypertension (4.5%), and others. (Table 4).

Factors affecting the treatment outcome of leprosy reaction with oral prednisolone

A multinomial logistic regression analysis was done to identify predictor variables. Clinically relevant variables were selected for a multinomial logistic regression model. After running the analysis, we found that only reaction severity /WHO disability grade has been significantly associated with treatment outcome (P < 0.05).

Reaction severity at the start of treatment showed a statistically significant impact on treatment outcome of patients with improved outcome as compared to patients with no change in outcome, as depicted in Table 5. The odds of improved outcome with treatment of prednisolone increase by 6.9 fold for patients who have WHO disability grade 0 (zero) compared to those having grade 2 disability at the start of treatment, which is statistically significant [AOR = 6.966; 95%CI: 2.238–21.681], (Table 5).

The data met the key assumptions for multinomial logistic regression, including absence of multicollinearity (all VIF < 2), independence of observations, and no influential outliers. Model fit was first evaluated using the omnibus likelihood-ratio chi-square test, which was statistically significant (χ² = 60.517, p = 0.001). This indicates that the final model provides a significantly better fit than the null model and that at least one of the predictors contributes meaningfully to explaining the outcome. Goodness-of-fit was further examined using the Pearson (χ² = 445.826) and Deviance (χ² = 343.574) statistics. Both tests were non-significant (p > 0.05), suggesting that the model fits the data adequately, as non-significance indicates no evidence of lack of fit. Pseudo-R-square values, including Cox and Snell (0.226), Nagelkerke (0.275), and McFadden (0.148), show that the model explains approximately 14.8% to 27.5% of the variance in the dependent variable. This represents a medium-sized effect for a multinomial regression model.

The effect of baseline WHO disability grade on improvement was strong for Grade 0 patients in both T1R and T2R reactions (AOR 22.1, 95% CI 4.7–103.2 for T1R; AOR 12.5, 95% CI 2.8–55.7 for T2R). This indicates that patients with Grade 0 disability who experienced T1R or T2R had significantly higher odds of improvement. In contrast, the odds of treatment improvement among patients with higher-grade disability were lower, even though not statistically significant (Table 6).

Discussion

This study evaluated the treatment outcome and safety of prednisolone in leprosy reaction patients managed under programmatic conditions at the ALERT center. Overall, 62.2% of patients experienced improvement in their leprosy reactions, with 63% in T1R and 61.1% in T2R. The observed effectiveness in T1R is consistent with previous systematic reviews reporting 60–70% improvement in nerve function [24]. Mechanistically, the response to prednisolone in T1R depends on the degree of cell-mediated immunity and the reversibility of nerve inflammation. Early nerve involvement is more responsive because inflammation remains reversible, whereas chronic nerve damage leads to fibrosis, limiting steroid efficacy.

Our finding is higher than the retrospective Indian study by Rao (2006), which reported 50% improvement, likely due to differences in patient population, timing of presentation, and programmatic support [12]. On the other hand, this observed improvement in this study is less than that of a prospective study conducted in India, which reported 27% of patients with no improvement [25] and a multi-centered randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted in four countries, which shows improved nerve function in 78% of patients with recent NFI [26]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in study design, patient selection, and strict adherence to standardized steroid regimens in RCTs, which are more likely to capture optimal treatment responses.

The findings of this study showed that recurrence accounts for 54.5% and 45.5% in T1R and T2R, respectively, showing consistency with a systematic review which reported that recurrence of NFI with prednisolone in T1R in T1R was 30–50% [24]. Recurrent reactions are less responsive to steroids due to pre-existing fibrosis and chronic nerve inflammation, highlighting the importance of early diagnosis and timely intervention. Furthermore, our results indicated that the pattern of treatment outcome in new reaction and recurrent reaction is 67.9% and 59.6%. Similar to this, the previous systematic review revealed that the new NFI has better rates of recovery than the chronic NFI [24].

We have also examined factors associated with treatment outcome. And, our analysis showed that there is no statistically significant impact of duration of treatment in leprosy reaction for improvement, which opposes the finding of a multicenter RCT on three steroid regimens for treatment of T1R, which concluded NFI in patients with ENL may be under-treated if the short ENL regimes are followed, and suggested longer periods of steroid treatment for NFI for better effectiveness [27]. This may reflect population-specific factors, such as the severity of reaction, presence of recurrent episodes, or programmatic differences in monitoring and supportive care.

The response to prednisolone treatment showed favor with the WHO disability grading at the start of treatment. We observed that an increase in disability inversely affected the treatment outcome of leprosy reaction with prednisolone, 80.2%, 48.8%, and 37.2% improvement were observed in disability grade 0, 1, and 2 patients, respectively. This finding supports mechanistic reasoning: mild NFI is more reversible, whereas advanced impairment reduces steroid effectiveness. Similar findings reported by previous studies, the Cochrane systematic review stated that the recovery rate in moderate motor impairment and paralysis were 55% and 11% respectively [22], RCT done in Nepal, Bangladesh, and Indonesia identified the presence of a significant correlation between the WHO disability grade and improvement and restoration from NFI with prednisolone use [26]. This significant association across all designs of study demonstrated lower recovery in patients with moderate or severe motor impairment. It also showed that WHO disability grading is an independent predictor of the outcome of leprosy reaction and supports early management of the reaction before leading to complications.

Our findings also showed that 79.4% of patients experienced at least one adverse event (AE), which is comparable to a prospective study conducted in France (71%) [28]. However, it is higher than the rate reported in a study from Nepal (54.5%) [29]. This difference may be attributed to variations in study design, sample size, and patient characteristics across diverse settings. Furthermore, the result of this study indicates that the odds of developing AEs increase slightly but significantly with an increment of prednisolone treatment duration. Specifically, for each additional week of prednisolone treatment, the odds of experiencing an AE rise by approximately 2% (AOR = 1.02). Similarly, for each additional 1 mg increase in the starting dose of prednisolone, the odds of developing an AE increase by about 4.9% (AOR = 1.049). On the other hand, age, gender, and previous history of treatment with steroids have no statistically significant value. This finding is similar to a study in a French center, which reported that dose, duration, and history of long-term prednisolone were associated with prednisolone-related AEs [28].

Finally, this study is not without limitations. Its retrospective design inherently relies on existing clinical records, which may have incomplete or inconsistently documented predictor or outcome variables. Moreover, since the study was conducted at a single-center and specialized referral hospital, the results may reflect a cohort with more severe disease and complications; thus, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other settings with different case mixes, resources, or clinical practices. Despite these limitations, several strengths enhance the robustness and credibility of the findings. The large, contemporary, real-world cohort drawn from an endemic tertiary referral center provides meaningful and clinically relevant evidence on prednisolone treatment outcomes and safety. Because the study reflects routine practice in a major leprosy center within a resource-limited setting, the results offer valuable insights that can inform national clinical guidelines and support policy decisions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the majority of patients showed clinical improvement during prednisolone therapy for leprosy reactions; however, a considerable proportion did not improve, and nearly four-fifths of patients experienced at least one adverse event. WHO disability grading at treatment initiation had a statistically significant influence on prednisolone treatment outcomes in patients with leprosy reactions, highlighting the importance of early detection and initiation of the therapy. Higher starting dose and longer treatment duration of prednisolone increased the odds of occurrence of AEs, underscoring the need for careful dosing schemes and close monitoring during therapy. These findings suggest the need for further research for consideration of the future treatment options that possibly improve outcomes and minimize adverse events.
