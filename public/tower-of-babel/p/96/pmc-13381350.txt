Estimated Whole Blood Viscosity in Smoldering Multiple Myeloma: Preliminary Data from a Retrospective Single Center Analysis
Authors: Melania Carlisi, Rosalia Lo Presti, Sofia Indelicato, Sergio Siragusa, Gregorio Caimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeIn recent years, several reports have been available about the hemorheological profile in plasma cell dyscrasias, but a specific mention of smoldering multiple myeloma is present in few papers. Therefore, we decideded to evaluate the calculated whole blood viscosity and some important related laboratory parameters in this clinical condition.Patients and methodsWe performed a specific analysis aimed to evaluate the calculated blood viscosity using the de Simone formula, and some laboratory parameters and erythrocyte indices, in a cohort of 80 patients with smoldering multiple myeloma (median age 68.50; 40 male and 40 female, median follow-up of 26 months).ResultsFrom our analysis it emerges that in IgA smoldering multiple myeloma there is a greater bone marrow plasma cell infiltration and a reduction of albumin levels. Furthermore, in relation to the risk of progression into multiple myeloma we observed, in the non-low risk subgroup, a significant increase in total proteinemia, without any variation in blood viscosity. The latter is in fact almost exclusively dependent on hematocrit values, and it is negatively correlated to the ratio of free light chains.ConclusionIn our group of smoldering multiple myeloma patients the calculated whole blood viscosity does not appear to change with the risk of disease progression, and it seems to depend almost exclusively on hematocrit value. Furthermore, we observed a negative correlation between blood viscosity and rFLC in which it might depend on the fact that the latter parameter is negatively correlated with the hematocrit values, that significantly affect blood viscosity.

Introduction

Smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM) is an asymptomatic plasma cell dyscrasia that is part of a disease spectrum characterized, at one extreme, by a premalignant and asymptomatic clinical conditions, and at the other by a full-blown neoplasms. Specifically, in the natural history of these diseases, SMM represents the evolutionary stage followings the monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), and precedes multiple myeloma (MM), with approximately 10% probability to progress to MM annually, in first 5 years after the diagnosis.1 The prevalence of this clinical condition is approximately 0.5% of people over 40 years of age, and it is higher in men than in women (0.7% vs 0.4%), and tends to increase in parallel with age.2

In 2014, the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) presented specific guidelines to define SMM, as serum monoclonal protein (M-protein) of ≥3 g/dL or urinary M-protein of ≥500 mg/24 h and/or clonal bone marrow plasma cell (BMPC) involvement of 10–60% in the absence of MM-related end-organ damage (MRE) or other MM-defining events (MDE).3 Although it is, by definition, a clinical asymptomatic condition, an alteration of the hemocoagulative structure in a thrombotic sense can occasionally be observed.4–6 In fact, in several literature reports, it has been ascertained an increase in the risk of thromboembolic events that can affect both the venous and arterial vascular districts.7 In addition to sporadic thromboembolic events, in patients with SMM it is sometimes possible to observe forms of peripheral neuropathy and proliferative glomerulonephritis.8

From a clinical and biological point of view, and especially in relation to the risk of progression to MM, SMM is a heterogeneous entity where it is possible to identify three patient subgroups. The first, in which the disease biology is similar to that of monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS); the second, in which the disease is slowly progressing to MM, and the third, in which the disease will progress into overt disease in a shorter time of approximately 2 years.9 In relation to the third topic, the risk of progression to MM has a decreasing trend over time, with a 3% per year for the next 5 years (65% in 10 years), and 1% in the following years (similar to the MGUS). An estimated 25% of patients with SMM never progress to symptomatic disease.10

This heterogeneity has determined the need for an accurate risk stratification, that primarily relies on tumor burden markers. Initially, the original Mayo Clinic model considered the M-protein >3g/dL, bone marrow plasma cells >10%, and an abnormal free light chain (FLC) ratio as a markers of disease progression.11 At the same time, the PETHEMA risk criteria attached great importance to the presence of ≥95% aberrant plasma cells in the bone marrow as a critical factor for disease progression and risk stratification.12 More recently the 2/20/20 model stratifies risk based on three criteria: serum M protein >2 g/dL, involved to uninvolved FLC ratio >20, and bone marrow plasma cell infiltration >20%. This model categorizes patients in to low, intermediate, and high risk groups, with a progression rates at 2 years of 6,18, and 44%, respectively.13 The IMWG model added cytogenetic data to the 2/20/20 model, such as t(4;14), t(14;16),+1q, and del13, identifying four risk categories (low, low-intermediate, intermediate, and high-risk), with a 2-year progression rate of 6, 23, 46, and 63% respectively.14 Adequate and accurate risk stratification of SMM is essential in the daily management of this clinical condition. For patients with low- or intermediate-risk SMM, regular monitoring is advised. Instead, evidence-based guidelines are not currently available for the optimal management of high-risk SMM. To date, however, there is insufficient evidence to treat SMM outside of clinical trials.15

In addition to the risk stratifications, several clinical and laboratory parameters play a non-secondary role, some of which can also be classified as potential prognostic predictors. Among the latter, an important role is in fact played by the hemorheological profile and some hemometric parameters and erythrocyte indices.16–20

In our analysis, the hemorheological profile includes data relating to estimated blood viscosity, both at high (208 s–1) and low (0.50 s–1) shear rates, calculated according to the de Simone formula, which uses the values of hematocrit and total plasma proteins.

In recent years, our attention has focused on the behaviour of the main hemorheological determinants, measured and calculated, in MGUS and MM,21–24 and numerous reports in the literature address the hemorheological profile in plasma cell dyscrasias, but a specific mention of smoldering multiple myeloma is present in only two papers.25,26 Therefore, considering the possible role of the hemorheological pattern in SMM, especially in relation to possible clinical events associated with higher levels of whole blood viscosity (including, for example, thromboembolic events), we performed a single center retrospective analysis to evaluate, in patients with a new diagnosis of SMM, and with a median follow-up time of 26 months, the behaviour of estimated whole blood viscosity, at low and high shear rates, considering also two important laboratory parameters in plasma cell dyscrasias, such as serum total proteins and serum albumin. Furthermore, we focused on a series of blood count and erythrocyte indices, including hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (Hct) and red cell distribution width (RDW). Previously, we exclusively analyzed the role of RDW in the context of overt newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM), and above all we considered the behavior of this parameter in relation to the isotype of the monoclonal component, the ISS stage, and some prognostic factors of MM, such as the value of albumin and beta2-microglobulin, LDH and the percentage of bone marrow plasma cells.27 In our opinion, the analysis of all these parameters, now conducted only in the context of smoldering multiple myeloma, it could be useful for a greater knowledge of this plasma cell dyscrasia. Specifically, we examined the behaviour of the above parameters in relation to the isotype (IgA and IgG), to the progression risk into MM (low risk and not low risk), and to the median values of monoclonal component (MC), bone marrow plasma cell (BMPC) infiltration, and the ration of free light chains (rFLC), evaluated as involved:uninvolved ratio.

Patients and Methods

Study Population

We evaluated 80 consecutive newly diagnosed SMM patients (40 female and 40 male; average age 67±11.78 years), evaluated at the Hematology Division of the “Paolo Giaccone” University Hospital in Palermo from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2024. For the diagnosis of SMM we used the revised International Myeloma Working Group criteria,2 and specifically we diagnosed a SMM in presence of 10% or more bone marrow clonal plasma cells or a biopsy-proven plasmacytoma, serum and/or urinary monoclonal protein (except in non-secretory patients), in the absence, however, of myeloma defining events (MDE). MDE consist of CRAB features (hypercalcemia, renal failure, anemia, or lytic bone lesions) to which they are added three specific biomarkers: clonal bone marrow plasma cells ≥60%, serum free light chain ratio ≥100 (provided involved FLC level is ≥100 mg/L), and more than one focal lesion on MRI. From an initial number of 110 studied patients, finally, data from 80 SMM patients were collected, with the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥18 years, (2) diagnosis of smoldering multiple myeloma according to the IMWG criteria, (3) Subjects who have not had malignancy in the 6 months prior to enrolment or who, at most, have carcinoma in situ undergoing treatment with successful total eradication of the neoplasm, (4) hematocrit between 32 and 53%, and total plasma protein value between 5.4 and 9.5 g/dL, using the de Simone formula for calculating whole blood viscosity. We excluded from our analysis patients with a paraprotein with IgM isotype, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), symptomatic multiple myeloma (MM), and amyloidosis. We also excluded patients with liver cirrhosis of any origin, infectious diseases, concomitant malignancy, and blood transfusion within the previous 3 months. In our study population we performed a risk stratification according to the IMWG (International Myeloma working Group) 2/20/20 risk stratification model. The clinical data were collected and reviewed retrospectively, by consulting the medical records and the laboratory history of the company computer system. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of Palermo (report no. 05/2026). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the study.

Methods

The baseline laboratory data of patients were obtained from blood tests performed at the laboratory of our hospital, from samples collected at the time of diagnosis. Specifically, we evaluated following data: whole blood viscosity (WBV) at 208 s−1, calculated according to the de Simone formula 0.12 × Ht + 0.17 × TPP − 2.07, and at 0.50 s−1, according to the formula 1.89 × Ht + 3.76 × TPP – 78.42; hematocrit (Ht) obtained with an automated hematology analyzer, hemoglobin expressed in g/dL and evaluated with the automated blood count, RDW expressed as a coefficient of variation (percentage) of red blood cell volume and evaluated with the automated blood count, serum albumin (g/dl) and total plasma proteins (g/dL), evaluated with the colorimetric method.

Statistical Analysis

We used GraphPad Prism v. 9.5 to perform statistical analysis. We reported the data as medians and interquartile ranges. The median comparison was made with the Mann–Withney test. We performed the correlations among different parameters using the Spearman test. The null hypothesis was evaluated for values of P ≤0.05. We used in this analysis non-parametric tests because the distribution of all the considered variable, analyzed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, it has showed a non-normal distribution for many of them.

Discussion

In recent years, our attention has focused on the alteration of the hemorheological profile in some plasma cell dyscrasias, including MGUS and MM.

The hemorheological evaluation was carried out both by measuring and calculating the main hemorheological determinants, observing how in these two different clinical conditions the alteration of blood viscosity does not seem to depend only on extra-erythrocyte factors, and therefore plasmatic, but also on intra-erythrocyte ones.21–24

To date, the only hemorheological data in the literature regarding the smoldering multiple myeloma has been found in a case report of a subject with both SMM and sickle cell anemia. In this paper, in relation to the examined hemorheological data, despite a marked reduction in hematocrit, a significant increase in measured viscosity it was observed, predominantly affected by the sickle cell component (sclerocythemic-type hyperviscosity).25 In the absence of robust data regarding SMM, we turned our attention to this specific nosographic entity included in the plasma cell dyscrasias group.

In the current analysis of SMM patients, which has suffered by the non-uniform numerical distribution of patients in the two identified subgroups (only 13 patients in the IgA isotype subgroup), we have not observed any variation in blood viscosity according to the different SMM isotype.

Furthermore, the estimated whole blood viscosity, at the two shear rates, it does not appear to distinguish subjects with SMM at low risk of progression from those not at low risk, unlike that which occurs in other plasma cell dyscrasias, such as MGUS, whose altered hemorheological profile we have already described. However, in relation to this last assertion, it is necessary to underline that, in our previous analysis on MGUS subjects, the small evaluated sample was compared to a group of control subjects, and that the blood viscosity was measured at the same time as the plasma viscosity. The latter, especially measured at the lowest shear rate, together with the hematocrit values, outlined the blood viscosity behavior.

However, if no changes are observed in the calculated blood viscosity, according to the risk class of SMM, our analysis instead revealed that in the two subgroups of patients, at low-risk and non-low risk, blood viscosity calculated at the two shear rates is correlated with hematocrit values exclusively, and never with total proteinemia values; while in the whole group of SMM patients, a specific correlation, with a low statistically significance, is also present between calculated blood viscosity, at low shear rate, and total plasma protein.

These results, although preliminary, lead us to hypothesize that the estimated whole blood viscosity, showing no differences between the low-risk and non-low risk groups of patients, has no particular influence and/or relationship with the progression of SMM to overt multiple myeloma. However, our interest in evaluating the hemorheological profile of patients with SMM is dictated by the possible and important clinical implications. Indeed, the diagnosis of SMM is reported in clinical settings in which blood viscosity can play a key role, such as arteriovenous thromboembolic events,7,28–30 pulmonary hypertension,31,32 cases of neuropathy and nephropathy,8,33 and skin complications.34

In relation to the thrombotic profile, widely described in monoclonal gammopathies and with a non-marginal contribute of blood viscosity,35 we believe it is necessary to underline also that, in our small sample of SMM patients, there was no previous history of thromboembolic events, nor were any recorded during the follow-up program, using venous color Doppler ultrasound examination.

Blood viscosity, at the two shear rates, in the whole SMM cohort, and in the subgroup at low risk of progression, shows, finally, a negative correlation with the rFLC parameter.

In our opinion, the negative correlation observed between blood viscosity and the rFLC parameter is due to the existence of a statistically significant negative correlation (data not shown) between the rFLC and the hematocrit values (r −0.2684; P<0.05). However, this correlation emerges from the evaluation performed on the entire population of SMM patients, but it is not found in the two considered subgroups (low-risk and non-low-risk of progression SMM), possibly due to the small number of patients included in each subgroup (49 patients in the low-risk subgroup, and 31 patients in the non-low-risk group).

Blood viscosity measured using viscometers, in addition to having a significant cost impact, requires dedicated equipment and laboratory personnel that not all centers have. Therefore, the availability of simple and reproducible formulas, such as the one used in this analysis, allows us to overcome these difficulties. This is further supported by the results of our previous and basic analysis that highlighted how the calculated whole blood viscosity showed a parallel trend with the same hemorheological determinant obtained with a direct measurement, in a group of subject with a diagnosis of MGUS and in a group of MM patients.

Conclusion

In our population of SMM patients, the trend of calculated blood viscosity does not appear to change with the risk of disease progression, and it seems to depend almost exclusively on one of the parameters (hematocrit) used to evaluate it, according to the de Simone formula. What appears to be happening, with respect to the correlation study, carried out with the Spearman test, between estimated blood viscosity, hematocrit and total plasma proteins, is, in our opinion, attributable to the more incisive influence of hematocrit values on whole blood viscosity compared to that of total plasma proteins, which in this group of patients present values at the upper limits of normal. Instead, the negative correlation found between blood viscosity and rFLC it might depend on the fact that the latter parameter is negatively correlated with the hematocrit values (that significantly affect blood viscosity at the two shear rates).

Our analysis has some limitations, such as the retrospective nature of the study, the small size of the sample, the estimated data of viscosity values, and a limited median follow-up duration. Furthermore, although we strictly adhered to the formula used for blood viscosity calculation, specifically considering only hematocrit and total plasma protein values within the identified ranges to consider the formula itself reliable, and although we have the results of our previous analysis showing a parallelism between measured and calculated blood viscosity data in plasma cell dyscrasias,36 another important limitation of our study is the absence of measured blood viscosity data in smoldering multiple myeloma.

However, this study can represent a starting point for future evaluations, possibly prospective and with a larger sample size and with more homogeneous characteristics, aimed at better defining the role of hemorheological profile in smoldering multiple myeloma, considering also the cytogenetic alterations, missing in this analysis. This could in fact allow for a more accurate clinical knowledge of this important plasma cell dyscrasia and, above all, a more complete and better evaluation of patients.
