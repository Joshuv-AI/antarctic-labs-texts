A genome-wide association study to identify the genetic loci underlying carbapenem resistance in Acinetobacter baumannii
Authors: Gurprit Sekhon, Balvinder Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) presents a significant public health concern, and is recognized as a key contributor to the global antimicrobial resistance (AMR) crisis. This study applied a bacterial genome-wide association study (bGWAS) framework to identify genetic factors beyond the canonical carbapenemase OXA-23, that may contribute to carbapenem resistance.MethodsA. baumannii isolates with publicly available whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data and carbapenem-specific antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) results were compiled (n = 1,601). Unitigs were extracted from de novo assemblies, and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were called from raw sequence reads mapped to seven selected reference genomes. The unitig- and SNP-based genome-wide association analyses were carried out, including conditional analyses based on the known resistance determinants. Structural analysis was performed for the select candidate mutations.ResultsThe population was highly clonal and comprised ten lineages, including three predominantly resistant lineages (ST1, ST2, and ST32). Known CRAB-associated loci, including OXA-23, the RND efflux pump regulator AdeL, and OXA-66 variants (Ile129Leu and Leu167Val), were readily identified. Structural analysis of mutations identified outer-membrane proteins as potential candidate loci, however these loci were not supported in the conditional analyses across all reference genomes. The candidate loci supported in the conditional analysis across reference genomes included an amino acid substitution in a LysR-type regulator adjacent to the biofilm-associated operon pgaABCD, and an intergenic substitution located between enzymes involved in LPS remodeling.ConclusionsOXA-23 is the major determinant of carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii. Additional resistance mechanisms may become significant in the absence of OXA-23, including efflux pump regulation (AdeL), membrane permeability (OprD, DcaP), lipopolysaccharide (LPS) transport (PqiB), LPS remodeling (acyltransferase, glycosyltransferase), and biofilm formation (LysR). Integration of the conditional analysis based on known resistance determinants and a multi-reference mapping framework may improve the detection and prioritization of candidate associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13111-0.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is an escalating global public health threat [1]. Acinetobacter baumannii has emerged as a major nosocomial bacterial pathogen that contributes substantially to the global AMR burden [2, 3]. Carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB) typically exhibits a multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotype, severely limiting therapeutic options [4]. CRAB increasingly compromises the effectiveness of carbapenem antibiotics, which are among the last-line options for treating MDR infections in clinical settings [5]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has classified CRAB as a critical-priority pathogen due to its high disease burden and potent ability to disseminate resistance genes [6].

Carbapenem resistance is predominantly mediated by carbapenem-hydrolyzing class B (metallo-β-lactamases) and class D (oxacillinases) β-lactamases [7, 8]. In A. baumannii, OXA-51–like β-lactamases are intrinsic and ubiquitous but exhibit only weak carbapenemase activity. In contrast, acquired OXA-23–family enzymes are the most widespread clinically relevant class-D carbapenemases [9]. However, even potent carbapenemases such as OXA-23 do not fully account for the carbapenem resistance observed in many clinical isolates [10]. This has motivated investigations into additional carbapenem resistance mechanisms. In particular, reduced outer-membrane permeability due to porin disruption and increased efflux activity via resistance–nodulation–division (RND) pumps have been implicated in modulating carbapenem susceptibility [11, 12].

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have proven valuable for identifying AMR determinants across diverse bacterial species [13, 14]. A hypothesis-free bacterial GWAS framework is well suited to identify genetic variants contributing to carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii, including those beyond established mechanisms. To enable such an analysis, publicly available A. baumannii genomes with matched carbapenem-specific antimicrobial-susceptibility testing (AST) data were curated, and unitig- and SNP-based genotype–phenotype association testing was conducted. To our knowledge, this represents the first CRAB-focused GWAS with a large sample size (n = 1,601).

Results

The genome dataset and quality control

A schematic overview of the study workflow is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Initially, 1814 publicly available A. baumannii genomes with carbapenem-specific AST data were identified. Of these, only 1565 isolates had raw sequence reads and were assembled de novo, while the genome assemblies for the remaining 249 isolates were retrieved from NCBI. The quality of raw sequence reads for all but eight isolates, which contained non-biological reads (adapter contamination), is summarized in Supplementary Fig. S2. Quality control was performed on the resulting genome assemblies (n = 1806) in two stages (Supplementary Fig. S3). In stage 1, 153 assemblies were excluded for poor reference coverage (< 80%) or low Contig-N50 (< 20 kb). In stage 2, an additional 52 assemblies were excluded for low genome completeness (< 99%) or high contamination (> 5%), yielding a final set of 1601 assemblies for downstream analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3). For these genome assemblies (1601), metadata such as geographic origin, isolation year, isolation source, and SRA/Assembly accessions are provided in Supplementary Information (“Metadata.tsv”).

Population structure and phylogenetic analysis

Bayesian clustering resolved the population into ten lineages (BAPS_1 – BAPS_10), corresponding to clades I – X in Fig. 1. BAPS_5 (n = 747), BAPS_4 (n = 305), BAPS_8 (n = 149), BAPS_2 (n = 120), and BAPS_9 (n = 116) contained more than 100 isolates. BAPS_2, BAPS_5, and BAPS_9 were predominantly resistant, whereas BAPS_4 and BAPS_8 were mainly susceptible (Fig. 1). The first ten MDS axes corresponded to the BAPS lineages (Supplementary Fig. S4), and explained more than 80% variation in population structure (Supplementary Fig. S5a). First two MDS axes separated the major resistant and susceptible lineages (Supplementary Fig. S5b).

The resistant lineages BAPS_2 and BAPS_5 formed a single clade in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1). Consistently, the first MDS axis accounted for both BAPS_2 and BAPS_5 (Supplementary Fig. S4) and these lineages clustered together in the MDS1-MDS2 plot (Supplementary Figures S5b). By comparison, the resistant lineage BAPS_9 formed a distinct clade on the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1) and also clustered separately from BAPS_2 and BAPS_5 in MDS analysis (Supplementary Fig. S5b). Sequence type (ST) assignments were consistent with the inferred population structure. ST1 and ST2 represented BAPS_9 and BAPS_2 & BAPS_5, respectively. Another smaller resistant lineage BAPS_10 (n = 88) belonged to ST32. BAPS_8 and BAPS_4 corresponded to ST3 and ST25, respectively (Fig. 1). The separation between resistant and susceptible lineages is apparent in the phylogeny (Fig. 1).

The geographical distribution of isolates and their isolation dates provided additional details about the population structure (Supplementary Fig. S6). BAPS_2, BAPS_9, and BAPS_8 were composed almost exclusively of isolates from the USA. Within the largest resistant lineage, BAPS_5, isolates from Asia and Oceania clustered separately from those originating in the USA (Supplementary Fig. S6). BAPS_8 contained a substantial number of isolates from earlier isolation years (2000–2004). The smaller resistant lineage, BAPS_10 (ST32, n = 88), comprised predominantly resistant isolates from the USA, sampled between 2002 and 2010 (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Genome-wide association study

The results from unitig- and SNP-based GWAS across seven reference genomes are shown in Fig. 2. Total number of unitigs and mean number of SNPs identified across reference genomes were 1,971,690 and 257,803 ± 8,353, respectively. SNP broadly includes small insertions and deletions (indels). Both intergenic and intragenic SNPs were included in the analysis. Effect of the population structure correction is illustrated by observed versus expected P value distributions for the unitig (Fig. 2a) and SNP (Fig. 2b) analyses. Using Bonferroni-adjusted significance thresholds of 5 × 10⁻⁸ for unitigs and 2.5 × 10⁻⁷ for SNPs, 999 significant unitigs and an average of 606 ± 64 significant SNPs across reference genomes, were identified. The significant associations are presented in Fig. 2c–p.

Known resistance associated loci

The acquired and intrinsic carbapenemase (OXA-23 and OXA-66, respectively) were annotated with identical labels across reference genomes (Fig. 2c–p). The genes were reassigned to their families based on sequence alignment. ATCC19606, ATCC17978, and K09 encoded only OXA-66, whereas the remaining reference genomes contained both OXA-23 and OXA-66 (Figs. 2). OXA-23 showed markedly stronger association than OXA-66 (Fig. 2i, k, m and o). The OXA-66 variant (Leu167Val) showed significant association across reference genomes in SNP-based GWAS (Fig. 2). Unitigs mapping to OXA-23 and OXA-66 showed different gene coverage patterns. While OXA-23-mapped unitigs covered the gene region rather uniformly (Supplementary Fig. S7), two prominent peaks were observed for OXA-66 (Supplementary Fig. S8). The major and minor peaks corresponded to Ile167Val and Ile129Leu substitutions, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S9). OXA-23 was present in ~ 80% of resistant isolates (n = 915), and ~ 98% of OXA-23-positive isolates were resistant (Supplementary Fig. S10).

The LysR-type transcription factor, adeL, showed significant association across reference genomes in unitig-based GWAS (Fig. 2). The adeL-mapped unitigs represented a C-terminally truncated AdeL protein, Adel(1 –95), associated with resistance (Supplementary Fig. S10). Comparison of AdeL sequences across the dataset revealed another C-terminally truncated AdeL protein, AdeL(1–210), as shown in the Supplementary Fig. 10. The truncated proteins were present almost exclusively in resistant isolates, with a single AdeL(1–95)-containing isolate being susceptible. Notably, AdeL(1–95)-containing isolates belonged to the largely susceptible ST3 lineage (BAPS_8), and the isolates containing these truncated proteins lacked OXA-23 (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Novel candidate loci

Based on association metrics, literature support, and available information, a subset of significant mutations was selected for structural analysis. Among these, Leu382Pro and ΔSer338 were mapped to extracellular vestibule loops in the AlphaFold-predicted structures of outer membrane porins, OprD and DcaP, respectively (Fig. 3). Multiple mutations in LptD, a protein involved in lipopolyschharide (LPS) assembly, were mapped to the β–turn region in the integral membrane domain (Fig. 3). The Arg24Cys substitution in a TetR-family regulator was mapped to the DNA-binding domain (Fig. 3).

To assess the independence of significant associations, conditional GWAS was carried out. The conditional GWAS was carried out as the single and double analyses. The single analysis included only OXA-23 as the conditional variable. The double analysis included both OXA-23 & the OXA-66 variant Leu167Val as conditional variables. The Δ (− log10P) between the single & default condition (Δ1), and the double & single condition (Δ2) were calculated. The Leu167Val (OXA-66) showed an increase of 3.38 log units in the Δ1 (Figure 3). Only Leu382Pro (OprD), ΔSer338 (DcaP), Leu405Phe (PqiB) showed Δ1 > 1.0 and Δ2 > 0.25 log units. The Leu382Pro (OprD), ΔSer338 (DcaP), and Leu405Phe (PqiB) met the threshold (Δ1 > 1.0 and Δ2 > 0.25 log units) across one, three and five reference genome(s), respectively. Mutations in LptD and TetR could not satisfy the threshold. Based on these observations, the associations meeting the minimum threshold (β > 0.3, βErr < 0.1, Δ1 > 1.0, Δ2 > 0.25) across three or more reference genomes were considered as the prioritized candidate loci. The prioritized intragenic and intergenic candidate loci are provided in the supplementary tables ‘Intragenic.tsv’ and ‘Intergenic.tsv’, respectively.

The intragenic and intergenic associations meeting the threshold across five or more reference genomes are shown in Fig. 4. Mutliple mutations in a LysR transcription regulator were supported in conditional analyses across the seven reference genomes (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table “Intragenic.tsv”). Among these, Leu167Phe was mostly conserved in the homologous protein sequences (Fig. 4). The operon containing LysR was located in proximity to the biofilm-associated pgaABCD operon (Fig. 4). The intergenic variants supported in conditional analyses across the reference genomes corresponded to several SNPs located between acyltransferase (Lpx) and glucosyl transferase (Supplementary Table “Intergenic.tsv”). Among these intergenic SNPs, the 3,528,659 C > T substitution in the reference genome ATCC17978 is shown in Fig. 4.

Discussion

Carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) remains a major clinical threat, and resistance is commonly attributed to the acquired carbapenemase OXA-23. However, the resistance in many CRAB isolates cannot be fully explained by carbapenemase activity alone [10]. In the present study a large-scale bacterial GWAS combining genomic and carbapenem susceptibility data was conducted on 1,601 publicly available A. baumannii isolates. In the dataset included in the present study, OXA-23 was present in the majority of resistant isolates (~ 80%) and showed near-complete association with carbapenem resistance (~ 98% of OXA-23-positive isolates were resistant). However, the absence of OXA-23 in ~ 20% of resistant isolates indicates the presence of alternative resistance determinants. Elucidation of additional resistance determinants may help better define the genetic basis of carbapenem resistance. A key strength of the present analytical framework is the integration of SNP-based GWAS across seven reference genomes and a reference-independent unitig-based approach, enabling improved detection and prioritization of resistance-associated candidate loci.

Clonal population structure is a major confounding factor in bacterial GWAS [16]. Accordingly, population structure of the dataset was characterized prior to association testing. Bayesian clustering resolved ten distinct lineages (BAPS_1 – BAPS_10). ST1 (BAPS_9), ST2 (BAPS_2, BAPS_5) and ST32 (BAPS_10) corresponded to major carbapenem-resistant sequence types (Fig. 1), whereas ST3 (BAPS_8) represented a predominantly susceptible lineage (Fig. 1). The ST1, ST2, and ST32 are the most prevalent CRAB sequence types. Multidimensional scaling (MDS) supported this lineage structure (Supplementary Fig. S4), with the first ten axes capturing more than 80% of the population structure variance. Major lineages were clearly separated along MDS1 and MDS2 (Supplementary Fig. S5a and S5b), consistent with the strong clonality reported in other bacterial pathogens [17]. The control of population structure in the present study is illustrated by the Quantile-Quantile plots (Fig. 2a, b), which indicate adequate control of inflation for the observed P values across the lower range (~ –log₁₀P < 2).

GWAS identified OXA-23 as the strongest association (Fig. 2), highlighting its role as the primary determinant of carbapenem resistance in this dataset. The efflux pump repressor AdeL [12] and OXA-66 variants [18] have been implicated in carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii. The present study identified two OXA-66 variants as significant associations. OXA-66 (Leu167Val) was identified in both unitig- and SNP-based analyses, whereas OXA-66 (Ile129Leu) was identified in only unitig-based analysis (Fig. 2). Residues Ile129 and Leu167 forms part of the enzyme binding pocket, and their substitution has been suggested to facilitate carbapenem binding and increased carbapenemase activity [18–20]. AdeL-associated resistance was observed in a small subset of isolates carrying truncated proteins, AdeL(1–95) (n = 12) and AdeL(1–210) (n = 13) (Supplementary Fig. S10). These truncated proteins were almost exclusively present in resistant isolates, with a single AdeL(1–95)-containing isolate being susceptible. Notably, AdeL(1–95)-containing isolates belong to the largely susceptible ST3 lineage (BAPS_8), and isolates with both the truncated AdeL proteins lacked OXA-23 (Supplementary Fig. S10). AdeL is the repressor of RND efflux pump adeFGH in A. baumannii and its overexpression leads to almost twofold increase in the Imipenem MIC [12]. Therefore, RND efflux pumps regulators, including AdeL, are attractive drug targets to combat AMR in A. baumannii [21]. The present study provides support for previously implicated resistance determinants.

A large number of significant SNPs (606 ± 64 across seven reference genomes) were identified. As an initial prioritization attempt, candidate loci were selected based on association metrics and available structural information. Structural mapping and literature evidence indicated that the mutations in membrane porins (Leu382Pro in OprD and ΔSer338 in DcaP) and proteins involved in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) transport (PqiB) and assembly (LptD) could be involved in altered membrane permeability (Fig. 3) [22]. However, these associations could be influenced by linkage with OXA-23 and the OXA-66 variants, as found for the LptD mutations in the conditional analysis (Fig. 3). The mutations in OprD, DcaP, and PqiB were supported in conditional analyses across one, three, and five reference genomes, suggesting their possible implication in carbapenem resistance (Fig. 3) [23–26]. A list of intergenic and intragenic loci supported in conditional analyses across five or more reference genomes are given in the supplementary information (“Intragenic.tsv” and “Intergenic.tsv”).

One of the intragenic association supported in conditional analyses across seven reference genomes corresponded to a mutation (Leu167Phe) in a LysR transcription regulator (Fig. 4). This regulator is located in proximity to the biofilm-associated pgaABCD operon, and residue Leu167 is mostly conserved across homologous sequences (Fig. 4). These observations suggest the potential functional relevance of this transcriptional regulator in antimicrobial resistance. One of the intergenic SNPs supported in conditional analyses across reference strains corresponded to the substitution 3,528,659 C > T (Fig. 4). This substitution is located between the acyltransferase (lpx) and glycosyltransferase enzymes. These essential enzymes are involved in the modification of Lipid A and LPS remodeling, the processes known to contribute to bacterial pathogenesis and antimicrobial resistance [27].

The carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii seems to be largely determined by the presence of OXA-23. However, other mechanisms may gain significance in the absence of OXA-23. The present study suggests that the processes related to efflux pump regulation (AdeL), membrane permeability (OprD, DcaP), LPS transport (PqiB), LPS remodeling (acyltransferase, glycosyltransferase), and biofilm formation (LysR) might be involved in carbapenem resistance. Interpretation of genotype–phenotype associations in bacterial GWAS is inherently challenging due to the clonal population structure, genome-wide linkage disequilibrium, limited sampling, and observational noise [28]. The present study suggests that combining conditional GWAS with a multi-reference mapping strategy may help prioritize robust candidate loci from a large set of significant associations. The underlying basis of this apparent robustness warrants further investigation. Co-occurrence analysis of the known resistance determinants and newly identified candidate loci may provide additional insights into the genetic basis of carbapenem resistance, but this analysis was not explored in the present study. The present study is constrained by reliance on heterogeneous publicly available AST data and the use of binary resistance phenotypes. The identified loci should be interpreted as prioritized candidates rather than confirmed causal determinants.

Methods

Acquisition, preprocessing, and quality control

Using the BV-BRC command-line interface (v1.035) [29], publicly available A. baumannii genomes were queried, yielding 11,824 genome identifiers. These identifiers were subsequently queried for experimental AST results, producing 19,413 resistant and 5,344 susceptible phenotype entries. AST records were filtered for carbapenems (doripenem, ertapenem, imipenem, and meropenem), generating a carbapenems-specific dataset of 862 resistant and 841 susceptible genomes. AST results for a subset of genomes (n = 45) indicated resistance to some carbapenems but susceptibility to others; therefore, these genomes were excluded from analysis. This resulted in a preliminary cohort of 817 resistant and 796 susceptible isolates. An additional 201 carbapenem-resistant isolates from a recent molecular epidemiological study from the Oceanic region were incorporated [30], yielding a final dataset of 1,814 genomes (1,018 resistant, 796 susceptible). SRA/Assembly accessions, geographic origin, isolation year, and isolation source metadata were retrieved for the 1814 isolates.

Sequence reads were retrieved from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) using Sra-tools (v3.1.0) [31]. Raw reads were quality-filtered and adapter-trimmed using Fastp (v0.22.0) [32], and quality summaries were assessed using MultiQC (v1.14) [33]. Trimmed reads were assembled de novo using SPAdes (v3.15.5) [34]. For isolates lacking raw SRA data, genome assemblies were downloaded directly from NCBI [35]. Assembly quality was evaluated in two stages using QUAST (v5.2.0) and CheckM (v1.2.2) [36, 37]. QUAST was used to assess assembly statistics relative to the AB5075 reference genome, and assemblies with > 80% reference coverage and N50 > 20,000 bp were retained. CheckM was then applied to estimate completeness and contamination based on lineage-specific marker genes; assemblies with < 99% completeness or > 5% contamination were excluded. Only genomes passing both quality filters were included in downstream analyses.

Phylogenetic analysis

Core genome SNP alignments were generated using Snippy (v4.6.0) with AB5075 as the reference genome. Variant sites were extracted using SNP-sites (v2.5.1) [38]. Recombinant regions were detected and masked using Gubbins (v3.3.1) [39]. The recombination-corrected alignment was used to infer a maximum-likelihood phylogeny in RAxML (v8.2.12) under the GTR + Γ substitution model with 500 bootstrap replicates [40]. The resulting tree was visualized and annotated in R (v4.3.3) using the ggtree (v3.10.1) and ggtreeExtra (v1.12.0) packages [41, 42]. The population structure was inferred using RhierBAPS (v1.1.4) [43, 44]. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was performed using mlst (v2.23.0).

Multi-reference strategy

To mitigate reference bias and capture pan-genome variation, raw sequence reads were independently aligned to seven representative A. baumannii genomes spanning clinical, environmental, and epidemiologically diverse backgrounds. The selected references included ATCC19606 (susceptible clinical isolate; BioSample SAMN12389466), ATCC17978 (clinical meningitis isolate; BioSample SAMN02604331), K09 (environmental isolate from soil; BioSample SAMN12769618), AB5075 (hypervirulent clinical isolate; BioSample SAMN31681936) [45], and three isolates from a recent CRAB epidemiological study in the Oceania region: FJ03, FJ16, and FJ20 (BioSamples SAMN38749311, SAMN37733145, SAMN37736705, respectively) [30].

Genome-wide association analysis

Raw sequence reads and genome assemblies (SRA data not available) were processed with Snippy (v4.6.0) to generate reference-aligned BAM files for each of the seven reference genomes. Variant calling was performed using joint genotyping in GATK (v4.5.0.0) [46]. Missing genotypes were imputed using the major allele at the corresponding site. Bifrost, wrapped as unitig-caller (v1.3.0), was used to extract unitigs [47]. GWAS was performed using the factored spectrally transformed linear mixed model (FaST-LMM) implemented in Pyseer (v1.3.11) [48]. The model incorporated geographic origin and isolation year as fixed covariates, and a phylogeny-derived kinship matrix as a random effect. For the conditional analysis, the known resistance determinants were included as additional covariates [49]. SNPs were annotated with SnpEff (v5.0e) [50]. Bonferroni correction was applied using the number of unique unitig and SNP patterns evaluated in the respective analyses.
