Melanocortin 3 Receptors Do Not Specifically Localize to Primary Cilia in Cultured Human and Rodent Neurons
Authors: Niels Vos, Alessandro Moro, Wim Van Hul, Lotte Kleinendorst, Ralph J. Florijn, Susanne E. la Fleur, Matthijs Verhage, Mieke M. van Haelst, Ruud F. Toonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe melanocortin‐3 receptor (MC3R) and the melanocortin‐4 receptor (MC4R), both expressed in hypothalamic nuclei, are key downstream effectors of leptin signaling and play important roles in energy homeostasis. While pathogenic variants in the MC4R gene represent the most common cause of monogenic obesity, the clinical significance of MC3R variants is less clear. MC4R localizes to the primary cilium, a sensory organelle present on nearly all human cells. To better understand the pathophysiological mechanisms of MC3R variants, we investigated whether MC3R localizes to the primary cilium and assessed the impact of rare MC3R variants identified in individuals with obesity on ciliary expression. Using human RPE cells, human NGN2‐induced iNeurons, and primary mouse hypothalamic neurons, we found that, in contrast to MC4R, neither wild type MC3R nor rare MC3R variants localized specifically to the primary cilium in vitro in any cell type, including hypothalamic neurons. These findings suggest that MC3R and MC4R may utilize distinct signaling pathways or that additional factors, such as accessory proteins, are required for MC3R targeting to primary cilia in vivo. Further studies are needed to clarify the role of MC3R variants in monogenic obesity and their broader implications for human disease.

Introduction

Cilia are hair‐like cellular membrane organelles that can be categorized as being motile or immotile. Motile cilia perform important biological functions through facilitation of flow of fluids and cells in various tissues, mainly in the respiratory and reproductive tract and specific cells in the brain [1, 2]. Most human cells are equipped with one single immotile primary cilium, an antenna‐like organelle. The primary cilium senses extracellular signals (both chemical and mechanical) and transduces them into the cell to exert intracellular effects [3, 4, 5]. Expression of various receptors on primary cilia and ciliary functioning is required for their signaling cascades. Obesity is a well‐described feature of several ciliopathies [6, 7]. It is not fully understood how disrupted ciliary function leads to obesity, but it is likely linked to proper signaling in the leptin–melanocortin pathway. In short, the leptin–melanocortin pathway consists of peripheral energy availability cues, like leptin secreted by adipocytes, that are monitored in the brain, particularly in the hypothalamus. Hypothalamic proopiomelanocortin (POMC) and Agouti‐related peptide (AgRP)/Neuropeptide Y (NPY) neurons subsequently stimulate or inhibit particular melanocortin receptors (MCRs). Via this signaling cascade feeding and energy expenditure are regulated to maintain energy homeostasis [8, 9]. Recent studies have shown that the melanocortin‐4 receptor (MC4R; OMIM #155541), an essential regulator of human energy homeostasis in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus, is expressed and functions at the neuronal primary cilium [10, 11, 12].

Similarly, the melanin‐concentrating hormone receptor 1 (MCHR1) is expressed in hypothalamic neurons and is believed to play a role in energy homeostasis [13, 14]. MCHR1 has also been shown to localize to neuronal primary cilia [15, 16]. Altered primary cilia expression of MCHR1 in Bardet–Biedl syndrome is thought to play a role in the energy balance disturbance in this syndrome [17]. MCHR1 is one of two receptors (MCHR1 and MCHR2) for the melanin‐concentrating hormone (MCH), which has an orexigenic effect [13, 14]. Conversely, stimulation of MC4R has an anorexigenic effect [18, 19, 20].

Pathogenic MC4R variants are currently the most common cause of monogenic nonsyndromic obesity in humans and explain severe obesity in approximately 3%–6% of affected individuals [21, 22]. Pathogenic MC4R variants typically result in hyperphagia and early onset obesity, most severely when these variants are present in homozygous or compound heterozygous state [21, 22]. Certain pathogenic variants seem to affect localization at the primary cilium [11].

MC4R, a G‐protein‐coupled receptor (GPCR), is one of five human MCRs (MC1R, MC2R, MC3R, MC4R, and MC5R). The melanocortin‐3 receptor (MC3R; OMIM #155540) is also highly expressed in the hypothalamus, but particularly in the arcuate nucleus [23, 24]. It is stimulated by POMC‐derived neuropeptides, so‐called melanocortins, specifically α‐ and γ‐melanocyte stimulating hormone [25]. MC3R plays a role in energy homeostasis and has been suggested to affect linear growth and sexual maturation [26, 27, 28]. Mice lacking Mc3r (Mc3r −/− mice) eat less (hypophagia), but due to a higher feed efficiency (i.e., ability to store a larger proportion of energy after consumption) develop a higher weight and in particular fat mass than wild‐type littermates [29]. The combination of hypophagia and reduced locomotor activity likely explains an observed lower lean mass in these mice [29]. Pathogenic MC3R variants have been previously associated with susceptibility to obesity in humans in dominant form [30], but more recent studies debate this and suggest a possible recessive inheritance [28]. Whether MC3R, like MC4R and MCHR1, also localizes and functions through neuronal primary cilia signaling in humans is currently less clear. Although a recent study did show some localization of MC3R at the primary cilium in inner medullary collecting duct 3 (IMCD3) cells of mice [10, 11], another study did not detect MC3R signal in primary cilia in mouse hypothalamus [31].

The aims of our study were to further investigate primary cilium localization of MC3R in vitro in alternative relevant cell types, to assess the potential of human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) derived neurons as relevant models to MCR ciliary targeting, and to evaluate this as an option to validate the effect of rare variants in MC3R. Additionally, we wanted to confirm previous reports that MC4R targets to primary cilia in all cell types we tested, including iPSC‐derived iNeurons.

Cell Culture

In this study, we used human HEK293T and hTERT‐RPE‐1 cellines, primary rat glia, primary mouse hypothalamic neurons, and NGN2‐induced human iNeurons.

HEK293T (ATCC, CRL‐3216) and hTERT‐RPE1 cells (ATCC, CRL‐4000) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), penicillin–streptomycin, and nonessential amino acids (Gibco). HEK293T cells were transfected at ~70% confluency in serum‐free DMEM using the calcium phosphate method. RPE1 cells were infected with lentivirus in serum‐free medium. Cells were cultured on Poly‐d‐Lysine (Sigma) coated 18 mm glass coverslips in 12‐well plates for imaging experiments.

Rat astrocytes were cultured from P1 newborn rat pups according to Meijer et al. [32].

Primary mouse hypothalamic neurons were cultured from embryonic day (E18) C57/Bl6 pups. Brains were removed and hypothalamus was isolated from the base of the skull and dissected in Hank Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, Thermo Fisher; #14170138) and treated with Trypsin for 20 min at 37°C. The isolated neurons were plated at a density of 50 K on rat glia feeder layer on 18 mm coverslips and cultured in Neurobasal medium (Thermo Fisher; #11570556) supplemented with 2% B‐27 (Thermo Fisher), 18 mM HEPES (Thermo Fisher), 0.5 mM Glutamax (Thermo Fisher), and 1× Pen/Strep (Thermo Fisher). Medium was replaced 50% once a week.

BioniC13 (BIONi010‐C‐13, EBiSC, African American 15–19 y male) iPSC cultures were maintained in TeSR‐E8 medium (# 05990, Stemcell Technologies) + 0.1% Pen/Strep (Invitrogen) on Matrigel‐coated plates. When confluent, cells were dissociated using Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (GIBCO, Thermo Fisher) and replated in TeSR‐E8 medium supplemented with 5 µM ROCK Inhibitor (Y‐27632; Tebu Bio). Genomic integrity of iPSC lines was periodically tested for copy number variations using single‐nucleotide polymorphism arrays (Illumina, Infinium Global screening Array) as previously reported in Meijer et al. [32]. In addition, cell cultures were regularly tested for mycoplasma (Eurofins). To start neuronal induction, E8 medium was replaced 8 days after plating by N2‐supplemented medium (DMEM/F12 medium, 200 mM Glutamax, 20% Dextrose, 1% N2 supplement B, and 0.1% Pen/Strep, all Invitrogen) supplemented with doxycycline hyclate (2 µg/mL, Sigma Aldrich) and dual SMAD inhibitors (100 nM LDN193189, 10 µM SB431542, 2 µM XAV939, from Stemgent, Tebu‐Bio, and Sigma‐Aldrich, respectively). On Day 2, medium was refreshed, and puromycin (concentration determined separately for each iPSC line, 1–3 µg/mL, Merck‐Millipore) was added. This step was repeated for another 24 h. On Day 4, cultures were refreshed with N2‐supplemented medium with 10 μM FUDR (Sigma‐Aldrich). The next day, iNeurons were replated onto 18 mm glial feeder layer coverslips (50k for mass culture). The glial feeder layer was created by plating rat glia grown on etched glass coverslips applied with 0.1 mg/mL Poly‐d‐Lysine and 0.2 mg/mL rat tail collagen (BD Biosciences) for mass cultures [32]. Four days after replating, mass cultures were treated once with 2 µM AraC (Sigma).

iNeurons were maintained in Neurobasal medium, supplemented with 200 mM glutamax, 20% dextrose, NEAA, B27, 0.1% P/S, 0.5% fetal bovine serum, 10 ng/mL BDNF, 10 ng/mL CNTF, 10 ng/mL GDNF, 2 µg/mL doxycycline hyclate, at 37°C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. Medium was replaced with 50% fresh medium twice a week.

Results

To study primary cilium expression of MC3R and MC4R in human neurons, we established in vitro primary cilia assays with RPE cells as positive controls, and NGN2‐induced human neurons (iNeurons) [34]. Subsequently, we assessed MC4R targeting to primary cilia of these cells. Widespread localization of EGFP‐tagged MC4R was observed throughout RPE cells, likely in Golgi and endoplasmic reticulum membranes, in addition to its localization to primary cilia marked with AcTubulin (Figure 1A,B), in line with previous reports [11]. Similar results were obtained using a doxycycline‐inducible promoter to drive expression of MC4R‐EGFP. Addition of doxycycline resulted in marked ciliary expression of MC4R‐EGFP at 1 and 2 days after induction (Figure S2). In human iNeurons at 5 weeks in vitro (5WIV), MC4R‐EGFP showed marked co‐localization with the primary cilia marker ACIII, 6 days after lentiviral infection (Figure 1C,D). Hence, MC4R‐EGFP efficiently targets to primary cilia in RPE cells and human iNeurons.

We repeated these experiments in iNeurons and RPE cells for MC3R and noticed that, while MC3R‐EGFP was expressed widely throughout NGN2‐induced iNeurons, MC3R‐EGFP did not specifically localize to the primary cilium (Figure 2A,B). Also, in RPE cells, MC3R‐EGFP showed widespread localization but no specific targeting to cilia (Figure 2C,D). Infection with a higher concentration of lentivirus (MC3R‐1.5×) did increase overall expression, but not primary cilia localization (Figure 2E). Hence, in contrast to MC4R, MC3R did not specifically localize to primary cilia in human RPE cells or induced iNeurons.

To address the possibility that MC3R requires specific accessory proteins for proper targeting to cilia, which might not be present in heterologous RPE cells or iNeurons, we used primary mouse hypothalamic neurons. Again, MC4R‐EGFP showed robust localization to ACIII‐marked primary cilia in 14DIV neurons fixed 3 DPI (Figure 3A,B). In addition, we showed that a canonical live marker for cilia, smoothened fused to mScarlet (Smo‐mScarlet), specifically targeted to cilia in these neurons (Figure 3C,D). In contrast to MC4R targeting, MC3R‐EGFP did also not target specifically to primary cilia in hypothalamic neurons (Figure 4A,B). Thus, also in neurons with endogenous expression of MC3/4 receptors we did not observe specific targeting of MC3R to cilia.

Finally, we assessed if changes in primary cilia expression can be observed in RPE cells with different rare MC3R variants. We used previously reported rare MC3R variants that have been identified in patients with obesity and have been functionally characterized [30, 35]. No effect on primary cilia targeting was observed for the NM_019888.3c.272A > G p.(Asn91Ser) (N128S), c.520G > A p.(Val174Ile) (V211I), c.784C > G p.(Leu262Val) (L299V), and c.893 T > G p.(Ile298Ser) (I335S) MC3R variants (Figure S3 and Table S1).

Discussion

In previous studies, MCHR1 was shown to localize to neuronal primary cilia [15, 17]. MC4R was shown to specifically localize to primary cilia in mouse embryonic fibroblasts, RPE and inner‐medullary collecting duct (IMCD3) cells and in PVN neurons of MC4R‐GFP/+ mice [10, 11]. Here, we show similar results of MC4R localization to primary cilia in RPE cells, human iNeurons, and mouse hypothalamic neurons. In addition, Smo‐mScarlet, which is known to be targeted almost exclusively to the primary cilium, also targeted to primary cilia in iNeurons (Figure 3C,D).

Like the clinical impact of MC3R variants, less is known about ciliary localization of MC3R in comparison to MC4R. One would expect similarities in ciliary localization based on the relatedness of these receptors, expression in hypothalamic tissues and assumed clinical effects. The aforementioned studies did assess localization of MC3R in IMCD3 cells, showing significantly less localization of MC3R to the primary cilium than MC4R in mice [10, 11]. This might be due to cell type‐dependent differences in localization, although also in hypothalamic nuclei of mice, MC3R immunofluorescent signals did not co‐localize with primary cilia [31] and in our human RPE cells and iNeurons, no specific localization of MC3R to primary cilia was observed either. Alternatively, certain accessory proteins might be required for correct and specific localization to primary cilia. Melanocortin 2 receptor accessory protein (MRAP) and MRAP2 have been identified as accessory proteins that play a role in MCR‐specific targeting and functioning [36, 37]. Tissue‐specific absence of these accessory proteins, required for proper MCR targeting and functioning, might be an explanation for reduced MC3R cilia localization. Transcriptomic and proteomic analysis did not detect expression of MRAP or MRAP2 in RPE cells or iNeurons [34, 38]. In hypothalamic neurons, which do express these proteins, we also did not observe specific localization of MC3R to the primary cilium. We were able to successfully increase MC3R expression, but this did not lead to more specific localization to the primary cilium (Figure 2), indicating that targeting is not dose dependent. Targeting was also not time dependent (Figure S2). We did not observe an effect of rare MC3R variants on primary cilia targeting (Figure S3).

Follow‐up studies that evaluate the effect of additional accessory proteins on neuronal ciliary expression and usage of patient‐derived induced neuronal cells could perhaps lead to new insights. Certain MC3R variants have been reported to increase susceptibility to obesity [35, 39, 40]. In clinical practice, segregation analysis of heterozygous MC3R variants of uncertain significance in patients and families with obesity is challenging and mostly inconclusive. The near absence of MC3R in primary cilia could point toward a less pronounced direct role of this receptor in the sensing of energy reserves and energy homeostasis, compared to MC4R. Obesity due to heterozygous pathogenic MC3R variants might be dependent on other genetic factors (oligo‐ or polygenic influences), epigenetic and nongenetic factors, for example, through interaction with other (G‐protein coupled) receptors, such as G‐protein coupled receptor 83 (GPR83) and growth hormone secretagogue receptor [41, 42, 43]. We aim to further study this by clinical comparison of patients with obesity with MC3R variants of unknown significance and their first‐degree carrier and noncarrier relatives, in combination with functional assessment of MC3R variants, studying patient‐derived iPSCs and polygenic risk scores. Clarification of pathophysiology can hopefully lead to better personalized therapeutic options for these individuals.

In conclusion, in contrast to the MC4R, the MC3R seems to be not specifically targeted to primary cilia, also not in hypothalamic cells.
