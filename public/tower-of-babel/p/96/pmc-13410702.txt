Health vulnerability and loneliness among adolescent and young adult populations: a comparison of generations based on a nationwide survey in Japan
Authors: Anri Gen, Masako Okamura, Yosuke Uchitomi, Maiko Fujimori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionUsing nationally representative data, we investigated differences in loneliness across generations in Japan, focusing on the unique factors affecting adolescents and young adults.MethodsWe analysed data from the 2022 and 2023 Basic Survey on Human Connection conducted by the Japanese Government. The dataset included responses from 22 361 individuals aged 16 years and older. Loneliness was assessed using the three-item University of California at Los Angeles Loneliness Scale (UCLA-LS). Multiple linear regression models adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, social and health-related variables were employed to identify the predictors of loneliness across age groups. Interaction terms between age group and explanatory variables were included to assess whether these associations differed across generations.ResultsAdolescents and young adults, as well as middle-aged adults, reported greater loneliness than older adults (mean UCLA-LS scores: 6.80, 6.84 and 6.29, respectively; p<0.001). Significant loneliness predictors included a lack of confidants, poor self-rated overall health, low life satisfaction, anxiety and non-participation in social activities. Interaction analysis demonstrated that the association between poor health and loneliness was significantly stronger in adolescents and young adults than in older adults (β=0.80, 95% CI 0.57 to 1.04; false discovery rate-adjusted p<0.001). In addition, the associations of anxiety and unemployment with loneliness were stronger among working-age populations than among older adults. These findings suggest age-related differences in the factors associated with loneliness.ConclusionLoneliness among adolescents and young adults appears to be more strongly associated with health vulnerability, whereas psychosocial and economic factors were more strongly associated with loneliness in working-age populations. These findings highlight the importance of developing age-specific strategies to reduce loneliness, particularly among younger individuals experiencing poor health.

Introduction

While loneliness is a subjective experience, social isolation refers to an objective lack of meaningful connections with others, including friends, communities and social groups.1 The former is recognised as a significant risk factor for various health conditions, including depression, cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality.2–5 Notably, depression has been strongly linked to suicide; similarly, loneliness is recognised to increase suicide risk.6 7 Recently, social and cultural changes have diminished everyday social interactions, creating conditions in which feelings of loneliness and isolation are more likely to arise.8 The COVID-19 pandemic has further exacerbated this issue by limiting opportunities for social contact, consultation and emotional support, thus intensifying underlying vulnerabilities.9

It is important to examine loneliness in Japan because it is one of the most rapidly ageing societies in the world, with major changes in household structure and growing concerns about social isolation among older adults, including solitary deaths and suicide. In addition, demographic and socioeconomic changes may shape age-specific vulnerabilities in the country, not only among older adults but also among younger populations. The proportion of single-person households has increased markedly in recent decades,10 and many young adults face labour market instability characterised by difficulties in securing stable employment and the expansion of precarious work.11 Furthermore, prolonged social withdrawal (hikikomori), first described in Japan, represents a persistent form of extreme social isolation and is widely reported among adolescents and young adults.12 These factors underscore the importance of examining loneliness within the Japanese context, particularly among younger generations. Although some of these factors are also present in other countries, their scale and demographic context in Japan may create distinctive patterns of vulnerability across generations.

Previous studies have reported that adolescents and young adults tend to be lonelier compared with other generations,13–15 experiencing unique life transitions, including starting school, entering the workforce, forming relationships and raising children, all of which can affect mental health. Therefore, addressing loneliness in this population is a serious public health concern. In Japan, this issue is particularly important because, although the overall number of suicides has declined over time, suicide remains the leading cause of death among individuals aged 15–39 years.16 This contrast between overall national improvement and persistent vulnerability among younger populations highlights the importance of examining loneliness among adolescents and young adults as a distinct public health concern.

Accordingly, several governments have implemented measures to reduce this condition. For instance, the UK launched several targeted initiatives directed to young adults, including the public campaign ‘Lift Someone Out of Loneliness’17 and the support platform ‘Tackling Loneliness Hub’.18 19 In 2021, the Japanese Government established the Office for Measures to Combat Loneliness and Isolation within the Cabinet Secretariat, reflecting growing recognition of loneliness and social isolation as national policy issues in the context of rapid population ageing and increasing concerns about solitary deaths and suicide among older adults.20 21 These developments underscore the importance of examining loneliness within the Japanese context across the population, while also highlighting younger generations as an important group of concern.

In addition to differences in loneliness levels across generations, several studies have investigated whether risk factors for loneliness vary by age group.13–15 A study using nationally representative data from the UK identified age-related differences in the key factors linked to loneliness. Among young adults aged 15–29 years, self-rated health, limitations in daily activities, depressive symptoms and frequency of social contact were all associated with loneliness; conversely, among older adults aged over 60 years, only self-reported depression was associated with loneliness.13 Contrarily, a US-based study did not identify any age-specific predictors of loneliness. Instead, variables such as household income, household size, marital status, health and the frequency of social interactions consistently correlated with loneliness across all age groups.15 In Japan, several large-scale studies have also examined loneliness using nationwide survey data. Murayama et al reported age-specific patterns in loneliness and social isolation during the COVID-19 pandemic using data from the JACSIS 2020–2021 surveys.22 Similarly, Stickley and Ueda identified higher odds of loneliness among younger adults compared with older adults in multivariable analyses of pandemic-era data.23 However, these studies primarily described differences across age groups or treated age as an independent predictor and did not investigate whether the associations between loneliness and risk factors varied across age groups using interaction analyses. Overall, despite the availability of nationwide data in Japan, evidence on generation-specific differences in the associations between loneliness and its risk factors remains limited.

Thus, we conducted an analysis using a large-scale, nationally representative dataset from Japan, one of the most extensive social surveys in Asia, to investigate generational differences in the risk factors for loneliness, focusing on adolescents and young adults. The primary aim of this study was to examine the sociodemographic and health-related factors associated with loneliness in the general population and to assess whether the strength and pattern of these associations differ across age groups. We hypothesised that the factors related to loneliness would vary by generation, reflecting age-specific vulnerabilities in social and health contexts. The findings may provide new insights into public health policies targeting loneliness in this population.

Methods

Study design

We used an anonymised dataset from the 2022 and 2023 Basic Survey on Human Connection24 25 conducted by the Japanese government, as a general survey under the Japanese Statistics Act, a national cross-sectional survey targeting approximately 20 000 individuals aged 16 years and above across Japan. Although the survey has been conducted annually since 2021, the question items have varied considerably across years; therefore, we used the most recent 2 years of data available at the time of the study, as these years shared relatively similar question items. The wording and response categories of all variables included in the present analysis were identical across the two survey years, ensuring comparability of measures. To account for potential temporal effects, survey year was included as a covariate in all regression analyses. Participants were selected by random sampling from the Basic Resident Register, a national population registry that covers virtually all residents legally registered in Japan. Random sampling from a national population register was used to reduce selection bias and enhance national representativeness across geographic regions and demographic groups. Respondents completed the survey either online or by mail. Respondents did not receive financial incentives for participation. The response rates in 2022 and 2023 were 56.1% (11 219 responses) and 55.7% (11 142 responses), respectively. Survey items included basic demographic characteristics, questions related to loneliness and social isolation and items on daily life and interpersonal interactions. Informed consent was not obtained from the respondents because the study was conducted in accordance with the Statistics Act of Japan. All data were anonymised before analysis, and only the aggregated results were reported to ensure the privacy of the participants.

Outcome variable

The University of California at Los Angeles Loneliness Scale (UCLA-LS), particularly Version 3 consisting of 20 items, is a widely used measure of loneliness internationally and has demonstrated strong reliability and validity.26 A Japanese version of the UCLA-LS Version 3 has also been developed and validated.27 We used the three-item short form derived from the Japanese version of the UCLA-LS Version 3,28 which was implemented in the national survey. The short-form version has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity in previous validation studies comparing the full 20-item scale and its short forms.29

The scale consisted of three questions: (1) Do you feel that you lack companionship?; (2) Do you feel left out? and (3) Do you feel isolated from others? Respondents answered each question on a 4-point Likert scale: 1, 2, 3 and 4 points were assigned to ‘Never’, ‘Rarely’, ‘Sometimes’ and ‘Always’, respectively. In the short-form version, no specific cut-off score was established. Instead, the responses were summed up to create a total score, with higher scores indicating greater loneliness.

Predictor variables

We included predictor variables based on previous studies10 11 29 30 that identified sociodemographic factors such as age, sex, marital status, household composition, household income, perceived financial situation, education level and employment status. Additionally, we incorporated social and behavioural variables, including frequency of outings, participation in social activities, availability of confidants, anxiety, providing support to others, physical and mental health conditions and life satisfaction. Anxiety was assessed using a binary self-reported item asking whether respondents currently experienced feelings of anxiety or worry in their daily lives (yes/no). In contrast, physical and mental health status was measured using a single-item global self-rated health question, rated on a 5-point scale from ‘very good’ to ‘very poor’. While anxiety reflects the presence of a specific psychological state, self-rated health represents a broader evaluation of overall physical and mental well-being. These variables therefore capture conceptually distinct constructs. Age was categorised into three groups: adolescents and young adults, defined as individuals younger than 40 years; middle-aged adults, identified as those aged 40–64 years and older adults, those aged ≥65 years. The variables were categorised according to previous research and practical considerations (online supplemental table S1).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means, SDs, medians and IQRs, were calculated for continuous variables across the three age groups. Group differences were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction. For categorical variables, frequencies and proportions were calculated for each group, and differences were assessed using the χ2 test.

A multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the associations between background factors and loneliness. All prespecified variables were included in the fully adjusted models based on theoretical relevance and prior literature. This approach was chosen to avoid potential biases associated with data-driven variable selection procedures such as stepwise methods. UCLA-LS was used as the dependent variable, and the following explanatory variables were entered simultaneously: age group, sex, marital status, household composition, education level, employment status, household income, frequency of outings, participation in social activities, availability of confidants, anxiety, providing support to others, physical and mental health status, life satisfaction, perceived financial situation and survey year (2022 vs 2023). Before model fitting, regression assumptions were evaluated. Linearity and homoscedasticity were assessed visually using residual-versus-fitted plots, and normality of residuals was examined using Q-Q plots. Multicollinearity was assessed using generalised variance inflation factors (GVIFs). For categorical variables with multiple df, adjusted values (GVIF1/(2×Df)) were used to allow comparability across predictors. Influential observations were assessed using Cook’s distance. Diagnostic plots are presented in online supplemental figure S1, and detailed results of these diagnostic assessments are provided in the online supplemental methods, online supplemental tables S2 and S3. To examine the differences in the associations between explanatory variables and loneliness across age groups, interaction terms between age group and each explanatory variable included in the main regression model were evaluated, except for survey year. To account for multiple testing across interaction terms, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Because the interaction analyses were exploratory and involved multiple tests, the FDR approach was used to reduce the likelihood of false-positive findings while maintaining adequate statistical power. For the regression analysis, a complete dataset of 20 990 participants was used after excluding 1631 individuals with missing data (7.2%). Multiple imputations were performed using predictive mean matching to assess the potential impact of missing data, and a sensitivity analysis compared results from the imputed datasets with those from the complete-case analysis. Two-sided tests were used to calculate all p values, and statistical significance was determined at the p<0.05 level. Statistical analyses were performed using R V.4.5.2. Additional R packages used included dplyr (V.1.2.0), mice (V.3.19.0), car (V.3.1-5) and dunn.test (V.1.3.7).31–35

Patient and public involvement

This study was conducted as part of a research project funded by RISTEX, in which individuals with lived experience participate as project members. However, they were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this specific secondary analysis study.

Results

Overall, 22 361 individuals completed the survey over 2 years, yielding an overall response rate of 56.6%. The participants’ mean age was 55.9 years (SD 18.7). The study population included 4823 adolescents and young adults, 9115 middle-aged adults and 8423 older adults. The overall UCLA-LS score had a mean of 6.63 (SD 2.21) and a median of 6.

A significant difference in loneliness scores was observed among the three groups (p<0.001). The mean UCLA-LS scores were 6.80 (SD 2.33), 6.84 (SD 2.21) and 6.29 (SD 2.09) for adolescents and young adults, middle-aged adults and older adults, respectively. Post hoc pairwise comparisons confirmed that loneliness scores were significantly higher in both adolescents and young adults, as well as in middle-aged adults, than in older adults (p<0.001 for both comparisons). Conversely, no significant difference was found between adolescents and young adults and middle-aged adults. Additional background characteristics are presented in online supplemental table S1.

The results of the multivariate analysis are presented in table 1. Factors associated with higher loneliness scores included lack of confidants, poor physical and mental health, low life satisfaction, not providing support to others, anxiety, non-participation in social activities, unemployment, being unmarried, higher education level, a lower frequency of outings and living alone.

Discussion

We investigated generational patterns of loneliness, focusing on adolescents and young adults. In this population, adolescents and young adults and middle-aged adults each reported greater loneliness than did older adults. Furthermore, poor self-rated overall health had a stronger association with loneliness than in older generations. A key strength of this study is that it is the first in Asia to examine factors associated with loneliness from a generational perspective, using a large-scale survey of over 20 000 participants. The large sample size enabled multivariable and interaction analyses and improved the precision of the estimated associations. This analytical approach enabled a more nuanced understanding of age-specific vulnerability.

Self-rated overall health conditions were more strongly associated with loneliness in adolescents and young adults compared with older generations. Although individuals in this age group are generally less likely to experience illness, encountering health problems at a young age may carry greater psychological and social challenges because such events can be unexpected and difficult to face. A previous study found that poor health and functional limitations correlated with loneliness among individuals aged 15–59 years, but not among those aged 60 years and older.13 Our findings are consistent with this pattern, and the interaction analyses further demonstrated that the association between poor self-rated overall health and loneliness was stronger among adolescents and young adults than among middle-aged and older adults. However, another study that conducted interaction analyses for predictors such as health reported no age-specific effects.15 Conversely, our study—using a large nationally representative Japanese dataset—revealed a significant interaction between health status and age: poor self-rated overall health was more strongly associated with loneliness among adolescents and young adults than among older adults, potentially reflecting cultural or contextual factors and underscoring the importance of exploring such interactions across diverse populations.

Several mechanisms may explain why poor self-rated overall health showed a stronger association with loneliness among younger individuals. Adolescence and young adulthood are developmental periods characterised by identity formation, peer integration and transitions into education and employment.36 During these stages, good physical health is often socially normative and expected; therefore, experiencing illness may be perceived as atypical, potentially leading to stigma, reduced participation in social activities and difficulties maintaining peer relationships. Previous studies have also reported stronger associations between poor health and loneliness among younger populations, including the work by Stickley and Koyanagi.37 In addition, a meta-analysis demonstrated that children and adolescents with chronic physical conditions experience significantly higher levels of loneliness than their healthy peers, partly because illness may restrict participation in social activities and peer interactions.38 In contrast, health decline is more common and socially anticipated in later life, which may attenuate its relative impact on loneliness among older adults. Moreover, younger individuals may have fewer coping resources and less established social networks to buffer the social consequences of illness. Together, these life-course and sociocultural mechanisms may help explain the stronger association observed in younger generations.

Given that a lack of confidants was identified as a significant risk factor for loneliness, it is important to establish a comprehensive support system that addresses the unique challenges faced by adolescents and young adults with health conditions and mitigates social isolation. Enhancing peer support networks and expanding online support services may effectively reduce loneliness and improve mental well-being.39 40 Because digital tools are widely used by this population, online platforms represent an accessible and effective means of delivering support. A well-designed and inclusive support system may not only improve physical and mental health but also enhance overall quality of life.

The relationship between age groups and loneliness demonstrated that both adolescents and young adults and middle-aged adults reported greater loneliness than did older adults; however, there was no statistically significant difference between adolescents and young adults and middle-aged adults. While previous studies have reported increased loneliness among adolescents and young adults,41 others have suggested a non-linear relationship between loneliness and age.13–15 Here, the use of three broad age categories may have contributed to the lack of significant differences between the two younger groups. Several factors, such as providing support to others, availability of confidants, life satisfaction, anxiety and self-rated overall health, were associated with loneliness across all generations. These findings are consistent with earlier studies’ results that identified these factors as important correlates of loneliness.42–48

The interaction analyses also revealed that the associations of anxiety and unemployment with loneliness were stronger among adolescents and young adults and middle-aged adults than among older adults. This pattern suggests that psychosocial and economic vulnerabilities may be particularly relevant among working-age populations. Employment provides social identity, daily structure and opportunities for social interaction during working-age years, and therefore unemployment may have a stronger association with loneliness before retirement age. Previous research has also shown that unemployment is associated with poorer mental health and psychological distress.49 Here, the association between employment status and loneliness did not differ significantly between adolescents and young adults and middle-aged adults. Nevertheless, this association was markedly weaker among older adults. Younger groups comprise the working-age population, where employment is crucial in daily life and social interactions, as many individuals are either employed or educated.

While limited research has focused on adolescents and young adults, a previous study reported that economic insecurity was linked to loneliness in young adults. However, a stronger association with employment status was observed in middle-aged individuals.50 Our findings suggest that unemployment may be an important factor associated with loneliness, particularly among working-age individuals. Nonetheless, among younger individuals, variables such as student status and employment conditions, including part-time or non-regular work, may also affect loneliness and should therefore be considered. Further research that accounts for these background factors is needed to better understand the intergenerational relationship between employment status and loneliness. Furthermore, although age-group differences in the associations between loneliness and variables such as education level and frequency of outings were observed, these associations were relatively modest within the fitted models. Since the predictors were measured on different scales and unstandardised coefficients were used, direct comparison across variables is not appropriate.

This study had some limitations. First, due to the cross-sectional design of the survey, causal relationships cannot be established, and the temporal order of the observed associations cannot be determined. Further research is needed to clarify these relationships. Second, although the dataset included a wide range of questions, some items were relatively general and lacked specificity. For instance, questions regarding health conditions did not distinguish between types of illness (physical or mental, chronic or acute, malignant or benign); consequently, it remains unclear which specific health problems are most strongly associated with loneliness. Third, although the survey was administered to individuals of all ages and included general questions, our analysis focused on loneliness among adolescents and young adults. To better capture the unique factors influencing loneliness in this population, future research should incorporate generation-specific questions, including those related to school life, relationships, marriage and childbirth. Fourth, because this was a self-administered questionnaire, response and recall bias may have occurred. Although the response rate was approximately 56%, which is comparable to other large-scale national surveys, non-response bias cannot be ruled out. Because we analysed anonymised secondary data, we were unable to directly compare respondents and non-respondents; therefore, caution is warranted when generalising the findings. Although the surveys were conducted in consecutive years, each annual sample was independently drawn from the nationwide Basic Resident Register using random sampling procedures. Given the size of the sampling frame and the independent selection process, the probability that the same individual was included in both years is considered very low. Because individual identifiers were not available in the anonymised dataset, overlap could not be directly verified; however, substantial duplication is highly unlikely. Finally, although poor physical and mental health had a stronger association with loneliness in adolescents and young adults than they did in older generations, the proportion of individuals with poor health in the adolescent and young adult population was lower (16.3%) than that in middle-aged adults (19.9%) and older adults (22.2%). While the sample size of adolescents and young adults with poor health (n=751) was sufficient for the analysis, future studies with larger samples may provide more precise estimates.

Taken together, these findings suggest that loneliness among adolescents and young adults may be more strongly associated with health vulnerability and psychosocial instability. By examining age-specific interactions using a large nationally representative dataset, this study provides new evidence that health vulnerability and psychosocial stressors may have different implications for loneliness across generations. These results highlight the importance of considering age-specific vulnerability when designing interventions and public health strategies to reduce loneliness.

Conclusion

Adolescents and young adults experienced more loneliness than did older adults, although loneliness levels were similarly elevated among middle-aged adults. Notably, the association between poor health and loneliness was particularly strong in this group, highlighting their heightened vulnerability to health-related and psychosocial stressors. Drawing on a large-scale dataset, this study provides new insights into the factors associated with loneliness among adolescents and young adults. The findings also suggest that psychosocial and economic factors, such as anxiety and unemployment, may have a stronger association with loneliness among working-age populations than among older adults. Future research should explore specific health conditions to better identify at-risk groups. Interventions tailored to generational characteristics, including digital tools, may be effective in reducing loneliness. Evaluating their effectiveness is essential to mitigate adverse outcomes, including suicide, and to enhance the well-being of this population. These findings highlight the importance of developing age-specific strategies to reduce loneliness, particularly among younger individuals experiencing poor health.
