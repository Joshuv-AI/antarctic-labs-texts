FAM135B suppresses glioblastoma angiogenesis via stabilizing the IKK complex and inactivating the NF-κB/IL-6 signaling pathway
Authors: Chuangyuan Wang, Fabing Zhang, Liwei Hao, Yujie Zhang, Chenya Feng, Nuozhou Weng, Peiqian Hu, Wanmei Lin, Taoliang Chen, Tianwei Wang, Yinian Zhang, Yiquan Ke, Liang Zhao, Zetao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM), the most aggressive primary central nervous system malignancy in adults, is hallmarked by robust angiogenesis and pronounced heterogeneity, with the mesenchymal subtype linked to the poorest prognosis. The median survival of GBM patients is merely ~ 14 months, and approved anti-angiogenic agents like bevacizumab only yield short-term benefits without improving overall survival, highlighting the urgent need for novel anti-angiogenic targets. FAM135B, a brain-enriched protein-coding gene, is implicated in tumorigenesis and chemoresistance in other cancers, but its role and underlying mechanism in GBM angiogenesis remain unelucidated.MethodsBioinformatic analysis: Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA), single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses were performed on GBM datasets from TCGA, CGGA and other databases to screen angiogenesis-related genes and explore the correlation between FAM135B expression and clinical prognosis. In vitro experiments: FAM135B-overexpressing/knockdown GBM cell models were constructed. Tube formation, Transwell migration and colony formation assays were used to detect the angiogenic potential of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) treated with GBM cell-conditioned medium. Co-immunoprecipitation, mass spectrometry, Western blot, RT-qPCR and ELISA were applied to verify protein-protein interactions and detect the expression of cytokines and signaling pathway molecules. Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed the transcriptional regulation of FAM135B. In vivo experiments: Nude mouse orthotopic and subcutaneous GBM xenograft models were established to assess the effect of FAM135B on tumor growth, angiogenesis and mouse survival. Rescue experiments with NF-κB inhibitor PDTC and recombinant IL-6 were conducted to validate the key role of the NF-κB/IL-6 axis. Clinical sample validation: Immunohistochemistry and magnetic resonance imaging were used to analyze the correlation between FAM135B expression, microvessel density and prognosis in 72 GBM clinical samples. Statistical analysis: SPSS 19.0 was used for data analysis with Kaplan-Meier, t-tests and Log-rank tests; the significance level was set at P < 0.05.ResultsFAM135B is a novel key regulator of GBM angiogenesis, with significantly downregulated expression in GBM tissues (especially the mesenchymal subtype). Low FAM135B expression correlates with enhanced endothelial cell communication, high angiogenic potential and poor progression-free/overall survival in GBM patients. FAM135B inhibits GBM angiogenesis in vitro and in vivo: its overexpression suppresses HUVEC tube formation, migration and colony formation, while knockdown exerts the opposite effect. In orthotopic xenografts, FAM135B overexpression slows tumor growth, prolongs mouse survival, and reduces intratumoral microvessel formation and M2 macrophage infiltration. FAM135B downregulates IL-6 expression to inhibit the JAK/STAT signaling pathway: its expression is negatively correlated with pro-angiogenic cytokines (notably IL-6) in GBM. FAM135B overexpression reduces IL-6 transcription and secretion in GBM cells, thereby inhibiting JAK/STAT phosphorylation in HUVECs; high FAM135B expression in GBM tissues is associated with low IL-6 and CD31 levels. FAM135B binds to the IKK complex (IKKα/IKKβ) to stabilize it, inhibit IKKβ activation and P65 phosphorylation, and block the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, which is the upstream mechanism of IL-6 downregulation. Co-overexpression of IKBKB partially reverses the anti-angiogenic effect of FAM135B, and PDTC rescues NF-κB activation induced by FAM135B knockdown. HNF4A is the upstream positive transcription factor of FAM135B: it directly binds to two key sites in the FAM135B promoter to promote its transcription and protein expression. HNF4A expression is positively correlated with FAM135B and favorable GBM prognosis; its overexpression inhibits GBM angiogenesis, and this effect is abrogated by FAM135B knockdown.ConclusionFAM135B acts as a critical negative regulator of GBM angiogenesis, whose low expression contributes to high tumor angiogenic potential and poor patient prognosis. Mechanistically, HNF4A transcriptionally upregulates FAM135B, which then binds to and stabilizes the IKK complex, inactivating the NF-κB signaling pathway and downregulating IL-6 expression, ultimately inhibiting the JAK/STAT-mediated angiogenic process. FAM135B also remodels the GBM tumor microenvironment by reducing M2 macrophage infiltration. Targeting the HNF4A/FAM135B/NF-κB/IL-6 signaling axis provides a novel and promising therapeutic strategy for GBM anti-angiogenic therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08397-3.Keywords: Angiogenesis, FAM135B, NFκB, IL-6, Glioblastoma

Introduction

Gliomas are the most common primary central nervous system malignancies in adulthood, and the current treatment paradigm mainly includes surgical resection, followed by radiochemotherapy regimens, targeted therapies, or immunotherapies. Patients with gliomas have poor prognoses despite standardized therapeutic approaches, particularly glioblastomas (GBMs), with a median survival time of only approximately 14 months [1, 2]. Angiogenesis is one of the pivotal malignant phenotypes in GBM, and notable heterogeneity exists among distinct GBM subgroups, reflecting the highly heterogeneous nature of GBM. Multiple studies have classified GBM into mesenchymal, classical and proneural subtypes based on transcriptomic profiles [3]. The mesenchymal subtype is associated with poorer prognosis and drives GBM malignant progression. Bevacizumab, an anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody, is approved for GBM and provides short-term benefits by reducing tumor growth and peritumoral edema [4, 5]. However, its combination with temozolomide and radiotherapy does not improve overall survival [6–8]. Thus, identifying novel and effective antiangiogenic targets is critical for GBM therapy.

In this study, by analyzing multiple glioma sequencing datasets, we identified FAM135B as a key regulator of angiogenesis in gliomas. Family with sequence similarity 135 member B (FAM135B) is a protein-coding gene highly expressed in brain tissue, but its specific biological functions remain unclear. Previous studies have demonstrated that FAM135B can predict poor prognosis in esophageal cancer and lung cancer patients and can lead to chemoresistance in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients [9–11]. Mechanistically, FAM135B promotes resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy in lung cancer patients by facilitating DNA damage repair [10]. Additionally, FAM135B can induce chemoresistance in CRC through alternative splicing [11].

Through integrated analysis of multiple glioma sequencing datasets, we identified FAM135B as a key angiogenesis suppressor in glioblastoma (GBM), and found that low expression of FAM135B is significantly associated with poor prognosis in GBM patients. Validated by in vitro and in vivo experiments, we confirmed that FAM135B can bind to the IKK complex, thereby silencing the NF-κB signaling pathway, which ultimately leads to the downregulation of IL-6 expression and inhibits the ability of GBM to induce endothelial cells (ECs) to form blood vessels. These findings provide novel insights for the development of antiangiogenic therapies targeting GBM.

Results

Identification of FAM135B as a key regulator of angiogenesis in glioblastoma

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common primary malignant tumor of the central nervous system, and angiogenesis plays a critical role in its tumorigenesis and progression. To identify key genes that regulate angiogenesis in GBM, we performed gene set variation analysis (GSVA) of RNA-Seq data from 172 GBM samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). On the basis of the scores of the angiogenesis-related gene sets, we divided the samples into three clusters (Fig. 1A) and identified genes that were differentially expressed between Cluster 1 (high angiogenic potential) and Cluster 2 (low angiogenic potential) (Fig. 1B). By integrating multiple large-scale datasets, we identified 37 candidate genes whose expression was downregulated in GBM, WHO grade IV glioma, mesenchymal subtype GBM, and Cluster 1 (Fig. 1C). Interestingly, we identified FAM135B among these candidate genes. While FAM135B has been shown to promote tumor malignancy in other cancers, its expression is paradoxically downregulated in GBM and associated with low angiogenic potential.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis of three GBM samples revealed lower FAM135B expression in samples with relatively high STAT1 and STAT2 transcription factor activities and low JAK-STAT and NF-κB signaling pathway activities (Fig. S1E-F). Additionally, we observed decreased endothelial cell communication in samples with low FAM135B expression (Fig. 1E).

Pancancer analysis revealed that FAM135B was highly expressed in normal brain tissue but significantly downregulated in GBM (Fig. S1A). Analysis of multiple glioma datasets confirmed lower FAM135B expression in glioma tissues than in normal tissues (Fig. S1B-C). Moreover, higher FAM135B expression was associated with longer overall survival in glioma patients (Fig. S1D).

Immunohistochemistry and magnetic resonance imaging analysis of glioma samples from Zhujiang Hospital revealed higher FAM135B expression in less differentiated gliomas with fewer microvessels. To analyze the differential gene expression associated with varying FAM135B expression levels and the survival in glioma patients, we divided the CGGA datasets into FAM135B-low and FAM135B-high groups using the median expression level of FAM135B as the cutoff. K‒M analysis revealed that higher FAM135B expression was associated with longer progression-free survival in patients (Fig. 2A-C).

In summary, our study identified FAM135B as a novel key regulator of angiogenesis in GBM. Its downregulation may contribute to the highly angiogenic phenotype of GBM and could be a potential therapeutic target for antiangiogenic therapies.

FAM135B targets GBM growth and angiogenesis in vivo

Next, we investigated the effects of FAM135B targeting GBM in vivo using a nude mouse orthotopic tumor xenograft model. Consistent with the previous results, the average fluorescence intensity was lower in the FAM135B-overexpressing group than in the control group 28 days after orthotopic tumor transplantation, and the overall survival of the overexpressing group was prolonged (Fig. 3A-C). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that FAM135B overexpression reduced microvessel formation and M2 macrophage infiltration in tumor tissues (Fig. 3D-E).

We selected U251 cells with high FAM135B expression and U87 cells with low expression for further studies. Transfection of U87 and U251 cells with Flag-FAM135B and FAM135B-shRNA confirmed the efficiency of transfection (Fig. 4B).

Conditioned Medium(CM) from FAM135B-overexpressing U87 cells inhibited HUVEC tube formation, whereas CM from FAM135B-knockdown U251 cells promoted HUVEC tube formation (Fig. 4C). Transwell migration assays revealed that CM from FAM135B-overexpressing cells inhibited HUVEC migration, whereas CM from FAM135B-knockdown cells promoted migration (Fig. 4D). Colony formation assays revealed that FAM135B overexpression inhibited HUVEC colony formation, whereas FAM135B knockdown promoted colony formation (Fig. 4E). Similar results were obtained using two primary glioma cell lines, GBM and G15 (Fig. S2 A-E). These results suggest that FAM135B suppresses the proangiogenic capacity of glioma cells.

FAM135B overexpression downregulated proangiogenic cytokines in glioma. Correlation analysis of glioma data from the CGGA301 database revealed that FAM135B expression was negatively correlated with the expression of several proangiogenic cytokines, including IL-6, IL8, and IL24 (Fig. 5A-B). Transcriptome sequencing of U87 cells overexpressing FAM135B revealed that the differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in cellular angiogenesis processes. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the Differentially expressed genes(DEGs) were significantly enriched in cytokine signaling pathways, including the TNF signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling pathway, and JAK-STAT signaling pathway (Fig. 5C).

To identify key angiogenic factors regulated by FAM135B, we performed angiogenesis factor array analysis using RNA from FAM135B-overexpressing U87 cells and control cells. FAM135B overexpression led to the downregulation of multiple angiogenic factors at the transcriptional level, with IL-6 showing the most significant downregulation (Fig. 5D). Integration of the angiogenesis factor array results with the RNA-Seq differential gene expression analysis identified eight overlapping genes whose expression was downregulated by FAM135B overexpression, including IL-6 (Fig. 5E-F). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that FAM135B overexpression downregulated the IL-6 signaling pathway and the JAK/STAT signaling pathway. GSEA of glioma microarray data (GSE53733) also revealed downregulation of the IL-6 signaling pathway and JAK/STAT signaling pathway in the FAM135B high-expression group (Fig. 5G-H).

FAM135B suppresses angiogenesis by targeting the IL-6/JAK/STAT signaling pathway in glioma

To determine whether FAM135B regulates angiogenesis through the IL-6/JAK/STAT signaling pathway in glioma, we performed spatial transcriptomic analysis of tissue sections from a GBM patient. The SpaGene R package was used to identify spatially colocalized patterns involving FAM135B, termed Pattern1 (Fig. 6A). Correlation analysis between the angiogenesis score, a critical gene set for angiogenesis that includes VEGFA, and Pattern1 revealed a negative correlation between FAM135B and angiogenesis. Spatial communication analysis revealed that the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway was negatively correlated with FAM135B (Fig. 6B).

Immunofluorescence analysis of glioma patient tissue sections revealed decreased IL-6 and CD31 expression in regions with high FAM135B expression (Fig. 6C). These findings suggest that elevated FAM135B expression suppresses microvessel formation in tumor areas by reducing IL-6 expression.

We further examined IL-6 expression in glioma cell lines with FAM135B overexpression or knockdown. RT‒qPCR and ELISA revealed that overexpression of FAM135B in U87 and GBM cells decreased both IL-6 transcription and extracellular secretion. Conversely, knockdown of FAM135B in U251 and G15 cells increased IL-6 transcription and secretion (Fig. 6D-E). Western blot analysis revealed that HUVECs treated with conditioned media from FAM135B-overexpressing U87 and GBM cells had decreased JAK and STAT phosphorylation. Conversely, HUVECs treated with conditioned media from FAM135B-knockdown U251 and G15 cells presented increased JAK and STAT phosphorylation (Fig. 6F). These results demonstrate that the JAK/STAT signaling pathway in HUVECs is regulated by FAM135B expression in glioma cells.

FAM135B regulates IL-6 expression by targeting IKBKB and suppressing NF-κB signaling

To identify key intermediates in the FAM135B-mediated regulation of IL-6, we performed immunoprecipitation experiments using A172 cells transfected with the Flag-FAM135B plasmid (Fig. 7A). Western blot analysis confirmed the transfection and pull-down efficiency, and protein mass spectrometry analysis was performed on the pulled-down proteins. The results revealed that FAM135B interacts with several proteins, including IKBKB (Fig. S3A).

We further constructed a MYC-IKBKB plasmid and cotransfected it with FAM135B in HEK293T cells and repeated the immunoprecipitation experiment, which confirmed the interaction between FAM135B and IKBKB (Fig. 7B).

We then examined the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in tumor cells. Western blot analysis revealed decreased phosphorylation of P65 in tumor cells overexpressing FAM135B but increased phosphorylation of P65 in tumor cells with FAM135B knockdown. Tube formation assays, transwell migration assays, and colony formation assays revealed that HUVECs treated with conditioned media from FAM135B-overexpressing tumor cells had inhibited angiogenesis, migration, and colony formation, whereas co-overexpression of IKBKB partially restored these abilities in HUVECs (Supplemental Fig. 2F-G). These results suggest that FAM135B downregulates IL-6 expression by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway in tumor cells, thereby reducing their proangiogenic capacity.

To further elucidate the key structural regions involved in FAM135B-IKBKB binding, we designed truncated protein plasmids of FAM135B and IKBKB on the basis of their known protein functional domains and verified their expression via Western blot analysis. We then cotransfected the truncated plasmids of FAM135B and IKBKB into HEK293T cells. Western blot analysis revealed that FAM135B bound to all the truncated regions of IKBKB and that IKBKB bound to the truncated regions of FAM135B (Fig. 7C, Fig. S3B).

To explore how FAM135B-IKKβ binding modulates the IKK complex, we performed novel co-immunoprecipitation assays and found that FAM135B overexpression enhanced IKKβ-IKKα binding. Reanalysis of FAM135B-IKKβ interaction and additional validation further confirmed physical binding between FAM135B and IKKα (Fig. 7D). We further examined the colocalization of FAM135B with IKKβ and IKKα using immunofluorescence assays. The results showed that FAM135B colocalized with both IKKβ and IKKα in cells (Fig. 7E). Meanwhile, we performed protein spatial structure modeling using the protein sequences from the NCBI database and conducted molecular docking analysis on the modeled structures. The results demonstrated that there are multiple binding sites between FAM135B and the IKK complex in their spatial conformations (Fig.S3 E). Thus, we hypothesize that FAM135B can bind to the IKK complex, thereby inhibiting the activation of IKKβ and ultimately downregulating the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Furthermore, we designed rescue experiments to investigate the relationship between FAM135B expression levels and NF‑κB signaling pathway activation. Pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate (PDTC) effectively inhibits IκB phosphorylation, prevents NF‑κB nuclear translocation, and reduces the expression of downstream cytokines. Therefore, we performed rescue experiments by adding PDTC to the conditioned medium of FAM135B‑knockdown U251 cells and recombinant human IL‑6 protein to the conditioned medium of FAM135B‑overexpressing U87 cells. Meanwhile, we established subcutaneous xenograft models in nude mice and administered recombinant human IL‑6 protein after tumor formation. The results showed that PDTC treatment effectively reversed the activation of the NF‑κB signaling pathway in tumor cells caused by FAM135B downregulation, whereas supplementation with recombinant human IL‑6 protein partially restored the endothelial cell dysfunction induced by FAM135B overexpression (Fig. S2G). Exogenous human recombinant IL-6 reduced the tumor size difference between FAM135B-overexpressing and control groups (Fig. 7F-G, Fig. S3C-D), further confirming that FAM135B represses tumor growth by downregulating tumor cell IL-6 expression.

HNF4A directly binds to the promoter of FAM135B and promotes its increased expression

Given the significant impact of FAM135B on glioma cells, identifying the upstream transcription factors that regulate it is crucial. We used three different types of transcription factor prediction databases to predict potential transcription factors associated with FAM135B. In the common intersection of these databases, only HNF4A was positively correlated with FAM135B expression and was therefore selected for further investigation (Fig. 8A, Fig. S4). Through bioinformatic analysis, we found that HNF4A expression levels were positively correlated with FAM135B, and patients with upregulated HNF4A expression had improved prognoses (Fig. 8B-C). In cells, overexpression of HNF4A led to an increase in FAM135B expression at the protein level (Fig. 8D). These findings further indicate that HNF4A positively regulates FAM135B expression.

Using the JASPAR database, we identified two potential HNF4A binding sites in the promoter region of FAM135B. Dual-luciferase reporter assays revealed that mutating either of the two binding sites prevented HNF4A overexpression from promoting FAM135B transcription (Fig. 8E). These findings suggest that both binding sites in the FAM135B promoter region are essential for HNF4A binding and the promotion of FAM135B transcription initiation.

Tube formation assays demonstrated that HNF4A overexpression attenuated the ability of glioma cells to promote vascular endothelial cell tube formation, whereas knocking down FAM135B restored this suppressed function (Fig. 8F). In summary, our results indicate that HNF4A enhances FAM135B transcriptional activity by binding to specific sites in the FAM135B promoter, thereby exerting its inhibitory effect on tumor angiogenesis.

Discussion

Gliomas, which are intracranial tumors originating from glial cells, are among the most common primary malignant intracranial tumors, accounting for approximately 70 to 80% of all malignant brain tumors [1, 2]. Glioblastoma is the most malignant type of glioma and originates in glial cells in the brain; it grows rapidly and has a short course of disease. One of the distinguishing features of glioblastoma (GBM) is its high heterogeneity, not only between different patients (intertumoral heterogeneity) but also within the same tumor (intratumoral heterogeneity) [12, 13].

At the genetic level, GBM exhibits a high degree of heterogeneity at the genetic, transcriptome, and functional levels. For example, as early as 10 years ago, a team from Harvard University analyzed the gene expression profiles of nearly 500 GBM patients and reported that GBM patients can be divided into four types, namely, neuronal (N), preneuronal (PN), classical (CL) and mesenchymal (MES), according to their different gene expression profiles [14]. Subsequent studies excluded the interference of normal neurons, thus eliminating the neural subtype. Different types of GBM patients respond differently to treatment. Mesenchymal GBM has a more complex immunosuppressive microenvironment and is generally associated with greater aggressiveness, treatment resistance, and a worse prognosis [15–17]. In addition, the subtype of GBM is not fixed but rather a plastic change in the subtype that may occur during treatment or disease progression.

In 2023, Pim J. French et al. performed RNA-seq on paired primary recurrent GBM samples from patients receiving standard care and reported that the above 3 subtypes form an interconnected continuous structure in two-dimensional space. These subtypes can be converted into each other during GBM progression and recurrence, preferentially evolving into the mesenchymal subtype, which is the most malignant and has the worst prognosis. This transformation is achieved primarily through microenvironmental remodeling rather than through the molecular evolution of tumor cells [3]. In this study, we examined gene expression profile differences between gliomas of varying malignancy grades to identify important genes involved in the malignant phenotype of GBM. Analysis of multiple batch expression spectrum datasets revealed that FAM135B was significantly underexpressed not only in GBM but also in the most malignant subtype of GBM. While some studies have preliminarily reported the role of FAM135B in cancers such as esophageal and colorectal cancer, there have been no direct reports involving its role in GBM. Our findings elucidate for the first time the critical role of FAM135B in the malignant phenotype and benign prognosis of GBM, highlighting its research value.

NFκB (nuclear factor κB) is a transcription factor that plays a key role in a variety of cellular processes, including inflammation, cell survival, and apoptosis. In glioblastoma (GBM), aberrant activation of the NFκB signaling pathway is closely related to tumor growth, aggressiveness, treatment resistance, and maintenance of cancer stem cell properties [18]. The classical NF‒κB activation pathway is mediated by the IκB kinase (IKK) complex. The IKK complex is composed of IKKβ, IKKα, and IKKγ, and extracellular stimulation triggers a signaling cascade that leads to the activation of the IKK complex, which ultimately leads to IκB activation and ubiquitination, releasing NF-κB dimers [19]. Thus, blocking the formation of the IKK complex inhibits the activation of the NF-κB pathway via the classical pathway. In the present study, we found that FAM135B blocks the activation of the IKK complex by binding to it, thereby reducing the release of NF-κB dimers via the classical pathway, ultimately decreasing IL‑6 transcription and inhibiting glioma angiogenesis.

Angiogenesis is a core pathological feature of glioblastoma (GBM), and the process is tightly regulated by multiple molecules. PTEN is a frequently mutated tumor suppressor in GBM, which negatively regulates angiogenesis by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway and blocking the expression of pro‑angiogenic factors.

In our study, similar to PTEN, FAM135B also negatively regulates angiogenesis, but through a distinct mechanism: FAM135B exerts its regulatory function by binding to the IKK complex, and does not exhibit the mutational inactivation characteristic of classical tumor suppressors. HIF1α is a hypoxia-responsive transcription factor and a key positive regulator of angiogenesis in GBM. It is stabilized under hypoxic conditions and upregulates the expression of downstream pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGFA. In contrast, FAM135B is a hypoxia-independent negative regulator whose angiogenic function does not require hypoxic induction, showing an opposite regulatory pattern to HIF1α. VEGFA is a core effector of angiogenesis that acts directly on vascular endothelial cells to promote vessel formation, and represents a major target for anti‑angiogenic therapy in GBM. Unlike VEGFA, FAM135B functions as an upstream regulator that indirectly modulates angiogenesis via signaling pathways or molecular interactions, rather than acting directly on endothelial cells. As a novel negative regulator of angiogenesis, FAM135B enriches the molecular regulatory network of GBM angiogenesis and provides new insights for the development of anti‑angiogenic therapeutic strategies for GBM.

The interaction between blood vessels and the immune microenvironment in cancer therapy has emerged as a promising target. Tumor blood vessels and the immune system form a complex network of interactions that have a profound impact on each other [20]. On the one hand, the tumor vasculature can secrete immunosuppressive factors such as VEGF to hinder the infiltration and function of immune cells such as T cells and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). In addition, intratumoral hypoxia caused by excessive angiogenesis further exacerbates immunosuppression. On the other hand, immune cells such as M2-polarized TAMs promote the formation of tumor-supporting vasculature by secreting proangiogenic factors such as FGF and the MMP. Highly effective antiangiogenic therapies are thought to have the potential to increase the efficacy of immunotherapy by remodeling the tumor vasculature to alleviate immunosuppression in the microenvironment. In this study, we found that FAM135B mediates remodeling of the tumor microenvironment in glioma, mainly characterized by reduced neovascularization and decreased M2 macrophage infiltration. This suggests, to some extent, that FAM135B has the potential to alleviate immunosuppression in the microenvironment and enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy. However, the mechanism underlying the effect of FAM135B on M2 macrophage infiltration in the tumor microenvironment remains unclear. It remains to be determined whether the effect of FAM135B on macrophages is directly mediated by IL-6 or through other cytokines. Furthermore, it is unknown whether FAM135B affects the infiltration of M2 macrophages or the M2 polarization of adjacent macrophages in the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, in future studies, we will further explore the interaction between FAM135B and relevant cells in the tumor microenvironment, with targeting FAM135B to reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment of glioma as one of our core research directions.

This study has certain limitations and should be carefully considered. In this study, we used coimmunoprecipitation to study the binding relationship between the truncated FAM135B sequence and the truncated sequence of the IKBKB protein to identify the key domains of FAM135B that bind to the IKK complex. However, the experimental results revealed that there were multiple binding sites between FAM135B and IKK complex. However, the complex interaction between FAM135B and IKK complex needs to be further studied.

In summary, we focused on the vascular regulatory network involved in glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) and identified FAM135B as a key gene in shaping the tumor-supportive vasculature and immune microenvironment. Its low expression is associated with increased tumor malignancy and a poor prognosis. Mechanistically, FAM135B binds to IKK complex, thereby consequently blocking the activation of the canonical NFκB pathway(Fig. 8G). This process leads to the downregulation of IL-6 transcription in GBM cells, thereby suppressing angiogenesis within the tumor tissue. In vivo studies have demonstrated that elevated expression of FAM135B significantly delays tumor progression and prolongs survival. Targeting the FAM135B/IL-6 signaling axis provides new insights and strategies for improving the prognosis of GBM patients and exploring antiangiogenic therapies for GBM.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and human tissue samples

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), the human brain glial cell line NHA, the human glioma cell lines U251, U87MG, A172, T98G, and LN229, and the human embryonic kidney 293T cell line were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The U87-Luc cell line was generated in our laboratory via transfection with a reporter gene encoding firefly luciferase as previously described. All the cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 100 IU/mL penicillin G and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. All the cell lines used in this study were confirmed within four years by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, and experiments were performed in cells propagated less than 6 months after resuscitation.

A total of 72 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded glioma samples were collected from glioma patients who underwent surgery between 2016 and 2018 at the Department of Neuro-oncological Surgery, Zhujiang Hospital. The expression of target genes profiled in glioma samples was identified through searches in the UCSC Xena [21], Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) [22] and GlioVis [23] data portals for visualization and analysis of brain tumor expression datasets: TCGA-GBM (n = 172), CGGA301 (n = 301), CGGA325 (n = 325), Gill (n = 92), Reifenberger (n = 70), and Ivy GAP (n = 270).

RNA isolation, reverse transcription, and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted via TRIzol (Invitrogen; Carlsbad, California). To quantify the expression of FAM135B and IL-6, total RNA was subjected to polyadenylation and reverse transcription (RT) via a ThermoScript™ RT‒PCR System (Invitrogen). Real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis was carried out via SYBR Green PCR master mix (Applied Biosystems; Foster City, California) on an ABI 7500HT system. β-Actrin was used as an endogenous control. All samples were normalized to internal controls, and fold changes were calculated through relative quantification (2−ΔΔCT). The primers used are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Lentiviral infection and shRNA transfection

As previously described, lentiviruses containing FAM135B oligonucleotides or FAM135B short hairpin RNA (shRNA) were constructed by Hanbio (Shanghai, China). The cells were incubated with lentiviruses and polybrene for 24 h. Stable control and specific knockdown/overexpressing cells were selected and maintained with puromycin (2 µg/mL, Solarbio, China). IKBKB oligonucleotides and active control sequences were purchased from Hanbio (Shanghai, China) and transfected via the Lipofectamine® 3000 reagent (Cat# L3000015; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

HUVEC migration assay

Transwell assays were performed to assess HUVEC migration. For the transwell assay, treated HUVECs were suspended in serum-free medium and seeded into transwell chambers with an 8 μm pore size insert (BD Biosciences). Medium containing 10% FBS was placed in the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 6 h at 37 °C, the cells that migrated through the membrane and adhered to the lower surface were stained with hematoxylin and counted under an optical microscope in five random fields (200×). The number of cells that migrated through the transwell chamber within the same time frame was counted to assess the migratory capacity of vascular endothelial cells.

HUVEC tube formation assay

A tube formation assay was used to evaluate the in vitro angiogenic potential of HUVECs by examining their capillary-like structure formation capability. Following treatment, HUVECs were plated onto Matrigel-coated 96-well plates at a density of 40,000 cells/well. After a 6-hour incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, the tubular networks were visualized via a microscope (200× magnification). Five randomly selected fields per well were quantified. The total capillary length of each group was quantified as a representative measure of tube formation. Pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate (PDTC), purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology, and recombinant human IL-6 (rhIL-6), purchased from Proteintech, were used to perform rescue experiments.

HUVEC colony formation assay

The colony formation assay measures the clonogenic survival and proliferative capacity of HUVECs. Treated cells were seeded in 6-well plates at low density (1,000 cells/well) and maintained in complete growth medium for 7 days under standard culture conditions. Subsequently, the colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution, and macroscopically visible colonies (typically defined as > 50 cells/colony) were enumerated.

Western blotting and coimmunoprecipitation

Total protein was extracted via RIPA lysis buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. Protein was quantified via a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce, KeyGEN Biotech, Jiangsu, China), separated via SDS‒PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were blocked with Tris buffer containing 0.1% Tween-20 and 5% skim milk at 4 °C. The membranes were incubated overnight with rabbit antibodies against FAM135B (Proteintech, 1:1000), p65, p-p65, JAK, p-JAK, STAT3, p-STAT3, GAPDH (1:1000, Proteintech), and β-actin (1:1000, Proteintech), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit IgG/anti-mouse IgG, CST, 1:10000). The signal was detected via an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Tennon5200, Shanghai, China) as described by the manufacturer.

Total protein was extracted with NP-40, incubated with antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with protein A/G magnetic beads (Selleck, China) for 8 h. Finally, the immunoprecipitates were analyzed via western blotting.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining

Surgical tumor tissue samples and xenograft samples from glioma patients were fixed in 4% formaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (4 μm). The tissue sections were then dewaxed and dehydrated, followed by incubation in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. After being blocked with a PBS solution containing 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, the slides were stained overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against FAM135B (Proteintech, 1:400), IL-6 (Proteintech, 1:200), CD31 (1:100, ZSGB Bio), Ki67 (1:100, ZSGB Bio), and CD163 (1:100, MCE). The corresponding secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Target molecules were detected by DAB staining for immunohistochemistry. Finally, the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin. Two independent researchers, who were blinded to the sample identification, were involved in the process: one researcher performed the staining, and another analyzed the glioma tissue sections.

For immunofluorescence staining, tissue sections and cells were immunostained overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against FAM135B (Proteintech, 1:200), IL-6 (Proteintech, 1:200), and CD31 (1:100, ZSGB Bio), followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. DAPI was added as a nuclear counterstain. Images were captured via a fluorescence microscope or a laser scanning confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Animals and intracranial xenografts

All animal experiments in this study were strictly performed in accordance with GB/T 35,892 − 2018 “Guidelines for Welfare and Ethical Review of Laboratory Animals”, GB/T 42,011 − 2022 “General Principles for Welfare of Laboratory Animals” and the 3Rs Principle (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement). All experimental operations complied with the ethical norms of animal experiments, fully protected the welfare and rights of experimental animals, and eliminated unnecessary harm. This study has been reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Southern Medical University, and the experimental process was strictly carried out in accordance with the requirements and relevant standards of this committee. Male BALB/c mice aged 5–8 weeks were purchased from the Central Animal Facility of Southern Medical University. U87-Luc cells stably transfected with either the FAM135B overexpression plasmid or the overexpression vector control were stereotactically injected into the right cerebral cortex of BALB/c nude mice. Each group consisted of 6 mice. Tumor growth was monitored via an in vivo imaging system (IVIS Lumina II, Caliper, USA) after intraperitoneal injection of the luciferase substrate D-luciferin (YEASEN, Shanghai, China). Rescue experiments were performed using 4–6-week-old female BALB/c mice purchased from the Central Animal Facility of Southern Medical University. Each group also consisted of 6 mice U87 cells stably transfected with the FAM135B overexpression plasmid or empty vector control plasmid were inoculated into the bilateral inguinal regions of BALB/c nude mice, respectively. After tumor formation, the nude mice were intraperitoneally injected with sterile PBS or human recombinant IL-6 protein at a dose of 3 µg/kg per mouse, with injections administered every 3 days. The experiment was terminated after 3 weeks of observation, the mice were euthanized humanely, and tumor sizes and volume in each group were quantified and analyzed.

Processing and analysis of spatial and single-cell transcriptomic data

In the context of analyzing spatial and single-cell transcriptomic data, we employed Seurat as our primary tool for comprehensive visualization and analysis tasks. To address the inherent missing value problem in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets, we utilized low-rank approximation of missing data (ALRA) for imputation, thereby increasing the completeness of the dataset [24, 25]. Tumor cells were extracted from the original dataset on the basis of the authors’ annotations and subsequently divided into two distinct groups: FAM135Bplus (characterized by the median expression value of FAM135B) and FAM135Bminus (displaying expression levels below the median). This segmentation facilitates a more nuanced understanding of the cellular heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted via Model-based Analysis of Single-Cell Transcriptomics (MAST), which is tailored for scRNA-seq data to identify genes that are significantly different between the FAM135Bplus and FAM135Bminus groups. Furthermore, to gain insights into the biological pathways enriched in DEGs, we extracted human signature gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) via the msigdb R package [26]. These gene sets served as the basis for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) via the GSEA R package, thereby elucidating functional significance.

To explore intercellular communication networks, we utilized the LIANA R package, which is specifically designed to decipher complex intercellular communication patterns [27]. Additionally, to reveal the transcriptional regulatory activities underlying the observed gene expression profiles, we applied the single-cell rEgulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) algorithm. Additionally, we utilized the DoRothEA package and the PROGENy package to analyze the expression of transcription factors and the changes in the activation levels of signaling pathways within the samples.

Transcription factor prediction

The prediction of upstream transcription factors for USP18 was based on information from multiple databases, including HumanTFDB (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/AnimalTFDB#!/), hTFtarget (http://guolao.wchscu.cn/hTFtarget/#!/) and Harmonizonme (http://maayanlab.cloud/Harmonizome/).

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

The FAM135B promoter region and mutant luciferase reporter plasmids, along with the control Renilla luciferase reporter plasmid, were constructed by Genechem. Plasmids containing the firefly luciferase reporter gene and a control Renilla luciferase gene were transfected into cells. After a specified time, the cell lysates were collected, and the luciferase activity was measured via dual luciferase assay kits (Cat. No. RG027; Beyotime Biotechnology).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed via SPSS 19.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Clinical data were analyzed via nonparametric tests (Wilcoxon and Mann‒Whitney) and Kaplan‒Meier analysis. Independent samples t-test was used to analyze differences between groups in the ELISA assay, Transwell assay, tube formation assay, and colony formation assay. Independent samples t-test was used to analyze the fluorescence intensity of in vivo imaging in orthotopic tumor-bearing mice at day 30. Paired t-test was used to evaluate differences in tumor weight and volume in subcutaneous tumor-bearing mice. Log-rank test was applied to analyze the survival time of orthotopic tumor-bearing mice. The significance of differences between groups was evaluated via Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) test, unpaired Student’s t test, or paired t test. All the statistical tests were two-sided. The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). The significance level was set at 0.05 for all statistical analyses.
