Australian pharmacist immunisers in vaccine conversations with patients: a qualitative study to explore communication support needs
Authors: K. T. Bolsewicz, M. S. Steffens, L. S. Cox, E. Hazelwood, J. Andreatta, Y. Bae, M. Bushell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSince the COVID-19 pandemic, uptake of childhood and adolescent vaccines recommended under the National Immunisation Program (NIP) in Australia has declined, while adult NIP vaccine uptake remains stable but suboptimal. Over the past 10 years, pharmacists have taken on a larger role in NIP vaccination, yet their communication and information needs are not well understood. Supportive vaccine conversations are key to improving uptake, but can be difficult, especially with hesitant patients. This study explored Australian pharmacist immunisers’ experiences and support needs around vaccine conversations with patients, aiming to inform evidence-based communication resources.MethodsQualitative interviews from 12 community, 7 aged care and 3 general practice pharmacist immunisers from across metropolitan, regional and rural Australia were analysed thematically. Most participants reported vaccinating adults and older adults.ResultsKey themes include: 1. The usual practice; 2. What works well; 3. The gaps and challenges; 4. Addressing the gaps: perspectives and recommendations from pharmacist immunisers. We found that pharmacists – especially in regional areas – are known to the community, offer valued advice and accessible vaccinations. While many reported using effective vaccine communication, they identified a need for further clinical guidance, scenario-based training and tools to address misinformation and emotional concerns. Other needs included tailored support for aged care, accessible vaccine resources for older adults, culturally and linguistically diverse communities, and those with limited literacy and peer forums for rural and sole pharmacists.ConclusionThe study offers insights into practices and information and communication support needs of Australian pharmacist immunisers, especially in underexplored areas like aged care and sole pharmacist rural practice. It highlights the need to better disseminate existing communication supports and develop new ones. Further research should explore sector-specific and systemic challenges, and communication needs with younger populations and their carers.

Background

Since the COVID-19 pandemic, Australia has experienced a consistent drop in uptake of childhood and adolescent vaccines recommended under the National Immunisation Program (NIP) (The National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance, 2025a). While uptake of vaccines recommended for adults, such as influenza and pneumococcal, has been more stable, it remains suboptimal. This raises concerns for severe disease, in particular among at-risk population groups. Access barriers such as the costs associated with vaccinations, poor availability and flexibility of appointments (Kaufman et al., 2026; Thomas et al., 2022) are among the key barriers to the uptake of vaccinations offered through primary care for both childhood and adult vaccinations (Eiden et al., 2022; Morrison et al., 2022). Living in regional or remote areas and experiencing social disadvantage further exacerbate these access barriers (The National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance, 2024a; Thomas et al., 2022). Acceptance barriers such as negative thoughts and feelings about vaccination, low risk perceptions of disease, and not trusting vaccination information from one’s healthcare provider also play a role (Eiden et al., 2022; Morrison et al., 2022; Pearce et al., 2015).

Historically in Australia, most of the NIP-funded childhood and adult vaccinations have been delivered in general practice; most adolescent vaccinations in schools. Currently, there is a shortage of general practitioners (GPs) in rural, remote and socially disadvantaged areas (Breadon & Wu, 2025), and of GPs offering bulk-billing services (Attwooll, 2025), especially in under-served areas (Breadon & Wu, 2025). In light of this, there is a need to re-think immunisation service delivery models with a view to effectively removing barriers to vaccinations (Bolsewicz et al., 2025), including immunisations in general practice, community nurse-led immunisation clinics, and via pharmacists. Pharmacist-administered vaccination is an important service delivery option to explore: pharmacists are accessible and trusted primary healthcare providers (Burson et al., 2016; Hart, 2024) and offer a potential for quick and convenient vaccination (Trent et al., 2021), in particular in regional and remote areas (Burson et al., 2016).

Pharmacist involvement in vaccinations in Australia has grown significantly since 2014, when Queensland first allowed trained pharmacists (hereafter pharmacist immunisers) to administer influenza vaccines (Hart, 2024). The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated this expansion (Hart, 2024). Between 2017–2023, the proportion of pharmacist-administered vaccinations increased in particular in relation to adult influenza vaccinations (Hull et al., 2026). By 2023, pharmacies delivered 26.9% of influenza vaccines to adults aged 20–64 and 11.7% to those 65+, second only to general practice (Hull et al., 2026). In 2024, the National Immunisation Program Vaccinations in Pharmacy Program (NIPVIP) enabled pharmacist immunisers to administer NIP-funded vaccines to patients aged 5+ (The Pharmacy Guild of Australia, 2025) in registered pharmacy locations (hereafter community pharmacies) and aged care and disability homes (hereafter aged care pharmacists) (McDerby et al., 2019; Pharmaceutical Society of Australia, 2025; Pharmacy Programs Administrator, 2025). Pharmacists working clinically within a general practice (hereafter general practice pharmacists) typically do not administer vaccinations but support vaccine education and recommendations, positively contributing to patient outcomes and helping to reduce healthcare costs (Sudeshika et al., 2023).

Despite Australian pharmacist immunisers’ expanding scope of practice, there is scarce research to understand their information and communication needs around having conversations about vaccines with patients. Supportive vaccine conversations can help address patients’ questions and concerns and reduce acceptance barriers thereby facilitating uptake (Leask et al., 2012). Research shows, however, that providers experience challenges speaking with patients who have many questions and concerns (Berry et al., 2017; Lip et al., 2023). While evidence-based vaccine communication supports for healthcare providers exist (The National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance, Last updated 2023), it is not known if pharmacist immunisers know about them, use them and whether these resources meet their needs.

The aim of this study was to address this gap. We interviewed Australian pharmacist immunisers in three pharmacy sectors: community pharmacy, aged care and general practice, to explore experiences, challenges and support needs around vaccine conversations with patients, with a view to inform evidence-based communication resources for pharmacist immunisers and patients.

Methods

This was a qualitative exploratory study using semi-structured interviews. The research team included an experienced pharmacist academic (MB), pharmacy students (YB, JA), and research assistants (LC, EH), all of whom received training in qualitative research methods, and social science immunisation researchers skilled in qualitative research methods (KB, MS).

Participants were recruited from May–August 2024 and in March 2025 via purposive and snowball sampling. Recruitment involved distributing fliers through healthcare networks, social media, newsletters, and email. Snowball sampling relied on pharmacist referrals. No incentives were provided. Eligible pharmacists were vaccination-accredited and either administered NIP vaccines (community/aged care) or regularly discussed vaccinations with patients (general practice).

The semi-structured interview guide was pilot tested with two practising pharmacists to assess clarity, relevance, and flow of the questions. These pharmacists were not included in the final study sample and did not participate in the main interviews. Feedback from the pilot testing informed minor refinements to the wording and sequencing of several questions to improve clarity and ensure alignment with the study objectives. Examples of questions include: ‘What is your usual vaccine conversation practice?’; ‘How confident are you discussing vaccinations?’; ‘What are some difficulties when discussing vaccinations?’; ‘Which communication skills you would like to strengthen?’; ‘How new resources could best support your vaccination conversations with patients?’. With supervision from MB, interviews lasting up to one hour were conducted one on one by YB, and LC (female) and JA (male) either online or by phone. Interviewers shared their research and pharmacy professional interests, and consented participants prior to commencing the interview.

Interview audio recordings were transcribed verbatim manually, and proofread by each interviewer. KB, EH, and MB conducted an inductive thematic analysis (Green & Thorogood, 2018), individually reviewing transcripts to identify categories reflecting pharmacists’ vaccination-related practices and communication support needs. Categories were drawn within and across work environments to highlight shared and unique elements, informing recommendations for communication supports applicable across or tailored to each setting. Through ongoing group discussions and comparisons between individual analyses, similar categories across interviews were grouped into overarching themes. Thematic saturation was reached at 8 community and 6 aged care interviews. Participants were not asked to provide feedback on findings.

This study received ethics approval from the University of Canberra Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC, approval number 13987).

Results

We interviewed 12 community pharmacists, 7 aged care on-site pharmacists, and three general practice pharmacists. No one dropped out of the study. Most participants resided in regional and rural New South Wales (NSW), followed by the metropolitan Australian Capital Territory (ACT). Participants had between 3 and 10 years of experience as registered immunisers. Most reported immunising adult and/or older adult patients (Table 1). We derived four themes with sub-themes: 1. The usual practice; 2. What works well; 3. The gaps and challenges; 4. Addressing the gaps: perspectives and recommendations from pharmacist immunisers.

Theme 1. The usual practice

Many community pharmacists have built relationships with the community in which they work

A number of community pharmacists reported having worked in the same location for a long time, which facilitates building relationships with their customers. Community pharmacists working in regional areas reported having built relationships with their close-knit community. The pharmacy I work at, I've been there for 22 years, so I’ve got quite a close connection to the community there and the patients that I see. (ID12, community)

The pharmacy I work at, I've been there for 22 years, so I’ve got quite a close connection to the community there and the patients that I see. (ID12, community)

Community and general practice pharmacists reported engaging in opportunistic vaccine conversations

General practice pharmacists reported engaging in opportunistic vaccination conversations and advocacy as part of patient’s visit.

Community pharmacists, in addition to vaccinating during scheduled vaccination clinics, reported bringing up vaccination opportunistically. The latter occurred with customers coming to fill a prescription, as well as with certain groups of customers whom pharmacists perceived to be at higher risk of disease, such as older patients, those with underlying health conditions, and travellers. So, a lot of it's opportunistic sort of conversation. So, when they're coming in for a prescription, I might ask them, you know, ‘Have you had your flu vaccine or your COVID vaccine this year or [your] Shingrix shingles vaccine?’. (ID12, community)

So, a lot of it's opportunistic sort of conversation. So, when they're coming in for a prescription, I might ask them, you know, ‘Have you had your flu vaccine or your COVID vaccine this year or [your] Shingrix shingles vaccine?’. (ID12, community)

Limited resources such as staffing and time have implications for vaccination conversations

Sole community pharmacists in regional areas reported that practical barriers of limited time and human resources constrain their vaccination conversations with patients. Often, I don't have a lot of time to do more than the very, very basic clinical requirements because I'm just too strapped. (ID11, community)Largely due to resource constraints, only a few community pharmacists reported engaging in longer conversations of up to 20 min, mostly in relation to new vaccines (e.g. Shingrix vaccination against varicella-zoster virus), with a focus on vaccine side effects, patient concerns and benefits of vaccination.

Often, I don't have a lot of time to do more than the very, very basic clinical requirements because I'm just too strapped. (ID11, community)

In aged care, vaccinations are typically offered in bulk, where an external pharmacist comes to the facility for a day to vaccinate consenting residents. While valuing the efficiency of bulk vaccination days, some pharmacists reported challenges of balancing this with the need for personalised communication to ensure residents receive sufficient information, especially when consent must be obtained from substitute decision-makers. The process is efficient, but it can be difficult to ensure that every resident gets the information they need when we are vaccinating a large group in a short time. (ID3, aged care)

The process is efficient, but it can be difficult to ensure that every resident gets the information they need when we are vaccinating a large group in a short time. (ID3, aged care)

Theme 2. What works well

Overall, pharmacists reported feeling confident in their vaccination knowledge and skills

Most participants across the three sectors expressed confidence in their vaccine knowledge and discussion skills. Many pride themselves on staying up to date with vaccination information and researching information to answer their own and patients’ questions and vaccination. I'm not afraid to say I don't have the answer for you right now, let me do a little bit of work on that and I'll get back to you. I'm happy to look up on the Australian Immunisation Handbook to find out the information that's needed for that particular person. (ID5, aged care)

I'm not afraid to say I don't have the answer for you right now, let me do a little bit of work on that and I'll get back to you. I'm happy to look up on the Australian Immunisation Handbook to find out the information that's needed for that particular person. (ID5, aged care)

Pharmacists spoke highly of resources available to support their own knowledge and skills

Most pharmacists reported regularly using government and professional websites, including state health sites and the Australian Immunisation Handbook. These resources were generally praised for being accessible, easy to navigate, searchable, clear and informative. Participants in particular appreciated vaccine webinars that were informative yet succinct (up to 1 h long); scenario-based; recorded to watch later; featured real work stories from experienced pharmacists; and included question and answer time with presenters. Community pharmacists working remotely and as sole pharmacists who reported a degree of professional isolation described additional benefits of webinars as important ways to socially connect with others. I use a lot of written information, but I think having that different source is quite good, probably because I'm pretty isolated … So actually, seeing another human talking about it is really good. (ID11, community)

I use a lot of written information, but I think having that different source is quite good, probably because I'm pretty isolated … So actually, seeing another human talking about it is really good. (ID11, community)

Pharmacists reported using techniques that facilitate vaccine discussions

Pharmacists reported using a variety of conversational approaches to facilitate vaccine informed consent. These included handing the patient their printed vaccination record to use as a conversation starter; conveying complex information using relatable examples; sharing information and medical facts; and reassuring patients about the safety of the vaccine development process. My strategy is mainly about giving the information about how it can protect them, how it can protect others, especially in an aged care facility. (ID6, aged care)To address patient’s vaccine concerns, some participants reported listening with empathy; staying calm; identifying vaccination benefits that are important for the patient; and acknowledging the patients’ choice. You just listen and understand their concerns. And then I try and mould a response to be empathetic to their concerns, but then also try and add in there the actual benefit for them as a person. (ID1, general practice)

Theme 3. The gaps and challenges

Pharmacists reported not having and/or not using written patient resources

Pharmacists reported lack of access to printable, user-friendly, translated patient resources. Some reported using Consumer Medicine Information sheets, provided by vaccine manufacturers containing information about vaccines, as patient resources. While helpful in some instances, these were often described as too technical or text-heavy, in particular for patients with limited literacy or aged care residents. You can get a CMI [Consumer Medicine Information sheet] and hand it out. But you can barely read the writing on it. When you're elderly, it's a bit harder to read. Yeah, it'd be really good to have something simple with some bigger writing on it and just some key points. (ID6, aged care)

You can get a CMI [Consumer Medicine Information sheet] and hand it out. But you can barely read the writing on it. When you're elderly, it's a bit harder to read. Yeah, it'd be really good to have something simple with some bigger writing on it and just some key points. (ID6, aged care)

Some pharmacists indicated the need for further clinical guidance

Some pharmacists expressed the need to receive further clinical guidance to better understand the NIP vaccine eligibility, vaccine brands, vaccine schedule and contraindications. This was highlighted in relation to new vaccines, vaccines in pregnancy, pneumococcal vaccine and administration of live vaccines. OK, I've never done a vaccination before with a live vaccine. So, I'm a little bit more cautious when it comes to live vaccines. Especially with contraindications. And, you know, immunocompromised patients. (ID12, community)

OK, I've never done a vaccination before with a live vaccine. So, I'm a little bit more cautious when it comes to live vaccines. Especially with contraindications. And, you know, immunocompromised patients. (ID12, community)

Some pharmacists reported vaccine communication challenges

While many pharmacists reported using helpful communication approaches (Theme 2), a number indicated difficulties managing complex conversations with patients who share strong negative views about vaccination, report vaccine misinformation and those who become highly agitated. You get the hesitant ones especially during COVID, all the people with the myths about the vaccine. It's very difficult to deal with. (ID1, general practice)Aged care pharmacists relayed additional challenges. To convey vaccine information to residents living with cognitive decline, participants typically rely on simplified language, calm reassurance, and the presence of family members. With dementia, if you are trying to get to their arm, they can get resistive or upset … In severe dementia, the discussion with the resident is minimal … It is important to have a family member there to help reassure the patient. (ID3, aged care)These strategies, while effective in some cases, are not always sufficient, including when family members interfere with the communication process, either by dominating the conversation or not getting involved.

You get the hesitant ones especially during COVID, all the people with the myths about the vaccine. It's very difficult to deal with. (ID1, general practice)

With dementia, if you are trying to get to their arm, they can get resistive or upset … In severe dementia, the discussion with the resident is minimal … It is important to have a family member there to help reassure the patient. (ID3, aged care)

For aged care pharmacists working with culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) residents and families, despite help from translators it can still be challenging to convey often complex vaccination information in ways that facilitate informed consent.

Predominantly the next of kin would be speaking in English, but some still have [difficulty understanding]. I guess trying to understand fully comprehending what the information that is being provided to them. (ID7, community and aged care)

Addressing the gaps: perspectives and recommendations from pharmacists

Many expressed the need for better resources to share with patients

Most participants would like to see concise (single page), easy to understand, visually appealing, translated patient resources written in large font, which convey essential vaccine information. Printed resources reportedly work particularly well for older patients and, when taken home, can function as a reminder to vaccinate or a conversation starter. Some suggested making written resources accessible via links and Quick Response (QR) codes which pharmacists could display in their workplace and share with families of aged care residents. One pharmacist also suggested putting a QR code on the back of prescription bags. Something to take away from a consultation or a conversation is much more powerful than just having that conversation because it gives them that prompt to when they go home. They might just chuck it on the fridge. (ID1, general practice)Some participants suggested sharing essential vaccine information also through audio recordings in pharmacies and aged care. Others proposed short videos with real stories of people affected by vaccine-preventable diseases, to highlight the potential impacts of not being vaccinated. One pharmacist praised the recent television advertisement about shingles for effectively linking the disease to vision loss. I know there's been a few ads on TV recently about the shingles vaccine, and I think they're focused particularly on eyesight and the fact that you could lose vision if you get it close to your eyes. I think that's good. It's that promoting a different aspect of it. (ID5, aged care)Aged care pharmacists expressed the need for patient resources tailored to the needs of residents living with cognitive impairment. Some proposed integrating basic vaccine information directly into consent forms to reduce the need for separate paperwork and help to ensure that residents and substitute decision-makers receive essential vaccine information (see Table 2). Some participants would like to see more scenario-based training to support their vaccination knowledge and communication skills, as well as a repository of resources (videos and printable documents). Participants proposed that these additional communication supports cover vaccine-related topics (listed in Section 3.2) as well as guidance on how to effectively manage and de-escalate complex vaccine conversations. And for people that maybe come across those clients that have very strong opinions. That maybe have not had to deal with a conflict resolution type situation before. Conversation guides for those sorts of things would be really handy. (ID5, aged care)

Discussion

With this qualitative study, we explored the practices of discussing and recommending vaccinations, and communication and information needs among pharmacist immunisers across Australia working in the community, aged care and general practice.

This study confirms earlier findings (Burson et al., 2016; Hattingh et al., 2016; Taylor et al., 2019) that many pharmacists, particularly those working in regional areas, are known to the community, provide valued medical advice and accessible vaccinations. Participants expressed pride in their expanding scope of practice, confidence in sourcing information to address knowledge gaps, and appreciation for governmental and professional pharmacy organisation resources, webinars and training. Many reported using communication approaches shown to facilitate vaccination informed consent with patients who are generally accepting of vaccination and those who have some questions and concerns (Leask et al., 2012).

At the same time, participants identified the need for further vaccine communication support. The request for additional clinical guidance, clearer information about vaccine eligibility and schedule is not surprising, given state and territory variations and changes in the NIPVIP vaccines that pharmacist immunisers can administer, who can get these vaccines, and whether the vaccine is free (The National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance, 2024b). The request for scenario-based training and conversation guides in how to effectively address patient concerns, misinformation, and strong emotions reflects the known challenges of communicating (Berry et al., 2017; Lip et al., 2023). It highlights that this type of communication extends beyond the pharmacists’ traditional focus on vaccine advocacy (Amare et al., 2025; Poudel et al., 2019). Finally, consistent with the work place core competencies identified by aged care pharmacists (Wang et al., 2025), participants in this study requested additional, tailored training on how to discuss vaccines with aged care residents.

Extensive literature highlights effective ways to address patient concerns, providing valuable guidance for pharmacist immunisers. For example, research with providers (Leask et al., 2012) suggests that pharmacist immunisers’ communication with patients who express deep concerns or who decline vaccination should be guiding rather than persuasive, elicit patients’ own motivations to vaccinate and avoid adversarial debates. Misinformation research (Lewandowsky et al., 2000) further recommends that providers resist the righting reflex, share correct information, warn that the information shared by the patient is a myth, respectfully explain why the false idea is incorrect, and repeat the facts. The above evidence (Leask et al., 2012; Lewandowsky et al., 2000) underpins the scenario-based training (eLearning module) in vaccine communication for healthcare providers currently available for free from the Sharing Knowledge About Immunisation (SKAI) website (The National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance, Last updated 2023). SKAI training equips providers with skills to identify patient attitudes towards vaccination and to use tailored, evidence-based communication for each group.

Regarding supportive communication with aged care residents, age-related changes in cognition, perception, and sensation can affect older adults’ ability to communicate. Understanding their communication preferences is essential for developing approaches that enhance patient-centred outcomes (Brooks et al., 2017). A global review of the literature (Sharkiya, 2023) identified that when providers initiate and maintain positive non-verbal (e.g. touching the patient on the arm, smile, gaze, head nod) and verbal communication, older patients report greater satisfaction and have better health outcomes. Components of positive verbal communication include: tailoring the discussion to patient’s needs; showing mutual respect; and appropriately addressing rather than avoiding conflict (Sharkiya, 2023). Communicating effectively with older adults relies on providers maintaining a respectful view of aging and demonstrating awareness and proficiency in non-verbal cues. (Wanko Keutchafo et al., 2022). We therefore recommend increasing awareness of the SKAI website and eLearning module among Australian pharmacists. We also recommend expanding existing training to cover ageism, non-verbal communication with older adults, and tailored scenarios for aged care residents and their substitute decision-makers.

Participants in this study also identified the need for patient resources that succinctly cover key information on diseases and vaccines and are easy to understand for patients who are older, from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) backgrounds and have limited literacy. The suggested format of patient resources (Table 2), including printable and audio-visual resources, is consistent with the published recommendations for education materials for older adults (Goodman & Lambert, 2023). Some participants also suggested integrating vaccine information on consent forms for aged care residents and ensuring that in-language resources for CALD patients are culturally appropriate.

Notably, participants were not aware of where to source patient resources for older adults, despite these being available through the websites that participants reported visiting and trusting (Health Direct, 2025a, 2025b; Immunisation Foundation of Australia, 2025; The National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance, 2025c). This highlights the need to better disseminate information about patient resources among Australian pharmacists. At the same time, an audit of such resources could help identify whether additional or revised patient materials are required to meet the needs of priority populations identified by study participants. Using participatory approaches to co-design communication resources for older adults with limited health literacy can be helpful in producing appealing and understandable patient resources (Koops van ‘t Jagt et al., 2016). Research indicates that co-developed resources that combined narrative, photo and video-based stories may be most appropriate (Koops van ‘t Jagt et al., 2016), which is consistent with pharmacist recommendations in our study.

Pharmacists in this study recommended centralising training and resources through a trusted hub. The Australian National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance (NCIRS) website, funded by the Commonwealth Department of Health, Disability and Aging (The National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance, 2025b), offers evidence-based materials, webinars, and vaccine information for providers, and hosts SKAI. The study suggests the need for wider dissemination of NCIRS resources among Australian pharmacists.

Finally, regional and sole pharmacists reported a level of professional isolation and suggested pharmacist forums for peer interaction, sharing local strategies and ongoing professional engagement, alongside webinars and support lines. This suggests that establishing or strengthening networks such as the Rural Pharmacy Support Network, Rural Pharmacists Australia, and the Rural Pharmacy Forum may help meet these needs.

Future research

This study examined the communication and support needs of pharmacist immunisers in Australia. At the same time, participants indicated some broader systemic challenges, including under-resourced regional pharmacies, professional isolation among sole practitioners, flawed vaccination reporting in aged care, and unclear guidance on protocol deviations. Further research is needed to explore these challenges and develop practical and context-specific solutions.

Strengths and limitations

In this study, we captured insights from pharmacist immunisers from diverse metropolitan, regional, and rural settings across Australia. This offers valuable insights into practices and support needs, especially in underexplored areas like aged care and sole pharmacist rural practice. Thematic analysis across sectors revealed both setting-specific and cross-sector needs, strengthening the relevance of findings for policy, training, and implementation.

Self-selection may have introduced response bias, favouring pharmacists more engaged in immunisation, potentially underrepresenting those less supported, experienced or confident. Few participants were from general practice or had experience immunising children or adolescents; future research should explore sector-specific challenges and communication needs with younger populations and their carers.

Findings are context specific and may not be directly transferable to other healthcare settings in other locations or countries. The immunisation services pharmacists deliver, as well as their experiences and communication needs, may differ substantially across settings. However, the description of the study context and participant characteristics and work environments will allow others to assess the relevance and potential transferability of the findings to different settings.

While participant feedback on study findings was not sought given the impracticability of re-contacting busy participants, a range of strategies enhanced rigour instead, including group analysis and regular team discussions on emerging categories and themes.

Conclusion

This study highlights the communication and support needs of pharmacist immunisers in Australia. While participants reported confidence in their vaccination skills and patient care, they identified gaps in accessible, tailored education materials, especially for older adults, aged care residents, those with limited health literacy, and culturally diverse groups. There is a clear need for concise, visually engaging, and culturally appropriate resources, as well as centralised platforms for professional education. Enhancing communication skills through scenario-based training and promoting tools like SKAI could improve vaccination discussions and informed consent. Addressing the unique needs of sole and rural pharmacists through peer forums and integrated professional networks may also support confidence.

Pharmacist-led immunisation is an important immunisation service delivery option in Australia, particularly in regional and rural areas where general practice is the least accessible. At the same time, we acknowledge the importance of evaluating the effectiveness of other immunisation service delivery options that help to address known vaccination barriers, including nurse-led drop in-immunisation clinics.
