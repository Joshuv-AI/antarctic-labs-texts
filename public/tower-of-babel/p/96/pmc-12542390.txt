CD55 may be an important prognostic factor of thymic epithelial tumors: a retrospective study
Authors: Jiao Ma, Yan Zhang, Jiamin Liang, Xiujuan Han, Shaojun Zhu, Jing Tian, Dongjing Ding, Kairui Duan, Xue Wang, Yu Han, Wei Zhang, Li Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThymic epithelial tumors (TETs) currently lack effective prognostic factors and novel treatment approaches, the aim of the study is to investigate the expression of CD55 in TETs and the relationship with clinicopathological characteristics and prognosis.MethodsFirstly, 250 TETs, including 206 thymoma and 44 thymic squamous cell carcinomas (TSCC), were retrospectively selected. Subsequently, the expression levels of CD55 protein and mRNA were assessed using immunohistochemistry (IHC) and qRT-PCR. Finally, the relationship among CD55, clinicopathological characteristics, and prognosis was analyzed.ResultsThe expression of CD55 mRNA and protein were higher in thymoma (p = 0.0148; p < 0.0001) and TSCC (both p < 0.0001) compared with normal thymus tissues. Immunohistochemically, the positive rate of CD55 was 80% (200/250) in TETs, mainly located in cytomembrane. 224 patients were successfully followed up, of which 33 patients developed progression. Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that histological type, Masaoka-Koga stage, tumor completeness of resection, adjuvant therapy, CD55 mRNA, and protein (all p < 0.01) were significant factors affecting progression free survival (PFS). Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that CD55 mRNA (p < 0.001), CD55 protein (p = 0.029), tumor completeness of resection (p = 0.010), and Masaoka-Koga stage (p = 0.006) were independent risk factors of PFS. In addition, Pearson’s correlation analysis indicated a significant correlation between CD55 mRNA and protein, histological type, tumor completeness of resection, adjuvant therapy, and Masaoka-Koga stage (all p < 0.001). Unpaired T-test showed that the high expression of CD55 mRNA and protein were significantly associated with incomplete resection, higher histological grade, Masaoka-Koga stage, and adjuvant therapy (all p < 0.01). Furthermore, multivariate Cox analysis showed that CD55 mRNA (p < 0.001), CD55 protein (p = 0.037), tumor completeness of resection (p < 0.05) were independent risk factors of PFS in the high-risk TETs subgroup.ConclusionsThe high expression of CD55 was related to poor prognosis of TETs. Moreover, its significant association with worse outcomes in high-risk TETs subgroup further underscores it its potential as a prognostic marker and therapeutic target, especially in TSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04051-2.

Introduction

Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs), mainly including thymoma, thymic carcinoma and neuroendocrine tumors, are a relatively rare group of tumors occurring in the anterior mediastinum. The incidence is approximately 1.3 to 3.2 per 1,000,000 persons [1], accounting for 25–30% of mediastinal tumors, of which 75–80% are thymoma. Thymoma is mainly classified into five histological subtypes, including type A, AB, B1, B2, and B3 according to the morphology of epithelial cells and the proportion of lymphocytes. In general, the prognosis of thymoma is relatively good, the 10-year overall survival (OS) rate for patients with Type A, AB, and B1 thymomas is approximately 82–100%, whereas the 10-year OS rates for Type B2 and B3 thymomas are 87% and 64%, respectively [2]. In addition, many studies have confirmed that thymoma is associated with autoimmune diseases (ADs), mainly myasthenia gravis (MG), and about 30% of patients are accompanied by MG [3]. Other paraneoplastic ADs associated with TETs include pure red cell aplasia (PRCA), hypogammaglobulinemia (Good syndrome), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and polymyositis (prevalence 1–5%), and other autoimmune defects [4, 5]. Thymic carcinoma is much less common than thymoma, accounting for 14–22% of all TETs [6], and the 10-year OS rate is about 40% [7]. Thymic squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) is the major histological subtypes of thymic carcinoma, and accounts for 61.8–74.5% [8]. Thymic neuroendocrine tumors, as an exceedingly rare category of neoplasms, constitute merely 2–5% of all TETs cases [9]. In the Chinese guidelines for clinical diagnosis and treatment of TETs (2021 edition), it is proposed that TETs can be simplified into three subgroups: low-risk group (types A, AB, and B1), high-risk group (types B2 and B3), and thymic carcinoma group based on their biological behavior [10]. Meantime, the prognosis of TETs is also correlated with various factors, including Masaoka-Koga stage and completeness of tumor resection.

At present, the treatment of TETs includes surgery, radiotherapy, neoadjuvant therapy, chemotherapy, or systemic treatment against any combination of the above option. Of them, surgery is the most common treatment for patients with resectable tumors. For patients with large or unresectable tumors, neoadjuvant therapy, especially chemotherapy, is also a major means. Although patients with thymoma or thymic carcinoma have a response to chemotherapy, the OS benefit may be affected by postoperative radiotherapy. Thus, until now, the prognosis and biological behavior of TETs have been a contentious topic [11].

CD55, also known as decay-accelerating factor (DAF), is a glycosylated phosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored regulator of the complement system. It can disrupt the host cell’s C3/C5 convertase, thereby preventing complement activation on its surface and the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) [12]. Additionally, CD55 is related to various diseases, such as CD55 deficient protein deficient bowel disease (CHAPLE) [13], paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) [14], rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [15] and other ADs. It is also considered as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in many malignant tumors, such as gastric carcinoma [16], gallbladder cancer [17], pancreatic cancer [18], and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [19], making it significant for research. Furthermore, CD55 is also involved in tumor initiation and progression by regulating multiple key signaling pathways, primarily including the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway, and the protein kinase C (PKC) pathway, among others, and it mediates cascade reactions of related signals [20–22]. Through complex interactions, these pathways collectively influence malignant biological behaviors of tumor cells such as proliferation, invasion, and metastasis.

However, it is unclear about the role of CD55 in TETs as a malignant tumor originating from immune organs and closely related to ADs. Whether it can also become a therapeutic target or an effective prognostic factor of TETs remains to be discussed.

In this study, we investigated the expression of CD55 in thymoma and TSCC by qRT-PCR and IHC staining, and analyzed the relationship with the clinicopathological characteristics and prognosis. The results demonstrated that CD55 might be used as a prognostic marker and therapeutic target for TETs.

Materials and methods

Tissue samples

All the surgically resected specimens with thymoma, thymic carcinoma and normal adult thymus tissues were consecutively retrieved from Department of Pathology between January 2019 and December 2021. Simultaneously, the cases with other malignant or borderline tumors were excluded. Normal adult thymic tissue was derived from thymic tissue inadvertently excised during surgery for intrathoracic nodular goiter, which was intermingled within the pathological lesions. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Commission of Tangdu Hospital. Informed written consent was obtained from all patients before surgery. All specimens were processed according to standardized pathological procedures, including fixation in 10% neutral buffered formalin, standardized sampling, dehydration in an automatic tissue processor, and paraffin embedding, etc. All thymoma and TSCC sections were carefully examined and diagnosed by two senior pathologists in accordance with the WHO guidelines for TETs.

Furthermore, their clinicopathological data, including patients’ gender, age, maximum diameter of tumor, histological type of tumor, Masaoka-Koga stage, treatment methods, medications, whether accompanied by MG, surgical methods, and tumor completeness of resection, were documented. Of them, the completeness of surgery resection was classified as R0: complete resection, R1: microscopic residual disease infiltrating resection margins, and R2: macroscopic residual disease.

IHC staining and assessment

First, all H&E stained sections of patients were examined to select the paraffin block with the most typical histology and the highest proportion of tumor tissue. Subsequently, IHC staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol, including sectioning, deparaffinization with gradient ethanol, dehydration with xylene, antigen retrieval and blocking, incubation with primary and secondary antibodies, DAB chromogenic development, counterstaining, and mounting.

The primary rabbit anti-human CD55 polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, USA) was diluted at a ratio of 1:1400. All slides were evaluated by three pathologists using a double-blind method according to the score of proportion and intensity of tumor cells with CD55 positivity. Of them, the proportion of tumor cells with positivity was defined as the ratio of positive cells in all cells, and divided into four levels: scored 0: negative; scored 1: 10%−30%, scored 2: 30%−60%, and scored 3: ≥60%. The staining intensity was graded into four levels: scored 0: negative, scored 1: light yellow, scored 2: brownish yellow, and scored 3: dark brown. The final result of the IHC staining was determined by the product of the percentage of tumor cells showing positivity and the staining intensity score.

Reverse transcription and qRT-PCR

Firstly, five 4-µm-thick sections were cut from representative paraffin blocks. RNA extraction was performed using a nucleic acid extraction kit (Tiangen, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and the extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Evo M-MLV reverse transcription kit (Accurate Biology, China). Subsequently, the expression levels of CD55 and GAPDH mRNA in TETs were detected using an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System. The primer sequences were as follows: human GAPDH (F: GAGTCAACGGATTTGGTCGT, R: GACAAGCTTCCCGTTCTCAG); human CD55 (F: TTCCAGTCGGTACTGTTGTGG; R: CCCGGATTAGGGCATGATTTCT).

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 statistical software package and GraphPad Prism 8 were used for data analysis, X-Tile software (Version 3.6.1) was utilized to determine the cutoff values. Continuous quantitative data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\overline{x}$$\end{document}±s), while categorical data were analyzed using unpaired T-tests or one-way ANOVA. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and log-rank testing. Univariate and stepwise forward multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were employed to identify risk factors for PFS, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships among risk factors. A two tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and statistically significant results were marked with asterisks in the figures and tables (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

Results

General characteristics of patients and follow-up status

Totally, 250 patients with TETs (206 thymoma and 44 TSCC) included 128 males and 122 females, with a mean age of 53.86 years (ranging from 22 to 81 years). Among them, 63 patients were accompanied by MG. The average diameter of TETs was 5.06 cm (ranging from 1.10 to 15.00 cm). Histopathologically, according to the 2020 WHO classification of thoracic tumors, low-risk type A, AB, and B1 thymoma were 21, 83, and 44 cases in turn; high-risk type B2 and B3 were 34 and 24 cases, respectively. Namely, thymoma with low and high risk group was 148 cases and 58 cases, respectively. In addition, among 44 patients with TSCC, there were 33 males and 11 females. Simultaneously, according to the Masaoka-Koga stage criteria for TETs, there were 46 cases in stage I, 126 cases in stage II, 48 cases in stage III, and 30 cases in stage IV. These were grouped into stages I/II and III/IV, resulting in 172 cases and 78 cases, respectively (Table 1).

Among 250 surgically resected cases, complete resection (R0), microscopic residual disease with infiltrated resection margins (R1), and macroscopic residual disease (R2) were observed in 234, 2, and 14 cases, respectively. Among 250 patients with TETs, 157 underwent surgery alone, while 93 received surgery combined with adjuvant therapy (including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy) (Table 1). The details of postoperative adjuvant treatments were as follows: 47 patients received radiotherapy alone; 2 received radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy; 24 received chemotherapy alone, among whom 13 (54.2%, 13/24) were treated with a paclitaxel plus nedaplatin regimen, and 1 received chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy. Additionally, 12 patients received postoperative radiotherapy combined with chemotherapy, and 3 patients received postoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy. Preoperative adjuvant therapy was administered in 4 cases, including 1 patient who received preoperative chemotherapy, 1 who received preoperative radiotherapy, and 2 who received preoperative chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy. Among the 8 patients who received immunotherapy (2 combined with postoperative radiotherapy, 1 with chemotherapy, 3 with chemoradiotherapy, and 2 with preoperative chemotherapy), all were treated with either pembrolizumab or rituximab.

Of 250 patients, 224 were successfully followed up, and 26 were lost. The median survival time was 43.94 months (range from 0.67 to 64.94 months). Tumor progression, including recurrence, metastasis, and death, occurred in 33 patients (14.73%, 33/224). The most frequent initial site of metastasis was lung and pleura (14 cases, 42.42%), followed by the liver (2 cases, 6.06%). Ten patients (4.46%, 10/224) died, two of these deaths were attributed to myasthenic crisis. The overall PFS rates in the first, second, and third years after thymectomy were 95.10%, 91.96%, and 91.02%, respectively (Fig. 1).

Expression of CD55 in different TETs

Due to the high degree of data dispersion, we applied a square root transformation to all protein expression and mRNA data to ensure the data conformed to a normal distribution, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the statistical analysis. The results demonstrated that the expression levels of CD55 mRNA and protein were significantly elevated in thymomas (p = 0.0148; p < 0.0001) and TSCC (both p < 0.0001) compared with normal thymic tissue. Specifically, the expression levels increased progressively with higher malignancy, indicating that the more aggressive the tumor, the higher the expression levels of CD55 (Fig. 2). Among them, based on the product of IHC staining intensity (scores: 0, 1, 2, 3) (Fig. 3) and the proportion of positive cells (scores: 0, 1, 2, 3) (Fig. 4), the positivity rate of CD55 in TETs was 80% (200/250). Moreover, CD55 was primarily localized on the cell membrane, with a minority observed in the cytoplasm.

Using X-Tile software (Version 3.6.1), a cutoff value analysis was performed for CD55 mRNA and protein to determine the optimal grouping criteria. Based on the 2−ΔΔCT values, CD55 mRNA expression levels were divided into a high-expression group (≥ 21.9) and a low-expression group (< 21.9). Similarly, according to the IHC staining results, CD55 protein expression was categorized into a high-expression group (the product of proportion and intensity, IHC score ≥ 5.7) and a low-expression group (IHC score < 5.7).

To avoid the potential impact of high correlation between CD55 mRNA and CD55 protein expression levels on model stability, we constructed two separate multivariate Cox proportional hazards models and performed VIF diagnostics. The VIF values for all variables in both models were at a low level. In the model containing CD55 mRNA, the VIF values ranged from 1.197 to 1.620, with a mean VIF of 1.402. In the model containing CD55 protein, the VIF values ranged from 1.182 to 2.141, with a mean VIF of 1.619 (Supplementary Table 1). All VIF values were well below the empirical thresholds of 5, indicating the absence of severe multicollinearity in the models. Therefore, the results of the subsequent multivariate analysis are stable and reliable.

Multivariate cox proportional hazards regression analysis for PFS

We included covariates with p<0.01 from the univariate Cox regression analysis into the progressive stepwise multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. To facilitate this analysis, we categorized tumor histological types into low-high risk group and TSCC. Multivariate Cox regression analysis indicated that only CD55 mRNA (HR=8.112, 95% CI: 3.683–17.865, p<0.001), tumor completeness of resection (HR=2.995, 95% CI: 1.304–6.879, p=0.010), and Masaoka-Koga stage (HR=4.142, 95% CI: 1.505–11.399, p=0.006) were independent risk factors for PFS, but histological type (p=0.382) or adjuvant therapy (p=0.567) were not associated with PFS of TETs (Table 3). Furthermore, when CD55 protein, Masaoka-Koga stage, tumor completeness of resection, adjuvant therapy and histological type were included, the progressive stepwise multivariate Cox regression analysis also demonstrated that the expression of CD55 protein (HR=2.330, 95% CI: 1.089–4.984, p=0.029), tumor completeness of resection (HR=3.001, 95% CI: 1.317–6.838, p=0.009), and Masaoka-Koga stage (HR=6.243, 95% CI: 2.337–16.672, p<0.001) significantly impacted PFS, but histological type (p=0.391) or adjuvant therapy (p=0.775) were not associated with PFS of TETs (Table 4). Association of CD55 with clinicopathological features and treatment modalities

This study systematically evaluated the correlation between CD55 mRNA, CD55 protein, and clinicopathological features using the Pearson's correlation analysis. The analyzed variables included thymoma-associated MG, age, gender, tumor diameter, histological type, tumor completeness of resection, Masaoka-Koga stage, and adjuvant therapy etc. The results showed that the expression of CD55 mRNA was significantly associated with the expression of CD55 protein (r=0.396, p<0.001), histological type (r=0.430, p<0.001), tumor completeness of resection (r=0.363, p<0.001), Masaoka-Koga stage (r=0.332, p<0.001), and adjuvant therapy (r=0.249, p<0.001). Prognostic value of CD55 in patients with high-risk TETs

Given that disease progression occurred in only two cases among low-risk thymoma patients, subsequent analyses in this study focused specifically on patients with high-risk thymoma (types B2 and B3) and TSCC (herein collectively referred to as high-risk TETs) to avoid potential confounding effects on overall prognostic assessment, aiming to independently validate the prognostic value of CD55 in this high-risk population.

Univariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that within the high-risk TETs, high CD55 mRNA (HR=6.680, 95% CI: 3.261–14.434, p<0.001), high CD55 protein (HR=2.398, 95% CI: 1.170–4.915, p=0.017), Masaoka-Koga stage III/IV (HR=2.916, 95% CI: 1.115–7.627, p=0.029), and incomplete tumor resection (HR=3.268, 95% CI: 1.443–7.399, p=0.005) were all significant predictors of poor prognosis. Although TSCC as a histological type (HR=1.798, 95% CI: 0.872–3.709, p=0.112) and adjuvant therapy (HR=1.863, 95% CI: 0.822–4.223, p=0.136) showed a trend toward increased risk, these associations did not reach statistical significance. No significant correlations were observed between PFS and gender (p=0.689), thymoma-associated MG (p=0.733), age (p=0.519), or tumor diameter (p=0.257) (Supplementary Table 4). In the CD55 mRNA model, the VIF values for all variables ranged from 1.123 to 1.489, with tolerance statistics all exceeding 0.672. Similarly, in the CD55 protein model, VIF values ranged from 1.108 to 1.692, and all tolerance values were greater than 0.591, indicating the absence of significant multicollinearity in both models (Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

CD55, also known as DAF, is a known inhibitor of complement system. It is not only closely associated with many conditions, such as malaria[23], protein-losing enteropathy (PLE)[24], PNH[25], and other ADs, but also plays a role in the growth and progression of various tumors. For example, one cause of PLE syndrome is the deletion of CD55. Eculizumab, a C5-targeted drug approved by FDA, can be used to alleviate the symptoms of PLE syndrome by inhibiting the activation of terminal complement pathway and preventing autoimmune damage[23]. Thus, it has also been approved by FDA for the treatment of atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, generalized MG, and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders[26]. Similarly, Pozelimab, the latest drug that can block C5 activity, has been used to treat CD55-deficient PLE and MG, etc[27]. The two drugs are targeted at patients with CD55 deficiency, but most malignant tumors, such as lung cancer [28], gastric cancer [16], ovarian cancer[29], leukemia[30], prostate cancer [31], cervical cancer[32], and even gastrointestinal stromal tumors[33], exhibit high levels of CD55 expression. Moreover, the high expression of CD55 is associated with poor prognosis of some tumors such as colorectal cancer[34] and breast cancer[35]. The main reason is that the increase of CD55 expression can reduce complement deposition on the cell membrane and complement-mediated lysis in these tumors, and also lead to the decrease of cell adhesion, thereby contributing to tumor invasion and metastasis[36]. However, it is still unclear about the role of CD55 in TETs originating from thymus.

In the study, we found that the expression of CD55 mRNA and protein were higher in TETs compared with normal thymus tissues, suggesting that the higher the malignancy of TETs, the greater the expression level of CD55. Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that CD55 mRNA, CD55 protein, tumor completeness of resection, histological types, adjuvant therapy, and Masaoka-Koga stage were all factors affecting PFS of TETs patients. Moreover, after the covariates with p<0.01 being incorporated into a multivariate Cox regression analysis, the results showed that CD55 mRNA, CD55 protein, tumor completeness of resection, and Masaoka-Koga stage were independent risk factors affecting PFS. In addition, Pearson's correlation analysis revealed significant correlation between CD55 expression, histological grade, tumor completeness of resection, adjuvant therapy, and Masaoka-Koga stage. However, the expression of CD55 was not associated with MG or diameter of tumors. The aforementioned research findings indicated that higher histological grade, advanced Masaoka-Koga stage, incomplete tumor resection, and adjuvant therapy were associated with elevated CD55 expression levels. In conclusions, our results indicated that elevated CD55 was often associated with poor survival outcomes. In subgroup analysis of high-risk group (types B2 and B3) and TSCC, high CD55 mRNA, high CD55 protein and incompleteness of resection remained independent risk factors for poor prognosis.

Interestingly, Pearson’s correlation analysis showed that the expression of CD55 protein was significantly correlated with gender and age. Specifically, the expression of CD55 protein was higher with the increase of age. Simultaneously, the expression of CD55 protein was higher in male patients than that in females. However, there was no statistical significance between mRNA level and the above parameters, although mRNA showed a positive correlation with protein expression. This discrepancy may be due to post-translational modification, mRNA stability, translational efficiency, and other factors that may lead to small differences between mRNA and protein levels. Meantime, univariate Cox regression analysis and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that gender and age had no significant effect on the prognosis of TETs patients. However, other studies have found that age[37] and gender[38] impact TETs prognosis, suggesting that they may be potential risk factors for TETs prognosis. This may be related to the short follow-up time of our patients, more samples are needed for further confirmation.

In addition, we found that there was no significant correlation between tumor diameter and PFS, CD55 mRNA, and protein expression levels. More surprisingly, there was no statistical significance though CD55 expression was higher in TETs without MG compared with those with MG. It may be caused by two factors. First, TSCC was included in the study, and it is hardly accompanied by MG; secondly, the down-regulation of CD55 may lead to over-activation of complement and increase the levels of C3 and C5, thus aggravating the symptoms of thymoma-associated MG. Moreover, the discovery was supported by the finding that C5 inhibiting drugs can relieve MG symptoms. Among the 10 deaths, 2 cases (20%) were due to MG crisis, indicating that MG remained an important cause of death though it was not an independent risk factor for TETs PFS. It highlights the necessity of strict monitoring and prevention of MG after TETs resection.

Additionally, adjuvant therapy was associated with an increased risk in univariate Cox regression analysis of TETs, and was more frequently administered to patients with high CD55 expression, this likely reflects substantial confounding by indication. Specifically, patients with poorer prognostic features are more likely to receive adjuvant therapy, creating the illusion of harm. The true effect of adjuvant therapy was clarified through multivariate Cox regression analysis, which confirmed that it was not an independent risk factor for adverse outcomes in TETs after adjusting for key covariates.

Surgery remains the primary treatment modality for resectable TETs. However, 10–30% of patients relapse after radical resection, even after 10–20 years[39]. For patients with locally advanced or unresectable TETs, perioperative therapy, such as chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy, should be considered to improve resectability and reduce the risk of recurrence[40]. For TETs patients with MG, symptom assessment, physical examination, and medical treatment should be performed before surgery. Of them, traditional medical treatments include cholinergic drugs, corticosteroids, and immunosuppressants, such as azathioprine and cyclophosphamide[41]. However, their serious side effects have brought great challenges to patients and physicians. Therefore, some novel therapies, including neonatal Fc receptor antagonists, B-cell targeted therapies, plasma cell targeted therapies, and complement inhibitors[42], have been applied in clinic. Complement inhibitors, in particular, can relieve MG symptoms by suppressing the upregulation of C3 and C5 in the complement activation pathway, and become a reality with FDA approval.

In subsequent research, we further observed a significant correlation between the high expression of CD55 and the suppressed state of adaptive immunity in TSCC (unpublished data), suggesting that CD55 also played a critical role in the immune evasion mechanisms of tumor cells. Of course, our research also has some limitations. Most importantly, we can't conduct experiments at the cell level to further explore the mechanism of CD55 because there are no commercial cell lines with thymoma or TSCC; Secondly, TSCC is relatively rare, and the number of observed cases is limited. Further research with a larger accumulation of cases is necessary to continue the investigation.

In conclusion, our studies demonstrated that the expression of CD55, tumor completeness of resection, and Masaoka-Koga stage were independent risk factors for PFS of TETs. Moreover, CD55 might be used as a prognostic marker and therapeutic target of TETs, particularly in high-risk TETs.

Conclusion

This study revealed that CD55 expression levels significantly increased in normal thymus tissues, thymomas, and TSCC in turn. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses demonstrated that CD55 mRNA, CD55 protein, tumor completeness of resection, and Masaoka-Koga stage were independent risk factors affecting PFS of TETs patients. Pearson's correlation analysis further indicated that CD55 was significantly associated with histological grade, tumor completeness of resection, and Masaoka-Koga stage. Subgroup analysis of high-risk TETs confirmed the prognostic significance of CD55. While univariate Cox analysis identified CD55 mRNA, CD55 protein, Masaoka-Koga stage, and tumor completeness of resection as significant factors for PFS, the multivariate Cox analysis refined these findings, establishing CD55 mRNA, CD55 protein, and tumor completeness of resection as independent risk factors.The aforementioned research findings indicated that higher histological grade, advanced Masaoka-Koga stage, and incomplete tumor resection were associated with elevated CD55 expression levels. In conclusion, our results indicated that CD55 might serve as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for TETs, especially in high-risk TETs.
