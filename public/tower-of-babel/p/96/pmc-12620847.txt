Exposure to Suaahara II Interventions and Knowledge of Maternal and Child Nutrition and Health Among Mothers, Grandmothers, and Male Household Heads: An Association Study in Nepal
Authors: Shalini Suresh, Edward A. Frongillo, Deependra K. Thapa, Ramesh P. Adhikari, Subir K. Kole, Femila Sapkota, Manisha Laxmi Shrestha, Kristine Garn, Pooja Rana Pandey, Indra Kshetri, Dipak Sharma, Kenda Cunningham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Suaahara was a USAID‐funded multi‐sectoral integrated nutrition program that aimed to improve the nutritional status of children under the age of five and their mothers in Nepal. The program included multiple interventions targeted to mothers and children, as well as other caregivers. Caregiver knowledge of optimal nutrition and health practices among household members is an important driver of healthy child and maternal nutrition behaviors. This study investigates the associations between exposure to Suaahara interventions and knowledge of maternal and child nutrition and health practices among mothers (n = 1239), grandmothers (n = 533), and male household heads (n = 551). Linear and logistic regression models were conducted using data from the endline survey of Suaahara's impact evaluation conducted in 2022. Exposure to Suaahara was associated with mothers' and grandmothers' knowledge of ideal child and maternal health and nutrition practices. Compared to those unexposed to Suuahara, mothers and grandmothers who were exposed to Suaahara interventions had 2.09 and 2.23 times higher odds respectively (p = 0.001; p = < 0.001), respectively, of having correct knowledge of exclusive breastfeeding, and 1.48 and 1.47 times higher odds respectively, of having correct knowledge of age of introduction of all types of complementary foods (p = 0.005; p = 0.070). Furthermore, exposed mothers had 1.49 times higher odds (p = 0.021) and exposed grandmothers had 1.42 times higher odds (p = 0.09) of knowing that young children should be fed more during illness. Male household heads who were exposed had 1.71 times higher odds of knowing that colostrum should be given to children immediately after birth (p = 0.027). Exposure to Suaahara was also associated with mothers' and grandmothers' knowledge of ideal maternal health and nutrition practices. Exposed mothers had 1.64 times higher odds (p = 0.011) and grandmothers had 2.92 times higher odds (p < 0.001) of knowing that mothers should take iron and folic acid supplementation for 180 days during pregnancy (p = 0.011; p = < 0.001). Exposed grandmothers had 1.87 times higher odds of having correct knowledge that mothers should have at least 4 antenatal care visits (p = 0.012). Exposed mothers and grandmothers had 1.88 and 1.90 times higher odds of having correct knowledge that mothers need at least 3 postnatal care visits (p = 0.002; p = 0.051). This study highlights multiple positive associations between exposure to Suaahara and knowledge of mothers and grandmothers around maternal and child health and nutrition practices. This effect was less pronounced in male household heads suggesting that tailored interventions are necessary to improve knowledge of appropriate child and maternal nutrition and health practices in this group. Future research should consider collecting data from multiple household members to better understand the paths between their knowledge and impact on maternal and child health and nutrition practices.

Introduction

Adequate nutrition is vital for maternal health as well as child health, development, and growth (World Health Organization 2023). Nepal has seen a marked decrease in child undernutrition, particularly the prevalence of stunting, over the past three decades. Ensuring nutritional well‐being, however, is still of high concern for women as well as children under 5 years of age in the country. As of 2022, the prevalence of stunting, underweight, and wasting among children under 5 years of age were 25%, 19%, and 8% respectively (Ministry of health and Population 2023). Additionally, nearly half (43%) of children 6–59 months of age have anemia. Among women 20–49 years of age, 10% are thin, 26% are overweight, and 8% are obese. Additionally, 34% of women 15–49 years of age in Nepal have anemia (Ministry of health and Population 2023).

Knowledge of appropriate nutrition and health practices among household members is an important driver of child nutrition (Aubel et al. 2021; Motebejana et al. 2022) along with maternal health and nutrition (Forh et al. 2022; Williams et al. 2012; Ickes et al. 2015). A recent global systematic review found that mothers' knowledge of nutrition practices is an essential factor in their ability to choose nutritious foods for their children (Prasetyo et al. 2023). Globally, nutrition programs have been effective at improving mothers' knowledge, attitudes, and practices as well as child nutritional status (Bhutta et al. 2013; Angeles‐Agdeppa et al. 2019; Forh et al. 2022). In Nepal, positive associations have been found between maternal nutrition knowledge and reducing stunting (Eklund et al. 2007), as well as maternal and grandmother knowledge with child nutrition practices, such as breastfeeding (early initiation of breastfeeding and colostrum feeding), and introduction of complementary foods (Karmacharya et al. 2017).

Still, global research suggests that adult household members' knowledge of ideal nutrition practices remains limited, especially among caregivers who have lower literacy rates (Forh et al. 2022; Gezimu et al. 2022). In Nepal, knowledge of ideal maternal and child health and nutrition practices may in part be inadequate due to limited access to high‐quality healthcare services, especially in rural areas (Mehata et al. 2017).

Recently, there has been growing acknowledgment of the importance of generating evidence among different household members. Studies have found that correct knowledge among household members such as grandmothers is connected to correct knowledge among mothers (Karmacharya et al. 2017). While mothers have traditionally been the focus of research, there has been little investigation into grandmothers, and even less men's, knowledge of ideal practices to support child and maternal nutrition, especially in Nepal (Aubel et al. 2021). Additionally, studies of associations between mothers' and grandmother knowledge and nutrition outcomes are primarily limited to infant and young child feeding practices. To more comprehensively understand the potential impacts of behavior change communication interventions on household members' knowledge of ideal maternal and child nutrition practices, there is a need to investigate multiple household members and linkages between different household members' knowledge and a wider variety of child and maternal nutrition and health outcomes. The aim of this study was to investigate the associations between exposure to social behavior change communication (SBCC) interventions in the Suaahara integrated nutrition program in Nepal and knowledge of maternal and child nutrition and health practices among mothers, grandmothers, and male household heads.

Methods

Suaahara Program

Suaahara was a USAID‐funded multi‐sectoral integrated nutrition program in Nepal that aimed to improve the nutritional status of children under the age of five and their mothers, ultimately scaled up to 42 of Nepal's 77 districts. The program spanned two implementation phases: Suaahara I from 2011 to 2016, and Suaahara II from 2016 to 2021 (extended to 2023 due to the covid‐19 pandemic and the Russia/Ukraine war). Suaahara's programming included integrated interventions across child and maternal nutrition, health services, family planning, agriculture, water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH), and good governance for nutrition. These programmatic interventions were supported by cross‐cutting themes including SBCC; gender equality and social inclusion (GESI); public‐private partnerships; emergency preparedness and response; and monitoring, evaluation, and research for learning (Frongillo et al. 2024).

Following new global evidence suggesting the importance of a family focus for maternal and child nutrition programs (Martin et al. 2021; Aubel et al. 2021), Suaahara expanded its target reach in the second phase by encouraging program participation among other household members such as grandparents and fathers. The four main categories of Suaahara II SBCC interventions were: (1) interpersonal communication (IPC), (2) community mobilization (CM), (3) mass media (MM), and (4) SMS messages (SMS). Suaahara II employed a variety of frontline workers and volunteers including community nutrition facilitators, field supervisors, village model farmers, WASH triggers and GESI champions, leading by example and provoking others in their community to adopt ideal behaviors. These cadres were trained in IPC techniques and tasked with conducting home visits and supporting CM activities to counsel families about ideal nutrition‐related practices. The capacity for nutrition counseling of Nepal's cadre of Female Health Care Volunteers (FCHV) was also strengthened through trainings and supervision. The FCHVs integrated nutrition messages into health mothers' group meetings in targeted communicates. Other CM activities included food demonstrations, key life events to celebrate special moments in the 1000‐day period such as pregnancy and a child turning 6 months and 2 years of age, WASH triggering sessions, and special day, week, and month celebrations, such as breastfeeding week. During the first phase, Suaahara designed and aired a radio program, known as Bhanchhin Aama; during Suaahara II this program was expanded to include episodes aired on Facebook and YouTube and production was decentralized to facilitate access in local languages and local ownership. Finally, a series of nutrition‐related text messages were designed and sent to mothers' and male household heads' mobile phones (Frongillo et al. 2024).

Survey Sample and Design

This study was conducted using data collected from the endline survey of Suaahara's quasi‐experimental impact evaluation, conducted in 2022, across four intervention districts (Sindhupalchowk, Syangja, Rupandehi, and Darchula) and four comparison districts (Ramechhap, Tanahu, Chitwan, and Jumla). The endline evaluation was designed in alignment with the baseline survey conducted in 2012. The study was powered to answer primary impact questions with the sample size of 2480 households with a child under 5 years included in the endline survey. The evaluation design and household sampling are further detailed in manuscript 5 of this issue.

In the endline survey, three adults, if available, in each household were included: (1) mother of the child under five (primary respondent); (2) household decision‐maker/household heads (male, when available), and (3) grandmother of the child under five (if residing in the same household). The household head can potentially be the father, grandfather, uncle, grandmother, or other member of the household.

Exposure Variables

The exposure of interest in this study was whether an individual was exposed to at least one of the four main Suaahara II SBCC interventions: interpersonal communication, community mobilization, mass media, and SMS. The measurement of each of the four was as follows: Interpersonal Communication (IPC): having met with any Suaahara frontline worker (field supervisor, village model farmer, WASH trigger, etc.) in the past 6 months; Community Mobilization (CM): any participation in Suaahara community events such as food demonstrations, key life events, ideal family celebrations, WASH triggering sessions, Community Health Score Board interactions and special day, week, and month celebrations. Among mothers, this also included participation in FCHV‐led health mothers' groups, mobilized by FCHVs; Mass Media (MM): having ever listened to the Bhanchhin Aama program that is designed, aired, and promoted by Suaahara; and SMS Messages (SMS): having ever received nutrition and/or health related text messages on their mobile phone. This intervention was applicable only to mothers and male household heads as it was not targeted to grandmothers.

Data Analysis

Since the analysis was focused on investigating associations between intervention exposure and knowledge of appropriate health and nutrition practices, only respondents residing in intervention districts were included in the sample (n = 1239 for mothers, n = 533 for grandmothers, and n = 551 for male household heads).

Descriptive analyses of exposure variables and a variety of socioeconomic demographic variables were conducted to understand characteristics of each of these three samples. Logistic regression models were used to examine associations between exposure to Suaahara intervention platforms and each maternal and child nutrition knowledge outcome. Linear regression models were used to examine these same associations but using the summary scales for knowledge across all sub‐themes for maternal and child‐related outcomes. All models were adjusted for child age (completed months of life), child gender (male vs. female), respondent age (completed years of life), education (completed years of formal studying), agroecological zone of residence (plains, hills or mountains), and caste/ethnicity (“upper caste” Brahmin and Chhetri vs. not upper caste) and socioeconomic status measured in quintiles using the Equity Tool (Metrics for Management 2018). Models also accounted for geographic clustering at the ward level using a sandwich estimator.

Distribution across caste, socioeconomic status, agro‐ecological zone of residency, child gender, and child age was similar across the three respondent groups, which is expected as these measures were collected at the household level. Mothers had the highest level of education achieved with an average of 8 years of education, compared to 6 years for male household heads and 1 year for grandmothers.

Child Nutrition

Male household heads who were exposed to Suaahara II interventions had 1.71 times higher odds of knowing that colostrum should be given to children immediately (p = 0.027) (Table 3). There was no association between exposure to Suaahara II interventions and knowledge of colostrum of mothers and grandmothers. Mothers' and grandmothers' exposures to Suaahara II interventions were positively associated with their, but not male household heads', knowledge that babies should be exclusively breastfed for 6 months: exposed grandmothers had 2.23 times higher odds and mothers had 2.09 higher odds of knowing this (p = 0.001 and p < 0.001 respectively). Relatedly, compared to those not exposed to any Suaahara II intervention platforms, exposed mothers and grandmothers had greater odds of knowing that the appropriate age of introduction of all foods is between 6 and 8.9 months. Specifically, exposed mothers and grandmothers, but not male household heads, had 1.48 times and 1.47 times higher odds, respectively, to have correct knowledge of age of introduction of all foods (p = 0.005 and p = 0.070 respectively). Mothers who were exposed to Suaahara II interventions, compared to those who were not, had 1.49 times higher odds of knowing that children should be fed more during illness (p = 0.021). Similar results were found for grandmothers who had 1.42 times higher odds of having correct knowledge (p = 0.097), but not for male HH heads.

Maternal Health and Nutrition

Male household head exposure to Suaahara II platforms was not associated with any maternal health and nutrition knowledge outcome (Table 3). Mothers' exposure to Suaahara II interventions was not associated with their knowledge of the importance of at least 4 antenatal care contacts; grandmothers, however, who were exposed to Suaahara II interventions had 1.87 times higher odds compared to grandmothers who were not exposed to any Suaahara II interventions (p = 0.012). Furthermore, mothers' and grandmothers' exposure to any Suaahara II intervention was positively associated with mothers' and grandmothers' knowledge that mothers need at least 180 tablets of iron and folic acid during pregnancy: exposed mothers had 1.64 times higher odds and exposed grandmothers had 2.92 higher odds of knowing this (p = 0.011 and p < 0.001). Additionally, exposure to Suaahara II interventions for both mother and grandmother was associated with 1.88 times and 1.90 times higher odds, respectively, of knowing that mothers should have at least 3 postnatal care visits with a health service provider (p = 0.002 and p = 0.051).

The scale of all three maternal health and nutrition knowledge showed a positive association with maternal and grandmother exposure to Suaahara II interventions (Table 4). Any exposure to Suaahara II was associated with a 0.2 point increase in mothers' knowledge outcome on the scale of 0‐3 maternal health and nutrition knowledge outcomes (p = 0.001). For grandmothers, exposure to Suaahara II was associated with a 0.4 point higher maternal health and nutrition knowledge outcomes (p < 0.001). The same linear associations were not found for male household heads.

Discussion

This study analyzed associations between maternal, grandmother, and male household head exposure to Suaahara II SBCC interventions and their knowledge of ideal maternal and child health and nutrition behaviors in Nepal. The most consistent associations found were between maternal and grandmother exposure and knowledge: those exposed had a greater likelihood of having unprompted, correct knowledge of multiple maternal and child health and nutrition topics, such as breastfeeding and timing of introduction of complementary foods. Male household heads' exposure, in contrast, was associated with higher odds of correct knowledge regarding the importance of colostrum but not with other maternal and child health and nutrition‐related knowledge indicators.

Although Suaahara II expanded from the Suaahara I mother‐child dyad focus to a family focus aiming to engage all adult family members, most of the SBCC interventions continued to primarily target mothers with other family members as secondary target populations. Nearly 75% of all mothers of young children residing in Suaahara II intervention areas were exposed to at least one intervention platform compared to 32% of grandmothers and 43% of male household heads. The positive association found between mothers' exposure to Suaahara II interventions and mothers' knowledge is corroborated by other research on integrated nutrition programs that have also found positive effects of SBCC interventions on mothers' knowledge of correct nutrition practices (Angeles‐Agdeppa et al. 2019).

Our finding of almost universal associations between exposure to Suaahara II and knowledge, especially for maternal health knowledge outcomes among grandmothers, is encouraging. Exposure to Suaahara II was positively associated with grandmothers' knowledge of all three of the maternal health practices and three of the six child nutrition and health practices included in this study. Other studies have also reported similar positive findings. One study in Nepal found that a positive association between grandmothers' and mothers' correct knowledge relating to infant and young child feeding (IYCF) practices, and in turn, appropriate IYCF practices in the household (Karmacharya et al. 2017). A global review highlighted the important role of grandmothers in childcare, suggesting their knowledge is a major influence of practices in the household (Aubel et al. 2021). Despite grandmothers having the lowest exposure to Suaahara II among the household members surveyed (32%), the positive associations found highlight the potential for nutrition programs to target grandmother directly to improve their knowledge, which can potentially further translate into improved nutrition practices in the household, such as age‐appropriate infant feeding (Aubel et al. 2021; Motebejana et al. 2022). In designing SBCC materials specifically targeted to grandmothers, program teams must acknowledge that they tend to have less education and there may be more challenges in reaching the grandparents. Suaahara interventions and this survey were only reaching grandmothers that lived in the same household as the young child participating in the program (usually the paternal grandparents); in Nepal, however, household structures vary. Some young children only live with their parents and do not live with any grandparents, whereas others live with the maternal grandparents and, even among those who live with their paternal grandmothers, it is common practice for mothers to go back to their maternal home for childbirth and the postpartum period. Hence, interventions should consider the role of all grandparents including those who do not live in the same household as the young child but may play a major role at the time of delivery, postpartum, or beyond.

Exposure to SBCC interventions for male household heads had minimal association with their child nutrition knowledge and no association with their maternal nutrition knowledge. The association for male household heads' exposure and colostrum knowledge was not found among mothers and grandmothers, likely because the knowledge of this outcome was high among those samples (96% among mothers and 91% among grandmothers). The limited associations found for men may also be because some Suaahara II interventions, such as community events primarily involve women, as they are often part of health mothers' group meetings and thus, even if men are invited, they may not be willing to join. In our sample, for example, only 4% of male household heads had exposure to community events. Similarly, due to the nature of the topics (e.g., breastfeeding and maternal healthcare) Suaahara staff may have engaged more with women (mothers and grandmothers) on these topics due to socio‐cultural norms. Furthermore, the sample of male respondents who completed the household head questionnaire includes fathers, grandfathers, and other potential male guardians who may have different levels of knowledge and engagement with maternal and child health and nutrition. In addition, male household heads may be in residence in their households for only some parts of the year due to migration trends. Nepal has an especially high rate of migration among adult males, with over half of the country's households having at least one migrant member (Rajkarnikar 2020). The frequency and prevalence of migration impacts household structures and dynamics around decision‐makers. Future programming should continue to adopt even more nuanced targeting, identification of platforms, and tailored design of interventions with the explicit intent of engaging male household heads, given these considerations.

These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence about family‐based programming to improve child and maternal nutrition and health (Aubel et al. 2021). A limitation of the study is that the exposure analysis was restricted to a binary construction for exposure to any Suaahara II intervention versus none. Given the sample sizes and limited exposure for grandmothers and male household heads, it was not possible to investigate each exposure (interpersonal communication, mass media, community mobilization, and SMS outreach) separately to test variation in magnitude of association by intervention or to construct an exposure index to assess a dose–response relationship. Other research on Suaahara II has found a positive dose–response association between exposure to Suaahara II platforms and improved maternal and child dietary practices (Suresh et al. 2019). With a larger sample size, this would be insightful to investigate with grandmothers and male household heads, to see if exposure to more interventions increases the likelihood of having correct knowledge of maternal and child nutrition practices.

This study was cross‐sectional as most of these variables were not available at baseline to facilitate impact testing for changes in knowledge outcomes; thus, these results are associations and cannot be interpreted causally. Therre are several pathways through which an intervention such as Suaahara II can result in behavior change, and knowledge is one such path. This does not imply that the sole focus of the intervention was to increase knowledge. Other limitations to consider include reporting bias as most data points were self‐reported, as well as recall bias, especially in cases where individuals with children older than 1 year of age had to recall knowledge of infant feeding practices. Finally, because Suaahara II is an integrated nutrition program including community and frontline worker activities on a range of maternal health, child nutrition, WASH, agriculture, and family planning topics, it is challenging to dissect which households received support on which topics and from where or which platform. This is an inherent challenge that at‐scale, integrated programs face when interventions span multiple platforms and multiple sectors (Cunningham et al. 2017).

Most demographic and health surveys (including the NDHS) do not currently track the health and nutrition‐related knowledge among respondents. Suaahara's household‐level data complements the publicly available data with a unique insight into household members' knowledge. Future analyses should also consider the relationship between household members' knowledge, and how mothers' knowledge and behaviors may be influenced by grandmothers' and fathers' knowledge.

The findings in this study build on the primary impact evaluation analyses that investigated the impact of the program on maternal and child nutrition and health status and practices (Frongillo et al. 2024). These new insights into the association of exposure to Suaahara II with knowledge outcomes among diverse caregivers including mothers, grandmothers, and male household heads help us to understand the programmatic impact on a more intermediate outcome in the intervention's theory of change. The findings suggest the need for further attention to diverse family members in both integrated nutrition programs and related research.
