Are FIB-4 and liver stiffness measurement interchangeable for HCC risk stratification in MASLD?
Authors: Yimeng Zhou, Xue Meng
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the study by Embacher et al., demonstrating that the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) score performs comparably to liver stiffness measurement (LSM) in predicting hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) risk across two independent cohorts.1 The clinical implications are substantial: a universally accessible blood test could democratize HCC risk stratification where vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) is unavailable.

However, an important question remains regarding the applicability of these findings to patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), now the most prevalent cause of chronic liver disease worldwide. The authors' conclusion relies on two complementary yet structurally mismatched datasets. The tertiary care cohort (Vienna) enabled direct FIB-4/LSM comparison but was predominantly viral (51.5%), with MASLD accounting for only 29.1% of participants. Meanwhile, the UK Biobank cohort provided a metabolically representative population but lacked LSM data, precluding direct FIB-4/LSM equivalence testing in MASLD. Consequently, the present study cannot determine whether FIB-4 and LSM are interchangeable specifically in patients with MASLD. This limitation is further highlighted by the supplementary analysis: FIB-4 stratification within the MASLD subgroup (n = 1,790) relied on only six HCC events, yielding hazard ratios with very wide confidence intervals (subdistribution hazard ratio [SHR] 38.2; 95% CI 4.1–357 for FIB-4 >2.67 vs. <1.75), limiting reliable inference regarding marker equivalence.2

This limitation is further illustrated by a multicenter study of 12,950 patients with MASLD, in which approximately 30% exhibited discordant FIB-4 and LSM results.2 Patients with elevated FIB-4 but normal LSM showed no significant increase in liver-related events (adjusted SHR [aSHR] 1.19; 95% CI 0.60–2.36; p = 0.610) and similar HCC risk compared with the low-risk concordant group (aSHR 1.02). This suggests that FIB-4 elevation in MASLD may largely reflect age-related arithmetic inflation – a recognized consequence of the age-weighted FIB-4 formula – rather than true fibrosis burden. By contrast, patients with normal FIB-4 but elevated LSM retained a fourfold increased risk of liver-related events (aSHR 4.12; 95% CI 2.10–8.08). When the two markers diverge in MASLD, LSM may better reflect underlying fibrotic risk.

The limitations of FIB-4 in MASLD are also supported by diagnostic studies. A multinational biopsy-confirmed analysis of 18,759 patients with MASLD across 41 countries found that FIB-4 accuracy declined with age (AUC 0.70 in patients ≥65 years vs. 0.79 in those <65 years; p <0.0001) and with type 2 diabetes. The cut-off required to maintain 80% sensitivity for advanced fibrosis ranged from 0.41 in those aged 18–25 to 2.08 in those ≥81 years, whereas LSM maintained consistent performance across diabetes strata.3 A dedicated diagnostic study similarly reported FIB-4 accuracy of only 68.8% for advanced fibrosis (Kleiner F3–F4) in MASLD – below the ≥80% threshold recommended by EASL – while newer metabolic indices showed superior performance.4

Evidence from clinical pathways further supports this concern. In a large primary care MASLD cohort (n = 9,111), among patients with FIB-4 <1.30 the prevalence of LSM ≥8 kPa was more than twice as high in those with diabetes (9.3% vs. 3.9%) and markedly higher in those with BMI ≥35 kg/m2 (10.9% vs. 3.3%), indicating that FIB-4 may systematically under-triage metabolically high-risk patients.5 Collectively, these findings raise questions regarding the assumed equivalence of FIB-4 and LSM for HCC risk stratification in MASLD.

A further concern is that a substantial proportion of MASLD-related HCC arises without cirrhosis. A multicenter cohort study reported that 35% of MASLD-related HCC occurred in non-cirrhotic patients, who were diagnosed incidentally in 72% of cases compared with only 7% through surveillance, resulting in larger tumors and more advanced disease at presentation.6 Both FIB-4 and LSM therefore have an intrinsic ceiling in identifying non-cirrhotic at-risk patients, limiting the generalizability of fibrosis-based thresholds as universal HCC surveillance triggers in MASLD.

These limitations underscore the need for studies specifically designed to evaluate FIB-4 and LSM simultaneously in adequately powered MASLD cohorts while accounting for metabolic confounders. Incorporating longitudinal FIB-4 trajectories and metabolic parameters into future risk models may offer discriminatory value beyond what a single baseline measurement can provide.7

In summary, the present study represents a meaningful step toward simplifying HCC surveillance through accessible non-invasive testing. However, extending this conclusion to MASLD requires dedicated validation. MASLD-specific data consistently show systematic divergence between FIB-4 and LSM, with FIB-4 frequently overestimating risk due to age-related and metabolic confounding, whereas LSM retains stronger discriminative performance. Until direct equivalence is demonstrated in a well-powered MASLD cohort with simultaneous assessment of both markers, FIB-4 should be used with caution as a surrogate for LSM-guided HCC surveillance in MASLD.
