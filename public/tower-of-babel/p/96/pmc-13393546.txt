Injectable polysaccharide hydrogel microspheres integrated with BPNS–ZnO nanocomposites restore redox homeostasis and protect cartilage in osteoarthritis
Authors: Yuqing Lei, Tao Ma, Xiaolong Lin, Yuting Sun, Chenlong Liu, Jiapei Yao, Jingwei Wu, Junyang Li, Xiaoshuang Tu, Bo Pang, Zhuoyi Lu, Dong Li, Ling Zhang, Jingjing Shang, Luming Nong, Xindie Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis (OA) is a progressive whole-joint disease driven by a persistent inflammatory–oxidative microenvironment that fuels cartilage destruction and osteochondral remodeling, while effective disease-modifying interventions remain scarce. Here, an injectable microsphere platform (HM@BPZn) is engineered by incorporating electronically coupled BPNS–ZnO hybrid nanosheets into a dual-polysaccharide network of methacrylated hyaluronic acid and gallic acid-grafted chondroitin sulfate. Interfacial electronic redistribution within the BPNS–ZnO hybrids enables active modulation of the pathological joint microenvironment. HM@BPZn suppresses reactive oxygen species accumulation and NF-κB signaling in macrophages, reducing inflammatory mediator production and promoting pro-resolving polarization. In inflamed chondrocytes, HM@BPZn activates the Keap1–Nrf2 pathway, upregulates antioxidant effectors including HO-1 and NQO1, and restores matrix homeostasis by suppressing catabolic markers while enhancing anabolic and lubrication-associated phenotypes. Transcriptomic profiling further verifies coordinated rewiring of inflammatory and antioxidant signaling networks. In an ACLT-induced OA model, intra-articular delivery of HM@BPZn markedly mitigates synovitis, preserves proteoglycan-rich cartilage, and attenuates pathological subchondral bone remodeling and osteophyte formation. These findings establish HM@BPZn as an injectable microenvironment-regulating platform for disease-modifying OA treatment.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Injectable microspheres, Black phosphorus–ZnO hybrids, Microenvironment regulation, Immuno-redox reprogramming, Cartilage protection

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common form of arthritis and a major driver of chronic pain and disability [1]. It is now understood as a whole-joint disease rather than simple “wear and tear,” involving synovial inflammation, oxidative stress, cartilage extracellular matrix (ECM) breakdown, lubrication failure, and pathological remodeling of the osteochondral unit [2]. After injury or aging-associated degeneration, inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species (ROS) rise and activate catabolic programs in chondrocytes, leading to upregulation of matrix-degrading enzymes (e.g., MMPs and ADAMTS) and progressive loss of proteoglycans and collagen networks [3]. As this process continues, synovitis, cartilage degradation, and subchondral bone changes reinforce one another, locking the joint into a self-sustaining degenerative microenvironment that is difficult to reset with conventional symptomatic therapies [4].

Injectable biomaterials have become a widely explored intra-articular strategy because they can localize treatment, increase joint residence time, and reduce systemic exposure [5]. Hyaluronic acid (HA)–based materials are clinically relevant due to their lubrication and viscoelastic supplementation [6], while chondroitin sulfate (CS) and other glycosaminoglycans provide cartilage-mimetic biochemical cues and can support ECM homeostasis [7]. Methacrylated polymers enable in situ crosslinking to form stable depots, and microgels offer additional advantages including injectability through standard needles, tunable porosity, and sustained release capacity [8]. Many current systems load anti-inflammatory drugs, antioxidants, or bioactive ions to reduce synovitis and slow cartilage catabolism [9]; however, most remain “static” in function—delivering an early burst or a single dominant activity—while OA evolves dynamically from early inflammatory/oxidative stress to later enzyme-driven matrix destruction [10]. Therefore, designing a stage-adaptive intra-articular material is essential. It should rapidly quench ROS and dampen inflammation in early OA, yet sustain longer-term suppression of catabolic enzymes to protect the ECM during later degeneration, while maintaining injectability and local retention.

Immune microenvironment tuning is particularly meaningful in arthritis because synovial macrophages act as upstream regulators that determine the inflammatory “set point” of the joint [11]. Pro-inflammatory macrophage activation amplifies cytokine networks and oxidative stress, which in turn activates NF-κB–dependent catabolic programming in chondrocytes and accelerates ECM degradation [12]. Shifting macrophages toward a pro-resolving phenotype can reduce synovitis and lower cytokine and ROS pressure on cartilage [13]. At the same time, reinforcing intrinsic cytoprotective pathways in chondrocytes—especially the Keap1–Nrf2 antioxidant axis—can increase redox buffering capacity, suppress stress-induced inflammatory signaling, and limit induction of MMP/ADAMTS-mediated matrix breakdown [14]. Therefore, a dual-cell strategy that couples macrophage immunomodulation with chondrocyte cytoprotection offers a rational route to break the self-amplifying inflammation–oxidation–catabolism loop in OA.

Black phosphorus (BP) crystals have emerged as a unique 2D nanomaterial with strong redox activity and broad electronic properties, making them attractive for scavenging excessive reactive oxygen species in inflammatory tissues [15]. However, pristine BP is prone to oxidative degradation and limited colloidal stability, which may compromise its sustained intra-articular performance [16]. Zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles, in contrast, provide bioactive ionic signaling and can modulate inflammation-related pathways, yet their effects are often constrained by uncontrolled interfacial interactions and non-programmed availability [17]. Polydopamine (PDA), inspired by mussel adhesion chemistry, offers a versatile coating that stabilizes nanomaterials and introduces abundant catechol/amine groups for robust interfacial assembly [18]. Therefore, integrating BPNS, ZnO, and PDA into a coupled nanohybrid may provide a rational route to combine rapid antioxidant protection with more sustained microenvironment regulation, bridging early inflammatory control with later suppression of cartilage degeneration.

Hence, in this work, an injectable composite hydrogel microsphere platform was engineered by integrating electronically coupled BPNS–ZnO hybrid nanosheets into a dual-polysaccharide network formed from methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA) and gallic acid–modified chondroitin sulfate (CS–GA) (depicted in Scheme 1). The resulting microspheres were designed for intra-articular administration to reprogram the osteoarthritic joint niche through coordinated immuno–redox regulation. We show that upon intra-articular delivery, the microsphere depot maintains local retention while undergoing microenvironment-dependent remodeling, enabling sustained interaction between the embedded nanohybrids and the inflammatory milieu. The composite hydrogel microsphere functions as an intra-articular, microenvironment-programming therapeutic modality that couples upstream immune resolution with intrinsic chondrocyte cytoprotection, offering a disease-modifying strategy for osteoarthritis.

Design, fabrication, and therapeutic mechanism of HM@BPZn hydrogel microspheres for osteoarthritis treatment. (1) Fabrication of electronically coupled BPNS–ZnO hybrid nanosheets and their encapsulation into HAMA/CS–GA hydrogel microspheres via gas-shearing microfluidics and 405 nm photocrosslinking. (2) Intra-articular injection of HM@BPZn microspheres into osteoarthritic joints. (3) Sequential regulation of the pathological joint microenvironment through ROS scavenging, inflammation suppression, and cartilage repair. (4) Dual-cell mechanism: HM@BPZn inhibits NF-κB signaling and promotes M2 polarization in macrophages, while activating Keap1–Nrf2 signaling in chondrocytes. (5) In vivo therapeutic outcomes.

Materials and methods

Preparation of BPNS–ZnO hybrid nanosheets

Bulk black phosphorus crystals were first ground into fine powders and subsequently exfoliated via liquid-phase ultrasonication in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) under an ice bath with nitrogen protection. After sonication, the suspension was centrifuged to remove unexfoliated bulk materials, yielding few-layer black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNS).

To improve colloidal stability and enable subsequent ZnO decoration, BPNS were functionalized with a polydopamine (PDA) coating. Briefly, BPNS (1 mg mL−1) were dispersed in carbonate buffer (10 mM, pH 8.5), followed by the addition of dopamine hydrochloride (40 mg). The reaction was carried out under nitrogen protection with gentle stirring for 1–2 h. The obtained BPNS–PDA were collected by centrifugation (10 000 g, 5 min) and washed thoroughly with HEPES buffer.

ZnO nanoparticles were subsequently immobilized onto BPNS–PDA via electrostatic interaction. ZnO nanoparticles (1 mg mL−1) were ultrasonically dispersed and added dropwise into BPNS–PDA suspension (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.8) containing 25 mM NaCl to screen excessive electrostatic repulsion. The mixture was stirred in the dark for 2–4 h. The resulting BPNS–ZnO hybrids were purified by centrifugation and redispersed in deionized water for further characterization.

Synthesis of hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA)

Hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA) was synthesized via esterification of hyaluronic acid (HA) with methacrylic anhydride under alkaline aqueous conditions. Briefly, HA (1.0 g) was dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water and stirred overnight at 4 °C to obtain a homogeneous solution. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 8.0–8.5 using 1.0 M NaOH.

Methacrylic anhydride (MA; 0.8 mL) was added dropwise under continuous stirring in an ice bath to minimize side reactions. The reaction mixture was maintained at pH 8.0–8.5 by periodic addition of NaOH and allowed to proceed for 6 h at 4 °C. After completion, the reaction was quenched by adjusting the pH to 7.0.

The resulting solution was dialyzed against deionized water for 3 days using a dialysis membrane (MWCO 3.5 kDa), with water changed twice daily to remove unreacted reagents and byproducts. The purified solution was subsequently lyophilized to obtain HAMA as a white fluffy solid.

Synthesis of gallic Acid–Modified chondroitin sulfate (CS–GA)

Gallic acid–modified chondroitin sulfate (CS–GA) was synthesized via a two-step carbodiimide-mediated conjugation. In the first step, adipic dihydrazide (ADH) was grafted onto chondroitin sulfate (CS) to introduce hydrazide functionalities. CS (1.0 g) was dissolved in 100 mL of MES buffer (0.1 M, pH 5.5). EDC (0.5 g) and NHS (0.3 g) were added sequentially to activate the carboxyl groups of CS, followed by the addition of ADH (1.2 g). The reaction was allowed to proceed for 12 h at room temperature under gentle stirring.

The reaction mixture was dialyzed against deionized water (MWCO 3.5 kDa) for 3 days to remove excess reagents, and the product (CS–ADH) was obtained by lyophilization.

In the second step, gallic acid (GA) was conjugated to CS–ADH via EDC/NHS coupling. Briefly, CS–ADH (0.5 g) was dissolved in 50 mL of MES buffer (0.1 M, pH 5.5). Gallic acid (0.15 g), EDC (0.25 g), and NHS (0.15 g) were added sequentially, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 8 h at room temperature in the dark. The resulting solution was dialyzed against deionized water (MWCO 3.5 kDa) for 3 days and lyophilized to obtain CS–GA as a pale-yellow powder.

Successful conjugation of ADH and GA onto CS was confirmed by FTIR and 1H NMR spectroscopy.

Fabrication of composite hydrogel microspheres

Hydrogel precursor solutions were prepared by mixing HAMA and CS–GA with or without BPNS or BPNS–ZnO. The mixture was processed through a gas-shearing microfluidic device under nitrogen protection to generate monodisperse droplets, followed by UV irradiation (405 nm) to induce in situ photocrosslinking. Three formulations were prepared: pristine hydrogel microspheres (HM), BPNS-loaded microspheres (HM@BP), and BPNS–ZnO-loaded microspheres (HM@BPZn). In some characterization panels, BPNS, BPNS–ZnO, HAMA, and CS–GA were analyzed separately as building blocks or intermediate components to verify nanocomposite formation and polysaccharide modification. These component-level analyses were performed because techniques such as XPS, FTIR, and 1H NMR are more appropriate for identifying specific surface chemical states or precursor structures. In contrast, HM, HM@BP, and HM@BPZn refer to the final hydrogel microsphere formulations used for microsphere-level characterization and biological evaluation.

Characterization of BPNS–ZnO nanocomposites

The morphology of BPNS and BPNS–ZnO nanocomposites was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Briefly, diluted nanoparticle suspensions were dropped onto copper grids, dried at room temperature, and imaged under TEM. The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of BPNS, BPNS-PDA, and BPNS–ZnO were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and electrophoretic light scattering, respectively. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed to analyze the surface elemental composition and chemical states of BPNS and BPNS–ZnO. Survey spectra and high-resolution C 1s, O 1s, Zn 2p, and P 2p spectra were collected and fitted after background subtraction.

Structural characterization of hydrogel microspheres

The chemical structures of HAMA, CS-ADH, and CS-GA were confirmed by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H NMR). Lyophilized samples were used for FTIR analysis, and samples dissolved in deuterated solvent were used for 1H NMR measurements. The morphology of HM, HM@BP, and HM@BPZn microspheres was observed by optical microscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). For SEM observation, microspheres were freeze-dried, mounted on conductive adhesive tape, sputter-coated with gold, and imaged under SEM.

Particle size analysis

The size distribution of HM, HM@BP, and HM@BPZn microspheres was analyzed from optical microscopy images using ImageJ software. At least 50 microspheres from each group were randomly selected for diameter measurement. The average particle size and size distribution were calculated and plotted.

Injectability and shape recovery evaluation

The injectability of HM@BPZn microspheres was evaluated using a commercial syringe. Microsphere suspensions were loaded into the syringe and manually injected through the needle to assess smooth extrusion and post-injection morphology. To evaluate shape recovery, individual microspheres were mechanically compressed and then released. The morphology of microspheres before compression, after compression, and after recovery was recorded by optical microscopy.

EDS mapping, line-scan, and XRD analysis

Elemental distribution was analyzed by SEM equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). BPNS and HM@BPZn samples were freeze-dried, fixed on conductive adhesive tape, and subjected to elemental mapping for P, Zn, O, and N. EDS line-scan analysis was further performed across selected regions to compare elemental intensity profiles. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to characterize the crystalline structures of BPNS and HM@BPZn. Dried samples were scanned over the selected 2θ range, and the characteristic diffraction peaks of BPNS and ZnO were identified.

Zn2+ release assay

HM@BPZn microspheres were incubated in different media, including PBS at pH 7.4, pH 6.5, pH 5.5, and OA-simulated synovial fluid. The OA-simulated synovial fluid was prepared using PBS supplemented with hyaluronidase and H2O2, with the pH adjusted to 6.5. At predetermined time points, the supernatant was collected and replaced with fresh medium. The Zn2+ concentration in the collected supernatant was measured by ICP-OES. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Rheological characterization

The rheological properties of HM, HM@BP, and HM@BPZn hydrogels were measured using a rotational rheometer. Frequency sweep tests were performed within the linear viscoelastic region to record the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″). Strain sweep tests were conducted to evaluate the strain-dependent viscoelastic behavior and determine the stability of the hydrogel network. Complex viscosity was also recorded as a function of frequency to assess the shear-thinning behavior of different hydrogel formulations.

Compression test

The mechanical properties of HM, HM@BP, and HM@BPZn hydrogels were evaluated by unconfined compression tests. Cylindrical hydrogel samples were compressed at a constant rate, and stress–strain curves were recorded. The elastic modulus was calculated from the initial linear region of the stress–strain curve. At least three samples were tested for each group.

Swelling assay

Lyophilized HM, HM@BP, and HM@BPZn microspheres were weighed and recorded as the initial dry weight. The samples were immersed in PBS at 37 °C. At predetermined time points, microspheres were removed, gently blotted to remove excess surface water, and weighed. The swelling ratio was calculated according to the weight change relative to the initial dry weight.

In vitro degradation assay

The degradation behavior of HM, HM@BP, and HM@BPZn microspheres was evaluated under different conditions. Lyophilized microspheres were weighed as the initial dry weight W0 and incubated in PBS at pH 7.4, pH 6.5, pH 5.5, or OA-simulated synovial fluid at 37 °C. At predetermined time points, the remaining microspheres were collected, washed, lyophilized, and weighed as Wt. The mass loss ratio was calculated as: Mass loss ratio (%) = (W0 − Wt)/W0 × 100%. For morphological observation during degradation, HM@BPZn microspheres were collected at days 3, 7, 10, and 14, freeze-dried, sputter-coated with gold, and observed by SEM. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cellular assays for immunoregulatory activity and NF-κB/ROS mechanism

Cell culture

Murine macrophages (RAW 264.7) were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM, high glucose) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Chondrocytes (primary murine articular chondrocytes) were maintained in DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin under the same conditions.

Preparation of nanomaterial working solutions and cell treatment

BP-based formulations (HM, HM@BP, and HM@BPZn) were sterilized by UV irradiation (30 min each side) and dispersed in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by brief sonication to obtain homogeneous working suspensions. For in vitro experiments, cells were treated with negative control (NC, Physiological saline) or nanomaterials at 50 μg mL−1. For inflammatory induction in chondrocytes, recombinant murine interleukin-1β (IL-1β) was applied at 10 ng mL−1 for 24 h to establish an OA-like inflammatory phenotype, followed by co-treatment or post-treatment with the indicated formulations for the specified durations.

Immunofluorescence staining of CD86/CD206 in RAW 264.7 macrophages

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded on sterile glass coverslips in 24-well plates (≈5 × 104 cells per well) and allowed to attach overnight. To model inflammatory activation, cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng mL−1) for 24 h, then treated with NC or BP-based formulations for 24 h.

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (10 min), and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, 1 h). Samples were incubated with anti-CD206 primary antibody (typical 1:200 dilution) overnight at 4 °C, followed by fluorescent secondary antibody (1:500, 1 h, room temperature). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (5 min). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope under identical exposure settings. CD206 signal was quantified as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) using ImageJ/Fiji from ≥5 randomly selected fields per group.

Chondrocyte inflammation model and RT–qPCR analysis

Chondrocytes were seeded in 6-well plates (≈2–3 × 105 cells per well). After reaching ∼70–80% confluence, cells were stimulated with IL-1β (10 ng mL−1, 24 h) and subsequently treated with NC or nanomaterials for 4 h.

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (or equivalent), and cDNA was synthesized using a commercial reverse transcription kit following the manufacturer's protocol. Quantitative PCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix on a real-time PCR system. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH (or ACTB) and calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis of NF-κB signaling in chondrocytes

After treatments, chondrocytes were washed with cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes.

Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against p-p65, total p65, p-IκBα, total IκBα, and β-actin/GAPDH. After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1 h, room temperature), bands were visualized using ECL reagents. Densitometry was performed in ImageJ. NF-κB activation was expressed as phosphorylation ratios (e.g., p-p65/p65 and p-IκBα/IκBα).

Intracellular ROS measurement in chondrocytes (DCFH-DA)

Chondrocytes were treated as described above. Intracellular ROS levels were measured using DCFH-DA. Briefly, cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (10 μM) for 20–30 min at 37 °C in the dark, washed three times with PBS, and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry (or quantified by fluorescence microplate reader, if used). ROS levels were reported as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) and normalized to the NC group.

Immunofluorescence of p65 nuclear translocation in chondrocytes

Chondrocytes grown on coverslips were subjected to IL-1β stimulation and subsequent treatments. Cells were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde, 15 min), permeabilized (0.1% Triton X-100, 10 min), and blocked (5% BSA, 1 h). Samples were incubated with anti-p65 primary antibody (typical 1:200) overnight at 4 °C, followed by fluorescent secondary antibody (1:500, 1 h). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Images were collected under identical settings. p65 nuclear translocation was quantified by calculating nuclear fluorescence intensity or the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic (N/C) fluorescence ratio using ImageJ/Fiji.

Cytocompatibility assays in chondrocytes (CCK-8 and live/dead)

For CCK-8 assay, chondrocytes were seeded in 96-well plates (≈5 × 103 cells per well) and treated with NC or formulations for 48 h and 72 h. CCK-8 reagent (10% v/v) was added and incubated for 1–2 h, and absorbance was read at 450 nm. Cell viability was calculated as a percentage of NC.

For live/dead staining, cells were stained using a commercial kit containing calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) (or equivalent dyes) at 24 h and 72 h post-treatment. Images were acquired by fluorescence microscopy and representative fields were recorded.

In vitro investigation of Nrf2 pathway activation coupled with anti-catabolic cartilage protection

Immunofluorescence staining of HO-1 and Nrf2

The inflammatory model and treatment were performed as described above. Chondrocytes were seeded on sterile coverslips in 24-well plates (≈5 × 104 cells per well). After treatments, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (10 min), and blocked with 5% BSA (1 h). Samples were incubated with primary antibodies against HO-1 (typical 1:200) or Nrf2 (typical 1:200) at 4 °C overnight, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:500, 1 h, room temperature, dark). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (5 min). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope under identical acquisition settings across groups.

Quantification: HO-1 signal was quantified as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) using ImageJ/Fiji from ≥5 random fields per group. Nrf2 nuclear translocation was quantified by measuring nuclear Nrf2 intensity within DAPI-defined ROIs.

RT–qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a commercial kit following the manufacturer's protocol. RT–qPCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix on a real-time PCR system. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, normalized to GAPDH (or ACTB). The gene panel included catabolic genes (MMP13, ADAMTS5), anabolic/cartilage markers (COL-II, ACAN, PRG4), and antioxidant/Nrf2-related genes (Nrf2, HMOX1, NQO1). Primer sequences were provided in Table S1.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay and equal amounts of protein (typically 20–30 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA (recommended for signaling proteins) and incubated with primary antibodies against PRG4, Nrf2, HO-1, NQO1, GCLC, GCLM, KEAP1, MMP-13, MMP-3, ADAMTS-5, COL-II, Aggrecan, SOX-9, and β-actin (typical 1:1000 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1 h, room temperature), bands were visualized using ECL and imaged with a chemiluminescence system. Densitometry was performed in ImageJ and normalized to β-actin; results were displayed as heatmaps.

Transcriptomic and protein-level mechanistic validation of NF-κB suppression and Nrf2 activation

IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes were divided into NC and HM@BPZn groups (n = 3). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol, and samples with qualified RNA integrity were used for library construction. Poly(A) + RNA libraries were prepared and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome, and gene expression was quantified. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and FDR <0.05. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to identify the biological processes and pathways associated with the DEGs. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database and visualized in Cytoscape. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was further performed to assess coordinated alterations in NF-κB- and Nrf2-related signaling pathways. The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject accession number PRJNA1472427.

For protein-level validation, total proteins were extracted from chondrocytes using RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against p-p65, p65, p-IκBα, IκBα, iNOS, COX-2, Nrf2, HO-1, NQO1, COL-II, and β-actin, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized by ECL and quantified by ImageJ, with target protein levels normalized to β-actin.

In vivo therapeutic evaluation in an ACLT-induced osteoarthritis model

Animals and ethics

Healthy adult male Sprague–Dawley rats (200–250 g) were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 8 per group). All procedures were approved by the Zhejiang University Animal Care and Use Committee. Animals were housed under standard conditions with free access to food and water and acclimatized for 1 week prior to surgery.

ACLT surgery to induce osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis was induced by anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT). Briefly, animals were anesthetized, the knee joint capsule was opened through a medial parapatellar approach, and the ACL was transected under direct visualization. Joint instability was confirmed by an anterior drawer test. The capsule and skin were closed in layers, and animals received routine postoperative analgesia. Sham controls (if included) underwent arthrotomy without ACL transection.

Intra-articular injection and treatment regimen

BPNS-based hydrogel microsphere formulations were prepared as sterile suspensions in PBS before intra-articular administration. One week after ACLT surgery, rats were randomly assigned to four groups: NC, HM, HM@BP, and HM@BPZn. The NC group received sterile PBS and served as the ACLT-induced OA model control, while the treatment groups received the corresponding hydrogel microsphere suspensions.

Intra-articular injections were performed into the operated knee joint at weeks 1 and 2 after surgery using a microsyringe equipped with a fine-gauge needle. For each injection, 50 μL of PBS or microsphere suspension was administered into the joint cavity. The microsphere suspension was prepared at a concentration of 10 mg mL−1, corresponding to 0.5 mg microspheres per joint per injection. Thus, each rat received two intra-articular injections, with a total microsphere dose of 1.0 mg per joint over the treatment period.

Animals were monitored daily for gait abnormality, joint swelling, wound condition, and signs of distress. Histological scoring and quantitative image analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the group allocation. Animals were excluded from analysis if severe postoperative infection, failed ACLT surgery, failed intra-articular injection, abnormal gait unrelated to OA modeling, or unexpected death occurred during the experimental period.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and quantitative bone analysis

At week 6, knee joints were harvested and scanned by micro-CT. A consistent region of interest encompassing the subchondral/trabecular bone was selected for all samples. Standard morphometric parameters were calculated using manufacturer software, including Sb.Th, Tb.Th, BV/TV, and osteophyte volume, following established protocols.

Histological processing and staining

After micro-CT, joints were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (48 h), decalcified in 10% EDTA (pH 7.2–7.4) for ∼4 weeks, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Serial sagittal sections (5 μm) were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for overall morphology, Masson's trichrome for collagen distribution/organization, and Safranin O/Fast Green for proteoglycan content. In parallel, liver and kidney tissues were collected at the endpoint for systemic biosafety evaluation. The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned into 5 μm slices, and subjected to H&E staining to assess potential histopathological abnormalities.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded joint sections were subjected to immunohistochemical (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining to assess inflammatory markers (IL-6, COX-2, p65), antioxidant-related proteins (Nrf2, FOXO3a), and cartilage metabolism-associated markers (ADAMTS-5, MMP-13, PRG4). After deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, sections were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the corresponding primary antibodies. For IHC, sections were further incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, visualized using DAB, and counterstained with hematoxylin. For IF, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired under identical settings for all groups. Quantitative analysis was performed in a blinded manner using ImageJ, with IHC results expressed as the percentage of positive cells and IF results as mean fluorescence intensity. For each animal, at least three sections and three to five fields within the weight-bearing region of cartilage were analyzed, and the averaged value was used as one biological replicate.

Statistical analysis

Experiments were performed with ≥3 independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism, with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results and discussions

Synthesis and physicochemical characterization of BPNS–ZnO hybrids

The fabrication of BPNS–ZnO hybrid nanosheets is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1a. Bulk black phosphorus crystals were exfoliated via liquid-phase sonication in NMP to obtain few-layer BPNS, followed by surface functionalization and electrostatic assembly with ZnO nanoparticles. This strategy enables intimate interfacial contact between BPNS and ZnO, which is essential for subsequent electronic coupling and ion-release regulation.

Electronically coupled BPNS–ZnO hybrid nanosheets: fabrication and physicochemical characterization.

(A) Schematic of the preparation of BPNS–ZnO hybrids through exfoliation of bulk black phosphorus, PDA coating, and subsequent ZnO nanoparticle immobilization. (B) Representative TEM images of BPNS and BPNS–ZnO. Scale bars as indicated. (C,D) DLS size distribution and zeta potential of BPNS and BPNS–ZnO. (E–I) XPS characterization of BPNS and BPNS–ZnO, including survey spectra (E) and high-resolution C 1s (F), O 1s (G), Zn 2p (H), and P 2p (I) spectra, confirming successful hybrid formation and altered interfacial chemical states. (J) Zn2+ release profiles of HM@BPZn microspheres under different microenvironmental conditions, including pH 7.4, pH 6.5, pH 5.5, and OA-simulated synovial fluid. (K–M) Particle size distributions of HM, HM@BP, and HM@BPZn microspheres, respectively. Data are presented as mean ± SD.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images demonstrate that pristine BPNS exhibit a typical lamellar morphology with lateral dimensions of approximately 100 nm (Fig. 1b). High-magnification images reveal thin, translucent nanosheets with clear layer contrast, confirming successful exfoliation. After ZnO loading, BPNS–ZnO display a markedly different surface morphology, with numerous dark nanoparticulate features uniformly distributed on the BPNS surface (Fig. 1b, right). These nanoparticles are consistent with ZnO in size and contrast, indicating successful decoration without obvious aggregation.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis further supports the formation of hybrid nanostructures. As shown in Fig. 1c, BPNS exhibit a relatively narrow size distribution, while BPNS–ZnO display a clear increase in hydrodynamic diameter, consistent with surface loading of ZnO nanoparticles. Zeta potential measurements (Fig. 1d) show a pronounced change in surface charge after hybrid formation, reflecting surface chemistry alteration and providing additional evidence of successful ZnO immobilization.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectra confirm the elemental composition of the hybrids (Fig. 1e). In addition to the characteristic C 1s and O 1s signals, BPNS–ZnO display distinct Zn 2p doublets, which are absent in pristine BPNS, directly verifying the presence of Zn-containing species. High-resolution C 1s spectra (Fig. 1f) can be deconvoluted into components corresponding to C–C/C=C, C–O, and C=O bonds, indicating the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups at the BPNS surface, which facilitate interfacial interactions with ZnO.

The O 1s spectra (Fig. 1g) provide further insight into the chemical environment of oxygen. Compared withHM, BPNS–ZnO exhibit an additional component attributable to Zn–O bonds, alongside P–O and organic oxygen species, confirming the coexistence of ZnO lattice oxygen and oxidized BP-related oxygen species. The Zn 2p high-resolution spectrum (Fig. 1h) shows two well-defined peaks assigned to Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2, consistent with Zn in the +2 oxidation state, characteristic of ZnO.

Importantly, high-resolution P 2p spectra (Fig. 1i) reveal notable changes in phosphorus chemical states after ZnO hybridization. While pristine BPNS predominantly exhibit P0-related doublets, BPNS–ZnO display an increased contribution from P–O (P3+-related) components. This shift suggests partial oxidation of BPNS and interfacial electron redistribution, consistent with electron transfer from BPNS to ZnO. Such electronic coupling is expected to modulate Zn2+ release behavior and underpins the subsequent functional performance of the hybrid system.

Accordingly, the Zn2+ release behavior of HM@BPZn microspheres was further evaluated under different microenvironmental conditions (Fig. 1J). HM@BPZn exhibited a sustained Zn2+ release profile without obvious burst release, indicating that ZnO was effectively retained within the BPNS–ZnO/hydrogel microsphere network and released in a controlled manner. Notably, Zn2+ release was accelerated under acidic and OA-mimicking synovial-fluid conditions compared with physiological pH 7.4, suggesting a microenvironment-responsive ion release behavior. Such controlled and condition-sensitive Zn2+ release may be beneficial for osteoarthritis therapy by providing prolonged local bioactivity while reducing the risk of excessive ion exposure.

In parallel, the size distribution of the hydrogel microspheres was analyzed to evaluate the reproducibility and stability of the microfluidic fabrication process (Fig. 1K–M). HM, HM@BP, and HM@BPZn microspheres exhibited comparable and relatively narrow size distributions, with average diameters of 229.27 ± 32.25 μm, 230.52 ± 34.70 μm, and 235.32 ± 31.51 μm, respectively. The negligible difference in microsphere size among the three formulations indicates that incorporation of BPNS or BPNS–ZnO nanocomposites did not disrupt droplet formation or photocrosslinking. This uniform microscale architecture is favorable for injectable intra-articular delivery, as microsphere size can affect injectability, joint retention, degradation kinetics, and local distribution. Together, these results demonstrate that HM@BPZn integrates a structurally stable BPNS–ZnO nanophase with controllable Zn2+ release and reproducible microsphere fabrication, providing a solid material basis for subsequent biological evaluation.

FTIR spectra provide direct evidence for successful chemical modification of the polysaccharides. As shown in Fig. 2c, HAMA exhibits new absorption features associated with methacrylate groups compared with pristine HA, while retaining the characteristic polysaccharide backbone signals, indicating that the HA structure remains intact after modification. Correspondingly, FTIR spectra of CS–GA (Fig. 2d) shows systematic changes in the amide-related regions following EDC/NHS-mediated conjugation, together with preserved sulfate-associated bands, confirming successful functionalization without degradation of the CS backbone.

1H NMR spectroscopy further confirms successful synthesis. In the 1H NMR spectrum of HAMA (Fig. 2e), distinct vinyl proton signals appear in the 5–6.5 ppm region, accompanied by a methyl signal near ∼1.9 ppm, which are absent in HA, confirming methacrylation. For CS–GA (Fig. 2f), new aromatic proton resonances in the ∼6.8–7.2 ppm range are clearly observed, providing definitive evidence for gallic acid grafting onto CS.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal the microstructure of the hydrogel microspheres. Pristine hydrogel microspheres (HM) exhibit a porous, interconnected internal architecture (Fig. 2g). Incorporation of BPNS (HM@BP) or BPNS–ZnO (HM@BPZn) does not disrupt microsphere integrity but slightly thickens the pore walls, suggesting effective nanofiller–polymer interactions. Optical microscopy images (Fig. 2h) show highly uniform microspheres with narrow size distribution, consistent with controlled microfluidic fabrication.

Injectability and structural robustness are demonstrated in Fig. 2i, where the microspheres can be readily delivered through a standard syringe without fragmentation. Compression–recovery tests (Fig. 2j) further show that HM@BPZn microspheres undergo substantial deformation under compression yet recover their original shape immediately upon load removal, indicating excellent elasticity and mechanical resilience.

The elemental composition and crystalline structure of HM@BPZn were further investigated to confirm the successful integration of BPNS–ZnO nanocomposites within the hydrogel microsphere system. As shown in Fig. 2K, EDS elemental mapping of pristine BPNS mainly revealed a dominant P signal, whereas HM@BPZn exhibited clear and spatially overlapped P, Zn, and O distributions. The homogeneous co-localization of P and Zn indicates that Zn-containing components were successfully incorporated onto or around BPNS rather than forming completely separated aggregates. Meanwhile, the O signal was markedly enhanced in HM@BPZn, which is consistent with the presence of ZnO and the oxygen-rich polysaccharide hydrogel network. The detected N signal may be attributed to the chondroitin sulfate-derived hydrogel matrix and modified functional groups, further supporting the successful construction of the hybrid microsphere system.

To further validate the elemental enrichment, EDS line-scan analysis was performed across the nanocomposite region (Fig. 2L). Compared with BPNS, HM@BPZn displayed markedly higher Zn and O intensity profiles along the scanning path, confirming the successful loading of ZnO-containing components. The consistent increase in Zn and O signals across the analyzed region suggests that ZnO was not merely adsorbed as isolated particles but was effectively distributed within the BPNS-associated hybrid structure. This spatial elemental evidence supports the formation of a BPNS–ZnO-integrated functional phase, which is essential for subsequent controlled Zn2+ release and redox-regulating activity.

XRD analysis was further used to examine the crystalline features of BPNS and HM@BPZn (Fig. 2M). Pristine BPNS displayed characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to black phosphorus crystal planes, including the labeled (020), (040), and (060) reflections. After incorporation into HM@BPZn, additional diffraction peaks assigned to crystalline ZnO appeared, including the characteristic reflections of the wurtzite ZnO phase. The coexistence of BPNS-related and ZnO-related diffraction peaks in HM@BPZn demonstrates that the crystalline characteristics of BPNS were partially preserved while ZnO was successfully introduced. Together with the EDS mapping and line-scan results, these findings provide solid structural evidence for the successful construction of HM@BPZn microspheres, in which BPNS and ZnO are integrated into a hybrid nanophase within the polysaccharide hydrogel framework. This stable structural integration provides the material basis for the sustained Zn2+ release, antioxidant regulation, and cartilage-protective effects observed in subsequent experiments.

Strain-sweep tests (Fig. 3b) reveal that all formulations maintain stable viscoelastic behavior at low strains and undergo gradual yielding at higher strains, demonstrating good structural stability. The corresponding tan δ values (Fig. 3c,d) remain low under physiological strain ranges, further confirming the predominantly elastic nature of the hydrogels.

Stress–strain curves (Fig. 3e) indicate that HM@BP and HM@BPZn exhibit higher stress-bearing capacity compared with pristine HM, while preserving large deformability. Quantitative analysis of elastic modulus (Fig. 3f) shows a modest yet consistent increase upon nanocomposite incorporation, reflecting effective stress transfer between polymer networks and nanofillers.

All hydrogels display pronounced shear-thinning behavior, as evidenced by the decrease in complex viscosity with increasing frequency (Fig. 3g), enabling facile injectability. Swelling studies (Fig. 3h) show that all formulations rapidly reach equilibrium swelling, with HM@BPZn exhibiting slightly reduced swelling ratios, consistent with increased crosslinking density and nanofiller reinforcement.

Enzymatic degradation assays (Fig. 3i) reveal that HM@BPZn undergoes accelerated mass loss under hyaluronidase treatment and acidic conditions compared with PBS, indicating responsiveness to pathological microenvironments. This behavior is attributed to the combined effects of BPNS-mediated ROS scavenging and Zn2+-associated enzyme regulation, which collectively modulate matrix stability during degradation.

The degradation behavior of the hydrogel microspheres was further investigated to evaluate their structural stability and microenvironment-responsive dissociation profiles. SEM observation revealed a time-dependent morphological evolution of HM@BPZn microspheres during degradation (Fig. 3J). At day 3, the microspheres maintained an interconnected porous architecture with relatively intact internal networks. With prolonged incubation, the hydrogel framework gradually became loosened and partially collapsed, accompanied by enlarged pores and fragmented network structures at days 7 and 10. By day 14, the microspheres displayed evident structural erosion and network disintegration, indicating progressive degradation of the polysaccharide hydrogel matrix. This gradual morphological transformation suggests that HM@BPZn microspheres do not undergo abrupt structural breakdown but instead degrade in a controlled manner, which is favorable for sustained intra-articular retention and long-term therapeutic action.

Quantitative mass-loss analysis further confirmed the time-dependent degradation profiles of different microsphere formulations under various conditions (Fig. 3K–N). In simple buffer systems, all microspheres exhibited gradual mass loss over 28 days, indicating the intrinsic degradability of the dynamic polysaccharide hydrogel network. Under pH 7.4, HM@BPZn showed the highest mass-loss rate among the three formulations, reaching approximately 80% by day 28, whereas HM and HM@BP displayed relatively lower degradation levels. A similar trend was observed under mildly acidic conditions, including pH 6.5 and pH 5.5, although the overall degradation rate remained moderate in these buffer environments. The slightly enhanced degradation of HM@BPZn may be attributed to the incorporation of BPNS–ZnO nanocomposites, which could increase local network heterogeneity and facilitate water penetration and hydrolytic erosion within the microspheres.

Notably, degradation was markedly accelerated in OA-simulated synovial fluid (Fig. 3N). Compared with simple buffer conditions, all groups displayed faster and more extensive mass loss, with HM@BPZn approaching nearly complete degradation by the end of the observation period. This result indicates that the pathological OA-like microenvironment, containing enzymatic and inflammatory stimuli, can promote the breakdown of the hydrogel network. Such microenvironment-responsive degradation is particularly beneficial for intra-articular therapy, as the microspheres can maintain structural integrity under relatively mild conditions while undergoing accelerated degradation in OA-relevant environments, thereby facilitating the sustained release of therapeutic components. Together, the SEM morphology and mass-loss results demonstrate that HM@BPZn microspheres possess a controllable and OA-responsive degradation profile, providing a material basis for prolonged retention, sustained Zn2+ release, and continuous regulation of the osteoarthritic microenvironment.

Mechanistic implications of HM@BPZn-Mediated osteoarthritic microenvironment regulation

Osteoarthritis is characterized by excessive ROS accumulation, inflammatory activation, abnormal catabolic enzyme activity, and progressive extracellular matrix degradation. The HM@BPZn microsphere system was therefore designed to integrate antioxidant regulation, sustained Zn2+ release, and injectable hydrogel-based local retention within one platform. The XPS results showed altered P 2p chemical states after ZnO hybridization, suggesting partial surface oxidation of BPNS and interfacial interaction between BPNS and ZnO (Fig. 1I). However, this result should be interpreted as evidence of interfacial interaction rather than direct proof of directional electron transfer. Accordingly, we no longer describe this system as an electronically programmed or strictly sequential therapeutic platform.

The material behavior of HM@BPZn provides a reasonable basis for its biological function. HM@BPZn exhibited sustained Zn2+ release without obvious burst release, and Zn2+ liberation was accelerated under acidic and OA-simulated synovial-fluid conditions (Fig. 1J). Meanwhile, the microspheres showed gradual structural erosion and enhanced degradation in OA-simulated synovial fluid (Fig. 3J–N). These results suggest that HM@BPZn can act as a microenvironment-responsive local depot, allowing sustained material degradation and ion release under OA-relevant pathological conditions.

It should also be noted that ZnO nanoparticles may generate ROS under photoactivation or high-dose exposure [13]. However, the present HM@BPZn system exhibited an overall antioxidant effect under the tested OA-relevant conditions. This may be attributed to several factors. First, the intra-articular cavity is a relatively dark inflammatory niche, which may limit the classical photocatalytic ROS-generating behavior of ZnO. Second, ZnO was confined within the BPNS–ZnO microsphere network rather than directly exposed as free nanoparticles, reducing abrupt oxidative stimulation. Third, BPNS possesses intrinsic ROS-scavenging capability and may continuously buffer excessive oxidative stress during microsphere degradation. Therefore, we speculate that the net redox effect of HM@BPZn is dominated by BPNS-mediated antioxidant activity, hydrogel confinement of ZnO, and controlled Zn2+ release rather than ZnO-induced ROS generation.

Taken together, HM@BPZn should be regarded as a microenvironment-responsive and sustained-release hydrogel microsphere platform rather than a strictly temporally programmed system. The present data support the successful integration of BPNS–ZnO nanocomposites with a dynamic polysaccharide microsphere network and demonstrate their contribution to redox regulation, inflammatory suppression, and cartilage matrix protection. Nevertheless, direct evidence for time-resolved sequential regulation remains insufficient. Future studies incorporating time-dependent ROS scavenging, Zn2+/phosphate release profiling, MMP activity assays, in situ ion tracking, and direct characterization of BPNS–ZnO interfacial charge redistribution would further clarify the dynamic regulatory mechanism of this platform.

HM@BPZn reprograms macrophage inflammation by disrupting the ROS–NF-κB axis

Macrophages are central regulators of the arthritic microenvironment, orchestrating synovial inflammation through the production of cytokines, reactive oxygen species, and other catabolic mediators that drive cartilage degeneration and osteochondral remodeling [19]. Accordingly, reprogramming macrophages from a pro-inflammatory state toward a pro-resolving phenotype has emerged as a key strategy for interrupting the immune–cartilage crosstalk that sustains arthritis progression [20]. In Fig. 4, we establish a macrophage-level mechanistic bridge linking the interfacial-engineered HM@BPZn to joint-relevant immunoregulation, and the dataset is most informative when interpreted as a coupled ROS–NF-κB–phenotype axis rather than isolated marker changes. In Fig. 4A, immunofluorescence staining reveals a clear polarization shift after exposure to HM@BPZn: the M1-associated marker CD86 is visibly reduced, whereas the M2-associated marker CD206 is enhanced compared with NC and the HM/HM@BP controls. This trend is quantified in Fig. 4B, where the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) changes confirm that HM@BPZn pushes macrophages away from an inflammatory state and toward a pro-resolving phenotype. Importantly, this is not a superficial “marker swap.” The transcriptional program underlying inflammatory activation is simultaneously suppressed, as shown by RT–qPCR in Fig. 4C, where HM@BPZn significantly downregulates canonical pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-6, iNOS, and TNF-α). This coordinated decrease across cytokine (IL-6, TNF-α) and effector enzyme (iNOS) outputs indicates pathway-level reprogramming rather than selective inhibition of a single inflammatory product.

The ratios of p-p65/p65 and p-IκBα/IκBα are direct indicators of NF-κB pathway activation, a central inflammatory axis in arthritis that drives cytokine amplification, oxidative stress, and catabolic signaling within the joint. Their reduction suggests effective suppression of NF-κB–mediated inflammatory transcription, which is mechanistically relevant to limiting synovitis, protecting chondrocytes from catabolic reprogramming, and slowing cartilage degeneration. Mechanistically, Fig. 4D and E identifies the key signaling hub responsible for this state change. Western blotting demonstrates that HM@BPZn reduces phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα (i.e., decreased p-p65 and p-IκBα), and the heatmap/densitometric summary in Fig. 4E confirms a broad reduction in NF-κB activation metrics (e.g., lower p-p65/p65 and p-IκBα/IκBα). This matters because NF-κB is not simply one inflammatory pathway among many; it is a network “gain controller” that integrates upstream stress signals and drives a large fraction of the pro-inflammatory transcriptome, including IL-6 and iNOS. Therefore, the simultaneous suppression of NF-κB phosphorylation and inflammatory gene expression (Fig. 4C–E) supports a causal chain: HM@BPZn reduces NF-κB activation → lowers transcription of inflammatory effectors → enables phenotypic drift away from M1-like polarization.

ROS are key pathogenic drivers in arthritis, because excessive oxidative stress not only damages cellular macromolecules directly but also amplifies inflammatory signaling such as NF-κB, thereby sustaining synovitis and catabolic reprogramming of chondrocytes. Therefore, reducing intracellular ROS is mechanistically important for interrupting the inflammation–oxidative stress feedback loop, preserving cartilage homeostasis, and slowing joint degeneration. Intracellular ROS measured by DCFH-DA flow cytometry in Fig. 4H and I is markedly decreased in the HM@BPZn group, indicating that the treatment reduces oxidative burden rather than merely blocking downstream cytokine production. This upstream positioning is critical in inflammatory degeneration, because ROS and NF-κB often operate as a self-reinforcing positive feedback loop: ROS can activate NF-κB signaling, and NF-κB-driven enzymes/cytokines can further elevate oxidative and nitrosative stress (e.g., via iNOS). By simultaneously suppressing ROS (Fig. 4H and I) and NF-κB activation (Fig. 4D and E), HM@BPZn effectively “breaks” this feedback architecture, which is a stronger and more durable intervention than inhibiting a single cytokine endpoint. Consistent with this, flow cytometry polarization profiling in Fig. 4F and G shows a reduction in M1-like fractions and an increase in M2-like fractions, aligning population-level phenotype with molecular signaling and redox changes.

The nuclear translocation assay provides an additional layer of mechanistic specificity. In Fig. 4J–K, p65 immunostaining shows reduced nuclear accumulation after HM@BPZn treatment, and quantification confirms diminished nuclear p65 signal. Because nuclear translocation is the functional step that converts upstream phosphorylation events into transcriptional activation, this evidence strengthens the interpretation that HM@BPZn suppresses NF-κB at the level of transcriptional competence, not only at a proximal phosphorylation readout. In practical terms, this helps explain why multiple inflammatory genes are downregulated in parallel (Fig. 4C): less nuclear p65 means reduced promoter engagement across the NF-κB-dependent inflammatory regulon.

Cytocompatibility controls indicate that the immunoregulatory phenotype is not a byproduct of toxicity. In Fig. 4L and M, viability and live/dead (or equivalent) staining show that macrophages remain largely viable over 48–72 h across treatments, supporting that the observed reductions in inflammatory markers are due to genuine immune reprogramming rather than cell loss or metabolic collapse. This point is essential for an OA context, where a clinically meaningful strategy must attenuate inflammatory tone without inducing collateral cytotoxicity that could worsen tissue damage.

Collectively, Fig. 4 supports a unified model in which HM@BPZn acts as an upstream microenvironment modulator: it reduces oxidative stress (Fig. 4H and I), inhibits NF-κB activation and nuclear signaling (Fig. 4D–E, J–K), suppresses pro-inflammatory transcriptional outputs (Fig. 4C), and ultimately biases macrophage polarization toward a pro-resolving phenotype (Fig. 4A–B, F–G). In osteoarthritis, this macrophage reprogramming is not an isolated benefit; it is expected to reduce synovial cytokine and ROS pressure on resident joint cells, thereby lowering the inflammatory “set point” that drives chondrocyte catabolism and cartilage breakdown. In this way, Fig. 4 provides the immunological upstream rationale for the later chondroprotective and in vivo joint-preserving outcomes: by damping the macrophage-driven inflammatory amplifier, HM@BPZn can interrupt the synovium–cartilage crosstalk loop that sustains OA progression.

Nrf2-driven antioxidant activation restores chondrocyte homeostasis and suppresses matrix catabolism

Given that oxidative stress is a major driver of chondrocyte dysfunction and matrix degradation in osteoarthritis, restoring endogenous antioxidant defense is essential for re-establishing cartilage homeostasis beyond simple inflammatory suppression. Fig. 5 demonstrates that HM@BPZn not only suppresses inflammatory signaling but also actively reprograms the stress-response and matrix-maintenance circuitry of inflamed chondrocytes, centering on Nrf2-driven antioxidant activation and downstream restoration of cartilage homeostasis. Under IL-1β challenge, chondrocytes typically enter a catabolic state characterized by elevated ROS, impaired antioxidant defenses, and induction of matrix-degrading enzymes (e.g., MMPs and ADAMTS family members), which collectively drive proteoglycan and collagen loss [21]. In this context, the immunofluorescence results provide an early mechanistic entry point: HO-1 staining is markedly enhanced by HM@BPZn (Fig. 5A–C), and Nrf2 shows a pronounced redistribution toward the nucleus (Fig. 5B and C), indicating activation of the canonical Keap1–Nrf2 axis. Importantly, nuclear enrichment of Nrf2 is a functional marker of transcriptional competence, suggesting that HM@BPZn does not merely elevate antioxidant proteins through indirect effects but engages the upstream transcriptional switch that controls cellular redox capacity.

This Nrf2 activation is accompanied by a coordinated transcriptional rebalance of matrix metabolism. RT–qPCR data show that HM@BPZn suppresses key catabolic mediators, notably MMP13 and ADAMTS5 (Fig. 5C), which represent two central enzymatic checkpoints in OA: ADAMTS-5 drives aggrecan cleavage and early proteoglycan depletion, while MMP-13 is a major collagenase responsible for collagen II network breakdown and irreversible cartilage structural failure [22,23]. The simultaneous downregulation of both enzymes indicates that HM@BPZn intervenes upstream of catabolic transcriptional programming rather than blocking a single protease post hoc. In parallel, HM@BPZn restores anabolic/cartilage-maintenance genes including COL2A1 and HMOX1/HO-1 (Fig. 5D), and further elevates ACAN and PRG4 alongside Nrf2 pathway genes (NRF2, NQO1) (Fig. 5E). This pattern is mechanistically meaningful: COL2A1 and ACAN reflect recovery of core matrix synthesis capacity [24], whereas PRG4 is a functional boundary lubricant associated with superficial-zone integrity and low-friction articulation [25]. Because PRG4 is often suppressed by inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress [26], its restoration suggests that HM@BPZn can protect not only bulk matrix composition but also surface-lubrication homeostasis, a factor increasingly recognized as critical in slowing mechanically driven cartilage wear.

Protein-level validation strengthens this mechanism and reduces the likelihood that the observed gene changes are transient. Western blotting shows that HM@BPZn robustly restores PRG4 expression (Fig. 5F and G), consistent with the transcriptional trend and reinforcing the concept of lubrication-related functional rescue. Moreover, HM@BPZn activates the antioxidant axis at the protein level: Nrf2 and its downstream effectors HO-1, NQO1, and glutathione synthesis-related proteins (GCLC, GCLM) are increased, while KEAP1 is decreased (Fig. 5H and I). This protein signature is characteristic of a genuine Nrf2 program shift, where reduced Keap1-mediated repression allows stabilization and nuclear accumulation of Nrf2, leading to sustained antioxidant enzyme expression and enhanced glutathione capacity. Functionally, these changes would lower intracellular ROS and thereby reduce redox-dependent activation of inflammatory and catabolic transcription factors (including NF-κB), providing a mechanistic bridge between antioxidant activation and decreased matrix degradation.

Consistent with this upstream-downstream coupling, cartilage matrix metabolism proteins show a convergent shift: catabolic enzymes (MMP-13, MMP-3, ADAMTS-5) are reduced, whereas anabolic/phenotype markers (COL-II, Aggrecan, SOX-9) are restored (Fig. 5J and K). SOX-9 is particularly informative because it is a master transcriptional regulator of chondrocyte identity [27]; its recovery implies that HM@BPZn helps prevent inflammatory dedifferentiation and supports a more stable chondrocyte phenotype under cytokine stress. Taken together, these results position Nrf2 activation as more than an “antioxidant add-on”: it appears to be the organizing mechanism that enables a system-level transition from an IL-1β-induced catabolic state toward a protected, matrix-preserving state.

Integrating Fig. 5 with the macrophage and NF-κB results in earlier figures yields a coherent, multi-cellular OA mechanism. In macrophages, HM@BPZn reduces ROS and suppresses NF-κB signaling, promoting a pro-resolving polarization bias; this would reduce synovial cytokine pressure on chondrocytes. In chondrocytes, HM@BPZn engages Nrf2 to increase intrinsic redox resilience while simultaneously lowering catabolic gene induction and preserving matrix/lubrication markers. The combined outcome is interruption of the classical OA positive-feedback loop in which oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokines amplify NF-κB signaling, induce MMP/ADAMTS expression, degrade matrix, and further stimulate inflammatory activation. By coupling external immunomodulation (macrophage reprogramming) with intrinsic cytoprotection (Nrf2 activation in chondrocytes), HM@BPZn provides a mechanistically grounded explanation for the subsequent tissue-level protection observed in vivo.

Unlike single-marker readouts that can be dominated by one cytokine axis, RNA-seq captures coordinated pathway shifts that more faithfully represent the disease-relevant state transition. The volcano plot (Fig. 6A) reveals a broad transcriptional reprogramming induced by HM@BPZn, with substantial numbers of genes significantly up- and downregulated, indicating that the material does not merely dampen inflammation “at the endpoint” but resets upstream regulatory circuits. Functional enrichment analyses corroborate this interpretation: KEGG and GO outputs (Fig. 6B and C) prioritize immune/inflammatory signaling and stress-response processes as major axes of change, consistent with HM@BPZn acting on the core pathological drivers of arthritis-like inflammation rather than on secondary byproducts. Importantly, the heatmap of arthritis- and inflammation-associated genes (Fig. 6D) shows that HM@BPZn suppresses a coherent pro-inflammatory signature while restoring a more homeostatic expression pattern. This coordinated shift is notable because OA progression is sustained by network effects (multiple cytokines and mediators reinforcing each other), and successful intervention typically requires attenuation of the entire inflammatory program rather than a single node.

To further resolve how these DEGs organize into functional modules, we constructed a PPI network (Fig. 6E). The resulting topology is characterized by dense connectivity among inflammatory regulators, implying that the transcriptional changes induced by HM@BPZn converge onto a limited set of interdependent signaling hubs. Such a network structure is mechanistically meaningful: in inflammatory tissues, upstream nodes like NF-κB-related regulators and inducible enzymes (e.g., COX-2 and iNOS) amplify cytokine signaling and propagate tissue catabolism [28]. Therefore, a treatment that reduces activity at these hubs can generate disproportionate downstream benefit by collapsing the positive-feedback architecture that maintains chronic inflammation. In line with this, GSEA (Fig. 6F–H) provides pathway-level confirmation that HM@BPZn shifts the global signaling landscape. Arthritis-associated inflammatory pathways and NF-κB signaling are enriched in the NC condition, whereas HM@BPZn pushes enrichment toward cytoprotective programs, prominently including the Nrf2 pathway. This reciprocal relationship—NF-κB suppression coupled with Nrf2 activation—is mechanistically compelling because these two axes represent a canonical “damage-amplification versus defense-restoration” pair in inflammatory degeneration: NF-κB drives transcription of cytokines and inducible enzymes that perpetuate inflammation [29], while Nrf2 induces antioxidant and detoxification systems that re-establish redox homeostasis and raise the threshold for inflammatory activation [30].

We then validated these transcriptomic predictions at the protein level (Fig. 6I–L), focusing on key proximal markers that define pathway activity rather than distal readouts alone. Western blotting (Fig. 6I and J) demonstrates reduced phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα in HM@BPZn-treated samples, indicating a genuine decrease in NF-κB pathway activation. This is accompanied by downregulation of iNOS and COX-2, two inducible effector enzymes that are both hallmarks and functional mediators of inflammatory tissue damage [31]. iNOS contributes to nitrosative stress and can synergize with ROS to exacerbate biomolecular injury [32], while COX-2 promotes prostaglandin production that sustains pain, swelling, and catabolic signaling [33]. The simultaneous suppression of these effectors is particularly important because it suggests HM@BPZn acts upstream enough to reduce the “inflammatory output capacity” of the system, rather than simply neutralizing a single mediator after it has been produced. In parallel, HM@BPZn elevates Nrf2 and downstream antioxidant proteins (Fig. 6K and L), including HO-1 and NQO1, and restores the cartilage matrix marker COL-II. Together, these changes establish a mechanistic chain from signaling reprogramming (NF-κB↓, Nrf2↑) to functional phenotype preservation (COL-II↑), providing a direct bridge between molecular control and tissue-relevant outcomes.

When integrated with our earlier cellular datasets, Fig. 6 clarifies why HM@BPZn yields broad anti-inflammatory and chondroprotective effects rather than narrow, transient suppression. In macrophages, HM@BPZn reduced ROS and NF-κB activation while promoting an anti-inflammatory polarization bias, consistent with lowering the cytokine burden that initiates and sustains synovitis. In inflamed chondrocytes, HM@BPZn activated Nrf2 signaling while suppressing NF-κB-driven catabolic programs, thereby limiting induction of matrix-degrading enzymes and preserving anabolic/cartilage-maintenance pathways. The transcriptomic architecture in Fig. 6 supports this “dual-cellular targeting” logic: HM@BPZn does not rely on one cell type to carry the therapeutic effect; instead, it reprograms the shared inflammatory circuitry that couples immune cells and resident joint cells. This is critical in OA, where synovial inflammation and cartilage catabolism amplify each other: macrophage-derived cytokines activate chondrocyte NF-κB, chondrocytes respond with catabolic mediators and damage-associated signals, and these further stimulate synovial immune activation. By simultaneously weakening NF-κB-mediated amplification and strengthening Nrf2-centered defense, HM@BPZn is positioned to disrupt this feedback loop at multiple levels.

Conceptually, the most significant implication of Fig. 6 is that HM@BPZn shifts the joint-relevant inflammatory state from a self-sustaining “high-gain” regime into a buffered regime with improved redox resilience. Nrf2 activation does not merely counteract ROS; it raises the system's tolerance to inflammatory perturbations, making NF-κB reactivation less likely under recurring stress. This may help explain why HM@BPZn shows superior performance compared with BPNS or HM@BP controls: beyond providing antioxidant capacity, the BPNS–ZnO hybrid appears to couple redox control with signaling rewiring, achieving a more stable phenotypic reset. Overall, Fig. 6 provides an “omics-to-protein” validation pipeline demonstrating that HM@BPZn acts as a microenvironmental reprogrammer, coordinately suppressing NF-κB inflammatory signaling and engaging Nrf2-dependent cytoprotection, thereby establishing a mechanistically grounded basis for its downstream anti-inflammatory and tissue-preserving efficacy. It should be noted that the transcriptomic analysis in this study was performed in chondrocytes to identify cartilage-related redox, inflammatory, and ECM-protective pathways. Macrophage modulation was evaluated by targeted polarization and inflammatory assays rather than systems-level transcriptomics. Therefore, macrophage reprogramming should be interpreted as a validated immunomodulatory effect rather than an independently established transcriptomic mechanism. Future macrophage RNA-seq or single-cell analysis would further clarify the immune regulatory network involved in HM@BPZn-mediated osteoarthritis therapy.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy in an ACLT-induced osteoarthritis model

Having established in vitro that the microsphere system attenuates inflammatory signaling and restores redox homeostasis in macrophages and chondrocytes, we next asked whether these cell-level effects could translate into structural protection in vivo. As outlined in Fig. 7A, an ACLT-induced OA model was used to mimic post-traumatic joint degeneration, followed by two intra-articular injections during the early disease stage and endpoint evaluation at week 6. Representative micro-CT images (Fig. 7B) revealed severe osteochondral deterioration in the NC group, characterized by irregular subchondral architecture and prominent osteophyte formation, whereas HM@BPZn-treated joints displayed a visibly preserved trabecular framework and reduced pathological bony outgrowth. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 7C) further confirmed this trend: Sb.Th and osteophyte volume were significantly reduced, while Tb.Th and BV/TV were markedly increased in the HM@BPZn group compared with NC and the other treatment groups. These changes indicate that HM@BPZn effectively attenuated OA-associated subchondral sclerosis and aberrant bone remodeling while preserving a more favorable trabecular structure. Because pathological remodeling of the osteochondral unit is closely coupled to inflammatory stress and cartilage breakdown, the normalization of these micro-CT parameters suggests that the material exerts protective effects beyond cartilage alone and instead re-establishes a more balanced whole-joint microenvironment.

Histological findings were fully consistent with the imaging data. In the NC group, H&E staining showed obvious cartilage surface roughening, structural disorganization, and focal erosion; Masson's trichrome revealed disturbed collagen distribution; and Safranin O staining demonstrated substantial loss of proteoglycans, collectively reflecting progressive cartilage degeneration (Fig. 7E). By contrast, joints treated with HM@BPZn exhibited a smoother articular surface, more regular cartilage architecture, and markedly stronger matrix staining, indicating preservation of both collagen organization and proteoglycan content. These histological improvements were supported by the semiquantitative scores in Fig. 7D, where both the OARSI score and synovitis score were significantly reduced in the HM@BPZn group. Importantly, the simultaneous reduction in synovitis and cartilage damage provides strong evidence that the treatment interrupts the pathological crosstalk between inflamed synovium and degenerating cartilage. This is mechanistically coherent with the preceding cellular results: at the upstream level, macrophage reprogramming reduced the inflammatory burden imposed on the joint, while at the resident-cell level, antioxidant activation and suppression of catabolic signaling enhanced chondrocyte resilience. Therefore, the in vivo efficacy observed here should not be interpreted as a simple symptomatic benefit, but rather as the tissue-level consequence of coordinated immuno–redox regulation. In addition to the local therapeutic effects in the knee joint, preliminary systemic biosafety was evaluated by H&E staining of major organs. No obvious pathological abnormalities, inflammatory infiltration, tissue necrosis, or structural damage were observed in the liver or kidney sections after HM@BPZn treatment, suggesting favorable short-term biocompatibility within the experimental period. Collectively, Fig. 7 establishes that HM@BPZn confers multi-compartment protection in OA, spanning synovial inflammation, cartilage matrix preservation, and subchondral bone remodeling, and thereby provides a crucial bridge from mechanistic cell studies to molecular validation of disease-modifying joint rescue in the subsequent analyses.

Molecular validation of Synovium–Cartilage niche reprogramming in vivo

Having established in the cell studies that the microsphere system suppresses macrophage inflammatory activation while restoring antioxidant and matrix-preserving programs in chondrocytes, we next evaluated whether the same regulatory framework operates in vivo within the osteoarthritic joint. As shown in Fig. 8A, immunohistochemical staining revealed that IL-6, p65, and COX-2 were strongly elevated in the NC group, whereas their expression was progressively reduced after treatment and reached the lowest levels in the HM@BPZn group. In contrast, FOXO3a staining was markedly enhanced in HM@BPZn-treated joints, and this trend was confirmed quantitatively in Fig. 8B. These molecular changes are highly informative because they do not simply indicate a general attenuation of inflammation; rather, they point to a coordinated suppression of an upstream inflammatory amplifier network. IL-6 is a pivotal cytokine that links synovial immune activation with chondrocyte catabolic reprogramming [34], p65 represents the active transcriptional arm of the NF-κB pathway [35], and COX-2 is a major downstream effector that sustains prostaglandin-associated inflammatory output and pain signaling [36]. Their simultaneous downregulation therefore suggests that the treatment reduces the inflammatory “gain” of the joint microenvironment at the level of signal propagation, rather than merely neutralizing a single effector molecule. This interpretation directly echoes the in vitro macrophage findings, where inhibition of the ROS–NF-κB axis reduced inflammatory outputs and shifted macrophages away from an M1-like state. In the tissue context, such suppression is particularly important because synovial macrophages act as upstream organizers of OA progression: once activated, they continuously release cytokines and oxidative mediators that reinforce chondrocyte stress, matrix catabolism, and osteochondral remodeling [37]. Accordingly, the reduction in IL-6/p65/COX-2 in vivo can be viewed as a tissue-level signature that the macrophage-driven inflammatory engine identified in vitro has been effectively restrained.

Equally important, the increase in FOXO3a adds a second mechanistic dimension by indicating that the treatment does not simply silence destructive signals, but also re-engages endogenous stress-adaptive programs within the joint. FOXO3a is a stress-responsive transcription factor closely associated with antioxidant defense, mitochondrial fitness, autophagy-associated quality control, and long-term cellular resilience [38]. In OA, where chondrocytes are chronically exposed to inflammatory cytokines, ROS, and abnormal biomechanical stress, impaired FOXO3a activity contributes to reduced stress tolerance and accelerated phenotypic destabilization [39]. Therefore, the increased FOXO3a signal in HM@BPZn-treated joints suggests that the tissue is being shifted from a high-gain inflammatory state toward a more buffered, adaptive state capable of tolerating stress without entering a self-amplifying degradative program. This is mechanistically aligned with the in vitro chondrocyte results, where antioxidant defense was restored under inflammatory challenge. In this sense, the IHC results reveal a dual-level tissue response: inflammatory amplification is suppressed, while cytoprotective competence is restored. Such dual regulation is particularly important in OA because blocking inflammation alone may transiently reduce symptoms, but restoring stress adaptation is what enables sustained maintenance of cartilage cell function under persistent pathological loading.

The immunofluorescence data further extend this interpretation by showing how this upstream pathway reset is translated into cartilage-specific metabolic and functional outcomes. As shown in Fig. 8C, Nrf2 fluorescence was markedly elevated in the HM@BPZn group, whereas ADAMTS-5 was substantially reduced; these changes were quantitatively confirmed in Fig. 8E. Similarly, Fig. 8D shows that PRG4 (lubricin) was restored whereas MMP-13 was strongly diminished, again with the same trends supported by fluorescence quantification in Fig. 8E. These findings are particularly powerful because they bridge intracellular stress signaling with extracellular matrix fate. Nrf2 activation indicates re-engagement of endogenous redox defense within cartilage, thereby increasing the capacity of chondrocytes to buffer oxidative stress and resist inflammatory reprogramming. ADAMTS-5 and MMP-13, in contrast, define the two principal degradative routes of OA progression: ADAMTS-5 drives aggrecan cleavage and early proteoglycan depletion, while MMP-13 mediates collagen network destruction and irreversible structural destabilization [40]. Their simultaneous suppression therefore suggests that the treatment interrupts both the early and late phases of matrix catabolism. At the same time, recovery of PRG4 is functionally significant because PRG4 is not merely a marker of cartilage preservation; it is a key boundary lubricant that reduces frictional shear at the cartilage surface [41]. In OA, loss of PRG4 accelerates surface wear, which in turn generates additional damage-associated signals and amplifies synovial inflammation [42]. Thus, restored PRG4 implies that the treatment not only preserves biochemical matrix composition but also re-establishes a functional lubrication interface that can reduce mechanically driven progression of disease.

When integrated with the earlier cell experiments, Fig. 8A–E provides the critical molecular bridge from in vitro mechanism to in vivo joint rescue. In macrophages, the material suppresses ROS-dependent NF-κB activation and reduces inflammatory output; in chondrocytes, it strengthens endogenous antioxidant defense and restrains catabolic conversion. In vivo, these two effects converge at the tissue level: inflammatory signaling is dampened in the synovium–cartilage niche, cytoprotective stress-adaptation pathways are reinforced, matrix-degrading enzymes are suppressed, and lubrication-associated protection is restored. This integrated response explains why the structural improvements observed in Fig. 7 are not limited to a single tissue compartment but instead involve coordinated benefit across synovium, cartilage, and subchondral bone. Therefore, the therapeutic effect is best understood not as a simple anti-inflammatory action, but as multi-level niche reprogramming that interrupts the positive feedback loop linking synovitis, oxidative stress, matrix degradation, lubrication failure, and osteochondral remodeling.

Despite these encouraging findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. Direct evidence for directional electron transfer and strictly sequential regulation is insufficient, and future Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM), electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), and density functional theory (DFT) analyses, together with time-dependent ROS, Zn2+/phosphate release, BPNS degradation, and MMP activity assays are needed. More quantitative material characterization, including AFM thickness, Zn loading efficiency, BPNS/ZnO ratio, and batch-to-batch analysis, would further strengthen the structure–function relationship. In addition, quantitative injectability, intra-articular retention tracking, sham controls, multi-time-point in vivo evaluation, macrophage transcriptomics, component-deconvolution controls, and long-term biosafety assessments should be performed in future studies.

Conclusions

In summary, this work presents the rational design and systematic evaluation of HM@BPZn-loaded polysaccharide hydrogel microspheres as an injectable platform for osteoarthritis therapy. The electronically coupled BPNS–ZnO interface enables effective microenvironment reprogramming by suppressing ROS–NF-κB-associated inflammatory signaling while activating Nrf2-mediated cytoprotective pathways, thereby promoting anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization and preserving chondrocyte homeostasis under inflammatory stress. In an ACLT-induced OA model, HM@BPZn microspheres markedly alleviated synovitis and cartilage degeneration, preserved proteoglycan content, and attenuated pathological subchondral bone remodeling and osteophyte formation. Together with their injectability, mechanical resilience, and modular design, these microspheres provide a versatile platform for further integration of additional therapeutic cues, offering a promising strategy for next-generation disease-modifying intra-articular treatment of OA.
