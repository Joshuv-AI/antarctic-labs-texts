Beneath the Surface
Authors: Andres Enriquez, Fermin Garcia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

The human atria are not bidimensional chambers made of uniform myocardial fibers, but rather complex multilayered structures with overlapping bundles running in different directions across varying depths. This layered distribution creates zones of anisotropy, where conduction velocity differs depending on fiber orientation and also explains the dissociation between epicardial and endocardial atrial activation. Abrupt changes in fiber orientation, especially at anatomical junctions such as the pulmonary vein ostium, create heterogeneous conduction patterns that favor the development of local reentry and triggered activity, ultimately enhancing arrhythmogenic potential.

Key components of the atrial myocardial architecture include the septopulmonary bundle (SPB)1 and the Bachmann's bundle (BB),2 2 major epicardial tracts that create pathways of preferential conduction and facilitate rapid interatrial and posterior wall activation, respectively. In an outstanding contribution in this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Gonzalez-Casal et al3 provide a thorough anatomical and functional characterization of the SPB, supported by anatomical dissections, histological analyses, and electrophysiological data. A number of important properties of the SPB are described in the article and deserve to be highlighted. First, the SPB is not a discrete muscular structure but a diffuse region of myocardial fibers with a uniform alignment. Second, the fibers of the SPB are not insulated from the surrounding myocardium but are continuous and intertwined with the deeper subendocardial fibers. Finally, although data from systematic endoepicardial atrial mapping remain limited, there may be significant spatial and temporal dissociation between the endocardial and epicardial layers of the left atrial (LA) posterior wall, with important implications for arrhythmogenesis and ablation strategies.

As the authors mention, there are compelling embryological and mechanistic arguments to believe isolation of the posterior wall should have a positive impact on patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) when done in addition to pulmonary vein isolation.4, 5, 6 However, randomized data have been discouraging, and several trials have consistently failed to prove such a benefit. One of the key limitations to interpret these findings lies in the limited ability of endocardial ablation to reliably achieve transmural and durable posterior wall isolation. In the CAPLA trial, successful posterior wall isolation was achieved in only 86.5% of patients, half of patients required focal ablation within the posterior wall box, and most of the patients who required repeat ablation within 1 year of follow-up were found to have reconnection of the posterior wall.7 The incorporation of pulsed-field ablation (PFA) has streamlined the task of posterior wall isolation, with near-universal acute success while also mitigating the concerns for esophageal injury.8 However, because PFA has a variable component of reversible effect, the long-term durability of lesions remains an important concern.

The SPB is the main determinant of why isolation of the posterior wall can be challenging and explains several electrophysiological observations. Especially when using radiofrequency (RF) energy to create a posterior wall box lesion set, conduction frequently persists despite apparently complete endocardial lesions7,9 and mapping often reveals focal centrifugal patterns of activation consistent with epicardial connections. Even when entrance block is achieved, high-output pacing from the posterior wall may result in atrial capture in a significant proportion of patients, unmasking capture of epicardial fibers.10 Moreover, there is evidence that roof-dependent reentry may exist in the presence of endocardial posterior wall isolation,11 as proved by entrainment of the tachycardia from the “isolated” posterior wall using high-output pacing. Importantly, after spreading across the LA dome and posterior wall, the fibers of the SPB organize in fan-like expansions that encircle the antra of the pulmonary veins, forming a conduction network that can bypass endocardial ablation lines and thereby contributing to the challenges of achieving durable pulmonary vein isolation.

High-density mapping has fundamentally changed how we interpret activation of the LA posterior wall. The use of multipolar catheters with small electrodes and close interelectrode space may detect low-voltage fractionated electrograms in the posterior wall that can be easily missed by a 3.5-mm tip ablation catheter. Posterior wall electrograms often have sharp (nearfield, high-frequency) and dull (farfield, low-frequency) components that represent activation of the endocardial and epicardial layers, respectively.12 While ablating the earliest nearfield signals with RF may result in endocardial isolation, transmural isolation requires targeting also the farfield components and may necessitate extensive posterior wall ablation. With PFA, interpretation of electrograms after posterior wall isolation is more challenging due to acute electrical stunning. However, data reporting simultaneous endo and epicardial mapping after ablation of the posterior wall using a pentaspline PFA catheter show that a proportion of patients may exhibit areas of persistent voltage and myocardial capture in the epicardium, consistent with incomplete lesion transmurality.13

Some studies have proposed a role for epicardial mapping and ablation to treat atrial arrhythmias that are refractory to standard endocardial ablation, especially when persistent conduction across the SPB or BB is suspected.14,15 This can be achieved via percutaneous epicardial access or through a hybrid approach. In the percutaneous strategy, the posterior LA wall is better accessed with an inferior epicardial puncture and the catheter is navigated into the oblique sinus, a pericardial recess bounded on the right by the inferior vena cava and on the left by the pericardial reflection connecting the 2 left pulmonary veins. If epicardial ablation is performed, care should be paid to the proximity to the esophagus, sometimes requiring strategies for esophageal deviation or cooling. The hybrid approach, on the other hand, combines minimally invasive surgical access with endocardial mapping and ablation.16 In this case, access to the pericardial space is achieved through a subxiphoid window, and using endoscopic visualization, a unipolar RF catheter is used to make parallel rows of ablation lines across the posterior LA wall between the pulmonary veins. Nonetheless, the potential risks and morbidity of epicardial access or a surgical procedure should be carefully weighted against the anticipated benefits before pursuing these approaches.

In conclusion, epicardial connections such as the SPB have relevant implications for arrhythmogenesis and ablation strategies. Recognizing and addressing these epicardial connections represents an important step toward more durable and effective ablation. Despite the advantages of PFA, uncertainties remain about the transmurality and durability of lesions. The role of epicardial mapping and ablation for atrial arrhythmias in the era of PFA is still unclear.
