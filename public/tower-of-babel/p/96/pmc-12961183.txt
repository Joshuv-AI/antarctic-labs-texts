Tailored for swift recognition: A structural perspective on secondary active plant hormone transporters
Authors: Bjørn Lildal Amsinck, Ines Benhammouche, Adriana Chrenková, Alexander JD Snow, Bjørn Panyella Pedersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYPlant hormone transporters are essential for regulating phytohormone distribution at the cellular, tissue, and organ levels in plants. These proteins control hormone movement across membranes, shaping the spatial and temporal hormone gradients that underpin growth, development, and stress responses. This review surveys secondary active transporters implicated in the transport of auxin, cytokinin, abscisic acid, and gibberellins. We focus on five major transporter superfamilies: MFS, BART, APC, TOG, and DMT, highlighting their structural organization, transport mechanisms, and substrate specificity. Despite differences in substrate and regulation, members within each superfamily share conserved folds and mechanistic principles. Transport typically proceeds via alternating access models, including elevator, rocker‐switch, and rocking‐bundle mechanisms, which enable directional movement across chemically distinct environments. We show that structural classification offers an informative framework for predicting substrate interactions, energy coupling, and regulatory motifs, ultimately improving our understanding of hormone transport in plants. As more plant transporters are resolved structurally, this approach will be critical for uncovering how transport is coupled to energy sources and integrated into broader signaling networks.Keywords: plant hormone transport, membrane transporters, auxin, cytokinin, abscisic acid, gibberellin, transporter superfamilies, protein structure, alternating access mechanism

INTRODUCTION

Cell‐to‐cell coordination is fundamental to multicellular life. In plants, intercellular coordination is mediated by (phyto‐)hormones, which regulate development and environmental responses (Waadt et al., 2022; Yamaguchi et al., 2010). This coordination is vital for plants because most of their development is postembryonic. Plants must adapt to their environment within the constraints of fixed cell positions and a stationary existence. Central to hormone‐based cellular coordination is the movement of hormones across membranes, between cells, and ultimately throughout the plant body. Transmembrane transporters play a key role in this process, influencing hormone availability and spatial patterning in ways that impact everything from local signaling events to organ‐level morphogenesis (Zhang et al., 2023).

Plants produce at least ten chemically distinct classes of hormones: auxins, gibberellins (GA), cytokinins (CK), abscisic acid (ABA), ethylene, salicylates, jasmonates, brassinosteroids, peptide hormones, and strigolactones (Munné‐Bosch, 2025). Among these, auxins, cytokinins, abscisic acid, and gibberellins are well studied, and our understanding of transporter‐mediated distribution is beginning to take shape. CK, GA, ABA, and strigolactones are derived from isoprenoids. Auxins, ethylene, and peptide hormones are derived from amino acids. Finally, brassinosteroids are derivatives of sterols; salicylates come from shikimic acid, and jasmonates from fatty acids (Gasperini & Howe, 2024; Munné‐Bosch, 2025). Of note, five of these classes—auxins, gibberellins, abscisic acid, salicylates, and jasmonates—contain a carboxylic acid group that is negatively charged at cytosolic pH (pH ~7). In the slightly acidic apoplast (pH ~5), a significant fraction of these acidic molecules remains protonated and uncharged, allowing passive diffusion across membranes. Once inside the cytosol, they deprotonate and become trapped inside the cell; a process known as the ion‐trap mechanism (Goldsmith, 1977; Kramer, 2006; Raven, 1975; Rubery & Sheldrake, 1974). While the ion‐trap mechanism contributes to intracellular accumulation of some hormone classes, it is difficult to employ for directional transport and tissue‐level regulation. For this, plants rely on dedicated membrane transport proteins that mediate uptake, efflux, and subcellular compartmentalization (Hammes & Panyella Pedersen, 2024). Notably, several herbicides act as substrates or inhibitors of hormone transporters, underscoring their physiological relevance and potential vulnerability to chemical interference (Schulz et al., 2025).

To date, all structurally characterized hormone transporters operate via the alternating access model, well‐described from other transporters involved in metabolite and ion transport across all kingdoms of life. In the alternating access model, first envisioned by Oleg Jardetzky in 1966, a membrane‐buried substrate‐binding site is exposed to only one side of the membrane at a time, shifting between at least three conformational states: inward‐open, occluded, and outward‐open, allowing transport against the electrochemical gradient of the substrate and between chemically distinct environments (Andersen et al., 2023; Jardetzky, 1966). These environments include, for example, the apoplast, organelles, and the cytosol, with each differing in composition, including pH and oxidation. Effective transport requires not only substrate recognition but also coordinated conformational changes that can expose the substrate‐binding site to these distinct environments. As transporters cycle through inward‐open, occluded, and outward‐open states, their substrate‐binding affinities shift. Rather than a single dissociation constant (K D), each conformational state presents a distinct interaction profile defined by the 3‐dimensional structure of the protein in interplay with the chemical environment, contributing to functional asymmetry and ultimately enabling directional transport (Drew et al., 2021; Hammes & Panyella Pedersen, 2024; Ung, Schulz, Kleine‐Vehn, et al., 2023).

Despite significant advances in understanding the molecular underpinnings of transporter biology during the last 20 years, several key mechanistic questions remain largely unresolved for plant hormone transporters. How do transporters switch between high‐ and low‐affinity states? How is substrate movement coupled to energy sources such as ion gradients or the proton motive force? How selective are the transporters regarding chemical variants of hormone classes? These questions are central to understanding hormone transport at the molecular level and ultimately key to facilitating a physiological understanding of environmental plant responses (Boxes 1 and 2).

Phytohormone transport involves both primary and secondary active transporters. Primary active transporters move substrates against their electrochemical gradient and are driven by external input of energy, often in the form of ATP hydrolysis. In plants, primary active proton‐transporters are used to generate an electrochemical membrane gradient called the proton motive force (PMF, consisting of a membrane potential and a chemical pH gradient). Secondary active transporters use this PMF to transport hormones against their electrochemical gradient (Andersen et al., 2023). Depending on their coupling mechanism, they may function as symporters (co‐transporting protons and substrate), antiporters (exchanging substrate and protons), or uniporters (facilitating passive diffusion of substrate). Of note, uniporters, also known as facilitators, can still support directional transport against a chemical concentration gradient of a charged substrate, moving the substrate with the electrochemical gradient that is, in part, defined by the membrane potential.

Currently, the only primary transporters known to be involved in hormone transport are ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) proteins. These utilize ATP hydrolysis to drive transport of the hormone, and their molecular mechanism has been reviewed in detail and will not be considered further here (Alam & Locher, 2023; Locher, 2016; Sajid et al., 2023; Ying et al., 2024).

Multiple transporter superfamilies that mediate secondary active transport have been identified in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana. These include the Major Facilitator (MFS) Superfamily, the Bile/Arsenite/Riboflavin Transporter (BART) superfamily, the Amino Acid–Polyamine–Organocation (APC) superfamily, the Transporter‐Opsin‐G protein‐coupled receptor (TOG) superfamily, and the Drug/Metabolite Transporter (DMT) superfamily (Figure 1, Table 1) (Zhang et al., 2023). Since many plant transporters and transporter families have a number of different names, in Table 1 we include aliases as well as the Transporter Classification Database (TCDB) coding. The secondary transporter superfamilies listed in Table 1 all share one theme: They contain remnants of gene duplication events leading to at least two amino acid regions in their sequence that share high sequence homology between them (called pseudo‐repeats), with each repeat coding for 3–7 transmembrane helices (TM). The two repeats within each superfamily fold up to exhibit 3‐dimensional pseudosymmetry and depending on the number of transmembrane helices in the repeats being even or odd, the two pseudosymmetrical elements will have a parallel or antiparallel orientation with respect to the membrane (Figure 2) (Drew et al., 2021; Feng & Frommer, 2016; Jack et al., 2001; Vosolsobě et al., 2024; Wong et al., 2012). This has significant implications for transporter function and shape, and consequently the affinity and selectivity of the substrate‐binding site (Forrest et al., 2011). Structural domain organization is not necessarily linked to the two sequence repeats and varies across superfamilies. In some cases, each repeat does indeed form a distinct functional domain (e.g. MFS, TOG, DMT). In others, helices from both repeats contribute to composite domains (e.g. BART, APC). To complicate matters further, additional transmembrane helices may be present at the termini or between sequence repeats. These insertions sometimes serve regulatory roles, but mostly their specific functions remain unclear and differ both between superfamilies and between individual members of each superfamily (Forrest et al., 2011). Importantly, transport mode does not map onto superfamily classification. Symport, antiport, and uniport mechanisms are all found across the MFS, BART, APC, TOG, and DMT superfamilies, varying between individual transporter isoforms.

This review focuses on the secondary active transporters known to be involved in phytohormone homeostasis, with particular emphasis on the relationship between structure and transport function, highlighting how structural features influence substrate binding, energy coupling, and conformational transitions.

THE MAJOR FACILITATOR SUPERFAMILY

The MFS superfamily remains the biggest superfamily of secondary transporters in all kingdoms of life. 218 Major Facilitator Superfamily proteins have been identified in Arabidopsis thaliana and grouped into 22 protein families (Drew et al., 2021; Niño‐González et al., 2019). One way in which these 22 families differ is in their transport substrate specificity, for example, by transporting specific sugars or nucleotides, phosphate, or heavy metals, while other families appear more promiscuous (e.g., the Nitrate/Peptide Transporter family, members of which can transport both nitrate, peptides and phytohormones). Among the 22 families, hormone transport has so far been attributed to the Nitrate/Peptide Transporter (NPF) and the Equilibrative Nucleoside Transporter (ENT) families, but more families are likely to be included in the future (Figure 1, Table 1) (Korobova et al., 2021; Li et al., 2003; Michniewicz et al., 2019).

In the NPF family, the proteins NPF6.3 (aka NRT1.1, CHL1), NPF7.3 (aka NRT1.5), and NPF5.12 (aka TOB1) facilitate the transport of indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA), indole‐3‐butyric acid (IBA) and other auxin variants, all derivatives of tryptophan, which is structurally similar to the di‐ and tripeptides that several members of the family transport (Krouk et al., 2010; Michniewicz et al., 2019; Watanabe et al., 2020). In addition, NPF1.1, NPF1.2, NPF2.1, NPF2.3, NPF2.4, NPF2.5, NPF2.7, NPF2.9 (GTR3), NPF2.10 (GTR1), NPF2.11 (GTR2), NPF2.12, NPF2.13, NPF2.14, NPF3.1, NPF4.1 (AIT3), and NPF4.6 have been implicated in gibberellin transport (Binenbaum et al., 2023; David et al., 2016; Niño‐González et al., 2019; Saito et al., 2015; Tal et al., 2016; Wulff et al., 2019). Some NPF proteins are also suggested to transport ABA, such as NPF2.12, NPF2.14, NPF4.1 (AIT3), NPF4.2 (AIT4), NPF4.5 (AIT2), and NPF4.6 (AIT1) (Binenbaum et al., 2023; Kanno et al., 2012; Léran et al., 2020). Notably, the same proteins from the NPF family are also involved in nitrate transport, making the physiological function and relevance of the proteins for hormone transport difficult to untangle. ENT family members ENT3, ENT6, and ENT8 are proposed to mediate cytokinin transport, leveraging their structural similarity to nucleosides, the canonical substrates of this family (Hirose et al., 2008; Sun et al., 2005). Despite the large number of MFS proteins in plants, structural data remain limited, with only the sugar transporters SUC1, STP6, STP10, and the nitrate/auxin transporter NPF6.3 resolved to date (Andersen et al., 2025; Bavnhøj et al., 2023; Parker & Newstead, 2014; Paulsen et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2014).

The MFS fold consists of twelve transmembrane helices organized into two six‐helix bundles: the N domain (TM1–6) and the C domain (TM7–12), connected by an intracellular loop (Figure 2a,b). These domains exhibit pseudo‐twofold symmetry, which supports the alternating access mechanism. Each domain contains two three‐helix motifs (TM1–3 and TM4–6 in the N domain; TM7–9 and TM10–12 in the C domain), themselves related by internal inverted pseudosymmetry. The substrate‐binding site is located in a central cavity formed by TM1, TM4, TM7, and TM10, with each representing the first helix of the respective three‐helix motifs. Residues within these helices mediate substrate recognition, proton coupling, and interdomain interactions and are prime candidates for modulating MFS function, as illustrated by the methyl‐group shift that can swap glucose for fructose preference in STP10 versus STP6 (Andersen et al., 2025). Of note, ENTs are predicted to have only 11 transmembrane helices, which have been confirmed at least for the human ENT homologs (Wright & Lee, 2019).

MFS transporters operate via ‘the rocker‐switch mechanism’. This involves coordinated movement of the N and C domains to pivot around a central substrate‐binding site, alternately exposing it to either side of the membrane. Conformational transitions are driven by alternating closure of the binding cavity by these helices. In the outward‐facing state, TM4 and TM10 seal the cavity from the cytoplasm; in the inward‐facing state, TM1 and TM7 close it from the extracellular side (Drew et al., 2021). This gating mechanism is central to the rocker‐switch model. While broadly conserved, structural flexibility in helices such as TM1 or TM4, often due to glycine‐rich sequences, can introduce local variation in binding site dynamics, as observed in STP10 and the fungal phosphate transporter PiPT (Bavnhøj et al., 2021; Paulsen et al., 2019; Pedersen Panyella et al., 2013). Also worth noting is the fact that since the two sequence repeats are each associated with distinct structural domains, gene reshuffling is possible, meaning that the N domain of one MFS protein might be sequentially and functionally related to the C domain of another (Reddy et al., 2012).

An additional structural feature common to MFS transporters is the superfamily‐defining ‘A‐motif’, located between TM2–3 in the N domain and TM8–9 in the C domain. This motif contributes to salt bridge formation between domains, stabilizing both inward‐ and outward‐facing conformations and modulating affinities and turnover rates (Drew et al., 2021).

The plant nitrate/auxin transporter NPF6.3 has been described as a dual‐affinity nitrate transporter. The crystal structure has been resolved only in the nitrate‐bound state and reveals a binding pocket largely shaped by hydrophobic residues (Leu49, Val53, Leu78, Phe511). A single polar residue, His356, stabilizes the nitrate anion through a salt bridge (Parker & Newstead, 2014; Sun et al., 2014). No structural evidence of auxin binding to NPF6.3 is currently available, but it is feasible that binding would mimic nitrate binding with the carboxylate group of indole‐3‐acetic acid stabilized by the same histidine. This type of auxin‐to‐histidine interaction, in which a conserved histidine coordinates the auxin carboxyl group, is also observed in AUX1 and LAX3, members of the APC superfamily (Swarup et al., 2008; Yang et al., 2025).

Functionally, many MFS transporters act as symporters or antiporters, coupling substrate movement to proton gradients. In proton‐driven symport, protonation of an acidic residue on M1 or M4 near the binding site triggers the transition to the inward‐facing state, allowing co‐transport of substrate and proton into the cytosol. A typical configuration includes a donor‐acceptor pair, usually an acidic and a basic residue, that facilitates proton translocation. Both are located within a single domain (usually the N domain) on the 1st and 4th helix (e.g., M1 and M4, or M7 and M10) (examples include Pedersen Panyella et al., 2013; Parker & Newstead, 2014; Sun et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2015; Paulsen et al., 2019).

THE BILE/ARSENITE/RIBOFLAVIN TRANSPORTER SUPERFAMILY

The BART superfamily is among the most well‐studied of transport proteins. Members of the superfamily typically transport inorganic or organic anions, including the plant hormone auxin.

In plants, the most prominent example of a BART family is the PIN‐FORMED (PIN) protein family, which mediates auxin transport across cellular membranes and plays a central role in auxin efflux during polar auxin transport (Hammes & Panyella Pedersen, 2024). Eight PINs are found in Arabidopsis thaliana, with structural data available for PIN1, PIN3, and PIN8 (Figure 1, Table 1) (Adamowski & Friml, 2015; Ding et al., 2012; Křeček et al., 2009; Petrásek et al., 2006; Su et al., 2022; Ung et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022). These proteins play roles in plant development and environmental sensing, regulating processes including photo‐ and gravitropism and organ development (Adamowski & Friml, 2015).

Structurally, BART transporters share a conserved core architecture composed of ten transmembrane helices, characterized by an inverted twofold pseudosymmetric arrangement of five transmembrane ⍺‐helices, which originate from opposing sides of the membrane (Mansour et al., 2007). The repeats likely arose from a tandem duplication event, and each contributes to a scaffold domain (TM1‐2, TM6‐7) and a transport domain (TM3‐5, TM8‐10) (Figure 2c,d) (Vosolsobě et al., 2024).

The scaffold domain provides structural stability and, in the case of PIN proteins, mediates dimerization. The transport domain consists of two three‐helix bundles which adopt similar folds but are oriented in opposite directions. TM4 and TM9 are interrupted helices (TM4a/b and TM9a/b) that cross near the center of the transporter, forming a substrate‐binding pocket. This crossover region aligns two positive and two negative helix dipoles on the same side, creating a distinct electrostatic environment that contributes to substrate recognition. In PIN proteins, for example, the carboxylic acid group of auxin interacts with the positive dipole within this region (Schulz et al., 2025; Ung et al., 2022).

The transport cycle of the BART superfamily is described as ‘the elevator mechanism’. Here the transport domain undergoes a vertical displacement relative to the scaffold domain with respect to the membrane plane. This shifts the substrate‐binding site, defined by the transport domain, to be exposed sequentially to the two sides of the membrane (Garaeva & Slotboom, 2020). In the case of PIN8, a combination of structural biology and activity assays reveals distinct inward‐open, occluded and outward‐open states, with multiple gating steps in between, confirming the elevator mechanism of these auxin transporters (Ung et al., 2022).

Although the BART superfamily maintains a conserved structural core, its global topology can vary across members (essentially some members are ‘inside out’ compared to others). The first observed global topology is exemplified by the bile acid/sodium symporter ASBT with TM1 of each repeat starting from the cytosolic side, while PIN proteins display an inverted architecture in which TM1 of each repeat begins on the extracellular side. This inversion results in extracellular localization of both N‐ and C‐termini. Other members, such as the Na+/H+ antiporter NAPA, include additional transmembrane helices, one preceding the first repeat and two before the second, that invert the core fold and produce a topology similar to that of the PIN proteins. Despite these differences, the crossover region and elevator mechanism remain conserved features across the superfamily, supporting a shared transport strategy (Lee et al., 2013; Ung, Schulz, Stokes, et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2014).

Post‐translational regulation of BART superfamily transporters varies depending on the transporter in question. For example, PIN proteins have family members that are sensitive to kinase modulation (known as long‐loop or canonical PINs) and some that are insensitive (known as short‐loop or noncanonical PINs). The basis for this lies in an unstructured cytosolic loop, that is significantly extended in canonical PINs (up to 300 residues). This loop has an autoinhibitory effect on auxin transport, requiring phosphorylation of key serine residues on the loop to release (Hammes & Panyella Pedersen, 2024; Janacek et al., 2024). Noncanonical PINs lack this function and are believed to be constitutively active based on findings from AtPIN8 (Ung et al., 2022). Similarly, protein kinase C is implicated in the regulation of bile transport by ASBT, also through serine modification. However, the molecular mechanism of this modulation also remains unclear (Nguyen et al., 2024).

THE AMINO ACID–POLYAMINE–ORGANOCATION SUPERFAMILY

The APC superfamily is the second largest group of secondary active transporters (Wong et al., 2012). In plants the superfamily facilitates the movement of amino acids, polyamines, and phytohormones (Ung et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2020). In Arabidopsis thaliana, the AUXIN RESISTANT 1/LIKE AUX1 (AUX/LAX) family including AUX1, LAX1, LAX2, LAX3 has been implicated in auxin transport (da Costa et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2021; Moreno‐Piovano et al., 2017; Péret et al., 2012; Swarup et al., 2008).

The AZA‐GUANINE RESISTANT (AZG) family members AZG1 and AZG2 are proposed to be involved in cytokinin transport (Tessi et al., 2021; Tessi et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024).

These transporters contribute to hormone homeostasis, directional auxin flow, and signal modulation across tissues (Figure 1, Table 1).

The core architecture of APC transporters consists of two inverted repeats, each comprising five transmembrane helices, forming a pseudosymmetrical 10‐TM structure (Ung et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Note that these repeats bear no sequence similarity to the repeats found in the BART superfamily described above. The bundle domain includes TM1–2 and TM6–7, while the scaffold domain comprises TM3–5 and TM8–10. Substrate binding occurs centrally within the bundle domain, where TM1 and TM6 are interrupted mid‐helix, forming flexible loops that contribute to substrate recognition. These loops, along with adjacent helix termini, create a dynamic binding pocket capable of accommodating chemically diverse substrates.

The scaffold domain can be further divided into two structural elements: a hash region formed by TM3–4 and TM8–9, which intersect in a grid‐like arrangement (a hash ‘#’) within the membrane plane, and the arms, formed by TM5 and TM10, which wrap around the bundle domain to stabilize its position (Figure 2e,f) (Ung et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Structurally, AZG transporters are a good example of the introduction of additional helices to the two sequence repeats. Here, each repeat is prefaced with two additional transmembrane helices. These insertions do not disrupt the overall inverted pseudosymmetry of the core architecture, and the mechanism of transport is unchanged (Wei et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2024). While AUX/LAX proteins appear to be monomers, AZG proteins form homodimers, with dimerization mediated by the additional 4 TM helices and the extracellular loops within the scaffold domain (Ung et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025).

APC transporters operate via ‘the rocking‐bundle mechanism’, first described for the bacterial leucine transporter, LeuT (Yamashita et al., 2005). Here, a mobile bundle domain containing the substrate‐binding site shifts relative to a more static scaffold domain, alternately exposing the binding site to either side of the membrane (Forrest & Rudnick, 2009; Licht et al., 2024). This conformational cycling enables substrate translocation while maintaining directional control. There are some similarities to the elevator mechanism found in the BART superfamily, and the literature sometimes conflates the two distinct mechanisms: elevator vs rocking‐bundle.

Proton coupling plays a central role in AUX/LAX‐mediated auxin transport. Recent structural studies of Arabidopsis thaliana LAX3 and AUX1 have identified two conserved histidine residues that coordinate proton binding and stabilize the substrate‐binding site. These residues are thought to facilitate proton‐driven conformational changes that enable auxin import (Ung et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025).

Within the AZG branch, functional assays suggest that AZG1 operates as a proton symporter, whereas AZG2 behaves more like a uniporter (Tessi et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2024). The exact energetic coupling of AZG2 remains unresolved, hinting at possible alternative driving forces that merit further investigation.

THE TRANSPORTER‐OPSIN‐G PROTEIN‐COUPLED RECEPTOR SUPERFAMILY

The TOG superfamily gathers a functionally eclectic set of membrane proteins, some even operating as ER chaperones. In plants, the superfamily is mainly represented by the Sugars Will Eventually Be Exported Transporter (SWEET) family, which was initially identified as sugar facilitators, central for phloem loading and unloading (Chen et al., 2010, 2012; Ji et al., 2022). Later, other studies have suggested that some SWEETs also transport hormones. SWEET13 and SWEET14 have been linked to gibberellin transport and SWEET15 to cytokinin transport, potentially expanding the functional scope of the family beyond carbohydrate flux (Figure 1, Table 1) (Chen et al., 2010; Han et al., 2017; Isoda et al., 2022; Kanno et al., 2016; Radchuk et al., 2023). Because the first proteins characterized contained a characteristic ‘PQ’ sequence motif, the family is often called the PQ‐loop family.

Structurally, TOG transporters are the smallest of the superfamilies discussed here. They consist of two three‐transmembrane helix repeats forming N and C domains and with the PQ motif found on the first TM helix of each three‐helix repeat (Figure 2g,h). These repeats are separated by a single transmembrane linker helix, which ensures that both domains originate from the same side of the membrane (Chen et al., 2010). This arrangement results in a pseudosymmetric topology as in the MFS superfamily, rather than the inverted pseudosymmetry observed in BART and APC superfamilies. Of interest, the 7‐TM topology corresponds to a functional duplication of the prokaryotic SemiSWEETS, which consist of 3 TMs and form homodimers, leading to a 6 TM functional unit with a perfectly symmetrical binding site (Lee et al., 2015). In plant SWEETs, the additional seventh helix (TM4) lies at the junction of the two repeats and serves as an inversion linker, orienting TM1–3 parallel to TM5–7 and thereby recreating a configuration reminiscent of the SemiSWEET homodimer. TM4 was found to be indispensable in plant SWEETs, mediating key contacts between the two 3‐TM protomers (Narayanan et al., 2024). Similarly to the bacterial SemiSWEET homodimers, the transport mechanism and substrate‐binding site of plant SWEETs are highly symmetrical (Han et al., 2017; Selvam et al., 2019; Tao et al., 2015). Additionally, some plant SWEETs have been shown to form homomeric trimers, where the protomers are coupled allosterically, introducing additional complexity to their function and regulation (Tao et al., 2015).

Transport by TOG family members proceeds via an alternating access mechanism that has not received a unique name but shares some of the functional features of the rocker‐switch model described for MFS transporters. Substrate translocation is achieved through coordinated, semi‐rigid domain movements that pivot around a central substrate‐binding cavity, alternately exposing it to either side of the membrane.

In Arabidopsis thaliana, the SWEET family of transporters is suggested to operate as uniporters moving substrates along their electrochemical gradient. Given the small size, it remains unclear if proton binding and release, and thus symport or antiport, could form part of their mechanism.

While initially characterized as sugars transporters, recent studies showing the ability of AtSWEET13 and AtSWEET14 to also transport a range of gibberellins, confirmed so far in yeast and Xenopus oocyte uptake assays, highlight the putative expanded functionality of the SWEETs (Kanno et al., 2016). AtSWEET15 has been demonstrated to transport different cytokinins in oocyte uptake assays, and several homologs from barley were shown to either transport both sugars and cytokinins or be exclusive for cytokinins (Radchuk et al., 2023). This suggests that SWEETs can accommodate a broader range of chemically diverse molecules than originally appreciated (Han et al., 2017; Kanno et al., 2016). The ability of SWEETs to handle these chemically distinct substrates is particularly notable given the chemical differences between sugars, cytokinins, and gibberellins: Cytokinins are derived from purines, while gibberellins are diterpene acids with substantially higher hydrophobicity than sugars.

THE DRUG/METABOLITE TRANSPORTER SUPERFAMILY

The DMT superfamily gathers a surprisingly diverse set of secondary active transporters found in eukaryotes, bacteria, and archaea (Jack et al., 2001; Västermark et al., 2011). DMT proteins mediate the translocation of diverse substrates, including nucleotide–sugar conjugates, amino acids, metals, toxins such as chloroquine, and some phytohormones (Jack et al., 2001; Ranocha et al., 2013; Tsuchiya et al., 2016). In plants, two DMT families have been implicated in hormone transport. The first is the Plant Drug/Metabolite Exporter (P‐DME, aka. UMAMIT) family with the member WAT1 known to function as a vacuolar auxin exporter, mediating auxin efflux from the vacuole to the cytosol (Ranocha et al., 2010; Ranocha et al., 2013).

The second is the Plant Organocation Permease (POP) family. Initially named the Purine Permease (PUP) family, it was thought to be exclusive for plants and was found to be involved in purine transport. It was later included into the larger POP grouping that expanded the role for the group towards plant secondary metabolism (Jelesko, 2012). The POP family has several members, PUP1, PUP2, PUP7, PUP8, PUP14, PUP21 linked to cytokinin transport (Hu & Shani, 2023). Cytokinins are adenine derivatives, consistent with a role in purine transport of the family (Figure 1, Table 1) (Bürkle et al., 2003; Girke et al., 2014; Jelesko, 2012). Some Arabidopsis POP members show distinct localizations: PUP1 and PUP8 occur at both the plasma membrane and endoplasmic reticulum, PUP7 and PUP2 are confined to the ER, while PUP14 localizes to the plasma membrane and functions during embryogenesis (Hu & Shani, 2023; Xiao et al., 2020).

Despite their considerable sequence divergence, DMT transporters share a conserved core architecture of ten transmembrane helices organized as two inverted five‐helix repeats related by pseudo‐two‐fold symmetry (Figure 2i,j) (Lu et al., 2011; Tsuchiya et al., 2016). These repeats bear no similarity to the repeats found in the BART and APC superfamilies despite a (cursory) similarity. Evolutionarily, this fold is thought to have arisen from small multidrug resistance (SMR) transporters, which consist of four helices per monomer, through the acquisition of an additional helix and subsequent duplication (Jack et al., 2001; Kim et al., 2019; Shcherbakov et al., 2021; Tsuchiya et al., 2016). This change from an even to uneven number of helices implies a topological flip, with implications for the broader functionality of the DMT superfamily (Jack et al., 2001; Woodall et al., 2017). The two repeats form structurally similar bundles that create a central substrate‐binding cavity. This cavity is mainly shaped by TM1 and TM3–4 from the first repeat, together with TM6 and TM8–9 from the second. TM2 and TM7 close one side of the cavity, while TM5 and TM10 seal the opposite side. The two sides TM1, TM8, and TM9 on one, and TM6, TM3, and TM4 on the other undergo semi‐rigid body movements around the cavity, producing the conformational changes required for transport. TM4 and TM9 act as hinge helices that facilitate these transitions (Ahuja & Whorton, 2019; Kim et al., 2019; Tsuchiya et al., 2016).

Transport by DMT proteins proceeds via an alternating access mechanism, in which coordinated helical movements expose the central substrate‐binding cavity to either side of the membrane (Tsuchiya et al., 2016). Structural insights from non‐plant homologs highlight functional diversity: the amino acid exporter YddG mediates uniport through a basket‐like cavity, while the triose‐phosphate transporter GsTPT2 and the chloroquine resistance transporter PfCRT function as antiporters, where electrostatic gating and substrate‐binding site composition enforce strict exchange properties (Ahuja & Whorton, 2019; Kim et al., 2019; Tsuchiya et al., 2016).

In plants, the fold is yet to be described, but the available experimental evidence points to a similar toolbox of mechanisms. Most DMT transporters are expected to couple substrate movement to the proton motive force either directly, as a symporter, or indirectly, as an antiporter that swaps substrate for a proton. Because the architecture is so modular, it seems plausible that subtle changes in a few residues could turn a symporter into an antiporter or adjust substrate selectivity enough to accommodate either auxin or cytokinin. Exactly how plant DMT proteins have tweaked the generic DMT scaffold for hormone transport remains an open question, and future high‐resolution structures will be essential to test these ideas.

CONCLUSION AND PERSPECTIVES

Across all transporter superfamilies discussed here—BART, MFS, APC, TOG, and DMT—a recurring theme is the conservation of core structural folds and transport mechanisms. Although individual transporters vary significantly in substrate specificity, regulatory control, and physiological roles, the basic architectural blueprint and mode of action stay the same within each superfamily. These conserved features reflect evolutionary constraints that shape how transporters function and define both substrate scope and energy coupling. Notably, the BART, APC, and DMT families each share a core of two repeats of 5 transmembrane helices, yet their folds and mechanisms differ significantly. For example, there are different helices in each fold that define the substrate‐binding site.

Hormone distribution in plants depends not only on synthesis and reception but also critically on membrane transport. Understanding how transporters move hormones across cellular and tissue boundaries requires careful attention to both their structural context and mechanistic principles. In this regard, comparing different transporters solely based on a similarity in substrate scope can be misleading. Instead, placing them in the context of their superfamily often yields clearer insight into function, energy coupling, and regulation (Box 1).

When functional data are sparse, structural information from superfamily members can guide hypotheses about substrate binding, conformational changes and coupling strategies, despite big differences in substrate selectivity. This structure‐based perspective is particularly valuable when investigating hormone transporters with limited functional data. As more plant transporters are resolved at high resolution, such approaches will continue to refine our understanding of hormone transport and its integration into developmental and environmental responses (Box 2).
