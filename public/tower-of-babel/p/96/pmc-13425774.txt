Metformin versus DPP-4 inhibitors and risk of parkinsonism in type 2 diabetes: an active-comparator cohort study with a landmark design
Authors: Mingyang Sun, Xiaoling Wang, Zhongyuan Lu, Yitian Yang, Shuang Lv, Mengrong Miao, Wan-Ming Chen, Szu-Yuan Wu, Jiaqiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a significant risk factor for Parkinson’s disease (PD). While metformin shows neuroprotective potential in preclinical models, clinical evidence remains conflicting, likely due to limited follow-up, comparator heterogeneity, and insufficient consideration of differential survival.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study (observing data from 2005 to 2025) using the TriNetX global network. Using an incident user design and a 1-year landmark period to ensure sustained therapy and minimize immortal time bias, we matched 75,535 metformin initiators with 75,535 dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP-4i) users via propensity scores. The primary outcome was a composite of new-onset idiopathic PD and secondary parkinsonism. Exploratory time-stratified landmark analyses assessed whether the association differed among patients alive and outcome-free at 5 and 10 years.ResultsFollowing the landmark period, over a median follow-up of 5.2 years (IQR, 2.8–8.4; total 785,564 person-years), the overall risk of the composite neurologic outcome was similar between groups (aHR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.87–1.09). Metformin users had lower observed all-cause mortality than DPP-4 inhibitor users (aHR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.73–0.77). In a 5-year landmark sensitivity analysis, no association was observed (aHR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.88–1.09). In exploratory analyses, a lower observed risk was seen among individuals surviving beyond 10 years (aHR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.75–0.97; p = 0.0152), but this analysis was conditioned on long-term survival and should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.ConclusionsThe primary analysis showed no overall difference in parkinsonism risk. Metformin users had lower observed all-cause mortality, which may reflect residual confounding or treatment-selection mechanisms. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD), a progressive neurodegenerative disorder affecting ~ 2% of individuals over age 65, imposes a growing burden on aging societies [1–3]. Emerging evidence links type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) with increased PD risk, faster progression, and greater disability [4, 5]. Given the overlapping burdens, identifying accessible therapies that mitigate neurodegenerative risk in T2DM is a pressing public health need [6–8].

Metformin, the globally preferred first-line oral antihyperglycemic agent, is safe, affordable, and well-tolerated [9, 10]. Beyond glycemic control, it has pleiotropic effects—anti-inflammatory actions, AMPK activation, oxidative stress reduction, mitochondrial enhancement, and potential cognitive benefits [11–13]. However, its relationship with PD risk remains uncertain [14–17]. While some studies suggest neuroprotection [16, 17], others report null or adverse outcomes, including B12 deficiency-related neurotoxicity [14, 15]. Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4is), another widely used antidiabetic class, show neuroprotective properties in preclinical models—reducing oxidative stress and enhancing neuronal insulin signaling—making them a relevant active comparator [18, 19].

Despite growing interest, long-term data on metformin’s impact on PD risk are limited [14–17]. To address these gaps, we conducted a large, real-world study using a federated electronic health record database to compare PD risk in metformin vs. DPP-4i users. Using propensity score matching, an active comparator design, and time-stratified survival analyses, we sought to compare parkinsonism risk between metformin and DPP-4 inhibitor users and to explore whether any association varied across longer follow-up strata.

Methods

Data source and study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX Analytics platform, which aggregates anonymized EHR data from 142 institutions worldwide. The dataset includes demographics, diagnoses, medications, labs, and genetic data, and complies with HIPAA and GDPR standards. Between Jan 20, 2005, and Jan 20, 2025 (the administrative study end date), we identified 893,945 adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). After applying eligibility criteria and 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM), two balanced cohorts were formed: 75,535 metformin users and 75,535 DPP-4 inhibitor (DPP-4i) users.

Study population

Eligible patients were aged ≥ 18 with an ICD-10-CM code E11 diagnosis of T2DM and ≥ 12 months of clinical data prior to first use of either metformin or a DPP-4i (index date). Exclusions included prior use of both drugs, existing PD (G20), secondary parkinsonism (G21), anti-parkinsonian agents (ATC N04), type 1 diabetes mellitus (ICD-10 E10), secondary or other specified diabetes mellitus (E08–E09, E13), gestational diabetes mellitus (O24.4), dementia (F01–F03), Alzheimer’s disease (G30), cognitive impairment due to known physiological conditions (F09), and mild cognitive impairment (G31.84). To avoid carryover effects, we excluded users of SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, or crossover between groups. Following exclusions, 83,445 DPP-4i and 716,982 metformin users remained (consistent with Supplemental Table 1). PSM yielded two matched cohorts (n = 75,535 each), enabling real-world comparison of the incidence of idiopathic PD and secondary parkinsonism.

Exposure and outcome

Exposure was defined by the initial prescription of metformin (A10BA) or DPP-4i (A10BH), with an incident user, active-comparator design within the TriNetX framework. To ensure the evaluation of sustained pharmacological effects and minimize immortal time bias, we implemented a 1-year landmark period; individuals were required to have at least one year of continuous prescriptions (defined by documented refills with a 30-day grace period) following the index date to be included in the analysis. Individuals who switched to the comparator, discontinued therapy, or experienced the outcome/death during this initial 1-year qualification period were excluded to preserve clinical contrast and reduce exposure misclassification. First-generation antidiabetics (e.g., insulin, sulfonylureas) were retained as covariates (Supplemental Table 1). The primary outcome was a composite of new-onset idiopathic PD (G20) and secondary parkinsonism (G21). Follow-up began one year post-index (at the end of the landmark period) to minimize reverse causation and continued until PD diagnosis, death, last record, or the administrative study end. A comprehensive schematic illustrating the index date, 1-year landmark period, exclusions, follow-up start, and censoring is provided in Supplemental Fig. 1. Median follow-up duration (with interquartile range) and total person-years were calculated for each cohort. Figure 1 shows unadjusted cumulative incidence of composite parkinsonism.

DPP-4 inhibitors were selected as the active comparator because they represent widely used second-line oral antihyperglycemic agents typically initiated at a comparable stage of diabetes management, ensuring treatment-indication comparability and minimizing confounding by indication. Utilizing an active comparator also reduces healthy user and surveillance biases. Furthermore, because DPP-4 inhibitors have demonstrated potential neuroactive properties in preclinical models [20], this active-comparator design provides a conservative comparison of metformin with another clinically relevant glucose-lowering therapy, rather than a comparison against a biologically inert placebo.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were prespecified and stratified by age (< 65 vs. ≥65 years), sex, glycemic control (HbA1c < 7% vs. ≥7%), and kidney function (eGFR < 60 vs. ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m²) (Fig. 2) to assess heterogeneity. Dementia (F01–F03, G30) served as a positive outcome control. Negative outcome controls included appendicitis, hematologic malignancy, infections (e.g., common cold), IBS, burns, and suicidality. Vitamin C use was employed as a negative exposure control (Fig. 3). These analyses validated the specificity of findings. To assess temporal patterns while acknowledging the potential influence of differential survival, we conducted exploratory landmark analyses among patients alive and outcome-free at 5 and 10 years. These analyses were considered strictly hypothesis-generating because conditioning on long-term survival may introduce survivor or collider bias (Fig. 4 and Supplemental Table 2).

Covariates and statistical analysis

Propensity score matching was performed using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement, with a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score, matching on demographic, clinical, metabolic, behavioral, and medication covariates. Covariate balance was confirmed post-matching using standardized mean differences (Supplemental Table 1). The primary estimand was an adjusted conditional hazard ratio within the propensity score–matched cohort. As all post-matching ASMDs were below 0.05 (Supplemental Table 1), indicating excellent balance, regression adjustment within the matched cohort was used to improve precision rather than to address residual imbalance. Adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% CIs for parkinsonism outcomes were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Risk ratios appear in Table 1. Kaplan–Meier survival is shown in Fig. 1; Supplement Fig. 2 shows overall survival. Inverse probability weighting and alternative matchings confirmed robustness.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Before matching, metformin users (n = 716,982) were generally younger (mean age, 60.6 vs. 67.7 years), had higher body mass index (32.5 vs. 30.1 kg/m²), and better renal function (eGFR, 66.9 vs. 55.2 mL/min/1.73 m²) compared to DPP-4is users (n = 83,445) (Supplemental Table 1). After 1:1 PSM, 75,535 patients were included in each group with excellent covariate balance (all ASMDs < 0.05). Key baseline characteristics, including age (67.3 vs. 67.6 years), sex distribution (50.7% vs. 50.0% male), BMI (30.4 vs. 30.6 kg/m²), HbA1c (7.65% vs. 7.73%), and eGFR (70.3 vs. 70.5 mL/min/1.73 m²), were comparable between groups. Cardiovascular comorbidities, medication profiles, and environmental exposures such as prior traumatic brain injury showed no meaningful differences post-matching. All post-matching ASMDs were below 0.05, indicating excellent covariate balance. Regression adjustment within the matched cohort was used to improve precision rather than to correct residual imbalance.

Primary and secondary outcomes

Following the one-year landmark period, during a median follow-up of 5.2 years (IQR, 2.8–8.4; total 785,564 person-years), the incidence of the composite outcome (idiopathic PD and secondary parkinsonism) was similar between groups, with 614 events (0.81%) in the DPP-4i group and 649 events (0.86%) among metformin users. The adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) for the composite outcome associated with metformin was 0.97 (95% CI, 0.87 to 1.09). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated overlapping cumulative incidence curves between groups throughout the study period (Fig. 1). In contrast, all-cause mortality differed substantially. Metformin users exhibited a lower mortality rate compared to DPP-4is users (16.98% vs. 20.70%; aHR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.73 to 0.77) (Supplemental Fig. 2). As an additional descriptive sensitivity analysis, we examined the composite endpoint of parkinsonism or all-cause death. This composite was lower in the metformin group (HR, 0.76; log-rank p ≤ 0.0001; Supplemental Fig. 3). In outcome-specific analyses, the aHR for idiopathic PD (G20), which comprised 85.0% of the composite events (1,074 of 1,263), was 0.98 (95% CI, 0.87–1.10; p = 0.76), consistent with the primary null finding. The descriptive analysis for secondary parkinsonism (G21; 189 events) yielded an aHR of 0.93 (95% CI, 0.70–1.24; p = 0.63) (Supplemental Table 3).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses showed consistent findings across predefined clinical strata, with no statistically significant effect modification. The adjusted hazard ratios for PD and parkinsonism remained close to 1.0 across categories of age, sex, glycemic control, and renal function (Fig. 2). Among patients aged ≥ 65 years, the aHR was 0.95 (95% CI, 0.83 to 1.09). These findings suggest that the neutral effect of metformin relative to DPP-4is during the median follow-up period is broadly consistent across patient populations.

Sensitivity and control analyses

Results were robust across sensitivity analyses, including inverse probability of treatment weighting. To assess specificity, we included control outcomes as supportive evidence. Metformin use was associated with a modestly lower risk of dementia (aHR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.89–0.98), while no association was observed with negative outcome controls, such as acute appendicitis or common cold (Fig. 3). Additionally, vitamin C use, employed as a negative exposure control, showed no significant association with PD, suggesting minimal confounding from healthcare utilization patterns.

Exploratory time-stratified analysis

To explore temporal patterns while acknowledging differential survival, we conducted exploratory landmark analyses among patients alive and outcome-free at 5 and 10 years (Fig. 4 and Supplemental Table 2). At the 5-year landmark, no significant difference in parkinsonism risk was observed (41,286 metformin and 39,844 DPP-4i patients at risk; 287 vs. 281 events; aHR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.88–1.09; p = 0.7995). Among individuals surviving beyond 10 years (12,418 metformin vs. 10,983 DPP-4i patients at risk; 83 vs. 94 events, respectively), a lower observed incidence of the composite neurologic outcome was seen in the metformin group compared with the DPP-4i group (aHR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.75–0.97; p = 0.0152). These temporal patterns require external validation, as conditioning on survival beyond 10 years may introduce survivor bias.

Discussion

Our study addresses a long-standing clinical uncertainty regarding whether metformin, the first-line therapy for T2DM, may reduce the risk of PD—a question of growing relevance given the aging population and increasing burden of neurodegenerative disorders [1–5, 14–17]. Prior observational studies have yielded conflicting results, often limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up durations, lack of active comparators, and insufficient consideration of differential survival [14–17]. Our findings offer important clarification: although metformin was not associated with a significantly lower risk of PD in the overall matched cohort, a lower observed risk was seen only in the exploratory subgroup of patients surviving beyond 10 years. However, patients surviving ≥ 10 years are a small, highly selected subset, potentially enriched for healthier metabolic profiles and greater treatment tolerability. Because metformin users had lower observed all-cause mortality, they may have had longer diagnostic opportunity for late-onset parkinsonian syndromes. Among patients who survived to the > 10-year landmark, the metformin group had more patients at risk (12,418 vs. 10,983) and fewer observed events; because this subgroup was conditioned on long-term survival, the finding remains vulnerable to survivor and collider bias and should be interpreted only as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating signal. By using DPP-4is as an active comparator and analyzing all-cause mortality separately and interpreting long-term neurologic findings in the context of differential survival (Supplemental Fig. 2), our study provides a large active-comparator EHR-based assessment. These results not only help reconcile previous inconsistencies in the literature but also provide clinicians with a more informed framework for understanding how long-term metabolic therapies may shape neurodegenerative risk. While idiopathic Parkinson’s disease and secondary parkinsonism are etiologically distinct, we utilized a composite endpoint to maximize diagnostic sensitivity within a federated EHR setting, where coding misclassification between these syndromes is well documented. Our outcome-specific analyses confirmed that the vast majority of events (85.0%) were attributable to idiopathic Parkinson’s disease (G20), and the isolated analysis of these G20 events demonstrated a null association identical to the primary composite finding. Therefore, the use of the composite outcome did not obscure a differential effect between the two diagnoses, and the overall neutral result is primarily driven by the incidence of idiopathic Parkinson’s disease. Our findings should not be interpreted simply as an absence of association, but as a clarification of the evidentiary boundary for metformin-related neurodegenerative hypotheses. In the overall active-comparator cohort, metformin was not associated with lower parkinsonism risk, arguing against a broad or short-term protective effect in routine diabetes care. The exploratory signal among long-term survivors, while biologically plausible, remains vulnerable to survival selection and should be used to guide future studies rather than clinical inference. Thus, this study helps reconcile prior conflicting literature by showing that comparator choice, follow-up duration, and conditioning on survival materially influence the apparent association between metformin and neurodegenerative outcomes.

The observed 25% reduction in all-cause mortality (aHR, 0.75) associated with metformin is relatively large and warrants cautious interpretation. Differences in prescribing patterns, cardiovascular risk, renal function, frailty, and treatment tolerance between the two groups may have contributed to this observed mortality difference, even after matching on measured covariates. We do not claim that metformin causally reduced all-cause mortality. Furthermore, while our additional descriptive sensitivity analysis showed a lower risk for the composite endpoint of parkinsonism or all-cause death in the metformin group (HR, 0.76), this result was primarily driven by all-cause mortality and should not be interpreted as evidence of a neurologic benefit.

Several factors may explain the absence of a short-term benefit in our analysis. First, DPP-4is, though traditionally considered metabolically focused agents, have demonstrated neuroactive properties in preclinical studies [18, 19]. Vildagliptin, for example, has been shown to reduce brain oxidative stress, improve mitochondrial function, and enhance central insulin sensitivity in animal models [21, 22]. These effects may render DPP-4is less suitable than placebo or neutral comparators for isolating subtle neuroprotective effects of metformin in shorter follow-up periods (Fig. 1). Second, the influence of competing mortality is especially important in older adults with T2DM [23]. Patients who die early in the disease course may never live long enough to exhibit neurodegenerative outcomes, potentially masking long-term treatment effects [23]. In exploratory landmark analyses, a lower observed parkinsonism risk appeared only among patients surviving beyond 10 years; however, this temporal pattern may reflect selection among long-term survivors and should not be interpreted as confirmatory evidence of neuroprotection (Fig. 4). Our findings are consistent with those of Shi et al., who in a large cohort of U.S. veterans aged ≥ 65 years reported that long-term metformin use (over 2 years) was associated with a reduced risk of multiple neurodegenerative disorders, including PD [24]. Other studies have also noted delayed benefits of metformin on neurocognitive outcomes, emphasizing the importance of treatment duration in modulating neurologic risk [25–28]. Preclinical models reinforce this time-dependent effect [26–29]. In MPTP-induced Parkinsonian mice, extended metformin exposure more effectively improved motor function, reduced dopaminergic neuron loss, and suppressed neuroinflammation compared to short-term treatment [26, 28]. These data align with the biological plausibility that neurodegenerative diseases develop gradually and may require long-standing intervention to meaningfully alter their trajectory.

At the mechanistic level, metformin exerts a wide range of actions that may cumulatively contribute to neuroprotection [30–33]. These include activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and inhibition of mTOR, enhancing autophagic clearance of misfolded proteins such as α-synuclein; reduction of oxidative stress through decreased ROS production and enhanced antioxidant defense; restoration of mitochondrial integrity and biogenesis; and anti-inflammatory effects through modulation of microglial activation [30–33]. Additionally, metformin may elevate brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a key regulator of synaptic plasticity and neuronal survival [26, 34–36]. These mechanisms are inherently slow-acting and may require extended, uninterrupted exposure to produce observable reductions in neurodegenerative disease incidence [26, 34–36].

Despite these findings, it is important to acknowledge that the literature on metformin and PD remains mixed [14–17]. Some studies have reported neutral associations, while others have raised concerns about potential harm [14, 15, 17, 37]. Notably, several observational analyses have linked long-term or high-dose metformin use to vitamin B12 deficiency, which is associated with cognitive impairment and peripheral neuropathy—factors that could confound or offset potential neurologic benefits [38, 39]. In certain inflammatory or toxin-induced animal models, metformin has failed to protect, or even worsened, dopaminergic neuron loss [30, 40]. These conflicting results suggest that metformin’s effects may be context-dependent, influenced by patient characteristics, comorbidities, and the specific mechanisms underlying neurodegeneration in different populations.

The clinical implications of our findings are nuanced. In the general management of T2DM, metformin does not appear to confer an immediate advantage over DPP-4is in reducing PD risk (Fig. 2; Table 1). This supports current treatment guidelines that prioritize glycemic efficacy, cardiovascular benefit, and safety profiles in choosing initial therapy [9, 10]. The exploratory long-term finding raises a question for future research, but it does not establish that metformin provides added neurodegenerative risk reduction in routine diabetes care. Whether cumulative metformin exposure is associated with neurodegenerative outcomes requires further validation. These data may inform patient counseling, long-term treatment planning, and future secondary prevention strategies in at-risk populations.

This study provides a large active-comparator EHR-based assessment of metformin and parkinsonism risk among patients with T2DM. Given the logistical and ethical barriers to conducting long-term randomized trials with neurodegenerative outcomes in this population, our findings provide valuable evidence that may guide future clinical research and hypothesis generation. Methodologically, this study also contributes by demonstrating the importance of comparator selection and time-conditional analysis in neuroepidemiologic research. By using DPP-4is as an active comparator and applying landmark analyses, we sought to reduce confounding by indication and clarify how follow-up duration and survival selection may influence observed associations.(27) Furthermore, our incorporation of both positive and negative outcome controls—including dementia, appendicitis, IBS, and suicidal ideation—and a negative exposure control (vitamin C) provided strong internal validation of the specificity of our results [41–43]. The consistent null associations across biologically unrelated outcomes support the specificity of the primary null finding and reduce concern that differential healthcare utilization alone explains the observed patterns [43].

Nonetheless, several limitations must be considered. Residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded despite the use of rigorous 1:1 PSM, inclusion of a comprehensive set of covariates—including demographics, comorbidities, metabolic markers, and medication history—and validation through multiple sensitivity analyses. Misclassification of exposure or outcome is possible due to reliance on electronic health records and diagnostic coding; however, any such misclassification is likely nondifferential and was minimized by requiring at least one year of continuous prescription data post-index to confirm sustained drug use. Granular pharmacy data—such as days supplied, cumulative dose, medication possession ratio (MPR), and proportion of days covered (PDC)—were unavailable within the federated TriNetX EHR platform. Although our 1-year landmark design required documented continuous prescriptions during the first year, which reduced short-term exposure misclassification and enriched the cohort for persistent initiators, the inability to directly measure long-term cumulative exposure or adherence remains an important limitation. Future studies utilizing linked pharmacy claims data or patient-level refill records are necessary to definitively evaluate dose-response and cumulative exposure-dependent associations. Moreover, our strict exclusion of patients with treatment switching, discontinuation, or subsequent exposure to SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists selects for a subgroup with unusually stable diabetes treatment trajectories. While this initiator-based design maximizes internal validity and preserves exposure contrast, it reduces external validity, as our findings may not fully generalize to patients requiring treatment intensification, combination therapy, or frequent medication switching in routine clinical practice. Furthermore, while our database observation window spanned 20 years, the median individual patient follow-up was approximately 5 years. Long-term follow-up in multi-institutional EHR networks is frequently incomplete due to care fragmentation and patient migration. Therefore, the selection processes governing who successfully reaches 10 years of continuous observation inherently create a small, highly selected survivor subset. Conditioning on this long-term survival introduces a high risk of survivor and collider bias. Consequently, the > 10-year protective association must be interpreted cautiously and is classified strictly as hypothesis-generating. We were also unable to evaluate serum vitamin B12 levels, which may influence neurologic outcomes in long-term metformin users; however, the absence of an increased PD risk in our cohort suggests that any B12-related confounding was likely limited. Lastly, our cohort was restricted to individuals with T2DM, which may limit generalizability to non-diabetic populations; however, our aim was to address PD risk specifically within a diabetic context, where both the exposure and potential susceptibility to neurodegeneration are most clinically relevant.

Future research should aim to clarify the temporal dynamics and mechanistic underpinnings of metformin’s potential neurologic effects. Randomized trials with long-term follow-up, biomarker endpoints, and stratification by neurodegenerative risk may further illuminate whether metformin has a role in disease modification. In parallel, mechanistic studies in human neuronal systems and well-characterized animal models will be essential to understanding the biologic plausibility, boundaries, and optimal conditions for such effects to occur.

Conclusions

In this active-comparator cohort study, the primary analysis showed no overall difference in parkinsonism risk between metformin and DPP-4 inhibitor users. Metformin users had lower observed all-cause mortality, but this finding may reflect residual confounding or treatment-selection mechanisms. The lower observed parkinsonism risk among patients surviving beyond 10 years was exploratory and may be influenced by survivor or collider bias. Further studies with longer complete follow-up, linked pharmacy data, and detailed neurologic phenotyping are needed to clarify whether cumulative metformin exposure is associated with parkinsonism risk.

Summary

This active-comparator cohort study compared metformin and DPP-4 inhibitors in relation to parkinsonism risk among patients with type 2 diabetes using a 1-year landmark design. The primary analysis showed no overall difference in parkinsonism risk. A lower observed risk among patients surviving beyond 10 years was exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and may be affected by survivor or collider bias. These findings highlight the importance of cautious interpretation when evaluating long-term neurodegenerative outcomes in EHR-based cohorts.
