Injectable hydrogel-mediated co-delivery of oncolytic adenovirus and melphalan for retinoblastoma control and vision preservation
Authors: Guoqing Wang, Kai Wu, Zongliang Zhang, Yongdong Chen, Yanfang Li, Xiaoshuang Jiang, Licong Liang, Yiliu Yang, Hongsong Fan, Jing Sun, Aiping Tong, Fang Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Retinoblastoma (RB) is the most common primary ocular malignancy in children, arising from the developing retina. While higher doses of local chemotherapy have improved tumor control, concerns regarding retinal toxicity and the development of chemoresistance remain significant. Oncolytic adenovirus (OA) presents a promising therapeutic approach for RB, but rapid clearance often limits its therapeutic effects. In this study, we engineered a genetically modified OA derived from human adenovirus 5 (Ad5), designed to selectively target and lyse RB cells. The combination of OA with low-dose melphalan demonstrates an enhanced antitumor effect, while minimizing retinal toxicity. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that melphalan significantly enhanced the antitumor effect of OA and extended ocular survival. More importantly, we developed a biocompatible injectable hydrogel delivery system based on the covalent coupling of collagen and aldehyde-modified cyclodextrin, which effectively enhances the loading efficiency of melphalan and enables sustained co-delivery of OA and melphalan. The mouse RB tumor model confirmed that this hydrogel system (OA-Mel@CCA) achieved localized and sustained delivery of both therapeutics, effectively controlling tumor growth and preventing brain metastasis. Additionally, retinal structure and function were notably preserved in mice treated with OA-Mel@CCA, with no observed retinal toxicity. These findings suggest that the injectable hydrogel-based co-delivery of melphalan and oncolytic adenovirus could represent a promising strategy for RB treatment.

Introduction

Retinoblastoma (RB) is the most prevalent primary intraocular malignancy in children, originating from retinal precursor cells1. Historically, radiotherapy and enucleation are the standard treatment approaches, however, have now been largely supplanted by chemotherapy and focal therapies. In advanced cases of RB, persistent or recurrent vitreous seeding is the leading cause of treatment failure, primarily due to the resistance of these seeds to both radiation and systemic chemotherapy. Intravitreal chemotherapy, including agents such as melphalan, topotecan, and carboplatin, has recently emerged as a highly effective therapeutic strategy for managing vitreous seeding in RB2. Among various chemotherapeutic agents, melphalan has shown considerable promise in addressing the persistence or recurrence of vitreous seeds in RB3. Studies have demonstrated that intravitreal melphalan, administered at doses of 20–30 μg, results in tumor remission and globe retention in up to 87% and even 100% of cases, respectively3,4. However, the use of intravitreal chemotherapy, particularly melphalan, is associated with potential toxicity, which in some instances may lead to complete retinal atrophy5.

Oncolytic virotherapy offers a promising therapeutic approach for the treatment of various advanced cancers due to its ability to selectively infect, replicate, and lyse tumor cells while leaving normal cells unharmed6. The antitumor effects of oncolytic viruses (OVs) are primarily mediated through direct tumor cell destruction and reprogramming of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment7,8. Unlike traditional cancer therapies, the mechanisms of OV-mediated tumor cell elimination are distinct, making OVs a rational choice for combination with other treatment strategies9. Given that chemotherapies target rapidly proliferating cells, the combination of OVs with chemotherapy is expected to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Indeed, both preclinical and clinical studies have shown that the therapeutic potential of OVs is significantly improved when used in conjunction with chemotherapy10, 11, 12.

OVs have been extensively investigated for retinoblastoma treatment, utilizing diverse viral platforms including adenovirus, herpes simplex virus, encephalomyocarditis virus, and picornavirus13, 14, 15, 16, 17. Among these, oncolytic adenoviruses (OAs) represent the predominantly employed vector type. For instance, the H101 oncolytic adenovirus demonstrated significant growth inhibition of retinoblastoma cells in murine models, highlighting its therapeutic potential14. Similarly, CRAd vectors delivering the herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase suicide gene effectively reduced human retinoblastoma tumor burden in vivo13. Here, we engineered an OA based on human adenovirus serotype 5 (Ad5) for RB targeting. To ensure tumor-selective replication, we exploited on the near-universal biallelic RB1 inactivation characteristic of RB by incorporating the E1AΔ24 modification18, 19, 20. This deletion confines viral replication to cells harboring dysfunctional RB1 pathways21,22. Furthermore, we employed the survivin promoter to drive E1A expression23,24.

OV monotherapy often yields suboptimal outcomes, necessitating combination with diverse modalities exhibiting distinct antitumor mechanisms—such as systemic chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and radiotherapy—to enhance efficacy9. The combination of OVs with chemotherapeutic agents represents a promising strategy in ocular malignancies25. However, OA-based combination therapies remain underexplored specifically for RB. One study has documented that combining the SG600 oncolytic adenovirus with vincristine induced potent antitumor effects against RB in vitro, supporting viro-chemotherapy as a feasible and potentially effective approach26. To address this gap, we evaluated a co-delivery strategy for OA and melphalan, designed to achieve RB tumor control while preserving visual function in a murine model.

The high metabolic activity within the vitreous body, combined with the rapid turnover of aqueous humor, results in accelerated clearance of viral vectors and antitumor drugs from the ocular environment, thereby limiting their retention time and posing significant challenges in maintaining therapeutic concentrations27,28. Consequently, this significantly restricts the duration and efficacy of treatment. In this study, we introduce a biocompatible, injectable hydrogel-mediated co-delivery platform for oncolytic adenovirus (OA) and chemotherapeutic agents (melphalan). Utilizing imine bond formation between aldehyde-functionalized cyclodextrin and collagen, we have engineered an injectable in situ gelation system featuring cyclodextrin covalently coupled to collagen. We hypothesize that the inclusion of complex formation between cyclodextrin and melphalan will augment drug solubility and stability, leading to enhanced bioavailability. Moreover, the collagen-based hydrogel serves as a highly biocompatible matrix for controlled drug release, facilitating sustained retention of OA and melphalan at the tumor site. This approach not only facilitates tumor eradication but also promotes the restoration of normal ocular structure and visual function following intravitreal administration (Fig. 1). Notably, the CCA system, constructed via Schiff base crosslinking, integrates temperature-responsive self-assembly (at 37 °C) with remarkable injectability and a robust loading capacity for viruses and melphalan. In contrast to conventional stimuli-responsive hydrogels, the collagen-cyclodextrin hydrogel system described here not only exhibits environmental responsiveness but also demonstrates superior biocompatibility, injectability, sustained drug release, and targeted therapeutic properties. These characteristics are crucial for the effective treatment of ocular tumors29, 30, 31. These findings indicate that the injectable hydrogel-based co-delivery system for oncolytic adenovirus and melphalan holds potential as an innovative therapeutic strategy for addressing retinoblastoma.Figure 1Schematic representation of the therapeutic potential of oncolytic adenovirus (OA) and melphalan (Mel)-loaded injectable CCA Hydrogels (OA-Mel@CCA) in a retinoblastoma model.Figure 1

Materials and methods

Material

Type I collagen was sourced from the Engineering Research Center in Biomaterials, Sichuan University (Chengdu, China). It was extracted from calfskin using pepsin digestion followed by salt precipitation. The solubilized collagen underwent further purification through sodium chloride fractionation and fibril assembly32. Sodium periodate (NaIO4, ≥98%), β-cyclodextrin (C42H70O35, β-CD, ≥98%) and Melphalan (C13H18Cl2N2O2, ≥95% Mel) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Other chemical reagents were obtained from Chengdu Kelong Reagent Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China).

The synthesis of β-cyclodextrin aldehyde (CDA)

CDA was synthesized by oxidizing the vicinal diols of β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) using sodium periodate. Specifically, β-CD (5 g, 4.4 mmol) was dispersed in 25 mL of distilled water, and a sodium periodate solution (NaIO4, 3.77 g, 17.6 mmol) prepared in 15 mL of deionized water was gradually added. The mixture was stirred at room temperature in the dark for 18 h to ensure complete oxidation. The reaction was quenched by adding ethylene glycol in a molar ratio of 1:1 with sodium periodate. The resulting solution was dialyzed against deionized water using a 500 Da molecular weight cut-off membrane for 3 days, with the water replaced three times daily. Finally, the dialyzed solution was freeze-dried to yield the desired CDA product.

Preparation of Col hydrogel and CCA hydrogel

First, a 0.5 mol/L acetic acid solution was used to prepare a CDA solution (3 mg/L), which was then used to dissolve collagen (7 mg/mL). The pH of the mixture was adjusted to 7.4 using 5 mol/L sodium hydroxide, and it was then incubated at 37 °C for 20 min to obtain the CCA hydrogel. If oncolytic adenovirus needs to be included, it can simply be mixed with the hydrogel pre-polymer solution at a specific concentration, and the gelation process can be repeated as described. For the Col hydrogel, only collagen needed to be dissolved in the 0.5 mol/L acetic acid solution, and the gelation process was carried out as described above.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis

The chemical structures of CD and CDA were analyzed using 1H NMR spectroscopy (Bruker Co., Ltd., AV II-600 MHz, Billerica, MA, USA) at 25 °C, with DMSO-d6 as the solvent.

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) analysis

The functional groups of CD and CDA were characterized using an FTIR spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., Nicolet 6700, Waltham, MA, USA) over a spectral range of 4000–400 cm−1.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were executed using AutoDock software version 1.5.7. The molecular structure of melphalan was constructed using ChemDraw Ultra software (Revvity, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), ensuring the inclusion of all necessary atomic details and bond types for accurate representation. For the host molecule, β-cyclodextrin (β-CD), the structure was retrieved from the PubChem database (PubChem CID: 444041). The obtained β-CD structure was then chemically modified within ChemDraw to introduce aldehyde functional groups, resulting in an aldehyde-functionalized cyclodextrin derivative (CDA). Both the melphalan and CDA structures were subjected to geometry optimization. This step ensured that the molecules were in their lowest energy conformations prior to docking simulations. Prepared ligand and receptor files were converted into formats compatible with AutoDock software. Post-docking analysis involved evaluating the docked poses of melphalan within CDA cavity. Binding energies, intermolecular interactions, and visual inspection of the complexes were conducted to identify the most favorable binding modes.

Rheological analysis

The rheological properties of hydrogel samples were evaluated using a rheometer (Anton Paar, MCR302, Graz, Austria) with 25 mm diameter parallel plates. Measurements were conducted at a fixed plate gap of 1 mm and a controlled temperature of 25 °C. Dynamic frequency sweep tests were performed to determine the frequency-dependent storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) over a range of 0.1–100 rad/s at a constant strain of 1%. Strain sweep tests were carried out across a strain range of 0.1%–500% at a fixed frequency of 1 Hz.

Compression testing

The Young's modulus (elastic modulus) of the hydrogels was determined using a dynamic mechanical analyzer (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) operated in controlled force mode with an initial preloading force of 0.01 N. The testing protocol involved applying a ramped force at a rate of 0.10 N/min up to 0.50 N. The Young's modulus was calculated as the absolute value of the slope from the linear region of the resulting stress-strain curve.

Swelling ratio test

The prepared hydrogels were weighed and the initial weight was recorded as W0. The hydrogel samples were then immersed in PBS. At specific time points, the hydrogels were retrieved, excess moisture was blotted off, and the wet weight was recorded as Wt. The swelling ratio was determined as Eq. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

Hydrogel samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently lyophilized using a lyophilizer (VirTis, Advantage Plus EL-85, Gardiner, NY, USA). The lyophilized samples were then coated with a thin layer of gold and imaged using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi Co., Ltd., S-4800, Tokyo, Japan) operated at an acceleration voltage of 5.0 kV.

Release performance evaluation

First, aldehyde-functionalized cyclodextrin loaded with melphalan (Mel@CDA) was prepared. The characteristic absorption peak intensity at 260 nm was measured, and the loading amount of Mel in CDA was calculated by comparison with a standard curve. Subsequently, Mel@CDA was added to collagen (Col) to prepare Mel@CCA hydrogels, while Col hydrogels containing only Mel served as controls. Both types of hydrogels (200 μL) were placed in transparent plastic cuvettes containing 2 mL of PBS solution and stored at 37 °C in the dark. At specific time points, samples were taken, and the absorbance at 260 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., Winooski, VT, China). Fresh PBS was used to replace the withdrawn solutions. The measurement results were compared with the standard curve to calculate the total amount of Mel released.

Cells

Human embryonic kidney cells (293A), human retinoblastoma cell lines (Y79 and WERI-Rb-1), human retinal cell line (ARPE-19), and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) were procured from ATCC. The RB7 cells were developed at West China Hospital, with RB7-Luc cells further modified to express the firefly luciferase (Luc) gene, enabling in vivo imaging applications. All cell lines were cultured under their respective optimized conditions. Specifically, 293A and HUVEC cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mmol/L l-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. ARPE-19 cells were grown in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 2 mmol/L l-glutamine. Y79, WERI-Rb-1, and RB7 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium with 10% FBS and 2 mm/L l-glutamine. All cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Mice

Six-to eight-week-old female athymic nude mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China) and housed under pathogen-free conditions. The mice were maintained on a standard 12 h light/dark cycle with unrestricted access to food and water. Prior to treatment, mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups, ensuring comparable tumor burdens across all groups.

Ethical approval and consent

All animal experiments performed in this study were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines established by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (approval number: 20241125003). Tumor samples from retinoblastoma patients were collected with approval from the same Ethics Committee. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection.

Construction of oncolytic adenoviruses

The oncolytic adenoviruses (OAs) utilized in this study were constructed following previously established protocols33. Briefly, all OAs were based on human adenovirus 5 (Ad5). The adenoviral E1 region was controlled by the survivin tumor-specific promoter, driving the expression of the E1AΔ24-IRES-E1B genes. The adenovirus death protein (ADP) gene was retained in the E3 region, while the remainder of the E3 genes were deleted. For the OA-GFP construct, the GFP gene was inserted into the E3 region under the regulation of a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter. All adenoviruses were propagated in the 293A adenoviral packaging cell line. Virus preparation involved harvesting both cells and supernatants one week post-transfection of the double plasmids, followed by three freeze-thaw cycles to lyse cells. The lysates were centrifuged and used to infect fresh 293A cells for amplification. Once at least 50% of the cells exhibited rounding, cells and supernatants were collected, lysed through additional freeze-thaw cycles, and centrifuged at 1500×g for 5 min (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., Multifuge × 1, Waltham, MA, USA). The process was repeated to maximize viral yield. The collected lysates were concentrated using polyethylene glycol 8000 (PEG8000) and purified via double CsCl density gradient ultracentrifugation. Viral titers were determined using a plaque-forming assay, and the purified viruses were stored at −80 °C until further use.

Viral infection, replication, and oncolysis experiment

Infection assay: Y79, WERI-Rb-1, and RB7 cells were seeded into 24-well plates and infected with OA-GFP at an MOI of 10. After 24 h, the percentage of GFP-positive cells was quantified via flow cytometry.

Viral replication assay: Viral replication was assessed using the TCID50 assay. Y79, WERI-Rb-1, and RB7 cells were plated and allowed to adhere for 24 h before being infected with OA at an MOI of 0.1 or 10. Two hours post-infection, the medium was replaced with fresh culture media. At specific time points, viral titers were determined using the TCID50 assay.

Oncolysis assay: In vitro oncolytic activity was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Y79, WERI-Rb-1, and RB7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with either PBS (control) or OA at an MOI of 10. Cell viability was measured at multiple time points post-infection. In a separate experiment, cells were infected with OA at varying MOIs (1, 10, 100, and 1000). After 48 h, cell viability was assessed, and the percentage viability of virus-treated cells was normalized against the PBS-treated control.

3D tumor spheroids coculture assays

Following our established protocol33, 3D tumor spheroids were generated by seeding RB7 cells into ultra-low attachment plates. The cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 1% B27 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., Waltham, MA, USA), 10 pg/mL human recombinant fibroblast growth factor (FGF; Sino Biological, Beijing, China), and 10 pg/mL human recombinant epidermal growth factor (EGF; Sino Biological, Beijing, China) for one week. Subsequently, RB7 spheroids were infected with varying titers of OA-GFP, and their morphology and viability were evaluated at different time points post-infection.

Cell viability measurement

Cells were plated in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Subsequently, they were exposed to varying concentrations of the test compounds for 48 h or as specified. After treatment, the cells were gently washed twice with prewarmed PBS, followed by the addition of 100 μL of fresh medium containing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent. The plates were incubated for 3 h, and relative cell viability was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF)

Retinoblastoma tissues from patients were fixed in paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and subjected to immunohistochemical staining using anti-CXADR, anti-SURVIVIN, and anti-RB1 antibodies. Eyeballs were harvested at 8 weeks post-treatment, fixed in a specialized eye fixation solution, and subsequently dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The tissue was then embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 8 μm slices. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on the sections for histological examination and imaging. For immunofluorescence (IF), Y79 and RB7 cells were infected with OA-GFP for 12 h, followed by staining with an anti-CXADR primary antibody and a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody. DAPI was used for nuclear counterstaining.

Western blotting

Y79, RB7 cells, and normal retinal tissues were harvested and lysed to extract total proteins. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes and transferred. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with anti-SURVIVIN (CST, Danvers, MA, USA; 1:2000) and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, Wuhan, China; 1:5000) primary antibodies. The membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; 1:2000). Western blot images were captured and analyzed using a digital imaging system (Clinx Science Instruments, Chemiscope 5300, Shanghai, China).

Flow cytometry

The expression levels of CXADR in Y79, WERI-Rb-1, and RB7 cells were assessed using an anti-CXADR antibody (Abcam, ab275683, Waltham, MA, USA). To evaluate the sustained release of oncolytic adenovirus (OA), OA@CCA (2 × 106 PFU) was placed in a cell strainer with an 8 μm pore size. This strainer was positioned in a 24-well plate seeded with 1 × 105 Y79 cells. Every 24 h, the strainer was rinsed twice with PBS and transferred to a new 24-well plate containing uninfected Y79 cells (1 × 105). Fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry were used to analyze the percentage of GFP-positive cells in the previously exposed Y79 cells.

Apoptosis analysis

For apoptosis analysis, RB cells were treated with OA (MOI = 10), melphalan (10 μg/mL), a combination of OA and melphalan, or PBS as a control. After 24 h of treatment, cells were collected and incubated with 7-AAD and Annexin V for 20 min. Apoptotic cell populations were analyzed using a NovoCyte Flow Cytometer (Agilent Biosciences, Hangzhou, China).

In vivo antitumor experiment

Retinoblastoma cells (2 × 105) were intravitreally injected into the eyes of 6–8-week-old athymic nude mice using a 33-gauge needle. Following the same procedure, intravitreal injections with various treatments were administered. Tumor growth was monitored through live luciferase imaging, and the survival times of the mice were recorded. In the ocular survival experiments, eyes were enucleated under anesthesia once the experimental endpoint was achieved, which occurred either when tumors extended into the posterior and anterior chambers, leading to eye proptosis, or at the predetermined survival observation time.

In the initial combination treatment experiment, Y79-Luc or RB7-Luc cells (2 × 105) were injected into the vitreous cavity of the right eye. Seven days post-injection, the mice were randomly assigned to one of four treatment groups (n = 5 per group): PBS control, OA, Mel, and OA + Mel. Each group received a 2 μL injection into the vitreous cavity containing PBS, OA (109 PFU), Mel (0.03 μg), or OA + Mel (109 PFU of OA and 0.03 μg of Mel). Tumor growth was monitored using in vivo live luciferase imaging. Additionally, electroretinography (ERG), histopathological examination, and survival analysis were performed at the specified time points.

In a subsequent experiment, the therapeutic potential of sustained OA and Mel release via an injectable CCA hydrogel was evaluated. Seven days after tumor implantation, nude mice with orthotopic RB (Y79-Luc or RB7-Luc) were randomly assigned to one of three treatment groups (n = 5 per group): CCA, OA + Mel, or OA-Mel@CCA. Each group received a 2 μL intravitreal injection containing either CCA (2% hydrogel), OA + Mel (109 PFU of OA and 0.03 μg of Mel), or OA-Mel@CCA (109 PFU of OA and 0.03 μg of Mel incorporated into 2% CCA hydrogel). Tumor progression was tracked using in vivo luciferase imaging. Additionally, ERG, histopathological assessment, and survival analysis were performed at designated time points.

In vivo imaging

Luciferase activity was assessed using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS, PerkinElmer, Norwalk, CT, USA) to track tumor growth in Y79-Luc or RB7-Luc-bearing mouse models. Briefly, 100 μL of D-Luciferin potassium salt (Beyotime) was administered via intraperitoneal injection. Following injection, mice were imaged using the IVIS system. The emitted luminescence intensity was quantified with the Living Image software (PerkinElmer).

Biodistribution and persistence of OA following intravitreal injection

To assess the biodistribution and persistence of oncolytic adenovirus (OA) following a single intravitreal injection, both tumor-free and tumor-bearing nude mice were utilized. OA and OA-Luc (which expresses luciferase) were employed in these experiments. In the tumor-free model, OA or OA@CCA (109 PFU/eye in 2 μL) was intravitreally administered into the left eye of mice. Blood samples were collected via tail vein at 1, 6, and 48 h post-injection to measure OA genomes in plasma. At 48 h, tissues including retinas, brains, optic nerves, and livers were harvested for viral genome quantification by qPCR. For the tumor-bearing model, RB7 cells (2 × 105) were implanted into the vitreous cavity of both eyes. Two weeks later, OA or OA@CCA (109 PFU/eye in 2 μL) was injected into the left eye of the mice. Blood samples were collected at 1, 6, and 48 h to measure OA genomes in plasma. Tumors, retinas, brains, optic nerves, and livers were collected at 48 h, 30 days, and 60 days post-injection, and viral genomes were quantified by qPCR, as previously described34.

In vivo safety evaluation

The ocular safety of OA-Mel@CCA delivered via intravitreal injection was assessed through histopathological analysis and ERG. Healthy mice were anesthetized both systemically and topically, and randomly assigned to one of four groups (n = 6 per group): untreated control, CCA, OA + Mel, and OA-Mel@CCA. A 2 μL intravitreal injection of either CCA (2% hydrogel), OA + Mel (109 PFU of OA and 0.03 μg of Mel), or OA-Mel@CCA (109 PFU of OA and 0.03 μg of Mel in 2% hydrogel) was administered to the right eye, while the left eye served as the control. The study involved histopathological examination, ERG testing, serum biochemical profiling, and body weight monitoring. At 28 days post-injection, eyes were harvested for paraffin embedding and H&E staining to assess the ocular tissue structure and morphology. Additionally, tissues from vital organs such as the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, brain, and bladder were also processed for histological analysis using paraffin embedding and H&E staining. Blood samples were collected to measure various serum markers, including alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatinine (CREA), uric acid (UA), total protein (TP), urea (UREA), and creatine kinase (CK).

Mouse ERG recording

Mouse ERG recordings and data analysis were conducted following various treatments. In brief, the mice were dark-adapted overnight and intraperitoneally anesthetized prior to the ERG examination. After topical anesthesia with 1% tetracaine, pupils were dilated using 1% tropicamide under dim red light. Genteal lubricant was applied to both eyes, and ERG recordings were performed using corneal electrodes. The reference electrode was placed subcutaneously behind the head, and the ground electrode was inserted into the tail. Mice body temperature was maintained at 37 °C throughout the procedure. The retinal responsiveness was assessed using a single flash stimulus at varying intensities (−1.0 to 1.0 log cd·s/m2) under scotopic conditions. Following 15 min of light adaptation, photopic ERGs were recorded using a stimulus intensity of 4.0 cd s/m2. The peak a-wave amplitude was determined from the baseline to the initial negative deflection, while the peak b-wave amplitude was calculated from the trough of the a-wave to the peak of the positive b-wave.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Company, v 8.0, Boston, MA, USA). Comparisons between two independent groups were conducted using Student's t-test, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was employed for multiple group comparisons. Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis was used to evaluate animal eye survival times, with P-values calculated using the log-rank test. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. Statistical significance was denoted as follows: ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

Results

Construction of engineered oncolytic adenovirus (OA) and the rationale for its infection and replication in retinoblastomas

Coxsackie adenovirus receptor (CXADR), a glycoprotein receptor involved in the binding and entry of adenoviruses into host cells35,36, exhibits elevated expression levels in the retinoblastoma cell lines Y79 and WERI-Rb-1, as well as in patient-derived RB7 cells (Fig. 2A and D). This suggests that RB are particularly susceptible to adenoviral infection. Survivin, a critical member of the Inhibitor-of-Apoptosis Protein (IAP) family, is extensively overexpressed in various cancers while exhibiting minimal expression in normal tissues37. We demonstrated that survivin was highly expressed in RB cells, whereas normal tissues, including the retina, showed negligible expression (Fig. 2B‒D). IHC analysis of tumor samples from 11 enucleated eyes further confirmed robust expression of CXADR and SURVIVIN proteins, alongside an absence of RB1 protein (Supporting Information Fig. S1). Based on the above findings, we developed an engineered oncolytic adenovirus (OA) derived from human adenovirus 5 (Ad5) according to our previous methods33. These oncolytic adenoviruses were engineered with a modified E3 region, retaining the adenovirus death protein (ADP) while deleting the majority of other E3 genes, thereby enhancing oncolytic efficacy and facilitating viral spread38. Furthermore, a 24-base pair deletion was engineered into the adenovirus E1A gene, effectively restricting viral replication to cells lacking functional RB1 (Fig. 2D). The E1AΔ24 gene was drived by the survivin promoter, enabling transcriptional regulation specifically in RB cells that exhibit elevated survivin expression39. Genetic schematics of the OA and OA with GFP insertion (OA-GFP) are shown in Fig. 2E. Confocal imaging revealed CXADR expression on the surface of RB cells and the successful GFP expression in RB cells infected with OA-GFP (Fig. 2F and Supporting Information Fig. S2). Furthermore, our engineered oncolytic adenovirus effectively infected RB7 tumor spheroids as early as 1 day post-infection at 50 multiplicity of infection (MOI), leading to a loss of spherical integrity and cell detachment by Day 5 (Fig. 2G and H). These morphological changes were associated with a marked, dose-dependent decrease in cell viability (Fig. 2I). Collectively, our findings underscored retinoblastoma as an ideal candidate for localized therapy using our engineered OA. Characterization of OA and its enhanced cytotoxicity with melphalan in vitro

Oncolytic viruses are distinguished by their ability to selectively infect, replicate within, and lyse tumor cells while sparing normal tissues40. To assess the characteristics of OA in retinoblastoma cells, we conducted in vitro experiments (Fig. 3A). Initially, we evaluated the infection efficiency of OA in three different RB cell lines (Y79, WERI-Rb-1, and RB7) by infecting them with OA-GFP at an MOI of 10. Flow cytometry analysis 24 h post-infection revealed that nearly 100% of tumor cells from all three lines were GFP-positive (Fig. 3B and C). Next, the replication ability of OA in RB cells was assessed. Y79, WERI-Rb-1, and RB7 cells were infected with OA at MOIs of 0.1 or 10, alongside a non-replicating adenovirus (Ad) control, and viral replication was monitored over time using TCID50 assays. The results demonstrated that OA replicated efficiently in all three RB cell lines in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 3D). Additionally, cytotoxicity test revealed that OA induced significant cell death in Y79, WERI-Rb-1, and RB7 cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 3E and Supporting Information Fig. S3A). Notably, no cytotoxicity was observed in non-tumor cell lines, such as HUVEC or ARPE-19 (Fig. S3B). After 48 h of treatment with 10 MOI OA, approximately 50% of the RB cells underwent oncolysis, indicating that this treatment regimen is sufficient to induce significant tumor cell death. To further augment the antitumor efficacy of OA, we hypothesized that combining it with chemotherapy could enhance its therapeutic potential. In this study, melphalan, a widely used chemotherapeutic agent for managing vitreous seeding in retinoblastoma41, was selected to combine with OA for combinatorial treatment (Fig. 3F). Cytotoxicity assays revealed that melphalan at a concentration of 10 μg/mL induced over 50% cell death in retinoblastoma cells, with minimal impact on the survival of ARPE-19 cells (Fig. 3G). Based on these results, we chose 10 μg/mL melphalan as the optimal concentration for combination therapy with OA (MOI = 10) to investigate the enhanced cytotoxic effects in vitro. Annexin V/7-AAD apoptosis assay demonstrated that the combination of OA and melphalan induced significantly higher levels of cell death in retinoblastoma cells compared to either OA or melphalan alone (Fig. 3H and I). Enhanced antitumor efficacy of OA and melphalan combination therapy in orthotopic RB mice models

Next, to assess the superior antitumor efficacy of combining OA and melphalan in RB, we established an orthotopic RB mice model by intravitreal injection of 2 × 105 RB cells into the right eyes of nude mice. The OA dosage was determined by a dose-escalation study designed to assess the safety profile of its therapeutic application. Three escalating dose levels (107, 108, and 109 PFU) were administered to assess the toxicities (Supporting Information Fig. S4). Since the maximum dose of 109 pfu treatment had no obvious toxicity, we selected this dose as the therapeutic dose for subsequent efficacy studies. The optimized melphalan dose of 0.03 μg was calculated through interspecies dose conversion based on comparative volumetric analysis of human and murine vitreous cavities, as supported by established anatomical scaling methodologies5,42. Seven days after tumor cell implantation, mice were randomly assigned to one of four treatment groups: PBS, OA, Mel, or OA + Mel. Mice were treated with an intravitreal injection with OA (1 × 109 PFU/2 μL), melphalan (0.03 μg/2 μL), a combination of both, or PBS as a control. Tumor growth was monitored through live luciferase imaging (Fig. 4A). In the Y79-bearing nude mice, both OA and melphalan monotherapies resulted in slower tumor progression compared to the PBS control. However, the combination of OA and melphalan led to significant tumor regression and notably increased survival times (Fig. 4B and C). Furthermore, combination therapy significantly reduced the average weight of implanted eyes compared to either monotherapy or PBS control at 20 days post-treatment (Fig. 4D). To assess the clinical relevance, we utilized RB7-Luc cells, which express luciferase, to establish an orthotopic patient-derived RB mice model. Similar to the Y79 model, combination therapy with OA and melphalan demonstrated enhanced tumor suppression and prolonged survival compared to monotherapy (Fig. 4E‒J). Retinal function was evaluated through electroretinography (ERG), which revealed a loss of retinal response in untreated tumor-bearing mice, indicated by a decrease in both scotopic a- and b-wave amplitudes and photopic a- and b-waves. In contrast, mice treated with the OA and melphalan combination exhibited significantly improved retinal function (Fig. 4K). The cone-rod homeobox (CRX) transcription factor, a marker for cone lineage cells, is expressed in both RB and normal retina but not in other tissues. Thus, CRX mRNA expression has been used as a marker for brain metastasis in RB models43, 44, 45, 46. We analyzed brain samples obtained from tumor-bearing mice either after enucleation or on Day 100. Notably, brain metastases were observed in only 1 of 11 mice treated with the OA and melphalan combination, significantly reducing the incidence of metastases compared to the PBS or monotherapy groups (Fig. 4L). Preparation and characterization of injectable collagen-based hydrogels (CCA) for intravitreal oncolytic adenovirus and melphalan delivery

Intravitreal administration of therapeutics commonly employs single bolus delivery, which is insufficient for maintaining prolonged intralesional persistence required for sustained antitumor efficacy. To address this limitation, injectable hydrogels that form in situ offer localized and extended drug release capabilities28,47. Here, we have engineered a biocompatible injectable hydrogel delivery system (OA-Mel@CCA) based on the covalent coupling of collagen (Col) and aldehyde-modified cyclodextrin (CDA), which effectively enhances the loading efficiency of melphalan (Mel) and enables sustained co-delivery of oncolytic adenovirus (OA) and Mel. Remarkably, β-cyclodextrin not only improves the solubility and stability of hydrophobic drugs through its distinctive inclusion complexation capability but also facilitates multifunctional modifications, making it widely utilized in drug delivery systems48,49. Collagen, a primary constituent of biological tissues, possesses outstanding biocompatibility and is capable of forming in situ gels at physiological temperatures50. Thus, incorporating aldehyde-modified β-cyclodextrin enhances the loading capacity for melphalan and promotes the formation of imine bonds with collagen amines, thereby improving the mechanical properties of the hydrogel (Fig. 5A and Supporting Information Fig. S5). As illustrated in Fig. 5B, the CCA hydrogel forms through self-assembly and imine bonding when collagen (Col) solution is mixed with CDA solution at neutrality and 37 °C. The injectability of the CCA hydrogel is demonstrated in the accompanying image. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy in Fig. 5C confirms the successful synthesis of CDA. The molecular docking analysis (Fig. 5D) shows that the formation of a stable inclusion between the hydrophobic cavity of CDA and Mel under hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, which in turn increases the solubility of Mel. Furthermore, rheological assessments indicate a stable viscoelastic network with G′ consistently exceeding G″, evidencing frequency-dependent structure (Fig. 5E). The gelation kinetics data indicate that the CCA prepolymer exhibits a rapid gelation profile under physiological conditions, suggesting the potential for in vivo application (Supporting Information Fig. S6). At low strain, the hydrogel maintains stability; however, high strain induces a gel-sol transition, characterized by increasing G″ over G′, illustrating the hydrogel's injectable nature (Fig. 5F). Similarly, Young's modulus data also confirmed that the introduction of aldehyde-modified cyclodextrins increased the mechanical strength of the CCA hydrogel up to about 4 Kpa (Fig. 5G). The CCA hydrogel demonstrates a minimal swelling ratio of only 20% over 48 h under physiological conditions, a property attributed to its enhanced network density and the increased cross-linking introduced by CDA incorporation (Fig. 5H). The degradation behavior of the CCA hydrogel was evaluated in PBS containing collagenase to mimic the in vivo microenvironment. The hydrogel displayed a slow degradation rate, attributed to its densely cross-linked structure, which forms a resilient network resistant to enzymatic breakdown. These findings suggest that the CCA hydrogel holds significant potential for in vivo applications (Fig. 5I). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images in Fig. 5J show that the CCA hydrogel has a fibrous network, which conforms to the basic characteristics of collagen-based hydrogels and facilitates virus loading. Regarding drug release profiles, Col hydrogel demonstrates explosive release due to network collapse, whereas CCA hydrogel shows controlled release attributable to its mechanical stability and effective encapsulation of hydrophobic Mel by CDA. Compared to the nearly complete release of melphalan from the Col hydrogel within 36 h, the CCA hydrogel released only approximately 40% of the drug over the same period, enabling a prolonged release duration of up to 96 h (Fig. 5K, Supporting Information Figs. S7 and S8). Then, fluorescent labeling of OA enables visualization, and confocal microscopy images confirm uniform distribution of OA within the porous CCA hydrogel structure (Fig. 5L and M). Evaluation of OA distribution and persistence following intravitreal injection of OA or OA@CCA

Tumor-specific localization is a critical safety attribute for oncolytic adenoviruses52. To investigate this, we first analyzed OA distribution 48 h after intravitreal injection into the left eye of tumor-free nude mice (Fig. 8A). Both OA and OA@CCA exhibited comparable viral distribution patterns, regardless of the CCA hydrogel encapsulation. Viral genomes were primarily located within the retina of the injected eyes (Fig. 8B). Viral genomes were undetectable in the plasma of mice receiving OA@CCA, while one case of viral shedding was observed in the OA-treated group (Supporting Information Fig. S11). Luciferase expression was confined to the retina of the injected eyes, with no detectable signals in extraocular tissues or contralateral eyes (Fig. 8C). Since abnormal tumor vasculature may influence viral distribution53, we further evaluated OA distribution 48 h post-injection in tumor-bearing nude mice (Fig. 8D). Consistent with tumor-free models, OA predominantly localized within the tumors of the injected eyes (Fig. 8E and Supporting Information Fig. S12A), with no detectable viral genomes in the blood following OA@CCA treatment (Fig. S10). Sustained viral persistence is essential for prolonged antitumor efficacy54. To assess this, viral persistence was evaluated at 30 and 60 days post-injection (Fig. 8D). The OA@CCA group demonstrated significantly higher viral retention in the tumor and retina compared to the OA group, while similar viral levels were detected in the optic nerve across treatment groups (Fig. 8F, Fig. S12B and S12C). Taken together, the encapsulation of OA within the CCA hydrogel restricts its distribution to the intravitreal environment of the injected eye, thereby preventing systemic viral leakage. Evaluation of eyeball structure, retinal function, and biocompatibility

Retinal toxicity and biocompatibility were evaluated following intravitreal administration of OA-Mel@CCA (Fig. 9A). Histological examination of retinal structure was conducted via H&E staining 28 days post-injection. No significant histological alternations were observed in paraffin sections from eyes treated with CCA, OA + Mel, or OA-Mel@CCA compared to untreated controls (Fig. 9B and C). Retinal function was assessed using ERG 28 days post injection. Scotopic and photopic ERG assessments showed no significant reduction in a-wave or b-wave amplitudes among the treatment groups, confirming the absence of retinal dysfunction (Fig. 9D‒G). To examine systemic safety, hematological and histological analyses were performed. Tissues from major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, brain, and bladder, displayed no significant morphological changes following OA-Mel@CCA treatment compared to untreated mice (Fig. 9H). Additionally, biochemical parameters indicative of organ function, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total protein (TP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CREA), uric acid (UA), and urea (UREA), were all within normal ranges after treatment (Supporting Information Fig. S13). Moreover, no body weight loss was detected in mice treated with OA-Mel@CCA (Supporting Information Fig. S14). In summary, following intravitreal administration, OA-Mel@CCA has been shown to exhibit excellent safety and biocompatibility. Discussion

Here, we present a groundbreaking approach to RB treatment by integrating an engineered OA with melphalan in a novel injectable hydrogel delivery system. This strategy capitalizes on the selective oncolytic ability of OAs and the chemotherapeutic potency of melphalan, addressing challenges in conventional RB treatments such as retinal toxicity, rapid clearance, and chemoresistance. The combined therapy demonstrated robust antitumor activity, with significant tumor suppression and extended survival in orthotopic RB mouse models. Notably, retinal structure and function were preserved, a critical advantage over standard intravitreal chemotherapy, which often risks retinal toxicity.

The sustained delivery system, OA-Mel@CCA, represents a key innovation. The biocompatible hydrogel enhances the solubility and retention of therapeutic agents, enabling prolonged intravitreal drug availability. This controlled release mechanism overcomes the limitations of rapid clearance typically observed in ocular therapies, ensuring effective therapeutic concentrations are maintained at the tumor site. This finding is consistent with previous studies on hydrogel-based delivery systems, such as those used for other intraocular conditions like diabetic retinopathy and age-related macular degeneration, which similarly demonstrated improved drug retention and minimized systemic exposure.

Compared to prior approaches for RB treatment, such as high-dose local chemotherapy or radiotherapy, this study's combined OA and melphalan therapy stands out for its ability to reduce tumor progression while preserving ocular integrity. Intravitreal melphalan has long been used for vitreous seeding but carries a risk of retinal toxicity, often leading to atrophic complications55. Various administration routes are also being explored. For instance, a study has demonstrated that a cell-penetrating peptide conjugated with melphalan achieved intraocular tumor inhibition and extraocular metastasis prevention via topical instillation56. In this study, we designed an innovative hydrogel-mediated delivery system to mitigate retinal toxicity risks, aligning with findings from studies employing sustained-release platforms for ocular applications57. For instance, research on corticosteroid-eluting implants in ocular inflammatory diseases has similarly highlighted the advantages of localized and sustained drug delivery, further validating the hydrogel's clinical potential58. While our data provide preliminary insights into the release behavior, a more detailed characterization of the release kinetics and mechanisms remains to be elucidated.

The use of oncolytic viruses as a targeted therapy has also been explored in other cancers, including glioblastoma and hepatocellular carcinoma, where they demonstrated enhanced efficacy when combined with chemotherapeutic agents59,60. However, unlike systemic cancers, RB's confined anatomical location makes it an ideal candidate for localized therapies like this study's hydrogel approach. Additionally, the incorporation of tumor-specific promoters, such as survivin, in the engineered OA offers enhanced specificity, reducing off-target effects. This innovation echoes findings from previous work with conditionally replicating adenoviruses in various cancers, further emphasizing the potential of tumor-targeted virotherapy61,62.

While the results of this study are promising, some limitations should be acknowledged. Long-term safety, particularly the potential for viral resistance or latent side effects, and the specific mechanism underlying the enhanced therapeutic effect of OA and melphalan remain an area for further investigation. Additionally, while the preclinical results are compelling, translating these findings to human clinical settings requires rigorous optimization of the hydrogel formulation and dosing protocols. The in vivo stability of the hydrogel, particularly the underlying mechanisms of enzyme-mediated degradation and depolymerization, as well as their specific contributions to viral release, remains to be further elucidated. Furthermore, this study employed the immunocompromised nude mice model for in vivo experiments. The selection was necessitated by the lack of immunocompetent murine RB cell lines capable of reliably recapitulating human intraocular tumor biology. Consequently, our experimental design precluded a comprehensive evaluation of immune-mediated implications on therapeutic effects. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that oncolytic viruses could prime antitumor adaptive immunity, render “cold” tumors “hot” and reduce tumor metastasis6,63. Thus, a more systematic evaluation of the implications for the immune system is required. To address this limitation, future investigations should systematically characterize tumor-immune interactions through three complementary approaches: establishment of immunocompetent RB models, longitudinal monitoring of immune cell infiltration dynamics, and quantitative assessment of immune checkpoint modulation following therapeutic intervention. Future studies might also explore combining the OA-Mel@CCA system with immune checkpoint inhibitors to further enhance therapeutic outcomes, as immune modulation has shown significant promise in cancer therapies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study represents a significant advancement in RB treatment, offering an innovative approach that combines localized and sustained delivery with therapeutic agents. By addressing the limitations of current therapies and prioritizing both efficacy and ocular preservation, the study not only broadens the horizon for RB management but also provides a framework that could be adapted for other intraocular and localized malignancies. This integrative strategy, building on and surpassing previous methodologies, holds promise for transforming the landscape of ocular oncology.
