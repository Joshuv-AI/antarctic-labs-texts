Pseudomonas-driven spoilage and identification of early-warning volatile markers in chilled Shimen Sanhuang chicken
Authors: Shuai Wang, Enqi He, Lei Zhou, Aihua Lou, Jian Liu, Jin Li, Wei Quan, Zhizhong Zhang, Yan Liu, Qingwu Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the spoilage mechanism of chilled Shimen Sanhuang chicken breast, a national geographical indication product from China, during 8 days of refrigerated storage at 4 °C. Integrated analysis revealed progressive quality deterioration, characterized by texture softening, increased pH, and intensified protein and lipid oxidation. Microbial succession analysis identified Pseudomonas as the specific spoilage organism, its relative abundance surging from 18.37% to 61.74%. Volatile profiling identified 83 compounds, and OPLS-DA screened key differential volatiles. Correlation analysis established significant positive correlations between Pseudomonas and spoilage-related volatiles like indole. Furthermore, indole and 1-octen-3-ol were strongly correlated with pivotal spoilage indicators, including protein carbonyls, TBARS, TVC, and the BAI. These findings systematically delineate the spoilage pattern of chilled Shimen chicken, proposing Pseudomonas as the core spoiler and nominating indole and 1-octen-3-ol as potential early-warning markers for quality deterioration, providing a theoretical basis for targeted preservation of premium chilled poultry.

Introduction

Chicken meat, particularly breast meat, is highly prized by consumers worldwide for its excellent nutritional profile, including high-quality protein, low fat content, and rich minerals (Barido et al., 2022). Shimen Sanhuang chicken, a nationally recognized geographical indication product from Hunan Province, China, represents a premium indigenous breed known for its distinctive texture, superior flavor, and high market value. With an annual production of 60 million birds and fresh poultry revenue exceeding 1 billion yuan (Xiangjia Animal Husbandry), this breed holds substantial commercial importance in China's premium poultry sector. In recent years, the demand for chilled fresh chicken, known as “cooled meat” in China, has surged due to its superior freshness, taste, and the absence of freeze-thaw damage compared to frozen products. However, the limited shelf-life of chilled chicken, primarily due to microbial proliferation and biochemical deterioration, remains a major challenge for the industry, leading to significant economic losses and potential food safety risks (Wei et al., 2016).

The spoilage of chilled chicken is a complex process driven by the joint action of microbial metabolism and endogenous enzymatic activities. During storage, the growth of specific spoilage organisms (SSOs) leads to the production of off-odors, slime formation, and nutrient breakdown. Concurrently, lipid oxidation and protein oxidation occur, resulting in rancidity, texture deterioration, loss of nutritional value, and the generation of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that contribute to unacceptable flavors (Barido et al., 2022). While the general spoilage patterns of muscle foods are well-documented, the precise succession of SSOs and their metabolic activities are highly dependent on the initial microbial load, processing conditions, and, importantly, the specific breed of chicken (Dominguez & Schaffner, 2009).

Extensive research has been conducted to monitor the quality changes in chicken during chilled storage (Balamatsia et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2022). Conventional indicators such as total viable count, TVB-N, TBARS are commonly used to assess the extent of spoilage. Furthermore, the evolution of VOCs provides a powerful fingerprint of the spoilage process, as these compounds are direct metabolites of microbial activity and chemical oxidation. Moreover, previous studies comparing indigenous and commercial chicken breeds have revealed notable differences in spoilage patterns (Bhoir & Kumari, 2021). For instance, indigenous breeds often exhibit higher initial microbial loads due to traditional free-range rearing, yet their meat may possess stronger antioxidative capacity, potentially delaying lipid oxidation (Peng et al., 2019, Wang et al., 2024). Conversely, commercial broilers, selected for rapid growth and high yield, tend to have different fatty acid profiles (e.g., higher polyunsaturated fat content), which may accelerate oxidative rancidity during chilled storage. However, most existing studies have focused on physicochemical changes rather than integrating microbial succession with volatile metabolite dynamics. Moreover, the specific spoilage organisms (SSOs) and their metabolic fingerprints vary significantly between breeds, underscoring the need for breed-specific investigations.

Despite this wealth of information, a significant research gap persists. Most studies have focused on commercial broiler breeds, while the spoilage dynamics of indigenous or specialty breeds, such as the Chinese Shimen Sanhuang chicken, remain largely unexplored. Shimen Sanhuang chicken is a premium indigenous breed recognized as a national geographical indication product. It possesses several distinctive attributes that set it apart from both commercial broilers and other native breeds. Its unique genetic lineage, developed through centuries of selective breeding in the mountainous region of Hunan Province, confers a specific lipid profile with higher intramuscular fat content and a favorable fatty acid composition (Wang et al., 2022). Additionally, the free-range rearing system exposes the birds to a diverse environmental microbiota, potentially leading to a unique initial microbial community on the carcass. Moreover, this breed exhibits relatively higher intramuscular antioxidant enzyme activities, which may modulate the progression of protein and lipid oxidation during post-mortem storage (Peng et al., 2019). These intrinsic characteristics likely lead to a unique spoilage trajectory compared to conventional breeds. A comprehensive understanding of its spoilage mechanism is crucial for developing targeted preservation strategies and ensuring its market quality.

Moreover, while previous studies often correlate microbial growth with physicochemical changes, an integrated analysis linking the succession of microbial communities (e.g., identified via high-throughput sequencing) with the parallel evolution of oxidative indices, BAs, and a wide spectrum of VOCs is still lacking for this specific breed. Establishing such detailed correlations is essential for identifying the true SSOs responsible for the characteristic spoilage of Shimnen Sanhuang chicken and for pinpointing key volatile markers that can serve as early warning signals of quality deterioration.

This study represents integrated investigation of spoilage dynamics in chilled Shimen Sanhuang chicken, a nationally protected geographical indication product. While previous studies on broiler chicken have reported microbial succession or volatile profiles separately, we uniquely combine high-throughput sequencing, physicochemical indices (including protein/lipid oxidation, TVB-N, biogenic amines), and comprehensive volatile profiling (83 compounds) with OPLS-DA and correlation network analysis. This multi-dimensional approach allows us to not only identify Pseudomonas as the core specific spoilage organism but also to propose, for the first time, indole and 1-octen-3-ol as candidate early-warning volatile markers for this indigenous breed. These novel insights provide a mechanistic basis for targeted preservation strategies tailored to premium chilled poultry.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The Shimen Sanhuang Chicken Breast Meate cuts was provided by Hunan Xiangjia Co., Ltd. (Huayuan, China). The chickens were slaughtered in a commercial abattoir using standard procedures, and the chicken breast was collected from 30 barrow carcasses. The meat was vacuum-packed and transported to the laboratory under refrigerated conditions at 4 °C. Upon arrival, the chicken breast was promptly trimmed to remove tendons, membranes, connective tissues, and other non-essential components. It was then segmented into pieces weighing about 200 g each (10 cm × 10 cm × 3 cm), sealed with plastic wrap, and packaged in transparent high-barrier plastic bags (O2 permeability: 0.21 cm3/(m2 24 h 0.1 MPa); water vapor permeability: 0.09 g/(m2 24 h)). These packaged chicken breast cuts were stored in a 4 °C refrigerator until spoilage, and samples were collected on days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8, labeled as D0, D2, D4, D6, and D8, respectively. For each sampling time, thoroughly mix three bags of chicken breast, and perform three biological replicates for each mixed sample.

Physico-chemical analysis

The pH value was measured directly on the sample surface using a portable pH meter (205; Testo, Shanghai, China), The texture profile analysis was carried out on chicken samples using a food texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, UK). The sample size was fixed at 10 mm in length, width, and height. The texture analyzer was calibrated with a 10 kg weight before any measurements were taken. Before testing, the chicken samples were placed in the center of the fixture base. All texture profile analyses used a 38.1 mm diameter cylindrical probe (TA5), with a pre-test speed of 2.0 mm/s, test speed of 2.0 mm/s, post-test speed of 2.0 mm/s, a trigger force of 5.0 g, and a compression strain of 50%. The repeated samples included in our design were statistically reasonable, and all TPA measurements were conducted at room temperature on the repeated samples.

Total viable count (TVC) analysis

The Total Viable Count (TVC) was assessed using Tian's method (Wang et al., 2025). Here, 25 g of the sample was diluted in 225 mL of sterile saline (0.85%) and homogenized for 5 min. Total viable counts (TVCs) were determined using plate count agar (PCA) from Aoboxing Biotech Company in Beijing, China, at 37 °C for 48 h. The results were presented as Log CFU/g. Each sample was measured in triplicate.

Measurement of lipid and protein oxidation

The levels of thiobarbituric acid reactive species (TBARS) in the chicken breast muscle were measured by the oxidation products reacting with 2-thiobarbituric acid, following the procedure outlined by Xu et al. (2023).

The results were expressed as milligrams of malonaldehyde (MDA) per kilogram of muscle. The sulfhydryl content of chicken breast protein was determined using the 5,5’-Dithiobis 2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) method, as described by (Zhang et al., 2019). The results were expressed in nanomoles of sulfhydryl per 1 mg of myofibrillar protein.

The carbonyl content in chicken breast protein was measured using 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH), following the method outlined by Oliver, Ahn, Moerman, Goldstein, and Stadtman with slight modifications (Oliver et al., 1987). The content of carbonyls was expressed as nmol/mg protein.

Total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) content analysis

The TVB-N level of chicken breast samples was evaluated using a semi-micro determination of nitrogen method, following Zeng's method modification (Zeng et al., 2023). The chicken breast was minced using a meat mincer (JYL-C022E, Joyoung, Jinan, China). 40 g of minced chicken breast was then transferred into a distillation tube, followed by the addition of 100 mL of distilled water, which was homogenized for 30 min. Finally, 1 g of magnesium oxide was added to the distillation tube containing the processed sample. The content of TVB-N was determined using the Automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (K9840, Hanon Instruments, Jinan, China). The results were expressed as mg of TVB-N per 100 g of chicken breast.

Biogenic amine analysis

According to the literature (Pang et al., 2024), 0.5 g of chicken breast samples were blended with 20 mL of 0.4 M perchloric acid and extracted repeatedly. The filter liquor volume was then adjusted to 50 mL with 0.4 M perchloric acid, and the mixture was centrifuged at 4500 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C using a 5810R centrifuge from Eppendorf in Hamburg, Germany. Next, 1 mL of the sample extract was added to 200 μL of 2 M sodium hydroxide, 300 μL of saturated sodium bicarbonate, and 2 mL of 10 mg/mL dansyl chloride. The mixture was then reacted at 40 °C in the dark.

After standing for 45 min at 25 °C, 100 μL of ammonia was used to remove the residual dansyl chloride, followed by a 30-min incubation at room temperature. The reaction mixture was then made up to 5 mL with acetonitrile and centrifuged at 11000 g for 5 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane, and 20 μL of the filtrate was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (1260; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled with a TC-C18 chromatographic column (Agilent, 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) at a flow rate of 0.9 mL/min using an isocratic elution with solvent A and solvent B. Solvent A consisted of acetonitrile, while solvent B was an Acetonitrile-0.01 M ammonium acetate solution (1:9/v:v) containing 0.1% acetic acid. The detection wavelength was set at 254 nm, and the column temperature was maintained at 35 °C.

The Biogenic Amine Index (BAI) was calculated by summing the levels of putrescine, cadaverine, histamine, and tyramine according to the idea proposed by Triki et al. (2018). The calculation formula of the Biogenic Amine Index (BAI) is BAI = His + Put + Tyr + Cad, with the unit mg/kg.

Volatile compounds analysis

The volatile compounds were extracted using headspace solid phase micro-extraction (HS-SPME) and were analyzed using a gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (GC/MS) system (QP-2010 Plus, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), as described by (Argyri et al., 2015) with some minor modifications. Minced chicken breast samples (2 g each) were placed in a 20 mL glass vial and sealed with a cap. VOCs were extracted with an aging SPME fiber (50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS; Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) at 60 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the fiber was removed from the vials and desorbed immediately for 5 min at 250 °C in splitless mode.

The compounds were separated using a DB-5 column with dimensions of 30 m length, 0.25 mm inner diameter, and a 0.25 μm film thickness. The carrier gas used was helium with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. For the analysis of volatile compounds, the oven temperature was initially set at 40 °C for 3 min, followed by a 2-min maintenance period. The temperature was then programmed to increase at a rate of 4 °C/min until reaching 130 °C, after which it was further increased to 230 °C at a rate of 8 °C/min and held for 5 min. The interface temperature was maintained at 280 °C throughout the analysis. The mass spectrometer operated in electron ionization (EI) mode with an electron energy set at 70 eV and a scan range of 35–550 m/z. The temperatures of the mass spectrometer source and quadrupole were set at 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively. Compounds were compared against reference standards in the National Institute of Standards and Technology 20 database, with a positive and negative matching value exceeding 800 considered for qualitative analysis.

Microbial diversity analysis

Chicken breast samples from various storage stages were chosen for Illumina sequencing. Initially, total genomic DNA was extracted from the samples using a DNA gel extraction kit (AxyPrep; Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions, and the quality of the extracted DNA was assessed through 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The bacterial 16S rDNA V3-V4 region was amplified using the specific primers 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). The purified amplifications were then sequenced on the MiSeq PE300/NovaSeq PE250 platforms (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., (Shanghai, China). The data were de-multiplexed and quality-filtered using Fastp software (version 0.20.0, Shenzhen, China).

Then, high-quality sequences were denoised using the DADA2 plugin with recommended parameters in the Qiime2 (2024 version) pipeline to achieve single-nucleotide resolution based on within-sample error models. Sequences denoised by DADA2 are commonly referred to as amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). To minimize the effect of sequencing depth on α-diversity and β-diversity measurements, the number of sequences per sample was rarefied to 20,000, still achieving an average Good's coverage of 97.90%. The taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed using the naive Bayes (or Vsearch, or Blast) consensus classifier implemented in Qiime2 and the SILVA 16S rRNA database (v138.2). Based on ASV representative sequences, PICRUSt2 (phylogenetic investigation of communities by reconstruction of unobserved states) was used to predict metagenomic functions. Bioinformatic analysis of the microbiota in chicken was performed using the Majorbio cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Based on ASV information, rarefaction curves and α-diversity indices, including observed ASVs, Chao1 richness, Shannon index, and Good's coverage, were calculated using Mothur v1.30.1. The similarity of microbial communities in different samples was assessed by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity using the Vegan v2.5–3 package.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as means ± standard deviation. Analysis of variance was performed using SPSS software (v.26.0, IBM, Chicago, IL, USA), and significant differences were defined as p < 0.05. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were compared with ROVA using reference standards in the NIST 20 database. Amplicons were sequenced on the MiSeq PE300/NovaSeq PE250 platforms (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were deduplicated, indexed, and quality-filtered using Fastp software (version 0.20.0, Shenzhen, China). Correlation heatmaps between microbiota and volatile flavor compounds were assessed based on Spearman correlation, with r > 0.80 and p < 0.05 selected as the correlation thresholds for microbiome-metabolite and metabolome-quality deterioration indicators. The Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons in microbiome-metabolite and metabolome-quality deterioration correlations. Only correlations with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 and |r| > 0.80 were considered significant.

Results and discussion

Changes in physicochemical properties of chilled chicken breast during storage

The physicochemical properties of chicken breast throughout the 8-day storage period at 0 °C, as summarized in Table 1. The texture parameters of chicken breast exhibited dynamic and significant changes (p < 0.05). The hardness dramatically increased from an initial value of 1585.4 g to a peak of 2963.91 g on day 2, indicating a substantial toughening of the muscle tissue. This initial increase in hardness is commonly attributed to the rigor mortis process and the contraction of myofibrillar proteins in the early post-mortem phase (Li et al., 2019). Subsequently, the hardness decreased sharply, reaching significantly lower values of 430.47 g and 135.97 g on days 6 and 8, respectively. This decline is likely a consequence of the progressive degradation of myofibrillar proteins and the disruption of muscle structure by endogenous enzymes and microbial activity, leading to muscle softening (Ding et al., 2022). Elasticity showed a decreasing trend initially but recovered and was significantly higher (p < 0.05) at the end of storage (0.89 on day 8) compared to day 0 (0.84). Viscosity also displayed a fluctuating pattern, with a significant drop on day 6 followed by an increase on day 8. The pH value first decreased from 6.17 to 5.86 on day 2, which can be explained by the post-mortem accumulation of lactic acid. Thereafter, the pH gradually increased, reaching a final value of 6.24 on day 8. Changes in protein degradation indicators and lipid degradation indicators during the storage of refrigerated chicken breast

Protein and lipid oxidation in chicken meat are interrelated chemical processes. As shown in Fig. 1A, significant (p < 0.05) decrease in total sulfhydryl groups was observed, from 3.99 nmol/mg prot on day 0 to 1.97 nmol/mg prot on day 8. Sulfhydryl groups are highly reactive and are readily oxidized to disulfide bonds or other covalent cross-links, leading to protein aggregation and loss of functionality (Soyer et al., 2010). The decline observed here confirms that myofibrillar proteins, particularly myosin, underwent severe oxidative modification during storage. Conversely, the total carbonyl content, a direct marker for protein oxidation, exhibited a remarkable and continuous increase from 2.11 nmol/mg prot to 13.26 nmol/mg prot over the 8 days. The formation of protein carbonyls is an irreversible process that alters protein structure, which directly correlates with the observed textural deterioration, such as the loss of water-holding capacity and the eventual softening of the meat (Zhang, Liu, et al., 2024). The strong negative correlation between sulfhydryl loss and carbonyl formation underscores the progressive nature of protein oxidation in chilled chicken breast. This extent of protein oxidation is comparable to values reported for chilled broiler breast after 7–9 days (Soyer et al., 2010), but higher than those observed in some indigenous breeds with stronger antioxidant capacity (Wang et al., 2024). The discrepancy may be attributed to Shimen Sanhuang chicken's higher intramuscular fat content, which can generate reactive carbonyl species that accelerate protein carbonylation. Moreover, the MDA value increased significantly (p < 0.05) from 0.29 mg MDA/kg on day 0 to 0.98 mg MDA/kg on day 8. This value remains below the generally accepted threshold for rancid off-odor in poultry (1.0–2.0 mg MDA/kg), indicating that lipid oxidation, while significant, was not the dominant spoilage driver compared to proteolysis. Lipid oxidation not only contributes to the development of rancid off-flavors and odors, which negatively impact sensory quality, but it can also promote protein oxidation, as the reactive carbonyl species from lipid peroxidation can attack amino acid side chains (Lund, Heinonen, Baron & Estévez, 2011). The concurrent progression of lipid and protein oxidation, as evidenced by the rising MDA and carbonyl values, suggests a potential synergistic interaction that accelerates the overall quality deterioration of chilled chicken breast.

Changes of TVB-N and biogenic amines during cold storage of chicken breast and their potential as indicators of quality deterioration

As shown in Fig. 1, TVB-N, which measures the release of basic nitrogenous compounds from protein degradation, serves as a classic indicator of meat freshness. The TVB-N value in this study exhibited a steady and significant (p < 0.05) increase from an initial 8.12 mg/100 g to 34.48 mg/100 g on day 8. According to regulatory standards in many countries, a TVB-N value exceeding 15–20 mg/100 g often indicates spoilage for poultry meat. Our results show that the sample surpassed this threshold between days 4 and 6, coinciding with the period of rapid BA accumulation. The increase in TVB-N is primarily a result of the deamination of amino acids and the breakdown of nucleotides by spoilage bacteria (Zhang, Yang, et al., 2024). The strong correlation observed between the rising TVB-N and BAI values confirms that microbial proteolysis is a central driver of quality deterioration in chilled Shimnen Sanhuang chicken breast.

Biogenic amines are crucial indicators of microbial activity, resulting from the decarboxylation of amino acids by spoilage bacteria. In the present study, the type and concentration of BAs changed dramatically throughout storage, See Table 2. Spermidine, a naturally occurring amine in muscle tissues, showed fluctuating levels but no consistent increasing trend. In stark contrast, the spoilage-associated BAs, namely putrescine, cadaverine, and tyramine, were undetectable at day 0 but emerged and increased exponentially (p < 0.05) as storage time progressed. Their concentrations remained low until day 4 but surged dramatically thereafter. By day 8, the levels of putrescine, cadaverine, and tyramine had reached 34.14 mg/kg, 65.05 mg/kg, and 24.43 mg/kg, respectively. Histamine, an amine of particular concern due to its potential health risks, was only detected at the end of the storage period (day 8), albeit at a relatively low concentration (1.69 mg/kg). This sequential appearance suggests a specific succession of decarboxylase-positive microorganisms, with histamine-producing strains becoming dominant only in the advanced stages of spoilage. The Biogenic Amine Index (BAI), which integrates the concentrations of key BAs, provides a comprehensive measure of the overall BA accumulation. The BAI showed a remarkable and significant (p < 0.05) increase from 0 on day 0 to 125.35 on day 8. This over 15-fold increase underscores the intense microbial metabolic activity in the later stages of storage, See Fig. 2. The rapid rise in BAI after day 4 strongly correlates with the exponential growth phase of the specific spoilage organisms, making it a highly sensitive chemical index for evaluating the extent of chicken spoilage (Uhlig et al., 2024). Dynamic changes of microbial community during chilled storage

Conversely, the relative abundance of Firmicutes increased from 12.55% (S0) to a peak of 45.99% (S2), before declining to 19.29% (S8). This pattern suggests that certain cold-tolerant members of Firmicutes (e.g., specific lactic acid bacteria and Bacillus spp.) were able to adapt and proliferate in the early storage phase. However, their competitive advantage appeared to wane in the later stages as Pseudomonadota became overwhelmingly dominant. In contrast, the abundances of Bacteroidota and Actinobacteriota gradually diminished from initial levels of 4.44% and 1.24% to negligible levels by day 8, indicating their minor role in the spoilage microbiota under these storage conditions.

A more nuanced understanding was gained from genus-level analysis, which identified ten dominant genera whose dynamics varied significantly across storage time. Pseudomonas emerged as the key spoilage organism, with its relative abundance skyrocketing from 18.37% (S0) to 61.74% (S8). This is consistent with its known characteristics as a psychrotrophic, aerobic bacterium capable of producing extracellular proteases and lipases at low temperatures, leading to slime formation, off-odors, and texture softening (Pellegrini et al., 2023).

Other notable genera included Staphylococcus, which increased from 9.47% (S0) to 40.08% (S2) before decreasing to 16.68% (S8), and Acinetobacter, which decreased from 30.09% (S0) to 6.06% (S2) and then partially recovered to 12.20% (S8). The rapid decline of Psychrobacter from 23.08% (S0) to near-undetectable levels (0.04% at S2) highlights the competitive microbial succession during storage. Additional spoilage-related genera such as Shewanella, Carnobacterium, and Weisella were also detected.

The spoilage potential of these microorganisms is profound. While Pseudomonas dominates aerobic spoilage, Staphylococcus can thrive in lower-oxygen niches and contribute to spoilage through enzymatic activity. The synergistic interactions between Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, and Lactobacillus likely accelerate quality deterioration, leading to the rapid increase in TVB-N, off-flavors, and overall shelf-life reduction. The succession pattern observed—culminating in the dominance of Pseudomonas—definitively identifies it as the Specific Spoilage Organism (SSO) for chilled Shimnen Sanhuang chicken breast under the studied conditions.(See Table 3).

Evolution of volatile flavor compounds during chilled storage

Based on a two-dimensional gas chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC-QTOF) platform and the NIST20 database, 83 volatile compounds were successfully identified in chicken breast samples from five different storage periods. These compounds included 7 hydrocarbons, 12 alcohols, 6 aldehydes, 3 ketones, 13 esters, 6 indoles and nitrogen-containing organic compounds, 14 alkanes, 11 sulfur-containing compounds, and 11 other volatile compounds.Among these, alcohols, aldehydes, alkanes, hydrocarbons, ketones, and esters are mainly products of lipid oxidation, while indoles, amines (nitrogen-containing organic compounds), and sulfur compounds are products of protein/amino acid degradation, a process influenced by bacterial proteases, decarboxylases, and desulfurases (Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2019).

The dynamic profiles of these VOC classes reveal three distinct phases of spoilage metabolism. Days 0–2: Lipid-derived compounds dominate, reflecting autoxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids initiated during post-mortem handling. The rapid decline of ketones by day 4 (to 6%) may indicate their further metabolism or evaporation. Days 2–6: Aldehydes, particularly hexanal and nonanal, peak at 34% relative abundance by day 4. Aldehydes are secondary lipid oxidation products with low odor thresholds and are known contributors to “painty” and “grassy” off-odors in oxidized meat. Their decline after day 6 suggests further oxidation to carboxylic acids or Schiff base formation with amino groups (Han et al., 2023; Yu & Gu, 2025). Days 6–8: Protein-derived volatiles like indoles, sulfur compounds emerge sharply, reaching 17–20% of total volatiles. Indole, a tryptophan metabolite with an intense fecal odor, is rarely detected in fresh meat but becomes prominent under high Pseudomonas loads. Sulfur-containing compounds e.g., dimethyl disulfide, methanethiol derive from methionine catabolism by pseudomonads and are potent odorants at sub-ppb levels (Hansen et al., 2012). The sequential dominance of lipid-derived then protein-derived volatiles provides a chemical fingerprint of spoilage progression that mirrors the microbiological shift from diverse initial flora to Pseudomonas dominance. Notably, the absence of indole until day 4 suggests that proteolytic volatiles are late-stage markers, whereas aldehydes could serve as mid-stage indicators. This temporal resolution of VOC classes is a key contribution of the present study, as most previous work has reported only endpoint volatile profiles.

Screening of differential volatile flavor compounds during chilled storage

To further identify potential differential compounds in the chicken breast samples, an orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model was used, with 83 volatile compounds as the dependent variables and storage days as the independent variable.As illustrated in Fig. 4B, the OPLS-DA score plot (R2 = 0.95, Q2 = 0.64)demonstrated clear clustering of the five chicken breast sample groups according to storage time, with minimal intra-group variability. Groups A and B (representing earlier storage points) were closely clustered, suggesting relatively similar volatile profiles, while Groups C and D also showed proximity, indicating a transitional phase in flavor compound evolution. The permutation test results in (Supplementary Figure 1; Supplementary Figure 2) show: P = 0.002, indicating that the probability of a randomly permuted model having a Q2 value equal to or greater than that of the original model is only 0.2%, which is far below the significance threshold of 0.05. This demonstrates that the model is highly statistically significant. To identify the compounds that contributed most to these separations, the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) index was used. The VIP value quantifies each variable's contribution to the classification accuracy of the OPLS-DA model, with VIP > 1 generally indicating high importance. As shown in Fig. 4C, fifteen volatile compounds with VIP scores exceeding this threshold were identified. Major contributors included 3,5,5-trimethyl-1-hexene, pentadecanol, indole, hexyl hexanoate, and 4-methyl-1-hexene. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, to further validate the reliability of the VIP values, compounds with VIP values greater than 1 were screened using ROVA values, among which hexanal, 3-octen-1-ol, nonanal, indole, γ-dodecalactone, and phenylethanol all had ROVA values greater than 1, with indole reaching 79.97 and 100 on the fourth and sixth days, respectively.

These differential volatiles can be broadly linked to specific biochemical pathways. Indole, for instance, is a recognized microbial metabolite of tryptophan, often associated with protein spoilage (Li et al., 2024). Similarly, phenylethanol commonly derives from phenylalanine metabolism (Roager & Licht, 2018). Several lipid oxidation products were also among the high-VIP compounds, including aldehydes (e.g., hexanal, nonanal), esters (e.g., hexyl hexanoate, hexyl butyrate), lactones (e.g., γ-dodecalactone, jasmonolactone), and long-chain alcohols (e.g., cyclopentadecanol). The detection of olefins such as trimethylhexene and methylhexene, along with alkane tetradecane, further underscores the role of fatty acid oxidation, cleavage, and modification in defining the spoilage-related volatile profile of chilled chicken breast.

Correlation analysis among quality deterioration indicators, key microbes, and volatile flavor compounds

The development of off-flavors in meat during storage is intrinsically linked to the metabolic activities of spoilage microorganisms. To elucidate these relationships, correlation analyses were performed between the 14 key volatile compounds (VIP ≥ 1) and the dominant microbial genera (top 10 in relative abundance). As depicted in the correlation heatmap (Fig. 5 A), distinct and significant associations were identified. Notably, the genus Pseudomonas exhibited a significant positive correlation with indole and phenylethanol. This finding aligns with established research indicating that certain Pseudomonas species, under specific conditions, are proficient in metabolizing amino acids, leading to the production of indole-related compounds and aromatic alcohols like phenylethanol. Concurrently, the genus Psychrobacter showed a significant positive correlation with hexanal and 1-octen-3-ol, both of which are well-characterized lipid oxidation derivatives. Given that indole itself possesses an intense and often unpleasant fecal odor, its strong association with a dominant spoilage genus underscores its potential as a key contributor to spoilage off-odors. To further decipher the direct impact of volatile compounds on meat quality parameters, we investigated the correlation between the 14 key volatiles (VIP ≥ 1.5) and a suite of physicochemical quality indicators (Fig. 5 B). The results revealed that specific volatiles, namely indole, hexanal, phenylethanol, and 1-octen-3-ol, were significantly correlated with the progression of quality deterioration. In particular, indole and 1-octen-3-ol demonstrated significant positive correlations with pivotal spoilage markers, including protein oxidation, lipid oxidation, TVC, and BAI. This robust correlation network suggests that the accumulation of indole and 1-octen-3-ol is synchronized with the core biochemical and microbiological events driving spoilage. Therefore, these two compounds can be proposed as sensitive molecular markers for predicting quality loss in chilled chicken, serving as early warning signals for both microbial growth and oxidative damage.

While the correlation analyses in Fig. 5A and B demonstrate significant associations among Pseudomonas, specific volatiles, and quality indices, the underlying mechanisms can be inferred from established microbial biochemistry. The strong positive correlation between Pseudomonas and indole (r > 0.85, p < 0.01) is consistent with the known capacity of many pseudomonads to produce indole via tryptophanase, which cleaves tryptophan into indole, pyruvate, and ammonia. The parallel increase in TVB-N and biogenic amines further supports active amino acid catabolism. Similarly, 1-octen-3-ol, has been reported as a major volatile produced by Pseudomonas through the lipoxygenase/hydroperoxide lyase pathway acting on linoleic acid. The significant correlation between 1-octen-3-ol and TBARS (r = 0.79, p < 0.05) suggests that lipid oxidation and microbial enzymatic activity synergistically generate this compound. Moreover, the temporal sequence where protein oxidation markersand BAI increase prior to the peak of Pseudomonas abundance implies that initial protein degradation creates a nutrient-rich environment that favors Pseudomonas proliferation, which in turn accelerates further proteolysis and off-flavor production. This positive feedback loop, rather than a simple one-way causality, explains the exponential deterioration observed in late storage.

Conclusion

The study provides a comprehensive characterization of the spoilage dynamics of Shimen Sanhuang chicken breast during 8 days of chilled storage (4 °C) through an integrated multi-omics approach. Quality deterioration was predominantly driven by the synergistic interplay between oxidative modifications of proteins and lipids and the metabolic activity of a dynamic microbial community. Microbial profiling identified Pseudomonas as the dominant specific spoilage organism (SSO), whose late-stage proliferation was closely associated with pronounced accumulation of TVB-N, biogenic amines, and off-flavor volatiles such as indole and phenylethanol. Strong positive correlations between key volatiles—including indole and 1-octen-3-ol—and fundamental spoilage metrics underscore their potential as sensitive early-warning biomarkers. These findings elucidate the mechanistic basis of off-flavor formation and provide a detailed map of the biochemical and microbial networks underlying spoilage. Importantly, they highlight practical molecular targets for the monitoring and control of quality deterioration in high-value indigenous chicken breeds. Future research should focus on the development of rapid detection methods for these biomarkers and targeted antimicrobial strategies against Pseudomonas to extend shelf-life and ensure product safety and quality. Collectively, this work advances both fundamental understanding and practical approaches for preserving chilled poultry quality.
