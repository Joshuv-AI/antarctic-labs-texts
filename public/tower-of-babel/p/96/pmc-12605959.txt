Surrogate Endpoints in Pivotal Clinical Trials for Drug Approval in Japan Compared to the United States
Authors: Yuko Yamamoto, Harumi Kosaka, Hanami Nojiri, Hideki Maeda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRegulatory guidance documents exist on surrogate endpoints in the United States. In Japan, there are no established rules or guidance regarding the use of surrogate endpoints, and various aspects remain unclear. The aim of this study was to investigate the use of surrogate endpoints in Japan for drugs approved in Japan from 1999 to 2022, referring to the list established by the Food and Drug Administration. Precisely 2307 drugs were approved in Japan during the 24‐year survey period. Of these, 1012 drugs (43.9%) were indicated for diseases for which surrogate endpoints were specified in the Surrogate Endpoint Table established by the Food and Drug Administration. After examining the endpoints used in clinical trials for 1012 drugs, 947 (93.6%) were approved based on the same surrogate endpoint as the Food and Drug Administration, whereas 65 (6.4%) were approved based on a different surrogate endpoint. In areas such as diabetes, there was a tendency to use surrogate endpoints established by the Food and Drug Administration, whereas for pharmaceuticals targeting pathogenic organisms, Japan's surrogate endpoints were typically used. Our findings demonstrate that several pharmaceuticals in Japan use surrogate endpoints similar to those of the Food and Drug Administration. These findings are expected to aid in the formulation of guidelines for the use of surrogate endpoints in clinical trials for future drug approvals in Japan.

Introduction

Innovative medicines that are expected to be highly effective must be made available to patients as quickly as possible [1]. The drug development process is lengthy, and the most time‐consuming and costly part of it is the clinical trial process [2, 3]. Efficacy endpoints are usually set in clinical trials, taking into account the characteristics of the disease. Although it would be preferable to establish the endpoint (TEP) for the disease in a clinical trial, it may be necessary to designate a surrogate endpoint (SEP) based on factors such as time, incidence frequency, feasible number of cases, among others [4, 5, 6]. The existence of SEPs that serve as substitutes for the TEP relevant to the disease can expedite the completion of clinical trials, resulting in earlier approval and enabling patients earlier access to new drugs [7, 8]. SEPs are endpoints intended to serve as surrogates for TEPs or clinical endpoints and to predict clinical benefits and risks [9, 10]. For example, in cancer clinical trials, overall survival is the TEP, but conducting clinical trials using response rate, which is a SEP, enables clinical trials to be completed in a shorter period of time, new drugs to be approved earlier, and patients to have earlier access to new drugs [11, 12]. Furthermore, since validated SEPs may be more readily measurable or occur more frequently than TEPs, the existence thereof may make clinical trial objectives easier to achieve, shorter in duration, and smaller in scale, potentially benefiting patients [13]. In this context, it can be inferred that using SEPs can yield various benefits.

In light of the above, requests for the use of SEPs have been made both by pharmaceutical organizations and by patient groups. In Japan, the use of SEPs is being considered in the “Toward the Creation of Innovative Medicines” initiative [14], but no notable results have been achieved yet. Nonetheless, some studies exist on SEPs and validation at the time of applying for approval in the field of anticancer drugs [5, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. There is a lack of comprehensive research in other fields. Additionally, there are regulatory guidance documents that can guide clinical trial sponsors in using SEPs who seek approval of novel therapeutics in the United States. There is a list of SEPs that can be used when applying for drug approval (SEP Table) [20] that is updated regularly. However, in Japan, there is currently no established list or guidance regarding the use of SEPs. Instead, discussions are based on past practices and consultations between regulatory authorities at the time of development of individual drugs, resulting in a situation that lacks transparency, universality, and academic merit. There is a need for further consideration and guidance regarding SEPs.

A comprehensive investigation is necessary regarding the use of SEPs in the history of approvals in Japan. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate and examine the current state of drug approval through clinical trials using SEPs for approved drugs and to investigate the circumstances under which SEPs are used for approval. Furthermore, the status of SEP use was compared with the US FDA SEP Table.

Materials and Methods

Selection of Drugs and Database for Research

In this study, we investigated prescription drugs approved in Japan over a 24‐year period from January 1999 to December 2022. We included initial New Drug Applications as New Molecular Entities, as well as supplemental NDAs for additional indications.

Data Collection

Data were collected from publicly available databases on the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) website (http://www.pmda.go.jp/english/index.html) and Drugs@FDA (https://www.fda.gov/drugs).

Data Abstraction

This study was prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline [21] for cross‐sectional studies. Diseases and drugs were classified by therapeutic effect according to the therapeutic group table [22]. For endpoint evaluation, only one pivotal trial (the largest or most relevant to the initial indication) was considered for each indication. Additionally, when the primary study endpoint was not clearly articulated and multiple endpoints met statistical criteria, the endpoint with the highest priority was taken for this analysis. We also cross‐referenced each disease and drug to determine whether it was listed in the FDA Surrogate Endpoint Table [20]. The classification of special regulatory pathways followed in Japan has been published in our previous report [23] and the terminology of therapeutic areas were based on the guidance for the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications of Japan [24].

Statistical Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to characterize the sample of new drugs and their indications. We used chi‐square tests to analyze the data. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using JMP Pro ver15.

Ethical Considerations

This study did not require institutional review board approval or informed consent because it was based on publicly available information and involved no patient records.

Results

Surveyed Drugs

A total of 2307 pharmaceutical products were approved in Japan during the 24‐year survey period (1999–2022). Of these, 1012 drugs (43.9%) were indicated for diseases for which a SEP was specified in the Surrogate Endpoint Table established by the FDA (Indication‐FDA). Investigation of the endpoints (EP) used in clinical trials for 1012 drugs revealed that in Japan, 947 drugs (93.6%) were approved using the same endpoint as listed in the table of surrogate endpoints by the FDA (hereinafter referred to as EP‐SEP), whereas 65 drugs (6.4%) were approved using a different endpoint from those listed in the table of surrogate endpoints by the FDA (hereinafter referred to as EP‐nSEP) (Figure 1). The proportion of drugs using the same SEP as the FDA (EP‐SEP) was significantly higher for metabolic drugs, compared with that using a different SEP (EP‐nSEP) across all therapeutic categories. Conversely, drugs against pathogenic organisms showed significantly fewer EP‐SEPs and a higher incidence of EP‐nSEPs. Among the 65 drugs, “unique SEP,” or those using a SEP specific to Japan, accounted for the largest number at 29 drugs (44.6%), followed by “TEP,” or those utilizing a true endpoint, at 20 drugs (30.8%), and then “creating a unique usefulness evaluation,” or those for which a unique usefulness evaluation was created in clinical trials, at 12 drugs (18.5%).

A representative example of a “unique SEP” is found in asthma, where the FDA designates “forced expiratory volume in 1 s” as the SEP, whereas in Japan, “peak expiratory flow” is used instead. In one example of “creating an independent usefulness evaluation,” the FDA defines “periodontal pocket depth” as the criterion for periodontal disease, whereas in Japan, “effectiveness” is used.

Discussion

In the US, the public posting of a SEP table for both reasonably likely and validated SEPs was mandated by the 21st Century Cures Act of 2016 and was achieved in July 2018 [25]. As part of the FDA's commitment to transparency, the SEP table is updated every 6 months to reflect current thinking and fulfill the legislative mandate. The SEP table enables stakeholders to determine which SEPs might reasonably be used for marketing applications, thereby enabling transparency and expediting future drug development [20].

In Japan, SEPs have naturally played an important role as endpoints in clinical trials when approving pharmaceuticals. For example, hypertension and hyperlipidemia are typical disease conditions that utilize SEPs [26, 27]. However, in Japan, little guidance has been published on whether SEPs can be used in clinical trials at the time of drug approval. During the actual drug development process, the appropriateness of using SEPs in clinical trials is confirmed during individual consultations with the PMDA. In Japan, there are no SEP tables or standards similar to those of the FDA regarding the conditions of SEP use, and the situation lacks transparency. We propose that a SEP table, comparable to the resource provided by the FDA, would be beneficial for the drug development and regulatory agency in Japan. Furthermore, the status and trends of SEP usage at the time of drug approval have been unclear. In this context, this study is the first to comprehensively investigate the use of SEPs in Japan to date.

The findings of this study showed that, of the drugs approved in Japan, just under half (1012 drugs [43.9%]) are indicated for diseases for which a SEP has been specified in the FDA's Surrogate Endpoint Table (Indication‐FDA), and of these, most (947 drugs [93.6%]) use the same SEP as the FDA (EP‐SEP). Due to recent globalization in pharmaceutical development, it has been suggested that Japan may be participating in international joint clinical trials with Europe and the United States, or that clinical trials may use the same endpoints as those in Europe and the United States. Our findings may suggest that the SEP established by the FDA has been endorsed by the PMDA, Japan's regulatory authority. There were 65 drugs (6.4%) that used a different SEP than that of the FDA (EP‐nSEP); however, annual trends revealed a decreasing trend of EP‐nSEP use in 2016–2022. Among metabolic drugs, all diabetes drugs used the same SEP as the FDA (EP‐SEP), and a significant difference against non‐diabetes drugs was observed. Evidently, setting glycated hemoglobin as the endpoint has become the standard in the field of diabetes in both Japan and the United States. However, for medications against pathogenic organisms, there was a tendency to use unique SEPs, especially in dental preparations. This could be attributed to the fact that previous clinical trials in the dental field often used usefulness assessments, such as the assessment of overall improvement [28], resulting in a significantly higher rate of EP‐nSEP. Additionally, among the special regulatory pathways, publicly known applications include application documents based on approval and usage records overseas, which may explain why they used the same SEP as the FDA (EP‐SEP).

Based on these results, it can be inferred that, although Japan does not have an SEP table like the FDA's, many pharmaceuticals use SEPs similar to those of the FDA. Regarding the use of SEPs in Japan, the design of clinical trials is currently agreed upon through individual consultations with the PMDA. However, by creating SEP guidance and tables in Japan as well, transparency, universality, and scientific validity would be improved, leading to increased predictability of development and approval. Furthermore, we believe that the results of this study will be useful in formulating guidance for SEPs for clinical trials when obtaining future drug approvals in Japan.

There are several challenges regarding the use of SEPs. For example, Wallach et al. showed that, for more than half of the SEPs used as primary endpoints in clinical trials supporting FDA approval of drugs for the treatment of chronic non‐oncology diseases, no meta‐analysis had been published examining the relationship between treatment efficacy evaluated using SEPs and clinical outcomes, and many lacked evidence of an association with clinical outcomes [29]. It has also been reported that drugs that received accelerated approval using SEPs did not perform well in subsequent clinical trials using the TEP [5, 30]. To resolve these issues, it is imperative that evidence for drugs approved through clinical trials using SEPs be promptly and transparently made available to the public, both pre‐ and post‐marketing.

This study had the following limitations: First, it covered only drugs that have been approved in Japan and did not include unapproved drugs or those only approved in the United States. Second, diseases and drugs were selected based on the FDA's Surrogate Endpoint Table, and SEPs for other diseases and drugs were not considered.

In conclusion, we compared the use of SEPs in 2307 drugs approved in Japan between 1999 and 2022 with the SEP table used in the FDA. Of the 2307 drugs approved over the 24‐year period, 1012 drugs (43.9%) were indicated for diseases for which SEPs were specified in the FDA Surrogate Endpoint Table (Indication‐FDA). Of these, 947 drugs (93.6%) were approved based on the same surrogate endpoint as the FDA (EP‐SE), whereas 65 drugs (6.4%) were approved based on a different surrogate endpoint than the FDA (EP‐nSEP). Particularly in the diabetes area, all drugs were approved based on EP‐SEP. Although there is no SEP table similar to that of the FDA in Japan, it has been observed that the approval of several drugs in the country is influenced by SEPs similar to those used by the FDA.
