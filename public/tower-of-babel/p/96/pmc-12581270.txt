Prevalence and distribution of surgical site infection in Cape Coast Teaching Hospital, Ghana
Authors: Kojo Bawuah Afran-Okese, Emmanuel Kobina Tabi Asidel-Asiedu, Martin T. Morna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the advent of medicine, surgical interventions have become the mainstay of survival and interventions for people’s health conditions. As the occurrence of trauma, cancer, and other related organ-specific diseases continues to increase, the effect of surgical procedures on patients continues to increase. Most commonly, surgical therapies are the only therapies that can be lifesaving or reducing imminent threats to patients. Many patients undergo surgical treatment due to conditions such as trauma, pregnancy-related issues such as caesarean sections, and removal of malignant cancers. Although surgery is intended to be lifesaving, unsafe surgical procedures can result in serious injuries. One of the most common surgical effects is surgical site infection, the implications for which must be examined. The objective of this study was to ascertain the prevalence and distribution of surgical site infections in Cape Coast Teaching Hospital, as well as the distribution in terms of age, sex, type of surgery, clinical features of SSI cases, and common predisposing organisms.MethodsThe methodology encompasses; Study site/location at Cape Coast Teaching Hospital, cases of pre-operatively prepared patients as subjects for the study, a retrospective study design that will include all patients who had surgery during the periods of the study, data from the local health information and management systems and theatre surgery references were used as part of the tools used for collection of data.ResultThe findings of this study revealed that the overall prevalence of surgical SSIs in the general surgical department of Cape Coast Teaching Hospital is 5.4%, with exploratory laparotomy having the highest SSI rate (41.7%). Patients with clean contaminated wounds developed SSIs after their surgery as compared to those with clean wounds. It was also revealed from the study that males had a higher prevalence of surgical site infection (55.4%) than females. Coagulase-negative S. aureus was the most common pathogen (2.3%).ConclusionThe study provides insight into the prevalence and distribution of surgical site infections at Cape Coast Teaching Hospital, highlighting the most affected age groups, types of surgeries, and commonly isolated pathogens. These findings underscore the need for strengthened infection prevention strategies and improved surgical care practices to reduce the incidence of SSIs.

Background

Despite advancements in prevention, infections at surgical sites remain a significant clinical issue because they are linked to higher rates of death and morbidity and place a heavy burden on healthcare resources and systems. ‘Surgical Site infections also referred to as SSIs are infections that damage the deep tissue or the incisional at the surgical site and usually diagnosed 30 days after surgery (or one year in surgery in which the patient is receiving implants).’. The development of SSIs can prolong hospital stays, increase the cost of care, and even result in patient mortality [1].

SSIs may be classified as deep incisional infections, infections involving organs or body spaces, or superficial infections. This classification is based on the extent of infection and the timing of its occurrence. The diagnosis of SSIs is based on clinical and laboratory findings for each type of surgical infection [2]. Microorganisms responsible for many surgical site infections are a part of the patient’s endogenous flora. Specific microbes vary depending on the type of surgery; Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, Enterococcus spp., and Escherichia coli are the most frequently isolated species [3].

Various factors contribute to the diagnosis of SSIs in most patients undergoing surgery. The most common are bacterial contamination, duration of surgery, and patient’s predisposing health condition [4]. The risk factors associated with SSIs can be patient-related, procedure-related, or both. In many cases, multiple factors are associated with SSIs. Patient-related risk factors for SSI include existing infections, low blood albumin levels, advanced age, obesity, smoking, diabetes, and ischemia resulting from vascular disease or radiation. Additionally, surgical risk factors include long procedures, inadequate surgical scrubbing, and skin preparation [5]. Physiological conditions, such as trauma, shock, blood transfusion, hypothermia, hypoxia, and hyperglycemia, also increase the risk of SSI. Other independent factors associated with higher SSI risk include abdominal surgery, dirty or contaminated operations, and more than three diagnoses upon discharge, which may indicate infection [5].

SSIs are considered the most preventable health care-associated infections (HCAIs) [6]. Preoperative preparation has been shown to prevent SSIs and facilitate a safe surgical experience for patients. Pre-operative preparation included hand hygiene, preoperative patient skin antisepsis, and antimicrobial irrigation. This often occurs before the surgical procedure [7]. It includes both physical and psychological preparation. Physical preparation for surgery as an intervention for medical conditions is very important for the prevention of surgical site infections. Poor physical preparation has a negative outcome on surgery, including longer hospital stays and poorer outcomes with added risk of nosocomial infections, which are as deadly as the failure to perform the intervention. With this impact of preoperative conditions, it is important that people in the surgical team gain a better understanding of how to prepare their patients preoperatively for better postoperative outcomes [8].

In sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence of SSI is 16%, ranging from 6.8 to 26% [9]. The prevalence of SSIs has been found to be higher in Africa than in developed countries owing to factors such as poor infrastructure, limited resources, and poor infection control practices [10]. Although there are limited data on the exact prevalence of SSIs in Ghana, a study found that the overall incidence of SSIs was 11.4%, with higher rates observed in patients who underwent emergency surgery and those who had a longer duration of surgery [11]. According to Mohan et al. [12], the overall prevalence of SSIs was 7.2%, with higher rates observed in patients who had undergone abdominal surgery.

Recent studies have reported a decline in the prevalence of SSIs, which may be attributed to the implementation of infection control measures and improved surgical practices [10]. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of SSIs in Cape Coast Teaching Hospital, determine the surgical procedures associated with SSIs, and determine the main risk factors for the development of SSIs. This could provide a roadmap for a comprehensive surgical site infection program in Ghana.

Design and method

This study focuses on understanding the prevalence and distribution of surgical site infections (SSIs) among patients who developed SSIs following general surgeries at Cape Coast Teaching Hospital between 2018 and 2021. Using a retrospective design, we specifically targeted hospital records of patients who underwent general surgeries during the period of the study.

This approach allows for a historical analysis of SSI prevalence by examining patient records for the type of surgery, comorbidities, and sex distribution among those who underwent general surgery. Due to the potential for selection and information bias inherent in retrospective designs, efforts were made to ensure data completeness and accuracy by thoroughly reviewing each patient’s records. General surgeries were included, while specialized surgeries (e.g., orthopedic, transplant, caesarean, neurosurgeries) were excluded to create a more homogeneous sample. The findings are thus tailored to the general surgical patient population and may not be generalizable to other surgical settings without additional validation.

This approach allows for an in-depth assessment of SSI occurrences, including analysis of factors such as type of surgery, comorbidities, and sex distribution among the SSI patient group.

SSIs were identified based on diagnostic criteria documented by the hospital, which followed standard infection control protocols.

Identification Process for SSIs: The Cape Coast Teaching Hospital records SSI diagnoses according to clinical guidelines for SSIs, which are based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) definitions. These include signs of infection such as redness, swelling, pain, drainage at the surgical site, or fever occurring within 30 days post-operation. Hospital staff used these criteria to diagnose and document SSIs in patient records, ensuring a standardized approach across cases. For this study, each selected record was verified to ensure it included an official SSI diagnosis documented by hospital staff.

A total of 222 patients who met the inclusion criteria of having underwent general surgeries at the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital, thus comprising our study population. To ensure consistency, specialized surgeries (e.g., orthopedic, transplant, caesarean, neurosurgeries) were excluded due to their distinctive surgical procedures and potential confounding factors unique to those settings. Patients lacking complete records on key study variables were excluded to maintain data reliability.

Definition of variables

Emergency surgery

This type of surgery is done because of an urgent medical condition that needs attention right away. The condition may even be life-threatening. Examples are acute appendicitis and trauma.

Elective surgery

surgery that is scheduled in advance because it does not involve a medical emergency. The scheduling of the surgery will not have impact on the treatment and recovery process.

Clean wounds

wounds that have no signs of inflammation or redness have a minimal chance of resulting in surgical site infections.

Clean contaminated wound

wounds that have no signs of infection at the time of surgery but do involve repairing and removing an internal organ. Examples include surgery on the lungs and appendixes.

Exploratory laparotomy

by definition, an exploratory laparotomy is a laparotomy performed with the objective of obtaining information that is not available via clinical diagnostic methods. It is usually performed in patients with acute or unexplained abdominal pain, in patients who have sustained abdominal trauma, and occasionally for staging in patients with a malignancy.

Data collection

Data was extracted from electronic hospital records to ensure completeness and accuracy, with each record specifically verified for documentation of an SSI diagnosis. Cases lacking critical data on variables related to SSIs (such as type of surgery and comorbidity history) were excluded to maintain data reliability. Additionally, coding was performed to ensure consistency across patient characteristics directly related to SSIs.

Data analysis

SPSS version 20.0 was used to perform both descriptive analyses. Descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions and bar charts, provided an overview of the data. Each variable, including comorbidities and surgery types, was carefully coded to ensure consistency and accuracy in the analysis. Although SPSS version 20 was used, newer versions are recommended for replication to leverage updated statistical tools.

Discussions

The age distribution for this study showed that the majority of study participants were between the ages of 41–60 years closely followed by participants between 19 and 40 years and those above 60 years. This conforms to a study conducted by Junxiu et al. (2014), which revealed that 60% of surgical patients were between the age ranges of 40-50years and 22% were above 50 years [13]. The prevalence of SSI in this study was relatively higher in male patients than in females. This was also consistent with a Tanzanian study, where the prevalence rate was higher in males than females [14]. In contrast to the results of this investigation, other studies detected a statistically significant difference between SSIs rates among males and females [15]. Most surgical patients had some form of education in this study, which is in line with the findings of a study conducted by Omar et al. (2020), which also revealed that most surgical patients have had some form of education [16]. Majority of patients had some form of employment with very few being unemployed [16].

Elective surgery dominated the type of surgery that the participants had. This was consistent with the findings of a different study, which also reported that the majority of participants in their study underwent elective surgeries [16]. Among the patients who underwent emergency surgeries, only a few developed surgical site infections. This finding was consistent with another study, which also revealed that less than 10% of emergency surgical procedures lead to surgical site infections [17]. Fewer patients who underwent elective surgery also developed SSIs. Again, this was confirmed by a study conducted in India, where they found that 5% of elective surgeries lead to SSI and 95% lead to no incidence of SSI [18]. A major predisposition to surgical site infection is the type of wound. This study showed that clean wounds had a minimal chance of resulting in a surgical site. This was confirmed by a finding that was carried out by Mehkla et al. (2019) whose findings were similar to this study [19].

Surgical site infections are more common in patients with comorbidities [20], however, the findings of this study revealed otherwise. The study revealed that diabetes, hypertension, and anemia were the most common comorbidities with patients who developed SSIs. This was supported by a study conducted by Setty et al. (2014), who reported similar findings [20]. ‘Conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and anemia were found to be strongly associated with the development of SSI, most likely due to their negative effects on wound healing. For instance, diabetes can cause vasoconstriction and impair tissue circulation, resulting in local tissue hypoxia’ [16].

The study findings revealed that the most common surgeries performed in the general surgery unit were exploratory laparotomy, hernia repair, appendicectomy, and breast surgeries with exploratory laparotomy, which had the highest incidence of SSIs, followed by appendicectomy and hernia repair. This finding is also consistent with another study, which revealed that the most common surgery performed in the general surgery theatre is exploratory laparotomy and hernia repair [16].

The prevalence rate reported in this study was 5.41%. This finding was supported by a study that also reported that about 300 people used for the study, and 6% of respondents developed SSIs [21]. Coagulase-negative staphylococci were the most common organisms isolated from surgical site infections, followed by E. coli, Enterococcus, and Staphylococcus aureus. However, this finding conflicted with a study by Afriyie (2011), who investigated the environmental factors and bacterial species that cause postoperative wound infections [22].

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings showed that the overall prevalence of surgical site infection in the general surgical ward of Cape Coast Teaching Hospital is 5.6%, with exploratory laparotomy being the type of surgery with the highest SSI rate (41.7%). It was also concluded from the study that males have a high prevalence of surgical site infection (55.4%) than females. Coagulase negative Staphylococcus aureus commonest pathogen (2.3%). Therefore, there should be continually vigorous strategies towards improving the infection prevention practices so that infection rates would be curbed to acceptable levels.

Limitation

We only use descriptive analysis since we were only interested in the prevalence and distribution of SSIs. However, inferential statistical analyses could have allowed us to establish relationships between different variables.

Recommendations

Based on our study findings, we make the following recommendations for consideration in order to help minimize the occurrence of surgical site infections.

There should be adequate identification and treatment of all infections and comorbidities prior to surgery since pre-existing remote body site infection and comorbidity is a contributory factor of occurrence of SSIs.The disease and infection control team should constantly be supplied with the needed logistics so they can ensure proper hygiene at the wards and theatres.As far as practically possible, elective surgeries should be clean surgeries, or site should be rendered very clean before surgeries are done.The laboratories should effectively take samples from surgical sites and properly culture the samples to enable effective identification of pathogens.
