Effect of pregnancy pillow used in the last trimester on sleep and comfort: randomized controlled study
Authors: Betül Uzun Özer, Hava Özkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep disorders are particularly common among primiparous women and in the last trimester of pregnancy. Unfortunately, there is no effective and systematic intervention method. This research was conducted to investigate the effects of pregnancy pillows used in the last trimester on sleep and comfort.MethodsThis study is an experimental randomized controlled trial. All stages of the study were reported and conducted according to CONSORT. The study was also registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with ID: NCT05790018. The study was conducted with two different groups (experimental and control). The study population consisted of primiparous pregnant women attending the outpatient clinic of Amasya Sabuncuoğlu Şerefeddin Training and Research Hospital between May and December 2023. The study was designed as a two-group (experimental and control) study. The sample size was determined using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7), assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.50), a significance level of 0.05, and a power of 80%. Considering possible data loss, the sample size was increased, and data were initially collected from 98 pregnant women. After exclusions, the study was completed with 75 pregnant women (38 in the experimental group and 37 in the control group). Randomization was performed using Random.org. Data were collected using a Personal Information Form, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the Prenatal Comfort Scale (PCS).ResultsThe mean PSQI score values showed a statistically significant difference across groups, regardless of time (p < 0.001). The mean PSQI score in the experimental group was 4.03, while the mean value in the control group was 8.12. A statistically significant difference was found between the mean PSQI score values across time periods, regardless of group (p = 0.039). The mean value obtained in the pretest was 5.57, the mean value obtained in the interim test was 6.04, and the mean value obtained in the posttest was 6.48. Lower PSQI scores indicate better sleep quality. The mean PCS score values showed a statistically significant difference across groups, regardless of time (p<0.001). The mean PCS score value was 65.7 in the experimental group, while the mean value was 50.9 in the control group. No statistically significant difference was found between the mean PCS score values across groups, regardless of time (p=0.100). Higher PCS scores reflect greater perceived comfort.ConclusionCompared with the control group, the experimental group showed significantly lower PSQI scores and higher PCS scores, indicating improved sleep quality and comfort with the use of a pregnancy pillow.Trial registrationThis study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05790018. Background

Sleep, one of the basic daily life activities, is an important factor affecting the quality of life of individuals as well as their health status [1]. The American Sleep Disorders Association reported that sleep patterns change during pregnancy and various sleep disorders occur. These disorders can be mild or severe [2].

Comfort is the state of being stress-free and relaxed in physiological, mental and social terms [3]. Although it is known that almost all women experience deterioration in their comfort and various sleep problems during pregnancy, midwives’ awareness on the subject is not sufficient [4–6]. It is known that deterioration in comfort and sleep problems experienced during pregnancy negatively affect maternal and fetal health and the birth process [7, 8].

Inadequate or disrupted sleep is associated with decreased maternal quality of life, psychological problems, obesity, diabetes, and hypertension; these effects may arise through increased stress hormones, inflammatory processes, and impaired placental function. From a fetal perspective, these processes have been shown to lead to adverse outcomes such as low birth weight and low Apgar scores. Furthermore, it has been reported that impaired sleep quality and comfort during pregnancy negatively affect the birth process; this condition is associated with increased pain perception during labor, slow progress of labor, and increased rates of assisted vaginal delivery and cesarean Sects [7, 8].

Sleep disorders are particularly common among primiparous women. Unfortunately, there is no effective and systematic intervention method [9]. In the literature, a number of interventions have been used to improve the sleep quality of pregnant women, such as evaluating sleep habits, creating nutrition and exercise programs, providing individuals with sleep hygiene, and regulating environmental conditions [10–12].

Today, individuals seeking professional help for sleep disordersare generally offered sleep hygiene recommendations, pharmacotherapy, or psychologicalinterventions [13]. Cognitive Behavioral Therapies are not sufficiently used in clinics due to their high cost orthe insufficient number of trained specialists [14]. The use of sleep medications during pregnancy should be limited due to their potential adverse effects on the fetus, increased risk of recurrent insomnia, and development of dependence [13]. In this context, it is particularly important to investigate effective non-pharmacologicalapproaches for treating pregnant women with impaired sleep quality. In this regard, it seems meaningful to evaluate the usability of the pregnancy pillow due to its low cost.

In a study conducted by Felder et al., pregnant women reported that physical discomfort had negative effects on sleep and requested content containing practical recommendations for methods to reduce physical discomfort [15]. In a study conducted by Mislu et al., a significant decline in sleep quality was observed as the pregnancy progressed. This indicates that women’s sleep quality deteriorates in the later stages of pregnancy. Interventions aimed at improving sleep hygiene, providing supportive prenatal care, and reducing pregnancy-specific physical discomfort may alleviate these problems and contribute positively to maternal and fetal health [16].

The use of a pregnancy pillow in the prenatal period is one of these applications. Using a pregnancy pillow in the third trimester, when sleep problems increase and comfort is negatively affected, can help improve the sleep quality and comfort levels of pregnant women [17]. A studies suggests that reducing physical discomfort and providing support may be important in improving pregnant women’s sleep quality and comfort levels [13, 15, 16]. Therefore, the pregnancy pillow used in the study is believed to provide physical support and reduce discomfort. The aim of this study was to examine the effect of a pregnancy pillow used in the last trimester on sleep and comfort.

Methods

Prenatal comfort was identified as the primary outcome based on its central relevance to the study objective and intervention framework. Sleep quality, assessed using the PSQI, was considered a secondary outcome reflecting an important but complementary aspect of maternal well-being.

Type of research

This study is an experimental randomized controlled trial. All steps of the study were reported and conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) Summary Reporting Guide. In addition, the study was registered on the “ClinicalTrials.gov” site with the ID: NCT05790018.

Place and time of the research

The study was conducted with pregnant women who applied to the Amasya Sabuncuoğlu Şerefeddin Training and Research Hospital polyclinic unit between May and December 2023.

Universe and sample of the research

The study population consisted of primiparous pregnant women who visited the Amasya Sabuncuoğlu Şerefeddin Training and Research Hospital outpatient clinic between May and December 2023. The study was conducted using a two-group (experimental and control) design. The sample size was calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9.7 program, based on a 95% confidence interval, 80% power (1–β), and a 0.05 significance level (α). In line with similar studies in the literature, a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.50) was accepted. The calculation determined that each group should have a minimum of 15 and a maximum of 39 pregnant women. Considering that 10–30% data loss can occur in pregnancy studies, the sample size was increased by 25%, and data were collected from a total of 98 pregnant women. Some pregnant women were excluded from the study for various reasons, and the study was completed with 75 pregnant women (38 experimental, 37 control) (Fig. 1).

Participants and eligibility criteria

The study included primiparous pregnant women who were between the 29th and 30th weeks of gestation, were healthy, and had no diagnosed sleep problems. Pregnant women with multiparity, high-risk pregnancies, or diagnosed sleep disorders were excluded from the study. In addition, participants who chose to withdraw from the study, did not use the pregnancy pillow effectively, were subsequently diagnosed with sleep disorders, or developed conditions that rendered the pregnancy high risk during the study period were excluded from the final analysis.

Randomization and allocation concealment

The unit of randomization was the individual pregnant woman. Randomization was performed using simple randomization via the Random.org website (https://www.random.org). The Random Integer Generator tool under the “Numbers” section was used to generate a single-column list of random integers consisting of the numbers 1 and 2 for all pregnant women who met the sample selection criteria.

Prior to the start of the study, a lottery was conducted to determine group allocation, whereby the number 1 was assigned to the experimental group and the number 2 to the control group. Accordingly, participants were allocated to groups based on the number assigned to them in the randomization list.

In cases where a participant withdrew from the study for any reason, the next eligible pregnant woman on the pre-generated randomization list was assigned to the corresponding group to maintain the predefined allocation sequence.

Allocation concealment was not fully ensured, as the randomization list was accessible during participant enrollment. Therefore, a potential risk of selection bias cannot be excluded.

Blinding of participants and researchers was not feasible due to the physically noticeable nature of the intervention material. This lack of blinding represents a methodological limitation of the study and may have introduced performance or detection bias.

Data collection tools and features

Personal Information Form, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and Prenatal Comfort Scale were used to collect research data.

In this study, sleep quality was operationally defined as the subjective evaluation of sleep characteristics measured using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), where lower total scores indicate better sleep quality. Comfort was defined as the perceived level of physical comfort during pregnancy and was assessed using the Pregnancy Comfort Scale (PCS), with higher scores indicating greater perceived comfort. These definitions formed the basis for the study hypotheses.

Personal information form

This form consists of questions about the introductory characteristics of pregnant women, their sleep duration and pillow usage [18–20].

Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)

PSQI is a scale developed to evaluate the one-month sleep quality of individuals in clinical studies [21]. This scale allows distinctions between “good sleep” and “bad sleep”. The total scale score can be between 0-21. Those with a total scale score of 5 and below are considered to have “good” sleep quality, while those with a score above 5 are considered to have “bad” sleep quality. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of the scale has been reported as 0.804 [21, 22]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was determined as 0.82.

Prenatal comfort scale (PCS)

This scale, which aims to determine prenatal comfort, was developed by Takeishi et al. in Japan in 2011. The short version of the scale consists of 15 items and 5 sub-dimensions. The sub-dimensions are: “The impact of improving relationships with one’s spouse on the fatherhood role - Spouse”, “Interacting with fetal movements - Fetus”, “Social support received from people in the environment - Social Environment”, “Acceptance of the motherhood role and bonding with the baby - Motherhood”, and “Noticing changes in oneself during pregnancy - Pregnancy”. The scale is a 6-point Likert scale. Each item is scored between 0 and 5 (0 = Strongly disagree, 1 = Disagree, 2 = Undecided, 3 = Slightly agree, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly agree). The scale is evaluated out of a total of 75 points. It is interpreted that a lower score indicates a lower level of comfort, and a higher score indicates a higher level of comfort. The scale has no reverse-scoring items or cutoff points. The Cronbach Alpha (α) value of the scale is 0.95 [6]. The Cronbach alpha internal consistency number of the scale, whose Turkish validity and reliability were conducted by Kaya Şenol et al., (2021) [23], is 0.82. In this study, the Cronbach alpha value of the scale was found to be 0.87.

Data collection

The contact information of the pregnant women included in the experimental and control groups through randomization was collected at the hospital. On the same day, at a convenient time, the pregnant woman was visited at her home and the research topic was explained in detail, and verbal and written consent was obtained. The data of the study were collected by the same researcher using the face-to-face interview technique in all 3 follow-ups.

Intervention material (features of pregnancy pillow)

The pregnancy pillow provides support and relief to five different areas of the body simultaneously. It helps protect the health of expectant mothers during pregnancy and reduces neck, abdominal, waist, back, and leg pain. The pillowcases are made of 100% cotton poplin fabric, which is sweat-proof and allergy-free. The pillow is filled with beaded fiber. It maintains its shape, doesn’t become felted, or flattened with use. The zippered cover makes it easy to remove and wash, and just as easy to put back on (Figs. 2 and 3).

When giving pillows to pregnant women, the left lateral position was recommended. They were told they could use the pillow at any time of day or night. No other interventions were performed on the groups other than the pillow. No allergic reactions were reported.

Safety considerations

The use of pregnancy pillows is generally considered a low-risk, non-invasive intervention. In the present study, no adverse events or clinically significant discomfort related to pillow use were reported by participants. Specifically, no complaints regarding musculoskeletal pain, restricted movement during sleep, overheating, or increased nocturnal discomfort were noted.

Nevertheless, individual differences in sleeping posture, body size, and personal comfort preferences may influence tolerance and perceived benefit. Therefore, while the findings support the safety of pregnancy pillow use in this sample, future studies may benefit from systematically monitoring and reporting potential minor discomforts or side effects to further inform clinical recommendations.

Evaluation of data

Data analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics and R software. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range) and baseline comparisons between groups were performed using SPSS. Normality, homogeneity of variance, and outlier assessments were also evaluated in SPSS.

To examine the effects of the intervention over time and between groups, robust mixed-design ANOVA models were conducted in R. This analytical approach was selected due to the feature-specific nature of the data, including deviations from normality, the presence of outliers, and potential heteroscedasticity, which may compromise the validity of conventional repeated-measures ANOVA models. Robust mixed ANOVA models offer improved resistance to assumption violations and allow for more reliable estimation of time, group, and interaction effects under such conditions. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Table 3 demonstrates a statistically significant main effect of group, showing that the experimental group had lower PSQI scores compared with the control group (p < 0.001). A significant main effect of time was also observed (p = 0.039), indicating that PSQI scores increased over time; however, post-hoc pairwise comparisons did not reveal significant differences between individual measurement points. While no significant difference was found between groups at the pre-test, significant differences emerged at the mid-test and post-test, demonstrating that the intervention had a positive effect on sleep quality.

PSQI scores by groups

Table 4 shows a statistically significant main effect of group, indicating that the experimental group had higher PCS scores than the control group (p < 0.001). As higher PCS scores reflect greater perceived comfort, this finding suggests that the experimental group experienced higher comfort levels overall. The main effect of time was not statistically significant (p = 0.100), indicating that PCS scores remained relatively stable across the measurement periods. However, a significant group × time interaction was observed (p < 0.001), demonstrating that the difference between the groups became more pronounced following the intervention. While no significant difference was found between the groups at the pretest, significant differences in favor of the experimental group emerged at the interim and posttest assessments. Overall, these findings indicate that the implemented intervention had a positive effect on comfort levels, particularly evident in the interim and posttest measurements (Table 4). Discussion

Following completion of the data collection phase, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted using the G*Power software. However, post-hoc power analyses should be interpreted with caution, as they are inherently dependent on the observed effect size and p-value and remain subject to methodological debate. Accordingly, the results of this analysis are presented for descriptive purposes only and should not be considered a substitute for an a priori power calculation. When the power analysis was calculated using a 95% confidence interval, a significance level of p = 0.05, and a sample size of 75, the power of the study was determined as 95% and it was concluded that the sample had a good representative power for the universe.

In this study, it was concluded that the pregnancy pillow increases the sleep quality and comfort of pregnant women. The findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of the study population. All observed changes in sleep quality and comfort were obtained from primiparous pregnant women in the third trimester, as this was the only group included in the study. Therefore, the reported improvements reflect the effects of the intervention specifically in primiparous women during late pregnancy.

The average sleep duration of the experimental group using the pregnancy pillow gradually increased in the interim and post-test, while the average sleep duration of the control group gradually decreased in the interim and post-test. Adequate sleep duration is very important for maternal and fetal health, especially during pregnancy. It also plays a role in the healthy completion of pregnancy and birth [1, 7]. The pregnancy pillow used in this study was found to be effective in increasing sleep duration during pregnancy. Therefore, it is thought that sleep duration can be increased with the use of a pregnancy pillow, especially in the third trimester, when sleep problems are more intense.

In this study, it was found that the pregnancy pillow used in the experimental group increased the sleep quality of the pregnant women. The obtained result confirms the hypothesis that “Pregnancy pillow used in the last trimester increases sleep quality”. The results obtained from this study showed that pregnancy pillow can be used as an alternative to increase the sleep quality of pregnant women. There is only one study in the literature using pregnancy pillows. In this study conducted by Thomas and his colleagues in 1989, the effect of the maternity pillow used in the last months of pregnancy on back pain and insomnia was examined and it was determined that the experimental group experienced less back pain and insomnia [17]. More studies are needed using pregnancy pillows.

From a clinical perspective, a reduction of approximately 4 points on the PSQI represents a meaningful improvement in perceived sleep quality. The PSQI is commonly interpreted using a global score threshold of > 5 to indicate poor sleep quality. Therefore, a 4-point decrease may be sufficient to shift some pregnant women from the “poor sleeper” range toward a clinically more acceptable sleep profile or, at minimum, to substantially reduce sleep-related symptom burden.

Practically, such an improvement may correspond to fewer nocturnal awakenings, reduced sleep latency, improved sleep efficiency, and better subjective sleep satisfaction. For clinicians, this magnitude of change suggests that the intervention may offer clinically relevant symptom relief, even if pregnancy-related physiological changes continue to influence overall sleep patterns.

Comfort is one of the key points of the care process during pregnancy and birth [24]. In the literature review, no studies were found examining the relationship between pregnancy pillows and prenatal comfort. In this study, it was found that the pregnancy pillow used in the experimental group in the third trimester increased comfort. This result confirms the hypothesis that “pregnancy pillows used in the last trimester increase comfort”. When experimental studies conducted in the literature to increase the comfort levels of pregnant women were examined, different results were reached [25, 26]. In a randomized controlled study examining the effect of music application on sleep quality and comfort levels in pregnant women, it was found that music had no effect on the comfort levels of pregnant women [26]. In a study conducted by İbici Akça et al., Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction Training was given to pregnant women and it was determined that the program increased the prenatal comfort levels of pregnant women [25]. In this sense, more randomized controlled studies should be conducted in larger sample groups with practices that may affect the comfort levels of pregnant women.

Although the pregnancy pillow was associated with improvements in perceived comfort, the directionality of time effects on sleep quality should be interpreted with caution. Sleep quality, as measured by the PSQI, may naturally deteriorate as pregnancy progresses due to physiological, hormonal, and biomechanical changes, such as increasing abdominal size, nocturnal discomfort, and frequent awakenings.

In this context, the observed time-related worsening in PSQI scores does not necessarily contradict the beneficial effects of the intervention. Rather, the findings suggest that the pregnancy pillow may have mitigated the extent of sleep deterioration over time, compared to what would be expected in the absence of supportive interventions. Therefore, the pillow’s effect should be understood as a moderating or buffering influence rather than a complete reversal of pregnancy-related sleep changes.

Although the group × time interaction indicated a substantial differential pattern between the intervention and control groups, the interpretation of inter-test and post-test comparisons should be approached with caution. Multiple comparisons across time points may increase the risk of type I error, particularly when pairwise contrasts are emphasized.

Therefore, the present findings are best interpreted in terms of overall interaction trends and directional changes over time, rather than isolated between-time-point differences. From this perspective, the results suggest a consistent pattern favoring the intervention group, while acknowledging that specific inter-test or post-test contrasts should not be overinterpreted in isolation.

The findings from this study indicate that non-pharmacological interventions aimed at improving sleep quality during pregnancy are feasible and effective. This highlights the need to integrate sleep quality, a fundamental component of pregnancy care, into midwifery practice. Midwives can provide effective interventions in many areas, such as providing sleep hygiene education, offering psychological support, teaching relaxation exercises, demonstrating appropriate positioning methods, and encouraging the use of low-cost non-pharmacological tools [27]. The low-cost, accessible, and easy-to-use design of the pregnancy pillow used in this study demonstrates its potential as an approach that can be incorporated into midwifery care protocols. Indeed, the literature reports that “sleep management” content has increasingly been included in midwifery education programs in recent years (Banaei). Therefore, it is important to consider sleep interventions not only as therapeutic but also as part of preventive and protective midwifery care.

Limitations and generalizability of the research

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, blinding of participants and researchers was not feasible due to the physically noticeable nature of the pregnancy pillow, which may have introduced performance and detection bias. Although outcome measures were based on validated self-report instruments, participants’ awareness of group assignment may have influenced their responses.

In addition, certain potential confounding factors were not fully controlled, including variations in gestational age progression, pre-existing sleep habits, daily physical activity levels, psychosocial stress, and environmental factors such as bedroom conditions. These uncontrolled variables may have contributed to inter-individual differences in sleep quality and perceived comfort. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should consider incorporating more rigorous control or adjustment for these factors.

Conclusion and recommendations

The pregnancy pillow used in the experimental group increased the comfort and sleep quality of the pregnant women. Considering the effects of sleep problems during pregnancy on maternal and fetal health and therefore the effect on health care costs, the use of a pregnancy pillow, which is a low-cost product, can reduce the financial burden on health care. In this direction, it is recommended to conduct cost-effective research.
