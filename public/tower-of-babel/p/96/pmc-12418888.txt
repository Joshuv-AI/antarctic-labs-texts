Characterization and antibody preparation of the gene products of grouper iridovirus ORF120L
Authors: Chu-Fan Cheng, Hsiang-Chieh Chuang, Yu-Shen Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•GIV-120L exhibited the highest homology with the SGIV-12L gene.•Polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies against GIV-120L were produced.•GIV-120L was confirmed as a late gene.•GIV-120L accumulates at the viral assembly site during late infection stages.Keywords: Grouper iridovirus, GIV-120L, Late gene, Antibody production

Introduction

Grouper (Epinephelus spp.) is one of the most economically valuable aquaculture fishes in many Asian countries. Iridoviruses are among the most significant viral pathogens of grouper (Wei et al., 2020; Chuang et al., 2022). Iridoviruses are large icosahedral DNA viruses with viral particle sizes ranging from 120 to 350 nm in diameter (Reviewed in Jancovich et al., 2024).

Iridoviruses are divided into the Alphairidovirus and Betairidovirus subfamilies. Alphairidoviruses infect primarily invertebrates such as insects and crustaceans, whereas Betairidoviruses infect primarily fish, reptiles, and amphibians. Among the Betairidoviruses, infection by Ranavirus and Megalocystivirus causes high mortality in fish (Jancovich et al. 2011; Chinchar et al. 2017; Jancovich et al., 2024). Originally isolated from grouper, GIV is a member of the genus Ranavirus that has caused significant economic losses to the grouper aquaculture industry (Lai et al., 2000; Murali et al., 2002). The GIV genome has a length of 139,793 bp and a G+C content of 49 %, and is predicted to contain 120 open reading frames (ORFs) ranging from 62 to 1,268 amino acids in length (Tsai et al., 2005; Hu et al., 2015; Chinchar et al., 2017).

Early studies have shown that enveloped viruses enter cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis, whereas non-enveloped viruses initiate infection by binding to the plasma membrane followed by entry of the viral core into the cytoplasm (Braunwald et al., 1985; Gendrault et al., 1981; Liu et al., 2016; Chinchar et al., 2017). Recent studies have proposed viral entry via pinocytosis or caveolin-mediated mechanisms (Guo et al., 2011; Guo et al., 2012; Jia et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2014; Chinchar et al., 2017). Similar to other nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses (NCLDV), the release of the viral DNA core occurs after the interaction between the internal viral membrane and either the plasma membrane, endosomal membrane, or nuclear membrane and the formation of a membrane channel (Mutsafi et al., 2014; Chinchar et al., 2017). Host and viral membrane fusion and channel formation require conformational changes within the capsid that facilitate these interactions. Regardless of the method of entry, viral DNA ultimately enters the nucleus and viral synthesis begins (Chinchar et al., 2017).

Iridovirus genes are expressed in three phases, accompanied by the sequential expression of immediate early (IE), delayed early (DE), and late (L) viral mRNAs (Ince et al. 2013). Following the entry of viral DNA into the nucleus, IE and early genes are transcribed using host RNA polymerase II. Because the naked viral genome is not infectious, one or more virion-associated proteins are required for the initiation of mRNA synthesis (Willis et al., 1979; Willis and Granoff, 1985). Virus-induced/encoded proteins are required for the synthesis of DE and L viral mRNAs (Thompson et al., 1986; Willis and Granoff, 1978). IE gene transcription requires a virion-associated trans-activating protein (VTAP). It is hypothesized that VTAP facilitates the transcription of IE genes with methylated promoters by altering the DNA template or the host RNA polymerase. Early viral gene products (viral DNA polymerase) synthesize DNA molecules between the size of unit and twice the unit length in the nucleus, which are then transported to the cytoplasm. There, they are methylated by cytoplasmic DNA methyltransferases (Goorha, 1982; Goorha and Dixit, 1984). The precise role of cytosine methylation is unknown, but methylation may protect viral DNA from degradation by a virus-encoded restriction enzyme or prevent recognition of viral DNA by Toll-like Receptor (TLR) (Ishii et al., 2007). In the cytoplasm, a second round of DNA synthesis leads to formation of large concatemers comprised of 10 or more molecules of viral DNA. Late gene expression is associated with DNA synthesis and is catalyzed by virus-encoded homologs of RNA polymerase II (vPOL-IIα and vPOL-IIβ). Viral assembly sites develop in the cytoplasm and are the sites of virion morphogenesis (Sample et al., 2007). Assembly site formation requires early viral gene expression and viral DNA synthesis, but not late protein synthesis (Chinchar et al., 1984; Sample et al., 2007). Early genes require very-early non-structural viral proteins that override methylation-induced transcriptional blocks. Early genes encode DNA replication-associated proteins, such as viral DNA polymerases, that promote the replication of viral DNA (Willis et al., 1989). The mechanism of virion assembly is believed to be similar to that of African swine fever virus (ASFV) and other NCLDVs (Milrot et al., 2016; Mutsafi et al., 2014; Suarez et al., 2015). Similarly, membrane fragments derived from the endoplasmic reticulum serve as scaffolds for the deposition of viral capsid and other viral proteins. Subsequently, these sheets of capsid-covered membrane coalesce to form recognizable icosahedral capsids. Although not completely understood, similar to a few other NCLDVs, viral DNA appears to enter nearly completed virions through a portal (Liu et al., 2016). Regardless of the mechanism, DNA packaging results in the incorporation of a full-length genome plus, depending on the virus species, a variable amount of extra genomic DNA (Goorha and Murti, 1982). As a result, virions contain linear, circularly permuted, and terminally redundant genomes. Mature virions have been detected within assembly sites, paracrystalline arrays, and budding from the plasma membrane (Goorhai and Dixit, 1984; Goorha, 1982, 1984; Lin et al., 2014; Hu et al., 2015; Jeudy et al., 2020; Jancovich et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2024).

We previously established seven GIV-sensitive cell lines derived from yellow grouper (Epinephelus awoara) (Lai et al., 2003) as well as two cell lines derived from golden grouper (Lates calcarifer) in which GIV induced apoptosis (Lai et al., 2008). These cell lines have proven valuable for the study of GIV-induced cell death (Pham et al., 2012). Antibodies are effective tools for diagnosis (Hu et al., 2015; Singh et al., 2018; Bayer, 2019) and characterization (Shi et al., 2003; Lin et al., 2014) of viral pathogen infections. Originally developed by Kohler and Milstein (1975), monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are currently used in a variety of academic, medical, and commercial applications (Carter et al., 1992; Waldmann, 2003; Hudis, 2007). The development and use of viral gene product-specific monoclonal antibodies has led to a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms of GIV pathogenicity (Yeh et al., 2008). To date, many viral genes have not yet been investigated.

GIV-120L, a late gene specific to Ranavirus, was selected in the present study due to the limited understanding of the viral assembly mechanisms associated with late genes. Unknown viral genes were predicted using bioinformatics, and specific antibodies were used for preliminary characterization. The findings of this study are expected to improve our understanding of the processes involved in viral assembly and facilitate the development of valuable tools for the diagnosis of GIV and further studies of its pathogenicity.

Materials and methods

Sequence analysis

GIV-120L gene homology analysis was performed using the NCBI website (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) as described by Tsai et al. (2005) (Table 1). DNA sequences were converted to amino acid sequences using ExPASy (http://www.expasy.org). The amino acid sequences were then analyzed for conserved domains and motifs using SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/). The results were subjected to analysis using the basic local alignment search tool (BLAST) in NCBI. The results were organized into a table and the sequences were downloaded from the EMBL-EBI website (http://www.ebi.ac.uk). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using Clustal Omega, and the alignment results were then edited using GeneDoc (http://genedoc.software.informer.com). Phylogenetic tree analysis of the alignment results was performed using MEGA 6 (http://www.megasoftware.net).

Cells and viruses

Grouper kidney (GK) cells (Lai et al., 2000) were cultured in Leibovitz's L-15 medium (1 bag/L L-15 powder, 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum, 1 % (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin) (Gibco, USA) at 28 °C. GIV purification and culture were performed using GK cells as previously described (Lai et al., 2000).

Expression vector construction

The complete ORFs of GIV-120L were amplified from GIV genomic DNA by PCR amplification using oligonucleotide primers containing previously designed restriction sites. GIV 120L primers (Table 2): Forward: 5′-CCGGAATTCATGGAAGCCGCCGCAAACGT-3′ (EcoRI restriction site underlined); reverse: 5′-CCGCTCGAGAAACCCGAGCGCGGACAAGA-3′ (XhoI restriction site underlined). PCR amplification was performed using a PCR cycler (Bio-Rad®, USA). Reaction conditions: 95 °C for 5 min, 35 cycles of denaturation (95 °C for 45 s), annealing (60 °C for 45 s), and elongation (72 °C for 1 min), followed by 72 °C at 10 min. This PCR product is a GIV-120L gene fragment measuring 1,470 bp in length (Supplementary Figure S1). An expression vector was constructed from the gene fragment and the pET23a plasmid and transformed into E. coli DH5α (DE3) cells. Plasmid DNA was extracted after liquid culture and digested with restriction enzymes to obtain two fragments, namely pET23a (∼3666 bp) and GIV-120L (∼1,470 bp) (Supplementary Figure S2). The successfully constructed vector was sequenced, and the sequence was searched in the EMBL-EBI website and the NCBI GenBank database. The results showed that the constructed pET23a-GIV-120L vector contained the GIV-120L sequence.

Preparation of recombinant protein

The pET23a-GIV-120L vector was transformed into E. coli Rosetta (DE3) cells. 500 μL of 1 M IPTG (isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside, Sigma, Germany) was added to E. coli Rosetta (DE3) bacterial broth containing the pET23a-GIV-120L vector and incubated at 37 °C for 20 h. Next, the bacterial broth was centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. The lysis buffer A containing 0.01 M Tris-base (J.T. Baker, USA), 0.1 M sodium dihydrogen phosphate (NaH2PO4) (Sigma, Germany), and 6 M guanidine-HCl (Sigma, Germany) at pH 8.0 was added to the liquid. The mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The insoluble cell debris was then discarded. The supernatant was then added directly to a Ni-NTA His•Bind® Resins column (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, USA) and washed with lysis buffer A containing imidazole (1, 5, 10, and 200 mM). Regeneration buffer (0.05 M EDTA, 0.5 M NaCl, pH 8.0) was added, and the eluate was collected and analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Bio-Rad®, USA). Samples were pooled and dialyzed in S100 buffer (0.2 mM EDTA, 1 mM glycerol, 25 mM HEPES, 100 mM KCl, 20 % glycerol, pH 7.9), incubated at 4 °C the following evening, and then subjected to protein electrophoresis. 10 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA) was added to dH2O and diluted to concentrations of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, and 1.4 mg/mL. A 5 μL volume of each diluted solution was transferred to a 96-well plate. Each concentration was set up in triplicate, yielding the protein standard set. In addition, the sample (recombinant protein) was diluted 1–4-fold. A 5 μL volume of diluted solution was transferred to a 96-well plate. Each concentration was set up in triplicate, yielding the sample set. Lastly, 250 μL of Bradford reagent was added and mixed by shaking for 30 s, and the mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature (25 °C) for 20 min, away from light. The absorbance of the mixture at 595 nm was measured using an ELISA reader. The absorbance of each diluted solution in the standard set was used to construct the standard curve (y = ax + b, R2 > 0.995). Next, the absorbance from the sample set (y) was substituted into the equation of the standard curve, the concentrations of the sample set (x) were determined, and the 0.1–1.4 mg/mL diluted solutions were used to determine the undiluted concentrations of the sample set (x * dilution factor), yielding the concentrations of recombinant protein.

Antibody preparation

Immunization of mice

Pathogen-free 12-week-old BALB/c mice were purchased from the National Laboratory Animal Breeding and Research Center, National Science Council, Taiwan. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Ethics Review Committee for Animal Experiments at National Ilan University. Recombinant protein solution (100 μg) was mixed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) to a total volume of 150 μL and loaded into a 5 mL syringe. Next, 150 μL of Freund’s complete adjuvant (Sigma, Germany) was added to the solution. This mixture was injected into the abdominal cavity of the mice. A second dose of recombinant protein mixed with Freund's incomplete adjuvant (Sigma, Germany) was administered 2 days later, a third dose was administered 14 days later, and so on until the fifth dose. Blood was collected via the tail vein at 3–4 days after the administration of the fifth dose, allowed to stand for 1 h at room temperature, and centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as serum and subjected to western blotting to confirm the efficacy of the polyclonal antibodies in the serum.

Hybridoma preparation

Mice were sacrificed, and the spleen was harvested and transferred to a 10-cm dish containing 30 mL Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (1 bag/L DMEM powder (Gibco, US), 3.7 g/L sodium bicarbonate (Sigma, Germany), >1 mM sodium pyruvate (Sigma, Germany), 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen, US), and 1 % (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, US), pH 7.4) at 37 °C. After the spleen was completely dissociated in DMEM, the mixture was allowed to stand for 5 min and the supernatant was collected. Splenocytes were counted and centrifuged at 180 × g for 8 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the splenocytes were resuspended in 5 mL of serum-free DMEM. Mouse Sp2/O myeloma cells were counted, one-fifth of the total splenocytes were centrifuged at 180 × g for 8 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the myeloma cells were resuspended in 5 mL of serum-free DMEM by pipetting. The resuspended myeloma cells were transferred to a centrifuge tube containing the splenocytes, mixed, and centrifuged again at 180 × g for 8 min, and part of the supernatant was discarded. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) solution (Sigma, Germany) was added at a rate of 100 μL/min. PEG (700 μL) was added with continuous rotation for 1 min, followed by 2 mL of serum-free DMEM within 2 min, followed by 7 mL of serum-free DMEM within another 2 min. Lastly, the cells were centrifuged at 180 × g for 8 min, the supernatant was discarded, the cells were resuspended in 10 mL of DMEM-HAT (20 g/L hypoxanthine-aminopterin-thymidine), and an additional 20 mL of DMEM-HAT was added to the cells by pipetting and mixed. Next, the hybridoma cells were evenly distributed into a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 4 days. 100 μL/well DMEM-HAT was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 3 days. The growth of the cells was observed and successfully fused hybridomas were selected. The medium in the wells were collected for western blot analysis. After confirming that antibodies were secreted by the cells in the wells, the cells were transferred to a 75T flask for expansion culture.

Western blot analysis

SDS-PAGE analysis of samples was performed in duplicate, with one gel used for Coomassie blue staining and the other used for western blot analysis. After transferring to a PVDF membrane, the membrane was removed with forceps and immersed in 5 % (w/v) skim milk/PBST-20 (PBS containing 0.1 % (v/v) Tween-20) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The 5 % (w/v) skim milk/PBST-20 solution was discarded and the membrane was washed three times with PBST-20. Next, the membrane was treated with primary antibodies by incubating with a 1:5,000 dilution of mouse serum/PBST-20 solution for 1 h, and the membrane was washed three times with PBST-20. The membrane was then treated with secondary antibodies by incubating with a 1:5,000 dilution of goat anti-mouse IgG-AP/PBST-20 solution for 1 h, and the membrane was washed three times with PBST-20. Lastly, 1 mL of NBT/BCIP substrate system was added for color development. The membrane was washed in PBST-20 after the appearance of bands, air dried at room temperature, and then imaged using a gel documentation system.

Transcriptome and proteome analysis

Infection

The 5 × 105 GK cells were inoculated in a 6-well cell culture plate. Cells were infected with culture medium containing 2.5 × 106 viral particles (multiplicity of infection (MOI) = 5) and collected 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, and 30 h post-infection (hpi). RNA was extracted using the PureLink® RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, USA), and SDS-PAGE and western blot were performed.

Drug inhibition

The 5 × 105 GK cells were inoculated into a 6-well cell culture plate and incubated at 37 °C until the following day. The culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed with PBS; thereafter, 40 μg/mL cytosine arabinoside (AraC) or 50 μg/mL cycloheximide (CHX) was added to each well and the mixture incubated for 1 h at 28 °C. After the solutions were discarded, the cells were washed with PBS and then infected by adding 3 mL of culture medium containing 2.5 × 106 viral particles (MOI = 5) to each well and incubating at 37 °C for 12 h. The viral solution was discarded, the cells were washed with PBS, and RNA was extracted using the PureLink® RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, USA). RQ1 DNase Stop Solution (Promega, USA) was used per the manufacturer's protocol to remove residual DNA.

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

cDNA was reverse transcribed using the SuperScript™ III Reverse Transcriptase kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and amplified by PCR using specific primers (Table 2). The reaction conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 5 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 42 s, 56 °C for 42 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, and finally 72 °C for 10 min and 15 °C to terminate the reaction. PCR products were confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Immunofluorescence assay

To observe the intracellular localization of GIV-120L protein, 105 GK cells were cultured on coverslips in a 12-well plate and infected with the virus at MOI = 5. At 0, 12, and 24 hpi post-infection, the culture medium was discarded, the wells were washed with PBS, and 500 μL of 1 % (v/v) formaldehyde was added and allowed to stand for 15 min. The wells were washed with PBS, and 500 μL of methanol was added and allowed to stand for 10 min. The wells were washed with PBS, and 0.1 % (v/v) Triton-100/PBS was added and allowed to stand for 10 min. The wells were washed with PBS, and blocking was performed by adding 500 μL of 10 % (v/v) FBS/PBS for 1 h. The wells were washed with PBS, and 500 μL of primary antibody (mouse serum in 10 % (v/v) FBS/PBS at a dilution of 1:100) was added and the mixture incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The wells were then washed with PBS, and 500 μL of secondary antibody (goat anti-mouse IgG-FITC in 10 % (v/v) FBS/PBS at a dilution of 1:300) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The wells were washed with PBS, and the coverslips were recovered, mounted with DAPI Fluoromount-GTM (Southern Biotech, USA), and observed and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Results

Sequence analysis

The sequence of the GIV-120L gene was searched in the NCBI database, and the accession number AAV91028 was returned. However, the amino acid sequence of AAV91028 in the database had a length of 1,008 a.a., which differs from the GIV-120L amino acid sequence length of 489 a.a. (Tsai et al., 2005). Thus, AAV91028 was tentatively designated GIV-120.5L (Eaton et al., 2007). BLAST analysis of GIV-120L using the NCBI database revealed 7 homologs, namely SGIV-12L with 98 % identity, GGRV-27R with 48 % identity, BIV-34R with 48 % identity, ECV-93R with 45 % identity, ToRV-58L with 43 % identity, ATV-61R with 43 % identity, and FV3-32R with 43 % identity (Table 1). All seven viruses belong to the genus Ranavirus. Except for BIV-34R and FV3-32R, which are neurofilament triplet H1-like proteins, SGIV-12L, GGRV-27R, ECV-93R, ToRV-58L, and ATV-61R are hypothetical or unknown proteins whose amino acid sequences all contain a Rho_N domain. GIV-120.5L demonstrates a greater similarity with other viral genes at amino acids 1–32 and features only two lysine-serine-proline (KSP) sites, in contrast to multiple KSP sites found in other viral genes. Functional domain analysis using the SMART bioinformatics tool revealed a Rho_N domain at amino acids 2–36 of GIV-120.5L as well as two short repeated amino acid sequences 44 a.a. in length at amino acids 161–204 and 235–278 (Fig. 1). Phylogenetic tree analysis using MEGA 6 software showed that GIV-120.5L exhibited high homology with SGIV-12L, followed by ToRV-58L, GGRV-27R, ATV-61R, FV3-32R, and ECV-93R in decreasing order (Fig. 2).

Preparation of recombinant protein

The constructed pET23a-GIV-120L vector was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) and purified and dialyzed after IPTG induction. No GIV-120L recombinant protein was expressed in the BL21 (DE3) and BL21 (DE3)-pET23a groups, whereas a large amount of GIV-120L recombinant protein with a molecular weight of 59.1 kDa was expressed in the BL21 (DE3)-pET23a-GIV-120L and BL21 (DE3)-pET23a-GIV-120L+IPTG groups after the addition of IPTG. GIV-120L-His, the recombinant GIV-120L protein containing a His-tag, was successfully purified and dialyzed (Fig. 3). Quantification of the purified recombinant GIV-120L protein was performed, and the absorbance of known concentrations of BSA and unknown concentrations of recombinant protein were determined using the Bradford protein assay. First, the absorbance of BSA and the known concentrations were used to construct a standard curve. Next, the absorbance of the recombinant protein was substituted into the equation of the standard curve to determine the concentration of the recombinant protein. As a result, the concentration of recombinant GIV-120L protein was 2.7 mg/mL (Table 3).

Antibody preparation

The recombinant protein was mixed with Freund's adjuvant and injected intraperitoneally into mice to produce an immune response. Serum was collected and tested for recognition of viral proteins by polyclonal antibodies. In the negative control group, other proteins were recognized by the serum, but not at the molecular weight corresponding to GIV-120L (59.1 kDa). In the experimental group, the results were the same as in the negative control group, with other proteins with molecular weights ranging from 72 to 125 kDa recognized by the serum, but not at the corresponding molecular weight (59.1 kDa) (Fig. 4). The mice were immunized with GIV-120L for monoclonal antibody production. After sacrificing the mice, the spleen was harvested and splenocytes were fused with myeloma cells to form hybridoma cells. The medium used to culture the hybridoma cells was collected and tested for the recognition of viral proteins. Subsequently, four hybridoma cell lines were screened for their ability to recognize the GIV-120L protein (Fig. 5).

Transcriptome and proteome analysis

Analysis of the time evolution of viral gene expression showed that the time of GIV-120L RNA expression ranged from 12 to 30 hpi and the time of RNA expression of the GIV-MCP control group ranged from 12 to 30 hpi (Fig. 6). The time of GIV-120L protein expression ranged from 18 to 30 hpi and the time of protein expression of the GIV-MCP control group ranged from 12 to 30 hpi (Fig. 7). Analysis of the phases of expression by inhibiting translation with CHX and inhibiting DNA synthesis with AraC showed that there was no GIV-120L RNA expression after inhibition, thus confirming GIV-120L as a late gene. In contrast, the control gene GIV-27L (an early gene) was not inhibited by AraC, and GIV-97L (an immediate-early gene) was not inhibited by either CHX and AraC (Fig. 8). Fluorescence microscopy was used to observe the localization of gene expression in host cells during viral infection. The results showed that GIV-120L was not expressed at 12 hpi but was concentrated adjacent to the nucleus at 24 hpi. DAPI fluorescence appeared at this site (Fig. 9), suggesting that this is the virus assembly site. We conclude from these results that GIV-120L accumulates at the virus assembly site during the late stage of viral infection.

Discussion

In a study of GIV by Tsai et al. (2005), it was found that the ORF 120L is located at a nucleotide position of 138,307-139,776 and that the translated amino acid sequence is 489 aa in length. In addition, GIV-120L did not exhibit any conserved domains or homologs, so it was not predicted to be functional and was therefore selected for the study. Notably, an NCBI database search of the recombinant pET23a-GIV-120L gene revealed that the sequence lengths of GIV-120L and the alignment match AAV91028 were 489 and 1,008 aa respectively. The amino acid sequence length and the genomic positions of the two genes differed significantly as well. However, the results of polyclonal antibody recognition of GIV-120L viral proteins showed that the molecular weight of the viral proteins ranged from 72 to 125 kDa (Fig. 4). Thus, we hypothesize that the actual length of the amino acid sequence consistent with these molecular weights would be 1,008 aa (Fig. 10, outer circle), not the length of 489 aa (MW 59.1 kDa) predicted by Tsai et al. (2005) (Fig. 10, inner circle). Using a comparative genomics approach (Eaton et al., 2007) with Viral Orthologous Clusters (VOCs) (Ehlers et al., 2002) and Viral Genome Organizer (VGO) (Upton et al., 2000) software, various iridovirus genomes were aligned and a new analysis with greater accuracy than previous unstandardized predictions was performed. The linearized circular genome is split in the middle of an ORF because the forecast starting point of the random search (Tsai et al., 2005) falls in the middle of an ORF, a phenomenon known as a split ORF (Goorha and Murti, 1982). The results showed that ORF001L and ORF120L, the first and last ORFs of GIV, respectively (Tsai et al., 2005), happened to be separated because the forecast starting points of the two ORFs fell in between (arrows in the inner circle of Fig. 10), thus confirming that the original two split ORFs were actually GIV-120.5L (1008 aa) with a molecular weight of 115.5 kDa. Subsequently, an alignment between GIV-120L and GIV-120.5L was performed, which confirmed that the second half of GIV-120.5L (1008 aa) starting at amino acid 520 was identical to GIV-120L (489 aa) (Fig. 11). Functional domain analysis using SMART showed that the N-terminal region of the GIV-120.5L amino acid sequence contains a Rho_N_domain, which is commonly found in rho transcription termination factors and plays a key role in DNA binding (Bogden et al., 1999). Whether viral DNA binds to the Rho_N_domain of GIV-120.5L or whether this binding is utilized for viral DNA encapsulation and assembly requires further experimental confirmation. In contrast, GIV-120L is a split ORF comprising 489 amino acids, but its function remains unclear. Furthermore, a BLAST search of the GIV-120.5L DNA sequence using the NCBI database revealed that only GIV and SGIV were homologs. The amino acid sequences were compared, and the results showed that some of the homologs were hypothetical or unknown proteins. However, BIV-34 and FV3-32R were neurofilament triplet H1-like proteins, and FV3-32R was similar to a neurofilament-H protein in rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus) (accession no. A43427), with 25 % identity (Tan et al., 2004). Previous studies have used neurofilament triplet H1-like protein, DNA polymerase, and MCP genes to differentiate among Ranavirus species that lacked obvious distinguishing characteristics. Sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree analysis of genes from short-finned eel ranavirus (SERV), Rana esculenta virus Italy 282/I02 (REV 282/I02), and pike-perch iridovirus (PPIV) with 11 other iridoviruses confirmed that SERV, REV 282/I02, and PPIV were members of Ranavirus. In addition, PCR using primers specific to the neurofilament triplet H1-like protein of FV3 showed that this gene was only present in Ranavirus. Furthermore, the number of repeats in short repetitive sequences may differ widely among different viruses and may be associated with viral transcription or homologous recombination (Holopainen et al., 2009; Williams et al., 2005). Indeed, two short repetitive sequences were found in GIV-120.5L. Eaton et al. (2007) constructed a table of genes sharing the same conserved sequence, classifying FV3-32R, TFV-33R, ATV61R, SGIV-12L, GIV-120.5L, LCDV-1-161R, and LCDV-C-33R as unknown proteins sharing the same conserved sequence. However, in the present study, the gene did not align with TFV-33R, LCDV-1-161R, or LCDV-C-33R. Previous studies have suggested that TFV-33R is a homolog of STIV-35R and EHNV-68R, with 86 % and 80 % identity respectively, as well as a conserved rho factor and a predicted function of neurofilament triplet H1-like protein (Huang et al., 2009; Jancovich et al., 2010). Thus, we hypothesize that GIV-120.5L and TFV-33R may be homologs. In addition, LCDV-C-33R may be a membrane-bound metallopeptidase and a homolog of LCDV-1-161R (accession no. NP_078746) (Zhang et al., 2004). The alignment between GIV-120.5L and LCDV-C-33R revealed only 29 % identity. Thus, we hypothesize that GIV-120.5L is not homologous to LCDV-1-161R or LCDV-C-33R, and that neurofilament triplet H1-like protein may be a protein specific to Ranavirus. In addition, SGIV-12L has been cited as a capsid protein (Chen et al., 2006), so its homolog GIV-120.5L may also be a capsid protein. The neurofilament (NF) triplet consists of the three subunits NF-L, NF-M, and NF-H, with predicted molecular weights of 61.5, 102.5, and 112.5 kDa, respectively. However, NF undergoes phosphorylation, glycosylation, and other post-translational modifications in the cell, which results in purified proteins having molecular weights of 68, 160, and 205 kDa (Gardner et al., 1984; Perrot et al., 2008). The phosphorylated sites are lysine-serine-proline (KSP) sites (Yang et al., 2011). Except for GIV-120.5L (two KSP sites) and SGIV-12L (one KSP site), all the other viral genes have 10 or more KSP sites. Further experiments are required to determine whether the KSP sites in GIV-120.5L are phosphorylated during viral infection.

In the present study, RT-PCR was used to analyze the time evolution of mRNA expression during viral infection. CHX has been found to effectively inhibit SGIV infection at concentrations of 50, 100, 200, and 500 μg/mL; and elevated CHX concentrations are associated with reduced mRNA expression levels of immediate-early genes (Chen et al., 2006). Therefore, 50 μg/mL was selected as the concentration for CHX treatment in the present study. Given that 40 μg/mL of AraC inhibited viral DNA synthesis (Lin et al., 2014), this concentration was used in the present study, with the result that the mRNA expression of GIV-120L was significantly suppressed by treatment with CHX and AraC, which provides preliminary evidence that GIV-120L is a late gene. This result was examined further using fluorescence microscopy to observe the localization of the GIV-120L gene product in host cells during viral infection. GIV-120L was found to be distributed primarily in the cytoplasm at 18 hpi but shifted to being concentrated in the nucleus at 30 hpi. This dynamic change in intracellular localization provides additional experimental evidence for GIV-120L being a late gene. Taken together, these results provide further evidence that GIV-120L is a late gene.

Mouse monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were found to target the GIV-55L and 97L proteins. Two high-affinity mAbs, namely GIV55L-mAb-2 and GIV97L-mAb-3, were identified through hybridoma screening with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and western blot. Both mAbs were of the IgG1 isotype and are effective at recognizing their respective target viral proteins. RT-PCR and western blot analysis of GIV-infected GK cells showed that GIV97L is an immediate-early gene, whereas GIV55L is a late gene. The localization of 55L and 97L in GIV-infected cells was further characterized through immunofluorescence microscopy using these mAbs. 55L protein accumulates primarily in the cytoplasm, whereas 97L protein is distributed in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm of infected cells (Hu et al., 2015).

The GIV-2L gene is 1,242 bp in length and encodes a protein with a predicted molecular weight of 46.2 kDa. It has significant similarities only with members of Ranavirus and Iridovirus. A mouse monoclonal antibody targeting GIV-2L protein was produced by immunizing mice with GIV-2L-His tagged recombinant protein. Inhibition of de novo protein and DNA synthesis in GIV-infected cells demonstrated that GIV-2L is a late gene in the viral replication process. Immunofluorescence microscopy showed that GIV-2L protein accumulates in the nucleus and cytoplasm of infected cells (Lin et al., 2014).

The GIV-59L gene is 1,521 bp in length and encodes a protein 506 amino acids in length with a predicted molecular weight of 53.9 kDa. Interestingly, GIV-59L and its homologs are present in all genera of the family Iridoviridae. The mouse monoclonal antibody GIV-59L(760-1518)-MAb-21 targeting the C-terminal domain of the GIV-59L protein (amino acids 254-506) was produced and proved suitable for various GIV immunoassays. RT-PCR, western blot, and pharmacological inhibition tests with CHX and AraC showed that GIV-59L is a late gene in GIV-infected grouper kidney cells. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that the GIV-59L protein accumulated primarily in the cytoplasm of infected cells and is eventually encapsulated into intact viral particles (Chen et al., 2015a).

The homologs of GIV-61L are found only in Ranavirus. Three mAbs targeting the GIV-61L protein were produced. GIV-61L, which was identified as a late gene through pharmacological inhibition tests, was used in western blot and immunofluorescence assays to demonstrate that the GIV-61L protein is contained in GIV particles, expressed at 18 hpi, and located exclusively in the cytoplasm of GIV-infected cells (Chen et al., 2015b).

Grouper iridovirus ORF 64L (GIV-64L) is predicted to encode an envelope protein and is conserved in all sequenced viruses in the genus Ranavirus. The complete nucleotide sequence of the GIV-64L gene (1,215 bp) was cloned into the IPTG-inducible prokaryotic expression vector pET23a. After induction and purification, recombinant GIV-64L-His protein with a molecular weight of approximately 50.2 kDa was obtained and used to immunize BALB/c mice. Three monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) against GIV-64L protein, all of which were IgG1-isotype antibodies, were produced using ELISA. RT-qPCR revealed that in the presence of the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide or the DNA synthesis inhibitor AraC, GIV-64L was a late gene during GIV infection of GK cells. The established monoclonal antibody GIV-64L-mAb-17 was used in both western blot and immunofluorescence assay to show that GIV-64L protein was expressed at 24 hpi and was localized only to the cytoplasm of GIV-infected cells and assembled into mature viral particles (Chen et al., 2016).

Late genes encode proteins from the DNA core to the envelope, including DNA-binding proteins, scaffold proteins, phosphorylation-related proteins, membrane proteins, capsid proteins, and envelope proteins. In conjunction with the results of previous studies, a more accurate functional characterization of the late gene GIV-120L can improve our understanding of GIV viral assembly.

Conclusion

In this study we carried out sequence and phylogeny analysis of the virus GIV and utilized bioinformatics to predict the function of GIV-120L. In addition, mouse polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies against GIV-120L were produced. Such antibodies have been demonstrated to be effective tools for diagnosis of GIV infection. These antibodies may serve as valuable tools for future studies on the biological function and pathological significance of GIV-120L in viral infections. The development of these antibodies represents significant advancement in the diagnosis of GIV and will facilitate further research into its pathogenicity.
