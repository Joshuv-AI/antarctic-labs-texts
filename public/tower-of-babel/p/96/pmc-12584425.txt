Recurrent arterial and venous thrombosis in a 39-year-old male with Factor V Leiden syndrome and aneurysmal disease: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Abdalrahman Mohammed, Hala O Abdallah, Alaa Kharouf, Ahmed Hamed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFactor V Leiden is classically linked to venous thrombosis, however arterial events in carriers are uncommon and usually context dependent.Case summaryWe describe a 39-year-old man with known Factor V Leiden who presented with central chest pain and an inferior ST elevation myocardial infarction. Introduction

Factor V Leiden (FVL) is a point mutation in factor V that eliminates a key cleavage site, increasing the risk of thrombosis. Heterozygous carriers are the most common inherited thrombophilia, affecting 1%–5% of the general White population and 10%–20% of individuals with venous thromboembolism [1, 2].

The main clinical consequence of FVL is an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE), most commonly presenting as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE). Although many carriers show only laboratory abnormalities, heterozygotes have a lifetime VTE risk of approximately 5%, rising to 20% in families with thrombophilia. FVL can also predispose to less common VTE sites, including cerebral, portal, hepatic veins, and Budd-Chiari syndrome, with risk amplified by factors such as oral contraceptive use [1, 3, 4].

The link between Factor V Leiden (FVL) and arterial thromboembolism remains controversial. While some studies have found no significant association between FVL and arterial events, others suggest a modest increase in the risk of coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction (MI), and stroke—particularly among women, younger individuals, and smokers. Overall, current guidelines do not consider FVL a major risk factor for arterial disease [5].

We report the case of a 39-year-old patient with a known FVL mutation, presenting with an inferior MI and a past medical history significant for ischemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, right femoral aneurysm, and previous DVT. This case represents a rare arterial phenotype of FVL, characterized by recurrent arterial thromboses and aneurysmal disease at a relatively young age. It underscores the importance of considering FVL not only in the context of VTE but also in selected clinical scenarios involving arterial pathology.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old male patient with a documented history of FVL, ischemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, and previous DVT, presented with chest pain. His past medical history includes prior coronary intervention with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) to the right coronary artery 2 months ago. The patient also has a history of peripheral vascular disease, with a right femoral artery stent placement, and a previous right femoral aneurysm treated with grafting and endovascular repair 13 years ago.

The patient developed central chest pain described as heaviness, radiating to the epigastrium, with a severity of 9 out of 10. This occurred two hours prior to his hospital admission on August 5. The chest pain was associated with palpitations and sweating. Additionally, the patient reported increased shortness of breath with minimal activity. He denied having a productive cough, fever, or loss of consciousness. An electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed ST-segment elevation in inferior leads (II, III and aVF) prompting urgent transfer for cardiac catheterization.

Upon admission to the Critical Care Unit (CCU), the patient’s past medical and comorbid history included FVL, peripheral vascular disease with a prior right femoral stent, a history of right femoral aneurysm treated with grafting and endovascular repair 13 years ago, uncontrolled hypertension with non-compliance to medications (which included nifedipine, valsartan, hydrochlorothiazide, and carvedilol), prior hydrocele with hydrocelectomy, atrophied kidney with elevated kidney function tests and ischemic heart disease with a prior inferior STEMI 2 months ago which was treated with PCI. He is also a heavy smoker with an estimated 30–40 pack-year history of smoking.

Initial catheterization during the current admission showed a normal left main artery, mild proximal disease in the left anterior descending artery (LAD), a normal left circumflex artery (LCX), and an ectatic right coronary artery (RCA) with total proximal in-stent occlusion. An intraluminal thrombus was noted in the posterior descending artery (PDA) (Figs. 1 and 2). The patient was managed with PCI to the right coronary artery (RCA), along with Aggrastat and heparin, and tested positive for troponin.

On August 6, the patient remained in the CCU, receiving Aggrastat and a heparin infusion per PTT protocol. He experienced no further chest pain and was started on ticagrelor.

By August 7, the patient was conscious, alert, and not in distress. Re-evaluation led to re-catheterization, revealing RCA in-stent thrombosis with TIMI III flow and thrombus in the PDA (Figs. 3 and 4). PCI was again performed on the RCA. Due to marfanoid features (tall stature and a high-arched palate), homocysteinemia was suspected, and his homocysteine level was found to be mildly elevated. Heparin infusion was stopped and replaced with enoxaparin.

On August 8, the patient experienced severe chest pain, with elevated CK and CK-MB levels, but symptoms improved, and troponin levels trended down. Echocardiography showed no mitral valve prolapse or aortic valve abnormalities, and the patient’s systolic and diastolic functions were normal. He was discharged in stable condition on the same day on the following medications nifedipine, aspirin, valsartan, hydrochlorothiazide, carvedilol, ticagrelor, rosuvastatin, and esomeprazole.

Follow-Up (Two Weeks Post-Discharge), The patient was asymptomatic, with normal vital signs and no evidence of distress. ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm with Q waves in the inferior leads (II, III, aVF), along with mild persistent ST-segment elevation and T-wave inversion, also noted in the lateral leads (V5–V6). Echocardiogram Showed a normal left ventricular size with moderately reduced systolic function (EF 35–40%) and severe hypokinesia. The right ventricle, atria, valves, and pericardium appeared normal, with no evidence of structural pathology. Findings are consistent with a prior inferior MI and the development of ischemic cardiomyopathy secondary to the previous infarction.

Evidence of multiple arterial thrombosis

The patient’s coronary involvement is documented through the presentation of an inferior STEMI with ST-segment elevation on ECG, and angiography revealing total proximal in-stent occlusion of the RCA along with an intraluminal thrombus in the PDA. PCI was performed in both cases.

The patient’s history of peripheral arterial disease with prior right femoral stent placement and a right femoral aneurysm treated with grafting and endovascular repair 13 years ago further supports the diagnosis of multiple arterial thrombosis.

Discussion and review of literature

FVL is the most common inherited thrombophilia in people of European ancestry and it predominantly increases the risk of VTE rather than arterial events. Contemporary reviews and reference texts describe the association between FVL and arterial thrombosis as controversial and, if present, small in magnitude, which frames the arterial phenotype in this 39-year-old as unusual [1, 5].

Although Factor V Leiden (FVL) is primarily linked to venous thromboembolism, its involvement in arterial thrombosis remains rare and incompletely understood. Most reported FVL-associated myocardial infarctions occur in patients older than 45 years and in the presence of conventional atherothrombotic risk factors such as diabetes or dyslipidemia. This 39-year-old male with recurrent coronary and peripheral arterial thromboses therefore represents an early and clinically significant presentation that broadens the phenotypic spectrum of FVL-associated arterial disease. This underscores the importance of considering inherited thrombophilias in premature or recurrent arterial events where atherosclerotic burden is limited [6–8].

Evidence linking FVL to MI is inconsistent. Large cohorts and meta-analyses that include unselected adults generally show no meaningful increase in risk of MI or coronary death among carriers, including patients with established coronary disease who were followed prospectively in the GENIUS CHD individual participant data analysis. Signals, when present, tend to appear in selected subgroups such as very young patients or specific populations [9–11].

In contrast, the relationship between inherited thrombophilias and ischemic stroke in the young is more consistent. Multiple meta-analyses report that FVL, along with prothrombin G20210A and natural anticoagulant deficiencies, is enriched among young adults with arterial ischemic stroke. These data support the concept that inherited prothrombotic states can contribute to arterial events when competing atherothrombotic mechanisms are less dominant, which helps explain why arterial thrombosis can emerge at younger ages in carriers [12, 13].

Beyond genotype, clinical context matters. This patient’s history includes prior coronary thrombosis requiring PCI and a surgically repaired femoral aneurysm. Aneurysmal or markedly dilated arterial segments create zones of low shear and flow recirculation that favor thrombus formation. Vascular society guidance highlights thrombosis as the most feared complication of peripheral aneurysms, underscoring the prothrombotic milieu inherent to this anatomy. In such settings, FVL is best viewed as a risk modifier that lowers the threshold for clinically overt arterial thrombosis rather than a solitary cause [14].

Potential cofactors can further amplify risk. Intermittent or mild hyperhomocysteinemia is biologically prothrombotic and has been co-reported with arterial thrombosis in FVL carriers in case-based literature. However, randomized trials show that routine homocysteine lowering with folic acid and B vitamins does not reduce major cardiovascular events in broad secondary prevention populations, so management should target correctable deficiencies rather than assume outcome benefit from vitamin therapy alone [15, 16].

Management of coronary events in carriers follows standard acute coronary syndrome and revascularization guidelines. Dual antiplatelet therapy reduces stent thrombosis and ischemic events at the cost of increased bleeding, and duration should be individualized according to clinical presentation, anatomy, and bleeding risk, rather than FVL status per se. Current ACC and AHA guidance supports longer courses in higher ischemic risk, with shorter courses reducing bleeding when appropriate. Genotype does not independently mandate chronic anticoagulation for arterial indications [17–19].

When arterial thrombosis is unexplained or recurrent at a young age, hematology frameworks recommend a targeted search for contributory conditions such as antiphospholipid syndrome, myeloproliferative neoplasms, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, and selected inherited or acquired prothrombotic states, paired with rigorous control of conventional risk factors. This approach aligns with viewing FVL as one part of a multifactorial thrombotic phenotype [20, 21]. In this patient, testing for antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, and β2-glycoprotein I antibodies) was negative, and no hematologic or clinical features suggestive of a myeloproliferative neoplasm were identified.

This 39-year-old illustrates a rare arterial expression of an otherwise venous thrombophilia. The literature supports three practical conclusions. First, FVL alone seldom explains arterial thrombosis, but in the presence of permissive vascular anatomy such as aneurysmal segments, the thrombotic threshold is lowered. Second, standard coronary care with evidence-based antiplatelet therapy and risk factor modification remains the cornerstone, with anticoagulation considered only in narrowly defined, high thrombosis burden scenarios. Third, evaluation for additional prothrombotic contributors and meticulous control of reversible risks are essential in preventing recurrence [1, 14, 17].

Limitations

This report describes a single-patient experience and cannot establish causality between FVL and arterial thrombosis. Genetic or functional platelet studies were not performed, which limits mechanistic interpretation. Larger case series are needed to better define the relationship between inherited thrombophilias and arterial events.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare arterial manifestation occurring in a patient with FVL, typically considered a venous disorder. In young adults, arterial thrombosis is more likely when vessel abnormalities or added risk factors, such as mild hyperhomocysteinemia, are present. Management follows standard ACS and revascularization guidelines, with therapy duration tailored to anatomy, ischemic and bleeding risks rather than genotype. Secondary prevention should focus on strict cardiovascular risk control and evaluation for other prothrombotic conditions.
