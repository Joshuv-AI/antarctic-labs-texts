Rifampicin induced purpura of left lower limb: Illustrative medical image
Authors: Alushika Jain, Amit Toshniwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRifampicin-induced purpura is a rare hypersensitivity reaction encountered during anti tubercular therapy (ATT). We report a 43-year-old male with cervical tubercular lymphadenitis who developed purpuric lesions with distinctive orange-colored serous discharge over the left lower limb after initiation of ATT. Investigations excluded thrombocytopenia and systemic vasculitis. A temporal relationship between drug intake and symptom onset confirmed rifampicin as the causative agent. Prompt withdrawal of rifampicin and initiation of systemic corticosteroids led to complete resolution of the lesions. This case highlights the need for vigilance regarding rare dermatological adverse effects in patients on ATT and the importance of prompt clinical recognition to prevent serious complications.Keywords: Purpura, Rifampicin, Tuberculosis, Drug toxicity

Case illustration

A 43-year-old man, recently diagnosed with cervical tuberculous lymphadenitis, was initiated on a standard anti-tubercular (ATT) regimen. The patient reported unusual purple-colored patches over the left lower limb with discharge from a few lesions. On examination, multiple reddish-brown lesions were observed with orange-colored discharge from lesions (Fig. 1), raising suspicion of purpura with orange-colored serous discharge from a few of them (Fig. 2). Systemic evaluation and laboratory workup were conducted to rule out thrombocytopenia and systemic vasculitis. Based on the temporal relationship between drug intake and symptom onset, a diagnosis of rifampicin-induced purpura was suspected [1]. Rifampicin was immediately discontinued from the ATT regimen. The patient was started on systemic corticosteroids to modulate the immune response. Following the cessation of the offending drug and the initiation of steroids, the purpuric lesions stabilized, the orange discharge ceased, and the skin began to heal without further complications. Rifampicin is the cornerstone of ATT [2], but it is associated with various side effects, ranging from minor cutaneous reactions to severe hematological complications [3]. Among these, rifampicin-induced purpura is rare but significant hypersensitivity reaction that requires prompt clinical recognition [1], [4]. This case highlights the importance of monitoring patients on ATT for rare dermatological side effects. The “orange discharge” likely represents the systemic excretion of rifampicin through serous fluid in the compromised skin barrier. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for drug-induced etiology in patients presenting with new-onset purpura while on ATT to prevent potentially life-threatening sequels [5].
