Comparison of Effects of Postextubation Noninvasive Ventilation with Passive Humidification and High-flow Nasal Cannula in Patients with High Risk of Extubation Failure: A Randomized Comparative Study
Authors: Nimisha S Patil, Madhulika Dubey, Mohd Mustahsin, Krishna P Mall, Supriya Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsWeaning patients with a high risk of extubation failure off the ventilator can be challenging and associated with complications. High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) and noninvasive ventilation (NIV) have been used after extubation in various settings. This study suggests the better modality between HFNC and NIV to prevent reintubation in these patients.Patients and methodsA single-centered, prospective, randomized comparative study was conducted in the adult medical intensive care unit (ICU) of our center. A total of 150 critically ill patients with a high risk of extubation failure were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either HFNC or NIV immediately after extubation for 48 hours. The rate of reintubation within 48 hours of extubation was compared between the groups.ResultsThe rate of reintubation within 48 hours was lower in the HFNC group than the NIV group (p = 0.041). The rate of postextubation respiratory failure after 7 days, incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), sepsis, intolerance to therapy, number of patients with hospital length of stay ≤7 days, and hospital mortality was lower in the HFNC group (p < 0.05). The incidence of nasal discomfort was lower in the NIV group (p < 0.05). The heart rate (HR) and respiratory rate (RR) were lower in the HFNC group at 1 hour and 48 hours, respectively, after extubation (p < 0.05). The partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) and mean arterial pressure (MAP) at 48 hours after extubation were higher in HFNC group (p < 0.05).ConclusionHigh-flow nasal cannula performed better than NIV in preventing reintubation at 48 hours in patients with a high risk of extubation failure.How to cite this articlePatil NS, Dubey M, Mustahsin M, Mall KP, Singh S. Highlights

The present study highlights the preferred mode of oxygen support between noninvasive ventilation and high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) to prevent reintubation in patients with a high risk of extubation failure.

Introduction

Extubation failure occurs in about 10–15% of patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU), going above 20% in high-risk groups, the overall reported rate being 10–30%.1,2 Reintubation is linked to high mortality, longer hospital stays, and increased financial burden.3,4 Given these risks and the challenges of weaning from mechanical ventilation, preventive oxygenation strategies to avoid reintubation warrant consideration.

Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) may prevent postextubation respiratory failure in high-risk patients; small randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses suggest reduced reintubation vs standard oxygen, but large-scale RCT evidence remains limited.5,6 Current guidelines recommend preventive NIV for patients at risk—especially hypercapnic, obese, or with heart failure—though the certainty of benefit is moderate.7,8 Evidence is mixed partly because pooled analyses included many lower-risk patients, and it remains unclear which subgroups benefit most from NIV vs high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC).1,9 Recently, a trial combining HFNC plus NIV was conducted, adding complexity to postextubation strategies.10,11

Noninvasive ventilation outcomes depend on clinician expertise, patient education/compliance, and device performance. Its use can be limited by side effects—skin injury, eye irritation, claustrophobia, oral/throat dryness, flatulence, and aspiration risk. Due to poor tolerance, about 15–25% of patients fail NIV and require endotracheal intubation.12,13

High-flow nasal cannula therapy is a modern form of noninvasive respiratory support that delivers high flows of heated and humidified gas through specialized nasal prongs. This system provides precise oxygen concentrations, generates a mild positive airway pressure, increases functional residual capacity, reduces dead space ventilation, increases alveolar volume, enhances both oxygenation and ventilation, and improves overall gas exchange. The heated and humidified gas also improves mucociliary function, facilitates secretion clearance, protects airway epithelial cells, and significantly increases patient comfort.14–17

Previous studies have shown that HFNC offers greater comfort and tolerability than NIV.18,19 Clinically, HFNC has emerged as an effective alternative to NIV. Large RCTs have demonstrated that HFNC is noninferior to NIV in preventing postextubation respiratory failure and reintubation among critically ill patients with a high risk of extubation failure.15,20 Consequently, HFNC is increasingly used in the ICU as a modality to reduce the risk of reintubation in critically ill patients.

Prior studies included a mixed ICU population with variability in the definition of high risk of extubation failure. The differences in HFNC settings, duration of therapy, and protocols for NIV application make comparisons across studies challenging. Moreover, limited data exist on the role of HFNC specifically as part of a structured weaning strategy in high-risk patients, rather than solely as postextubation support. Hence, the present study was designed to clearly define high-risk patients for extubation failure, directly compare HFNC and NIV in a pragmatic, clinically relevant setting, standardized intervention protocols, and evaluating multiple clinically meaningful outcomes, including reintubation rates, effect on physiological variables at different time intervals, duration of respiratory support, and ICU/hospital stay.

Patients and Methods

This prospective randomized comparative study was performed in the medical adult ICU of our center between February 2024 and February 2025. The Institutional Ethical Committee (registration number—ELMC&H/R-Cell/2023/10) approved the research protocol. The patients were enrolled in the study after informed consent from them or their next of kin. The trial was registered with clinical trial registry of India (CTRI number—CTRI/2024/02/062564).

Critically ill patients admitted to the ICU who were ventilated for ≥24 hours and planned for extubation after passing the spontaneous breath trial (SBT) were screened for inclusion.

Patients were considered as high risk of extubation failure if any one of the specified high risk criteria was present, that is, age >65 years, history of chronic respiratory disease, congestive heart failure, prolonged mechanical ventilation (≥7 days), hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45 mm Hg) prior to extubation, difficult weaning, obesity (body mass index >30), acute physiology and chronic health evaluation (APACHE) II score >12 on day of extubation or airway patency problems.15,21,22 The following patients were excluded from the study: Do not resuscitate orders, tracheostomized patients, accidental or self-extubation, do not reintubate orders, and contraindication for NIV therapy (e.g., recent faciocervical surgery, active upper GI bleeding, chronic neuromuscular diseases, traumatic brain injury).

The minimum sample size required comes out to be n = 75 for each group.

The patients were reviewed daily for readiness to wean according to the standard criteria for weaning readiness (the cause of the respiratory failure has improved, partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/FiO2 ≥ 150 or SpO2 ≥ 90% on FiO2 ≤ 40% and positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) ≤ 5 cm H2O, pH > 7.25, able to initiate an inspiratory effort, adequate mentation, hemodynamic stability, i.e., no or low dose vasopressor medications, minimal or thin secretions, presence of cough or gag reflex and hemoglobin ≥ 7 gm/dL). The patients who were ready to wean were given a pressure support ventilation (PSV) trial, using low levels of pressure support (PS 6 cm H2O, PEEP ≤ 5 cm H2O, FiO2 ≤ 0.4–0.5). The tolerance of a 30-minute SBT prompted consideration for permanent ventilator liberation. Failure of the SBT was indicated by any form of clinical instability during the trial. This included respiratory rate (RR) >35 breaths/minute, increased accessory muscle activity, SpO2 persistently <92% (or < 88% in case of underlying chronic lung disease) on FiO2 ≥ 0.4, hemodynamic instability defined as heart rate (HR) > 140 bpm or systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 180 mm Hg, with signs of hypoperfusion (appearance of cyanosis or mottling) or depressed mental status or agitation.24,25 Patients who passed the SBT were planned for extubation. The criteria used for extubation screening were resolution of the primary indication for intubation; adequate oxygenation and gas exchange (FiO2 ≤ 0.4, PaO2 > 60 mm Hg, PEEP: +5 to +8); stable chest imaging; effective airway protection (intact cough, manageable secretions, and adequate consciousness); along with stable hemodynamic, neurological, and metabolic status and successful completion of SBT.26

After written informed consent from the patient or the next of kin, patients were included in the study and randomized to one of two groups: NIV with passive humidification or HFNC in an allocation ratio of 1:1. Randomization was achieved using the Sequentially Numbered, Opaque, Sealed Envelope (SNOSE) technique to ensure allocation concealment.

In the NIV group of patients, NIV with passive humidification was started immediately after extubation. Humidified gas was continuously delivered by bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) through a size-fitted facemask. Passive humidification was provided through a heat and moisture exchanger (HME) attached to the NIV circuit. A heat and moisture exchanger filter was placed between the patient's mask and the Y-piece in the ventilator circuit. Noninvasive ventilation settings, including inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP) and PEEP, were adjusted to maintain a target RR of <26 bpm with a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg of predicted body weight and optimum gas exchange (SpO2 ≥ 92).

In the group of patients randomized to receive HFNC, it was also applied immediately after extubation through a specific size-adjusted nasal cannula. The initial flow rate was set between 30 and 50 L/min, with FiO2 adjusted to maintain SpO2 ≥92%.

After 48 hours, both HFNC and NIV were discontinued, and all the patients were transitioned to conventional oxygen therapy (COT) if necessary, regardless of their clinical status. Crossover between HFNC and NIV was not allowed. Patients were reintubated upon failure of the assigned therapy. Both groups were treated by the trained medical, nursing, and respiratory therapy staff and received similar standard medical management.

Patients were continuously monitored for respiratory parameters, including RR, HR, blood pressure, SpO2, and arterial blood gases (ABG) at baseline, 1 hour, 12 hours, 24 hours, and 48 hours postextubation.

The primary outcome was to estimate all-cause reintubation within 48 hours after extubation. The secondary outcomes were postextubation respiratory failure within 7 days of extubation, respiratory infection [ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) or ventilator-associated tracheobronchitis], sepsis or multiple organ failure, ICU and hospital length of stay, ICU and hospital mortality.

The collected data were entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, tabulated, and analyzed using SPSS software version 25.0. Descriptive statistics such as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation were used to summarize the data. Inferential statistics such as the Chi-square test and t-test were used to test the significance of differences between groups. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the study, no significant difference was observed in the mean arterial pressure (MAP) and RR at 1 hour and 24 hours between the NIV group and the HFNC group. However, at 48 hours, the MAP was significantly lower in the NIV group (78.4 ± 10.2 mm Hg) compared to the HFNC group (82.2 ± 12.9 mm Hg) (p = 0.047). Similarly, the RR at 48 hours was significantly lower in the HFNC group (21.9 ± 1.7 bpm) compared to the NIV group (23.8 ± 2.3 bpm). The HR was significantly higher in the NIV group (101.7 ± 16.1 bpm) compared to the HFNC group (94.5 ± 15.2 bpm) (p < 0.05) at 1 hour only. The pH levels and PaCO2 levels at different time intervals were similar between the two groups.

The PaO2 levels at baseline, at 1 hour, and 24 hours were comparable between the groups. However, at 48 hours, PaO2 was significantly lower in the NIV group (92.9 ± 14.2 mm Hg) compared to the HFNC group (98.4 ± 18.0 mm Hg) (p = 0.039) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this randomized comparative study, HFNC was associated with a lower rate of reintubation within 48 hours of extubation compared to NIV with passive humidification in critically ill patients at high risk of extubation failure.

The baseline characteristics and comorbidities were comparable between the two groups like the previous studies.20,23,27 Although the baseline physiologic variables at the end of SBT prior to extubation were comparable between the two groups, significant differences were found at various intervals between the two groups. Mean arterial pressure was significantly lower in the NIV group at 48 hours, HR and RR were significantly lower in the HFNC group at 1 hour and 48 hours, respectively, PaO2 was significantly higher (at 48 hours) in the HFNC group, while no difference was observed in the PaCO2 level between the two groups. The previous study by Rittayamai et al., who enrolled 40 patients with acute dyspnea and hypoxemia in the emergency department and randomized them into HFNC and COT groups, found that the RR at 30 minutes and the HR, as well as the dyspnea score at the end of the study period, were significantly less in the HFNC than the conventional group.28 Furthermore, Makdee et al. found that HFNC reduced the severity of dyspnea in patients with acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema in comparison to COT.29 Tan et al., in their multicentric RCT comparing HFNC vs NIV for COPD patients after extubation, and they found that the HR and MAP within 48 hours after extubation in the two groups were not significantly different from baseline levels before extubation.27 However, the RR 24 hours after extubation in the HFNC group had decreased to its baseline and was lower than the RR in the NIV group [20 (17.3–24.5) vs 24.5 (18–27) breaths/minute, p < 0.050]. The NIV group's RR was still higher than its baseline level. They explained it by the relatively poor tolerance to NIV and the increase in effective alveolar ventilation caused by the washout effect of dead space in the HFNC group.

These effects of HFNC can be explained by their effects on respiratory physiology. They provide gas flow at a higher rate, which can meet or exceed the patient's peak inspiratory flow rate during dyspnea. This will reduce the PCO2 level also.15,28 The heated and humidified flow facilitated secretion clearance. Excessive secretion is one of the major causes of extubation failure. These physiological benefits ultimately result in an increase in the oxygenation index, reduce the average arterial pressure, HR, and RR.30,31

In the present study, the reintubation rate within 48 hours of extubation for the HFNC group was significantly lower than that of the NIV group. A similar study by Hernández et al. found that the all-cause reintubation within 7 days after extubation was significantly lower in the NIV group than the HFNC group.23 The reasons for this difference can be, firstly, our study used NIV with passive humidification while Hernández et al. used NIV with active humidification.23 While physiological data favor active humidification over passive one, this superiority is less evident when clinical endpoints are evaluated.32 Secondly, the study included patients with even a single high-risk feature for extubation failure, whereas Hernández et al. enrolled patients with ≥4 risk factors, representing a population with substantially higher baseline risk and potentially different responses to respiratory support strategies.23 Thirdly, in the study by Hernández et al., some risk factors, more likely to benefit from positive pressure ventilation, were more prevalent in the NIV group (e.g., acute heart failure, COPD, hypercapnia at the end of SBT), which may have favored NIV outcomes in their cohort.23 Additionally, Hernández et al. assessed the primary outcome at the end of 7 days, while the present study included 48 hours as the timeline for the primary outcome.23 Importantly, their exploratory analysis examining reintubation within the first 3 and 5 days showed that the reintubation rate was not significantly higher in the HFNC group, suggesting that discrepancies may partly relate to differences in outcome time frames rather than true early efficacy. Hernández et al. reported that the majority of late reintubations in the HFNC group occurred after discharge, raising the possibility that these events may reflect new clinical deterioration rather than failure of the initial postextubation strategy.23 Similarly, Mungan and Turan in their RCT comparing NIV with HFNC and COT found the lowest rate of reintubation in the HFNC group.33 Various other studies have found HFNC to be equally effective as NIV in preventing extubation failure.15,20,27

The other secondary outcomes, like incidence of postextubation respiratory failure, VAP, and sepsis, were significantly lower in the HFNC group than in the NIV group. This could be due to better tolerance to HFNC, ultimately leading to a lower rate of treatment failure in the HFNC group. This is in consistent with various other studies which have shown better tolerance to HFNC, lesser nasal discomfort, and lower rate of treatment failure than NIV.20,23,27 The lower incidence of reintubation and treatment failure could have been the reason for the lower incidence of VAP and sepsis in the HFNC group. The study by Hernández et al. did not find any significant difference in the incidence of VAP and sepsis between the HFNC and NIV groups.23

The physiological effects of HFNC, along with better tolerance to this modality, lesser incidence of VAP and sepsis, and lower rate of extubation failure, could have led to a lower number of patients with hospital stay of ≤7 and lower rate of mortality days in the HFNC group. The incidence of mortality was comparable in the two groups in the study by Hernández et al.23 However, other previous studies have also found lower length of hospital stay and lower mortality in the HFNC group as compared to NIV.11,33–35

It is worth noting that, although differences favoring HFNC were observed in some secondary outcomes, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Such differences are unlikely to be directly attributable to the allocated postextubation strategy and are probably multifactorial. Confirmation in larger, adequately powered trials is therefore required.

This study's strengths include its focus on a clinically relevant high-risk population, direct comparison of two commonly used noninvasive respiratory strategies, use of a patient-centered outcome (reintubation), and strong potential to influence postextubation management in the ICU. The study adds on the current literature comparing HFNC and NIV and suggests HFNC to be a better mode of oxygen support in these patients.

The study had certain limitations. Firstly, the attending teams could not be blinded to the study group, and a possible bias was unavoidable. Secondly, the study was single-centered study with a small sample size. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to other institutions with different patient populations, staffing patterns, or weaning protocols. Finally, the study primarily evaluated short-term outcomes and did not assess long-term clinical or functional outcomes.

Conclusion

This randomized comparative study provides insight into the clinical outcomes of HFNC and NIV in patients at high risk of extubation failure. High-flow nasal cannula applied immediately postextubation was associated with a lower rate of reintubation at 48 hours compared with NIV in our study population. However, given the single-center design and relatively small sample size, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Larger, adequately powered multicenter studies are required to validate these results and to determine whether HFNC confers consistent clinical benefit over NIV in high-risk extubation patients.

Clinical Significance

The present study helps the intensivists to choose the better modality of oxygen support to prevent reintubation in patients with high risk of extubation failure.
