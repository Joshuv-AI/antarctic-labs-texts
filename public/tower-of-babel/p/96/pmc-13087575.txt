Language-Mediated Thought Blocking in a Bilingual Patient Diagnosed With Schizoaffective Disorder: A Case Report
Authors: Sheila Rivera Avilés
Published: 2026

Thought blocking (TB) is a disturbance in the flow of thinking, characterized by sudden interruptions in the thought stream or speech, often followed by difficulty resuming the original topic (Uzman Özbek and Alptekin, 2021). TB is most associated with schizophrenia spectrum disorders and reflects disorganized cognitive processing and impaired thought organization. In bilingual individuals, cognitive and linguistic systems are further complicated by the need to regulate and switch between two languages—each potentially engaging distinct neural circuits (Marian et al., 2007). The relationship between bilingualism and psychotic symptoms has received limited empirical attention. Some studies suggest that symptoms may manifest differently across languages, with greater severity often reported in the first language (L1) (Schoeman, 2007). However, the current case presents an inverse pattern—greater TB in the second language (L2)—raising important questions about cognitive load, linguistic automaticity, and emotional access in psychotic language production. This linguistic variability aligns with a study by Stegmayer et al. (2017), who showed that specific features of formal thought disorder -such as thought blocking- are linked to underlying disruptions in laguage-related neural networks, highlighting how psychopathology can differentially affect language processing.

The patient was a mid 20-year-old bilingual (Spanish-English) female with a diagnosis of schizoaffective disorder. Patient has a history of multiple psychiatric hospitalizations, typically triggered by periods of elevated psychosocial stress and medication non-compliance. Upon admission on early September 2025, a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation was conducted. Clinical assessment was supported by the SCID-5-PD, PHQ-9, GAD-7, Social Phobia Inventory (SPIN), and the Young Schema Questionnaire (YSQ). A urine drug screen was negative for illicit substances. Patient has a medical history of hypertension, hyperprolactinemia, and vitamin D deficiency. She was oriented to person and place but exhibited severe verbigeration and perseveration in speech when interviewed in English. Initial pharmacological intervention included Lithium 600 mg and Abilify 10 mg daily for mood stabilization and psychosis. However, after two days with no significant clinical improvement or reduction in thought blocking, the psychiatry team transitioned her to Clozapine.

During the initial interviews, clinicians noted that the patient frequently paused mid-sentence, appeared to “lose her train of thought,” and struggled to resume conversation. The multidisciplinary treatment team—including psychiatry, psychology, and social work—observed that communication in English (L2) remained consistently fragmented and effortful. When the interviewing language was switched to Spanish (L1), the patient's speech became more continuous and expressive, allowing for a clear description of her symptoms, affective experiences, and psychosocial stressors. Team determined that patient was experiencing vivid dreams, delusions related to metaphysical themes and preoccupation with internal stimuli. The reduction in TB was evident across several sessions with improved spontaneity and prosody (15-day inpatient stabilization). No major medication changes coincided with the language switch, suggesting that the improvement in verbal flow was language-related rather than pharmacological. The patient was highly fluent in English and used it daily in professional and academic settings, indicating that the difference was not due to limited proficiency. Rather, it appeared linked to the emotional and cognitive accessibility of her native language.

This case highlights how language mediates cognitive and affective processes in psychosis. The patient's reduction in TB when using her native language may reflect greater automaticity and cognitive ease, allowing more efficient retrieval of semantic and emotional content. Research indicates that bilinguals process L1 more automatically, relying on implicit memory systems, whereas L2 processing engages more effortful, cortical-based control networks (Marian et al., 2007). In psychosis—where executive function and working memory are compromised—these demands may exacerbate cognitive disruptions, including TB. This is consistent with evidence showing the disruption of L2 during acute psychotic states, such as the 2014 study on bilingual teenagers which demonstrated a phenomenon of language regression to the mother tongue (El-Gabalawi & Khouzam, 2009). This regression suggests that the higher-order cortical functions required for L2 are more vulnerable to the neurocognitive strain of psychosis than the deeply ingrained, implicit networks of L1. Furthermore, L1 often carries richer emotional associations and facilitates affective communication (Pavlenko, 2006). During heightened emotional arousal, using L1 may allow for more integrated access to autobiographical and emotional memory networks, supporting coherent narrative formation (Pitta et al., 1978). In contrast, L2 can function as a “psychological buffer,” creating emotional distance but requiring greater conscious monitoring, which may increase vulnerability to speech blocking or derailment.

Recent computational linguistic work supports that individuals with schizophrenia demonstrate measurable disruptions in lexical-semantic connectivity and speech coherence, particularly under cognitive or emotional load (Arslan et al., 2025). In bilinguals, these disruptions could interact with the added cognitive demands of cross-language control. A study by Martín et al. (2024) further suggests that bilingual patients with psychosis exhibit differential neural activation when switching between languages, implicating prefrontal and temporal regions linked to both language control and thought organization. Clinically, these findings underscore the importance of assessing psychotic symptoms in both languages when working with bilingual patients. Language switching during assessment may not only reveal symptom variability but also improve diagnostic clarity and therapeutic engagement. For example, facilitating communication in L1 can reduce cognitive strain and allow for more authentic emotional expression, which in turn enhances rapport and treatment planning.

This case illustrates that language context can meaningfully modulate thought blocking and other formal thought disorder symptoms in bilingual individuals with psychosis. The observed improvement in cognitive flow when using the native language supports the hypothesis that L1 processing involves greater automaticity and reduced cognitive effort, facilitating more coherent thought expression. Clinicians should consider linguistic context as a critical factor in psychiatric evaluation and intervention, particularly for bilingual or multilingual patients presenting with psychotic symptoms.
