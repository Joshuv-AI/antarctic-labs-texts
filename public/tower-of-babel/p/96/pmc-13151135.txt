Evaluation of a patient educational brochure to increase knowledge of cervical cancer screening among women living with HIV in La Romana

Introduction

Persistent human papillomavirus (HPV) is the primary cause of cervical cancer, with approximately 70% of cervical cancer diagnoses around the world attributed to HPV types 16 and 18 [1]. In the Caribbean, it is estimated that approximately 15.8% of women are living with at least one of these two high-risk types [2]. Although cervical cancer is among the most preventable human malignancies [3], it is the second leading cause of cancer death among women in the Dominican Republic with over 1000 diagnoses and 700 deaths from cervical cancer yearly [2]. Among those who are most at risk for cervical cancer are women living with HIV (WLH) who face a six-fold increased risk of developing cervical cancer compared to the general population [4]. In part due to immunosuppression, WLH experience heightened vulnerability to persistent and reactivated HPV infection but differential risks persist even with viral suppression on antiretroviral therapy [3].

Women can significantly reduce their risk of acquiring high risk HPV types through a highly effective vaccine [5]. This vaccine is a primary cervical cancer prevention method that is optimal when given before onset of sexual activity [5]. Secondary prevention measures include cervical cancer screenings, which are recommended yearly for women between the ages of 30 and 49 through cytology (Pap smear) and HPV testing [5]. Despite well-established guidelines for prevention of cervical cancer, inequitable implementation of prevention methods across the globe results in stark disparities in cervical cancer diagnoses and deaths [5]. Therefore, global elimination goals prioritize offering prevention methods in lower and middle-income countries where, on average, only 44% of women have ever been screened for cervical cancer [5, 6].

In 2025, the Dominican Ministry of Health released updated guidelines for cervical cancer screening based on World Health Organization’s (WHO) Screen, Triage and Treat algorithm [7]. When the previous guidelines were released in 2007, there was no distinction made for screening among WLH versus women without HIV [8]. Still, most public and private centers base their cervical cancer screening procedures on these old guidelines recommending a traditional pap smear, then if the result is Atypical Squamous Cells of Undetermined Significance (ASCUS) or worse, referral for DNA HPV testing. According to the 2007 guidelines, if the DNA-HPV test is positive, the patient is referred for colposcopy biopsy to determine the corresponding treatment (Loop Electrosurgical Excision Procedure [LEEP], thermal ablation, hysterectomy, etc.) [8]. While public hospitals offer traditional pap smears free of cost, all screening procedures across the Dominican Republic are generally limited due to lack of resources, infrastructures and specialized personnel for diagnosing cervical cancer, which drives women to seek private healthcare services. The total cost of a pap smear, HPV test, and colposcopy in a private setting, on average, equals almost two-thirds of minimum monthly wage in DR (around RD$10,000.00 [about USD$160.0]), presenting a significant barrier to access [9, 10].

Further, a systematic review found that a prevalent barrier to cervical cancer screening in lower and middle-income countries is lack of knowledge about cervical cancer and screening among persons with HIV [11]. In the Dominican Republic specifically, stigma and fear regarding HPV, and inconsistent follow-up of abnormal Pap results due to cost and uncertainty about provider recommendations emerged as key themes during focus groups with 64 Dominican women from urban, suburban, and rural locations [12]. In another study, surveys from gynecological providers in Santo Domingo and Monte Plata found that providers often do not follow age-based screening guidelines nor have they adopted annual screening procedures due to cost, high demand for follow-up services, and reduced capacity to reach populations who may benefit from regular screening [13]. Further, in the Dominican Republic, studies report low health literacy, which is associated with more hospitalizations, greater use of emergency care, and decreased use of preventative services [14–16]. Thus, focusing on increasing knowledge through carefully designed patient education materials can promote prevention and use of life saving health services [17].

Given widespread patient barriers to cervical cancer screening, including low health literacy, thoughtful provision of patient educational materials in a clinical setting is warranted so that WLH are informed about prevention opportunities, symptoms, and ways to improve their health [11]. In line with a growing emphasis on self-advocacy for prevention and management of cancer [18], we created a patient-facing brochure on cervical cancer to be distributed within a clinic, Clínica de Familia, which serves WLH in La Romana, Dominican Republic.

The brochure includes information on HPV and cervical cancer including WHO recommendations: suggested frequency of testing, symptoms, prevention strategies; and locations for testing. It was translated to Spanish and evaluated by 30 patients at Clínica de Familia as formative work for a clinical trial called Implementation of Screen, Treat, and Triage for Women Living with HIV in La Romana (iSTAR). The goals of this paper are to (1) summarize the findings of cognitive interviews conducted with women evaluating the brochure to assess the acceptability and perceived usefulness of this approach, and (2) to provide recommendations for the design and content of future educational materials on cervical cancer that can adequately address knowledge gaps among WLH and facilitate patient self-advocacy in a clinical setting.

Methods

Inclusion criteria

Participants were 18 years and older, of female sex at birth, able to read and write in Spanish, living with HIV, and receiving HIV care at Clínica de Familia La Romana.

Ethics

The Institutional Review Board of Columbia University Medical Center (Approval Number AAA3441) and the Dominican Republic’s ethics committee, Consejo Nacional de Bioética en Salud (CONABIOS, Registry number 066–2024), approved all research activities. All data collection took place at Clínica de Familia in La Romana, Dominican Republic.

Prior to the commencement of recruitment and data collection, three research staff were trained in research design and data collection, ethical conduct of research, and cultural awareness by the on-site Principial Investigator, Dr. Pamela Baez Caraballo. Research staff at the clinic explained the study procedures to the study participants at the time of recruitment, and written documentation of informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. Data collection was conducted by three fluent Spanish-speaking research staff: one permanently resides in the Dominican Republic and is employed at Clinica de Familia and the other two are students obtaining their master’s degrees in public health in the United States.

Setting

Located in La Romana, in the southeastern part of the Dominican Republic, Clínica de Familia is one of the largest community-based, non-governmental outpatient clinics in the country. Clínica de Familia provides comprehensive primary adult and pediatric care, HIV-specialized services, and sexual and reproductive health services to diverse and vulnerable populations. Clínica de Familia was founded in 1999 through a partnership among Columbia University’s International Family AIDS Program, Complejo Micaelinao/Casa de Paz, and the MIR foundation, and has been operating independently since 2010. As a registered HIV unit under the Dominican Ministry of Public Health, Clínica de Familia works with a multidisciplinary staff of approximately 135 employees delivering free comprehensive primary and HIV-specialized outpatient care, psychosocial support, community- and home-based services, and an annual summer camp for children and adolescents living with HIV. The clinic also offers free services in STI diagnosis and treatment, as well as obstetrics and gynecology care, and an on-site laboratory with CD4 and HIV viral load testing to over 3,000 people living with HIV.

Development of the patient brochure

The brochure was initially conceptualized by the research team, who identified key information and recommendations. Afterwards, it was translated to Spanish by a bilingual researcher at Columbia. Once translated, several providers at Clínica de Familia (2 OB/GYNs and 6 general practitioners) provided feedback to ensure accuracy and clarity and made any necessary changes. Cultural appropriateness was ensured by careful revision of the brochure by the research team and providers, paying particular attention to the translations to include simple language and colloquialisms, reducing medical jargon, and encouraging readers to seek local public clinics for low-cost pap smear available at most public hospitals in DR, and showcasing the mixed nature of Dominican women’s ethnicity. Recruitment

Participants were recruited at Clínica de Familia through provider referrals during routine clinic visits, using purposive and convenience sampling approaches [19]. Clínica de Familia has an established HIV program serving people with HIV, and providers were aware which patients were enrolled in the program. Providers were informed of the study eligibility criteria and identified potentially eligible patients during routine care. Interested patients were then referred to the research team, who assessed interest in participation, confirmed eligibility, reviewed study procedures and obtained informed consent. Thirty interested participants gave their consent at the time of recruitment.

Data collection

Interviewers followed a semi-structured interview guide (Appendix I) to examine the clarity of language, emotional reactions, and visual design, as well as the cultural adaptation of an educational brochure on cervical cancer screening and prevention (Fig. 1). Sessions were audio-recorded and supplemented with notes. Data on sociodemographic, health status, barriers to care, and health literacy (Short Test of Functional Health Literacy in Adults [STOFHLA]) [20] were collected via a self-reported questionnaire on REDCap administered by research staff. Interviews lasted between 9 min and 32 s and 38 min and 36 s with an average of 19 min and 8 s (SD ± 6.8). Each participant was compensated with RD$500 Dominican pesos for their time, which is roughly equivalent to US$10.

Cognitive interviews

Cognitive interviewing is a research method that invites participants to verbalize their thoughts and feelings as they examine information, with the goal of identifying comprehension and problematic language or material [21]. While it is commonly used for evaluating survey measures, cognitive interviews can be applied to evaluation of other content such as printed or video materials [22, 23]. Cognitive interviewing employs purposeful recruitment whereby researchers select participants with desired qualities or experiences [21]. We intentionally recruited a population of WLH receiving care at Clinica de Familia to understand how they perceived the information prior to sharing the brochure with other WLH who receive care at Clinica de Familia. It is a particularly important strategy in global health research when material is developed by researchers who differ from the intended target population in terms of sociodemographic, or other aspects of their identity [24]. In the case of our study, the brochure was developed by healthcare professionals with advanced knowledge of cervical cancer and evaluated by a group of WLH who received care from these professionals.

Data analysis

Interview recordings were transcribed word-for-word and analyzed through thematic analysis [25]. Findings were guided by two frameworks: (1) the Health Information Technology (IT) Evaluation Model (Health-ITUEM), a framework developed for usability evaluation of health IT tools that comprises four factors that demonstrate strong internal consistency reliability: quality of work life, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and user control [26]; and (2) the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (PEMAT) for printable materials, an instrument with strong internal consistency used to measure the understandability and actionability of patient educational materials [27].

Health-ITUEM

While originally created as a technology evaluation tool [28], we decided to utilize this model to guide our qualitative coding process due to the applicability of its key components to brochure evaluation. To illustrate, the sub-constructs of information needs [28]] and memorability [28]] under the theme of perceived ease of use highlight the utility of the educational material in reinforcing screening or other preventative measures or alternatively the lack of such information in the brochure. Similarly, the flexibility and customizability [28]] and competency [28]] sub-constructs of perceived usefulness guide identification of stylistic or information needs to facilitate retention of information. Quality of work life and user control were deemed irrelevant to our use case.

PEMAT

The two key constructs of PEMAT “Understandability” and “Actionability” were merged with Health-ITUEM themes to identify characteristics or details of the brochure that made the content (1) easy or difficult to understand, and (2) actionable. In particular, under the Health-ITUEM construct of “Perceived Ease of Use,” we added a code for the simplicity of the language “Plain Language,” attractiveness “Design Attractiveness” and perception “Visual Cues” of visual cues, and readability of the text “Easy to Read Text.” Under the “Information Needs” construct, we added a code for specific “Cues to Action.” Themes were derived from the Health-ITUEM model and merged with PEMAT domains and characteristics.

Using these two frameworks, one of the two multiple principal investigators (MPIs) developed an initial codebook. The codebook was shared with the five-member coding team. Two of the coding team members, who conducted the cognitive interviews, provided feedback on the initial codebook and adjusted code definitions to ensure applicability to key takeaways from the interviews. Once all team members agreed upon an initial codebook, it was translated to Spanish and added to Dedoose software in a project alongside the 30 interview transcripts.

Afterwards, five bilingual researchers coded 10% of the interview transcripts independently and met over video call to discuss initial findings, reconcile any discrepancies between the five researchers, and update code definitions. After this initial meeting, the final codebook was agreed upon. Transcripts that had informed the initial codebook development were subsequently re-coded to ensure consistency with the final version. Theoretically-guided codebook and definitions

Using the theoretically-guided codebook, the five researchers divided 20 of the remaining transcripts amongst each other, with 2 coders assigned to each transcript. After meeting 3 times, finishing the 20 transcripts, and discussing findings, the 7 final transcripts were independently coded, with 3 researchers coding 2 transcripts each, and 1 researcher coding 1 final transcript. The group continued to meet weekly to discuss any memos added to Dedoose or resolve discrepancies until all 30 transcripts were fully coded. Code definitions were iteratively updated to address coding discrepancies, enhance clarity of code meanings, and ensure consistent application across coders. To mitigate potential bias, analytic decisions were discussed iteratively among team members, discrepancies were resolved through consensus, and reflexive memos were used to document evolving interpretations. This process ensures rigor and trustworthiness of the final analysis [25]. A single coder reviewed all transcripts and brought any remaining code discrepancies to the entire group so that each researcher could explain their rationale for the particular way they coded the text and the group could collectively reach a consensus [29].

Positionality and reflexivity

The research team engaged in ongoing reflexive practice throughout study design, data collection, and analysis. The MPIs have extensive experience conducting community-engaged HIV care and research in the United States and Dominican Republic, including long-standing partnerships with community-based organizations serving populations disproportionately affected by HIV. Several team members hold backgrounds in public health, which informed sensitivity to the experiences of people with HIV navigating healthcare systems, stigma, and structural barriers to care. Data collection was conducted by trained research staff with experience working with individuals affected by HIV. Nonetheless, the team acknowledges that professional roles may have shaped how participants framed their responses and how themes were interpreted.

Results

Participant characteristics

Participant age ranged from 20 to 59 years with an average of 41 years (SD ± 11). Most women (n = 28; 94%) identified as heterosexual, 1 (3%) as bisexual and 1 (3%) did not identify with any sexuality. Half (n = 15) of the participants were single, 10 (33%) were in a relationship, 2 (7%) were married, 2 (7%) were widowed, and 1 (3%) was in an open marriage. Most participants (n = 17; 57%) were unemployed, 11 participants (36%) worked full-time, and 2 (7%) worked part-time. The highest proportion of women (N = 9; 31%) reported that elementary school was their highest education level, followed by some high school, no diploma (N = 7; 23%) and high school diploma or equivalent (N = 6; 20%), then some college (N = 4; 13%), a college degree (N = 2; 7%), no formal education (N = 1; 3%), and professional or graduate degree (N = 1; 3%). Over half of the WLH (n = 16; 53%) reported one or more comorbid health conditions including wasting syndrome from HIV (n = 6), high cholesterol (n = 5), shingles (n = 3), thrush (n = 3), tuberculosis (n = 2), pneumocystis carinii pneumonia (n = 2), insomnia (n = 2), cardiovascular disease (n = 2), arthritis (n = 1), high blood pressure (n = 1), toxoplasmosis (n = 1), hypertension (n = 1), tachycardia (n = 1), cancer (n = 1), diabetes (n = 1), alcohol abuse (n = 1), anxiety (n = 1), bipolar disorder (n = 1), depression (n = 1), and sexual dysfunction (n = 1).

Qualitative findings

Information needs

Participants identified the most impactful information in the brochure which included the recommended frequency of testing, vaccination, and prevention strategies such as condom use and smoking cessation, among others. One woman said,

“Every three years, the medical community recommends that we have it [cervical cancer screening] done every three years, which means we have to keep a close eye on it. Because sometimes we don’t do it, and when we get so busy with work, with the kids, with the family at home, we sometimes forget. And we have to be consistent, we have to be persistent.” (Que cada 3 años, la sociedad médica nos ofrece que nos lo hagamos cada 3 años, o sea, tenemos que tenerlo bien pendiente. Porque a lo mejor a veces no lo hacemos y cuando viene a ver por tan ocupado la ocupación, que los muchachos, que la familia en el hogar, cuando viene a veces lo olvida. Y eso hay que estar constante, hay que ser persistente.) (P2; 43 years old).

Another commented on the image of vaginal secretion, odor, and blood noting,

“That’s something, as I say, that’s extremely important to me, because sometimes doctors ask us questions, and sometimes we don’t answer out of embarrassment, but if we’re looking at the image, for example, we can say, look, it’s similar to this, and that helps us feel a little more confident with the doctors.” (Eso es algo, como les digo, que para mí es sumamente importante, porque a veces los médicos nos preguntan algo, y a veces uno por vergüenza, a veces no contesta, pero ya si en la imagen está uno, por ejemplo, dice, miren, parecido a esto, y algo que nos ayuda a tener un poco más de confianza con los médicos.) (P15; 41 years old).

Some women said that the symptoms section caught their attention the most. One participant mentioned the importance of the text “where it says that [symptoms] normally [disappear] on [their] own.” (Adonde dice que normalmente desaparece por su propia cuenta.) (P1; 47 years old).

Importantly, the brochure indicated necessary cues to action. Several women said the educational material motived them to get tested, act upon symptoms such as pain when urinating, and “get vaccinated, practice safe sex, for example, by using condoms, not smoking, and things like that, and get your routine checkup.” (hacerse la vacuna, practicar el sexo seguro, por ejemplo, usando preservativos, no fumar, y cosas así, hacerse su chequeo rutinario.) (P11; 39 years old).

Many urged that the brochure was particularly important for women who have never been screened before. One woman mentioned she was going to see her doctor about pain that she had been experiencing after reviewing the content,

“Well, I learned a lot from this brochure because I have pain here, and here I am looking at it. Yes. So now I know that I have to come to the doctor, go to the doctor to get good treatment.” (Bueno, sobre este folleto yo aprendí mucho porque a mí me da un dolor acá y aquí lo estoy viendo. Sí. Entonces ya yo sé que yo tengo que venir al médico, acudir al médico para tratar bien.) (P8; 32 years old).

While participants were generally pleased with the brochure, some identified more information was needed. Specifically, women wanted to know more about the vaccine, including at what age someone can be vaccinated, the cost of the vaccine and where to access it. Additionally, some mentioned they would like more information about different types of HPV and what it means to be living with cervical cancer. Some women also wanted more concise, updated screening recommendations for the most vulnerable populations, indicating that the screening should be more frequent than every three years. One said,

“And when I also mention, excuse me, that vulnerable populations should be tested every three years, I repeat, three years is too long. In vulnerable populations, the time frame should be reduced to a minimum of every six months.” (Y cuando menciono también, perdón, en las poblaciones vulnerables, que deben hacerse la prueba cada tres años, vuelvo y repito, tres años es mucho. Deberá de ser, en poblaciones vulnerables, deberá de bajarse el tiempo, deberá de ser mínimo cada seis meses.) (P21; 52 years old).

Additionally, several participants mentioned the cigarette icon in the brochure and were curious to learn more about how smoking cessation can help with prevention.

“Actually, the issue of cigarettes, the issue of whether they smoke and that sort of thing. Because I didn’t know, I didn’t know that it could affect our bodies.” (Realmente el tema de los cigarros, el tema de si fuman y ese tipo de cosas. Porque no sabía, no sabía que podía afectar en nuestro organismo) (P30; 30 years old).

Memorability

Participants reported key takeaways from the brochure which included the importance of annual check-ups, looking out for symptoms, asking for routine screening, using a condom, visiting the doctor when experiencing symptoms, starting to screen at an early age, and avoiding smoking.

“Well, at least it covers the basics, which is knowing what cervical cancer is, what symptoms women may experience, where they can get tested to find out if they have cervical cancer, and how to prevent it.” (Bueno, porque por lo menos tiene lo básico, que es saber qué es el cáncer cérvico uterino, cuáles son los síntomas que puede presentar la mujer, y dónde también puede hacerse la prueba para saber si tienen cáncer de cuello uterino y también cómo prevenirlo.) (P22; 50 years old).

One woman noted the increased vulnerability to the disease experienced by WLH, “Women who are HIV positive, women with a family history, they’re people that are at risk, and that are exposed to cervical cancer.” (Mujeres VIH positiva, mujeres que tienen antecedentes familiares, son personas que tienen un riesgo, y que están expuestas a tener cáncer de cuello uterino.) (P21, 52 years old).

Another woman thought the brochure, “Is something important because it’s about how to prevent cancer – cervical cancer – and if it’s detected on time, early, it may be possible to treat it, but if it’s detected at an advanced stage, there’s no solution at all.” (Es algo importante porque se trata de los puntos como uno puede prevenir el cáncer - del cuello uterino - y si lo detectan a tiempo, temprano, puede ser que lo puedan, que tenga solución, pero si lo detectan ya avanzado, no hay solución para nada.) (P4; 39 years old).

One woman highlighted, “Through exams and cell checks, you become aware of the situation you may be going through and can prevent it.” (A través de los exámenes y los chequeos celulares uno se viene dando cuenta de la situación que uno puede estar pasando y poder prevenir sobre él.) (P16; 46 years old).

Several women said the content of the brochure was easy to remember and can be helpful in future settings. Specifically, participants noted the usefulness of a combination of text and images and how they facilitate retention of information. One said,

“In other words, apart from defining [HPV], they also put it here in abbreviated form, so that if you see it somewhere else, in another site, in another context, you’ll know what they’re talking about. Or on social media, anywhere, you’ll already know [what it means].” (O sea, aparte de que lo definen, también lo ponen aquí en abreviatura, que si uno lo ve en otro lugar, en otro sitio, en otro conjunto, ya uno va a saber de qué están hablando a uno. O en alguna red social, cualquier sitio, ya uno sabe.) (P27; 31 years old).

Perceived ease of use

Participants said they appreciated and were easily able to understand the plain language used in the brochure. One said,

“Because it’s clear, I mean, for all types of women, whether they’re young, of legal age, or even adolescents, I think they can see something in this.” (Porque está claro, o sea, para todo tipo de mujer, ya sea joven, sea mayor de edad, hasta una adolescente, o sea, yo creo que pueden ver algo de esto.) (P27; 31 years old).

Some believed that women of any age could benefit from and understand the educational material since it did not use “complicated terms.”

“Here it breaks down what cancer is, so if it’s broken down there, one must understand what it is and understand that there’s nothing to improve, because it explains everything concretely.” (No, porque aquí desglosa lo que es el cáncer, entonces ya si ahí está desglosado, uno debe de entender lo que es y entender que no hay que mejorar nada, porque está explicando todo concretamente.) (P16; 46 years old).

The attractiveness of the design “motivated me to keep reading,” (me motivaba más a seguir leyendo.) (P15; 41 years old) one participant said. Women commented on the colors used, with one saying her favorite color was pink and another appreciating the use of pink since she perceived it to be a “feminine” color.

“And it doesn’t tire your eyes when you look at it. Because there are colors that when you look at them, you can’t see them very well. And I can’t see very small print. But I can see this one perfectly. Because it has colors, like a play of colors. And they are very striking colors. Pink, fuchsia. It’s a very striking color. Especially for women.” (Y como que no te fatiga al mirarlo. Porque hay colores que cuando tú los miras como que no los puedes ver bien. Y yo no veo la letra muy pequeña. Pero esta yo la veo perfectamente. Porque tiene unos colores, como que tiene un juego de colores. Y son colores como muy llamativos. Rosado, fucsia. Es un color muy llamativo. Especialmente para las mujeres.) (P2; 43 years old).

Easy to read text was reported by many participants who said the size, color, and font of the text was adequate. One said, “[The words] are very readable. They are very understandable. Anyone can read them and understand them.” (Son muy legibles. Son muy entendibles. Cualquiera persona puede leerlo y puede entender.) (P9; 35 years old).

Another woman commented, “And for the woman who may not have much knowledge, perhaps of how to read, the images, as if they were infographics, would help her a lot.” (Y para aquella mujer quizá que no tenga un poco conocimiento, quizá de lectura, las imágenes, como si fuera una infografía, le ayuda mucho.) (P22; 50 years old).

Visual cues helped to improve understandability of the brochure content. Women noted they liked the image of the cervix, vaginal symptoms, and condoms, in particular. While some women thought diversity of women portrayed in the brochure could be improved, they generally thought that the graphics were representative of Latina women,

“I mean, just like any other brochure. And something I liked is that they included several characteristics. Women. It doesn’t just refer to women with light or dark skin.” (O sea, como cualquier otro folleto. Y algo que me gustó es que incluyeron varias como características. Mujeres. No se refiere solo a las mujeres del claro ni piel oscura.) (P18; 28 years old).

Flexibility/personalization

Participants suggested design changes such as using more “striking” colors like turquoise or similar shades of blue. Participants also discussed the visibility of the material; one woman suggested using darker colors like black for the font, so the text is easier to read. Another urged,

“Make it a little clearer, because there are many people who suffer from poor eyesight, and colors appear blurry to them.” (Aclarar un poco más, porque hay muchas personas que sufren de la vista, y como que los colores se ven así borrosos.) (P24; 25 years old).

Contrastingly, one woman suggested making the font smaller to add space for more information, “at the bottom we can provide any information, let’s say about social services, for example, and we can add the part about breasts, about breast cancer.” (Y abajo podemos brindar cualquier información, vamos a decir como servicio social, por ejemplo, y podemos agregar lo de la mamá, lo del cáncer de mamá.) (P9; 35 years old).

One participant suggested changing the overall design of the brochure, noting,

“My experience was that I realized that pink is not very favorable in terms of what it has to do with the brochure…Pink is a sign that there will be an infection if there isn’t one already.” (Mi experiencia fue, que más o menos yo me di cuenta que el rosado no es muy favorable en cuestión de lo que tiene que ver con el folleto… Pues ya el rosado es señal de que si no hay va a haber infección.) (P3; 57 years old).

Several participants wanted to either add or remove images. Some women suggested adding more images for people who cannot read or have difficulty reading, “so they are more aware,” (Para que estén más concientizadas.) (P10; 59 years old).

One participant wanted to change the images altogether, “These images where it says how to prevent cervical cancer. Those graphics, I don’t know, to me, I find them a bit childish. These are more visible, but I find them a bit childish.” (Estas imágenes donde dice como si, como prevengo el cáncer cérvico uterino. Esos gráficos, no sé para mí, para mi gusto yo los veo como un poco infantil. Estos se ven más, pero estos los veo como un poco como infantil.) (P22; 50 years old).

Further, others wanted the images to be larger, so they were easier to understand, and suggested adding arrows to highlight important imagery in the brochure.

Participants also suggested important and relevant topics to add to the brochure such as strategies for how to stay in contact with a care provider when you don’t have a phone or other consistent form of communication, information about breast cancer screening, ultrasounds, and other gynecological procedures, ovarian cancer, and men’s health. One person also suggested adding factors that increase vulnerability to cervical cancer,

“You could also add genetic factors, such as people who are at risk, as we just mentioned, sex workers, HIV-positive patients, and patients who have genetics, that is, who have had family members who have gone through the process.” (También se le podría agregar los factores genéticos, como las personas que están en riesgo, como acabamos de mencionar, las personas mujeres de los negocios, paciente VIH positiva, y las pacientes que tienen genética, o sea, han tenido familiar que ha pasado por el proceso.) (P21; 52 years old).

Finally, women called for more positive, affirming language such as reinforcing that cervical cancer can be prevented and treated if detected early enough, as well as gentle reminders to screen regularly.

Competency

Overall, participants had a positive perception of the brochure content, noting that it helped them understand more about cervical cancer. One said,

“Well, to be honest, I don’t really understand much about this. Now that I’m understanding a little more and reading up on it, I see that you’re right, because I see here that it says to get tested every 5 or 10 years starting at age 30, and I’m already 30 years old, so I’d like to get tested because I didn’t really know about this before.” (Bueno, en verdad yo no entiendo mucho de esto. Ahora que me estoy entendiendo un poquito más y estoy leyendo y veo cosas que en verdad tienes razón, porque veo aquí que dice prueba cada 5 o 10 años a partir de los 30 años de edad y ya yo tengo 30 años de edad y digo me gustaría hacerme eso porque no tenía conocimiento en verdad de esto.) (P7; 30 years old).

Similarly, another participant said, “If you go to the doctor for an illness that you are not aware of, at least you are not going to the doctor and investigating. You are now prepared, whether you have an illness or not, at least you know that you don’t have it because you went to the doctor.” (De ir al médico para tener una enfermedad que ya uno no se da cuenta, por lo menos no va al médico y investiga. Ya usted está preparado, sea que tenga una enfermedad o no la tenga, por lo menos ya usted sabe que no la tiene porque fue al médico.) (P3; 57 years old).

Discussion

Thorough evaluation of patient-facing materials by the target population is vital for ensuring that content is comprehensive and understandable prior to distribution to the public. Women living with HIV have distinct information needs for cervical cancer prevention because HIV-related immunosuppression increases susceptibility to persistent HPV infection and progression to cervical dysplasia and cancer [30–32]. As a result, screening recommendations for WLH often differ from those for women without HIV and may vary further based on prior results and clinical history. In our interviews, participants’ attention to screening frequency and follow-up steps suggests that HIV status shapes both risk perception and the practical questions needed to initiate or sustain screening (e.g., “how often,” “where,” and “what happens if my result is abnormal”). These findings underscore the importance of explicitly linking HIV-related vulnerability to clear, actionable screening guidance that supports self-advocacy during clinical encounters.

We used the Health-ITUEM and PEMAT frameworks to guide analysis of how brochure content, readability, visual design, and actionability were perceived by WLH. A particular strength of this formative evaluation is using complementary, well-validated frameworks (Health-ITUEM and PEMAT) to synthesize design and comprehension issues relevant to patient education. Although the Health ITUEM was developed for health IT, its constructs (perceived usefulness, ease of use, memorability) are directly applicable to printed educational materials because they describe how users engage with and recall health information. Health-ITUEM therefore complements PEMAT’s focus on understandability and actionability, yielding a richer implementation-oriented interpretation of brochure strengths and limitations. PEMAT assesses clarity and actionability well, but adds little about perceived usefulness or implementation considerations (for example, how patients will act on the information, whether they perceive it as credible, or whether content creates cognitive burden, areas where Health-ITUEM added useful insight). These characteristics are not limited to digital tools; they are central to designing printed materials that patients will notice, remember, and use to engage with care. Systematic reviews of educational strategies for cervical cancer prevention show that education alone can increase knowledge, but that the largest and most consistent improvements in screening participation occur when education is combined with navigation or other support services [33, 34]. Overall, participants perceived the brochure as acceptable, useful, and generally comprehensive, but they requested clearer, HIV-specific guidance and but wanted clarification about the frequency of recommended procedures. For example, one woman felt the three-year interval cited in parts of the brochure was too long for WLH and suggested six-monthly screening — a comment that highlights confusion about risk-stratified guidance rather than a firm preference for a non-evidence-based interval. WHO guidelines differentiate risk groups: screening intervals for women without HIV can be longer (e.g., multi-year intervals depending on modality), whereas WLH typically require more frequent surveillance and prompt follow-up after abnormal results; these nuanced differences need to be stated plainly in patient materials to avoid misinterpretation [4]. Moreover, WLH with prior abnormal findings or immunosuppression require more intensive surveillance (shorter intervals and quicker diagnostic follow-up), so the brochure should explicitly describe (in simple terms) how recommendations change after an abnormal test [35]. We therefore recommend expanding the screening section to: (a) clearly state the routine interval for WLH versus women without HIV, (b) provide explicit cues-to-action for abnormal results (e.g., “If your result is abnormal, you may need to return in X months for a follow-up or colposcopy”), and (c) include contact/clinic navigation details for cost and access questions. Participants’ feedback also emphasized heterogeneous learning needs (visuals, plain text, and culturally representative images) and the importance of including concrete, local access information (where to get vaccinated or screened, approximate costs, and clinic navigation). Several participants urged the need for more and larger graphics for those with limited literacy — a finding that aligns with prior literature showing visual aids and simplified materials improve comprehension and recall in low-literacy populations [36].

Specifically, WLH asked for clearer information about HPV vaccination eligibility and access, more plain-language explanation of HPV types and symptoms, and clearer instructions on what to do if symptoms or abnormal results occur. Request about vaccine access are particularly salient in contexts where national campaigns have emphasized younger cohorts; patients need clear wording about who is eligible, whether the vaccine is available to adults, and where to obtain it locally [37]. Our findings did not provide a conclusion as to whether participants were unaware of adolescent vaccination campaigns or were asking about adult access; either way, adding clinic-specific information and referral pathways would reduce uncertainty. Although requests to expand content (e.g., to include breast cancer) were beyond the brochure’s scope, these suggestions indicate patient interest in clinic-based educational pamphlets and support the feasibility of this format for broader preventive topics. Systematic reviews indicate that education can improve knowledge and screening behavior, but the largest effects on uptake are seen when education is paired with navigation or community outreach; thus, while brochures are acceptable and useful as cues-to-action, they are most likely to increase screening when embedded within a multicomponent implementation strategy [38].

Prior evaluations of printed materials show mixed impact on behavior in high-resource settings, and more positive effects in lower-resource contexts when materials are paired with navigation or outreach; this heterogeneity underscores the need to test brochure distribution as part of a package rather than as a stand-alone intervention [39–43]. For example, a U.S. clinic-based trial found limited impact of a pamphlet al.one on knowledge and beliefs, whereas studies in lower-resource settings have reported greater uptake when printed education is part of a broader, supportive program [43]. However, this study was conducted in the U.S., where internet access is high in contrast to settings with limited access to digital information or strong structural barriers to care. In resource limited settings, printed materials paired with navigation have tended to be more effective in increasing screening participation [44, 45]. Consistent with this point, trials in resource-limited settings (including Ethiopia) have found increased screening uptake following health education interventions; these results bolster the idea that brochures can contribute meaningfully to outreach when local access and navigation supports are available [39]. Taken together, the evidence indicates brochures are acceptable and can increase awareness, but their effect on sustained uptake is context-dependent and typically greatest when paired with navigation — a key consideration for iSTAR implementation planning. With targeted edits (clear HIV-specific guidance, larger/culturally representative visuals, and local access/navigation information), the brochure is acceptable and likely to improve knowledge and self-efficacy among WLH, but its capacity to increase sustained screening uptake should be tested as part of a multicomponent strategy (e.g., brochure + navigation) in a formal implementation evaluation.

Limitations

Selection bias [46] was a limitation. We experienced several challenges when recruiting participants to complete a cognitive interview. Despite their interest, many women had limited literacy and could not read. There were also several language barriers as many women who receive care at Clinica de Familia are from Haiti and their primary language is Haitian Creole. Thus, our sample is not necessarily representative of the population of WLH that Clinica de Familia serves and could differ from the women attending other HIV care centers in the Dominican Republic which significantly limits generalizability. These recruitment challenges highlight a need for additional strategies to share cervical cancer screening information with women at the clinic which may include translating the brochure to other languages and including more representative visuals so that women do not have to rely on the text to absorb key information. We aim to further develop this brochure to include more visuals and less text so that we can expand inclusion criteria and include those who may not be able to read or write in Spanish. Social desirability bias is another limitation [47]. Although we sought to gather both negative and positive feedback regarding the brochure’s content, researchers and clinicians involved in recruitment and data collection were employed at the clinic where WLH received care which could have influenced the way they responded to questions about the strengths and weaknesses of the brochure. Lastly, we used a deductive approach for analyzing our qualitative findings. Heavy reliance on a theoretically pre-specified codebook may have constrained inductive discovery.

Conclusion

Overall, overwhelmingly positive feedback indicated printed learning materials in a clinical setting are an acceptable and useful means of sharing crucial information to support patient uptake of and adherence with screening procedures. Findings indicate educational brochures such as the one evaluated in the present study are needed to increase awareness of cervical cancer among WLH. Feedback will help guide changes in future iterations of the brochure prior to wider distribution among women at Clinica de Familia or other sexual health clinics. Future research should assess whether educational brochures lead to greater health literacy about cervical cancer and screening. Providers’ perspectives should also be explored to better understand how educational materials can be integrated into routine clinical practice and patient education.
