Tail‐Group Modulated Covalent Functionalization of Solvent‐Dispersible MXenes for Selectivity‐Tunable Gas Sensing at Room Temperature
Authors: Seongeun Lee, Tae Yun Ko, Ajit K Jena, Hohyung Kang, Junyeol Jeon, Woosuk Yang, Seok Hyeon Kim, Hee‐Tae Jung, Seung‐Cheol Lee, Seon Joon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe advancement of highly selective and sensitive gas sensors is essential for various fields, and MXene has emerged as a strong candidate for chemiresistive gas sensing due to its high electrical conductivity, tunable surface chemistry, and ability to operate at room temperature. This study demonstrates a versatile strategy for covalently functionalizing Ti3C2T x MXenes using diazonium salts with diverse tail groups, enabling tunable gas selectivity. By systematically modulating surface energy through the grafting of hydrophilic (sulfanilic acid diazonium) and hydrophobic (4‐octylaniline diazonium and 4‐(heptadecafluorooctyl)aniline diazonium) tail groups, MXene surfaces are tuned to interact strongly with specific target gases. Using this strategy, this work achieves distinct gas response patterns and cross‐reactivity toward other gases is minimized, while density functional theory calculations revealed enhanced gas adsorption energies due to ligand‐specific interactions. Functionalized MXenes via diazotization chemistry exhibits high selectivity toward either ammonia, acetone, or ethanol while exhibiting a large enhancement in gas sensitivity compared to pristine MXene. This ligand‐tailored functionalization strategy enables precise tuning of gas selectivity by controlling the tail group structure and ligand grafting density, providing a versatile platform for developing advanced MXene‐based multi‐target gas sensors.Keywords: gas sensor, MXene, selectivity, surface functionalization, tail group modulation

Introduction

The development of gas sensors is critical for a wide range of applications, including environmental monitoring, healthcare, and industrial safety.[ 1 , 2 ] Among the various types of gas sensors, semiconducting gas sensors based on metal oxide semiconductors (MOS) are widely utilized due to their high sensitivity, cost‐effectiveness, and compatibility with conventional semiconductor fabrication processes.[ 3 , 4 , 5 ] However, achieving selective gas detection remains a significant challenge. MOS gas sensors typically suffer from low gas selectivity and require high operating temperatures of 200–300 °C, leading to excessive power consumption that hinders their use in portable and integrated devices.[ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ]

To address these limitations, 2D materials have gained attention due to their high specific surface area, surface tunability, and ability to operate at room temperature.[ 10 , 11 , 12 ]

Among them, MXenes, a versatile family of 2D transition metal carbides, nitrides, and carbonitrides, stand out as the most promising candidates for chemiresistive gas sensors.[ 13 , 14 , 15 ] Unlike other 2D materials, MXenes combine high electrical conductivity with a dense distribution of surface functional groups,[ 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] resulting in exceptional gas sensing sensitivity and low limit‐of‐detection (LOD) at room temperature. Additionally, the ease of processing MXenes into dispersible inks[ 20 , 21 , 22 ] and fabricating them into thin films[ 23 , 24 , 25 ] on diverse substrates provides substantial advantages for developing advanced gas sensors.

While considerable progress has been made in enhancing the sensitivity of gas sensors, achieving selective gas detection remains a critical yet challenging goal. Selectivity is crucial because gas sensors are often exposed to complex gas mixtures, and distinguishing target gases accurately is essential for practical applications. Conventional approaches to enhancing gas selectivity involve tuning the material composition to modify charge transfer[ 26 , 27 ] or electronic band structures,[ 28 , 29 ] as well as adjusting surface chemistry to improve affinity toward specific molecules.[ 30 , 31 ] These strategies are often employed in sensor arrays, where multiple sensors with distinct response patterns are combined, and data mining techniques are applied. However, achieving a broad range of distinct response patterns requires a highly tunable material platform.

In this context, MXenes present exceptional potential for selective gas sensing due to their inherently versatile chemical structures. MXenes not only maintain high electrical conductivity and dispersibility but also offer a broad range of chemical compositions that interact differently with gas molecules. The densely populated surface functional groups of MXenes can be chemically modified to alter surface energy and molecular affinity, further expanding opportunities for achieving selective detection. A promising approach to modulate the surface chemistry of MXenes is through the attachment of organic ligands. Previous studies have explored this strategy; for instance, Kim et al. developed ultrathin films of ADOPA‐functionalized MXene composites via a self‐assembly method, achieving high sensitivity toward volatile organic compounds (VOCs). [ 32 ] Similarly, Chen et al. reported that fluoroalkylsilane functionalization of MXene through surface treatment enhanced gas‐sensing performance for oxygen‐containing VOCs.[ 33 ] Also, Ding et al. demonstrated that MXene functionalized with [3‐(2‐aminoethylamino)propyl]trimethoxysilane (AEAPTMS) exhibited excellent performance toward sulfur dioxide sensing.[ 34 ] While these approaches significantly improved gas response and stability under humid conditions, they have certain limitations in functionalization with diverse terminal groups for fine‐tuning gas selectivity. Therefore, a novel strategy is needed to achieve selective gas detection through advanced ligand grafting methodologies.

In this study, we present a strategy for tailoring the gas‐sensing selectivity of Ti3C2T x MXenes through covalent surface functionalization using diazonium salts with various tail groups. This strategy leverages the tunability of organic ligands to significantly enhance target gas selectivity while minimizing cross‐reactivity with other gases. By employing different diazotization routes based on the chemical nature of the aniline derivatives, we successfully grafted hydrophilic and hydrophobic ligands onto the MXene surface. Modifying the terminal groups via this method enhances MXene's dispersibility in organic solvents, enabling uniform coating onto sensor substrates. We investigated the influence of tailored surface chemistry on gas sensing performance, demonstrating significantly enhanced sensitivity and tuned selectivity toward different target gases. The ligand‐dependent gas response behavior was analyzed through experimental and computational approaches such as density functional theory (DFT) calculations. By implementing a ligand‐tailored functionalization strategy, our study not only improves the selectivity of MXene‐based gas sensors but also establishes a highly adaptable platform for detecting a broad range of gases with customized selectivity.

Results and Discussion

Design and Fabrication Strategy of Diazonium‐Functionalized MXenes for Selectivity‐Tunable Gas Sensing

Figure 1a presents the overall strategy for synthesizing tail group‐modulated, covalently functionalized MXenes for selectivity‐tunable gas sensors. First, Ti3C2T x MXene was synthesized by etching Ti3AlC2 MAX phase powders using a modified MILD method, as detailed in the Experimental Section. To covalently graft ligands onto the MXene surface, MXene was mixed with diazonium salts containing various tail groups. The diazotization reaction enabled the versatile attachment of organic molecules to the MXene surface. To prepare diazonium salts with different tail groups, a range of aniline derivatives was converted into diazonium salts, as shown in Figure 1b. Specifically, aniline and four aniline derivatives, sulfanilic acid, 4‐octylaniline, 4‐tetradecylaniline, and 4‐(heptadecafluorooctyl)aniline, were converted into their respective diazonium salts, named H‐DA, SO3H‐DA, CH8‐DA, CH14‐DA, and CF8‐DA. In this nomenclature, the prefix represents the tail group of each ligand, which is located at the para‐position of the benzene ring. Consequently, Ti3C2T x MXene functionalized with these diazonium salts was designated as H‐MX, SO3H‐MX, CH8‐MX, CH14‐MX, and CF8‐MX, respectively.

a) Scheme for synthesizing tail‐group modulated, covalently‐functionalized MXenes using diazonium salts. b) Types of aniline derivatives and diazonium salts used in this study and their synthesis routes. c) Scheme for fabricating selectivity‐tunable gas sensors based on surface‐modulated MXene sensors.

Previous studies on MXene surface functionalization using diazonium salts typically employed the NaNO2/HCl synthesis route.[ 35 , 36 ] While effective for certain aniline derivatives, this method has limitations in synthesizing diazonium salts with hydrophobic tails. To overcome this challenge, we adopted two different diazotization routes based on the tail group chemistry of the aniline derivative. SO3H‐DA and CH8‐DA were synthesized using the NaNO2/HCl method, whereas H‐DA, CH14‐DA, and CF8‐DA were synthesized using the HBF4/t‐butylnitrite method. Each diazonium salt was then reacted with MXene at a specific mixing ratio via a simple batch reaction in water. During this process, the diazonium salt reacted with hydroxyl groups on the MXene surface, forming Ti─O─C covalent bonds.[ 37 ] The functionalized MXenes were subsequently dispersed in water or organic solvents through a solvent exchange process. Finally, dispersions of MXenes with different tail group modifications were coated onto sensing electrodes where the optical microscope images in Figure S1, Supporting Information show the coverage of MXene in between the electrodes. Coated sensing electrodes were then loaded into a gas sensing chamber to evaluate their gas sensing properties and selectivity (Figure 1c) where the setup is shown in Figure S2, Supporting Information. Among synthesizable samples, SO3H‐MX, CH8‐MX, and CF8‐MX were mainly investigated in this study as the surface energy of the tail groups between these three samples are significantly different, which can potentially lead to largely different gas sensing properties.

Characterization of Functionalized MXenes Via Diazotization Chemistry

A crucial factor in utilizing MXene nanosheets for gas sensors is achieving uniform, single‐sheet dispersion in solvents. Such dispersion is essential for ensuring reliable electrical contact between adjacent MXene sheets in the sensing channel, which in turn minimizes electrical noise. To evaluate this, we assessed the dispersibility of MXenes functionalized with diazonium salts. Figure 2a presents scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images and solvent dispersion properties of pristine MXene, SO3H‐MX, CH8‐MX, and CF8‐MX. The images confirm that all functionalized MXene sheets retain their individually delaminated states, similar to pristine MXene, without noticeable defects or a reduction in lateral sheet size. Similar observations were made for H‐MX and CH14‐MX (Figure S3, Supporting Information). SO3H‐MX exhibited excellent dispersibility in both dilute and concentrated aqueous solutions, comparable to pristine MXene, due to the hydrophilic nature of the sulfonic acid tail group. Conversely, CF8‐MX and CH8‐MX demonstrated high dispersibility in ethanol across a range of concentrations, likely due to the relatively hydrophobic nature of their fluorocarbon and hydrocarbon tail groups. Overall, all functionalized MXenes exhibited excellent solution dispersibility. Surface energy differences were quantified using water contact angle measurements (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Pristine MXene exhibited a contact angle of approximately 68°, indicating a hydrophilic surface. SO3H‐MX displayed an even more hydrophilic surface, with a contact angle of 39°, consistent with its high aqueous dispersibility. In contrast, the grafting of alkyl chains increased surface hydrophobicity, with CH8‐MX and CF8‐MX showing contact angles of 83 and 117°, respectively, the latter demonstrating the strongest hydrophobicity. These results confirm that the surface energy of MXene can be systematically tuned through tail group‐modulated surface grafting. X‐ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were conducted to investigate how the incorporation of alkoxide groups influences the interlayer spacing in MXene films (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Both pristine Ti3C2T x MXene and the functionalized MXene variants exhibited distinct (00l) reflections. Notably, depending on the size of the ligands, the (002) diffraction peaks for SO3H‐MX, CH8‐MX and CF8‐MX shifted from 8.36° in pristine MXene to 7.18°, 7.06° and 6.70°, respectively. These shifts correspond to increased d‐spacings, from 10.6Å in pristine MXene to 12.3, 12.5, and 13.2Å in SO3H‐MX, CH8‐MX and CF8‐MX, respectively.

To further prove the successful grafting of diazonium salts, X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses were performed, representatively on CF8‐MX. Ti 2p peak fitting was carried out using Gaussian–Lorentzian shape lines with doublets of Ti 2p1/2 and Ti 2p3/2 with an area ratio of approximately 0.5:1 (Figure 2b). The Ti 2p spectrum of CF8‐MX was deconvoluted into four major species, C─Ti+‐T x (455.0 eV), C─Ti2+‐T x (455.8 eV), C─Ti3+‐T x (456.9 eV), and Ti4+ (458.7 eV), where each doublet peak was separated by 5.8 eV.[ 13 , 38 ] The area ratio of the Ti4+ (TiO2) peak was very low and the overall peaks highly resemble those in pristine Ti3C2T x (Figure S6, Supporting Information), showing that the original MXene chemical structure was well preserved with negligible oxidation. In the C 1s spectrum, a large Ti‐C peak at 282.2 eV confirms the presence of Ti3C2 lattices. Other peaks at 284.2, 285.0, 286.3, and 290.3 eV corresponding to C─C, ─CH2─, and ─CH3, C─O, and ─COO chemical bonds could also be observed, consistent with previous studies and peaks from pristine MXene in Figure S6, Supporting Information. Additionally, a strong peak at 291.2 eV was observed in CF8‐MX, which is a peak that is not present in pristine MXene. This peak corresponds to ─CF2‐ chemical moieties, which is an indicator for the successful grafting of CF8‐DA on MXene. The F 1s peak was deconvoluted into two major peaks, Ti‐F (685.2 eV) and C─Fn (688.1 eV) (Figure 2d). The former originates from the intrinsic functional group of pristine MXene while the latter originates from the C─F bonds of the attached diazonium salt, further confirming the successful grafting. For additional confirmation on diazotization, the S 2p spectrum was additionally analyzed for SO3H‐MX (Figure 2e). A single peak at 168.5 eV was observed, which typically corresponds to ‐SO3H moieties, proving the grafting of SO3H‐DA as sulfur‐related peaks are otherwise not present in pristine Ti3C2T x . Furthermore, peaks were fitted in the O 1s region to analyze the chemical bonding nature of diazonium salts onto MXene. The O 1s spectrum of pristine MXene was deconvoluted into four major peaks corresponding to TiO2 (529.2 eV), Ti─O–Ti (530.0 eV), Ti─OH (531.7 eV), and Ti─H2O (533.6 eV), which is typical. Meanwhile, in the case of CF8‐MX, an additional Ti─O─C peak (531.6 eV) was observed, while the four existing peaks from pristine MXene were still present (Figure 2f). Additionally, the Ti─OH peak exhibited a lower intensity compared to pristine MXene. Similarly, in Figure 2g, the Ti─O─C peak was also detected in SO3H‐MX, along with S═O (531.3 eV) and S─OH (532.0 eV) peaks. In both cases, the presence of the Ti─O─C bond, along with the decreased intensity of the Ti─OH peak, strongly suggests that the organic moieties from the diazonium salts are covalently bonded to the MXene surface where Figure 2h summarizes the expected chemical bond structure analyzed through XPS.

The covalent bonding nature was further revealed using Raman analysis where previous studies have shown that when organic ligands are covalently grafted on MXene, noticeable shifts occur where the A1g (Ti, C, O) peak downshifts and A1g (C) peak upshifts.[ 39 , 40 ] In our study, the surface bonding characteristics of SO3H‐MX, CH8‐MX and CF8‐MX were additionally analyzed using Raman spectroscopy under 785 nm laser excitation (Figure S7, Supporting Information). Raman spectroscopy revealed clear shifts in the A1g modes upon diazonium functionalization, including a slight downshift of the A1g (Ti, C, O) mode (201 cm−1 to 198 and 196 cm−1) and a notable upshift of the A1g (C) mode (713 cm−1 to 722–725 cm−1). These changes suggest structural rearrangements and increased interlayer spacing, supporting the formation of covalent bonds between diazonium groups and the MXene surface.

Chemiresistive Sensing Performance of Diazonium‐Functionalized MXenes

To evaluate the gas sensing performance of tail‐group modulated MXenes, each dispersion either in water or ethanol was coated into a thin film on chemiresistor‐type sensing electrodes. Figure S8, Supporting Information shows the schematic illustration of the structural morphology of the MXene film in between the electrodes. The MXene sheets form a percolated network bridging the electrode gap through both in‐plane and inter‐sheet conduction pathways. This structure enables a reliable current flow, and the electrical resistance changes upon adsorption of gas molecules on the terminal groups of the MXene surface. Then, samples were loaded into an air‐tight chamber and the electrical resistance was dynamically measured as target gases were accordingly injected. We initially measured the gas responses toward 100 ppm acetone, ethanol, toluene, and NH3, in which the four gases each have distinct chemical moieties (ketone, alcohol, aromatic carbon, and amine). Figure 3a–c shows the dynamic gas sensing response of CF8‐MX, SO3H‐MX, and CH8‐MX toward these four gases where real‐time resistance variations are shown in Figure S9, Supporting Information. All samples exhibited increased resistance upon exposure to all tested gases, regardless of gas type, which can be attributed to the metallic nature of MXene. The three samples each demonstrated a distinct gas sensing pattern. In the case of CF8‐MX, the highest gas response was observed for acetone with a gas response of 7.1%. A secondary significant response toward ethanol was also observed, with a gas response of 3.5%. Meanwhile, SO3H‐MX displayed a significantly different gas response pattern, as shown in Figure 3b. SO3H‐MX showed a dominantly selective response toward NH3 with a gas response of 17.0%. The response toward acetone and ethanol was relatively low, with a gas response of 2.4 and 4.3%, respectively. CH8‐MX showed a similar gas response pattern with that of CF8‐MX, but with even higher selectivity only toward acetone gas with a gas response of 16.0% (Figure 3c). For all samples, a meaningful response toward toluene gas was not observed. The average maximal gas response of each sample toward the tested gases is summarized in Figure 3d where the differences in sensing performance could be directly visualized. For comparison, the highly conductive pristine Ti3C2T x MXene used in this study was solely drop‐casted into gas sensors using the same technique, and the gas response was measured (Figure S10, Supporting Information). While the gas response values and selectivity pattern of pristine MXene largely differs depends on multiple factors such as the baseline resistance, interlayer spacing, or particle morphology, the pristine MXene sensor with a low initial resistance of approximately 76 Ω in our study showed a very low response below 0.1% toward all gases. This well aligns with previous studies on the gas sensing performances of neat films fabricated from highly conductive Ti3C2T x MXene.[ 32 , 41 ] When calculating the gas response enhancement factors ([Gas response]functionalized MXene/[Gas response]pristine MXene), diazonium‐functionalized MXenes showed up largely increased gas response values (by a factor of 1600, 66, and 200, for acetone, ethanol, and NH3, respectively), as shown in Figure 3e. The selectivity patterns were further assessed by normalizing the gas response of each sample, where the highest gas response value in each sample was set to 1 (Figure 3f). It can be seen that the four different samples have distinct selectivity patterns with CH8‐MX having a sharp selectivity toward acetone and SO3H‐MX having a sharp selectivity toward NH3. Thus, it can be concluded that after the functionalization, the sensitivity was improved and the selectivity was changed.

To further evaluate the stability of the gas sensor, we investigated its repeatability, extended stability, and humidity resistance, mainly using CF8‐MX as it is the most hydrophobic among the prepared samples. Figure S11, Supporting Information shows the gas response of CF8‐MX toward 10 repeated injections of 100 ppm acetone. The response values were consistently maintained in the range of approximately 7–8% over multiple cycles, demonstrating good repeatability of the sensing performance. In order to evaluate the stability over extended periods of time, the sensor was stored under ambient conditions for 5 days, followed by gas sensing measurements. During this period, a clear gas response toward 100 ppm acetone could be observed, while the gas response decreased from 8.0 to 3.5% (Figure S12, Supporting Information). While the gas sensing performance is not perfectly preserved, CF8‐MX still retained considerable sensing capability compared to pristine MXene, which is known to rapidly degrade under ambient conditions.[ 32 ] We speculate that the decreased gas response may be due to the inherent defect sites of the MXene backbone and non‐functionalized surface terminal groups. Finally, the humidity effects were assessed by measuring the gas response of CF8‐MX toward 20 ppm acetone under different humidity conditions. For 20 ppm acetone, the gas response value was approximately 1.0% across 20, 40, and 60% RH (Figures S13 and S14, Supporting Information). Although the gas response slightly decreased from approximately 1.4% in the dry environment, CF8‐MX showed a stable response. These results demonstrate that CF8‐MX exhibits reliable repeatability, improved long‐term stability, and humidity resistance compared to pristine MXene.

The dynamic gas‐sensing performance of functionalized MXenes toward various concentrations of gases (5–100 ppm) was further investigated (Figure 3g–i) where the gas of highest response for each sample was tested; acetone for CF8‐MX, CH8‐MX and NH3 for SO3H‐MX. For all samples, a clear response could be observed at a low gas concentration of 5 ppm, and the gas response gradually increased for higher gas concentrations. The overall relation between gas concentration and response was summarized in Figure 3j, which was each fitted to the Langmuir isotherm model (Figure S15, Supporting Information). Due to differences in gas affinity and saturation behavior, their adsorption curves exhibit distinct shapes. Using this model, the limit of detection (LOD) of CF8‐MX, CH8‐MX, and SO3H‐MX was calculated to better understand their sensitivity. First, gas responses were transformed into signal‐to‐noise‐ratio (SNR) values by dividing them by electrical noise. Then, the reciprocal of the gas concentration versus the reciprocal of the SNR was plotted to obtain a linear plot (Figure S16, Supporting Information), and the concentration at which SNR reaches three was determined as the LOD. Here, the LOD of SO3H‐MX toward ammonia was calculated to be 0.03 ppm, and the LOD of CF8‐MX, CH8‐MX toward acetone was calculated to be 0.36 and 0.18 ppm, respectively, showing their excellent sensitivity.

Tunable Gas Sensing Behavior Depending on Ligand Mixing Ratio

To experimentally elucidate the contribution of covalently grafted ligands to the selectivity of MXene gas sensors, we investigated the gas sensing properties of MXenes functionalized with diazonium salts at different ratios. The ligand mixing ratios were varied between 50, 200, and 500 wt% relative to the MXene weight.

Figure 4a–c presents the corresponding results for SO3H‐MX. TGA in Figure 4a confirms that a higher ligand mixing ratio results in increased grafting, as evidenced by the characteristic weight loss observed between 100 and 400 °C, which is attributed to ligand detachment. Subsequently, SO3H‐MX samples with varying grafting degrees were tested for NH3 sensing, the only gas they exhibited selectivity for. As shown in Figure 4b, the ligand grafting degree significantly influenced NH3 sensing performance. At a 50 wt% mixing ratio, the response to 100 ppm NH3 was 0.8%, while at 200 and 500 wt%, it increased to 4.6% and 14.0%, respectively. Notably, this correlation between grafting ratio and gas response was observed exclusively for NH3 among the tested gases. While NH3 response increased with higher ligand mixing ratios, the responses to acetone, ethanol, and toluene remained nearly unchanged (Figures 4c, S17, Supporting Information). These findings strongly suggest that the grafted SO3H‐DA ligand plays a dominant role in enhancing NH3 selectivity, while the responses to non‐selective gases were independent of ligand grafting. A similar analysis was conducted on hydrophobic CH8‐MX. TGA data in Figure 4d showed a comparable weight loss trend, with estimated grafting degrees of 2.2, 3.4, and 4.5 wt% for CH8‐DA mixing ratios of 50, 200, and 500 wt%, respectively. This time, gas response was measured for 100 ppm acetone at different ligand grafting degrees. At a 50 wt% mixing ratio, the response was 0.9%, increasing to 1.5% and 16.1% for 200 and 500 wt% mixing ratios, respectively (Figure 4e). As observed for SO3H‐MX, only the selectively responding gas, in this case, acetone, exhibited an increased response with higher ligand mixing ratios, while the responses to ethanol, ammonia, and toluene remained largely unchanged (Figure 4f). A similar trend was observed in CF8‐MX samples, where increases in gas response were exclusively observed for the selectively responding acetone and ethanol gases (Figure 4g–i). In summary, the above results strongly suggest that each ligand grafted onto the MXene surface plays a dominant role in enhancing the gas sensing performance and modulating the selectivity toward various gases.

Gas Adsorption Energy Calculations

To further investigate the role of grafted ligands in enhancing the gas response of functionalized MXenes, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed (computational details are provided in the Experimental section). The gas sensing mechanism of sensors based on 2D materials, particularly those operating at room temperature, is typically governed by the direct adsorption of gas molecules onto the outermost surface, followed by charge transfer. Accordingly, the same sensing mechanism can be applied to MXene‐based gas sensors.[ 42 ] In our study, the MXene surface is grafted with tail‐group‐modified organic ligands, which form the outermost layer. Consequently, the gas sensing behavior is largely determined by the physicochemical interactions between gas molecules and these organic ligands. Based on this mechanism, a stronger interaction between the gas molecules and the surface‐modified MXene is expected to result in a higher gas response, which can be initially predicted through DFT calculations. The interaction between NH3 and SO3H‐MX was selected as a representative case, and adsorption energy calculations were conducted to analyze this interaction. Given that diazonium salts graft onto Ti3C2T x MXene via Ti─O─C bonds, oxygen‐terminated MXenes were considered as the pristine reference for theoretical analysis. An initial assessment of the dynamic configuration of SO3H‐DA on Ti3C2 MXene revealed that SO3H‐DA preferentially adopts a vertical alignment (Video S1, Supporting Information), consistent with previous studies. To determine the minimum energy configuration, SO3H‐DA was placed vertically at different sites on oxygen‐terminated MXenes (Figure S18, Supporting Information). The energy differences among five representative configurations were at most 0.07 eV, and the lowest‐energy configuration (configuration (v)) was selected for further calculations. Similarly, to identify a favorable adsorption site for NH3, multiple configurations were evaluated for NH3 placement within the optimized SO3H‐MX structure (Figure S19, Supporting Information). The results indicate that NH3 preferentially adsorbs near the SO3H moiety at the ligand edge (configuration (v)), which is expected given that NH3 is an electron‐donating molecule, while the SO3H group is electron‐withdrawing, enhancing their electronic interactions.

In comparison, NH3 adsorption on pristine Ti3C2O2 MXene yielded an E ads of −0.54 eV (Figure 5b), aligning with previous reports.[ 13 ] Meanwhile, the NH3 adsorption energy on pristine Ti3C2(OH)2 and Ti3C2F2 MXene was −0.62 and −0.13 eV, respectively (Figure S20, Supporting Information), showing the superior adsorption on SO3H‐MX even when considering other intrinsic functional groups. To further confirm the role of SO3H‐DA in enhancing NH3 adsorption, the E ads of NH3 on SO3H‐DA alone (without MXene) was calculated to be −1.38 eV (Figure 5c), highlighting the strong interaction between these two moieties. Notably, the NH3 adsorption strength observed for SO3H‐MX is among the highest reported for MXene‐based sensors (Figure 5d, Table S1, Supporting Information) and is significantly greater than that of ADOPA‐functionalized MXene sensors in our previous work, where E ads for NH3 adsorption was −0.59 eV.[ 31 ]

The gas response values obtained from sensing experiments were further analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA). Experiments were conducted using an array of SO3H‐MX, CF8‐MX, and CH8‐MX to evaluate their discrimination capabilities. As shown in Figure 5e, each functionalized MXene exhibited distinct gas responses, enabling clear separation of the three gases without overlap in the PCA plot. Gas clusters were well‐defined and distinguished using only two principal components, with PC1 accounting for 92% of the variance and PC2 for 7%. This high discrimination capability is likely attributed to the distinct surface properties introduced during material fabrication, which promote selective interactions with gas molecules. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed synthesis strategies in generating diverse sensing channels, enabling accurate gas differentiation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study presents a successful approach to enhancing gas selectivity in MXene‐based chemiresistive sensors through covalent functionalization with diazonium salts. By systematically modifying the tail groups of surface‐grafted ligands, we achieved distinct selectivity patterns and significantly improved sensitivity toward specific gases, including acetone, ammonia, and ethanol. The results demonstrated that the chemical nature of the grafted ligands plays a dominant role in modulating gas responses, with selectivity directly influenced by the degree of ligand grafting. Experimental results, supported by DFT calculations, provided valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms driving gas selectivity. The proposed strategy not only enhances the performance of MXene‐based gas sensors but also offers a versatile platform for tuning selectivity through molecular engineering. These advancements contribute to the broader goal of developing highly selective, room‐temperature gas sensors suitable for real‐world applications in complex gas environments.

Experimental Section

Materials

MAX phase (Ti3AlC2, <40 µm powder) was obtained from Jilin 11 Technology Co., Ltd., China. Lithium fluoride (LiF, 98.5%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%) was acquired from Daejung Chemical, Korea. Hydrofluoric acid (HF, 48 wt%) was obtained from Sigma Aldrich. Aniline (99%), sulfanilic acid (99%), 4‐octylaniline (99%), 4‐tetradecylaniline (97%) and 4‐(heptadecafluorooctyl)aniline (95%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Sodium hydroxide beads (97%) were obtained from Daejung Chemical. Sodium nitrite (97%) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Tetrafluoroboric acid (HBF4, ∼50% w/w aqueous solution) was acquired from Alfa Aesar. tert‐Butyl nitrite (90%) was obtained from Sigma Aldrich. Ethanol was purchased from Daejung Chemical.

Synthesis of Ti3C2Tx MXene

MXene was synthesized using a modified MILD (Minimally Intensive Layer Delamination) method, involving the selective etching of aluminum from the MAX precursor. First, 1.6 g of LiF was added to an etching solution that mixture of 20 mL solution of 9 M HCl and 3 mL of 48 wt% HF solution in a polypropylene (PP) bottle. The mixture was stirred at 360 rpm at 35 °C for 5 min. Once the LiF powders completely dissolved in the etching solution, 1 g of Ti3AlC2 powder was slowly added to the solution while stirring, and the reaction continued at 35 °C for 24 h. After 24 h, when the reaction is completed, the mixture was centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was removed. Deionized (DI) water was added to the conical tube and hand‐shaking was performed to disperse the sediment completely in water. This procedure was repeated until the pH of the supernatant approaches 6. Then, DI water was added and vigorously shaking for 20 min to delaminate the multi‐layered MXene. To eliminate the unreacted MAX and multilayered MXene, the solution was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 30 min, and the supernatant containing the single layer was extracted.

Synthesis of 4‐(Heptadecafluorooctyl)Aniline Diazonium Salt (CF8‐DA)

4‐(Heptadecafluorooctyl)aniline (2.1 mmol) was dissolved in 3.2 mL of ethanol, and fluoroboric acid (4.2 mmol) was added to the mixture and stirred for 5 min. tert‐Butyl nitrite (4.2 mmol) was then added to the solution, and the mixture was stirred for 2 h in an ice bath. After the reaction, the solid product was washed with diethyl ether three times and dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 2 h.

Synthesis of Sulfanilic Acid Diazonium Salt (SO3H‐DA)

Sulfanilic acid (2.1 mmol) and sodium hydroxide (2.1 mmol) were mixed in DI water, and sodium nitrite (22.8 mmol) was slowly added while cooling in an ice bath (0–5 °C). HCl solution (12 M, 11.52 mmol) was then poured into the mixture and stirred for 2 h at room temperature.

Synthesis of 4‐Octylaniline Diazonium Salt (CH8‐DA)

4‐Octylaniline (2.1 mmol) and sodium hydroxide (2.1 mmol) were mixed in 20 mL of ethanol. Sodium nitrite (22.8 mmol) was added to the reaction mixture, which was maintained at 0–5 °C using an ice bath. HCl solution (12 M, 11.52 mmol) was then added, and the mixture was stirred for 2 h in an ice bath. After the reaction, the solid product was washed with diethyl ether three times and dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 2 h.

Synthesis of 4‐Tetradecylaniline Diazonium Salt (CH14‐DA)

4‐Tetradecylaniline (1.0 mmol) was dissolved in 3.2 mL of ethanol, and fluoroboric acid (2 mmol) was added to the mixture and stirred for 5 minutes. tert‐Butyl nitrite (2.0 mmol) was then added to the solution, and the mixture was stirred for 2 h in an ice bath. After the reaction, the solid product was washed with diethyl ether three times and dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 2 h.

Synthesis of Aniline Diazonium Salt (H‐DA)

Aniline (5.3 mmol) was dissolved in 1.6 mL of ethanol under an argon atmosphere, and fluoroboric acid (10.6 mmol) was added to the mixture and stirred for 5 minutes. Then, tert‐butyl nitrite (10.6 mmol) was then added to the solution, and the mixture was stirred in an ice bath for 2 h. After the reaction, the solid product was washed three times with diethyl ether and dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 2 h.

Functionalization of MXene with Aniline Diazonium Salt

Aqueous solution of sulfanilic acid diazonium salt (10 mg mL−1) was added to an aqueous MXene solution (1 mg mL−1) and stirred at room temperature overnight. After the reaction, the mixture was centrifuged at 11,000 rpm for 30 minutes for 1 h and the supernatant was discarded. The sediment was dispersed in DI water for storage and further experiments.

Functionalization of MXene with sulfanilic acid‐, 4‐octylaniline‐, 4‐tetradecylaniline, and 4‐(heptadecafluorooctyl)aniline diazonium salts: Aniline, 4‐octylaniline, 4‐tetradecylaniline or 4‐(heptadecafluorooctyl)aniline diazonium salts were dissolved in 10 mL of ethanol. These solutions were then separately added to an aqueous MXene solution (1 mg mL−1) and stirred at room temperature overnight. After the reaction, the solutions were washed using a centrifuge at 11,000 rpm for 30 minutes and then for 1 h and the supernatant was discarded. The sediment was dispersed in ethanol for further experiments.

Gas Sensing Measurements

To monitor the dynamic resistance response of the sensors, the electrodes were patterned on a SiO2 wafer using a customized hard mask. A 10 nm Ti adhesion layer and a 100 nm Au conducting layer were sequentially deposited. For the electrical connection to the sensing material, two parallel gold lines with a spacing of 100 µm were designed using a hard mask. The gas‐sensing equipment was prepared as follows. Initially, SiO2/Si wafers with deposited Au/Ti electrodes were cleaned via bath sonication with acetone, ethanol, and isopropyl alcohol (IPA), each for 10 minutes. Following this, the substrates were subjected to air plasma for 5 min. Then, the sensing films were fabricated by drop‐coating 2 µL of diluted sample solutions (concentration: 0.5 mg mL−1) on the gold electrodes on a hot plate heated to 80 °C. The resulting coating was in the shape of a circle with a diameter of approximately 0.3–0.5 cm. Gas sensing measurements were conducted using a system that included multiple gas lines, an airtight homemade gas‐sensing chamber connected to a source meter (Keithley 2400), and a computer. First, the sensing electrodes coated with samples were transferred into the homemade gas‐sensing chamber. Then, the electrical resistance of the sensor was recorded in real‐time on the computer while baseline gas and testing analytes were purged into the chamber. For all gas types, we used a commercially purchased certified gas mixture of 100 ppm target gas (either acetone, ethanol, toluene, or NH3) in dry air for the sensing test. To control the gas concentration to below 100 ppm, the original gas mixture was mixed with pure dry air at controlled flow rates using a mass flow controller (MFC). Density Functional Theory Computations

Within the framework of pseudopotentials (PPs) and plane wave basis sets Kohn‐Sham Hamiltonian was solved using QUANTUM ESPRESSO (QE)[ 43 ] density‐functional theory (DFT) package (unless otherwise specified). The exchange‐correlation potential of the Hamiltonian was approximated by the generalized gradient approach[ 44 ] through the use of Optimized Norm‐Conserving Vanderbilt PPs.[ 45 ] The kinetic energy cutoff for the wave functions are taken as 80 Ry and for the charge densities, default energy cutoff (four times of the wave functions cutoff) was considered. The value of the Gaussian spreading for the Brillouin‐zone integration was taken as 0.005 Ry. Van der Waals dispersion correction was introduced through DFT‐D3 approximation.[ 46 ]

Principal Component Analysis Details

The PCA was programmed using the SVD method via MATLAB with reference to a previous study and was confirmed using an IRIS dataset.[ 47 ] The response values from three channels upon gas measurements (27 data composed of 3 gases, 3 channels, and 3 sets each) were collected and its response value used for PCA via MATLAB. Each data measurement was performed in identical experimental condition.

Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Regulus 8230, Hitachi) was used to investigate the morphology of MXene sheets. X‐ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained with D/MAX‐2500/PC (Rigaku, Japan) Cu‐Kα1 radiation (40 kV, 200 mA, λ = 0.15 406 nm) at a 2 theta range of 3°–70° with a scanning step of 0.02°, a scan speed of 2° min−1. Chemical structures were obtained using X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI 5000 VersaProbe, Ulvac‐PHI, Japan) using glancing incident X‐ray diffraction method at an incidence angle of 0.5° by Al Kα as the X‐ray source with a power of 25 W. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Q50, TA Instruments, USA) was performed in a N2 atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 from 30 to 600 °C.
