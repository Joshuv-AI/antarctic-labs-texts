Asymmetric dimethylguanidino valeric acid as an independently associated risk factor for early hypertriglyceridemia and dyslipidemia
Authors: Yueyuan Han, Qianling Xiong, Jiahui Wang, Anxin Wang, Qian Chen, Yan Liu, Ken Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHypertriglyceridemia (HTG) is a crucial risk factor for cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes. However, its early detection remains challenging due to the dynamic fluctuations in plasma triglyceride levels and their limited utility in predicting long-term dyslipidemia.ObjectivesThis study aimed to elucidate the role of asymmetric dimethylguanidino valeric acid (ADGV) in HTG through integrated cohort analyses and mechanistic cellular experiments.MethodsCirculating ADGV levels were quantified in a cross-sectional cohort from southern China (n = 588) and assessed its prospective association with incident HTG in an independent prospective cohort from northern China (n = 348, median follow-up: 1.8 years). Hepatocyte experiments were performed to examine biological plausibility and explore potential mechanisms linking ADGV to hepatic triglyceride metabolism.ResultsElevated ADGV levels were significantly associated with increased risk of HTG and remained independently associated after adjustment for age, gender, BMI, eGFR, lifestyle factors, and liver function. Complementary in vitro studies in hepatocytes revealed that ADGV promoted triglyceride accumulation by stimulating de novo fatty acid synthesis, mediated through regulating the expression of key metabolic genes (ACC, CPT1α, and PGC1α).ConclusionHigher circulating ADGV was independently associated with prevalent and incident hypertriglyceridemia across a cross-sectional cohort and an independent prospective cohort, and hepatocyte experiments support a contributory role of ADGV in hepatic lipogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11306-026-02409-5.

Introduction

Asymmetric dimethylguanidino valeric acid (ADGV), a novel endogenous metabolite with a molecular weight of 201.1186 Da, was first identified in human plasma in 2017 (O’Sullivan et al., 2017). It is a stereoisomer of dimethylguanidino valeric acid (DMGV) (Bonnitcha et al., 2022), derived from the metabolism of arginine-containing proteins and renally excreted in healthy individuals (Martens-Lobenhoffer et al., 2011; Rodionov et al., 2014). According to published articles (Ottosson et al., 2019; Rodionov et al., 2016), ADGV is synthesized in the liver and kidney via alanine–glyoxylate aminotransferase 2 (AGXT2)-mediated transamination of asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA), a known inhibitor of nitric oxide (NO) synthesis. This metabolic pathway suggests ADGV may play regulatory roles in vascular homeostasis, immune modulation, and systemic metabolism (Huang et al., 2023; Rodionov et al., 2022). Accumulating evidence from cohort studies links elevated ADGV levels to various metabolic disorders, including obesity (Ottosson et al., 2019, 2022; O’Sullivan et al., 2017), metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) (Bonnitcha et al., 2022; O’Sullivan et al., 2017; Qadri et al., 2024), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (O’Sullivan et al., 2017.; Smith et al., 2022; Wali et al., 2020), and coronary artery disease (CAD) (Ottosson et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2022; Vernon et al., 2021; Wali et al., 2020), highlighting its importance in metabolic disorders. Furthermore, lifestyle factors, such as diet and exercise, can notably modulate circulating ADGV levels (Koay et al., 2021; Robbins et al., 2019; Wali et al., 2021), underscoring its potential involvement in metabolic regulation. However, to the best of our knowledge, the absence of commercial ADGV standards has limited most studies to qualitative analyses. Consequently, the concentration distribution of ADGV in populations with metabolic disorders remains uncharacterized, which hinders both its clinical translation and the investigation of its mechanism.

Hypertriglyceridemia (HTG) is a disorder of triglyceride (TG) metabolism and a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and T2DM (Nordestgaard & Varbo, 2014; Smellie, 2006; Yuan et al., 2007). Its early detection and management remain challenging, as TG levels fluctuate with many factors such as diet, lifestyle, obesity, and chronic conditions (Smellie, 2006; Yuan et al., 2007). Conventional biomarkers, such as fasting TG levels, are used to diagnose HTG, but their utility for predicting long-term lipid disorders is limited by poor specificity and sensitivity. Therefore, identifying stable endogenous metabolites that reflect early TG dysregulation is of significant clinical value. Notably, elevated ADGV levels have been associated with dyslipidemia, characterized by elevated low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C), high TG, and low high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C) in human plasma (Ottosson et al., 2019, 2022; O’Sullivan et al., 2017). Nevertheless, the role of ADGV in HTG requires further validation in large-scale, prospective cohorts, and its regulatory role in lipid-related pathologies remains unclear.

To address the above questions, this study investigated the correlation between circulating ADGV and HTG using an in-house-established HPLC-MS/MS-based quantitative method (Han et al., 2025) and cohort-based epidemiological approaches. The cross-sectional association between ADGV and prevalent HTG was first evaluated in a cohort from southern China, and the prospective association between baseline ADGV and incident HTG was subsequently assessed in an independent prospective cohort from northern China. Furthermore, ADGV-intervention experiments in hepatocytes were conducted to examine its contribution to HTG pathogenesis. This integrative strategy provides deeper insights into the role of ADGV in dysregulated lipid metabolism.

Materials and methods

Cross-sectional cohort from Southern China

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health at Sun Yat-sen University (ethical approval number: 2019-127) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Residents of Dongguan City, Guangdong, China, who had lived in the area for over five years, were invited to participate in health screening through flyers and advertisements. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 30 and 65 years; and (2) stable body weight (< 10% change) for at least 3 months prior to the study. The exclusion criteria included: (1) a history of malignant tumors, pancreatitis, severe hepatic or renal insufficiency, chronic hepatitis, thyroid dysfunction, autoimmune diseases, or severe cardio-cerebrovascular diseases; (2) pregnancy or lactation; and (3) administration of medications known to affect TG levels within the past 3 months, including lipid-lowering agents, systemic glucocorticoids, estrogens, retinoids, antipsychotics, and other relevant pharmaceuticals. A total of 630 participants were initially recruited, of whom 12 were subsequently excluded. As previously described (Korhonen et al., 2024), outliers of plasma ADGV were defined as values below Q1 − 1.5×interquartile range (IQR) or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR. Thirty-two participants with ADGV outliers were excluded, resulting in a final analytical sample of 588 participants (Fig. 1A).

Covariate collection and biochemical measurements

Demographic and lifestyle data were collected using structured questionnaires administered by trained personnel. Smoking status was defined as smoking at least one cigarette per day for more than six months, while drinking status was considered positive if an individual consumed alcoholic beverages at least once per week for the past six months (Luo et al., 2023). Physical activity levels were assessed with the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). Based on established guidelines, participants were classified into low, moderate, or high activity groups; those with moderate or high activity levels were considered as engaging in regular exercise. Physical examinations were conducted by qualified staff (Luo et al., 2023).

Fasting blood samples were collected after a 10–12 h overnight fast and analyzed at a local clinical laboratory. Biochemical parameters, including fasting glucose, TG, total cholesterol (TC), HDL-C, LDL-C, uric acid (UA), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), were measured using a BS-800 Biochemical Autoanalyzer (Mindray, China). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation (Levey et al., 2009). Insulin resistance (IR) was estimated using the following indices: the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) = [fasting blood glucose (mmol/L) × fasting insulin (mU/L)]/22.5, and the liver insulin resistance index (Liver-IR) = [fasting insulin (mU/L) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)]/1250 (the EGIR-RISC Study Group et al., 2011). Blood pressure was measured on the right upper arm in a seated position after a 10- to 15-minute rest using a validated digital automatic monitor (Omron HEM-7136).

Definition of HTG and other metabolic abnormalities

HTG was defined as a fasting TG concentration ≥ 1.7 mmol/L (150 mg/dL), in accordance with the Chinese Multidisciplinary Expert Consensus on the Clinical Management of Hypertriglyceridemia (Ge et al., 2023). Based on this criterion, participants were divided into a normal TG group (< 1.7 mmol/L) and a high TG group (≥ 1.7 mmol/L). A total of 263 participants met the criteria for high TG (HTG group), while 330 had normal TG levels (Control group).

Other metabolic abnormalities were also assessed, including overweight, T2DM, hyperuricemia, dyslipidemia, and hypertension. Overweight was defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 24 kg/m2, according to the Clinical Management and Treatment of Obesity in China (Zeng et al., 2021). T2DM was defined as a fasting glucose level ≥ 7.0 mmol/L according to American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria, a self-reported physician-diagnosed diabetes, or current use of hypoglycemic agents (American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee, 2025). Hyperuricemia was defined as a serum UA level > 420 µmol/L (7 mg/dL) in males or > 360 µmol/L (6 mg/dL) in females. Dyslipidemia was defined as meeting at least one of the following: TC ≥ 5.2 mmol/L (200 mg/dL), LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L (130 mg/dL), HDL-C < 1.0 mmol/L (40 mg/dL), or TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L (150 mg/dL). Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, a self-reported physician diagnosis of hypertension, or current use of antihypertensive medications (NCD 2021; Wang et al., 2018). For each condition, participants who did not meet the diagnostic criteria were classified into the corresponding control group.

A prospective cohort from Northern China

The prospective cohort comprised participants recruited from the Kailuan study, which is an ongoing prospective cohort project (ethical approval number: KS-2006-5) conducted at Qianjiaying Hospital, an affiliated institution of Kailuan Group in Tangshan, China (Liu et al., 2024). Baseline data were collected in 2016, with follow-up assessments conducted every two years until December 2018. The same inclusion and exclusion criteria used for participant selection in the cross-sectional cohort were applied. Further exclusions at baseline included fasting TG > 1.7 mmol/L, diagnosed HTG, or use of lipid-modulating medications within the preceding 3 months. Additionally, participants without available plasma samples or who were lost to follow-up were excluded. A total of 348 participants were included (Fig. 1B). Plasma ADGV levels were measured at baseline in 2016 for prospective analysis. Incident HTG was defined as the follow-up visit during which fasting TG reached ≥ 1.7 mmol/L; 32 participants developed incident HTG during follow-up. Further details regarding the Kailuan cohort design have been described previously (Liu et al., 2024). Baseline characteristics of the cross-sectional and prospective cohorts are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Targeted quantification of plasma ADGV-related metabolites using HPLC-MS/MS

Fasting blood samples from both cohorts were collected in EDTA tubes and processed immediately. All plasma samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis. ADGV-related metabolites, including ADGV, ADMA, BAIBA, L-citrulline, homoarginine, NMMA, and SDMA, were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS), following the published article by Han et al. (2025) (Han et al., 2025). The cross-sectional cohort was measured using a 6500 + QTrap (AB Sciex) with d3-β-aminoisobutyric acid (BAIBA) as the stable-isotope internal standard for ADGV, and the validation cohort was analyzed by Ultivo MS/MS (Agilent) with d6-ADGV as the internal standard. The concentrations of ADGV were calculated using d3-BAIBA and then adjusted to d6-ADGV according to the published protocol (Han et al., 2025).

Cell culture and treatment

The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2 was purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Biosharp) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ExCell) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL, Sigma). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. After overnight adhesion, cells were serum-starved for 12 h using DMEM containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Following starvation, cells were treated with 200 µM oleic acid (OA) and 20 nM ADGV for 12–24 h to mimic the median level of plasma ADGV detected in individuals with HTG. Cells treated with DMEM containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) were used as the control.

Oil Red O Staining

Lipid accumulation was visualized using an Oil Red O staining kit (Solarbio G1262, China). Cells were fixed for 30 min, followed by rinsing with 60% isopropanol and staining with a freshly prepared Oil Red O solution for 10–20 min. After washing, nuclei were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin. Images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2R microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantification of TG in hepatocytes

TG levels in cell lysates were quantified using the GPO-PAP method with a commercial TG assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing). Briefly, cells were lysed with 1% Triton X-100 for 30 min under sonication in an ice-water bath. Absorbance was measured at 500 nm, and sample TG concentrations were determined by comparison with standards and blanks. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, TG levels were normalized to the total cellular protein content determined by a BCA assay (Beyotime, Shanghai).

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cell samples using TRIzol reagent (Sigma, USA) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA by the GoScript™ Reverse Transcription System (Promega, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-qPCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher) with the GoTaq qPCR Master Mix kit (Promega). All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai; sequences provided in Supplementary Table S2). Design & Analysis 2 software was applied for raw data acquisition and analysis. The relative mRNA level was normalized to β-actin as a loading control. Statistical analysis of the results was performed using the 2−△△Ct method.

Western blots for protein expression

Cells were washed with cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, Shanghai). Proteins were separated using a 7.5% PAGE gel (Yamei, Shanghai) and then transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA in TBST buffer (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1‰ Tween 20) to prevent unspecific binding. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies from Proteintech: phospho-ACC1 (Ser79) recombinant antibody (1:5000; 80281-2-RR), ACC1 polyclonal antibody (1:1000; 21923-1-AP), PGC1α monoclonal antibody (1:5000; 66369-1-Ig), CPT1α polyclonal antibody (1:2000; 15184-1-AP), and β-tubulin monoclonal antibody (1:20000; 110094-1-AP). After washing with TBST to remove unbound primary antibody, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibodies for 40 min at room temperature. The secondary antibodies were anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (DyLight™ 680 conjugate; 1:15000; CST) and anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (DyLight™ 8004X PEG conjugate; 1:30000; CST). Protein signals were detected using the Odyssey imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences) and analyzed with Image Studio Lite (version 5.2).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism 10. Continuous variables were first examined for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed variables, data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For skewed data, the median and IQR were reported. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage) and compared using the chi-square test. Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed with the independent Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data) or the Mann-Whitney U test (for skewed data), as appropriate.

In the cross-sectional cohort from Southern China (n = 588), Spearman rank correlation was performed to assess the relationships between plasma ADGV and other metabolites, including ADMA, BAIBA, L-citrulline, homoarginine, NMMA, and SDMA, and clinical biochemical parameters, such as BMI, SBP, DBP, TG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, fasting glucose, Liver-IR, HOMA-IR, ALT, AST, AST/ALT, UA, and eGFR. Furthermore, plasma ADGV levels were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as the data were not normally distributed. The comparisons included male vs. female, as well as control group vs. HTG, overweight, hyperuricemia, dyslipidemia, T2DM, and hypertension. Additionally, sex-specific associations between ADGV and TG levels were examined using generalized linear models.

Furthermore, the association between plasma ADGV and HTG risk was examined in the cross-sectional cohort using multivariable logistic regression, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). ADGV was analyzed both as a continuous variable (log₂-transformed, per SD increase) and a categorical variable (dichotomized at the median). Four sequential models were constructed with adjustment for covariates as follows: Crude model (unadjusted); Model 1 (adjusted for age, gender, BMI, and eGFR); Model 2 (Model 1 plus lifestyle factors: physical activity, drinking, and smoking); Model 3 (Model 2 plus disease status: T2DM and hypertension); and Model 4 (Model 3 plus liver function: AST/ALT ratio). Subgroup analyses were conducted based on gender, overweight status, T2DM status, hypertension status, and eGFR (< 90 vs. ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m²), using Model 4. Effect modification was evaluated by incorporating interaction terms, and significance was determined using likelihood ratio tests. To evaluate the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted. ADGV outliers, defined as those below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR, were labeled as NA and imputed using multiple imputations with the “mice” package in R (Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). The logistic regression was then reapplied to the imputed datasets. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, consistent with the primary analysis.

In the prospective cohort from Northern China (n = 348), Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to evaluate the association between ADGV exposure and incident HTG. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated through a series of multivariable adjustments. Due to the limited data availability in this cohort, drinking status, physical activity, and the AST/ALT ratio were not included in the adjustment. The models were constructed sequentially as follows: Crude model (unadjusted); Model 1 (adjusted for age, gender, BMI, and eGFR); Model 2 (Model 1 plus smoking status); Model 3 (Model 2 plus T2DM and hypertension status); and Model 4 (Model 3 plus ALT). Limited incident events yielded low event-per-variable (EPV) values, suggesting a potential risk of overfitting and imprecision in conventional Cox proportional hazards models; therefore, Firth’s penalized Cox regression was additionally performed as a sensitivity analysis using the “coxphf” package (version 1.13.4) in R. To evaluate whether ADGV provided incremental prognostic information beyond non-HDL cholesterol (non-HDL-C), non-HDL-C was calculated as total cholesterol minus HDL-C, and two nested Cox models were fitted: (i) a base model including non-HDL-C plus the fully adjusted covariate set (Model 4) and (ii) an extended model additionally including ADGV (modeled per SD increase) on top of Model 4. Incremental discrimination was quantified using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), and the absolute change in C-index (ΔC) between the extended and base models was reported. Improvements in model fit were assessed using the likelihood ratio test (LRT) for nested models and the Akaike information criterion (AIC). All Cox models were fitted using the “survival” package in R.

To address whether ADGV provides incremental prognostic information beyond standard lipid markers, we further compared a fully adjusted non-HDL-based Cox model with an extended model additionally including ADGV (per SD). Non-HDL-C was calculated as total cholesterol minus HDL-C. Incremental discrimination was quantified using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) derived from the fitted Cox models. Improvements in model fit were evaluated by nested model comparison using the likelihood ratio test (LRT; df = 1) and by the Akaike information criterion (AIC), with lower AIC indicating better fit after penalizing model complexity. This incremental analysis was conducted using one completed imputed dataset (the first imputation) for consistency with the time-to-event modeling.

All data from cell-based experiments were presented as mean ± SEM (n = 5 biological replicates). Statistical comparisons against the OA control group were performed using a one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test. This parametric approach was chosen after confirming that the data met the assumptions of normality (Shapiro-Wilk test, P > 0.05) and homogeneity of variances (Bartlett’s test, P > 0.05). When either assumption was violated, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s test was used. Significance levels were defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001; ns denotes not significant.

Results

Significant role of ADGV in HTG and other metabolic abnormalities in the cross-sectional cohort

In the cross-sectional cohort from Southern China, the median plasma ADGV concentration was 15.67 nM, with a range of 1.51 to 63.61 nM (Fig. 2A). ADGV levels differed significantly by gender, with higher concentrations in males than in females (median: 17.75 nM vs. 13.73 nM; Fig. 2A). Spearman correlation analysis revealed that plasma ADGV levels were positively correlated with ADMA, BMI, SBP, DBP, TG, Liver-IR, HOMA-IR, ALT, AST, and UA, while negatively correlated with BAIBA, homoarginine, SDMA, HDL-C, and AST/ALT ratio (all P < 0.05; Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S3). These results indicate that ADGV levels are closely associated with its metabolic substrate, ADMA, as well as competitive AGXT2 substrates (BAIBA, homoarginine, and SDMA), and various metabolic conditions, such as dyslipidemia, impaired glycemic homeostasis, and hyperuricemia.

To further assess the relevance of ADGV across different metabolic states, participants were stratified according to specific metabolic abnormalities (Table 1). Plasma ADGV concentrations were significantly elevated in individuals with HTG (P < 0.001), overweight (P < 0.001), hyperuricemia (P = 0.007), and dyslipidemia (P = 0.014) compared with their respective control groups (Supplementary Table S4). In contrast, no significant differences in ADGV levels were observed between control and participants with T2DM or hypertension. Both correlation and variance analysis between groups indicated that the association between ADGV and TG dysregulation was the most pronounced.

In the cross-sectional cohort, a total of 258 participants (43.88%) were diagnosed with HTG, and these individuals exhibited significantly higher plasma ADGV levels than those with normal TG concentrations (P < 0.001; Fig. 2C). Baseline characteristics stratified by the median plasma ADGV concentration (15.67 nM) are presented in Table 1. Individuals with ADGV levels above the median were more likely to be male and had higher BMI, DBP, ALT, AST, TG, and LDL-C levels. Conversely, higher ADGV levels were inversely associated with the AST/ALT ratio and HDL-C.

Correlation of ADGV with HTG in the cross-sectional cohort

In the crude logistic regression model, participants with ADGV levels above the median had over 1.8 times higher odds of HTG compared to those with lower levels (Fig. 3A). This association remained consistent after sequential adjustment for age, gender, BMI, and eGFR (Model 1); lifestyle factors (physical activity, drinking, smoking; Model 2), disease status (T2DM, hypertension; Model 3), and liver function (AST/ALT ratio; Model 4), with elevated ADGV levels corresponding to a 1.52–1.79-fold increase in HTG odds across models (Fig. 3A). When analyzed as a continuous variable, each per SD increase in log₂-transformed ADGV was associated with approximately 1.5 times higher probability of HTG (Fig. 3A). These findings indicate that ADGV was the only variable consistently linked to an increased risk of HTG, and multivariable adjustments had little impact on the observed association (Fig. 3A).

Subgroup analyses were further performed to examine whether the association between ADGV and HTG varied by key demographic and clinical factors. No significant interactions were observed with gender, overweight, T2DM, or hypertension status (Supplementary Figure S1). In addition, sensitivity analyses show that when participants with ADGV outliers were included and imputed, elevated ADGV levels remained independently associated with increased odds of HTG. Moreover, a dose-dependent relationship was observed across all adjusted models, with ADGV emerging as the sole variable exhibiting this progressive risk pattern (Supplementary Figure S2).

Association of ADGV with the HTG incident in the prospective cohort

In the prospective cohort from Northern China, participants were followed for a median of 1.8 years, with a range of 1.1 to 3.9 years. During follow-up, 32 out of 348 participants (9.2%) developed HTG. The ADGV levels ranged from 0.56 to 71.08 nM, with a median value of 18.28 nM. In the fully adjusted multivariable model, which was adjusted for age, gender, BMI, eGFR, smoking, and ALT, higher ADGV levels remained positively associated with the risk of incident HTG. Specifically, for each SD increase in ADGV, the HRs for HTG were 1.87 (95% CI: 1.16–3.02, P = 0.018), as shown in Fig. 3B. Considering the potential risk of overfitting due to low EPV in the adjusted model (EPV range: 3.56–6.10; Fig. 3B), we conducted a sensitivity analysis using Firth’s Cox regression. Results were highly consistent (HR per SD increase: 1.83; 95% CI: 1.14–2.95; P = 0.012; Supplementary Figure S3).

To further assess whether ADGV provides incremental prognostic information beyond non-HDL-C, we compared a fully adjusted non-HDL-C–based Cox model with an extended model additionally including ADGV (per SD). Adding ADGV improved model discrimination (C-index: 0.836 to 0.866; ΔC = 0.030) and overall model fit (LRT χ² = 7.37, P = 0.007; AIC: 284.70 to 279.33) (Supplementary Table S5).

ADGV stimulated the synthesis of TG in hepatocytes

To investigate the impacts of ADGV on TG synthesis, HepG2 cells were treated with BSA, OA, or OA+ADGV for 12 and 24 h. Oil Red O staining revealed markedly enhanced intracellular lipid deposition in the OA+ADGV group compared to the OA group, indicating increased lipid storage (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analysis of intracellular TG levels confirmed these results, showing a significant, time-dependent increase in TG content following ADGV treatment (Fig. 4B). The highest TG level, about 0.25 mmol/g protein, was observed in HepG2 cells treated with OA+ADGV for 24 h. These findings demonstrate that ADGV has a significant stimulatory effect on TG accumulation in hepatocytes.

ADGV upregulated the expression of enzymes responsible for fatty acid synthesis

To elucidate the regulatory role of ADGV in lipid metabolism, RT-qPCR was first employed to examine the mRNA expression of key genes involved in fatty acid synthesis (ACC, FAS, SREBP1c, and SCD1), fatty acid oxidation (CPT1α and PGC1α), and other lipid metabolism-related genes (ABCA1, ABCG1, ABCG5, ABCG8, CD36, CYP7A1, FDPS, HMGCR, HMGCS, LDLR, LXRα, SQLE, SRB1, SREBP2, and PCSK9). The results revealed that ADGV downregulated the expression of PGC1α, CPT1α, and ABCG5, while upregulating ACC mRNA (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Figure S4). Consistent with this, western blot analysis confirmed a significant increase in expression of ACC protein levels after ADGV treatment. However, no significant changes were observed in the p-ACC/ACC ratio, CPT1α, or PGC1α protein expression (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that ADGV promotes fatty acid synthesis primarily through regulating ACC expression.

Discussion

Previous studies across multiple cohorts, including the Framingham Heart Study, Malmö Diet and Cancer Cohort, Jackson Heart Study, and MAFLD cohorts, have established associations between ADGV and metabolic disorders, such as T2DM, early-stage cardiometabolic disease, hepatic steatosis, and MAFLD (O’Sullivan et al., 2017; Ottosson et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2022; Vernon et al., 2021; Wali et al., 2020). Most of these investigations, however, relied on non-targeted metabolomic approaches, which are limited by relatively low specificity and quantitative precision. In comparison, targeted detection methods typically yield more definitive and biologically specific results. Accurate quantification of analytes is further crucial for both clinical translation and mechanistic exploration. To date, only two studies have reported quantitatively measured ADGV levels (Han et al., 2025; Qadri et al., 2024). The potential causal effect of ADGV remain uncertain, as they are constrained by the cross-sectional design and limited sample sizes of the available studies. Here, utilizing a sensitive targeted LC-MS/MS approach across two independent community-dwelling cohorts (cross-sectional survey, n = 588; prospective longitudinal cohort, n = 348), we sought to bridge these gaps. This rigorous dual-cohort design allowed us to not only characterize the population distribution of ADGV with high analytical specificity but also delineate its temporal relationship with lipid traits, demonstrating a robust and potential causal role of ADGV in lipid dysregulation.

Our study provides a comprehensive quantitative assessment of ADGV in diverse Chinese populations. The dual-cohort design covered geographically distinct regions with distinct dietary and lifestyle patterns (Ye et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021), with a median plasma ADGV concentration of 15.67 nM in the cross-sectional cohort (Southern China) and 18.28 nM in the prospective cohort (Northern China). Notably, our measured concentrations align closely with the values reported in a recent diagnostic study by Qadri et al. (Qadri et al., 2024), lending further technical validity to our targeted assay. By characterizing the distribution of circulating ADGV across these distinct cohorts, we were able to evaluate ADGV–HTG relationships in complementary settings, enhancing the generalizability of our findings.

In the initial cross-sectional cohort, ADGV showed robust associations with key metabolic parameters (i.e., BMI, blood pressure, HDL-C) and multiple metabolic abnormalities (i.e., overweight, hyperuricemia, dyslipidemia). Higher circulating ADGV was independently associated with prevalent HTG, showing a ~ 1.5-fold higher odds per SD increase after extensive multivariable adjustment. These findings are consistent with its recognized role as a marker for metabolic diseases (O’Sullivan et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2022; Vernon et al., 2021). Strikingly, across the range of metabolic traits examined, triglyceride-related measures (TG and HTG) exhibited the strongest and most consistent associations with ADGV, aligning with previous reports linking ADGV to hepatic fat content and circulating lipids (O’Sullivan et al., 2017; Ottosson et al., 2019; Qadri et al., 2024). While we also examined associations with insulin resistance (IR) and T2DM, these relationships were less pronounced compared to HTG in our population. Given that HTG is a core pathway linking IR, T2DM, hepatic lipogenesis, and cardiovascular risk, we focused our subsequent investigation on the potential causal effect of ADGV in promoting the incidence of HTG in a prospective analysis and in vitro experiments.

Importantly, unlike prior studies that were primarily cross-sectional and limited by potential reverse causality, we expanded existing evidence by leveraging our independent prospective cohort, restricted to participants free of HTG at baseline. Although the number of incident cases was modest (n = 32; median follow-up, 1.8 years), sensitivity analysis using Firth’s penalized Cox regression yielded directionally consistent results, substantiating the temporal precedence of elevated ADGV relative to the onset of HTG. We also examined whether the ADGV–incident HTG association persists when accounting for established lipid indices by additionally adjusting for non-HDL-C. The association remained evident and statistical support for the extended model improved, suggesting that ADGV captures metabolic variation linked to HTG that is not fully represented by routine lipid measures. While these prospective findings warrant confirmation in larger cohorts with extended follow-up, this evidence lends further support to the hypothesis that ADGV is not merely a bystander but a contributor to the variation in host lipid metabolism.

To decipher the biological plausibility underlying these epidemiological observations, we integrated our cohort findings with in vitro mechanistic assays. Cellular experiments confirmed that ADGV treatment promoted TG accumulation in hepatocytes. In the present study, hepatocytes were treated with 20 nM ADGV, corresponding to the median plasma concentration observed in individuals with HTG, to evaluate its regulatory role in TG metabolism at a pathophysiology-relevant level. Mechanistically, ADGV up-regulated ACC expression, a key fatty acid synthesis enzyme, at both mRNA and protein levels, while down-regulating mRNAs levels of key genes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation, including PGC1α and CPT1α. Phosphorylation at Ser79 typically inhibits ACC activity; here, ADGV increased total ACC protein without altering its phosphorylation status, suggesting elevated ACC expression rather than enhanced enzymatic activity. Collectively, these integrated results highlight an important regulatory role for ADGV in dyslipidemia and HTG progression. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms of ADGV, including dose-response experiments and intracellular quantification to characterize its cellular pharmacokinetics and biological activity.

Intriguingly, our study also observed a pronounced sex-dependent difference in circulating ADGV levels. ADGV is primarily generated by the AGXT2 enzyme in the liver or the kidney (Rodionov et al., 2014). Although sexual dimorphism in AGXT2 expression has not been explicitly reported, its primary upstream regulator, HNF4α, exerts profound effects on sex-specific hepatic gene expression (Holloway et al., 2008). Consistently, male mice exhibit greater susceptibility to hepatic steatosis under the same metabolic stress conditions, suggesting an important role for hormonal influence in liver metabolism (Norheim et al. 2017). Collectively, these integrated results highlight a direct regulatory role for ADGV in disrupting hepatic lipid homeostasis, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed epidemiological associations.

From a translational perspective, ADGV quantification currently relies on targeted HPLC–MS/MS and is therefore more costly than routine lipid panels. However, routine lipids typically become abnormal after dyslipidemia is established, whereas our incident-HTG analysis suggests that elevated ADGV can be detected earlier and remains independently associated after adjustment. Thus, ADGV is not intended to replace standard lipid testing at present, but may complement it for prevention-oriented identification in higher-risk settings. These implications require validation in larger, multi-ethnic cohorts with longer follow-up; broader adoption and assay standardization would be expected to reduce costs over time.

Our study is strengthened by its large-scale dual-cohort design involving geographically distinct Chinese populations from Southern and Northern China. This setting not only provided a substantial sample size to ensure statistical power but also allowed us to characterize ADGV under diverse lifestyle and dietary contexts, thereby enhancing the generalizability and robustness of our findings. Moreover, by integrating a cross-sectional survey with a prospective longitudinal cohort, we were able to examine the ADGV–HTG relationship in complementary frameworks, combining the breadth of population distribution with the rigor of temporal validation. It should be noted that differences in baseline characteristics arising from these distinct study designs warrant cautious interpretation. Additionally, the use of targeted LC–MS/MS ensures high analytical specificity and quantitative precision compared to semi-quantitative profiling.

However, several limitations merit consideration. First, although we adjusted for a broad set of covariates, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly from unmeasured diet-related factors and other upstream exposures. Relatedly, we did not directly assess inflammatory pathways or include dedicated inflammatory biomarkers; therefore, we cannot determine whether inflammation mediates, modifies, or confounds the ADGV–HTG association, despite the established links between systemic inflammation, cardiometabolic risk and insulin resistance (Wali et al., 2020). Second, follow-up was relatively short and the number of incident HTG events was modest, which limits precision and necessitates confirmation in larger cohorts with longer follow-up. Third, while ADGV was quantified using targeted LC–MS/MS, we did not measure its stereoisomer SDGV in parallel, precluding assessment of isomer specificity and structure–activity relationships. Finally, because both cohorts comprised Chinese participants, the generalizability of our findings to other ancestries remains to be established.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings identify ADGV as an independently associated risk factor for HTG and incident HTG after multivariable adjustment. This paves the way for earlier interventions to prevent the progression to more severe cardiometabolic diseases. Our in vitro experiments further demonstrated that ADGV directly stimulates fatty acid synthesis in hepatocytes, suggesting a regulatory role of ADGV in hepatic lipid accumulation. Future research should employ in vivo studies to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which ADGV regulates ACC expression and drives HTG development, and confirm the ADGV–HTG associations in larger cohorts with longer follow-up.
