A 30-year-old woman presented with acute shortness of breath and pleuritic chest pain to the Accident and Emergency Department. She was a never-smoker with a background of seropositive non-erosive rheumatoid arthritis (RA) diagnosed in 2014. At diagnosis, her anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP) levels were 261 units·mL−1 and rheumatoid factor (RF) was 30 IU·mL−1. She was managed with disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, such as hydroxychloroquine and methotrexate, for 3 years until 2017. In 2017, methotrexate was replaced by leflunomide due to a poor response and nausea. She remained on leflunomide for another 3 years and it was then stopped in 2019 in view of pregnancy planning. In 2019 subcutaneous certolizumab was added on to her treatment and she had been on certolizumab for 18 months at the time of presentation.

Her chest radiograph (CXR) on presentation showed a large right-sided pneumothorax (figure 1). She had a normal pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate and oxygen saturations on air at rest. However, simple exertion such as walking a few steps made her very breathless. As per established local guidance, she was offered the choice between conservative management, admission with chest drain insertion or ambulatory management with either a 12 French gauge (FG) Seldinger chest drain (which would be connected to an ambulatory bag) or an 8FG Rocket Pleural Vent. Northumbria Healthcare NHS Trust has a well-established ambulatory pneumothorax service [1]. The ambulatory devices are inserted by Accident and Emergency Department practitioners and the subsequent management takes place on the Medical Ambulatory Care unit by acute medicine physicians, with the respiratory team getting involved in case of complications or non-resolution. With the premise that this was a primary pneumothorax (as the patient had no apparent lung disease), and because the priority of the patient was admission avoidance, she was seen by the pleural team and ambulatory management was initiated with an 8FG Rocket Pleural Vent.

Chest radiograph showing a large right-sided pneumothorax.

A repeat CXR showed good placement of the pleural vent with significant re-expansion of the right lung. She was discharged the same day and follow-up was arranged in 48 h, in accordance with local protocols. A repeat CXR then showed a small right residual pneumothorax alongside a new small left pneumothorax and a subtle cavitary lesion in the left upper zone (figure 2). A chest computed tomography (CT) scan was thus performed. This showed bilateral small pneumothoraces and cavitating lesions in both lungs (figure 3). There was no associated lymphadenopathy or pulmonary emboli. The differential diagnoses of such lesions include infection, infarcts, disseminated malignancy and in her case, rheumatoid lung nodules. A CXR carried out 6 months before the above presentation was normal. Given the rapid appearance of her pulmonary nodules, she was felt to have accelerated nodulosis, potentially secondary to certolizumab or less likely, leflunomide.

Chest radiograph showing the pleural vent in situ (orange arrow) with a small right residual pneumothorax (blue arrow) and a new small left pneumothorax (green arrow).

Computed tomography of the chest showing a) bilateral pneumothorax and cavitating lesions in both lungs, and b) bilateral pneumothorax.

The patient was asymptomatic from the left pneumothorax, and all breathlessness and pain had resolved at her 48-h review. It was decided that the left pneumothorax should be managed conservatively, at least initially. However, it was clear that there was an ongoing air leak on the right-hand side, given the persisting radiological appearances and the movement of the blue disc membrane in the pleural vent. The pleural team was asked for a review.

Answer 2

As per the recent British Thoracic Society 2023 guidelines [3], the indications for surgical management of a pneumothorax are persistent air leak, recurrence (i.e. second ipsilateral or first contralateral pneumothorax), radiologically demonstrated large bullae, spontaneous haemopneumothorax, incomplete expansion of the lung (despite chest drainage and suction), tension pneumothorax, bilateral pneumothoraces, spontaneous pneumothorax in a high-risk occupation and in whom recurrence prevention is deemed important (e.g. pilots, divers), and suspicion of malignancy. Surgical options are video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) with bullectomy and the method of pleurodesis can be chemical, mechanical abrasion or via pleurectomy, or more rarely now, via thoracotomy.

The CT scan showed probable rheumatoid nodules, and the aetiology of the pneumothoraces was felt to be the rupture of those nodules. Hence, this pneumothorax was not a primary spontaneous one, but rather a secondary one (due to underlying lung disease). The underlying lung disease was not apparent on the initial CXR. Thus, due to a persisting air leak in a secondary pneumothorax, and the appearance of a contralateral pneumothorax, she was referred for cardiothoracic surgery. Initially, a right-sided VATS with bullectomy, talc pleurodesis and nodule resection was performed. Repeated CXRs post-operatively showed an increase in the size of the left pneumothorax (figure 4) and she underwent the same surgical procedure on the left. Her post-operative course was first complicated by port site infections, then pneumothorax recurrence on the right, which was treated by an image-guided drain, and finally by pleural empyemas bilaterally requiring bilateral VATS washouts and insertion of tunnelled drains that were eventually removed a few months after the initial presentation. The patient is now fully recovered, with no pneumothorax recurrence experienced and no respiratory sequelae.

Discussion

RA is a multisystem disease which can have numerous respiratory complications. Interstitial lung disease either due to RA or drugs used to treat RA (for example methotrexate), progressive airway obstruction, pulmonary nodules, pleural effusions, pneumothorax and pulmonary vascular disease can all develop in patients with RA. Pulmonary rheumatoid nodules have a prevalence of <0.4% in radiological studies and up to 32% in lung biopsies, and are more common in males with positive RF, smokers, and in those with skin nodules and long-term methotrexate [4]. They are usually asymptomatic and do not require intervention. If the RA lung nodules have pleural contact, pleural effusions, infection, bronchopleural fistulas and pneumothoraces can develop [5].

Accelerated nodulosis (rapid growth of new or previous nodules) was initially described by Kremer and Lee [6] in 1986, who reported nodules in three out of 29 patients with RA treated with methotrexate. Timescales for accelerated nodulosis have varied from 3 months to 12 years, alongside a wide range of cumulative doses. Accelerated nodulosis has been seen with methotrexate, leflunomide, azathioprine, etanercept and infliximab [7]. Suggested risk factors are male sex, smoking and high RF as well as CCP factors. The pathogenesis of drug-related nodulosis is not clear. In the case of leflunomide, a putative mechanism is the decrease in monocyte activity and corresponding increase in RF [8]. Alveolar macrophages can then become a nidus for RF, eventually leading to formation of a nodule. For those on anti-tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α agents (such as etanercept or infliximab), an autoimmune reaction from the decrease in circulating TNF-α is suggested. Accelerated pulmonary nodulosis secondary to certolizumab has not been previously described in the literature [7–9].

Given the relatively rapid onset of nodulosis in close proximity to the introduction of certolizumab and negative staining with stains such as Ziehl–Neelsen and Grocott, the clinical–radiological impression was of rheumatoid nodules. Anti-TNF-α drugs are recognised to cause reactivation of latent tuberculosis and therefore exclusion of infections, such as pulmonary tuberculosis, was important. On stopping certolizumab and leflunomide, her RA became more active. The relative immunosuppression probably also contributed to the development of port site infections and resultant empyemas. Her RA was managed with a slow, tapering course of oral steroids (prednisolone) over 20 months with infrequent courses of intra-articular and intra-muscular steroid injections along with hydroxychloroquine.

Once fully recovered from infection, she was commenced on two pulses of rituximab every 6 months. As TNF-α levels are low in nodules, rituximab is thought of as a good treatment option rather than prioritising anti-TNF-α drugs. However, our patient had minimal clinical effect from this and after two paired pulses was switched to the Janus kinase 1 inhibitor upadacitinib along with sulfasalazine and hydroxychloroquine. Her disease has remained quiescent on this regime with no worsening of subcutaneous nodules clinically and a repeat CT of the thorax a year later revealed no new or residual nodules.

The management of her pneumothorax fell within accepted local and regional protocols. Bilateral spontaneous pneumothorax is rare; the incidence is thought to be between 2% and 8% of all pneumothoraces [10]. A variety of causes have been purported, from spontaneous cases being most common in a cohort from Tunisia to tuberculosis or to widespread lung disease [11, 12]. In the acute phase, decompression of at least one of the hemithoraces is suggested (due to the risk of tension pneumothorax in the case of bilateral pneumothoraces), and surgery is the recognised treatment of choice for such cases.
