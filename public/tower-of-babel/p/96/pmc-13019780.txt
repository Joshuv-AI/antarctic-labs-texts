Transparency in reporting frameworks in Osteoarthritis and Cartilage Open: Research designs and value streams
Authors: Robin Christensen, Tobias Haugegaard, Henning Madry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transparent and complete reporting is essential to the credibility and utility of health research, yet the quality of such reporting remains inconsistent. Also, in osteoarthritis (OA) research, poor reporting undermines reproducibility, synthesis, and translation into practice and policy. Therefore, clear and accurate reporting is not merely a bureaucratic exercise but foundational because it establishes trust and confidence in the reported outcomes. This editorial emphasizes the ultimate importance of adopting reporting guidelines developed by the Enhancing the Quality and Transparency of Health Research (EQUATOR) Network. Such guidelines include CONSORT for randomized trials, STROBE for observational studies, PRISMA for systematic reviews, and SPIRIT for protocols. Altogether, these helpful tools provide structured frameworks that enhance clarity, rigor, and reproducibility across study designs.We also adapt the concept of “value streams” from lean management as a metaphor for scientific reporting: each checklist item coherently contributes incremental value within a logical, unidirectional flow that transforms research into coherent and trustworthy publications. Early adoption of these value streams and reporting guidelines by authors—and their integration into the publishing process, including reviewer training—will strengthen the quality, relevance, and long-term value of OA research and help ensure that reporting frameworks are truly transparent and accessible.

Clear, complete, and transparent reporting is essential to the usefulness and trustworthiness of health research. However, reporting quality is often inconsistent, limiting reproducibility, synthesis, and application of findings [1], including in osteoarthritis (OA) research [2]. Poor reporting compromises research utility at every stage of the academic value stream - from design and execution to interpretation, translation, and implementation [3]. For OA research to meaningfully inform experimental and clinical practice and policy, scientific journals must lead the way by enforcing rigorous reporting standards, ensuring that findings are fully applicable—thereby facilitating accurate editorial review and helping systematic reviewers extract essential information later. Researchers, in turn, need to adopt structured, purpose-built frameworks from the earliest stages of study design.

This editorial advocates for adopting established reporting guidelines and principles from the Enhancing the Quality and Transparency of Health Research (EQUATOR) Network. The EQUATOR Network is an international initiative aimed at improving the quality of scientific publications through the promotion of transparent and accurate reporting [4]. Their most influential contributions are a series of widely adopted reporting guidelines that have become gold standards across clinical and health research [3]. A range of well-established reporting guidelines were developed to improve clarity, transparency, and reproducibility of health research. Among the most successful guidelines are CONSORT (and its extensions) for randomized trials; STROBE for observational studies; PRISMA and PRISMA-P for systematic reviews and their protocols; SPIRIT for clinical trial protocols; STARD and TRIPOD for diagnostic and prediction studies; CARE for case reports; AGREE and RIGHT for clinical practice guidelines; SRQR and COREQ for qualitative research; ARRIVE for animal pre-clinical studies; SQUIRE for quality improvement research; and CHEERS for economic evaluations [5]. While researchers are not expected to know the details of all 673 currently listed guidelines, cultivating an awareness of this landscape and the ability to see the big picture is mandatory. Such a familiarity helps researchers, reviewers, and editors to ensure studies are reported with adequate methodological rigor and completeness.

Value streams: embracing reporting checklists

Although scientific research is largely dissimilar to business management, its concepts may be applied also to the process of scientific reporting. In Lean Management (a holistic approach to increase productivity), a value stream represents the entire sequence of activities required to deliver a product or service to the customer - from initial concept to final delivery. When applied to academia, this includes all the steps and processes, spanning different scientific contributors, from those addressing design issues to methodologists and biostatisticians. Value stream mapping (a lean management technique used to analyze and streamline all steps in a process) is a key tool for visualizing and analyzing these streams. Reporting checklists, such as those developed by the EQUATOR Network, can be conceptualized as value streams—ordered sequences of essential steps that transform raw research into publications of high scientific value with a clear structure. Just as in traditional value stream mapping—where each step adds incremental value and must occur in the correct order—checklist items are not arbitrary; they reflect a logical, methodological sequence, typically a unidirectional flow (often visualized as arrows in one direction). Another important resource for researchers who aim to publish with a respected biomedical journal is the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) who has developed recommendations to promote best practices and ethical standards in the conduct and reporting of research and other material published in medical journals [6]. These guidelines are intended to assist researchers/authors, editors, and peer reviewers in creating and disseminating accurate, clear, reproducible, and unbiased medical journal articles.

The ICMJE Recommendations urge authors to follow their guidance alongside journal-specific instructions [7] and to consult EQUATOR Network standards relevant to their study type. Original research articles typically follow the “IMRaD” structure - Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion - which mirrors the scientific process. While EQUATOR emphasizes checklist items, the value stream analogy offers a helpful perspective on how these elements align within a logical, methodological flow. Clear subheadings within these sections remain essential for organizing content effectively. The sections of Table 1 may serve as a logical 'value stream,' guiding researchers and thereby the reader through the scientific rationale, design, findings, and implications. Using Table 1 as a value stream, means focusing on the matter at hand, the order of these sections reflects the intended flow of scientific argumentation; the “implied arrows” are unidirectional, and adhering to this order helps ensure transparency, coherence, and reproducibility. As an added benefit, researchers planning a study can largely reuse the same paragraph headings when writing the protocol, where the content is essentially the same but typically phrased in the future tense.

As evident from the many EQUATOR reporting guidelines available, while the content varies by study design, maintaining the prescribed order is essential to preserve scientific logic and enable appraisal and reproducibility. From a value stream perspective - a lean management concept mapping the flow of value from idea to impact - reporting failures create blockages. Poorly reported studies degrade valuable scientific research by preventing appraisal, reuse, or implementation. Each checklist item depends on proper execution and reporting of prior steps, reflecting a unidirectional flow. Skipping or omitting early elements disrupts both internal coherence and external credibility. In other words, researchers must follow the “first things first” principle: clearly defining objectives, justifying sample size, describing allocation and blinding, before interpreting outcomes. Viewing checklists as structured value streams encourages treating manuscript preparation not as box-ticking, but as a stepwise process building trustworthiness. Each completed item adds clarity, reduces ambiguity, and increases the chances the manuscript will inform practice, policy, or future research. Furthermore, when established reporting guidelines are understood as value streams, they function as practical organizing structures rather than administrative add-ons. Reporting frameworks make the “first things first” logic explicit, thereby preventing avoidable omissions, inconsistencies, and revisions later in the editorial and peer-review process. In this way, reporting guidelines guide scientific processes rather than burden it.

Aligning reporting with specific research designs

Authors of research articles frequently neglect to report details about the performed study, which may be important for future readers to know. This can delay publication and stop the work being used, cited or replicated. Altogether, this represents a considerable waste of the human and financial resources invested in research. One of the most effective ways to enhance reporting is to align it tightly with study design. Each design carries distinct methodological and reporting demands. Confusing or blurring study types (for example, presenting a retrospective case series as if it were a cohort study) invites misinterpretation and potentially weakens scientific dialogue. Clear labeling of research designs, use of the appropriate checklists, and adherence to study-specific reporting norms allow better peer review and synthesis. Moreover, reporting guidelines help authors avoid common pitfalls and omissions that undermine their work's credibility and impact.

The EQUATOR Network originally developed a simple flow chart to guide authors, editors, and peer reviewers in selecting the most appropriate checklist and reporting guideline (see adapted Fig. 1). Getting it out in the open

Transparency is fundamental to scientific progress and public trust. In OA research, this principle is not just aspirational - it is also practical. Given the global burden of disease and the diversity of interventions studied, clear and consistent reporting enhances cross-study learning, supports meta-research, and strengthens the evidence base for clinical decision-making.

Osteoarthritis and Cartilage Open already aligns with these principles through its Instructions for Authors, which require adherence to appropriate reporting guidelines. Building on these existing practices, we highlight the following actions that can support sustained, consistent, and effective implementation of high-quality reporting standards:•Journal Integration: Maintain and reinforce the requirement for design-specific EQUATOR guideline adherence within author instructions and submission workflows and encourage checklist submission and protocol registration.•Reviewer Training: Provide guidance to peer reviewers on assessing reporting quality, including how to use the EQUATOR tools.•Author Resources: Develop resources or workshops to support authors in identifying and applying the correct guidelines.•Early Uptake: Promote the use of reporting guidelines at the study planning phase. This can be implemented through education, template protocols, or links to funder requirements.•Continuous Review: Periodically revisit and update journal policies to reflect evolving reporting standards and community needs.

Conclusion

Improved, clear and accurate reporting is not merely a bureaucratic exercise; it is a foundational investment in the scientific process because it ensures integrity, enables research reproducibility, and thus establishes trust and confidence in the reported outcomes. By aligning research designs with EQUATOR guidelines and embedding these tools throughout the scientific value stream, the OA research community will further strengthen the credibility, reproducibility, and relevance of its work. Let us get our frameworks out in the open! Let us ensure that the work we publish today also holds value tomorrow!
