Lung cancer remains the deadliest form of cancer, responsible for 1.8 million deaths annually, with a growing incidence projected to reach 4.62 million new cases by 2050 [1]. In 20–25% of cases, when lung cancer is detected early, curative treatment through surgery is the primary intervention, yielding 5-year survival rates between 53% and 93%, depending on the clinical stage [2]. However, even with successful treatment, individuals previously treated for lung cancer continue to experience significant impairments in daily living activities, high levels of dyspnoea and pain, and reduced exercise capacity and health-related quality of life [3]. Therefore, it is crucial that our care extends beyond survival alone, addressing the broader burden experienced by survivors.

Rehabilitation is a cornerstone intervention for various chronic diseases and should be an integral component of lung cancer care. Research indicates that rehabilitation after lung cancer surgery can significantly enhance functional exercise capacity and muscle strength, and supports individuals coping with the anxiety, uncertainty and stigma often associated with a lung cancer diagnosis [4]. Despite these benefits, the widespread implementation and endorsement of rehabilitation for lung cancer have been hindered by a lack of robust study designs and standardised outcomes to guide clinical practice, resulting in a low certainty of evidence for clinically meaningful outcomes such as dyspnoea. Consequently, future rehabilitation trials remain necessary to strengthen this evidence base [4]. Furthermore, the lack of a unified framework for measuring rehabilitation outcomes has impeded benchmarking across rehabilitation centres, which is a critical step in ensuring quality of care and optimising rehabilitation programmes [5, 6].

In this issue of ERJ Open Research, Edbrooke et al. [7] present a newly developed core outcome set (COS), along with core measurement instruments for evaluating lung cancer rehabilitation in clinical practice. A COS is an evidence-informed consensus on a minimum set of critically important outcomes needed to inform decision-making in a specific healthcare area [8]. Core measurement instruments represent the most rigorous methods for measuring each selected outcome [8]. By standardising what is measured and how, a COS ensures the outcomes most relevant to patients, health professionals and other stakeholders are consistently captured. The adoption of standardised rehabilitation COS can have significant implications in both clinical and research contexts (figure 1). In the clinical research context, COSs ensure that trials or clinical studies address the most critical outcomes, thereby maximising their interpretability and value while reducing research waste [8]. In parallel, they facilitate data pooling, enabling meta-analyses and stronger evidence syntheses and recommendations. In the clinical setting, COSs highlight the key aspects of disease burden, guiding health professionals to assess patients holistically and optimise treatments for maximum clinical benefit, beyond survival [8]. From a health system perspective, they facilitate benchmarking across rehabilitation programmes, helping identify best practices and optimise rehabilitation delivery.

To develop this COS, the Lung Cancer Rehabilitation Collaborative employed a state-of-the-art methodology that was based on a transparent, prospective protocol [10]. This included a systematic review of lung cancer rehabilitation trials to identify outcomes and measurement instruments [11], followed by an international, multistakeholder consensus process involving two modified Delphi surveys and consensus meetings as needed. Ultimately, consensus was reached on the inclusion of six core outcomes: breathlessness, activities of daily living, physical function, health-related quality of life, emotional and mental wellbeing, and pain [7]. Moreover, the panel recommended using the 6-min walking test for assessing physical function and to measure health-related quality of life, the European Organisation for the Research and Treatment of Cancer core questionnaire (EORTC-QLQ-C30) [7]. The EORTC QLQ-C30 assesses quality of life in people living with cancer across key domains including functional status, symptom burden, global health and financial impact [12]. Collectively, these outcomes provide a holistic evaluation of the patient via straightforward measures, thereby supporting cost-effectiveness, equity and broad applicability in everyday clinical practice.

The main limitation of the lung cancer rehabilitation COS was the relatively small number of patient and caregiver participants: only 11 contributed to outcome prioritisation and just two to the selection of measurement instruments. This reflects the broader challenge of involving patients in COS development, particularly instrument selection, due to the complexity of discussing and translating technical outcome definitions, navigating consensus on rating–scale nuances, and ensuring comprehension of measurement instrument properties among patient and caregiver participants [13]. Notably, for outcome prioritisation, patient and caregiver feedback was evaluated independently from other stakeholders, ensuring their perspectives were not overshadowed. This separate consideration was not feasible during the measurement instrument selection; however, the primary criteria for choosing instruments are based on their psychometric properties, with patient input mainly relating to feasibility and acceptability [14]. Consequently, the limited patient and caregiver participation was less critical at this stage.

Geographic representativeness was another limitation. All patients and carers were from Australasia, with minimal representation from the Americas and Africa across all stakeholder groups. Moreover, it remains unclear whether low-income countries were adequately represented. While the authors acknowledge this constraint, the strong consensus on outcome inclusion and exclusion achieved after the second Delphi round suggests limited heterogeneity in stakeholder opinions.

Finally, the panel did not recommend instruments for all outcomes, perhaps due to limited psychometric data for certain instruments in this patient population, feasibility concerns or stakeholder disagreement, underscoring the need for further research.

Unfortunately, a major hurdle lies ahead for the study group: achieving widespread uptake and implementation of the COS. This is one of the primary challenges in COS development and the main measure of its success [15]. The panel will need to disseminate the COS and engage relevant stakeholders, including patient organisations, scientific societies, regulatory authorities, healthcare professionals and researchers, to raise awareness and gain support. Establishing clear guidelines, training healthcare professionals and ensuring adequate resources will be crucial for widespread adoption. Piloting the COS in real-world clinical settings would help identify and overcome logistical barriers, while integrating it into electronic health record systems could streamline data collection and enhance large-scale analytics for benchmarking.

Fortunately, over the last few years, a growing number of core outcomes have been developed for various respiratory conditions [5, 16–21] and cancers [22, 23], leading to increased recognition of their importance. Support from international scientific bodies such as the European Respiratory Society, American Thoracic Society, Union for International Cancer Control and the International Agency for Research on Cancer as well as the relevant patient organisations is crucial. While these societies have endorsed COS in principle, more active endorsement and advocacy are still required [24].

To place these findings in context, when compared to the COS for pulmonary rehabilitation in people living with COPD, there is considerable overlap in selected core outcomes (∼50%) [5, 9]. However, outcomes such as pain and emotional wellbeing, deemed critical for lung cancer rehabilitation, are only secondary in the COPD COS [5]. This distinction underscores the necessity for disease–specific COSs to address the unique challenges faced by different patient populations and their most prevalent treatable traits.

Although this COS was developed for assessing lung cancer rehabilitation in clinical practice, it was established through a multistakeholder agreement on the most critical outcomes. Thus, it can also serve as a useful framework in clinical research until a dedicated research-focused COS is developed. The key distinction of a clinical practice COS is its emphasis on feasibility and equity in routine care, prioritising outcomes deemed most meaningful by patients and healthcare professionals, even if some research–focused endpoints (e.g. mechanistic biomarkers or long–term survival metrics) lie outside its scope; this focus does not diminish the relevance of these outcomes for research purposes [25].

Overall, the development of this COS marks a major step forward in elevating the lung cancer rehabilitation standards. Its ultimate success depends on widespread implementation and consistent use in both research and clinical settings, which will help bridge the gap between aspirational guidelines and everyday practice [24]. By unifying outcome measurement, the COS paves the way for more robust comparisons, stronger evidence synthesis, and consequently more targeted, effective interventions [8, 24]. This, in turn, stands to improve patient care, enhance survivorship experiences and foster better long-term outcomes for those affected by this disease [8, 24].
