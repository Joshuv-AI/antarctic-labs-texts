Integrated Inpatient Trauma-Focused Treatment for Children and Parents: A Longitudinal Evaluation Study
Authors: L T Lysbert Zeinstra, W J Wendy Post, J Jana Knot-Dickscheit, A M N Anne-Marie Huyghen, A Abigail Akeredolu, F Femy Wanders, A T Annemiek Harder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis longitudinal study evaluated the outcomes of the KINGS inpatient trauma-focused family treatment program for families experiencing complex and multiple problems. The program integrates trauma treatment for both parents and children with interventions targeting emotion regulation, parenting, and the parent–child relationship, delivered in an inpatient setting that provides safety and treatment intensity. A total of 68 families (132 children, 78 parents) participated, with data collected at baseline, post-treatment, and at 3- and 6-month follow-up. Results showed significant reductions in child and parental trauma symptoms and child psychosocial problems, which were maintained across the follow-up period. Parenting stress and quality of family and parenting conditions also improved significantly during the program, although these gains showed a decline during the follow-up period, indicating the need for continued support. The findings highlight the potential of intensive inpatient trauma-focused treatment for families with severe trauma-related problems who have not benefited from previous interventions. Future research should further investigate mechanisms of change and strategies for sustaining outcomes after discharge.Keywords: Trauma, Family treatment, PTSD, Parenting stress, Family functioning, Emotional and behavioral problems

Introduction

There is increasing scientific and societal attention to the long-term effects of childhood exposure to traumatic events (Hamby et al., 2021; Struck et al., 2021). Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) – including abuse, neglect, or household dysfunction – have been shown to significantly contribute to negative physical and mental health outcomes throughout life (Felitti et al., 2019). In recent years, the focus of ACEs research, practice, and policy has shifted from describing how ACEs affect long-term outcomes to developing strategies to prevent ACEs in children (Narayan et al., 2021). Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of parents’ own early life adversity as a key risk factor for ACEs in their children (Narayan et al., 2020, 2021; Schofield et al., 2018). Parental ACEs are associated with parental mental health problems and traumatic stress, which can impair parenting skills and threaten child well-being (Hays-Grudo & Morris, 2020). To address these risks, the U.S. Center for the Study of Social Policy (CSSP) states:

parents need interventions that help them to manage clinical symptoms and reactions to their own histories of poor attachments and trauma, to protect children from adversity and trauma as best they can, and to provide more nurturing care that promotes secure emotional attachment and healthy development in their children.

(Harper Browne, 2014, p.24).

One group within the child welfare system that is particularly vulnerable to ACE exposure consists of families facing an accumulation of persistent socioeconomic (e.g., financial), psychosocial (e.g., mental health), and parenting problems. These families, also referred to as families experiencing complex and multiple problems (FECMP; Van Assen et al., 2020; Tausendfreund et al., 2016; Ghesquière, 1993), are at higher risk than the general population for exposure to traumatic events (Bunting et al., 2017; Deković & Bodden, 2019), including complex trauma (CT). CT refers to prolonged and repeated exposure to invasive traumatic experiences, such as abuse or neglect, typically occurring within close relationships during developmentally sensitive periods (Courtois & Ford, 2009; Lawson & Quinn, 2013).

Children exposed to CT are at increased risk of developing not only posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) but also a range of additional problems, including problems in attachment, behavior, and emotion regulation, as well as low self-esteem and interpersonal problems (D’Andrea et al., 2012; Spinazzola et al., 2021). For parents, the simultaneous experience of PTSD symptoms and multiple stressors can negatively affect parenting practices and emotional availability. Parental PTSD is associated with decreased sensitivity and increased parental hostility toward their children (Christie et al., 2019; Greene et al., 2020; Van Ee et al., 2012), as well as increased child distress and behavior problems (Lambert et al., 2014; Van Ee et al., 2012).

To mitigate the intergenerational transmission of trauma-related problems and their associated adverse outcomes in FECMP, an integrated treatment approach is needed that addresses both child and parental trauma symptoms and related problems in areas such as attachment, behavior, emotion regulation, and parenting (Figley & Kiser, 2013; Kinniburgh et al., 2005; Van der Hoeven et al., 2023). However, there is currently a gap in the availability of such integrated treatment options (Barth et al., 2023; Van der Hoeven et al., 2023).

On the one hand, international guidelines identify trauma therapies such as Trauma-Focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (TF-CBT; Cohen & Mannarino, 2015) and Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR; Shapiro, 2018) as first-choice treatments for PTSD in both children and adults (NICE, 2018; WHO, 2013). Yet, clearinghouses differ in their ratings of the strength of evidence (e.g., Prevention Services Clearinghouse, 2019; CEBC, 2024), underscoring the need for cautious claims about their effectiveness across populations and settings. Moreover, these interventions typically target either the parent (i.e., adult) or the child in isolation, without fully addressing the interconnectedness of their trauma experiences (Fictorie et al., 2022; Mooren et al., 2023; Stolper et al., 2022). This lack of integration is particularly problematic when parents face high levels of parenting stress and have limited capacity to support their child’s recovery by providing emotional support, positive experiences, or acting as co-therapists (Meijer et al., 2025; Mevissen et al., 2020; Shapiro et al., 2017). In addition, the complex symptomatology of children exposed to complex trauma often leads to challenges such as extreme avoidance, behavioral dysregulation, and treatment motivation problems, which can impede engagement in standard trauma-focused therapies and result in suboptimal outcomes or treatment dropout (DeLorenzi et al., 2016; Wesselmann & Shapiro, 2013; Struik et al., 2017; Van der Hoeven et al., 2022).

On the other hand, systemic home-based interventions are commonly provided to FECMP, such as Families First (FF), Intensive Family Treatment (IFT), Parent Management Training-Oregon (PMTO), and Triple P level 4–5. These programs typically aim to improve parenting practices, reduce child behavior problems, and strengthen family functioning. However, their effectiveness varies substantially between interventions, between countries, and even between studies of the same program (Evenboer et al., 2018). Meta-analyses indicate that while these interventions produce modest improvements in family functioning and child behavior during treatment, the sustainability of effects is low (Al et al., 2012; Lippens et al., 2025; Van Assen et al., 2020). Many children continue to experience emotional and behavioral problems and stressful life events at case closure, and out-of-home placement rates increase in the months thereafter (Van Assen et al., 2020, 2023). One explanation may be that, although such programs target the family context, they primarily focus on parenting practices and child behavior, without directly addressing the trauma-related difficulties that disrupt both parent and child functioning (Visscher et al., 2020b, 2022). Moreover, while designed to involve all family members, these interventions often emphasize parents as the primary agents of change, with relatively limited involvement of children themselves (Tausendfreund et al., 2015; Visscher et al., 2020a). This focus may hinder their effectiveness in families with complex trauma histories.

Overall, there remains a gap in treatment options for traumatized families that target both child and parental PTSD and family functioning in a setting that ensures the safety of all family members. To address this need, a Dutch child and adolescent psychiatric treatment center developed the KINGS (a Dutch acronym for Child IN Healthy System) program, which is an intensive, inpatient treatment program that addresses the entire family system while focusing on trauma processing, regulation, parenting, and the parent–child relationship. Children and parents in families referred to KINGS have been exposed to traumatic events, such as violence, sexual abuse, neglect, and physical and/or emotional bullying (Ploeg & Wanders-Mulder, 2019; Zeinstra et al., 2024). KINGS aims to improve family functioning by reducing trauma symptoms in both children and parents, reducing emotional and behavioral problems in children, and improving parenting skills and positive parent–child interactions. The treatment model consists of three phases: (1) psychoeducation, motivation, and skills, (2) trauma processing, and (3) skill integration and relapse prevention. Parents and children go through the same phases, with the parent moving ahead of the child in each phase. This allows the parent to support the child during treatment.

The aim of this preregistered study1 is to evaluate the longitudinal outcomes of the innovative KINGS trauma-focused family treatment program. The study addresses the urgent need to evaluate integrated approaches that address both child and parental trauma in order to break the cycle of intergenerational transmission of adversity and promote healthier family dynamics. The first objective was to examine the severity of problems in KINGS families at the start of the intervention. Based on the target population of KINGS (Ploeg & Wanders-Mulder, 2019) and previous case-file research on the characteristics of KINGS families (Zeinstra et al., 2024), we hypothesized that the majority of families would score in the subclinical or clinical range at baseline on measures of child and parental trauma symptoms, child psychosocial problems, parenting stress, and quality of the family and parenting conditions indicating a higher level of problems compared to the general population (Hypothesis 1).

The second objective was to examine whether participation in KINGS was associated with reductions in child and parental trauma symptoms, child psychosocial problems, and parenting stress, as well as improvements in the quality of the family and parenting conditions. Based on the intended outcomes of KINGS (Ploeg & Wanders-Mulder, 2019) and previous research on the effects of intensive individual treatment for (complex) PTSD (e.g., Hendriks et al., 2017; Matthijssen et al., 2024; Van Woudenberg et al., 2018; Voorendonk et al., 2020) and intensive home-based systemic interventions (Al et al., 2012; Asscher et al., 2012; Van Assen et al., 2020, 2023; Van der Pol et al., 2017), we hypothesized a decrease in problems and an improvement in family functioning. Specifically, we expected reductions in child and parental trauma symptoms (Hypotheses 2a and 2b), child psychosocial problems (Hypothesis 2c), and parenting stress (Hypothesis 2d), as well as improvements in the quality of the family and parenting conditions (Hypothesis 2e), from baseline to the end of treatment, and at 3-month and 6-month follow-up.

In addition, we hypothesized that families would show variability in outcomes (Hypothesis 3). This hypothesis reflects the expectation that, because FECMP present with diverse constellations of problems (Zeinstra et al., 2024; Taussendfreund et al., 2016), changes would not be uniform across families. Demonstrating such between-family variance is important as a first step toward understanding differential responsiveness to the program.

Method

Design

We used a longitudinal observational design to examine the outcomes of the KINGS family treatment program. Questionnaires for each of the dependent variables were completed at the start of KINGS (T0; baseline), at the end of KINGS (T2), and at 3-month (T3) and 6-month (T4) follow-up. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of Erasmus University Rotterdam (20-064).

Intervention

The KINGS program typically lasts 6 to 8 weeks, with families living in a separate housing unit on the treatment center grounds. This consists of either small family houses situated in a green campus-like environment (site A; capacity 5–6 families) or a residential house in a regular neighborhood (site B; capacity 2 families). Families are referred by their primary care physician, social services, or child protective services, followed by an orientation meeting with the family and clinicians to assess suitability and prepare the family for admission. During their stay, families receive ongoing support from family support workers, who help them identify and change dysfunctional family patterns. These support workers are trained in cognitive-behavioral techniques and trauma-informed care, focusing on providing choices, fostering collaboration, empowering the family, recognizing trauma triggers, and understanding emotional and behavioral issues in the context of trauma histories, ensuring safety for the families involved (Gardiner et al., 2017; Ploeg & Wanders-Mulder, 2019). Care is tailored to the needs and goals of the family. Therapy sessions and family meetings with therapists take place at a central facility on the site.

The program consists of three phases, each lasting on average 1–3 weeks. Parents and children move through the same phases, with parents always one phase ahead of their child. This sequencing ensures that parents have the emotional and cognitive capacity to effectively support their child during treatment, as their own trauma processing enhances their ability to respond from a more regulated and stable state (Ploeg & Wanders-Mulder, 2019; Ploeg et al., in press).

The first phase focuses on creating a safe environment and educating families about trauma and attachment. Professionals educate parents about how trauma affects both their own and their child’s behavior and use motivational interviewing techniques (Greenwald, 2009; Miller & Rollnick, 2013) to help families increase their motivation for (trauma) treatment. Parents participate in body-oriented therapy that combines techniques from trauma-sensitive mindfulness (Treleaven, 2018), trauma-sensitive yoga (Emerson, 2015), and compassion training (Gilbert, 2005) to manage stress and prepare for trauma processing. In addition, video feedback (Wels, 2001; Balldin et al., 2016) is used repeatedly across the program: short recordings of everyday family interactions (e.g., mealtimes, play, routines) are reviewed with parents to identify strengths, highlight moments of attunement, and practice alternative parenting responses. Applied behavior analysis (Cooper et al., 2019) is used to further improve family dynamics and help parents become more attuned to their children's signals.

The second phase involves intensive trauma treatment, with parents receiving daily therapy sessions using EMDR (Shapiro, 2018) and Imaginal Exposure (IE; Foa et al., 2007) conducted by certified EMDR therapists. As parents near the end of their own therapy, their children begin trauma treatment using the same evidence-based approaches (EMDR/IE), adapted to the child’s developmental level and attention span (e.g., several shorter sessions per day rather than one long session). Parents are encouraged to be present before, during, and after these sessions, offering co-regulatory support from a more regulated emotional state developed through their own prior trauma treatment.

The third phase takes place during the final part of the inpatient admission and focuses on integrating new skills and preventing relapse. Parents learn to apply new or reinforce existing parenting strategies in daily life while continuing to support their child's ongoing treatment. As part of discharge planning, a relapse prevention plan is developed with the family and referring professionals to address future challenges and maintain the progress made during the program. Follow-up support is provided through transfer to local services, and families have access to telephone consultations with program staff when needed. A more detailed description of the program theory and care provision of KINGS can be found in Ploeg and Wanders-Mulder (2019), Ploeg et al. (in press) and Zeinstra et al. (2025).

Participants and Procedure

We recruited participants from the two inpatient sites offering the KINGS program, both of which are part of a child and adolescent psychiatry organization located in the northern and central parts of the Netherlands. The study aimed to include all families who participated in the KINGS program during the research period (November 2021 – November 2023). We approached 82 families during the program’s intake process and provided them with both verbal and written information about the study. The inclusion criteria for participation were as follows: (a) children who participated directly in the study through self-reports had to be at least 8 years old, and (b) parents and children had to have sufficient Dutch language proficiency to complete the questionnaires. We obtained legal consent from the parents and, where applicable, from children aged 12 years or older. There were no specific exclusion criteria.

Of the 82 families approached, 13 declined participation. The main reasons were feeling overburdened by their family situation and therefore unable to take on additional commitments (n = 10), privacy concerns (n = 1), and unspecified reasons (n = 2). Of these 13 families, eight ultimately completed the KINGS program, three started but left the program prematurely, and two withdrew before admission. The remaining 69 families consented to participate in the study. One family (n = 1 parent; n = 1 child) later objected to the use of their data for research purposes after completing the KINGS program, resulting in a final sample of 68 families. This comprised 78 parents (87.2% mothers) and 132 children (52.3% boys), of whom 66 children participated directly through self-report (50% boys). The mean age of all participating children was 8.1 years, and the mean age of the children who provided self-report data was 10.9 years. Instruments

We measured exposure to potentially traumatic events and the presence of PTSD according to DSM-5 criteria in children aged 8 years and older and parents. The Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS-5; Boeschoten et al., 2014; Weathers et al., 2013), the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for Children and Adolescents DSM-5 (CAPS-CA; Pynoos et al., 2015), and the Diagnostic Interview Trauma and Stressors – Intellectual Disability (DITS; Mevissen et al., 2018) all assess symptom severity based on the 20 DSM-5 PTSD criteria, which are divided into four clusters: intrusive symptoms, avoidance symptoms, cognitive and mood symptoms, and arousal and reactivity symptoms. We used the CAPS-5 to measure PTSD in parents and the CAPS-CA DSM-5 for children, or the DITS in cases of mild intellectual disability or difficulty understanding or accurately answering the questions of the CAPS-5 or CAPS-CA DSM-5. The Dutch versions of the CAPS-5, CAPS-CA DSM-5, and DITS have good psychometric properties (Boeschoten et al., 2018; Diehle et al., 2013; Mevissen et al., 2016).

We measured parental trauma symptoms using the Dutch version of the PTSD Symptoms Scale, Self-Report version (PTSD-SR) (Engelhard et al., 2007; Foa et al., 1993). The PTSD-SR consists of 17 items assessing PTSD symptoms across three subscales: re-experiencing (5 items), avoidance (7 items), and hyperarousal (5 items). Items are scored on a 4-point Likert scale according to the frequency with which the respondent has experienced symptoms in the past week: ‘never’ (0), ‘once a week’ (1), ‘2–4 times a week’ (2), and ‘5 times a week or more’ (3). The total score ranges from 0 to 51, with a score of 15 or more indicating the presence of PTSD. The PTSD-SR has satisfactory internal consistency, high test–retest reliability, and good concurrent validity (Foa et al., 1993).

We measured children’s trauma symptoms using the Dutch version of the Children’s Revised Impact of Event Scale (CRIES-13; Perrin et al., 2005; Verlinden et al., 2014a, b), a self-report measure designed to screen children for PTSD. Children aged 8 years and older and their parents completed the CRIES-13. The scale consists of 13 items across three subscales: intrusion (4 items), avoidance (4 items), and hyperarousal (5 items). Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale based on the frequency of experience in the past week: ‘not at all’ (0), ‘rarely’ (1), ‘sometimes’ (3), and ‘often’ (5). The total score ranges from 0 to 65, with scores of 30 (child report) or 31 (parent report) and higher indicating an increased risk for PTSD. The CRIES-13 has good reliability and validity (Verlinden et al., 2014a, b).

We measured children’s psychosocial functioning using the Dutch version of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) (Goodman, 2001; Van Widenfelt et al., 2003). Parents completed the parent version for their children aged 4 years and older, and children aged 11 years and older completed the self-report version. The SDQ consists of 25 items evenly divided into five subscales: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, peer relationship problems, and prosocial behavior. Items are scored on a 3-point Likert scale: ‘not true’ (0), ‘somewhat true’ (1), or ‘certainly true’ (2). Items from all scales, except the prosocial behavior scale, can be combined to form the total difficulties scale. The total problem score ranges from 0 to 40, with scores of 11 or higher (ages 4–12) or 9 or higher (ages 13 +) for parent reports and 13 or higher for youth self-reports indicating (sub)clinical problems. The Dutch SDQ has acceptable internal consistency and sufficient concurrent validity (Van Widenfeldt et al., 2003; Theunissen et al., 2016, 2019).

We measured parenting stress using the Dutch Parental Stress Questionnaire [PSQ] (Opvoedingsbelasting Vragenlijst; Vermulst et al., 2015). Parents completed the PSQ for the child with whom they experienced the greatest parenting challenge prior to starting KINGS (i.e., maximum of one child per family). The PSQ consists of 34 items and five subscales: parent–child relationship problems (6 items), problems with parenting (7 items), depressive mood (7 items), role limitations (6 items), and health complaints (8 items). Items are scored on a 4-point Likert scale: ‘not true’ (1), ‘somewhat true’ (2), ‘quite true’ (3), or ‘very true’ (4). Total problem scores range from 0 to 136, with T-scores of 60 and higher indicating (sub)clinical problems. The PSQ has good reliability and validity (Vermulst et al., 2015).

We assessed problems related to the quality of the family and parenting conditions using the Dutch Family Questionnaire [FQ] (Gezinsvragenlijst; Van der Ploeg & Scholte, 2008). Parents completed the FQ again only for the child with whom they experienced the greatest parenting challenge before KINGS. The FQ consists of 45 items evenly divided into five subscales: responsiveness, communication, organization, partner relationship (if applicable), and social network. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale: ‘strongly disagree’ (5), ‘somewhat disagree’ (4), ‘neither agree nor disagree’ (3), ‘somewhat agree’ (2), and ‘strongly agree’ (1). The total problem score ranges from 0 to 225 for parents with a partner relationship and from 0 to 180 for parents without a partner relationship. Scores of 90 (with partner) or 73 (without partner) indicate (sub)clinical problems. The FQ has good internal consistency (Van der Ploeg & Scholte, 2008).

Analyses

We followed a preregistered analysis plan2 to test our hypotheses.

Baseline problems (Objective 1)

To examine the functioning of KINGS families at the start of the intervention, we used descriptive statistics (percentages, means, standard deviations) in SPSS Statistics 28. We assessed the severity of problems using available Dutch norm groups.

Change over time (Objective 2)

To explore whether data from the two sites offering KINGS could be combined, we first performed visual analyses using box plots, followed by independent samples t-tests in SPSS Statistics 28 (two-tailed, α = 0.05). Because we did not find significant differences, we combined data from both sites for subsequent analyses.

To address missing data, we examined potential mechanisms underlying missingness by comparing baseline characteristics (i.e., baseline scores and family demographics) between families who completed follow-up measurements and those who dropped out. Results indicated that families with higher baseline parental PTSD scores, larger household size (i.e., more children), and younger parental age were more likely to drop out, suggesting that the data were Missing at Random (MAR) and that these variables were relevant confounders to consider for inclusion in the models (see below). Families who declined participation (n = 13) did not provide study data, which prevented us from comparing their baseline characteristics with those of participating families.

We analyzed changes over time in child trauma symptoms (parent and child reports), parent trauma symptoms, child psychosocial problems (parent and child reports), parenting stress, and the quality of family and parenting conditions using separate multilevel models. For variables with data from multiple participants per family (i.e., SDQ, CRIES, PTSD-SR), we applied three-level models, with families at Level 3, participants at Level 2, and repeated measures at Level 1. When participant-level variation could not be reliably estimated due to limited within-family variation, we computed a weighted average per family, resulting in one score per family. Consequently, a two-level model was applied, with families at Level 2 and repeated measures at Level 1. For variables with data from only one participant per family (i.e., PSQ, FQ), these two-level models were used by default.

We specified all models according to Snijders and Bosker’s (2012) principles for model specification and estimated them using MLWiN (Charlton et al., 2017). Time was dummy-coded with baseline (T0) as the reference category. Therefore, the intercept represents the predicted outcome score at T0, and the coefficients for T2, T3, and T4 represent the mean differences from baseline at each follow-up point. To evaluate whether these time effects differed significantly from each other, we also compared a model with separate coefficients for T2-T4 to a model in which these coefficients were constrained to be equal. A non-significant deviance difference indicates that these improvements relative to baseline remained stable across time points.

To assess the proportion of variance in the outcome variables attributable to differences between families, we calculated family-level intraclass coefficients (ICCs). To examine whether the extent of change over time varied across families, we added random slopes for the time points and compared models with and without these random slopes using deviance tests (Snijders & Bosker, 2012), with the significance level set at α =.05. When the random slope model significantly improved model fit, we interpreted the covariances between baseline scores (T0) and time effects. Negative covariances suggest that families with higher baseline scores (i.e., more severe symptoms or problems) tend to show less improvement over time, whereas positive covariances suggest that families with higher baseline scores tend to show greater improvement. When random slopes did not significantly improve model fit, we retained the simpler model with fixed time effects as the final model to ensure parsimony. Finally, we initially included household size and parental age as covariates to account for potential confounding effects related to dropout. However, because their inclusion did not notably change the parameter estimates or model fit, we excluded these covariates from the final models to maintain parsimony and interpretability.

We conducted an intention-to-treat analysis on the full sample, followed by a completers analysis that included only families who completed the KINGS program as intended (n = 60). The completers analysis resulted in minimal changes in parameter estimates. Therefore, we chose to present only the most conservative estimates from the ITT analysis. In addition, we reported the percentage of participants who scored above the clinical threshold at each time point.

Parental trauma symptoms

Parental trauma symptoms showed significant reductions at the end of treatment (bpost = − 14.22; 95% CI [− 16.74, − 11.69], p <.001). These reductions remained significant at the 3-month (b3-month follow-up = − 13.98; 95% CI [− 16.27, − 11.69], p <.001) and 6-month (b6-month follow-up = − 12.48; 95% CI [− 15.31, − 9.66], p <.001) follow-ups. Improvements compared to baseline were stable across follow-ups (χ2 (2) = 2.93, p =.231). After accounting for the fixed effects of time, 62.5% of the variance in parental trauma symptoms was attributable to differences between families. Random slopes indicated significant variation in symptom trajectories across families (χ2 (8) = 39.06, p <.001), with families with higher initial symptom levels showing less improvement over time. At the end of KINGS, the percentage of parents at risk for PTSD had decreased substantially, with 22.6% (n = 14) still at risk. This percentage continued to decrease at the 3-month follow-up (12.5%, n = 5) before increasing slightly at the 6-month follow-up (20.0%, n = 7).

Child trauma symptoms

Child reports showed significant reductions in trauma symptoms at the end of treatment (bpost = −17.30; 95% CI [− 21.64, − 12.96], p <.001), which were maintained at the 3-month (b3-month follow-up = −16.56; 95% CI [− 21.13, − 11.99], p <.001) and 6-month (b6-month follow-up = −18.23; 95% CI [− 23.71, − 12.75], p <.001) follow-ups. Parent reports also indicated significant reductions at the end of treatment (bpost = − 17.47; 95% CI [− 21.39, − 13.54], p <.001), with reductions sustained at the 3-month (b3-month follow-up = − 12.81; 95% CI [− 17.24, − 8.39], p <.001) and 6-month follow-ups (b6-month follow-up = − 12.53; 95% CI [− 17.27, − 7.79], p <.001). Improvements compared to baseline were stable across follow-ups for both child reports (χ2 (2) =.324, p =.850) and parent reports (χ2 (2) = 5.553, p =.062).

After accounting for the fixed effects of time, 28.7% of the variance in child trauma symptoms was attributable to between-family differences for child reports and 32.8% for parent reports. However, random slopes did not improve model fit, indicating no significant between-family variation in symptom trajectories. At the end of KINGS, the percentage of children at risk for PTSD had decreased considerably, with 26.2% (n = 11) still at risk based on child reports and 23.3% (n = 14) based on parent reports. According to child reports, this percentage decreased further at follow-up (3-months: 22.2%, n = 8; 6-months: 9.0%, n = 2). In contrast, according to parent reports, the percentage increased during follow-up (3-months: 43.3%, n = 19; 6-months: 37.8%, n = 14).

Child psychosocial problems

Child self-reports indicated no significant improvement at the end of treatment (bpost = −0.26; 95% CI [− 2.84, 2.33], p =.847). However, significant reductions were observed at the 3-month (b3-month follow-up = − 2.09; 95% CI [− 4.00, − 0.178], p =.034) and 6-month (b6-month follow-up = − 3.21; 95% CI [−4.81, − 1.60], p <.001) follow-ups. Improvements compared to baseline were significantly larger at follow-ups than immediately posttreatment (χ2 (2) = 6.933, p =.031). After accounting for the fixed effects of time, 52.8% of the variance in child self-reported psychosocial functioning was attributable to differences between families. Random slopes indicated significant variation in trajectories (χ2 (8) = 19.952, p =.011), with families with higher initial levels of child-reported psychosocial problems showing less improvement over time. At the end of KINGS, 35.0% (n = 7) of the children reported clinical problems, which decreased further to 27.3% (n = 6) at the 3-month follow-up and 21.4% (n = 3) at the 6-month follow-up.

Parent reports showed significant reductions at the end of treatment (bpost = − 6.13; 95% CI [− 7.47, − 4.73], p <.001), which were sustained at the 3-month (b3-month follow-up = − 5.85; 95% CI [− 7.47, − 4.33], p <.001) and 6-month (b6-month follow-up = − 4.78; 95% CI [− 6.37, − 3.23], p <.001) follow-ups. Improvements compared to baseline were stable across follow-ups (χ2 (2) = 2.776, p =.250). Most variance in parent-reported scores occurred within families rather than between families (9.5%). Random slopes did not improve model fit, indicating no significant between-family variation. Compared to baseline, fewer children had parent-reported clinical scores after KINGS. Nevertheless, the proportion with persistent parent-reported clinical problems remained considerable: 47.1% (n = 40) at the end of KINGS, 44.6% (n = 25) at the 3-month follow-up, and 52.1% (n = 25) at the 6-month follow-up.

Parenting stress

Parenting stress showed significant reductions at the end of treatment (bpost = − 20.81; 95% CI [− 24.46, − 17.15], p <.001). Improvements remained significant at the 3-month (b3-month follow-up = − 16.14; 95% CI [− 20.18, − 12.10], p <.001) and 6-month (b6-month follow-up = − 12.40; 95% CI [− 17.57, − 7.23], p <.001) follow-ups. However, improvements compared to baseline decreased across follow-ups (χ2 (2) = 11.053, p =.004). After accounting for the fixed effects of time, 68.2% of the variance in parenting stress was attributable to between-family differences. Random slopes indicated significant variation in stress trajectories across families (χ2 (8) = 21.488, p =.006), with families with higher initial levels of parenting stress showing less improvement over time. At the end of KINGS, 40.4% (n = 21) of parents still reported clinical-range parenting stress, which increased to 56.4% (n = 22) at the 3-month follow-up and 59.4% (n = 19) at the 6-month follow-up.

Quality of the family and parenting conditions

The quality of the family and parenting conditions improved significantly at the end of treatment (bpost = − 22.04; 95% CI [− 26.78, − 17.31], p <.001). Improvements were maintained at the 3-month follow-up (b3-month follow-up = − 14.96; 95% CI [− 20.87, − 9.05], p <.001) but were no longer statistically significant at the 6-month follow-up (b6-month follow-up = − 6.28; 95% CI [− 14.187, 1.635], p =.123). Improvements compared to baseline decreased across follow-ups (χ2 (2) = 18.878, p <.001). After accounting for the fixed effects of time, 66.9% of the variance in the quality of the family and parenting conditions was attributable to differences between families. Random slopes indicated significant between-family variability in how these conditions changed over time (χ2 (8) = 24.088, p =.002), with families with higher initial problems showing less improvement over time. At the end of KINGS, 16.3% (n = 8) of parents reported clinical problems related to family and parenting conditions, a proportion that increased during follow-up (37.8%, n = 14 at 3 months; 35.5%, n = 11 at 6 months), although it remained considerably lower compared to baseline.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to evaluate the longitudinal outcomes of the KINGS program, an innovative trauma-focused family treatment designed to address the intergenerational transmission of trauma in families experiencing complex and multiple problems (FECMP). For these families, (often many) other attempts at evidence-based (trauma) treatment have been unsuccessful (Zeinstra et al., 2024; Mevissen et al., 2020). KINGS provides six to eight weeks of structured, intensive, inpatient treatment for the entire family system, focusing on trauma processing, emotional regulation, parenting, and the parent–child relationship.

Our findings confirm our hypothesis that families entering KINGS report (sub)clinical levels of trauma symptoms, psychosocial problems, parenting stress, and problems in the family and parenting conditions compared to available Dutch norm groups, highlighting their severe clinical burden. The reported traumatic experiences of both parents and children, primarily physical and emotional abuse and neglect, reflect the complex, interpersonal nature of the trauma histories that characterize the families entering the KINGS program. Successful treatment for these families requires overcoming several challenges, including avoidance, behavioral dysregulation, mistrust, and strained parent–child relationships (Struik et al., 2017; Wesselmann & Shapiro, 2013; Wesselmann et al., 2018). In the present study, significant improvements were observed across all outcome measures, indicating that KINGS holds promise as a treatment option for this vulnerable population. Below, we discuss these findings in detail.

First, as hypothesized (Hypotheses 2a and 2b), both child and parental trauma symptoms decreased significantly from the start to the end of KINGS, and these reductions were maintained at follow-up. This is consistent with findings from other intensive trauma-focused interventions for adults and adolescents with complex PTSD, which have demonstrated rapid and substantial reductions in PTSD symptom severity (e.g., Hendriks et al., 2017; Oprel et al., 2021; Van Woudenberg et al., 2018). In addition, the dropout rate in our study (n = 8 families; 11.8%) is comparable to other intensive trauma treatments (Hendriks et al., 2018; Rentinck et al., 2025; Van Woudenberg et al., 2018) and lower than the 15–22% typically observed in weekly trauma therapy (Imel et al., 2013). While direct comparisons are complicated by differences in how dropout is defined (family vs. individual level), these findings support the potential of intensive treatment formats to enhance treatment retention and reduce dropout (Matthijssen et al., 2024; Sciarrino et al., 2020). Although the inpatient format requires families to temporarily leave their homes, routines, and social environments, it may simultaneously remove barriers that often complicate outpatient treatment (e.g., unsafe home environment, transportation, lack of social support; Bodden & Deković, 2016; Mevissen et al., 2020). This combination of respite from daily stressors and a structured therapeutic context may help explain why families were able to remain engaged despite the temporary disruption of home life, making the intensive, inpatient format of KINGS a viable alternative for FECMP.

Second, children’s psychosocial functioning showed more nuanced improvements. Consistent with Hypothesis 2c, parents reported significant reductions in their children’s psychosocial problems immediately at the end of KINGS, which were sustained at follow-up, whereas children themselves did not report significant improvements until follow-up. This delayed effect, which was not hypothesized but is similar to findings from home-based family preservation services (Lippens et al., 2025), may reflect a sleeper effect in which changes become more apparent over time. The discrepancy between parent and child reports may reflect normative differences in how desired and problematic behaviors are perceived (Van Roy et al., 2010). Despite the significant reductions, substantial psychosocial problems persisted in children, consistent with findings on intensive home-based treatments for families with multiple problems (Asscher et al., 2012; Damen et al., 2021; Van Assen et al., 2020, 2023). Persistent child behavior problems may serve as an ongoing stressor for parents, potentially causing parenting stress and increasing the risk of negative parenting behaviors, which in turn may sustain or amplify child behavior problems (Damen et al., 2021).

Third, we observed a significant reduction in parenting stress during KINGS, with stress levels remaining significantly lower at follow-up than at baseline. However, the magnitude of this change decreased over time, and a substantial proportion (approximately half) of parents continued to experience clinical levels of parenting stress at follow-up. Similarly, the quality of parenting and family conditions significantly improved during KINGS, but we observed a decline in effect at follow-up, with changes no longer significant at six months after the intervention. While these outcomes remained better than at baseline, in line with our hypotheses (2d and 2e), the observed decline in effect was not explicitly anticipated.

This decline may reflect the challenges families face when transitioning from an inpatient setting to home, where external stressors may interfere with the application of learned skills and strategies (Preyde et al., 2019; Thunnissen et al., 2000). Case file research has shown that families receiving KINGS face persistent environmental stressors, including financial hardship, work stress, housing instability, and limited social support networks (Zeinstra et al., 2024). These stressors are linked to increased parenting stress (Cassells & Evans, 2017; Damen et al., 2021), which in turn may contribute to negative parenting behaviors and sustained child behavioral problems (Anthony et al., 2005; Damen et al., 2021; Holly et al., 2019). While KINGS focuses primarily on improving family interactions and addressing trauma-related difficulties, it does not directly target structural stressors such as financial problems or housing instability. As a result, families may struggle to maintain gains made during treatment when they return to environments where these stressors remain unaddressed.

These findings underscore the complexity of sustained change in FECMP affected by (intergenerational) trauma and related problems. While KINGS clearly facilitates initial improvements in parental attitudes and behaviors, the consolidation of these changes into long-term patterns requires ongoing reinforcement in the home environment. This underscores the need for structured, well-integrated outpatient follow-up care that supports parents in reinforcing effective parenting strategies and helps them navigate posttreatment challenges. Importantly, KINGS is not designed as a stand-alone intervention, but rather as an intensive treatment phase embedded within a broader continuum of care (Ploeg & Wanders-Mulder, 2019). As such, ensuring a seamless transition to outpatient follow-up care is critical to consolidating treatment gains. Future research should examine what forms of posttreatment support are provided, whether they meet families’ needs, and how they contribute to sustaining treatment gains. A qualitative study on the experiences of families who completed the KINGS program (Stegerman, 2025; Zeinstra et al., 2024) revealed that parents expressed a desire for structured follow-up sessions or booster weeks aimed specifically at reinforcing parenting strategies and managing family interactions. Thus, while more extensive follow-up care may be needed to address external stressors, families may also benefit from continued support in applying the skills acquired during KINGS in their daily lives.

Finally, consistent with Hypothesis 3, we observed considerable variability in families’ trajectories of change, particularly in parental trauma symptoms, child-reported psychosocial functioning, parenting stress, and quality of parenting and family conditions. Families with more severe problems at the start of KINGS generally showed less improvement over time, highlighting the importance of thorough assessment at baseline to identify families who may need additional support or more tailored treatment. This finding is consistent with previous research on outpatient services showing that FEMCP experience heterogeneous patterns of change, reinforcing the need for individualized treatment approaches (Chaffin et al., 2011). Furthermore, variation emerged not only between families but also within families, with indications of sibling differences in symptom trajectories. From an ecological or dynamic systems perspective (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006; Smith & Thelen, 2003), such within-family variation suggests that change unfolds at multiple levels, including individual family members, their interactions with each other, and the broader family environment. Understanding these complex dynamics may provide valuable insights into the mechanisms that drive change in families during and after treatment.

The present study has several strengths. The longitudinal design with multiple measurement occasions allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of both immediate and sustained outcomes of KINGS, incorporating multiple perspectives (parent and child) and examining multiple domains related to child, parent, and family functioning. A key strength is the large clinical sample, with 83% of the families who participated in KINGS during the study period included in the analysis. This is particularly valuable because families facing interrelated problems are often excluded from research due to the complexity and comorbidity of their problems and concerns about overburdening families who are already under considerable stress. Finally, the use of multilevel models provided nuanced insights into within-family and between-family variability, which is particularly relevant for understanding outcomes in heterogeneous populations with multifaceted needs that typically show dynamic patterns of change (Chaffin et al., 2011).

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the absence of a control group limits the ability to establish causality. While regression to the mean cannot be ruled out as a partial explanation for observed improvements, the fact that improvements continued over multiple follow-up assessments suggests that at least some of the observed effects may reflect genuine intervention effects rather than statistical artifacts. While our measures were administered at the parent and child levels, significant improvements were observed across both perspectives, as well as in family and parenting conditions. Taken together, these findings indicate improved functioning within families. Nevertheless, because most measures capture outcomes at the individual level, caution is warranted in generalizing to the family system, and future research should incorporate systemic measures (e.g., observational assessments of family interactions) to more directly capture family-level processes and outcomes. In addition, investigating the cost-effectiveness of KINGS in comparison to other interventions could further inform policy decisions regarding its broader implementation. Such analyses would also help determine whether the additional logistical and financial resources required for residential family treatment are outweighed by the benefits in terms of treatment engagement, symptom reduction, and potential long-term cost savings (e.g., reduced relapse, lower risk of out-of-home placements).

Second, based on missing data analysis, we assumed that data were Missing at Random (MAR), although the possibility of Missing Not at Random (MNAR) cannot be ruled out. Dropout, particularly at follow-up, resulted in smaller sample sizes, a common problem in evaluation studies of services for families with complex and multiple problems (e.g., Boddy et al.., 2011; Van Assen et al., 2023). In addition, because families who declined participation did not provide study data, potential selection effects could not be formally examined. Due to the small sample size, child and family characteristics were not included as covariates in the multilevel models, limiting our ability to examine how these characteristics were related to treatment effects. However, we did find considerable between-family variance in treatment effects. Future research with larger sample sizes and sensitivity analyses could provide more insight into the potential impact of MNAR mechanisms and how family characteristics (e.g., family dynamics, external stressors) and care characteristics (e.g., variations in treatment fidelity) contribute to treatment success.

Third, the majority of families in our sample were single-parent families with mothers as the head, which may limit generalizability beyond female parents and single-parent households. However, in terms of gender, several studies have shown comparable trauma treatment outcomes between males and females (Danzi & La Greca, 2021; De Roos et al., 2021). In addition, similar proportions of parent gender and family composition have been reported in other Dutch studies involving families with multiple and complex problems (Bodden & Dekovic, 2016; Tausendfreund et al., 2014). This reflects broader issues related to gender disparities in child welfare (e.g., Choi & Pyun, 2014; Ewart-Boyle et al., 2015; Zanoni et al., 2014), as well as the challenges of involving fathers, especially when their contact with their children in daily life is limited or inconsistent. Of the 13 families that were approached for our study but declined participation, most were also single-parent families headed by mothers (n = 9), alongside four two-parent families. Although numbers are small, this pattern suggests that the predominance of mothers in our study is consistent with the family composition of both participants and non-participants. Future research could explore how to engage fathers more effectively when possible, addressing both the challenges and potential benefits of their involvement for a more systemic approach to treatment.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that the intensive six- to eight-week inpatient KINGS treatment program, which focuses on trauma processing, regulation, parenting, and the parent–child relationship, is associated with positive outcomes for children and their parents suffering from PTSD and comorbid problems resulting from complex traumatization. This study thereby contributes to the growing body of research on integrated, family-centered treatment approaches, which have gained increasing international recognition for their potential value, yet empirical evaluations of their outcomes remain scarce (Barth et al., 2023; Falkov et al., 2016; Foster et al., 2016). Overall, KINGS appears to offer new perspectives for families experiencing chronic, pervasive, intergenerational problems that are considered difficult to resolve and often result in long-term personal suffering and high costs for mental health and medical care, as well as the risk of out-of-home placement for children (Mevissen et al., 2020). However, to sustain positive outcomes and optimize long-term impact, continued professional support through structured follow-up care may be required. This underscores the importance of integrating KINGS within a broader continuum of care, ensuring that families receive ongoing support to reinforce the skills and strategies learned during treatment and effectively navigate posttreatment challenges.
