Immunogenicity dynamics and covariate effects after satralizumab administration predicted with a hidden Markov model
Authors: Rory Leisegang, Hanna E. Silber Baumann, Siân Lennon‐Chrimes, Hajime Ito, Kazuhiro Miya, Jean‐Christophe Genin, Elodie L. Plan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunogenicity is the propensity of a therapeutic protein to generate an immune response to itself. While reporting of antidrug antibodies (ADAs) is increasing, model‐based analysis of such data is seldom performed. Model‐based characterization of factors affecting the emergence and dissipation of ADAs may inform drug development and/or improve understanding in clinical practice. This analysis aimed to predict ADA dynamics, including the potential influence of individual covariates, following subcutaneous satralizumab administration. Satralizumab is a humanized IgG2 monoclonal recycling IL‐6 receptor antagonist antibody approved for treating neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD). Longitudinal pharmacokinetic (PK) and ADA data from 154 NMOSD patients in two pivotal Phase 3 studies (NCT02028884, NCT02073279) and PK data from one Phase 1 study (SA‐001JP) in 72 healthy volunteers were available for this analysis. An existing population PK model was adapted to derive steady‐state concentration without ADA for each patient. A mixed hidden Markov model (mHMM) was developed whereby three different states were identified: one absorbing Markov state for non‐ADA developer, and two dynamic and inter‐connected Markov states—transient ADA negative and positive. Satralizumab exposure and body mass index impacted transition probabilities and, therefore, the likelihood of developing ADAs. In conclusion, the mHMM model was able to describe the time course of ADA development and identify patterns of ADA development in NMOSD patients following treatment with satralizumab, which may allow for the formulation of strategies to reduce the emergence or limit the impact of ADA in the clinical setting.

INTRODUCTION

Immunogenicity is the propensity of a therapeutic protein to generate an immune response to itself. Developing a deeper understanding of immunogenicity can enable better decisions in the drug development process 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 and impact clinical practice 6 , 7 ; while also ensuring alignment with evolving health authority guidelines around the immunogenicity testing of therapeutic protein products. 8 , 9 The questions raised in health authority interactions concerning antidrug antibodies (ADAs) testing and reporting may include “When, for how long, for whom, how much immunogenicity is developing, and what is the impact?” Addressing such questions is multi‐faceted; therefore, health authority guidelines on reporting remain limited. 7 , 10 For example, identifying and quantifying ADAs may be technically complex and is not standardized 11 , 12 , 13 and this, in turn, can curtail efforts to describe and understand their impact on exposures and clinical outcomes using modeling and simulation approaches. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Furthermore, published ADA incidence rates vary significantly within the broader literature between same‐class products and even patient populations. 20 , 21 The reasons behind these observed differences are poorly understood, 8 , 22 further contributing to the uncertainty. In a recent systematic review, the authors highlight the importance of developing evidence‐based algorithms and guidelines in addition to the standardization assays. 23 Without a broader consensus, the role and value of therapeutic drugs and ADA monitoring may be limited. 24

Broadly speaking, where ADAs bind with a drug and interfere with its pharmacological function, they are classified as neutralizing antibodies (NAb); non‐neutralizing antibodies (non‐NAb) bind with a drug and do not interfere with its pharmacological function. 8 ADAs have been associated with prolonged or shortened half‐lives of biologics; ADAs have also been associated with impacting pharmacodynamic effects through other mechanisms. 22 The binding of a drug with either NAbs or non‐NAbs can, however, result in immune complex formation, comprised of drug‐ADA aggregates, and this may increase drug clearance and therefore reduce efficacy. 2 , 6 , 22 , 25 , 26 ADAs have also been associated with a higher incidence of adverse events, which is thought to result from immunotoxicity in some patients who develop ADAs. 12 , 22 , 27 , 28 , 29 Quantifying the impact on clearance in a routine population pharmacokinetic analysis is feasible and pragmatic 6 , 30 ; where clearance is increased, the associated reduction in drug exposure may be associated with a reduced clinical response. 31 , 32 Similarly, evaluating the association between ADA prevalence and safety and outcome measures can also be accomplished in routine analyses. 20

Identifying target populations and appropriate dosing strategies, together with concomitant medication, requires a deeper understanding of the underlying processes and may contribute positively to risk mitigation strategies for reducing ADA prevalence either through reducing ADA emergence or promoting ADA dissipation. 2 , 7 , 8 , 17 , 26 , 29 , 31 , 33 Commonly observed limitations of publications include that the impact of patient and drug factors on ADA prevalence is seldom explored or presented. 29 , 33 , 34 It is essential to develop and validate ADA assays to ensure the reliability of the generated ADA data. One of the main challenges of ADA assays is their tolerance to the presence of the drug in the sample, which can lead to false‐negative results at certain drug concentrations. 35 Additionally, ADA assays can be prone to interference from soluble targets, resulting in false‐positive outcomes, particularly when the target is in a multimeric form and a homogeneous bridging assay method is used. 36

Model‐informed analysis and characterization of factors affecting treatment‐induced ADAs may enable drug development decisions aiming to understand and potentially even decrease the immunogenicity risk. 37 Hidden Markov models (HMMs) are a class of probabilistic models connecting predictions of observable variables to hidden underlying states, between which unobserved transitions may occur. Their implementation in NONMEM has been described previously, 38 , 39 , 40 including the parameterization of mixed effects Markov models (mHMM), as well as their application to ADAs and using a bivariate input (BV‐MHMM). 14 , 15 , 16 Further developing this analytical framework may assist in establishing consensus on good practices regarding how ADAs are reported and understood, including the ability of covariates to explain differences.

The objective of this analysis was to predict ADA dynamics following subcutaneous administration of satralizumab in patients with neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) based on the findings from the pivotal Phase 3 studies. Satralizumab is a humanized IgG2 monoclonal recycling IL‐6 receptor antagonist antibody approved for the treatment of NMOSD. Quantifying the potential influence of individual covariates, including demographics and background therapy, on ADA prevalence over time, including the presence of immunomodulators, may lead to a greater understanding of their impact in clinical settings. 31

The Phase 3 study, NCT02028884, was a randomized, double‐blind, and placebo‐controlled trial in adult and adolescent patients aged 12–74 years of age with NMOSD who were seropositive or seronegative for aquaporin‐4 antibody (AQP4‐IgG). Participants were randomly assigned, in a 1:1 ratio, to receive either satralizumab, at a dose of 120 mg, or placebo, administered subcutaneously at weeks 0, 2, and 4 and every 4 weeks thereafter, in addition to ongoing immunosuppressant treatments including azathioprine (AZT), mycophenolate mofetil (MM), and oral corticosteroids (OS). 41 The Phase 3 study, NCT02073279, was a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, and parallel‐group trial in adults aged 18–74 years with NMOSD who were seropositive or seronegative for (AQP4‐IgG). Participants were randomly assigned, in a 2:1 ratio, to receive either satralizumab, at a dose of 120 mg, or placebo, administered subcutaneously at weeks 0, 2, 4, and every 4 weeks thereafter as monotherapy. 42 Both studies offered open‐label extension phases in which all patients received active treatment with satralizumab. Participants could enter the open‐label extension following a relapse in the disease or at the end of the double‐blind period. 42 Pharmacokinetic (PK) and ADA data were collected repeatedly throughout the study, mainly at trough, until the patients left the trial or until clinical cut‐off. In the Phase 1 study in healthy volunteers, satralizumab was given as a single dose (ranging from 30 to 240 mg) either through an intravenous (IV) or SC administration route. As rich PK data were collected, the data were included in the current analysis to inform the PK model.

Informed consent and ethics

All study protocols were approved by institutional review boards and/or independent ethics committees at each site. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant or a parent/legal guardian if the participant was <18 years of age.

Assay methods

The satralizumab concentrations in human serum samples were assessed by a sandwich ELISA method using established and validated immunoassay procedures. The lower limit of quantitation was 0.2 μg/mL. A homogeneous bridging Electrochemiluminescence Assay (ECLIA), including an acid dissociation procedure, was used to semi‐quantify the presence of antibodies against satralizumab in human serum samples on the MSD (MesoScale Discovery) platform. A three‐step approach was implemented where screen‐positive samples (signal above negative control multiplied by 1.14) were tested in the confirmatory assay (% of inhibition >18.1% to confirm the presence of ADA). Finally, the titer level was determined for all samples confirmed positive. The assay sensitivity (defined using a surrogate‐positive control) was 1.50 ng/mL. The assay was optimized for both drug and target tolerance. At a reference antibody concentration of 100 ng/mL, and up to 1200 ng/mL of sIL6R, the assay could tolerate >70 μg/mL of satralizumab, which significantly limits the risk of false‐negative readouts. Due to the fact that the soluble IL‐6 receptor is a dimer, the presence of a soluble target can impact the readout by generating false‐positive results. In the presence of satralizumab ≥10 μg/mL, the assay could tolerate more than 1200 ng/mL of sIL6R. In the presence of satralizumab <10 μg/mL interpolated sIL6R tolerance levels were determined using the intercept method, ranging from 189 ng/mL at 0.4 μg/mL satralizumab to over 1200 ng/mL at 10 μg/mL satralizumab. Overall, it was concluded that the risk of false‐positive readouts was low with the range of observed satralizumab and sIL6R concentrations. The cut‐points for the determination (threshold between positive/negative) were predefined for each study population.

Population pharmacokinetic model

A population pharmacokinetics (PopPK) model was previously developed, which described the satralizumab pharmacokinetic with a two‐compartment model, with parallel linear and saturable (Michaelis–Menten) elimination and a first‐order absorption following SC administration. 26 Several covariates were identified as impacting the pharmacokinetics of satralizumab. The most important covariates were bodyweight on clearance and volume according to allometric scaling (power of 0.75 and 1 for clearance and volume parameters, respectively). In addition, ADA status entered the model in two ways: ADA status was included on bioavailability as a fixed effect (bioavailability was reduced by 13% for patients developing ADAs) and on linear clearance at the time points where ADA status was positive (linear clearance was increased by 45% in the presence of ADAs).

For the current analysis, the PopPK model was adapted to characterize only PK exposures in the absence of ADAs. Concentration‐time data points where it could be suspected that ADAs may have had an impact were excluded a priori as follows: in participants who presented with at least one nonzero ADA titer, the concentration‐time data points leading up to the first nonzero titer were retained; in participants who presented with no positive ADA titers, all concentration‐time data points were retained. The remaining individual exposure estimates in the absence of ADA were predicted based on a PopPK model, where the parameters were re‐estimated with the reduced dataset. The rationale for determining PK exposures in the absence of ADA was to avoid a bi‐directional causal relationship that would be problematic in this setting of a direct effect, that is, the concentration‐time profile of satralizumab in the presence of ADAs may be affected when ADAs are present, and, if the new exposure were evaluated as the predictor for the same presence of ADAs.

Population ADA dynamics model

A mixed HMM (mHMM) was developed, including two linked components: first, a submodel related to observable events (e.g., ADA titer), and second, a sub‐model related to an unobservable (or hidden) sequence of states (e.g., ADA positive or negative). In essence, the time course of an individual's observations would be expected to reflect the individual's journey through the underlying Markov states. These profiles naturally represent the information necessary to inform the transition probabilities that would support the most likely sequence of Markov states, called a Markov chain. The Markov states composing the Markov model can be hypothesized and formally evaluated in the development of the model and could include absorbing states (i.e., a state where an individual remains once it has been entered) in addition to dynamic states (i.e., a state where an individual can move in and out of). 27 As in non‐HMMs, the key parameters estimated are the transition probabilities between states, which are interpretable as the likelihood of transitioning from one state to another for a given Markov cycle.

Regarding the hidden layer (the Markov states, the initial distribution within, and subsequent transitions between these Markov states), the emissive probabilities representing the initial distribution within the Markov states at time zero were modeled using logistic functions to constrain the values to between 0 and 1. The transition probabilities were also modeled using logistic functions to constrain the values between 0 and 1. The sum of the Markov emissive and transmission probabilities was further constrained to add up to 1. The impact of including absorbing Markov states (e.g., non‐ADA developer, ADA always positive) was evaluated on the base model. Random effects were implemented in the mHMM where supported by the data. Finally, covariate relationships were evaluated as structural covariates or through a stepwise covariate analysis on all initial distribution and transition probabilities between dynamic and absorbing Markov states. The covariates considered included demographic and laboratory findings and immunosuppressive therapy (ISTs).

Regarding the observed layer, the ADA titers were described with either (a) a Poisson distribution to ensure a sufficiently low lambda (0.001) for the ADA‐negative states and (b) a truncated Poisson distribution for the ADA‐positive states. Negative binomial and generalized Poisson distributions, which include additional model parameters, were formally evaluated in the model development for ADA‐negative states but the additional parameterization was not supported by the available data.

Software

All data manipulation, tables, and figures were prepared in SAS (version 9.4) or R (Microsoft Open R version 3.4.1). The population PK and ADA analyses were conducted with nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling with NONMEM (Version 7.4.3). The first‐order conditional estimation method with the INTERACTION option (FOCEI) was used for the pharmacokinetic models, and the importance sample estimation method (IMP) was used for the ADA models. The Viterbi subroutine in NONMEM was used in a post hoc step to generate individual chains of Markov states. Model‐based simulations were performed in NONMEM. Graphical outputs and all other statistical analyses, including evaluation of NONMEM outputs, were performed using R (Microsoft Open R version 3.4.1) and Perl‐Speaks‐NONMEM (version 5.3.0).

Model evaluation/assessment

Model evaluation was performed graphically alongside numerical considerations (i.e., objective function value and precision of parameter estimates). Simulation‐based model assessments were undertaken to investigate the model's performance against the hidden (e.g., Markov states) and non‐hidden (e.g., ADA titers and other covariates) elements. 43 Simulations were generated as 5000 copies of profiles of interest to illustrate the impact of covariates on ADA incidence.

RESULTS

Study data

Data from 154 patients and 72 healthy volunteers were available for analysis. In total, 5126 drug concentration samples were collected from 226 individuals and ranged from a single dose with rich sampling in healthy volunteers over 10 weeks in the Phase 1 study to multiple doses with sparse sampling in patients with NMOSD over a median (interquartile range) [range] of 1.83 (1.43, 3.15) [0.16, 3.95] and 2.96 (0.94, 3.54) [0.15, 4.61] years in studies NCT02028884 and NCT02073279, respectively. A more detailed description of the data is provided in Table 1.

The PK dataset was based on longitudinal satralizumab concentration data from all 226 individuals from the Phase 1 and Phase 3 studies. PK data where the satralizumab concentration was below the limit of quantification (n = 402), where collection time was missing (n = 9), and where samples were collected on and subsequent to a nonzero ADA titer assessment (n = 2872) were excluded. Following exclusions, 1843 concentration‐time data points from 213 participants were retained for the analysis, where the PopPK model parameters were re‐estimated, and the individual predicted exposures derived.

The ADA dataset was based on longitudinal ADA titer data from the Phase 3 studies only. At least one positive ADA sample was detected in 58/80 (72.5%) and 34/65 (52.3%) of the individuals in NCT02028884 and NCT02073279, respectively, in either the double‐blind or open‐label part of the study. In total, 3805 data points from 154 participants were included in the dataset that was used for the ADA model development. Selected demographic variables (body weight, body mass index [BMI], age, and sex), C‐reactive protein (CRP), as well as patient‐level information about concomitant medications, were included in the dataset and evaluated as covariates in the ADA model.

Final pharmacokinetic model

The PopPK model had been previously developed using all available satralizumab PK data. 26 The model is described in the methods section. For this analysis, the model was used to generate individual exposure metrics reflecting the exposure to satralizumab in the absence of ADAs. Initially, the parameters were re‐estimated based on a subset of the PK data in which any concentration measure which could be impacted by ADA was removed. The parameter estimates of the original and re‐estimated model are presented in Table S1, where no residual standard error (RSE) exceeds 33%. For the final analysis, the individual PK parameter estimates (Empirical Bayes Estimates [EBEs]) were used to derive the individual's average steady‐state concentration (C av,ss). C av,ss was used as a covariate in the ADA model.

Final ADA model

A graphical description of the final mHMM ADA model, including the hidden Markov states, their emission and transition probabilities, associated covariate relationships for the probabilities, and ADA titers associated with the hidden Markov states, are shown in Figure 1. A total of three states were identified: one absorbing Markov state for non‐ADA developer and two dynamic Markov states for transient ADA negative and transient ADA positive. Patients were assumed to be ADA negative at baseline (before the start of the study), and could either fall into the non‐ADA or transient ADA‐negative state. Two patients in the study were ADA positive at baseline but with low titers, and therefore, this assumption was considered acceptable. The parameter estimates are shown in Table 2. Parameter values were estimated with high precision (low RSEs with the exception of RSEs of 59%, 40%, and 43% for the time and the BMI effects), covariate relationships were estimated on several emissive and transition probabilities, and mixed elements were incorporated on the ADA titer.

The typical titer associated with the transient ADA‐positive Markov state was 2.97 (log(2) scale). An estimated 34.8% were identified as being non‐ADA developers and 65.2% as being ADA developers (transient ADA negative and transient ADA positive). While the transition probabilities governing the flux from transient ADA negative to transient ADA‐positive (21.6%) and back (15.5%) typically favored the transient ADA‐positive Markov state initially, the likelihood of ADA emergence declined exponentially over time (half‐life of ~270 days), thus favoring the transient ADA‐negative Markov state over time. A half‐life on the transition probability from transient ADA positive to transient ADA negative, while clinically plausible, was not identifiable. Finally, auto‐correlation between observations was accounted for with an exponential function on the transition probability (half‐life of ~10 days).

A feedback loop was identified with the ADA‐positive Markov state, whereby ADA titers are negatively correlated with the transition probability to the transiently ADA‐negative Markov state, meaning that a high ADA titer reduces the likelihood of transitioning back to ADA negative. The covariate analysis identified BMI and drug exposure as significant covariates on several transition probabilities. Increasing time since starting treatment, lower BMI, and higher exposure all corresponded with a lower ADA prevalence over time. DISCUSSION

ADA status following satralizumab treatment is dynamic, with ADA prevalence peaking at around 3 months and then waning over time for all patient groups. We found that the dynamic time course of ADAs and factors affecting this dynamic time course are seldom reported and yet may be clinically relevant. 37 A key novel finding was that opposing and sometimes even time‐varying covariate effects markedly impacted ADA prevalence. Efforts to understand the dynamics further are warranted and should include the factors related to drug, study, and patient characteristics. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 27 , 44 , 45 HMMs offer a deeper understanding of the hidden or latent dynamics and, through simulations, the potential to separate out the impact of specific aspects. Furthermore, incorporating mixed and nonlinear elements of HMM in NONMEM (mHMM) increases power, model stability, parameter precision, and biological plausibility. 16

The analytical framework (mHMM) presented in this analysis, together with the inclusion of covariate effects, builds on previous work 14 , 15 , 16 , 18 , 45 and offers significant advantages over traditional statistical approaches, including those required by regulatory agencies. 44 , 46 Several novel insights emerged in this analysis using a mHMM framework: (a) persistent Markov states should be evaluated in addition to transient Markov states and (b) time on treatment, BMI, and exposures should be all considered in a covariate analysis. For satralizumab, higher BMI and lower satralizumab exposures were key risk factors for the emergence and persistence of ADAs, respectively. Additionally, ADA prevalence peaked at 3 months and then fell steadily, faster in participants with higher exposures and/or lower BMIs. Simulations, besides confirming the model's performance across both studies evaluated, supported our understanding of the prevalence and clinical relevance of the findings, demonstrating the transient appearance of ADAs or time‐varying ADA status for a large patient population over varying BMI (IQR 18.5–31.5 kg/m2) and satralizumab exposure (IQR C ave,ss 30–80 μg/mL).

In terms of covariate relationships, studies remain limited. 47 Shifts in immune cell composition and higher levels of activation have been associated with a higher BMI in various clinical settings. 48 , 49 , 50 Our first finding that BMI may determine the distribution between transient and persistent ADA‐negative Markov states is novel and clinically plausible and adds another dimension to previous findings which supported the influence of exposure instead. 45 Our second finding that higher satralizumab exposures were associated with lower long‐term ADA prevalence is encouraging, however, is not necessarily generalizable to other therapies or settings. Given the current clinical practice of “treating through,” efforts to understand the time‐varying nature of ADAs and the impact of covariates for other compounds in line with what has been done in this analysis would be recommended to understand whether any clinically relevant associated decrease in efficacy or increase in safety concerns would persist and to what extent. 45 The impact of several ISTs on ADA prevalence has been previously described, with low‐dose methotrexate being shown to promote immune tolerance across several studies and for different compounds. 29 Our finding that the evaluated ISTs had no impact on ADA prevalence is unexpected, but could result from confounding factors due to the unbalanced distribution of covariates (e.g., more low‐weight patients were treated with concomitant IST). Additional patient‐specific factors, such as smoking, which was not evaluated as a covariate in this study, have also been reported to have an impact on ADA development. 44

There are several limitations of the presented analysis. The structural Markov model proposed did not allow for increasing ADA titers over time within the transient ADA‐positive Markov state, despite increasing ADA titers initially followed by more consistent ADA titers over time being a standard feature within an individual's profile. Instead, the individuals' averaged ADA titer was described and may be biased for individuals with early observations only. The current model predicts that all ADAs will dissipate with time. Therefore, extrapolation beyond the scope of the existing data may not be relevant. A fourth Markov state, ADA persisting, was investigated but not supported statistically despite the presence of some individuals who developed ADAs early and then remained ADA positive. The pharmacokinetic metric used to explore the impact of satralizumab exposure on the prevalence of ADAs was the average steady‐state concentration predicted in the absence of ADAs. The reason for using an average steady‐state concentration (C ave,ss) was to use a time constant measure and thereby obtain an invariant predictor for the dynamics observed. The use of time delays to resolve this limitation may be explored in the future, though may represent computational challenges.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that an mHMM could describe the dynamic nature of ADAs in patients with NMOSD receiving satralizumab, including quantifying the covariate effects on incidence and, therefore, prevalence within the cohort over time. Developing a deeper understanding of ADA dynamics is important, especially where a potentially clinically relevant associated decrease in efficacy and/or increase in safety signal is considered. In our case study, we could demonstrate that individuals with higher BMIs are overall more likely to develop ADAs and for these ADAs to persist. We could also show that individuals with higher drug exposures to satralizumab had reduced ADA prevalence over time despite a tendency for more individuals to develop ADAs with higher titers initially. Simulations are important tools for understanding the time‐varying nature of ADAs and covariate effects, including potential persistence of ADAs and competing effects, respectively. A better understanding of patterns and causes of ADA development may allow the formulation of strategies to reduce the emergence or limit the impact of ADA in the clinical setting.
