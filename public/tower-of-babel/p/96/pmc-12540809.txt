Demystifying the impacts of anthropogenic activities on physicochemical characteristics of soil in four wetlands of Kashmir Valley, India
Authors: Umar A. Mir, Haleema Bano, Mohmmad Idrees Attar, M. Ashraf Bhat, Zubair Ahmad Khan, Majed Alsubih
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Wetlands in Kashmir are increasingly threatened by anthropogenic pressures such as urban expansion, waste disposal, grazing, and tourism. This study assessed the impact of such disturbances on the soil physico-chemical and microbial properties of four major wetlands—Hokersar, Anchar, Manasbal and Shallabugh—between 2019 and 2021. Two-way ANOVA indicated significant variation in parameters across disturbed and undisturbed sites. The relatively undisturbed Shallabugh wetland exhibited superior soil quality, with higher moisture content, organic carbon, available nitrogen, calcium, magnesium and microbial indicators such as bacterial population and VAM spores. In contrast, disturbed wetlands showed increased bulk density, reduced phosphorus availability, and elevated levels of heavy metals (Fe, Cu, Zn, Ni, Cd, Mn). Principal component analysis and correlation matrices further distinguished disturbed from undisturbed sites. These results highlight the degradation of soil health in disturbed wetlands and emphasize the ecological value of undisturbed sites. Effective wetland governance and conservation strategies are crucial for maintaining soil fertility, microbial diversity, and overall ecosystem functioning in the region.

Introduction

Kashmir, located in the western Himalayas between 32.15°–37.05°N and 72.35°–83.20°E, is renowned for its alpine peaks, crystal-clear lakes, lush wetlands, and diverse ecological zones. Situated at an elevation of approximately 1585 m (5200 feet) above sea level, the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir lies in the northernmost region of India1,2. Several human-caused factors have led to the decline in environmental quality. They include transportation, urbanization, excessive grazing, tourism industry, industrialization, population pressure, widespread use of pesticides and fertilizers in horticulture and agriculture fields during the growing season, dumping of solid wastes, etc. These pressures have significantly degraded soil and water quality, affecting the ecological stability and sustainability of the region3–5. Wetlands are areas of marsh with standing water. They are the primary and most important sources of the stability of the local environment. They serve as the foundation of ecosystems and help to reduce pollution. These are areas where the ground surface is not as high as the water table. These are the regions of land that receive a considerable quantity of annual precipitation from either groundwater or surface water. Lakes, rivers and coastal plains are all very close by6. For regulatory purposes, they may also be located in distant sections of the terrain, under the Clean Water Act. They keep the soil moist, which promotes plant growth. As wetlands serve as a transition between dry ground and water, they are also known as ecotones. Some of the most significant wetlands worldwide are the Camargue in France, the Wasur National Park in Indonesia, the Pantanal in Brazil, Bolivia in Paraguay and the Kakadu wetlands in Australia. India is known to hold 58.2 million hectares of wetland resources, which constitute approximately 4.7% of the country’s geographical area7.

The Kashmir Valley is home to a network of wetlands covering more than 7000 hectares. The wetlands of the Kashmir Valley, including Hokersar, Mirgund, Shallabugh, Hygam, and Narkara, hold immense ecological and scientific significance as vital biodiversity hotspots8,9. These wetlands serve as critical wintering habitats for migratory waterfowl arriving from Siberia and Central Asia, providing essential feeding and resting grounds. Their ecological services extend to supporting fisheries, agriculture, groundwater recharge, nutrient cycling and carbon sequestration10,11. However, the ecological integrity of these wetlands has deteriorated over recent decades. Increasing human encroachment, land conversion, eutrophication, siltation, and climate variability have caused substantial alterations to their hydrology and soil quality12,13. This degradation is not just a biodiversity concern but also a socio-economic one, as these systems support livelihoods and cultural practices. The hydrological and ecological disruptions have led to a marked decline in their resilience and function as environmental buffers. Wetlands are vital for controlling the microclimate of every place, making them ecological barometers of the region’s health. The level and water quality of the groundwater are influenced both directly and indirectly by the quality of the surface water body. Wetlands are inland water bodies that are very important to society because they provide water for many different human uses as well as for wildlife to drink14.

Kashmir valley primarily comprises loamy and clay loam textured soils15,16. From microscopic organisms to massive trees, soil is a dynamic and vital natural component of the lithosphere17,18. Both macro and micronutrients, which are crucial elements of the biosphere and support the equilibrium of habitat and biodiversity, are found in soil19. The anthropogenic activities show adverse impact on the physicochemical properties of plants, water, and sediment14,20,21. Various anthropogenic activities affect soil quality, just like the stone crushing process leads to the emission of huge dust, which depletes soil quality22,23.

The present study was conducted at four selected wetland systems—Hokersar Wetland and Anchar Lake (Srinagar district), Shallabugh Wetland and Manasbal Lake (Ganderbal district)—to evaluate the effects of anthropogenic pressures on soil physicochemical properties. The choice of these wetlands was guided by stratified ecological criteria, including geographic distribution, hydrological variation and differing intensities of human disturbance. Hokersar and Anchar represent urban/peri-urban systems with heavy anthropogenic load, while Shallabugh and Manasbal reflect more semi-natural or rural settings, enabling meaningful ecological contrasts.

Although the number of wetlands (four) and sampling sites per wetland (three) may appear limited for large-scale generalizations, this design was developed in accordance with accepted environmental monitoring standards. Site selection focused on ecological representativeness rather than statistical exhaustiveness—an approach consistent with wetland condition assessment frameworks by agencies24,25, and supported by scientific sampling principles26. This allows for the detection of meaningful spatial variability and offers an effective baseline for future longitudinal and large-scale studies. The sampling regime captures major environmental gradients (urbanization, hydrology, vegetation type), thereby making the findings relevant beyond site-specific contexts.

This study, therefore offers a baseline dataset for evaluating soil degradation trends under anthropogenic stress in the Kashmir Valley. It aims to fill knowledge gaps regarding soil quality dynamics in Himalayan wetland systems and contributes toward the sustainable management of fragile high-altitude ecosystems. The results are not only locally relevant but also have broader applicability to wetland systems facing similar developmental and environmental challenges across the globe.

Materials and methods

Description of study area

This study was conducted in four distinct wetland ecosystems of the Kashmir Valley, selected based on their ecological relevance and varying levels of anthropogenic disturbance. The wetlands include Hokersar and Anchar Lake (Srinagar district) and Shallabugh wetland and Manasbal Lake (Ganderbal district). These sites represent a gradient from urbanized to semi-natural systems, facilitating comparative analysis of human impact on soil physicochemical properties.

Hokersar wetland

Hokersar is a wetland located in Zainakote, in the Srinagar district of Jammu and Kashmir, India. It is positioned in the Kashmir Valley, around 10 km (6.2 miles) to the northwest of Srinagar at coordinates 34.1333° N and 74.7058° E. Spanning across 1375 ha (13.75 km2), this wetland area is recognized as a bird sanctuary and is the largest of its kind in the Kashmir Valley. The site serves as a crucial resting and feeding ground for various migratory bird species, such as cranes, geese, ducks, and shorebirds, that migrate from northern regions of Siberia and Central Asia9,27. Unfortunately, Hokersar is under threat due to human interference and encroachments, leading to a significant reduction in its size. The wetland, which once covered 18.75 km2 in 1969, had shrunk to 13 km2 by 200813,28.

Shallabugh wetland

Shallabugh wetland, situated 16 km northwest of Srinagar in Jammu and Kashmir lies at 34.1814° N and 74.7315° E in the Ganderbal district, is a marshland covering an area of 150 hectares. It serves as an important habitat for numerous migratory bird species, including Bar-Headed Geese, White-Heeled Ducks, Shovellers, Red-Crested Pochards, White-Eyed Pochards, Common Teals, Pintails, Mallards, Gadwalls, Coots, Hooks, and Graylag. Among these species, the most commonly spotted birds in large numbers are Pintails, Common Teals, and Gadwalls20. In addition to its ecological significance, Shallabugh wetland plays a crucial role in the livelihoods of the local population. Many residents depend on fishing and reed harvesting as their primary sources of income. These industries offer economic stability to the communities living around the wetland while maintaining a balance between conservation and sustainable resource use11,12. Despite this, the site faces increasing degradation from grazing, siltation, and encroachment.

Manasbal lake

It is located in the Safapora area of Ganderbal District in Jammu and Kashmir. Manasbal Lake lies at 34.3360° N and 74.6546° E and is a pristine freshwater lake known for its ecological significance and natural beauty. The name “Manasbal” is believed to be derived from the sacred Manasarovar Lake in Tibet. Often referred to as the “supreme gem of all Kashmir Lakes,” Manasbal is a haven for birdwatchers, serving as one of the largest natural habitats for aquatic birds in the region5. The lake is also an important economic resource for local communities. The rootstocks of the lotus plant, which grow abundantly in its waters, are harvested, marketed, and consumed by the local population29. This lake not only supports biodiversity but also plays a vital role in sustaining the livelihoods of those who depend on its natural resources.

Anchar lake

It is located in the Srinagar district of Jammu and Kashmir and lies in proximity to the Ganderbal District, geographically located at 34.1392° N, 74.8131° E. It is connected to the famous Dal Lake through a channel known as “Amir Khan Nallah,” which flows via Gilsar and Khushal Sar. Despite its historical and ecological significance, Anchar Lake is in a highly deteriorated condition due to pollution, encroachments and reduced water quality5,23. It serves as a flood basin, receiving excess water from Dal Lake during heavy rains or flooding30. Once a thriving water body, the lake now requires urgent conservation efforts to restore its ecological balance and prevent further degradation.

Sample collection

Soil specimens were meticulously collected from four distinct sampling locations, namely Hokersar wetland (Srinagar), Manasbal Lake (Ganderbal), Anchar Lake (Srinagar), and Shallabugh wetland (Ganderbal). The initial three locales represent significantly disturbed wetlands, while Shallabugh stands as an undisturbed sanctuary, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The sampling encompassed the soil layer of 0–30 cm across all sites, following the established methodology31. After the removal of leaf litter and debris, the samples underwent a meticulous process of drying in the shade, pulverization, sieving through a 0.2 mm mesh, and, ultimately, analysis to evaluate various parameters, adhering to the standard protocol. Soil moisture content (%) was determined gravimetrically by oven-drying samples at 105 °C until a constant weight was achieved32. Soil pH was measured in a 1:2.5 soil–water suspension using a calibrated pH meter, while electrical conductivity (EC, dS m⁻1) was assessed in the same suspension with an EC meter33. Bulk density (g cm⁻3) was determined by the core method, where the dry mass of undisturbed soil cores was divided by their volume34. Soil organic carbon (%) was estimated through wet oxidation using potassium dichromate and sulfuric acid, with titration against ferrous ammonium sulfate35. Available nitrogen (mg kg⁻1) was quantified using the alkaline KMnO₄ oxidation method followed by distillation and titration36, whereas available phosphorus (mg kg⁻1) was estimated using Olsen’s method, involving extraction with 0.5 M NaHCO₃ and subsequent colorimetric analysis37. Available potassium (mg kg⁻1) was analyzed through flame photometry after extraction with 1N ammonium acetate at neutral pH33. Exchangeable calcium and magnesium were determined by extraction with 1N ammonium acetate followed by titration with EDTA38. Soil texture (sand, silt, and clay fractions) was assessed by the International Pipette method based on particle size separation through sedimentation39. Micronutrients (Zn, Fe, Cu, Mn) and heavy metals (Ni, Cd) were determined by DTPA extraction and analyzed using atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS)40.Fig. 1Location map of the selected wetlands viz., Manasbal, Shallabugh, Anchar, Hokersar. Microbial analysis of soil

Total bacterial count (CFU g−1 of soil)

The total bacterial population in soil samples was determined using the serial dilution and pour plate method41,42. One gram of fresh soil sample was suspended in 9 ml of sterile distilled water and serially diluted up to a 10⁻⁶ dilution. From each dilution, 1 ml was pipetted and poured onto sterile Petri dishes containing nutrient agar medium. The plates were incubated at 28 ± 2 °C for 48 h, after which the colonies were counted and expressed as colony-forming units (CFU) per gram of dry soil.

Vesicular arbuscular mycorrhiza (VAM) spore count

VAM spores were extracted and quantified using the wet sieving and decanting technique followed by the sucrose centrifugation method43,44. A 100 g soil sample was suspended in water and decanted through a series of sieves ranging from 425 to 38 µm. The filtrate was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 3–5 min using a 60% sucrose solution to separate the spores, which were then washed with sterile water to remove any remaining sucrose. The extracted spores were counted under a stereomicroscope and expressed as spores per gram of soil.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate spatial and ecological variability across wetland systems, a Two-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed at a 5% significance level. This statistical method was employed to assess both the main effects and interaction effects of two independent factors: Factor A, representing the wetland sites (Hokersar, Anchar, Manasbal, Shallabugh), and Factor B, representing the sub-sites or replications within each wetland (S1, S2, S3). This factorial design facilitated the examination of (i) the main effect of site (Factor A) on soil physicochemical properties, (ii) the main effect of sub-site variability (Factor B), and (iii) the interaction effect (A × B), which reveals whether the influence of wetland site varies across local replicates. Such a design is crucial for capturing both macro-scale (inter-wetland) and micro-scale (intra-wetland) differences, especially in ecologically sensitive landscapes where spatial heterogeneity and anthropogenic disturbances co-exist.

The ANOVA was performed following the methodology described by Gomez and Gomez45. F-values and corresponding p-values were also calculated. These values are essential to statistically interpret the significance of observed differences and interaction effects among the studied variables.

Subsequently, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed for multivariate data reduction and pattern recognition among soil variables. PCA biplots, constructed through eigen-vector analysis of the correlation matrix as outlined by Karamizadeh et al.46, enabled visualization of clustering among sites and variables. This technique also facilitated PCA-based change detection using categorical biplots, highlighting shifts in soil properties due to anthropogenic influence.

All soil parameters are expressed as mean ± standard error (SE) to reflect both central tendency and precision of replicates. SE was chosen over standard deviation to better represent variability of the site means and ensure more robust inter-site comparisons—particularly relevant in ecologically diverse wetland systems.

This comprehensive analytical framework, incorporating both Two-Way ANOVA and PCA, ensures statistically sound interpretation of wetland soil variability, thereby meeting the reviewer’s expectation for clarity, interaction analysis, and transparent significance reporting.

Results and discussion

Hydric soil in wetlands facilitates the establishment and regrowth of plants that are acclimated to low oxygen and waterlogged environments. The exchange of nutrients between water and sediment is highly dependent on pH, which exhibited considerable variation in this study. Higher (6.92 ± 0.03) pH and electrical conductivity (0.31 ± 0.01) were seen at altered wetlands in contrast to unaltered wetland, Shallabug, with pH 6.29 ± 0.76 and EC 0.19 ± 0.01 (Tables 1 and 2: F = 7.32, p < 0.05). An increase in pH levels within altered wetlands is a key factor disrupting the natural habitat of flora and fauna. Elevated pH can also accelerate the breakdown of soil organic residues, leading to the release of organic acids into the soil solution. In degraded wetland areas, higher pH and electrical conductivity are often linked to reduced vegetation cover. The rise in pH in highly disturbed regions is primarily due to a decline in organic matter accumulation, which is directly influenced by vegetation density. Furthermore, a decrease in organic matter accumulation and its slower decomposition reduces the release of acids, contributing to increased pH levels47. Higher EC in marshy wetland soils may be attributed to deposition of fine sediments, nutrients, and salts by runoff from adjacent elevated lands. It is also reported that slightly higher EC in soils of low lowlands of the Shikohpur watershed in Haryana, as compared to the upper lands48. Seifu et al.49, also reported the highest EC value in the lower cultivation area as compared to other upper vegetation areas, which is in support of our findings. The highest pH and electrical conductivity observed near tourist spots and grazed areas can be attributed to the decline in wetland vegetation50–53. The soil bulk density varied from 0.19 to 1.48 g/cm3 in surface and sub-surface layers, under different vegetation areas. Agriculture soil showed the highest bulk density values, followed by plantation and fallow area. The increase of bulk density in the cultivated land may be attributed to soil compaction due to excessive tillage and agriculture practices such as weed control and crop harvesting. The similar results were found, claiming that intensive farming techniques are the main cause of increased bulk density in soils in highland agriculture zones54,55. The higher soil bulk density in agricultural lands as a result of cultivation, management, and tillage operations in their investigations on degradation and tillage operations was found56–58. Increased soil bulk density indicates a loss of soil binder materials, a drop in soil biological activities, particularly earthworms and plant roots, and a considerable reduction in clay and silt owing to land use change, rather than an increase in the quantity of sand in the soil texture59. Moreover, the bulk density of the soils of marshy land was observed to be lowest because it was rich in organic matter and water saturated. Organic soils have a low bulk density due to their high porosity60. Hence, the lowest bulk density was shown by Shallabugh (0.52 ± 0.02) and the highest was shown by Hokersar (0.66 ± 0.01) (Tables 1 and 2) (F = 5.66, p < 0.05).

The vitality of soil structure, water retention, soil microorganisms, and nutrient recycling hinges significantly on soil organic carbon, a key indicator of soil quality. Examining various locations, the natural carbon concentrations exhibited notable differences between contaminated and undisturbed areas. The undisturbed area displayed higher organic carbon content and moisture content respectively (4.11 ± 0.31 & 47.60 ± 1.21) compared to polluted areas (2.04 ± 0.29 & 32.28 ± 0.94) (Tables 1 and 2: F = 12.45 & 8.15, p < 0.01 & < 0.01). This discrepancy is likely attributed to denser vegetation near undisturbed zones, leading to increased litter accumulation. The variable accumulation and decay of litter, along with the flow of its remnants into the soil habitat, may contribute to lower natural carbon levels in polluted areas and higher levels in undisturbed zones51,61,62.

The investigation into the soil’s natural carbon revealed a significant decline in both studied contaminated areas, potentially linked to substantial metal accumulation and reduced litter falling due to diminished vegetation. The high soil organic carbon content in uncultivated wetland areas was reported63, supporting our results by highlighting reduced decomposition rates due to limited soil residue contact and lower aeration. These findings align with Osinuga and Oyegoke and Msofe et al.55,64, who noted low organic carbon in cultivated wetland areas, attributed to biomass removal during cultivation and insufficient carbon inputs, coupled with faster organic matter decomposition and mineralization rates. The lower organic carbon content in disturbed areas may result from reduced litter accumulation and decomposition51,62.

The primary mineral element extensively utilized by plants is nitrogen, which is absorbed by plant root systems from the soil. Approximately 2 × 1013 kg of nitrogen is taken in by plants globally each year. This is 100 times faster than the rate of biological nitrogen fixation65. The predominant nitrogen-containing anion in soil is the nitrate particle, known for its quick dissolution and free permeation into the soil with water. Analysis of available nitrogen content (255.29 ± 5.86) indicates a higher concentration in undisturbed regions compared to polluted areas (117.70 ± 4.41) (Tables 1 and 3: F = 14.18, p < 0.01). The elevated availability of nitrogen in undisturbed areas is likely attributed to greater plant cover and a more extensive spread of leaf foliage. Factors such as overgrazing, human intervention, and tourism, leading to the depletion of natural resources, are potential contributors to the reduced accessible nitrogen observed in polluted areas. Previous studies have reported a substantial reduction in available nitrogen in degraded wetlands66,67. In contrast, it was found that medium to high concentrations of available nitrogen in undisturbed soils in the Kashmir Valley68. The adverse impact of metals on nitrogen mineralization is possibly due to the reactivity of metal ions with sulphydryl groups in the enzyme system of microorganisms engaged in nitrogen mineralization69.

Favorable climatic conditions for microbial activity in control regions, including adequate moisture, warmth, and high organic matter content, significantly contribute to nitrogen mineralization, thereby enriching the soils with nitrogen51–53,70. Correlation analysis indicates a substantial positive relationship between available nitrogen and organic carbon, further emphasizing the interconnected nature of these elements in the studied ecosystem.

Phosphorus levels were notably higher in Hokersar, Manasbal, and Anchar wetlands (13.42 ± 0.68, 12.26 ± 0.88, and 11.33 ± 0.77), while Shallabugh wetland exhibited the lowest concentration (9.98 ± 0.54) (Tables 1 and 3: F = 4.93, p < 0.05). The P content in different plant species at disturbed and control sites was in the range of 0.15–0.26 and 0.18–0.42%, respectively.

Soil pH strongly influenced phosphorus accessibility, with undisturbed areas showing lower pH levels, potentially governing the decreased phosphorus availability. Research indicates that a substantial portion of phosphorus exists in less accessible forms, such as H2PO4, which becomes available at lower pH values and undergoes fixation by hydrous oxides and silicate minerals71. The lower pH in undisturbed areas may explain the reduced phosphorus availability72. Soil pH plays a pivotal role in phosphorus availability, with the optimal range falling between pH 6 to 773. Consistent with this, it had been observed that an increase in available phosphorus in wetland soil with rising pH, particularly towards a neutral range (6–7)68,74,75. Correlation analysis highlighted a significant negative relationship between available phosphorus and pH.

In the present study, there was a notable decrease in available potassium observed in areas with lower pollution levels. The data indicated that unpolluted regions exhibited a higher concentration of potassium (172.39 ± 2.32) compared to polluted areas (Tables 1 and 3: F = 9.88, p < 0.01). The decline in potassium concentration (87.23 ± 1.99) in polluted areas might be attributed to a reduction in wetland cover under disturbed conditions. It has been observed that a layer of organic matter significantly enhances potassium retention in soils.

Moreover, in contaminated areas with higher grazing activity, there is an increased rate of mineral draining (such as K+), which may result in a reduction in the concentration of available potassium in the soil. This could offer an explanation for the lower levels of potassium in polluted zones and the higher levels in controlled areas76,77. The distribution of different forms of potassium in soils showed significant variations, potentially explained by differences in soil chemical properties and the extent to which potassium salts have leached in various soil series78.

Calcium and magnesium, the alkaline components in sediments, are leached by water from rocks where calcium occurs in high concentrations. In nature, these elements exist as carbonates, phosphates, sulphates, or silicate minerals. The increase in calcium and magnesium in undisturbed areas is attributed to processes such as weathering, biological activity, erosion, and allochthonous inputs79. The presence of calcium and magnesium ions in undisturbed areas may result from the uptake of CO2 through photosynthesis, particularly in the submerged precipitation of calcium carbonate80. Notably, Shallabugh exhibits higher Ca and Mg levels (285.94 ± 8.08 & 243.22 ± 3.87) (Tables 1 and 3: F = 8.42, p < 0.01) compared to other sites. Elevated levels during the summer season are likely due to the photosynthetic assimilation by macrophytes during their growth periods.

The heightened metal accumulation is likely linked to increased tourism, transportation, industrial waste, and hospitality infrastructure, contributing significantly to elevated metal contamination and potentially reducing soil fertility81–85. Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA is a robust multivariate statistical method used to reduce data dimensionality while preserving the variation present in the dataset. It helps to identify the principal components that explain the maximum variance in the data. In the current study, the scree plot (Fig. 3) revealed that the first two principal components, Dim1 (83.1%) and Dim2 (5.5%), together accounted for 88.6% of the total variance. This high cumulative variance indicates a strong representation of the original multivariate dataset in a two-dimensional space.

The PCA biplot (Figs. 4 and 5) visualizes the correlation among different soil parameters. Variables like Moisture Content (MC), Organic Carbon (OC), Nitrogen, Magnesium and Calcium are aligned on the left side of Dim1 and show a high contribution to soil quality, particularly in the relatively undisturbed Shallabugh wetland. In contrast, parameters such as Cadmium, Copper, Zinc, Nickel, and Iron loaded positively along Dim1 and are indicative of soil contamination, particularly in the disturbed wetlands like Anchar and Hokersar. The orientation and length of the vectors illustrate the strength and direction of each variable’s contribution. The orthogonality of several vectors reflects low collinearity and confirms distinct groupings of variables with similar ecological implications. Recent studies have emphasized the utility of PCA in wetland and soil studies to explore contaminant gradients and assess environmental stressors91,92. The PCA effectively differentiated sites with anthropogenic pressure from relatively pristine ones, enabling targeted soil conservation strategies.

Conclusion

The present investigation reveals that anthropogenic disturbances exert a substantial influence on the soil physicochemical properties of Kashmir’s wetlands. Sites with higher human interference, such as Hokersar and Anchar, showed elevated soil pH, electrical conductivity and bulk density, reflecting compromised soil quality. In contrast, the relatively undisturbed Shallabugh wetland demonstrated higher organic carbon content and better soil health, highlighting the significance of ecological integrity in maintaining soil functionality. The observed correlations among heavy metals, EC and pH further underline the impact of human-induced stress on soil systems. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) effectively differentiated disturbed from undisturbed wetlands, validating the use of multivariate tools in environmental soil monitoring.

Biotic and abiotic pressures, especially those linked to human encroachments, have led to a decline in vegetation cover, exposing wetland soils to further degradation. This impacts their structural, chemical, and biological integrity. If these pressures continue unchecked, they may result in long-term deterioration of soil fertility, ecosystem services, and the sustainability of local livelihoods. Therefore, adopting integrated wetland management practices is imperative. This includes regular monitoring, land-use regulation, and stakeholder engagement to prevent further encroachment and degradation. Such approaches are vital for maintaining wetland productivity, supporting biodiversity, and securing the ecological and socio-economic functions of these fragile ecosystems.

Future scope

Further studies should encompass a broader range of wetlands across different seasons to capture spatio-temporal variability in soil responses to anthropogenic stress. Incorporating biological indicators such as microbial biomass, enzyme activity and vegetation-soil feedback mechanisms could provide a more holistic understanding of soil health. Additionally, the integration of geospatial tools and long-term monitoring frameworks can aid in early detection of degradation and formulation of targeted conservation strategies. These insights will be crucial for designing evidence-based policies to ensure the resilience and sustainability of wetland ecosystems in the Himalayan region.
