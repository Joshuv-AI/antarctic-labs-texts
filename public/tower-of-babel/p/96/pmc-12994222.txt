Clinical characteristics of ground-glass opacities in young patients : a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Xiaozhen Song, Peixuan Hu, Xiangning Fu, Shengling Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPulmonary ground-glass opacities (GGOs) are increasingly being detected through screening in younger patients, this study aimed to characterize the features of young patients with GGOs.MethodsPatients aged ≤ 55 years who underwent surgical treatment for GGOs at Tongji Hospital of Wuhan from August 2018 to July 2021 were retrospectively analyzed. Clinical and pathological data were collected and compared between age groups.ResultsAmong 1,283 patients, 70.1% were female and 87.0% were nonsmokers. Patients were divided into a young group (18–45 years) and a middle-aged group (46–55 years). Compared with the middle-aged group, young patients had a higher proportion of incidental cases detected during health check-ups (74.6% vs. 66.7%), fewer underlying diseases (2.7% vs. 15.3%), and a lower prevalence of family history of tumors (17.9% vs. 27.6%). Their GGOs were smaller (median 9 mm vs. 10 mm) and more often pure (77.2% vs. 67.0%), with higher rates of adenomatous hyperplasia (4.2% vs. 3.2%), adenocarcinoma in situ (24.3% vs. 19.8%), and minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (34.5% vs. 28.0%). A greater proportion of stage 0 and stage IA1 lesions was also observed (83.0% vs. 70.7%). Wedge resection and segmentectomy were performed more frequently in the young group, whereas lobectomy was less common. The EGFR mutation rate was also lower (47.5% vs. 75.5%).ConclusionsAn increasing number of patients under the age of 46 with GGO have been diagnosed with early-stage lung cancer during their medical examinations, but most of them do not have risk factors. Young patients exhibit distinct imaging, pathological, surgical, and genetic characteristics, underscoring the need for further large-scale studies.Clinical registration numberTJ-IRB20221294.Keywords: Ground-Glass opacity (GGO), Young patients, Clinical characteristics, Lung cancer

Introduction

Ground-Glass Opacity (GGO) is a radiologic finding on LDCT showing a hazy opacity that does not obscure underlying bronchial structures or pulmonary vessels [1, 2]. As a nonspecific radiologic finding, GGOs can be benign lesions, preinvasive lesions and malignancies [3, 4]. Persistent GGOs are more likely to be malignant and have a higher rate of malignancy than solid nodules, so proper management is essential [5].

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, many people did not undergo pulmonary examinations, or underwent chest X-rays instead of chest CT scans. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many people performed pulmonary CT scans (instead of X-ray plain films), which surprised us to discover the presence of GGO in many young patients, arousing our attention to young GGO patients.

The detection of GGOs has grown dramatically with the increasing use of LDCT and the improvement of LDCT imaging resolution [5, 6]. Meanwhile, GGOs in young patients have been increasingly encountered in routine clinical practice [7, 8]. However, we do not know much about the clinical features of young patients with GGOs, and few studies have tried to customize treatment strategies.

This is a retrospective analysis of the clinical data of 1283 young patients who were diagnosed with GGOs and underwent surgical treatment, which is the largest sample size of relevant studies reported thus far. This study aimed to clarify the clinical features of young GGOs, so that we can better manage young GGOs.

Methods

Patients

From August 2018 to July 2021, the Department of Thoracic Surgery of Tongji Hospital affiliated with Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology treated 1357 GGO patients aged 55 and below from more than 20 provinces in China, 74 of whom were excluded due to missing data, resulting in 1283 patients available for analysis, including 448 young patients (≤ 45 years old) and 835 middle-aged patients (46–55 years old). Surgical treatment was considered after the persistence of the GGO nodules. The inclusion criteria were as follows [9]: patients with radiologically proven GGO nodules; nodules greater than 5 mm; 18 to 55 years old; complete excision; and no other cancer or metastatic disease. After assessment of the nodules and the patient’s overall condition, the patient was eligible for surgical treatment.

We refer to the NCCN Cancer Clinical Practice Guidelines for the management of GGO patients. Solitary smaller pure GGOs and mixed GGOs (< 6 mm) followed by surveillance CT every year, whereas solitary larger pure GGOs (≥ 6 mm) require an initial follow-up surveillance CT within 6–12 months, followed by surveillance CT every year. Solitary larger mixed GGOs (≥ 6 mm) require follow-up surveillance CT within 3–6 months. PET-CT, biopsy, or surgical resection is considered for nodules with a solid component ≥ 6 mm in size.

Radiologic evaluation

The diameter of the tumor was defined as the largest axial diameter of the nodule on the pulmonary window, where consolidation was defined as an area of increased opacification that completely obscured the underlying bronchial structures and vascular markings. The CTR, defined as the ratio of the solid portion size to the total size. GGOs can be subdivided into 3 groups according to their CTR: pure GGO (CTR = 0), GGO dominant (0 < CTR ≤ 0.5), or solid-dominant (0.5 < CTR < 1). Multiple nodules refer to two or more nodules with diameters between 5 mm and 30 mm in the CT image. Each nodule on the preoperative CT scan was blindly reviewed by two imaging specialists.

Histologic evaluation

All specimens were examined and recorded by three professional pathologists. Tumor stage was evaluated according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer ninth edition cancer staging manual [10]. Cancer subtypes were classified based on the 2021 World Health Organization Classification of Lung Tumors [11].

Procedures

Patients were placed in the lateral decubitus position, with double-lumen endotracheal intubation in most patients and bronchial blockers in a few patients. Most patients underwent uniportal VATS, and a few patients underwent biportal VATS. Thoracoscopic exploration was performed first, and if pleural adhesion was severe or minimally invasive surgery was not possible, thoracotomy was performed. The specific location of the nodules was determined by three-dimensional reconstruction of the nodules, hook wire positioning guided by CT before surgery or finger exploration. Multiple nodules in some patients were located by intraoperative ENB. ENB was performed using the SuperDimension™ navigation system (version 7.0; Medtronic, Minneapolis, USA) for intraoperative nodule localization. When ENB-guided microwave ablation was required, a microwave generator (MTC-3; Vison Medical, Nanjing, China) operating at a frequency of 2450 ± 50 MHz was used, with a power of 40–80 W for 2–4 min per site, depending on tumor size, shape, and distance from the pleura. Multiple ablations were performed if necessary to ensure complete coverage of the lesion [12]. Surgical treatment was determined based on the location of the nodules, imaging features, rapid pathological results, and the patient’s physical condition.

Postoperative recovery

All cases were followed from the day of surgery until discharge.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were examined for normality. Variables with a normal distribution are presented as means ± standard deviations and were compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables, including the number of patients (Table 1), tumor diameter (Table 3), and length of hospital stay after surgery (Table 6), are expressed as medians (range) and were compared using non-parametric tests. Categorical variables are reported as counts (percentages) and were analyzed using the χ² test. Percentages were rounded to one decimal place, and totals may equal 100% or show minimal deviation due to rounding. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 19.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.).

Results

Prevalence trends

Table 1 shows the clinical characteristics of patients at different time periods. There were 237, 173 and 873 patients in the periods of 2018.8 to 2019.7, 2019.8 to 2020.7 and 2020.8 to 2021.7, with median ages of 49, 48 and 48 years, respectively. These patients were divided into three groups according to the ages of 18–35, 36–45 and 46–55 years for analysis. The results showed that the proportion of patients aged 18–35 increased year by year.

Table 1 also shows another trend, the proportion of pulmonary nodules found by check-up gradually increased (from 54.0% to 72.6%), while the overall incidence of multiple GGOs remained stable over time. To manage multiple nodules, in the third year, we used an ENB to locate and ablate pulmonary nodules during surgery [13].

Clinical characteristics

The characteristics of the enrolled patients are shown in Table 2. The proportions of never smokers in the young group and the middle-aged group were 89.3% and 85.7%, respectively(P > 0.05). Regarding the reasons for seeking medical treatment, there were fewer patients with symptoms in the young group than in the middle-aged group (16.2% vs. 22.2%), and more patients were found through check-up or by accident (74.6% vs. 66.7%, P < 0.001). In the young group and the middle-aged group, the proportion of patients with a family history of lung cancer was 9.6% and 13.8%, respectively, and the proportion of patients with a family history of other tumors was 8.3% and 13.8% (P < 0.001), respectively. The middle-aged patients with hypertension, diabetes and coronary heart disease accounted for 11.0%, 1.4% and 0.8%, respectively, which were significantly higher than in the young group (1.8%, 0.2% and 0.0%, respectively, P < 0.001).

Radiologic features

Representative CT images of typical GGOs in young patients are shown in Fig. 1. We analyzed the number of GGOs in patients. The proportion of patients with one, two, three, four, five or more GGOs was 83.9%, 12.1%, 2.5%, 1.1%, and 0.4% in the young group and 80.4%, 15.3%, 2.9%, 0.6%, and 0.8% in the middle-aged group, respectively (P > 0.05). We further analyzed the distribution of nodules in patients with multiple GGOs. The two groups of multiple GGO distributions showed no significant differences.

Representative CT images of GGOs in young and middle-aged patients. Red circles indicate the location of the GGOs

Further analysis was performed in units of GGOs. There were a total of 1562 GGOs in 1283 patients. The results are shown in Table 3. The median diameter of the GGOs was 9 mm in the young group and 10 mm in the middle-aged group (P < 0.001). The stratified analysis of nodular diameter showed that GGOs with diameters of 5–10 mm, 10–20 mm and 20–30 mm accounted for 72.5%, 25.5% and 2.1% of the young group and 51.9%, 39.6% and 8.5% of the middle-aged group, respectively (P < 0.001). In addition, by analyzing the components of the nodules, it was found that pure GGOs, GGO-dominated nodules and solid component-dominated nodules accounted for 77.2%, 10.9% and 12.0% of the young group and 67.0%, 17.0% and 16.0% of the middle-aged group, respectively (P < 0.001).

In addition, the proportions of nodules in the right upper lobe, right middle lobe, right lower lobe, left upper lobe and left lower lobe in the young group were 31.6%, 7.1%, 17.0%, 27.5% and 16.7%, respectively. In the middle-aged group, the proportions were 38.9%, 5.8%, 15.1%, 28.5% and 11.7%, respectively (P < 0.05).

Pathological characteristics

Table 4 shows the differences in the pathological results of the 1464 resected pulmonary nodules. The constituent ratios for AAH, AIS, MIA, IAC, IMA, and benign lesions were 4.2%, 24.3%, 34.5%, 22.3%, 0.2%, and 14.9% in the young group and 3.2%, 19.8%, 28.0%, 35.8%, 0.3%, and 12.8% in the middle-aged group, respectively (P < 0.001). AAH, AIS and MIA were more common in the young group. We then compared the pathological stages of GGOs, the results showed that the proportions of stage 0 and stage IA1 in the young group were significantly higher than those in the middle-aged group, while the proportions of stage IA2, IA3, IB and IIB were significantly lower than those in the middle-aged group, suggesting that tumors were found earlier in the young group.

Surgery

We analyzed the surgical methods of all patients. As shown in Table 5, for patients with a single GGO, wedge resection, segmentectomy, lobectomy, ablation and compound surgery accounted for 19.9%, 58.8%, 20.5%, 0.3%, and 0.5% in the young group and 15.1%, 48.6%, 35.9%, 0.0%, and 0.4% in the middle-aged group, respectively (P < 0.001). For patients with multiple GGOs, wedge resection, segmentectomy, lobectomy, ablation and compound surgery accounted for 11.1%, 47.2%, 11.1%, 1.4%, and 29.2% in the young group and 8.5%, 32.3%, 22.6%, 0.0%, and 36.6% in the middle-aged group, respectively. In addition, to manage multiple GGOs, some patients underwent intraoperative pulmonary nodule localization and microwave ablation with ENB.

Postoperative recovery

Postoperative recovery information is presented in Table 6. Among the 1283 patients, except for one death and one patient with aggravated pulmonary infection, worsening renal function, a total of 58 short-term complications occurred in 1281 patients. There were 7 cases of infection, 3 cases of chylothorax, 28 cases of pneumothorax, 15 cases of pleural effusion requiring thoracentesis and 5 cases classified as “other” (including hemoptysis, pulmonary embolism, air leaks, or multiple complications). Group analysis showed no significant difference between the two groups. Although the median length of hospital stay was the same in both groups, nonparametric tests suggested a statistically significant difference between the two groups, with a shorter hospital stay in the young group. The means after 5% pruning support this conclusion (the mean hospitalization time is 6.85 days for the young group, and 7.28 days for the middle-aged group).

EGFR mutation

A total of 202 patients underwent genetic testing after surgery, and the EGFR mutation information of 216 nodules was obtained, as shown in Table 7. The proportion of EGFR mutations in the young group (47.5%) was significantly lower than that in the middle-aged group (75.5%),P < 0.001. In addition, the incidence of 19DEL and L858R mutations in the young group was lower than that in the middle-aged group, especially the L858R mutation (P < 0.05).

Discussion

We found a significant increase in the number of patients enrolled in the third year, which may be associated with the increased detection of pulmonary nodules following the COVID-19 outbreak. The value of chest CT in diagnosing COVID-19 has been widely recognized [9, 14, 15]. Due to the widespread use of chest CT, a large number of small pulmonary nodules were found, and in this study, more than 70% of lung GGO patients under the age of 55 in the last two years were detected through check-up, and most of them had early lung cancer. An increasing number of enterprises, public institutions and ordinary people have accepted the idea that LDCT should be used for LCS rather than chest X-ray.

According to the NCCN Guidelines for LCS, the high-risk population was traditionally defined as individuals aged ≥ 55 years with a significant smoking history [16], and many randomized controlled trials have therefore focused on LCS in people aged 50, 55, or even older than 60 [17]. As a result, younger, nonsmoking individuals with GGOs would not have been recommended for screening, and their cancers might have been missed. To specifically address this gap, our study focused on patients younger than 55 years. Within this group, we further divided patients into a “young” group (≤ 45 years) and a “middle-aged” group (46–55 years). Both our data and previous reports have suggested a trend toward younger onset of GGO patients [8, 18]. Some studies have even drawn attention to extremely young cases—for example, using 40 years as a threshold [8] or reporting cases below 20 years of age [19] —but such low cut-offs are unlikely to be practical in population-based screening because of increased costs and limited yield. Therefore, we chose 45 years as a more pragmatic boundary to reflect the clinical trend toward younger age at diagnosis while maintaining relevance to real-world screening strategies. On the other hand, the 2024 NCCN update has already lowered the age threshold for high-risk screening to 50 years [20], reflecting recognition of the trend toward earlier onset. Our decision to analyze patients under 45 years separately provides exploratory insight into this younger population that remains outside even the revised screening criteria.

Here, we focused on young GGO patients under the age of 45 and found a large number of early-stage lung cancers, many of which were in nonsmoking women, suggesting that screening criteria for lung cancer may need to be adjusted. It was found that female sex (70.1%) and nonsmokers (87.0%) were more common among young GGO patients. Several studies [7, 8, 21, 22] have obtained similar conclusions from different perspectives.

Imaging analysis showed that patients with multiple GGOs accounted for 18.4% of young GGO patients, and there was no significant difference in the distribution of multiple nodules in different lobes between the young group and the middle-aged group. The surgical treatment of multiple GGOs is difficult, involving the localization of the nodules, the treatment of the nodules and the scope of surgical resection. To better treat multiple GGOs, we used ENB technology to locate multiple nodules intraoperatively and ablated some deep GGOs. The proportion of pulmonary GGOs treated with intraoperative ENB have increased year by year. Surgical excision was performed on the primary lesion, and resection or ablation was performed on the secondary lesions.

Our study of the size of the nodules showed that the diameter of GGOs in the young group was smaller than that in the middle-aged group. A stratified analysis of the size of the nodules showed that the proportion of patients with GGOs under 10 mm was higher in the young group, while the proportion of GGOs between 10 and 30 mm was higher in the middle-aged group. In addition, the proportion of pure GGOs in the young group was higher in the middle-aged group, while the proportion of GGOs containing solid components was higher in the middle-aged group. The size of the nodules and the proportion of GGOs have a certain relationship with the pathological types of nodules [23–25]. Accordingly, we conducted a comparative analysis of the pathological types of nodules. Compared with the middle-aged group, the proportions of AAH, AIS and MIA were higher in the young group, and the proportion of stage 0 and IA1 patients was higher, indicating that the nodules in the young group had more ground-glass components, a lower degree of malignancy and earlier staging. It is worth noting that the constituent ratios of AAH, AIS, MIA, IAC and benign lesions in our youth group are 4.2%, 24.3%, 34.5%, 22.3%, and 14.9%, which is close to Wang J’s data (6.4%, 15.6%, 50.35%, 15.6%, and 12.1%) [26].

In clinical practice, many patients experience excessive anxiety, and considering their quality of life, we will perform surgical treatment at their strong request. On the other hand, for patients with multiple GGO, if we evaluate the need for surgical treatment for their main nodules and allow for simultaneous removal of other small nodules, multiple nodules will be removed with their informed consent. These will all lead us to include some pure GOOs with smaller diameters. The phenomenon has also been observed in other articles: for example, in Fang W’s study [27], the 2D mean diameter of the AAH/AIS group was 8.54 ± 2.23 mm, and the proportion of pure GGO is 85.45%; in Hu F’s study [28], 55.52% of patients had CT characteristic size < 10 mm, and in Gong J’s two cohorts [29], over 40% had GGO Diameter < 10 mm; in Yagi T’s study [30], the mean diameter of the AIS-MIA group was about 0.81 mm. We also compared the pathological types of GGO in their articles. The proportion of AIS, MIA, and IAC in Fang W’s study [27] was 24.6%, 41.5%, and 33.9%; In Hu F’s research [28]: 22.1%, 39.2%, 38.7%; In Gong J’s research [29]: 23.6%, 15.4%, 22.0%; In Yagi T’s study [30]: 15.8%, 31.7%, 66.3%. It is basically consistent with our data (24.3%, 34.5%, 22.3%). Of course, the proportion of AAH and AIS in clinical practice is generally high, and more and more studies are paying attention to this phenomenon, and we will also strive to avoid it.

Consistent with the pathological type and stage, wedge resection and segmentectomy were more common in the young group regardless of single or multiple nodules. For young patients and those with small GGOs, some nodules are deep and are located between several lung segments, and lobotomy should be carried out prudently. Sufficient observation time should be given, or the nodules can be treated by ENB-MWA to reduce the loss of pulmonary function [13]. We performed ENB-MWA in a young patient with a single GGO in a deep position, and a higher proportion of patients with multiple nodules were treated with ENB and ENB-MWA.

After comparing the postoperative recovery of the two groups, the postoperative complications of the young group and the middle-aged group were similar, while the actual hospital stay of the young group was slightly shorter than that of the middle-aged group (6.85 days vs. 7.28 days, P = 0.002).

We paid special attention to the gene mutations in young GGO lung cancer patients. Our results showed that the incidence of EGFR gene mutations in young patients was lower than that in the middle-aged group. The finding is partially consistent with previous studies reporting correlations between GGO volume/diameter and EGFR mutation rates [25, 31]. Young patients in our cohort had a higher proportion of pure GGOs (77.2%) and smaller nodule diameters (median 9 mm), features that are less frequently associated with driver mutations, which may explain their lower EGFR mutation rates. In terms of mutant subtypes, the incidences of EGFR-19DEL and EGFR-L858R in young patients were lower than that in middle-aged patients. It is worth noting that many studies [31–33] have pointed out that the gene mutation frequency of GGOs is higher in women and nonsmokers, but our study did not confirm this conclusion (Table 8). However, EGFR mutation data were available for only 202 of 1,283 patients (15.7%), which may introduce selection bias. Therefore, the interpretation of mutation frequencies should be made with caution.

There are still some limitations to our study. First, this is a single-institution retrospective study, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts are needed to further validate and extend our results. Second, we focused mainly on perioperative outcomes and short-term complications, while long-term follow-up data such as recurrence and overall survival were not available. Although most studies [9, 25] have suggested that GGOs generally have a favorable prognosis, young patients may have higher expectations regarding long-term survival and quality of life. Therefore, prospective studies with extended follow-up periods are necessary to fully assess the outcomes of this population. In addition, we did not incorporate emerging approaches such as AI-assisted imaging analysis or molecular profiling of GGOs, which may provide additional insights in the future.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the number of patients (< 46 years) with GGO detected through routine check-up has been increasing. Most of these patients were non-smoking women without classical risk factors or underlying diseases. Compared with middle-aged GGO patients, young GGO patients presented with distinct clinicopathologic and genetic profiles, including smaller nodules, a higher proportion of pure GGOs and precancerous lesions, fewer invasive adenocarcinomas, lower EGFR mutation rates, and earlier pathological stages. Wedge resection and segmentectomy were the predominant surgical procedures, and lobectomy was less common. Multiple GGOs occurred in more than 15% of young patients, and ENB played an important role in localization and ablation. These findings highlight that young GGO patients constitute a unique subgroup of lung cancer with different biological characteristics, suggesting that current screening strategies may need to be reconsidered in future studies. Larger multicenter investigations with long-term follow-up are warranted to validate and expand these observations.
