A potential protective role of congenital peritoneal encapsulation coexisting with metastatic colon cancer: a rare case report
Authors: Youssef Ahmad, Joud Fahed, Jebrail Mousa, Fadi Souleiman
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Congenital peritoneal encapsulation (CPE) is a rare condition in which the small intestine is encased within a mesothelial-lined sac. The following case is an extremely rare description of the co-existence of both colon cancer and peritoneal encapsulation, highlighting the potential role of this co-existence in preventing the spread of metastases and tumor implantation.Case-presentation:A 60-year-old female was diagnosed with metastatic colon cancer. During the operation, a thin fibrous membrane was found covering the small intestine, which suggested CPE. The tumor was removed, and the additional membrane was totally excised. The patient is currently receiving chemotherapy for metastatic treatment and is in good health.Clinical discussion:CPE is a medical condition that results from abnormal peritoneal development, and it is often confused with other differential diagnoses resulting from inflammatory causes. It can occur at different ages and remains asymptomatic for the majority of cases. However, it can be a potential cause of bowel obstruction. An intraoperative diagnosis can easily differentiate the case. The co-existence with colon cancer has been described only once in the literature.Conclusion:Peritoneal encapsulation is a rare, mostly asymptomatic condition. However, it may play a protective role in preventing metastatic colon cancer from affecting the small intestine, thus potentially opening up new avenues for the treatment of cancer metastases.

Congenital peritoneal encapsulation (CPE) is an extremely rare anomaly that is formed due to an accessory peritoneal layer encapsulating the small bowel. The majority of cases of CPE cases remain asymptomatic and are diagnosed accidentally during autopsy and/or surgery1. However, CPE can result in some symptoms including colicky abdominal pain and in rare cases bowel obstruction2. According to the medical literature, the differential diagnosis should include sclerosing encapsulating peritonitis (SEP) and abdominal cocoon. Radiologic methods are very limited and unspecific for diagnosing CPE. Both ultrasonography and radiography will show no pathology on routine check-up3. Computed tomography (CT) scan, however, is a highly recommended method as it can show some specific feature of CPE3. The association between CPE and colon cancer has been discussed only once in the literature and in this case, we report a very rare association between an asymptomatic CPE case with a metastatic colon cancer in a 60-year-old female. The work has been reported in line with the Surgical CAse REport (SCARE) 2020 criteria4 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/MS9/A264).

Case-presentation

A 60-year-old female patient presented to the hospital with complaints of constipation and mild abdominal pain. Upon admission, the patient was found to be afebrile with normal vital signs. Laboratory tests were also normal. Further inspection revealed that the patient was infertile without any other gynecological problems and had no prior surgical or medical history, except for mild constipation. An abdominal radiograph was performed, which did not reveal any significant findings. An abdominal CT-scan showed a (5.61 cm×7.16 cm) mass in the right colon, which was diagnosed with colon cancer (Fig. 1A). A CT-scan also showed a peculiar location of the small intestine. It was all located in the left hypochondrium (Fig. 1B). A colonoscopy was demanded to take samples, but the right colon appeared to make a 180° angle with the cecum, making a colonoscopy difficult to be conducted. A radical tumor resection procedure was conducted by a general surgeon in the general surgery department. During the surgery, the patient did not have ascites. A thin translucent membrane was found encasing the small intestine and extending between the parietal peritoneum and posterior abdominal wall. Despite this, the small intestine appeared normal and peristaltic when viewed through the membrane (Fig. 2). Further inspection revealed multiple nodules within the omentum, suspecting direct tumor invasion. However, after opening the thin membrane, no nodules were observed within the small intestine, and the intestine loops were found to be viable. The tumor was removed and sent for pathological examination, and the additional membrane was completely resected. The surgery was uneventful, and vital signs were within normal limits. The patient was discharged 2 days postoperatively. Macroscopic examination showed a 65 cm-long colon that reveals a 6 cm-diameter infiltrative mass 15 cm from the nearest surgical incision (Fig. 3A). Histopathological examination revealed a moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma with wide necrosis invading the muscularis propria and sub-serosal fat (Fig. 3B). The patient is currently receiving chemotherapy and is in good health.

Discussion

Cleland was the first to describe CPE in 1868. It is an extremely rare condition, with no more than 60 reported cases to date1. CPE refers to the presence of an additional thin layer that covers part of or all the small intestine. It results from abnormal peritoneal development during the 12th week of gestation, when the yolk sac returns with the small intestine into the abdominal cavity to form this layer instead of remaining in the umbilical pedicle. The peritoneal membrane extends between the ascending and descending colons laterally and between the transverse colon superiorly and the posterior surface of the parietal peritoneum inferiorly2,3. The majority of cases remain asymptomatic, making the real incidence of CPE still undefined. However, different case reports showed that CPE can cause bowel obstruction through different mechanisms, such as putting pressure on intestine loops, an acute increase in intra-abdominal pressure, and adhesions, respectively2,3,5. Differential diagnosis of CPE includes SEP and cocoon syndrome. In contrast to CPE, these two cases are of inflammatory origin5.

CPE can be diagnosed incidentally during laparotomy for other reasons. X-ray findings are usually normal or nonspecific. CT can be helpful for preoperative diagnosis since it can show the Helix sign, in which small loops are arranged in a helix form surrounded by a membrane. Intraoperative characteristics of CPE include small intestine that can move freely within a thin, translucent, and highly vascularized membrane without adhering to each other. In contrast, in SEP and cocoon syndromes, small bowels are adherent to each other, and the surrounding membrane is thick, white, and collagenous3.

Treatment of CPE usually includes excision of the additional membrane. In the presence of bowel obstruction, surgeries like resection of gangrenous bowel loops and adhesiolysis may be performed3,5.

A recent case report described the first association between sigmoid colon cancer and CPE in a 70-year-old female. The patient had cancer metastasis to the pelvis, rectum, and omentum, but the small intestine was intact6.

In our case, a CT-scan showed right colon cancer. During the procedure, multiple nodules were noticed in the omentum, representing metastases. Another important finding was a thin fibrous membrane completely surrounding the small intestine. However, the bowels were viable and, most importantly, free from nodules. Reviewing the medical literature, we clinically diagnosed this finding as CPE. This is the first case in the literature to report the co-existence of CPE with right colon cancer. Since the congenital membrane only covers the small intestines, the metastases still managed to reach other organs through the humoral pathway, which could explain the presence of metastases in the omentum and liver. However, the absence of metastases in the small intestine may be a sign that the membrane managed to protect the intestines from invasion, and thus we hope that further research is done to discuss and reveal the physiologic role of this membrane, which may represent a great hope for treating cancer implantation and metastases.

Conclusion

Despite the fact that CPE is an extremely rare case, it has been described in several case reports. Preoperative diagnostic methods can be very helpful in diagnosing the case, especially since the majority of cases are asymptomatic. The co-existence of CPE with colon cancer is extremely rare, with very limited published data. We hope that further research could be done to confirm the physiologic role of CPE in blocking colon cancer and metastases from spreading, thus providing a new treatment for metastatic colon cancer.
