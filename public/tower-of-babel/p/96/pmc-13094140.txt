Nurturing empathy in new medical professionals through a community-based educational program: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Tzu-Hung Liu, Kai-Yen Chang, Chiu-Feng Wu, Yao-Kuang Wu, You-Chen Chao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmpathy is a core component of medical professionalism and is associated with better clinical outcomes and patient satisfaction. However, empathy decline has been reported during clinical training. Community-based educational program, informed by service-learning principles, has shown promise in fostering empathy among medical and nursing students, but its impact on newly practicing physicians and nurses remains underexplored.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods study at a tertiary hospital in Taiwan. A structured “Newcomer Camp” was implemented for first-year resident physicians and newly hired nurses. The program included didactic role-modeling sessions, a half-day experiential home visit to underserved patients, and guided reflective practice. Empathy was measured quantitatively using the Jefferson Scale of Empathy–Health Provider version (JSE-HP) before and after the camp. Qualitatively, participants responded to open-ended reflective prompts; their written reflections were analyzed thematically. Statistical analysis compared pre- vs. post-intervention JSE-HP scores (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, α = 0.05). Thematic analysis of reflections was conducted by two independent researchers to elucidate mechanisms of empathy development.ResultsA total of 116 newcomers (20 residents, 96 nurses) participated. After the camp experience, JSE-HP empathy scores increased significantly in both resident physicians (mean ± SD: 115.9 ± 15.2 to 123.0 ± 11.6, p = 0.013) and nurses (113.1 ± 14.3 to 120.5 ± 14.6, p < 0.001). Participants rated the program’s role modeling, experiential learning, and reflection components as highly impactful for their empathy (mean impact scores 6.3, 6.2, and 5.9 out of 7, respectively). Thematic analysis of 116 reflective journals revealed three major themes: Benefiting Society, Cultivating Empathy, and Learning from Service. New clinicians reported heightened awareness of social inequities and personal privilege (perspective-taking), emotional resonance with patients’ suffering (affective empathy), and strengthened altruistic motivation to help others. Role modeling by veteran volunteers inspired selfless attitudes, and hands-on service experiences “put them in patients’ shoes,” fostering both cognitive and affective empathy.ConclusionsA structured community-based program was associated with an immediate increase in self-reported empathy in new medical professionals. Role modeling, direct patient service, and reflection worked synergistically to promote perspective-taking and compassionate, altruistic attitudes. These findings support incorporating well-designed community experiences into early residency and nursing orientation to enhance empathy and sustain compassionate care in clinical practice.

Background

Empathy in healthcare is the ability to understand a patient’s emotions and perspective and to communicate that understanding back to the patient [1]. It is widely regarded as a crucial element of professionalism and effective clinical performance [2]. Despite the diverse interpretations and definitions of empathy in the literature, empathy is generally agreed to be a multidimensional construct comprising cognitive and affective components [3, 4]. In the medical field, clinical empathy places particular emphasis on the cognitive dimension of empathy [5]. Empathetic clinicians tend to exhibit better patient relationships and outcomes, including higher patient satisfaction and treatment adherence, as well as improved clinical results [6–11]. Empathy in providers has also been linked to lower burnout and improved well-being among providers themselves [12, 13] Despite its importance, numerous studies have documented a worrying decline in empathy during medical training, particularly as trainees progress through intense clinical phases [14–17] This phenomenon, often termed “empathy erosion,” has been observed in medical students—most notably during the third year of medical school—and may persist into residency [15]. Contributing factors include increased workload, stress, time pressure, and emotional fatigue in clinical environments, which can dampen empathetic engagement.

Instilling empathy and humanism is increasingly recognized as a critical goal in medical and nursing curricula [18]. Accordingly, educators have sought methods to cultivate and preserve empathy in health professions trainees. A variety of educational interventions, from communication skills workshops and narrative medicine to mindfulness training and reflective writing, have shown promise in fostering empathy [19–21]. A systematic review found that targeted programs can modestly improve physicians’ empathy, suggesting that empathy is a trainable attribute rather than a fixed trait [20]. Another review likewise revealed that educational interventions can enhance nursing students’ empathy, particularly when the interventions combine experiential learning with structured reflection [21].

For conceptual clarification in this manuscript, we use empathy to refer to a multidimensional capacity involving both cognitive understanding of another person’s perspective and affective resonance with their emotions. Compassion is conceptually related but distinct: it is typically described as empathic concern in response to another’s suffering accompanied by a motivation or intention to alleviate that suffering [22, 23]. Altruism refers to selfless helping behaviors or motivations that may arise from compassion, but is analytically distinct from the empathic or compassionate psychological response itself [22].

Community-based education has emerged as a particularly powerful pedagogical approach for nurturing empathy and altruism [24]. In community-based education, there are many examples of program designs informed by service-learning principles [24–26]. Service-learning is defined as a form of experiential education in which learners engage in organized service activities that meet identified community needs, combined with structured opportunities for reflection and learning [22]. Rooted in John Dewey’s educational philosophy of learning through experience, service-learning allows students to confront real-world social issues and interact with underserved populations, thereby humanizing abstract concepts of care and professionalism [27]. Prior studies in medical education have reported that service-learning and community service experiences can increase students’ empathy and compassion [28–31]. For example, medical students who participate in longitudinal community service programs have demonstrated higher empathy and a better understanding of patients’ social contexts [28]. A service-learning curriculum at one U.S. medical school improved students’ empathy and attitudes toward underserved populations [24]. Similarly, nursing students involved in service outreach projects have reported enhanced empathic awareness and caring attitudes toward patients [32–34]. These findings align with the idea that face-to-face service fosters perspective-taking and emotional engagement, which are key components of empathy.

Although the benefits of community-based service-learning on empathy have been demonstrated among undergraduate medical and nursing students, there is limited research on its impact among newly practicing health professionals, such as first-year resident physicians and newly graduated nurses. These individuals are at a critical transition, entering clinical practice with baseline professional training while facing new stresses and responsibilities that could challenge their empathy. Early-career doctors and nurses often encounter heavy workloads, high-acuity patients, and systemic pressures that may lead to detachment or compassion fatigue [35, 36]. Interventions at this career stage might be pivotal in reinforcing empathy and preventing its erosion. However, few studies have examined structured empathy-enhancing programs in residency orientations or nurse onboarding contexts.

To address this gap, we implemented a community-based educational program, informed by service-learning principles, as part of a newcomer orientation “empathy camp” for incoming residents and nurses at a teaching hospital. While service-learning has traditionally been employed to foster empathy and other attributes, its strict implementation requires reciprocal co-design with community partners, which can be logistically challenging in standardized hospital orientation programs [37]. Therefore, we implemented a community-based educational intervention. This program integrates key service-learning principles (experiential service and structured reflection) within a feasible orientation curriculum. We aimed to investigate whether this community-based educational experience could measurably improve empathy in these new medical professionals and to explore qualitatively how the experience influenced their empathy development. We utilized a mixed-methods design, combining quantitative pre-post assessment of empathy with qualitative analysis of participants’ reflective narratives. We hypothesized that the newcomer camp would increase empathy scores and that participants’ reflections would reveal key mechanisms of empathy growth, notably enhanced cognitive and affective empathy, as well as strengthened altruistic motivation to help those in need. Ultimately, this study seeks to inform medical educators on how structured community-based education can be leveraged to nurture empathy and humanism in new healthcare providers.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted an exploratory, mixed-methods study involving first-year resident physicians and newly employed nurses at Taipei Tzu Chi Hospital, a tertiary teaching hospital in Taiwan. The study was conducted in September 2020 as part of the hospital’s mandatory newcomer training program. Given the institutional requirement that all newcomers receive the same onboarding curriculum within a fixed timeframe, it was not feasible to withhold the intervention or randomize participants during the implementation period. Therefore, we used a single-group pre–post design to examine short-term changes and feasibility while triangulating quantitative findings with qualitative reflections to strengthen interpretability. All incoming postgraduate year-1 residents (PGY-1, just out of medical school) and all newly hired nurses (in their probationary period) were invited to participate. This cohort was chosen because participants were at the very beginning of their professional practice. A total of 30 new residents and 141 new nurses attended the mandatory newcomer orientation camp; among these, 20 residents (67%) and 96 nurses (68%) consented to enroll in the study and completed all study components.

Intervention: newcomer orientation “Empathy camp”

The intervention was a one-day community-based camp designed explicitly to nurture empathy. The program curriculum included a combination of didactic sessions, role modeling, experiential learning, and reflective practice, structured as follows:

Role modeling and preparation (Didactic)

The day began with a two-hour session highlighting role models in compassionate care. A senior Tzu Chi humanitarian volunteer delivered a talk describing his experiences providing medical relief to Syrian refugees, exemplifying empathy and altruism in action. Additionally, local volunteer leaders presented case studies of impoverished patients (“care recipients”) in the community who were slated for home visits. Participants were briefed on these cases, including the patients’ living conditions and challenges, and engaged in group discussions to prepare care plans and needed supplies for the visits. This segment aimed to inspire participants through real examples and prepare them to understand the lives of underserved individuals.

Experiential learning (Service activity)

Following the preparation session, participants divided into small interprofessional teams (mixing residents and nurses) for a four-hour home-visit service project. Each team visited one of the identified families living in extreme deprivation in the local area. During the home visits, the new doctors and nurses actively provided basic care and assistance: performing health checks and tending to minor medical needs, offering emotional support, delivering donated food and household goods, and helping to clean and improve the living environment (including tidying clutter or improving sanitation in the home). These visits allowed participants to directly engage with patients facing poverty, illness, and social isolation. The experiential component was designed to put participants face-to-face with patients’ realities, thereby fostering perspective-taking and compassionate, hands-on care.

Reflective practice

After the home visits, participants reconvened at the hospital for a debriefing and reflection period (2 h). Participants were assigned to profession-specific reflection groups of approximately 8–10 members (residents and nurses met separately), each facilitated by a senior facilitator from the same professions. They were given 20 min to individually write in reflective journals addressing three open-ended prompts about their experiences (detailed below under Qualitative Analysis). This was followed by within-group sharing sessions (40 min) in which participants discussed their feelings, insights, and challenges with peers and facilitators. Key insights were subsequently shared in a brief plenary discussion (60 min) to consolidate learning across groups. The reflection phase was intended to encourage critical thinking about their service experience, consolidate lessons learned, and allow emotional processing in a supportive setting.

Throughout the camp, experienced facilitators (including the volunteer speakers, senior nurses, and faculty physicians) guided discussions and served as role models, emphasizing empathetic communication and patient-centered attitudes. The entire program was conducted in alignment with the hospital’s humanitarian mission, creating a safe space for newcomers to express empathy and learn by doing.

Quantitative measurement

We assessed participants’ empathy using the Jefferson Scale of Empathy–Health Care Providers version (JSE-HP). The JSE-HP is a validated self-report instrument specifically developed for measuring empathy among physicians and other health professionals [5]. It consists of 20 Likert-scale items (rated 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree) that yield a total empathy score (maximum 140) and can be subdivided into three components reflecting key aspects of empathy: Perspective Taking, Compassionate Care, and Standing in the Patient’s Shoes. The JSE-HP has been previously translated into Chinese and validated among Taiwanese healthcare providers, demonstrating excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.90) and a three-factor construct consistent with the theoretical framework of clinician empathy [38]. In this study, participants completed the JSE-HP questionnaire immediately before the camp (pre-test, baseline) and again after the camp (post-test, same day in the evening) to measure any change in empathy.

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata v15.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Given the sample size and the non-normal distribution often observed for Jefferson Scale of Empathy (JSE) scores, nonparametric tests were used. Pre- and post-intervention empathy scores were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (paired test) for each group (residents and nurses). Changes in JSE-HP subscale scores (Perspective Taking, Compassionate Care, and Standing in Patient’s Shoes) were analyzed in the same manner. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant demographics and baseline characteristics. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Because the total JSE-HP score was the primary outcome and the three JSE-HP subscale scores were secondary outcomes, a Bonferroni correction was applied for secondary outcomes, adjusting the significance threshold to 𝛼 < 0.0167 (0.05/3). Furthermore, to interpret the magnitude of the intervention’s impact independent of sample size, effect sizes (r) were calculated for the non-parametric tests using the formula r = Z/√N, where N is the total number of observations. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (0.1), medium (0.3), and large (0.5). Additionally, as part of the program evaluation, participants were asked to rate the impact of each camp activity on their empathy development. Using a 7-point Likert scale (1 = not at all effective, 7 = extremely effective), they evaluated three components: the role modeling session, the experiential home visit, and the reflective practice session. Ratings were collected at the end of the day to determine the components that participants perceived as most beneficial. These data were analyzed descriptively (mean and proportion giving a positive rating).

Qualitative data collection and analysis

To gain deeper insight into how the community-based experience influenced the participants’ empathy, we collected qualitative data through written reflective journals. At the reflection session following the home visits, all participants were asked to respond in writing to three open-ended prompts in a journal. The prompts were: (1) “How do you feel after hearing the volunteers share their experiences?”—targeting reactions to the role-modeling session; (2) “Was the preparation for the home visit helpful? Why or why not?”—targeting insights about the preparatory phase; (3) “What benefited you the most during your home visit? Why?”—targeting overall lessons and impactful moments from the service. These questions encouraged participants to describe their feelings, thoughts, and learning in relation to empathy and caring for patients. A total of 116 reflective journal entries (one per participant) were collected.

We analyzed the qualitative data using a thematic analysis approach, following established guidelines for rigor in qualitative research. Two researchers (THL and KYC) independently analyzed the reflection texts. First, they engaged in repeated reading of the narratives to immerse and familiarize themselves with the content. Next, they performed initial open coding, assigning codes to meaningful units of text (phrases or sentences) that captured distinct ideas or experiences. The researchers compared and discussed these preliminary codes, and through an iterative process, they organized codes into candidate themes and subthemes. A codebook was developed to define each code and ensure consistent application. We used Dedoose® software (SocioCultural Research Consultants, Los Angeles, CA) to aid in coding and organizing the data. To ensure reliability, both researchers coded all journal responses and then compared their coding. Inter-rater reliability was high; Cohen’s kappa was approximately 0.90, indicating almost perfect agreement in coding. Code discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. We also examined co-occurrence of codes to explore relationships between concepts (for example, whether descriptions of feeling “touched” co-occurred with mentions of role models or specific activities). Through continuous refinement and review, prominent themes were identified that captured the essence of participants’ experiences. Each theme was defined and supported by illustrative quotes from the journals. We finalized a thematic structure encompassing major themes and subthemes, which we present in the Results. The qualitative analysis was conducted inductively, allowing themes to emerge from the data rather than being imposed a priori, though our interest was in mechanisms of empathy development (e.g., perspective-taking, affective empathy, altruism), which we indeed looked for in the data. The final thematic findings were translated into English (where original responses were in Chinese) for reporting purposes, with care taken to preserve the meaning of participants’ words.

Mixed-methods integration

We integrated quantitative and qualitative findings at the interpretation stage using a triangulation approach. Specifically, we examined whether changes in JSE-HP total and subscale scores aligned with qualitative themes derived from reflective journals, thereby generating a coherent explanatory account of how the program may influence empathy-related outcomes.

Ethical considerations

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Tzu Chi Hospital (IRB No. 09-XD-119). The IRB granted a waiver of written informed consent given minimal risk and anonymized data collection. Participation in the study was voluntary. All participants provided oral informed consent for their survey responses and reflections to be used for research purposes, with assurance of confidentiality. The community-based activities were part of the standard training program and posed minimal risk beyond everyday clinical interactions. In both the reflective writings and this report, individuals and patients served are described anonymously (any potentially identifying details were altered to ensure privacy). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations for research involving human participants.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 116 new medical professionals participated in the study, including 20 resident physicians and 96 nurses. Of the 30 PGY-1 residents invited, 20 (67%) completed the study measures. All resident participants were enrolled in general medicine tracks (not yet in specialty training), as this was the beginning of residency; 60% (12) were male. Among the 141 newly graduated nurses invited, 96 (68%) participated; 91.7% (88) were female, reflecting typical nursing demographics in Taiwan, and all were in their initial rotation through general ward nursing. These residents and nurses worked together in interprofessional teams during the camp activities.

Despite differences in training background, the two groups demonstrated comparable baseline empathy scores. Baseline (pre-camp) JSE-HP scores were 115.9 ± 15.2 (mean ± SD) for residents and 113.1 ± 14.3 for nurses. This difference was not statistically significant, suggesting similar starting levels of self-reported empathy. Table 1 summarizes the demographic data and baseline scores.

Quantitative outcomes: empathy scores pre- and post-intervention

After the one-day newcomer camp, participants exhibited a significant increase in empathy as measured by the JSE-HP (Table 2). Among resident physicians, the total JSE-HP score rose from 115.9 ± 15.2 before the intervention to 123.0 ± 11.6 after the intervention, representing a large effect size (r = 0.55) that was statistically significant (p = 0.013). Similarly, the nurses exhibited an increase in mean JSE-HP score from 113.1 ± 14.3 to 120.5 ± 14.6, corresponding to an even larger effect size (r = 0.62, p < 0.001). When both groups were combined for overall analysis (n = 116), the total empathy score increased from 113.6 ± 14.4 to 120.9 ± 14.1, which was highly significant (p < 0.001).

Importantly, empathy improvements were observed not only in the aggregate scores but also across key dimensions of empathy. For resident physicians, the subscale “Compassionate Care” increased significantly with a large effect size (mean pre 45.2 ± 10.5 to post 50.0 ± 4.6, r = 0.55, p = 0.012). This suggests that residents felt a greater inclination towards caring and compassionate attitudes after the intervention. The “Perspective Taking” subscale for residents increased slightly (62.2 ± 5.6 to 63.3 ± 4.9); however, this change was not statistically significant (p = 0.26). The “Standing in the Patient’s Shoes” subscale (which has fewer items and often yields lower scores) also rose modestly (8.6 ± 4.1 to 9.8 ± 4.3) without reaching significance (p = 0.25). Thus, for residents, the main quantitative gain was in the compassionate, patient-caring aspect of empathy.

For nurses, improvements were significant across all three JSE-HP components. Perspective Taking increased from 60.4 ± 6.4 to 62.8 ± 6.1 (p < 0.001); Compassionate Care from 43.7 ± 8.9 to 47.4 ± 9.1 (p < 0.001); and Standing in Patient’s Shoes from 9.0 ± 3.2 to 10.2 ± 3.4 (p < 0.001). Although the absolute changes in subscale means were modest (generally 1 to 3 points), the medium-to-large effects (r = 0.42–0.60) indicate that nurses felt that they had improved in their understanding of patients’ perspectives, level of compassionate engagement, and ability to put themselves in patients’ situations after the service experience.

In addition to the JSE-HP, participants’ feedback ratings underscored the perceived value of each component of the program (Table 3). On a 7-point scale (7 = most effective), the Role Modeling session received the highest average rating (6.3 ± 0.8), with 95.7% of participants assigning a positive impact rating (defined as a score in the upper range, e.g., ≥ 5 out of 7). The Experiential Home Visit component had a mean rating of 6.2 ± 1.0, with 89.7% positive responses, and the Reflective Practice session had a mean of 5.9 ± 1.2, with 83.6% positive.

Emergent themes related to community-based activities in the Newcomer camp

Benefiting Society: Participants reflected on the broader meaning of their service, often framing it in terms of social responsibility and altruism. They became more aware of social inequity, noting the stark disparities between their own lives and those of the patients they visited; this awareness prompted empathy and gratitude. For many, witnessing extreme poverty firsthand was an eye-opening experience: “It was the first time I saw such a dirty house; it was hard to imagine people could live in such an environment, and I realized how fortunate I am,” wrote one participant (Case No. 58, Nurse, Female), upon seeing a chronically ill patient living in squalid conditions. This recognition of inequality helped participants adopt the perspective of underserved individuals. Participants also described developing a sense of unselfishness and commitment to helping others. They expressed an altruistic motivation to use their role as healthcare providers to benefit those in need: “When we see a person who is both poor and ill, we should reflect on whether there are aspects of their environment in which we can help,” one resident (Case No. 67, Resident, Female) stated, indicating a lasting intention to act with compassion beyond the program. Many wrote about feeling “inspired” or hopeful, recognizing that even small acts of service can make a difference. One nurse (Case No. 108, Nurse, Female) noted, “I feel very touched. I can do my part to help those who need help!” This emotional uplift (“Being inspired”) reflects how engaging in service rekindled their idealism and sense of purpose in medicine. Under this theme, the newcomers connected their experience to a larger altruistic ethos, seeing their work as benefiting society and reaffirming why they entered a caring profession.Cultivating Empathy: This theme captured the internal changes related to empathy skills and feelings. Participants explicitly described gains in cognitive empathy (perspective-taking) as well as affective empathy. They learned to better understand others’ viewpoints and needs: “Knowing a little bit about the home care family beforehand, and then visiting them, you are better able to know what they truly need,” wrote one resident, (Case No. 63, Resident, Female), illustrating how learning about patients’ lives enabled perspective-taking. Through direct interaction, they practiced stepping into the patients’ shoes mentally, imagining what it is like to struggle with poverty and illness, thereby strengthening their cognitive empathy. Many also reported experiencing strong affective reactions, feeling compassion and sorrow for the patients’ plights. “Behind every case we cared for, there is really a lot of painful history that we didn’t know about,” one nurse (Case No. 101, Nurse, Female) reflected, indicating that hearing patients’ stories and seeing their hardship elicited heartfelt empathy. Some described tearing up or feeling a pang of pain when confronted with the patients’ suffering, which signifies affective empathy (emotional resonance). These emotional experiences, while difficult, were often cited as important turning points that “opened my heart” and deepened their commitment to care. By combining understanding and emotion, participants cultivated empathy within themselves and learning to integrate intellectual insight into patients’ conditions with genuine compassion.Learning from Service: Participants highlighted the learning process, attributing much of their growth to two key factors: role modeling and hands-on experience. The presence of inspiring role models was frequently mentioned. Many were impressed by the dedication and empathy demonstrated by the volunteer leaders. “Every volunteer’s contribution is made with love. People who do not even know the patient are willing to do so much to help him, just to improve his living environment,” observed one nurse (Case No. 56, Nurse, Female), referring to the example set by senior volunteers. Witnessing such altruistic behavior in action provided a concrete model for the kind of empathetic, selfless clinician they aspire to become. Participants learned that empathy is not just a feeling but an active endeavor. The volunteers “taught” empathy by example, showing how to translate compassion into service. The experiential learning aspect (actually visiting patients’ homes and engaging directly) was described as an unparalleled lesson. “You’ll be able to empathize more when you actually visit and get to know [the patient] in person,” a nurse (Case No. 30, Nurse, Female) wrote, emphasizing that no classroom could replicate the insight gained from real-world encounters. Through practical experience, participants absorbed the “spirit of home care” and realized the impact of environmental and social factors on patient well-being. They mentioned that doing simple tasks like cleaning a cluttered room or delivering necessities taught them humility and empathy in a way that lectures could not. Many commented that the combination of seeing and doing made the learning “stick.” This theme underscores that participants learned from service itself: the structured program allowed them to derive lessons about empathy through both observation (role modeling) and action (experiential learning).

Benefiting Society: Participants reflected on the broader meaning of their service, often framing it in terms of social responsibility and altruism. They became more aware of social inequity, noting the stark disparities between their own lives and those of the patients they visited; this awareness prompted empathy and gratitude. For many, witnessing extreme poverty firsthand was an eye-opening experience: “It was the first time I saw such a dirty house; it was hard to imagine people could live in such an environment, and I realized how fortunate I am,” wrote one participant (Case No. 58, Nurse, Female), upon seeing a chronically ill patient living in squalid conditions. This recognition of inequality helped participants adopt the perspective of underserved individuals. Participants also described developing a sense of unselfishness and commitment to helping others. They expressed an altruistic motivation to use their role as healthcare providers to benefit those in need: “When we see a person who is both poor and ill, we should reflect on whether there are aspects of their environment in which we can help,” one resident (Case No. 67, Resident, Female) stated, indicating a lasting intention to act with compassion beyond the program. Many wrote about feeling “inspired” or hopeful, recognizing that even small acts of service can make a difference. One nurse (Case No. 108, Nurse, Female) noted, “I feel very touched. I can do my part to help those who need help!” This emotional uplift (“Being inspired”) reflects how engaging in service rekindled their idealism and sense of purpose in medicine. Under this theme, the newcomers connected their experience to a larger altruistic ethos, seeing their work as benefiting society and reaffirming why they entered a caring profession.

Discussion

This exploratory mixed-methods study suggests that a brief, well-designed community-based educational intervention may be a feasible approach to activate and support empathy-related attitudes among new medical professionals, including both fresh resident physicians and novice nurses. Quantitatively, participation in a one-day community-based camp led to a measurable increase in empathy scores on the JSE. This finding is noteworthy given prior reports of declines in empathy levels during the transition from training to practice [14–17]. Our results suggest that empathy can also be restored or strengthened through targeted educational experiences at the early career stage. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to demonstrate empathy improvement in first-year residents and new nurses via a structured program, addressing a gap in the empathy-training literature that has largely focused on medical students.

The qualitative insights from participants’ reflections shed light on the mechanisms by which the community-based experience nurtured empathy. Participants described a transformative learning process involving perspective expansion, emotional connection, and moral motivation, aligning closely with theoretical frameworks of empathy development. According to social psychology, empathy has both cognitive and affective components [3]. In our study, cognitive empathy (perspective-taking) was fostered as participants confronted realities of poverty and illness outside their usual frame of reference. Many realized the extent of social inequities and mentally “stood in the patients’ shoes”, which helped them understand and contextualize patients’ struggles. This kind of perspective-taking is known to generate empathic concern and reduce biases [24] At the same time, the affective component of empathy was engaged: seeing patients’ living conditions and hearing their stories triggered genuine compassion among the newcomers. Rather than becoming numb, participants allowed themselves to feel what the patients felt, which was a critical step in developing deep empathy. Crucially, participants then processed these cognitive and emotional experiences through reflection, which seemed to consolidate an altruistic motivation—a pure selfless desire to help others. Indeed, participants frequently expressed that the experience renewed their commitment to patient service and humanitarian values. Empathy thus moved beyond a transient emotion to a motivation for compassionate care.

Our findings were consistent with existing literature, and we made discoveries. Previous community-based service-learning studies in health professions education have reported improvements in students’ empathy and attitudes towards the underserved population. For example, a study in Singapore revealed that medical students who participated in a structured community service program developed increased empathy towards migrant workers and improved communication skills [28]. Our results mirror the revelations by these students and further extend them to practicing clinicians. The fact that our resident physicians and nurses, who have completed formal education, still benefited significantly from community-based education indicates that empathy training should not cease at graduation. It supports the idea of continuing empathy development into residency and professional onboarding. Additionally, a prior study revealed that Taiwanese medical students with more preclinical service-learning experience demonstrated higher empathy during clinical clerkships [31]. Our intervention essentially provided a concentrated dose of such experience at the start of clinical practice, which in turn may boost empathic dispositions as the trainees progress in their careers.

Our qualitative themes align with established best practices in empathy education. First, the influence of role models was clearly evident. Participants were deeply inspired by observing senior volunteers who embodied compassion. This finding supports the literature that role modeling is a key factor in transmitting professional virtues, including empathy [28]. Residents and nurses often develop behavioral attitudes by observing their mentors; in our program, the mentors happened to be humanitarian volunteers and senior caregivers who set an empathetic tone. Second, experiential learning in an authentic community setting proved invaluable. By directly engaging with patients in their own environment outside the hospital, participants gained insights impossible to glean from simulations or lectures. This echoes findings from student-run free clinics, where students report increased empathy and understanding of social determinants of health after direct service experiences [39]. Our study provides analogous evidence for early-career professionals—real patient contact in challenging settings is a powerful empathy teacher. Third, reflection played a crucial role. Guided reflection allowed participants to articulate and thus better internalize what they learned and felt. Reflection is widely recognized as critical in converting experience into learning [40]. We suspect that without the reflective component, the impact on empathy might have been less enduring or clear to the participants themselves. The reflective journals and group discussions gave them the opportunity to make meaning of their service—for instance, to transform a feeling of pity into a resolve to help, or an observation of inequity into a commitment to advocate. This meaning-making is essential for long-term attitudinal change.

Our findings also suggest differences between the physician and nurse participants that merit discussion. Although both groups improved in total empathy, the nurse group demonstrated significant gains across cognitive and emotional dimensions, whereas the residents’ gains were more concentrated in the compassionate attitude domain. One possible explanation is that the new nurses, having less prior clinical exposure than the residents, had more to gain in perspective-taking; many might not have fully appreciated patients’ home contexts during nursing school, so the home visits were revelatory and substantially redirected their cognitive empathy. In contrast, the residents likely had some exposure to patient perspectives during medical school (e.g., family visits, community medicine rotations), which may suggest that they started the program with a relatively higher level of perspective-taking, making additional gains harder to detect statistically in our small sample.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

In our study, while the JSE-HP largely measures the cognitive orientation of empathy, our qualitative findings capture the activation of affective empathy and the resulting compassion and altruistic motivation. Quantitatively, the JSE-HP subscale “Compassionate Care” increased in both residents and nurses, which aligned with qualitative descriptions of empathic concern, emotional resonance with patients’ suffering, and intentions to help. Among nurses, significant gains in “Perspective Taking” and “Standing in the Patient’s Shoes” corresponded to reflections highlighting social inequity awareness, perspective expansion, and the unique learning afforded by home visits. In contrast, residents showed a more concentrated quantitative change, which may reflect limited power due to a relatively small sample size (n = 20) and/or higher baseline exposure; nevertheless, residents’ narratives similarly emphasized perspective taking and compassionate attitudes. Together, these convergent data suggest that the program may influence both cognitively oriented clinical empathy and compassion-related motivations.

Limitations

We acknowledge two major limitations to our study. First, the lack of a control or comparison group limits our ability to attribute the empathy increase solely to the intervention. Because outcomes were assessed immediately after a one-day program and relied on self-report, alternative explanations include novelty effects, social desirability, and heightened emotional arousal following exposure to disadvantaged living conditions. Nevertheless, participants’ reflections provided convergent evidence describing specific cognitive (perspective-taking) and affective (empathic concern) processes and intentions for future practice, which strengthens interpretability beyond a purely reactive mood effect. Second, our post-test likely captures immediate empathic activation and shifts in empathic attitudes rather than durable changes in empathic disposition. Future studies should include delayed follow-up (e.g., 3–6 months) and, ideally, behavioral or patient-reported outcomes to evaluate sustainability and clinical relevance.

Our findings encourage medical educators and hospital administrators to incorporate community-based educational program, informed by service-learning principles, into early career training. A structured day of community service, partnered with deliberate reflection and mentorship, can jump-start empathy in new clinicians and potentially counteract the known empathy declines of training. The intervention we describe is logistically feasible, leverages community partnerships, and can be integrated into orientation programs. Key components for replication include involving inspiring role models, ensuring meaningful contact with underserved patients, and facilitating reflection afterward.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that empathy in early-career medical professionals, including doctors and nurses, can be actively nurtured through a carefully designed community-based educational experience. After participating in a one-day service camp focused on caring for the underprivileged, these medical professionals exhibited greater empathy on objective measures and articulated a richer understanding of patients’ lives, deeper compassionate feelings, and stronger altruistic motivations. Key mechanisms of empathy development identified include enhanced perspective-taking, affective empathy, and commitment to altruism, all facilitated by the combination of inspirational role models, hands-on patient engagement, and reflective practice. These findings align with and extend prior research in medical education, underscoring that empathy is a professional competency that can be cultivated beyond the classroom and even after formal schooling. We recommend that healthcare training programs incorporate community-based education and reflection opportunities into early residency and nursing orientation, as a strategy to enhance empathy and sustain compassionate care in clinical practice.
