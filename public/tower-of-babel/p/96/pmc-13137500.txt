Metagenomic sequencing reveals the dynamic changes of pig gut fungal composition following the ages and identifies fungal species associated with diarrhea in piglets
Authors: Guanyue Wei, Min Liu, Lusheng Huang, Congying Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fungi are crucial components of the pig gut microbiome, influencing host immunity and metabolism. However, the investigation about gut fungi via metagenomic sequencing remains challenging due to analytical complexity. Here, we characterized pig gut fungal profiles using 750 metagenomes collected from public repositories and our previous datasets based on a comprehensive collection of fungal reference genomes, and revealed dynamic compositional changes of pig gut fungi from birth to market (7d, 14d, 21d, 28d, 35d, 70d, and 140d). Weaning significantly shaped the gut fungal community, affecting key fungi like Lachancea kluyveri and Kazachstania slooffiae. Inter-kingdom interaction analysis revealed significant correlations between fungi and bacteria, such as between L. kluyveri and Lactobacillus amylovorus (r = -0.48) and between K. slooffiae and Lactobacillus johnsonii (r = 0.75). We identified 87 diarrhea-associated fungal species at the significance threshold of LDA > 2.0 in three experimental piglet cohorts. In antibiotic-free piglets, fungal species from Saccharomyces and Aspergillus, some of which have been considered as potential probiotics, were enriched in healthy individuals, whereas in antibiotic-treated groups, Saccharomyces spp. and K. slooffiae were higher in diarrheal piglets. Notably, K. slooffiae were negatively correlated with the pathogen M. circinelloides, suggesting a protective role during gut dysbiosis. This study provides a foundation for developing fungal-based interventions to improve pig health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-026-00551-y.

Introduction

Mammalian gut harbors various microorganisms including bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, archaea, and parasites. Compared to the comprehensive studies on gut bacteria and viruses in recent years, significantly less studies have been reported on gut fungi. Although the abundance of fungi accounts for less than 1% of the total abundance of gut microbiota, they play crucial roles in the gut microbial ecosystem and host physiologies, such as immunity and metabolism [1, 2]. Recent studies have revealed the important contributions of gut fungi in maintaining gut microecological balance, regulating immune function, participating in metabolism and nutrition absorption, and preventing host diseases [3]. Moreover, gut fungal dysbiosis has been closely linked to various diseases including inflammatory bowel disease [4], diabetes [5], and obesity [6].

Compared to bacterial genomes, fungal genomes are significantly larger and often contain intron structures, which brings a big challenge for fungal genome assembly from metagenomic sequencing data using conventional methods used for bacteria. For example, in a recent study of gut microbiome in the Hadza population, only 17 fungal genomes were assembled from metagenomic sequencing data [7]. Thus, DNA barcoding of the fungal 18S rRNA gene and (or) the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions of ribosomal DNA (rDNA) has been broadly used to investigate the phylogenetic compositions and diversity of the gut mycobiome over the past decade [8, 9]. Recently, a landmark study on the gut mycobiome was dedicated to the large-scale culturing and sequencing of human gut fungi and constructed the largest reference genome database of fungi to date, which encompassed 760 gut fungal genomes across 48 families and 206 species, along with comprehensive metabolic functional capacity analyses [10]. This study not only uncovered the diversity of human gut fungal composition and their potential roles in common diseases, but also substantially expanded the existing genomic resources of gut fungi which provided critical reference data for further exploration of gut fungal community structure and functional capacities.

Although more and more studies have uncovered the importance of gut fungi to their host, most existing studies have focused on the human gut mycobiome. Our understanding to the composition, diversity, and physiological function roles of gut fungi in other mammals, such pigs, has been significantly less. Pigs, as important farm animals, have provided mass of meat for human consumption. Gut microbiota plays important roles in pig health and production traits [11, 12]. However, the systematic studies on pig gut fungi are still lacking. The composition and diversity of pig gut fungi and its interactions with other microbes, such as bacteria and viruses have largely unknown. Additionally, the effect of gut fungi on pig health and production traits have been less investigated. Moreover, most of previous studies on pig gut fungi have relied on the sequencing analysis of 18S rRNA gene and (or) ITS region, which provided limited phylogenetic resolution. And the inter-kingdom interactions between fungi and bacteria could not be explored at the species level. Metagenomic sequencing-based studies which could make up for the above defects have been rarely performed in pig gut mycobiome.

To systematically uncover the diversity and composition of pig gut fungi, and to investigate their interactions with other gut microbes as well as their associations with host health, we first established a non-redundant gut fungi reference genome database containing 3,156 high-quality assembled genomes. Based on 750 pig gut metagenomes collected from public repositories and our previous datasets, we comprehensively investigated the composition and abundance of fungi in pig gut microbiota, identified the factors influencing the pig gut fungal compositions, and especially characterized the dynamic changes of pig gut fungi from birth to market. Additionally, we explored the potential associations between gut fungal composition and post-weaning diarrhea (PWD) of piglets. This study not only expanded our knowledge about the composition and diversity of pig gut fungi with metagenomic sequencing data, but also provided an essential data source for further systematical studies on the influence of gut fungi in pig production traits.

Methods

Collection of fungal genome sequences and metagenomic sequencing data of pig gut microbiome

A total of 14,172 fungal genome sequences were downloaded from the NCBI GenBank (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/genbank/). All genome sequences were filtered to remove those genome sequences with low assembly quality at the thresholds of N50 < 10 kb and the number of contigs/scaffolds > 500 [13, 14]. Finally, a total of 7,645 fungal genomes with high quality were left for further analyses.

To characterize the gut fungal community structure and the distribution of fungal taxa in broad pig populations, we used 750 metagenomic sequencing data of pig gut microbiome (Supplementary Table S1) (Figure S1). In details, 500 metagenomic sequencing data from eight pig populations (breeds) were used in this study. This dataset covered a wide range of sample sources spanning various ages, sexes, geographical areas, breeds, domestication status, and gut locations [15]. The largest number of 325 metagenomic sequencing data in this dataset were from a mosaic population which was constructed by random hybridization amongst four Western breeds (Duroc, Landrace, Large White, and Pietrain) and four Chinese pig breeds (Bamaxiang, Erhualian, Laiwu, and Zang) [15]. Metagenomic sequencing data of four ileum content, 10 cecum content, and 297 feces samples from this mosaic population at the age of 240 days were included. A total of 160 metagenomic sequencing data of fecal samples that were collected at 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 70, and 140 days of ages from 24 Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire (DLY) pigs were downloaded from the public repository database [16] and used in this study for investigating the dynamic changes of gut fungal compositions. These 24 pigs were divided into three groups according to their weaning ages: Group 1: eight piglets weaned at the age of 14 days, group II: eight piglets weaned at the age of 21 days, and group III: eight piglets weaned at 28 days of age. To investigate the changes in the gut fungal compositions between healthy and diarrheal piglets, we obtained metagenomic sequencing data of feces samples from three piglet cohorts. The cohort #1 from Tao et al. (2022) contained 24 piglets including 12 healthy and 12 diarrheal piglets [17]. And the cohort #2 was comprised of 13 healthy and 11 diarrheal piglets from Zhou et al. (2022) [18]. All piglets from the cohorts #1 and #2 were Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire, weaned at the age of 21 days, and did not receive any antibiotic treatment before sample collection. The cohort #3 was comprised of 20 diarrheal piglets (14 males and 6 females) which were received antibiotic treatment (mequindox) prior to sampling and 22 healthy piglets (5 males and 17 females) from our previous study. All piglets were weaned at 28 days of age and were housed in a uniform farm under the same management.

Construction of fungal genome database

To construct a comprehensive and representative fungal genome database, all 7,645 high-quality fungal genome sequences were dereplicated using the options -nc 0.3 (coverage threshold of 30%), -pa 0.9 (primary cluster at 90%), -sa 0.99 (secondary cluster at 99%) with dRep (v3.4.3) [19]. To assess the quality of fungal genomes, we used EukCC (v 2.1.0) [20] with default parameters to evaluate the completeness and contamination rate of non-redundant genomes. Finally, we obtained 3,156 non-redundant and high-quality fungal genomes as a fungal reference genome set for further analyses (Figure S2).

Gene prediction and functional annotation of fungal genomes

Funannotate pipeline (v1.8.16) was used to predict protein-coding genes from 3,156 fungal reference genomes (https://funannotate.readthedocs.io/en/latest/) with default parameters, including the steps of sorting the assembly by length, repeat masking, and gene prediction. To understand the functions of fungal genes, the protein sequences were functionally annotated against multiple databases. We first assigned protein sequences to the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) databases (downloaded on June 2023) using kofascan (v1.3.0, -E 1e-5). The annotated KOs were then matched to KEGG pathways based on the pathway annotation files (ko0001.keg) downloaded from the KEGG official website [21]. Next, we used the hmmscan program of HMMER (v3.3.2) [22] to align protein sequences of each fungal genome to the dbCAN2 database (HMMdb v. 11) [23] for carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) annotation at the significance threshold of E-value < 1e-5. For all protein sequence alignments, the annotated hit(s) with the highest score (or the lowest E-value) was selected for downstream analyses.

Identification of fungal species in the pig gut microbiome and determination of their abundances using metagenomic sequencing data

Raw sequence reads of all 750 metagenomic sequencing data were first performed the quality-control using fastp (v2.5.1) (-W 4 -M 20 -n 5 -c -l 50 -w 3 --cut_tail) to remove adapters and low-quality bases. Host genomic DNA sequence contamination was removed by mapping reads to the pig reference genome (Sscrofa11.1) using Bowtie2 (v2.5.1)/Bwa (v0.7.17) with default parameters. And then, to avoid the interference of prokaryotic genomic sequences, clean sequence reads were aligned against 6,339 prokaryotic metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) of the pig gut microbiome constructed in our previous study [15]. All clean reads that were aligned to these prokaryotic microbial genomes were removed using bowtie2 (v2.5.1) with default parameters. The remaining clean reads were mapped to non-redundant fungal genomes to identify fungal taxa in tested samples using the bowtie2 (v2.5.1) with default parameters. Only those reads with unique alignments, aligned sequence > 80% of sequence length, and MAPQ values over 30 were retained. The count of clean reads mapped to each fungal genome was calculated, and the reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (RPKM) values that were obtained using Coverm (v0.6.1) to normalize the effect of different sequencing depth and genome size on the abundance of fungi were treated as the abundance of fungal taxa in tested samples (Figure S2). Fungal species detected in more than one sample were used for further analyses. The phylogenetic tree of fungal species was plotted by GraPhIan software (v1.1.3) with fungal species with a prevalence greater than 10%.

Prokaryotic taxonomic profiling of pig metagenomic sequencing data

To analyze the interaction between fungi and bacteria in the pig gut, we also determined the bacterial compositions of 160 metagenomic sequencing data of fecal samples from 24 DLY pigs and 90 metagenomes from three piglet cohorts with diarrhea phenotypes (described above). Metagenomic sequencing data were first performed the quality control and removed the contamination of host genomic sequence reads to obtain clean reads. And then, the compositions of prokaryotic communities and the abundances of microbial taxa in tested samples were determined using MetaPhlAn (v4.0.6) with default parameters [24].

Statistical analysis

The effects of different factors on the compositions of the pig gut fungi

To examine whether the scale of datasets was enough for capturing most of pig gut fungal species, we performed a Rarefaction analysis using all 750 metagenomes. To evaluate the effects of host factors including gender, age, and gut location on fungal composition, we used a total of 325 metagenomic sequencing data from the mosaic population which was raised under uniform housing conditions with the same commercial feeds. The contributions of these factors to the variation in gut fungal community structure were assessed based on Bray-Curtis distances using Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA). The analysis was implemented using the adonis2 function in the vegan R package with the multivariate formula distance ~ Gender + Age + Gut_Location (by = “terms”, permutations = 999).

Comparison of the α- and β-diversity of pig gut fungal composition

The α-diversity of gut fungal communities was assessed using the Shannon and Simpson indices which were calculated with the vegan package in R software (v4.3.3). A Wilcoxon rank-sum test was conducted to compare the α-diversity of the gut fungal compositions among ileum, cecum, and feces in the mosaic pig populations [15], among seven different age stages from birth to market in 160 metagenomic sequencing data from 24 DLY pigs [16], and between healthy and diarrhea groups in three piglet cohorts [17, 25]. The β-diversity was evaluated based on the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and visualized using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Group-wise differences in β-diversity were tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with the adonis function from the vegan package. Comparisons were conducted across different gut locations (ileum, cecum, and feces), developmental age stages, and health status (health vs. diarrhea). All visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package in R (v4.3.3).

Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis

To identify fungal taxa showing differential abundances between healthy and diarrheal piglets, across different gut locations, and between pre- and post-weaning stages, we performed a LEfSe analysis in three piglet cohorts with phenotype records of diarrhea [17, 25] using the LEfSe tool [26]. The analysis consisted of three steps: (1) a nonparametric factorial Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test to detect the features with significantly differential abundances; (2) a set of pairwise tests between subclasses using the unpaired Wilcoxon rank sum test to further assess the consistency of these features; and (3) linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to estimate the effect size of each differentially abundant feature [26]. P value of 0.05 and an LDA score of 2.0 were set as the significance threshold.

Establishing the interaction network between fungi and bacteria in the pig gut with the Sparse Correlations for Compositional Data (SparCC) analysis

To investigate dynamic changes of the interaction networks between bacteria and fungi in the pig gut microbiome from pre-weaning to post-weaning stage, the SparCC network was constructed with fungal and bacterial species having ≥ 20% of prevalence in 160 metagenomic sequencing data from 24 DLY pigs [16] using a fast and parallelizable implementation of the SparCC algorithm in FastSpar under the default parameters [27, 28]. This implementation showed the feature of an unbiased p-value estimator that ensured the swift and reliable correlation estimation. The 1000 times of bootstrap were employed to infer pseudo p-values. The networks were constructed at 14 and 21 days of ages for piglets weaned at the age of 14 days, 21 and 28 days of ages for piglets weaned at the age of 21 days, and 28 and 35 days of ages for piglets weaned at the age of 28 days using species-level abundance data, and were visualized by Cytoscape (v3.10.1). To identify potential synergistic or antagonistic interactions between gut bacteria and diarrhea-associated fungal species, we also constructed co-abundance networks between gut bacteria and diarrhea-associated fungal species in diarrheal piglets and healthy piglets, respectively, using the same method described above.

Result

A comprehensive fungal genome database and its characterization of taxonomic compositions and potential functional capacities

A total of 14,172 fungal genome sequences were collected. After filtering low-quality and redundant genome sequences, a final set of 3,156 high-quality and non-redundant fungal genomes was obtained. We evaluated the quality of these genomes using EukCC. Among them, over 90.97% (2,871 genomes) exhibited > 90% completeness, and 94.23% (2,974 genomes) had contamination rates below 10% (Fig. 1A). Compared with that in previous studies [29–33], the fungal genome database constructed in this study included a significantly higher number of fungal genomes. These 3,156 fungal genomes were classified into nine phyla. Ascomycota (76.24%), Basidiomycota (18.31%), Mucoromycota (2.19%), Microsporidia (1.01%), and Chytridiomycota (1.01%) were the predominant phyla and accounted for 98.86% of 3,156 genomes (Fig. 1B). At high resolution taxonomic levels, these 3,156 fungal genomes covered 49 classes, 140 orders, 366 families, 810 genera, and 2,370 species. Filamentous fungi within Ascomycota, such as Fusarium (158 genomes), Aspergillus (149 genomes), Penicillium (130 genomes), and Alternaria (70 genomes) were the predominant genera in the fungal genome database. Several well-known fungal genera, such as Saccharomyces (39 genomes) and Candida (27 genomes) were also contained in this database (Supplementary Table S2).

To ensure the comparability and accuracy for potential functional capacities of fungal genomes for downstream analysis, we performed uniform functional annotations for non-redundant fungal genomes. A total of 30,306,162 fungal genes were obtained from 3,156 fungal genomes, of which 29,716,374 genes were predicted to be protein-coding genes. Among them, 23,654,368 genes could be classified into 444 distinct KEGG pathways in six functional pathway modules (Metabolism, human diseases, genetic information processing, cellular processes, organismal systems, and environmental information processing). The KEGG pathways with a large number of fungal genes mainly included Neurodegenerative Disease (2,457,770 genes), Signal Transduction (1,596,766 genes), Carbohydrate Metabolism (1,434,345 genes), Amino Acid Metabolism (1,387,362 genes), Transport and Catabolism (1,271,617 genes), and Cell Growth and Death (1,097,897 genes) (Fig. 1C). Given the important role of fungi in carbohydrate degradation and utilization [34], we further analyzed carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) genes in 3,130 fungal genomes (26 genomes were excluded due to low completeness). In total, 1,657,542 CAZyme genes were identified. Among them, the most abundant classes were Glycoside Hydrolases (GHs, 638,460 genes), Glycosyl Transferases (GTs, 392,740), and Auxiliary Activities (AAs, 316,558) (Fig. 1D).

A landscape of pig gut fungal communities based on 750 metagenomes

Based on the fungal reference genome database constructed in this study, we collected 750 metagenomic sequencing data of luminal content and feces samples from pigs spanning various ages, genders, geographical areas, breeds, domestication status, and health status to characterize the composition and diversity of pig gut fungal communities. Clean reads of metagenomic sequencing data were mapped to the fungal reference genome database to determine the fungal composition and the abundances of fungal taxa in each sample. Fungal reads were successfully detected in 745 out of 750 metagenomes (99.3%). There were five metagenomes that were not detected any mappable fungal reads and were excluded from downstream analyses. Overall, fungal reads accounted for a very small proportion in total clean reads of a metagenome with an average of 0.036% (ranging from 0 to 5.88%). Specifically, 95.87% (719/750) of metagenomes exhibited < 0.1% of fungal reads, while only 4.13% (31/750) of metagenomes had fungal reads > 0.1% (Figure S3, Supplementary Table S3). The numbers of clean reads mapped to fungal genomes in 745 metagenomes exhibited an approximately normal distribution (Fig. 2A). A total of 1,298 fungal genomes belonging to 1,049 fungal species were detected in 745 metagenomes. An average of 39 fungal species were identified for each sample with the range from one to 128 species. There were five metagenomes that were not identified any fungal species. Among 1,049 fungal species, 791 species were present in at least two samples and retained for further analyses. These 791 fungal species belonged to seven phyla, 188 families, and 325 genera (Supplementary Table S4). Rarefaction analysis found that the number of detected fungal genomes reached saturation when the sample size exceeded 400, suggesting that the majority of gut fungal species were captured (Fig. 2B). Notably, three phyla of Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Mucoromycota dominated the pig gut fungal compositions and occupied more than 99.0% of mapped fungal sequence reads (Fig. 2C). Among 791 fungal species identified, 110 species showed a prevalence > 10%, and only 42 species were identified in > 20% metagenomes. A taxonomic tree representing fungal species with ≥ 10% prevalence along with their total abundances across samples is shown in Fig. 2D. The top ten most abundant fungal species in the pig gut included K. slooffiae, Saccharomyces paradoxus, Mucor racemosus, L. kluyveri, Zalaria obscura, Hypoxylon rickii, Mucor circinelloides, Scedosporium apiospermum, Ustilaginoidea virens, and Kazachstania bovina (Fig. 2D). Especially, K. slooffiae was widely distributed in various pig populations and detected in 83.22% (620/745) of all tested samples (Fig. 2E). Additionally, the abundance of K. slooffiae varied among tested samples (Fig. 2F).

To explore the population heterogeneity in the gut fungal compositions, we investigated the phylogenetic composition of gut fungi in eight different pig cohorts using 495 metagenomic sequencing data from our previous study [15]. Although K. slooffiae was the predominant fungal species in seven (Duroc, Licha, Berkshire × Licha, Laiwu, Tibetan, Wild boars, and Mosaic population) out of eight pig populations with an average of 20.9–59.1% of relative abundance, distinct fungal compositions were observed among different pig populations (Fig. 3A). For example, S. paradoxus were identified with high abundance in six out of eight pig cohorts; The abundance of Blumeria hordei was ranked in the top eight in five out of eight pig cohorts; Hypoxylon rickii, Z. obscura, and L. kluyveri were detected in the top eight lists of four out of eight pig populations. The abundance of Malassezia restricta was ranked in the top eight in both Berkshire × Licha and Licha cohorts. In addition, Pichia kudriavzevii, Farysia itapuensis, Trichosporon ovoides, Phaffia rhodozyma, Sporisorium graminicola, M. racemosus, Vavraia culicis, and Naumovozyma castellii were specifically identified in the top eight abundance lists of Berkshire × Licha, Duroc, Large White, Laiwu, Licha, Mosaic population, Wild boars, and Tibetan pigs, respectively (Fig. 3A). However, in Large White piglets, the most abundant gut fungal species was S. paradoxus (Fig. 3A). Compared with that in commercial and Chinese indigenous pigs, more fungal species, such as Clavispora fructus, Lachancea fermentati, and Vavraia culicis were specifically identified in the gut of Wild boars although their abundances were low.

The factors affecting the composition of pig gut fungi

Metagenomic sequencing data used in this study were obtained from samples covering different pig cohorts, different ages, multiple gut locations, and both genders. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) indicated significant variations in the gut fungal composition across eight pig populations (Fig. 3B). However, we did not perform direct comparisons of gut fungal composition across cohorts because multiple confounding variables, such as pig breed, housing environment, feed, and geographical location mixed together and made it difficult to identify the primary factors driving population-level variation. Instead, we assessed the contributions of age, gut location, and sex to the variation of gut fungal compositions in pigs of the Mosaic population raised under uniform housing conditions with the same commercial feeds using the adonis2 function from the vegan package in R (Supplementary Table S5). The analysis revealed that both age and gut location had significant effects on gut fungal composition based on Bray-Curtis distances (PERMANOVA, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3C), explaining 2.22% and 2.13% of the total variation, respectively. In contrast, pig genders showed no significant effect on the composition of pig gut fungi. The results about the effects of gut location and age on the pig gut fungal composition were described in detail as follows.

Distinct fungal compositions among ileum, cecum, and feces samples

The mosaic pig population was raised under a uniform housing condition and provided the same commercial feeds. This provided an opportunity to assess the variation of gut fungal compositions across different gut locations. Thirteen pigs had metagenomic sequencing data available from at least two of three gut locations: ileum content (n = 4), cecum content (n = 10), and fecal samples (n = 13). This allowed us to directly compare fungal compositions among three gut locations. The α-diversity analysis based on the Shannon, Simpson, and Richness indices showed that the cecum had the higher diversity of fungal composition than the ileum and feces, although only the Richness achieved statistical significance level (P < 0.005) (Fig. 4A). The PCoA also indicated the distinct fungal community structures (β-diversity) of fungal compositions among three gut locations (Fig. 4B). We further identified fungal species that were differentially enriched in each of three gut locations. A total of 16 species were enriched in the ileum, 19 species in the cecum, and 9 species in feces. (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Table S6).

Dynamic changes of pig gut fungal compositions from birth to market

A previous study investigated the effect of weaning age on the development of pig gut bacteria in a commercial pig population [16]. Here, we used this dataset to evaluate the dynamic changes of pig gut fungal compositions following the ages. In brief, twenty-four experimental pigs could be equally divided into three groups according to their weaning ages of 14, 21, and 28 days. Fecal samples were collected from these pigs at 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 70, and 140 days of age (n = 160) and performed metagenomic sequencing [16]. As the results, the PCoA based on Bray-Curtis distance revealed that weaning was a key event influencing gut fungal community structure. Distinct gut fungal compositions were observed between samples collected before and after weaning. However, the gut fungal compositions (β-diversity) among ages from birth to weaning and among ages from weaning to the market exhibited the high similarity (Fig. 5A). This dynamic change pattern of the gut fungal compositions was observed in all three pig groups weaned at different ages (Fig. 5A).

We then examined the dynamic changes of the α-diversity of pig gut fungal composition following the ages. We observed a trend that the α-diversity (both Shannon and Simpson indices) decreased from seven days of age to the age of 35 days, and then significantly increased and remained stable from 70 to 140 days of age. The lowest α-diversity was found at the age of 35 days, and the samples collected from piglets (7 ~ 35 days of age) had significantly lower α-diversity of fungal compositions than the samples harvested from adult pigs (70 and 140 days) (Fig. 5B, Figure S4A). It was strange that the α-diversity was high in fecal samples of 28-day-old piglets from the group weaned at 21 days of age. However, large deviation was observed in these samples harvested at this age stage.

We further identified differential fungal taxa enriched at each age stage. At the phylum level, a total of five phyla were identified in 160 tested samples. Ascomycota was the most abundant phylum across different ages, followed by Basidiomycota. These two phyla accounted for 95.96% ~ 99.99% of the total abundance of gut fungi in tested samples. The abundance of Ascomycota was lowest at the age of 14 days. And then, its abundance increased with age and achieved the highest abundance at the age of 35 days. Finally, its abundance decreased again following the age. The reverse tendency was observed for the abundance of Basidiomycota (Figure S4B). At the species level, as described above, weaning was an important event influencing the relative abundance of pig gut fungal species. Several species including L. kluyveri, Trichosporon ovoides, B. hordei, and S. paradoxus were significantly enriched at all age stages before weaning. In contrast, K. slooffae was highly abundant at all ages after weaning although its abundance decreased at 70 and 140 days. Additionally, Candida orthopsilosis and Yarrowia bubula were also enriched at all age stages after weaning (Fig. 5C). L. kluyveri and K. slooffae became two most important markers distinguishing samples before and after weaning.

Considering the dramatic changes of gut fungal composition across the weaning, but relative stability among pre-weaning age stages and among post-weaning ages, we further identified differential fungal species between two age stages around weaning (14 vs. 21days of age, 21 vs. 28 days of age, and 28 vs. 35 days of age in three pig groups, respectively). For piglets weaned at 14 days of age, 20 differential fungal species were detected between 14 and 21 days of age, including eight species enriched at the age of 14 days and 12 species having high abundances at the age of 21 days. Correspondingly, 10 and 13 differential fungal species were identified in piglets weaned at 21 and 28 days, respectively (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table S7). The dynamic changes in the average abundances of these differential fungal species at all seven ages from birth to the market are shown in Fig. 6. L. kluyveri exhibited significantly high abundance at all pre-weaning age stages, whereas K. slooffiae showed markedly increased abundance at the post-weaning age stages. Finally, Procrustes analysis was employed to assess the correlations in temporal abundance shifts between fungi and bacteria. The significant correlations between the changes in fungal abundances and the shift in bacterial abundances were only identified in two piglet groups weaned at 21 and 28 days of age (Figure S5).

Dynamic changes of the interaction networks between bacteria and fungi in the pig gut microbiome from pre-weaning to post-weaning stage

To explore the potential interactions between bacteria and fungi in the pig gut microbiome, and to investigate the dynamic changes of the interaction networks from pre-weaning to post-weaning stage, we applied the SparCC algorithm to construct inter- and intra-kingdom co-abundance networks in three piglet groups weaned at the age of 14, 21, and 28 days. The interaction networks were constructed in the datasets between two age stages around weaning. In piglets weaned at the age of 28 days, a total of 2,604 and 4,089 pairwise correlations were identified under the lenient threshold condition of P < 0.05 at the age of 28 and 35 days, respectively, suggesting the extensive interactions across the kingdoms. Among them, 31.34% and 26.14% of the correlations belonged to inter-kingdom correlations, highlighting an extensive interaction between bacteria and fungi in the pig guts. There were 25.63% (21 days) and 25.96% (28 days) of pairwise correlations belonging to inter-kingdom interactions in piglets weaned at the age of 21 days. In piglets weaned at 14 days of age, 39.69% (14 days) and 28.49% (21 days) of pairwise correlations were inter-kingdom interactions (Figure S6). To exclude potential false positives, all correlations with |correlation coefficient| > 0.3 in three pig groups are shown in Figure S7. Notably, L. kluyveri exhibited high node degrees in the co-abundance networks at the preweaning age and emerged as key hub species, while K. slooffiae and C. orthopsilosis were the key hub species in the post-weaning networks.

Here we deeply exhibited the strongly significant correlations between fungal and bacterial species or between different fungal species with |correlation coefficient| > 0.5 (Fig. 7). Among fungal taxa, L. kluyveri exhibited the highest number of significant correlations with bacterial species. In piglets weaned at 21 days, it was strongly and positively correlated with the bacterial species Escherichia coli (r = 0.76), Clostridium porci (r = 0.63), Phocaeicola faecicola (r = 0.70), and Phocaeicola plebeius (r = 0.56). Similar associations were observed in piglets weaned at 28 days, including positive correlations with E. coli (r = 0.69) and P. faecicola (r = 0.58). In contrast, L. kluyveri showed negative correlations with several bacterial species-level genome bins (SGBs) unknown taxa in piglets weaned at the age of 14 days. Especially, it was negatively associated with the core bacterial species L. amylovorus at pre-weaning stage of piglets weaned at the age of 28 days (r = -0.48). K. slooffiae which had the second highest number of significant correlations, showed predominantly negative associations with other fungal species at both pre- and post-weaning stages of piglets weaned at 21 days and at post-weaning stage of piglets weaned at 28 days. However, it exhibited positive correlations with Prevotella SGB1615 (r = 0.57 at the pre-weaning), Treponema porcinum (r = 0.51 at the pre-weaning), and two SGBs unknown taxa (r = 0.50 and 0.46 at the post-weaning) in piglets weaned at the age of 21 days. Particularly, it was positively associated with L. johnsonii (r = 0.75 at the post-weaning) in piglets weaned at the age of 28 days. This strongly positive correlation suggested potential synergistic interaction between K. slooffiae and L. johnsonii. We also observed several other interesting correlations. For example, B. hordei was negatively associated with K. slooffiae (r = -0.54) and Hungatella hathewayi (r = -0.51) at the pre-weaning stage of piglets weaned at the age of 21 and 14 days, but positively associated with E. coli (r = 0.61) at the post-weaning stage of piglets weaned at the age of 28 days. (Fig. 7).

Identification of gut fungal species associated with piglet diarrhea

Fungi play critical roles in host diseases through influencing the host immune response and gut homeostasis [35–37]. In this study, we identified gut fungal species associated with piglet diarrhea using metagenomic sequencing data from three piglet cohorts including two cohorts without treatment with antibiotics [17, 18] and one cohort treated with antibiotics. In two cohorts without treatment with antibiotics, we observed the population heterogeneity in both α- and β-diversity of gut fungal compositions between healthy and diarrheal piglets (Figure S8A). In the piglet cohort #1, there were no significant differences in the α- and β-diversity of gut fungal compositions between healthy and diarrheal piglets. However, in the piglet cohort #2, compared to healthy piglets, diarrheal piglets had significantly higher α-diversity (both Shannon and Simpson indices) and showed different gut fungal compositions (β-diversity) (Figure S8A). The abundances of K. slooffiae, S. paradoxus, and F. itapuensis were ranked in the top 10 in both piglet populations (Figure S8B). LEfSe analysis identified 12 and 13 fungal species showing differential abundances between healthy and diarrheal piglets in the piglet cohort #1 and cohort #2, respectively (Fig. 8A) (Supplementary Table S8). There were no differential fungal species shared in both piglet populations. This may be attributed to differences in gut fungal compositions between the two piglet populations (Figure S8C). However, we found that two fungal species belonging to Saccharomyces (S. paradoxus and S. cerevisiae) and three species belonging to Aspergillus (A. chevalieri, A. niger, and A. unguis) were significantly enriched in healthy piglets (Fig. 8A).

We additionally identified fungal species associated with piglet diarrhea in a piglet cohort comprising 20 diarrheal and 22 healthy piglets (cohort #3). Both diarrheal and healthy piglets received the antibiotics intervention. The results showed that both Shannon and Simpson indices were significantly higher in diarrheal piglets compared to healthy piglets (Figure S9A). PCoA based on the Bray-Curtis distance and the composition analysis of dominant fungal species revealed the distinct gut fungal compositions between diarrheal and healthy piglets (Figure S9). LEfSe analysis identified a total of 62 differential fungal species between diarrheal and healthy piglets (LDA > 2.0 and P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S8). Here we only showed those differential fungal species with LDA value > 3. A total of 16 fungal species were significantly enriched in healthy piglets with S. apiospermum showing the most significant association, while 14 fungal species had higher abundances in diarrhea piglets (Fig. 8B). Consistent with the observation in the piglet cohort #1 and cohort #2, six fungal species from Aspergillus showed significant enrichments in the gut of healthy piglets. However, three Saccharomyces spp. including S. cerevisiae, S. pastorianus and S. paradoxus, T. microellipsoides (Saccharomycetaceae), and K. slooffiae were enriched in diarrheal piglets (Fig. 8B). Of note, several pathogens or opportunistic pathogens including Candida albicans [38, 39], M. circinelloides [40], and M. racemosus [41] were enriched in feces samples of diarrhea piglets (Fig. 8B).

We further constructed co-abundance networks between bacteria and differential fungal species. At the significant threshold of correlation coefficient (r) > 0.3, in diarrheal piglets, four fungal species belonging to Saccharomycetaceae and P. rhodozyma were positively correlated with each other. But S. cerevisiae was negatively associated with Limosilactobacillus reuteri. Significantly positive correlations were observed between two pathogenic fungi of M. circinelloides and M. racemosus, and between M. circinelloides and Piptocephalis tieghemiana, but M. circinelloides was negatively associated with Lactobacillus amylovorus. Particularly, K. slooffiae showed a significant negative correlation with M. circinelloides (Fig. 8C). In healthy piglets, S. apiospermum which was most significantly enriched in healthy piglets was positively associated with L. reuteri, L. amylovorus, and L. johnsonii. However, S. apiospermum was negatively associated with K. slooffiae (Fig. 8C). Furthermore, Procrustes analysis was performed to evaluate the correlation between fungal and bacterial compositions in healthy and diarrheal piglets of all three piglet cohorts. Intriguingly, significant correlations were observed in healthy piglets of all three cohorts, while such correlation was absent in diarrheal piglets (Figure S10). This pattern was consistent regardless of antibiotic intervention.

Discussion

Fungi are important members of gut microbial ecosystem. Although there are several studies investigating pig gut fungal compositions based on the ITS or 18S rRNA gene sequencing [9, 42–44], compared to gut bacteria and viruses, fewer studies have systematically characterized the composition and diversity of pig gut fungi, and its relationships with host diseases using metagenomic sequencing data. In this study, we first developed a fungal genome database comprising a non-redundant collection of fungal reference genomes with broader taxonomic coverage and a greater number of genomes than those used in previous metagenomic studies [29–33]. Based on this fungal reference genome database and using metagenomic sequencing data, we systematically investigated the composition and dynamic changes of pig gut fungi from birth to the market, evaluated the factors influencing gut fungal composition, and investigated the interaction networks between bacteria and fungi in the pig gut microbiome. Especially, we identified fungal species related to piglet diarrhea. This study not only investigated the composition of pig gut fungi and its interactions with gut microbiota at the species level, but also gave important insights about the effect of gut fungi on pig diarrhea.

Compared with the previous studies on pig gut fungi, this study used a broad sample source including feces samples from different pig breeds and wild boars, feces samples from pigs at different ages from birth to the market, and lumen contents of ileum and cecum. This broad sample source significantly increased the diversity and ecological representativeness of pig gut fungal profiles. The rarefaction analysis suggested that the majority of fungal diversity has been captured (Fig. 2B). Our results provided a comprehensive overview of the core fungal taxa presented in the pig guts. In addition, compared to amplicon-based approaches, our metagenomic strategy not only enabled the higher-resolution taxonomic profiling of pig gut fungi, but also avoided the bias of PCR amplification on the detection and abundance determination of gut fungi. To our knowledges, this comprehensive pig gut fungal profiling at the species level is lacking in the majority of current literatures. This study offered improved insights into the gut fungal community structure and its potential relevance to host phenotypes. However, one of the major challenges for detecting gut fungal species with metagenomic sequencing data was the low abundances for most of fungal species in the pig gut microbiome. Low sequencing depth should restrict the detection of gut fungal species. Compared to the assembly-based analysis, mapping-based approach might facilitate to identify gut fungal species with low abundance using metagenomic sequencing data. Another deficiency with mapping-based approach was the closely related genomes in the fungal reference genome database. It should cause false positives in the detection of gut fungal species. In this study, 3,156 fungal reference genomes covered 2,370 species. Most of fungal species only had a representative genome. This should effectively avoid part of this bias.

Compared with previous studies about pig gut fungi, which focused on specific pig cohorts and gut location (mostly feces) [9, 42, 44], we uncovered the composition profiles of pig gut fungi with various sample sources from different pig breeds, ages and gut locations. Gut fungal compositions varied among different pig breeds or populations. This should be caused by host genetics, geographic locations, and environments because the samples used this study were from eight pig breeds (populations), and seven out of these eight pig populations were raised in different farms. However, K. slooffiae consistently emerged as the dominant fungal species. This was consistent with the previous reports that K. slooffiae was the core fungal species in the pig guts [45–48]. It exhibited the highest prevalence and relative abundance across most samples, and was the most abundant fungus in seven out of eight tested pig breeds (Fig. 3A). Strangely, its abundance was low in Large White pigs. One possible explanation was that the samples from Large White piglets were all collected at the pre-weaning stage. However, K. slooffiae is typically high abundance in post-weaning pigs [45]. As a core member of the pig gut mycobiome, K. slooffiae might play crucial roles in host health and metabolic processes. A recent study has highlighted its involvement in intestinal glycolytic metabolism via lysine desuccinylation [45]. We found that core fungi in the pig guts were completely different from core fungi in the human and mouse guts [3, 49]. This difference was reflected by the unique dominance of K. slooffiae in the pig guts, which should be caused by host species, intestinal environment, diets, and so on.

We found that age and gut location were the main factors influencing the variations of pig gut fungal composition. Consistent with previous observations in gut bacterial communities [50], distinct fungal compositions were found in different gut locations. Cecum exhibited the highest fungal diversity, whereas ileum showed the lowest diversity. This significant spatial heterogeneity was consistent with the results from the previous amplicon-based study [51] and a recent report which described the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of the porcine gut mycobiota by characterizing the mycobiome across different gastrointestinal locations [52], suggesting that fecal samples alone are insufficient to fully represent the spatial variation of gut mycobiota. Previous studies have investigated the longitudinal development of the pig gut microbiota from birth to market [53, 54]. However, these studies predominantly focused on bacteria [50]. In this study, we found that pig gut mycobiome was highly dynamic and unstable during early life, which was concordance with the observations in amplicon-based studies [44, 47, 51]. The diversity of gut fungal compositions changed like the V-shaped pattern, with the lowest α-diversity observed at 35 days of age. In addition, we found that weaning was the landmark event influencing gut fungal composition, especially causing the abundance changes of predominant fungal species, such as L. kluyveri and K. slooffiae. Harlow et al. (2023) also indicated that weaning transition rather than exogenous fungal supplementation was the key driver shaping porcine gastrointestinal fungal communities across developmental stages [52]. This should be caused by the dietary shifts from milk to solid feeds, weaning stress, and the development of immune system [55]. This result was also consistent with that obtained in the amplicon-based studies [44, 47, 51], highlighting the profound effect of weaning on porcine gut microbial composition. Notably, the highest α-diversity of gut fungal composition was found at the age of 70 and 140 days, indicating the maturation and stabilization of the fungal community. Several fungal species playing a critical role in the digestion of complex carbohydrates (e.g., S. paradoxus and Pichia kluyveri) [56, 57] or in modulating the immune system (e.g., S. cerevisiae and C. orthopsilosis) [58–60] were enriched in the gut of pigs at the age of > 70 days.

Although significant correlations between fungal and bacterial communities were only observed in piglets weaned at 21 and 28 days of age in the Procrustes analysis, we identified several significant correlations between fungal and bacterial species. For example, L. kluyveri showed strongly positive correlations with E. coli, but negative correlation with L. amylovorus. L. kluyveri is a budding yeast degrading sugars in the absence of oxygen. It makes a more efficient use of glucose for energy production [61]. L. amylovorus is able to break down glucose from starch and use it quickly for bacterial growth. Competitive utilization of glucose might result in a negative correlation between their abundances of L. kluyveri and L. amylovorus. Conversely, the positive correlation between L. kluyveri and E. coli suggested a potential metabolic cross-feeding relationship, where yeast fermentation byproducts or oxygen scavenging might create a favorable niche for Enterobacteriaceae [62]. These interpretations remain speculative and require further experimental validations. As another example about the interaction between bacterial and fungal species, K. slooffiae was positively correlated with L. johnsonii in abundances. Consistent with this, Kazachstania has been found to be positively correlated with Lactobacillus based on the amplicon analysis and co-culture experiments in previous studies [46, 48]. However, the possible mechanism about the interaction between K. slooffiae and L. johnsonii in the pig gut remain unclear and would need to be further investigated.

We identified dozens of fungal species associated with piglet diarrhea. In the piglet cohorts #1 and #2, both of which did not receive antibiotic treatment, S. paradoxus and S. cerevisiae were enriched in healthy piglets. Consistent with this result, previous studies have also documented the benefit effects of S. cerevisiae on weaned piglets, particularly its roles in alleviating diarrhea symptoms [63–66]. In addition, several Aspergillus species, including A. chevalieri, A. unguis, and A. niger were also enriched in healthy piglets. Interestingly, it has been reported that A. chevalieri has protective effects on mice with acute ulcerative colitis, and A. niger was also considered as probiotics [67]. The result suggested that these fungal species might be served as the potential biomarkers for diarrhea resistance in piglets. In the piglet cohort #3 with antibiotic intervention, we observed a consistent enrichment of six Aspergillus species in healthy piglets. In contrast, three Saccharomyces spp., T. microellipsoides (family Saccharomycetaceae), and K. slooffiae had higher abundance in diarrheal piglets compared with that in healthy piglets (Fig. 8B). Interestingly, a recent ITS-based study also reported a significant enrichment of S. cerevisiae and K. slooffiae in diarrheal piglets from a cohort without antibiotic intervention [51]. These consistent observations from different studies suggested that the expansion of these fungi might be a general feature of dysbiosis associated with diarrhea, rather than a specific result by antibiotic treatment. Another previous study reported a 10,000-fold increase in the colonization level of Saccharomyces boulardii in antibiotic-treated mice compared to non-treated controls [68]. Antibiotics could increase the overall fungal load in neonatal piglets potentially by altering bacterial composition and reducing interspecies competition [69]. Therefore, the observed enrichment of S. cerevisiae and K. slooffiae in diarrheal piglets might reflect their opportunistic overgrowth under gut microbial imbalance, rather than a direct causal role in pathogenesis. K. slooffiae has been suggested as a potential probiotics [46]. In this study, it was negatively correlated with pathogenic fungi (Fig. 8C). This inferred that it might exert indirect protective effects by suppressing specific pathogens or modulating bacterial communities. Whether its overgrowth under gut microbial imbalance would benefit the recovery of piglets from diarrhea should require further researches. Additionally, the interaction network analysis found that S. apiospermum which was most significantly enriched in healthy piglets was positively associated with L. reuteri, L. amylovorus, and L. johnsonii. All these three Lactobacillus spp. have been reported to play important roles in preventing piglet diarrhea [70–72]. Whether S. apiospermum promoted the colonization and growth of three Lactobacillus spp. or Lactobacillus spp. increased the abundance of S. apiospermum, remains to be elucidated.

Conclusions

We revealed a landscape of pig gut fungal communities using 750 metagenomes from public repositories and our previous datasets, and identified the significant effects of gut location and age on the pig gut fungal composition. Especially, we exhibited the dynamic changes in the pig gut fungal composition at different ages from birth to market, and found the distinct fungal compositions between pre- and post-weaning stages. We also uncovered the significant interactions between bacterial species and fungal species in the pig gut. Finally, we identified fungal species associated with diarrhea in piglets. However, several limitations should be addressed in the future studies. First, our method depended on the current availability of fungal genomes. More fungal genomes should be sequenced and integrated into the database. Second, piglet diarrhea-associated fungal species would be required further experimental validation, and the mechanism should need to be elucidated. Nevertheless, this study provided the important resource and method that was different from the amplicon sequencing-based approach for studying the pig gut fungal community. The identification of piglet diarrhea-associated fungal species provided important bases for further developing the additives and probiotics for the treatment of diarrhea in piglets.
