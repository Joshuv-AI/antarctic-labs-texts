How can integration of oncology and palliative care services be achieved for optimal patient benefit? Stakeholders’ perspectives in Zimbabwe
Authors: Kennedy Bashan Nkhoma, Dickson Chifamba, Zvavahera Mike Chirenje, Adlight Dandadzi, Catherine J Evans, Natsayi Mujuru, Mudiwa Mundawarara, Lovemore Mupaza, Patience Musaringo, Nkosiphile Moyo, Sunga Mzeche, Anna Mary Nyakabau, Agnes Chipo Tererai, Tanaka Tsiko, Richard Harding, Rebecca Bona, Rebecca Bona, Harriet Boulding, Helen Buchanan, Dickson Chifamba, Zvavahera Mike Chirenje, Emeka Chukwusa, Adlight Dandadzi, Clare Ellis-Smith, Catherine Evans, Lindsay Farrant, Liz Gwyther, Richard Harding, Veronica Murray, Irene Higginson, Joy Hunter, Joy Joachims, Dorothea Kanthack-Chan, Yvonne Karamagi, Elly Katabira, Donald Kawavza, Ali Kibuule, Grace Kivumbi, Rene Krause, Emmanuel Luyirika

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer is a main driver of death and serious health-related suffering. The growing body of evidence for early integration of palliative care alongside oncological treatment to optimizes patient-reported outcomes is almost exclusively from high-income countries, while the greatest need is in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). We aimed to explore the perspectives of patients, families and clinical staff on what constitutes a feasible, acceptable and appropriate model of integrated palliative and oncology care in Zimbabwe.Materials and MethodsWe conducted an exploratory qualitative study underpinned by indicators for integrated oncology and palliative care. Participants comprised healthcare professionals, patients, and families. Semistructured guides were developed and iteratively refined. Deductive framework analysis was conducted to Hui’s evidence-based framework of integrated oncology and palliative care indicators. The data were then further inductively coded into the framework.ResultsAnalysis of data from 45 stakeholders (n = 15 per group) identified 19 of 38 indicators (50%) aligned with Hui’s model, indicating partial and uneven integration. Alignment was strongest in clinical processes (n = 9) and education (n = 5), with more limited support for administration (n = 3) and structure (n = 2). No findings aligned with the research domain. Inductive coding identified 3 indicators that aligned with clinical processes (routine discussion of prognosis, advance care planning and goals), clinical structure (physical environment) and education (training needs).DiscussionWhile the Hui model is broadly applicable, substantial contextual adaptation is required. Integration cannot be achieved through coordination alone and requires system-level investment in workforce, training, infrastructure, and policy. We propose a phased “minimum package” for integration tailored to Zimbabwe and similar LMIC settings.

Our findings highlight the need to strengthen multiprofessional collaboration and communication in oncology settings. Although tumor board meetings involve surgeons, oncologists, radiologists, and pathologists, they do not routinely include palliative care specialists such as social workers and psychologists. Including palliative care team members could broaden discussions to address symptom control, goals of care, and communication of difficult news. Developing a contextually relevant model that incorporates both existing and newly identified indicators of integration may help patients, families, and health systems realize the benefits of integrated oncology and palliative care.

Introduction

Using WHO mortality data, it is projected that by 2060, 83% of all deaths with serious health-related suffering (SHRS) will occur in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) (5.14 million in low-income countries, 16.84 million in lower-middle countries, and 17.93 million in upper-middle countries).1 The greatest proportional increase will also occur in LMICs (155%). An estimated 70% of incident cancer occurs in LMICs.2,3 Cancer deaths in sub-Saharan Africa are predicted to double to an estimated 889 604 by 2030.4,5 Globally, cancer is expected to become the leading cause of death associated with SHRS by 2060.6 In LMICs, this represents a 407% increase from 2016 deaths to 1.65 million annually, in lower-middle income countries a 169% increase to 4.76 million; and in upper-middle income countries, a 96% increase to 6.67 million per year.

Palliative care is recommended “throughout the illness course” for cancer patients in LMICs.7 The World Health Assembly (WHA) resolution 67.19 calls for palliative care to be “integrated throughout the life course” and for new data on effective interventions.8 A strong body of evidence from high-income countries (HICs) has found that early integration of palliative care into cancer and organ failure management improves patient and family-reported outcomes, costs, and survival.9–13 It also improves quality of life and symptom burden and has led to guideline and practice change.14,15 In LMICs, palliative care is also cost-effective, and improves patient and family reported outcomes.16 Effect sizes from the integration of palliative care to strengthen health services may be greater in LMICs, given limited health resources.17

Evidence from Kenya showed that early palliative care integrated into HIV care on active treatment, and additional palliative care in Uganda for patients with multi-drug-resistant tuberculosis in Uganda alongside treatment, improved self-reported outcomes.18–20

A key reference framework for the integration of oncology and palliative care is provided by the work of David Hui and his colleagues.21 They conducted a comprehensive systematic review of integration models in oncology settings. This systematic review of palliative care and oncology integration synthesized 101 studies and identified 38 clinical process, structure, educational, research, and administrative indicators. Studies were predominantly conducted in North America (63%) and Europe (27%). None of the included studies was conducted in a low-income country.21 The framework has since been widely used to conceptualize and benchmark integration in high-income countries. However, its applicability to LMICs, where health system capacity, workforce, and resources differ substantially, remains underexplored. This highlights the urgent need for contextually appropriate models to improve outcomes and minimize costs in LMICs.22

Palliative care is included in Universal Health Coverage (UHC) goals as an essential health service.9 This may address the global inequity in palliative care as reported by the Lancet Commission and WHO Atlas of palliative care, which found less than 10% of those who need palliative care receive it, and 80% of those in need live in low and middle-income countries (LMICs).23 A review of UHC palliative care implementation recommended research to identify and implement appropriate models within health systems in LMICs.24,25 Incurable cancer brings psychological, economic/social, physical, and spiritual concerns to patients in LMICs, and largely female family caregivers, with financial implications for families and health systems.9–13

A systematic review of palliative care needs in Africa (37% of studies in cancer populations) identified context-specific needs: physical (eg, nutritional support); psychological (eg, worry about medical bills); social and practical (eg, selling assets) and spiritual (rites associated with cultural and religious beliefs).26

Recent literature calls for research to translate effective integrated palliative care into feasible health systems models globally,22 including analysis of cost outcomes.27 Despite this, it is unclear what model of integration is appropriate for sub-Saharan African settings. We aimed to explore the perspectives of patients, families, and clinical staff on what constitutes a feasible, acceptable, and appropriate model of integrated palliative and oncology care in Zimbabwe with the goal of informing locally relevant implementation strategies.

Methods

Design and setting

Qualitative in-depth interviews at 2 oncology national referral hospitals, which offer surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy to cancer patients across Zimbabwe. These are the only public state-funded sites that provide oncology services in Zimbabwe.

We recruited adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with a diagnosis of prostate or cervical cancer. These are the 2 malignancies with the highest mortality in the WHO African region (ICD C53 cervix, ICD C61 prostate).4 Further, the Zimbabwe 2017 National Cancer Registry Report identified these as the 2 most common cancers in Zimbabwe (cervix 20% and prostate 10%).4 In addition, participants had to have an estimated prognosis of 6-24 months from their oncologist (in line with trial entry criteria for early integrated palliative care trials).28,29

Patients were recruited alongside their family caregivers. They were purposively sampled on age, gender, education, income, treatment, and relationship with the caregiver to ensure sample heterogeneity.

We sampled and recruited healthcare professionals by cadre (nurse, doctor, social worker, radiotherapists, and radiologists), department (nursing, medicine, oncology, radiotherapy, palliative care) across the 2 facilities.

Data collection

Interviews with patients and families used semi-structured guides exploring views on early integration, and perceived optimal intervention (content, terminology, frequency, potential benefits). Patients and family interviews were held separately. Healthcare professional topic guides explored current standard practice, optimal models of early palliative care in terms of goals, content, frequency, payment, perceived benefits and challenges, how to promote collaboration between cancer and palliative care for the intervention, and health system capacity to integrate cancer palliative care. All topic guides were iteratively developed after successive interviews. PM and TS collected data at 1 hospital; NM, NkM, and SM collected data at the other. All researchers have considerable experience in collecting qualitative data. PM, NM, SM, and TS are female, while NkM is male. They are all behavioral scientists. PM and SM hold a Master’s degree, NM is a palliative care physician with a Master’s degree, and NkM and TS hold a Bachelor’s degree. All data were collected privately in a safe space using a digital encrypted audio recorder.

Data analysis

Following verbatim transcription and translation of the interviews (into English where needed) by AD, PM, TS, and SM, the research team (KN, AD, DC, PM, NM, and TS) deductively and inductively coded the transcripts thematically following the stages of framework analysis30 using NVIVO software. The deductive coding frame was based on the Hui systematic review of integration of palliative care and oncology services (see Figure 1).

Adaptation of the Hui and Bruera (2015) indicators of palliative care and oncology integration model, using data from N = 45 interviews in Zimbabwe.

We chose this framework because it provides a comprehensive evidence-based structure sets of 38 indicators identified from a systematic review of international literature. These indicators are structured around 5 main areas (clinical processes, clinical structure, education, research, and administration) of palliative care and oncology integration.27 The clinical processes include 13 indicators, clinical structures and research contain 4 indicators each, with a further 8 education and 9 administration indicators. This model was selected to create a priori codes as it provides evidence-based comprehensive, practical guidance for integration and implementation of palliative care in oncology settings. It offers practical guidance for operationalizing integration within oncology settings and has been used extensively in high-income contexts to evaluate integration. In this study, the framework enabled systematic mapping of stakeholder perspectives onto established indicators, while also allowing for inductive identification of additional, context-specific factors relevant to the Zimbabwean setting.

Following line-by-line reading of all transcripts, KN coordinated data analysis by developing an initial coding framework using patient (n = 2), caregiver (n = 2) and healthcare professional (n = 2) transcripts. The framework was shared with the research team (AD, DC, PM, NM, and TS), who applied it to additional transcripts. New codes were generated inductively where data did not align with the Hui framework. Differences were resolved through discussion and consensus. The final coding framework was then applied to all remaining transcripts. This process enabled identification of similarities and differences across participant groups and exploration of relationships between categories. In the final stage, we adapted the Hui model to demonstrate where findings supported the original indicators, where new indicators emerged inductively, and where indicators were unsupported by the data.

Rigor, trustworthiness, and reflexivity

We adopted several strategies to enhance the rigor and trustworthiness of the study. Reflexivity was maintained throughout data collection and analysis; the research team comprised multidisciplinary behavioral scientists and a palliative care physician with prior experience in qualitative research in similar settings. Team members reflected on how their clinical and research backgrounds, as well as their positions within or external to the study sites, may have shaped data interpretation. Regular analytic meetings were held to discuss emerging codes, challenge assumptions, and ensure balanced interpretation across perspectives.

Sampling adequacy was guided by the principle of information power, whereby sample size was considered sufficient when the data provided rich, relevant, and specific insights aligned with the study aim. With a total sample of 45 participants across patients, caregivers, and healthcare professionals, we observed thematic stability, with no substantially new themes emerging in later interviews.

To ensure accuracy and preservation of meaning, interviews conducted in local languages were translated into English by bilingual researchers and independently reviewed. Where necessary, original interviewers were consulted during analysis to clarify contextual nuances and verify interpretations. This iterative process supported fidelity to participants’ intended meanings across languages.

Ethical approvals

Approvals were secured from the Medical Research of Zimbabwe (MRCZ/A/3029, Joint Research Ethics Committee for the University of Zimbabwe Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences and Parirenyatwa Group of Hospitals (JREC 180/2023), Research Council of Zimbabwe (MRCZ/A/3029) and administrative approvals from sites where data were collected. We also obtained ethics approval from King’s College London (HR/DP-22/23-34208).

Administration factors

Three of the 9 indicators were supported: Opioid availability, Palliative care and oncology within the same department, and Public awareness and advocacy. Indicators such as formal centers of excellence or models of integration, national standards or policy, palliative care recognition as a specialty, reimbursement/funding mechanisms, and leadership support/regional organization were largely absent.

Opioid availability

Healthcare professionals reported challenges with access to morphine and some patients not wanting to take morphine. However, improved education and information provision increased patients’ acceptance (Quote 1).

Palliative care and oncology within the same department

Health professionals highlighted that palliative care and oncology were not co-located. This had a negative effect on the flow and continuity of care, and mitigated against comprehensive care provision. They suggested that both trainees and qualified healthcare professionals should rotate between oncology and palliative care departments in order to gain knowledge and skills to provide comprehensive care (Quote 2).

Public awareness and advocacy

Participants stressed the importance of raising public awareness of cancer and palliative care. Early screening, diagnosis, and symptom recognition were viewed as critical to improving outcomes. Collaboration with community organizations and policymakers was seen as essential to strengthening awareness and access (Quotes 3-4).

Clinical processes to support integration

Nine of the 13 indicators were identified, including Availability of palliative care service, Referral criteria to palliative care and other services, Combined multidisciplinary tumor boards, Communication and coordination, Early palliative care involvement, Interdisciplinary palliative care teams, Palliative care nurse practitioner, Simultaneous care, Routine symptom screening in oncology clinics. One additional indicator Routine discussion of prognosis, advanced care planning and goals was identified inductively.

Four of the 12 indicators which included clinical care pathways, embedded clinics, specified timing of palliative care referral, and supportive care guidelines were not supported.

Availability of palliative care service

Healthcare professionals emphasized that accessible palliative care services are essential for integration. Integration with existing services, particularly HIV care, was noted as effective, given the high burden of comorbidity such as cervical cancer among people living with HIV (Quote 5).

Referral criteria to palliative care and other services

Referral systems were in place, primarily for pain and symptom management. Patients were also referred to social services for financial support, particularly when unable to afford diagnostic tests or treatment. However, referral practices were variable and often inconsistent (Quotes 6-8).

Combined multidisciplinary tumor boards

Multidisciplinary meetings were conducted, although not consistently. While clinical cases were reviewed (Quotes 9-10), these meetings often excluded key professionals such as social workers, psychologists, and physiotherapists, limiting a holistic, person-centered approach (Quotes 11-12).

Communication and coordination

Communication was recognized as important but often inadequate. Patients were not always well informed about treatment delays, care pathways, or symptom management. Coordination between healthcare providers, patients, families, and external palliative care organizations was limited (Quotes 13-14). Although referrals to palliative care were reported, these were not always timely or well-integrated (Quote 15).

Early palliative care involvement

Healthcare professionals emphasized that early palliative care and oncology involvement must start with early screening, and from diagnosis the patient should receive both cancer treatment and palliative care. They noted that in practice, referrals were often delayed until the terminal phase, limiting the potential benefits of palliative care (Quotes 16-18).

Interdisciplinary palliative care teams

Interdisciplinary and multi-disciplinary teams working together may be best achieved at structured weekly or monthly joint meetings. It was also emphasized that existing palliative care teams should be pro-active in reaching out to oncology (Quotes 19-21).

Palliative care nurse-practitioner

Healthcare professionals observed that there are palliative care nurses who work in hospital departments not necessarily in the oncology department, and that there are not enough palliative care nurses (hospital and hospice) to meet demand. They proposed that oncology staff should be trained in basic palliative care so that they can provide the services to oncology patients within the hospital oncology unit (Quotes 22-24).

Simultaneous care

Participants highlighted the importance of delivering palliative care alongside cancer treatment. Patients and families reported significant physical and psychological burdens, including treatment side effects, financial stress, and nutritional challenges. Palliative care services, including referral to social welfare, played a key role in addressing these needs (Quote 25).

Routine symptom screening in oncology clinics

Healthcare professionals stated that symptom screening is occasionally done. They reported using standardized tools to assess pain in line with the WHO analgesic ladder to assess and manage pain (Quote 26).

Routine discussion of prognosis, advanced care planning and goals (marked with an asterisk)

Healthcare professionals reported that discussions around prognosis, advanced care planning and goal setting were conducted occasionally. Usually, on request from the patient or family, which means the discussions are not pro-actively initiated (Quote 27).

Structural factors to support integration

Two of the 4 structural indicators were supported. These included Community-based teams and Inpatient consultation teams. One additional factor, physical environment, was inductively coded. Outpatient clinics and dedicated palliative care units, were not supported by our data.

Community-based teams

Community structures including community healthcare workers, village health workers, and traditional leaders played an important role in care delivery. Patients discharged from hospital often received follow-up care at home including health education and basic clinical support, thereby reducing the need for hospital visits (Quotes 28 and 29).

Inpatient consultation teams

Healthcare professionals reported that inpatient consultation teams provided multidisciplinary care including doctors, nurses, social workers, pharmacists, and nutritionists. Most of the patients have late-stage cancers at diagnosis, therefore palliative care is offered or provided to reduce the burden of symptoms (Quote 30).

Physical environment (marked with an asterisk)

Physical environment was inductively coded. Patients and caregivers highlighted challenges to the physical care environment. For example, limited space, lack of privacy, and inadequate facilities. Staff shortages further contributed to workload pressures and reduced quality of care (Quotes 31-32).

Education to support integration

Five of the 8 factors aligned with education. These included Lectures and curriculum on palliative care for oncologists and fellows, Palliative care competence in oncologists, Undergraduate palliative care curriculum, Palliative care rotations for oncology fellows, and Oncology rotations for palliative care fellows. For the indicator on rotations for oncology and palliative care fellows, healthcare professionals reported similar findings, therefore we present the indicators as a combined theme.

We also identified f) training needs as an additional factor that we coded inductively.

Three of the 8 education indicators were not supported by our data. These included formal testing of palliative care skills in examinations, conference attendance on palliative care for oncology professionals, and continuing medical education for oncologists.

Lectures and curriculum on palliative care for oncologists and fellows

Healthcare professionals reported limited inclusion of palliative care in oncology training programs. They emphasized the need to integrate palliative care into curricula to improve management of cancer-related symptoms (Quote 33).

Palliative care competence in oncologists

There was a recognized need for training across all healthcare professionals working in oncology settings. Participants emphasized workshops, peer learning, and knowledge exchange as essential to keeping pace with evolving evidence and improving patient care (Quotes 34 and 35).

Undergraduate palliative care curriculum

Healthcare professionals felt that undergraduate curricula for training nurses should incorporate palliative care. They also suggested that oncology nurses in practice should be offered training in palliative and oncology care (Quote 36).

Palliative care rotations for oncology fellows and oncology rotations for palliative care fellows

Clinical rotations between oncology and palliative care were seen as critical to building mutual understanding and enabling holistic care. Such exposure would enhance knowledge of both cancer treatment and palliative care approaches (Quote 37).

Training needs (marked with an asterisk)

Healthcare professionals highlighted the need for peer-led training and ongoing professional development, including training in advanced symptom management, psychological and spiritual care, evidence-based practice, and advance care planning. These were viewed as essential for delivering person-centered care (Quote 38).

None of the research-related indicators were represented in our data.

Our findings indicate that Hui’s model of integrated oncology and palliative care is largely applicable in a low-resource LMIC setting, but alignment is not uniform across all domains. Our findings both validate and challenge the Hui model, suggesting the need for contextual adaptation rather than direct transfer.

Discussion

Our findings show that David Hui et al, model of palliative care and oncology integration, although derived largely from high-income settings, is broadly applicable to an LMIC context, particularly in relation to clinical processes, structural components, and education. However, the absence of data aligning with the research domain, and the need to inductively identify additional indicators (eg, physical environment, routine discussions of prognosis, and context-specific training needs), highlight the importance of adapting this framework to reflect local health system realities. This reinforces the need for context-sensitive models of integration rather than direct transfer of frameworks developed in resource-rich settings.

Clinical processes and education domains showed strong alignment, whereas administrative and structural domains were only partially supported, and no findings aligned with the research domain. Areas of misalignment reflect both contextual constraints (eg, workforce shortages and limited funding) and limitations of the Hui model, which assumes availability of resources typical of high-income settings.

Four main domains underpin integration in this context: (1) administration, (2) clinical processes, (3) structure, and (4) education. Education has been recognized as one of the most important routes to foster the integration of oncological and palliative care.31 A consensus study recommended that oncologists undertake at least 1 month of palliative care rotation32; however, only 25% of oncology fellows do so in the USA.21,33 Oncology trainees develop competencies in symptom management, communication, understand referral processes, and build collaborative relationships with palliative care team through clinical rotations. Reciprocal exposure for palliative care trainees to oncology settings can further enhance understanding of cancer trajectories and treatment decision-making. These exchanges may reduce stigma and strengthen interdisciplinary partnerships.21,34

Clinical processes highlight persistent challenges in referral practices and routine symptom screening in oncology. Uncertainty about who, when, and how to refer patients remains shaped by local policies, service availability and workforce capacity.21,22 Although evidence from randomized controlled trials supports early integration based on diagnosis and prognosis,28,29,35 most clinicians refer patients based on care needs.21 Referral applying selective criteria based on a combination of time from diagnosis, patient prognosis, and personal care needs might help to streamline referral practices in this context.21 However, in sub-Saharan Africa late cancer diagnosis is common, and so the concept of “early integration” may be even more appropriate (and effective) than in high-income countries.

While improved coordination such as inclusion of palliative care in multidisciplinary tumor boards and structured communication between teams is a critical enabler, it is insufficient on its own to achieve effective integration in the Zimbabwean context. Workforce shortages limit holistic care and staff capacity for joint working, while targeted training and cross-rotations are needed to build shared competencies. Sustainable integration also depends on adequate funding for essential medicines, infrastructure, and community services, alongside policy-level support to enable full implementation.

Structural challenges are particularly evident in LMIC settings, where patients often live far from oncology centers and face prohibitive transport costs.36 Clear delineation of roles across primary, secondary, and tertiary care is essential to ensure continuity. Greater focus on primary palliative care is recommended by the WHO, and African models have been developed.37,38

Our findings also emphasize the importance of multiprofessional collaboration and communication. Multidisciplinary tumor boards often exclude palliative care professionals such as social workers and psychologists. Their inclusion could broaden discussions to address symptom burden, goals of care, and patient preferences.39

However, representation alone is insufficient. Hierarchical structures may limit the influence of palliative care perspectives. Meaningful integration requires clearly defined roles, structured opportunities for contribution, and institutional support, alongside training in interdisciplinary communication and visible leadership endorsement to legitimize palliative care input.

This study had several limitations. Language differences between data collection and analysis may have led to loss of meaning because local researchers in Harare did not speak the Ndebele language in which researchers had conducted the interviews in Bulawayo. An independent language expert translated the topic guide and data; however, this was mitigated through involvement of research assistants. While this study has enabled us to collect and analyze data from the 2 main centers of public oncology service delivery, it cannot be generalized to other countries. We propose that the Hui model be appraised and adapted in other geographical areas planning cancer palliative care integration.

The originality of this study lies in applying and extending an evidence-based integration model within a high-need setting in sub-Saharan Africa. By mapping empirical data to the Hui framework, we identify both transferable components and context-specific adaptations. We propose that effective integration in LMICs requires a phased, systems-oriented approach, supported by a Minimum Package of Integrated Oncology and Palliative Care (see Tables S1 and S2). This approach may enable patients, families, and health systems to realize the established benefits of integrated care while addressing workforce, financing, and institutional constraints.
