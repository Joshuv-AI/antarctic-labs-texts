Role of WIF-1 in regulating chondrocyte dedifferentiation and matrix production through WNT pathway
Authors: Yu-Ying Chu, Yukiyo Asawa, Naoki Tsuji, Tomoaki Sakamoto, Dan Liu, Kazuto Hoshi, Atsuhiko Hikita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionChondrocyte dedifferentiation during monolayer expansion is a major barrier to producing clinically reliable cells for cartilage regeneration. The upstream regulators governing this phenotypic instability are not fully defined. This study identifies WNT inhibitory factor 1 (WIF1) as a potential key molecular determinant of chondrocyte stability.MethodsHuman auricular chondrocytes (HACs) were expanded to early (P2) and late (P7) passages. Transcriptomic profiling, qPCR, GAG quantification, and histology were performed in 2D monolayer and 3D atelocollagen pellet cultures. WIF1 function was interrogated using lentiviral knockdown and overexpression. β-catenin, p-SMAD2/3, p-JNK, COL1A1, COL2A1, and WIF1 were evaluated by immunohistochemistry.ResultsLate-passage HACs showed marked dedifferentiation, including reduced WIF1, COL2A1, ACAN, and SOX9 with concomitant increases in COL1A1, ACTA2, and MMP9. WIF1 knockdown in early-passage cells reproduced this phenotype, inducing β-catenin nuclear accumulation, increased SMAD2/3 and JNK activation, diminished GAG synthesis, and loss of cartilage-specific ECM architecture. Conversely, WIF1 overexpression in P7 chondrocytes restored chondrogenic markers, enhanced proteoglycan-rich ECM formation, and suppressed fibroblastic gene expression. WIF1 overexpression re-established a signaling profile resembling early-passage cells, characterized by low β-catenin, reduced p-SMAD2/3, and attenuated p-JNK.ConclusionsWIF1 functions as a critical upstream regulator that maintains chondrocyte identity by simultaneously suppressing WNT/β-catenin, TGF-β/SMAD, and JNK stress signaling. Its loss is a hallmark of dedifferentiation, while its restoration reverses transcriptional drift and rescues ECM-producing capacity. These findings highlight WIF1 as a promising therapeutic target to improve the stability of expanded chondrocytes and enhance the quality of regenerative cartilage constructs.

Introduction

Cartilage tissue engineering offers a promising approach for treating articular defects—conditions with limited self-repair capacity—by harnessing autologous or allogenic chondrocytes expanded ex vivo [1,2]. A critical barrier in this field is the rapid and progressive dedifferentiation of cultured chondrocytes in monolayer [3], resulting in the loss of their native phenotype within a few passages [4,5]. Phenotypic drift features repression of chondrogenic genes such as COL2A1, aggrecan (ACAN), and SOX9, while gaining fibroblastic characteristics including increased expression of COL1A1 and ACTA2(3), which collectively result in fibrocartilage formation and suboptimal functional and mechanical restoration.

A variety of strategies—ranging from supplementation of growth factors (TGF-β, IGF-1, FGF-2) [6,7], implementation of three-dimensional (3D) scaffolds, and application of mechanical stimulations [8]—can transiently promote matrix synthesis and phenotype maintenance [9]. However, the molecular circuitry supporting chondrocyte homeostasis and stability is still not fully defined. Understanding these upstream triggers remains essential for stabilizing expanded chondrocytes.

Chondrocyte dedifferentiation involves not only loss of cartilage-specific genes but also activation of additional catabolic pathways that accelerate phenotypic drift [[10], [11], [12]]. Of the diverse signaling axes, the WNT pathway—encompassing canonical (β-catenin-dependent) and noncanonical (PCP/JNK and WNT/Ca2+) branches [13]—critically governs chondrocyte fate decisions and matrix metabolism. While canonical WNT/β-catenin signaling stimulates proliferation and matrix remodeling, its hyperactivation is implicated in dedifferentiation and cartilage destruction [14]. By contrast, inhibition of aberrant WNT activity preserves or rescues the chondrogenic phenotype [15,16]. Beyond canonical WNT/β-catenin signaling, stress-responsive cascades such as JNK/MAPK and NF-κB contribute to fibroblastic transition by inducing MMPs, promoting cytoskeletal remodeling, and suppressing SOX9 [17], [18]. These pathways interact to create a feedforward loop in which reduced matrix synthesis and enhanced ECM remodeling further reinforce dedifferentiation.

WNT inhibitory factor 1 (WIF1) is a secreted WNT antagonist, directly sequestering WNT ligands and abrogating receptor activation [16]. Although widely studied for its role as a tumor suppressor in cancer contexts [19], WIF1 is also highly expressed in developing cartilage and bone [20], while its postnatal and adult role in maintaining chondrocyte stability is less well characterized. Emerging data indicate WIF1 expression is downregulated in osteoarthritic and aging cartilage [21], correlating with disease progression and matrix loss, although its function in preventing dedifferentiation during chondrocyte expansion is not fully elucidated.

In this study, we re-analyzed previous microarray data, and identified WIF1 among downregulated genes during passage culture of human auricular chondrocytes (HACs). To decipher the multifaceted role of WIF1, we engineered lentiviral-based systems to modulate WIF1 in HACs and employed both monolayer and 3D (atelocollagen) cultures to assay ECM deposition. qPCR, Western blot, and immunostaining enabled quantitative expression profiling of canonical (β-catenin), noncanonical (JNK), and TGF-β/SMAD pathway mediators—highlighting their putative convergence under WIF1 regulation. Inclusion of an IGF-1-stimulated group addressed potential synergistic effects on ECM restoration.

Materials and methods

Human chondrocyte samples

HACs were isolated from discarded auricular cartilage tissues obtained during otoplasty procedures, following institutional ethical approval (#0622) and informed consent. Primary chondrocytes from three independent donors were used for WIF1 protein silencing, over expression and results analysis of RT-qPCR, GAG quantification, tissue section and immunohistochemical staining to ensure reproducibility. For each donor, technical triplicates were conducted per assay, and biological results were averaged across donors.

Microarray data analysis and visualization

Gene expression data from the microarray experiment [22]were analyzed using a standard bioinformatics pipeline to identify and visualize differences between the Passage 2 (P2) chondrocytes represented the early differentiated stage, and Passage 7 (P7) cells represented the dedifferentiated phenotype.

Data processing and differential expression

Raw microarray intensity data were initially processed for quality control and background correction. The data were then normalized using the quantile normalization to ensure comparable distributions of expression values between arrays. Expression values corresponding to replicate probes were averaged at the gene level. Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis between the P2 and P7 groups was performed using the Welch's t-test. Genes were considered differentially expressed (DEGs) based on two primary criteria: a statistically significant adjusted p-value <0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (log2FC) greater than 1.0.

Data visualization

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the normalized gene expression matrix to assess the overall relationship and clustering among the samples. The two primary components, PC1 and PC2, which accounted for 20.6% and 17.8% of the total variance, respectively, were plotted to visualize the separation of the P2 and P7 groups.

A Volcano Plot was generated to provide a comprehensive visual summary of the DGE results, plotting the (log2FC) on the x-axis and the negative log10-transformed adjusted p-value on the y-axis. Genes meeting the significance criteria were highlighted.

Finally, a Heatmap was constructed to visualize the expression profiles of the top 20 differentially expressed genes (top 10 up-regulated and top 10 down-regulated). Sample and gene clustering were applied to group samples and genes with similar expression patterns. All analyses and figures were generated using Python with the following packages: pandas, numpy, scipy, matplotlib, and seaborn.

Cell culture and passaging

To rigorously compare the proliferative capacity and phenotypic fidelity of chondrocytes under distinct microenvironmental architectures, primary HACs were expanded using both conventional planar monolayer and physiologically relevant three-dimensional (3D) type I collagen gel culture systems (Fig. 2a). This dual-system approach enables critical investigation of how biomechanical and biochemical context modulate chondrocyte phenotype stability, given that 3D microenvironments more closely recapitulate native cartilage ECM and preserve chondrogenic gene expression than do standard 2D cultures [23].

For monolayer expansion, HACs were seeded on type I collagen-coated 10-cm dishes (IWAKI, Tokyo, Japan) at an initial density of 2 × 105 cells per dish. The culture medium consisted of Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/F12 (DMEM/F12; Gibco), supplemented with 5% human serum (pre-screened and batch-consistent to minimize inter-donor variability), 10 μg/mL recombinant human insulin, and 10 ng/mL fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2); this serum-supplemented, growth factor-enriched regimen is widely shown to promote chondrocyte proliferation while mitigating spontaneous dedifferentiation [24]. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator and subcultured upon reaching 90–100% confluency. Passage 2 (P2) and passage 7 (P7) cells were used to represent early- and late-passage populations, respectively, aligning with prior literature that tracks phenotypic drift during serial passaging [3].

For 3D expansion, an acid-solubilized solution of type I collagen (porcine origin; Nippi, Tokyo, Japan) was neutralized to physiological pH (7.4) on ice to create a pre-gel matrix. HACs previously expanded in monolayer (P2 or P7) were suspended in the collagen solution at 1 × 105 cells/mL, gently mixed to ensure homogeneous distribution, and then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to complete gelation. Uniform cell-matrix integration was visually validated prior to further culture. The same proliferation medium as monolayer culture was added following gelation [24]. Constructs were cultured under standard conditions for one week, a time frame selected based on well-established protocols for in vitro cartilage matrix deposition [25].

After culture, gels were enzymatically digested with 0.3% collagenase (Fujifilm Wako, Cat. No. 032-22364, JAN 4548995001917) at 37 °C with intermittent gentle agitation until fully dissolved. The enzymatic reaction was neutralized using FFI medium; released cells were harvested by centrifugation (1500 rpm, 5 min) and re-counted. The resulting populations, designated passage 3 (P3) and passage 8 (P8), reflect an additional round of culture post-monolayer expansion in the 3D environment, consistent with protocols for analyzing transcriptomic adaptation to microenvironmental shifts [25]. Immediately following recovery, cell pellets were processed for RNA extraction using ISOGEN reagent (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan), with RNA integrity and concentration evaluated spectrophotometrically (A260/A280 between 1.9 and 2.1, and confirmed by microfluidics-based quality analysis as needed). This integrated dual-culture system facilitates comparison of WIF1 and ECM gene expression between cells subjected to planar versus 3D collagen-embedded conditions.

Lentiviral vector construction and transduction

All lentiviral vectors used in this study were third-generation, replication-incompetent constructs designed for safe and stable gene delivery. Plasmids were first amplified in competent Escherichia coli DH5α cells or ccdB Survival™ 2 T1R Competent Cells. (Invitrogen, USA) by standard heat-shock transformation and cultured in LB medium containing ampicillin (100 μg/mL) or kanamycin (50 μg/ml). Plasmid DNA was purified using the Plasmid Maxi Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol and quantified by spectrophotometry. All constructs were sequence-verified by Sanger sequencing across the full-length insert and cloning junctions prior to transfection.

Lentiviral particles encoding WIF1 cDNA or WIF1-targeting shRNA were generated by transient transfection of HEK 293T cells using PEIMax (Polysciences). Cells were seeded on type I collagen-coated 10-cm dishes (IWAKI, Tokyo, Japan) and maintained in high glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) until they reached 70–90% confluence. All procedures involving lentivirus production and infection were performed under biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) containment, following institutional biosafety and ethical regulations approved by the University of Tokyo Biosafety and Ethics Committee (#50 and G2025M021).

All packaging and backbone plasmids were obtained from the RIKEN BioResource Center (Tsukuba, Japan), including pCAG-HIVgp (RDB04394) [26], pCMV-VSV-G-RSV-Rev (RDB04393) [26], CS-RfA-EVBsd (RDB06090) [27], and pENTR4-H1 (RDB04395) [26]. Plasmid integrity was confirmed by restriction enzyme digestion and sequencing before use.

The WIF1 shRNA sequence used was 5′-GATCTTAAATCACTGAGCTGATATTTCTCGAGAAATATCAGCTCAGTGATTTATTTTTGGAT-3′, and the scramble sequence used was 5′-GATCTCCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCGCTCGAGCGAGGGCGACTTAACCTTAGGTTTTTGGAT-3′.

These sequences were cloned into pENTR4-H1 and transferred into CS-RfA-EVBsd via Gateway recombination.

The WIF1 cDNA construct was synthesized and cloned into pLV-EF1a-hWIF1-EGFP:T2A:Puro and pLV-EF1a-ORF-stuffer-EGFP:T2A:Puro (VectorBuilder, Chicago, USA). All plasmids were confirmed by full-length sequencing before transfection. This lentiviral system provided a stable and efficient platform for modulating WIF1 expression.

Biochemical assay for glycosaminoglycan (GAG) content

Glycosaminoglycan (GAG) content was quantified at day 21 using the Blyscan™ assay kit (Biocolor). A standard curve was generated using chondroitin sulfate C (0–50 μg/mL), and absorbance was measured at 525 nm. GAG values were normalized to both DNA (cell number) and total protein content to ensure matrix yield per viable cell. Both metrics demonstrated consistent trends.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Sample size (n = 3 donors) was determined based on pilot variability analysis, achieving >80% power to detect 2-fold differences (α = 0.05, two-tailed). Data normality was confirmed by Shapiro–Wilk test prior to Welch's t-test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance for qPCR and GAG assays was determined using Welch's t-test with p < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Early-passage vs. late-passage chondrocytes exhibit distinct morphology and transcriptomes

Early-passage human auricular chondrocytes (HACs; P2) maintained a compact, rounded, polygonal morphology reflective of mature cartilage (Fig. 1a, scale bar 400 μm), while late-passage cells (P7) showed pronounced elongation, spindle shapes, and heterogeneity—hallmarks of dedifferentiation (Fig. 1b, scale bar 400 μm). Quantitative analysis (mean ± SD, n = 3) revealed reduced proliferation and viability in P7 compared with P2 (Fig. 1c).

Transcriptomic profiling reveals marked phenotypic divergence between early- and late-passage chondrocytes and overview of experimental design

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying chondrocyte dedifferentiation, we compared global gene expression profiles between early-passage (P2) and late-passage (P7) human auricular chondrocytes using microarray-based transcriptomic analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 1d) clearly separated the two groups along both PC1 (20.6%) and PC2 (17.8%), indicating robust transcriptional divergence associated with long-term culture. This separation reflects a marked loss of chondrocytes’ identity and the emergence of fibroblastic features during serial passaging.

Volcano plot analysis further demonstrated extensive transcriptional reprogramming, with more than 600 genes significantly dysregulated (|log2 fold change| > 1, p < 0.05) between P2 and P7 (Fig. 1e). Notably, WNT inhibitory factor 1 (WIF1) was among the most strongly downregulated genes in P7 cells, along with other chondrogenic markers such as COL11A1, FMOD, and SOX8. In contrast, fibroblast-associated and stress-related genes, including MGP, SERPINA3, and EFNA1, were significantly upregulated. These findings suggest that reduced WIF1 expression may coincide with loss of the chondrogenic transcriptional program and activation of signaling pathways associated with matrix degradation and remodeling.

Consistent with this interpretation, heatmap analysis normalized by z-score of the top differentially expressed genes (top 10 upregulated in P2 and top 10 upregulated in P7) revealed distinct expression clusters (Fig. 1f). Early-passage chondrocytes (P2) exhibited strong expression of cartilage-related genes (WIF1, SOX8, FMOD, COL11A1) and ECM modulators, whereas late-passage cells (P7) showed elevated expression of fibroblast- and stress-related markers (MGP, GFAP, SERPINA3, KRTAP1-5). These transcriptional patterns indicate a transition from an anabolic cartilage-producing state to a catabolic, fibroblastic phenotype, with WIF1 positioned as a potential upstream regulator of this switch.

Together, these transcriptomic data identify WIF1 downregulation as a hallmark of chondrocyte dedifferentiation, supporting its potential role as a molecular gatekeeper that maintains the chondrogenic phenotype during expansion.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying human auricular chondrocyte (HAC) dedifferentiation, a series of comparative experiments were designed across different passage numbers, gene modulation conditions, and culture environments. The overall workflow is illustrated (Fig. 1g), showing stepwise analyses including proliferation assays, gene expression, extracellular matrix (ECM) formation, and pathway interrogation in both 2D monolayer and 3D atelocollagen-based pellet systems.

The gene expression of WIF1 and differentiation markers decreased in late-passage chondrocytes

In the monolayer condition (Fig. 2b), late-passage chondrocytes (P7) exhibited significant phenotypic dedifferentiation compared with early-passage cells (P2). The expression of COL1A1 was markedly upregulated in P7 cells (p < 0.01) and ACTA2, markers associated with fibroblastic and contractile phenotypes, also presented upregulated trend. In an opposite manner, the chondrogenic marker ACAN showed a significant downregulation (p < 0.001), and WIF1 expression was also substantially decreased ∼4.7-fold in P7 (p < 0.001).

After one week of pellet culture under IGF-1-supplemented differentiation conditions (Fig. 2c), both P2- and P7-derived chondrocytes displayed altered transcriptional responses. ACTA2 expression increased robustly in P7-derived pellets (p < 0.01), consistent with enhanced cytoskeletal activation and early fibrotic remodeling, while ACAN and COL2A1 exhibited relatively lower expression levels than in P2-derived pellets (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively). These findings suggest that IGF-1 stimulation partially restores matrix synthesis in early-passage chondrocytes though fails to reverse dedifferentiation in late-passage cells, which maintain higher levels of fibroblastic gene expression and tendency of reduced WIF1, indicating the loss of cartilage-specific characteristics during serial passaging.

3D collagen scaffolds boost redifferentiation but late passage imposes transcriptional limits

We also compared conventional 2D monolayer versus 3D type I collagen hydrogel proliferative culture (Fig. 2a), analyzing gene expression in P2 (expanded to P3) and P7 (expanded to P8) HACs. For P3 3D cultures, RT-qPCR demonstrated significant upregulation of ACAN and SOX9 (4.3 ± 2.5-fold and 2.0 ± 0.5-fold, respectively; p < 0.05), with moderate COL2A1 and mild WIF1 increases (1.9 ± 1.1-fold). Negative controls and dedifferentiation genes (COL1A1, ACTA2) showed no significant change (Fig. 2d).

However, P8 cells in 3D did not rescue chondrogenic gene expression, underscoring the irreversibility of transcriptional repression during prolonged expansion (Fig. 2e). These findings indicate that 3D matrices partially restore differentiation in early-passage cells but are insufficient for late-passage HACs.

WIF1 knockdown and overexpression reciprocally regulate chondrocyte gene expression

Lentiviral-mediated WIF1 knockdown in P2 and overexpression in P7 cells enabled functional assessment (Fig. 3a). Infection efficiency exceeded 90% by EGFP fluorescence (Fig. 3 b-c). To further investigate the functional role of WIF1 in chondrocyte phenotype maintenance, WIF1 expression was silenced in early-passage chondrocytes (P2) and overexpressed in late-passage chondrocytes (P7). In the WIF1 knockdown group (Fig. 3d), P2 cells transduced with shRNA-WIF1 exhibited a marked upregulation of ACTA2 (p < 0.05). Though not significant, COL1A1, also revealed upregulated trend, consistent with enhanced fibroblastic transition and cytoskeletal activation. Conversely, the expression of chondrogenic markers COL2A1 was reduced significantly (p < 0.05), accompanied by a significant suppression of WIF1 itself (p < 0.05). ACAN also revealed decreasing trend, though not significant. These transcriptional changes indicate that loss of WIF1 promotes dedifferentiation and matrix degradation, mirroring the late-passage phenotype.

In contrast, overexpression of WIF1 in P7 chondrocytes (Fig. 3e) strongly restored the cartilage-specific molecular profile. Compared with Empty vector controls, COL2A1 expression increased more than tenfold (p < 0.05), and ACAN and SOX9 levels were also shown elevated (insignificant), whereas ACTA2 and COL1A1 expression remained low(insignificant). These findings suggest that WIF1 activation re-establishes the chondrogenic transcriptional program even in late-passage cells, counteracting fibroblastic dedifferentiation.

Quantification of glycosaminoglycan (GAG) content further supported these molecular results (Fig. 3f). GAG normalized to total protein was significantly decreased in P7 compared with P2, and in WIF1-silenced P2 pellets compared with controls (p < 0.05), while WIF1 overexpression in P7 cells led to a notable increase in GAG production without significance. These results demonstrate that WIF1 acts as a key regulator of chondrocyte differentiation status, promoting cartilage matrix synthesis and suppressing dedifferentiation-associated genes.

These findings are consistent with WNT–SMAD–JNK antagonism reported in prior mechanistic studies and support WIF1 as a master upstream inhibitor of canonical WNT signaling [[28], [29], [30]].

Counter to this, WIF1 overexpression in P7 pellets substantially attenuated nuclear β-catenin, reducing p-SMAD2/3 activation, and p-JNK levels—collectively shifting the signaling landscape toward a P2-like configuration. The diminish of SMAD activity is particularly notable, as TGF-β/SMAD signaling is required for COL1A1 transcription, suggesting that WIF1 re-establishes an anabolic regulatory environment. Suppression of p-JNK further aligns with reduced catabolic matrix turnover and decreased fibroblastic traits in WIF1-overexpressing pellets.

Collectively, Fig. 7 demonstrates that WIF1 coordinates a multi-pathway regulatory network that stabilizes chondrocyte phenotype. Through simultaneous inhibition of β-catenin nuclear entry, prevention of SMAD2/3 phosphorylation, and suppression of JNK activation, WIF1 maintains the chondrogenic transcriptional program and prevents the adoption of a fibroblast-like, matrix-degrading phenotype. These findings support a model in which WIF1 acts not only as a WNT antagonist but also as a central molecular node coordinating crosstalk among canonical WNT, TGF-β/SMAD, and MAPK–JNK pathways—providing a mechanistic explanation for its ability to block dedifferentiation and restore matrix production even in late-passage human auricular chondrocytes.

Discussion

WIF1 protein features and its inhibition of dedifferentiation via WNT signaling

WNT signaling comprises the canonical (β-catenin-dependent) and several noncanonical (PCP/JNK, Wnt/Ca2+) branches [31,32], each with specific implications for chondrocyte function. WIF1 is a secreted antagonist that binds WNT ligands, preventing activation of WNT signaling at the extracellular level. Structurally, it contains a conserved WIF-domain responsible for high-affinity interaction with multiple WNT proteins (Wnt3a, Wnt4, Wnt5a, etc.), thus broadly suppressing canonical and noncanonical signaling outputs [33,34]. The dual blockade of both WNT axes by WIF1 therefore places it as a potent regulator of the signaling landscape that controls matrix homeostasis and differentiation.

In recent literature, WIF1 was found to plays a multifaceted role in regulating WNT signaling by guiding lipid-modified WNT proteins through their secretion and interaction with target molecules. By transferring WNT ligands from membrane-associated components and forming soluble WNT–WIF1 complexes, it supports ligand delivery to receptors such as glypicans, Frizzled, and NOTUM. Notably, WIF1's function depends on its abundance: high levels predominantly suppress WNT activity by sequestering ligands, whereas lower levels favor its transport role, indirectly enhancing WNT signaling efficiency. Thus, WIF1 acts not only as an inhibitor but also as a context-dependent regulator shaping WNT pathway dynamics [35].

In canonical signaling, WIF1 usually prevents β-catenin accumulation, thereby blocking activation of TCF/LEF target genes implicated in proliferation, matrix catabolism, and dedifferentiation. Likewise observed in our study, low expression of WIF1 in P7 cells coincides with increased β-catenin nuclear accumulation and induction of downstream targets including CyclinD1, DKK2, and BCL2—a transcriptional signature characteristic of proliferative but dedifferentiated chondrocytes. Silencing WIF1 in P2 cells phenocopied this late-passage state, strongly elevating catabolic WNT activity, whereas restoring WIF1 in P7 cells suppressed β-catenin and reinstated chondrogenic transcription factors such as SOX9 alongside key matrix genes (COL2A1, ACAN). These findings establish WIF1 as an upstream regulator that maintains the canonical WNT axis in a low-activity, phenotype-preserving state.

Unexpectedly, WIF1 upregulation resulted in paradoxical rise of LEF1 expression, one of the classical WNT/β-catenin target genes. However, abundance and activity of LEF1 can be shaped by signaling cues beyond β-catenin. Multiple studies have shown that TGF-β/SMAD [36], MAPK/JNK, NF-κB [37] and context-dependent chromatin regulators can induce or stabilize LEF1 independently of WNT ligand availability [38]. Thus, under conditions of diminished canonical WNT activity, compensatory or stress-responsive pathways could elevate LEF1 expression as part of a broader transcriptional rewiring.

These models highlight a regulatory landscape in which LEF1 behaves less as a simple WNT target and more as an integrative transcriptional node. Nevertheless, these interpretations remain speculative, and dissecting the precise mechanisms will require targeted perturbations—particularly LEF1 chromatin occupancy profiling and quantitative β-catenin–LEF1 interaction assays.

In the noncanonical context, WIF1 inhibits activation of PCP/JNK and Wnt/Ca2+ signaling, which are involved in cytoskeletal remodeling, cell migration, and stress responses [34]—processes closely tied to loss of chondrocyte identity and fibroblastic transition. The JNK/MAPK pathway, including ERK, JNK, and p38, promotes chondrocyte dedifferentiation by stimulating proliferation, increasing type I collagen (COL1A1) and ACTA2 expression, and repressing cartilage-specific genes such as COL2A1 and SOX9 [39]. Inhibition of MAPK signaling, via pharmacological agents or gene silencing, restores chondrogenic markers and preserves matrix synthesis, thereby preventing phenotypic drift and maintaining cartilage function [40,41]. In our study, late-passage HACs and WIF1-silenced P2 cells exhibited increased p-JNK, consistent with activation of catabolic transcription factors such as AP-1. This shift paralleled the upregulation of COL1A1, ACTA2, and MMP9, key markers of fibrocartilage formation and matrix degradation. WIF1 overexpression markedly suppressed p-JNK in P7 cells, indicating that WIF1 indirectly restrains noncanonical WNT/JNK signaling in addition to its canonical actions. The present study demonstrates that loss of WIF1 expression is a molecular hallmark of chondrocyte dedifferentiation—late-passage cells (P7) exhibited significant downregulation of WIF1 concurrent with increased fibroblastic marker expression (COL1A1, ACTA2, MMP9). Functionally, WIF1 knockdown in early-passage chondrocytes induced a notable shift toward fibroblastic and catabolic gene profiles, inhibited cartilage matrix formation (GAG, COL2A1), and promoted COL1 expression. Conversely, forced expression of WIF1 in dedifferentiated chondrocytes robustly reinstated chondrogenic gene expression and enhanced matrix production, indicating that WIF1 is both necessary and sufficient to sustain chondrocyte differentiation status. Our data underscore WIF1 as a central gatekeeper preventing dedifferentiation and suggest its manipulation could substantially improve the stability of expanded chondrocytes for tissue engineering.

WIF1 crosstalk with NF-κB and TGF-β pathways in chondrocyte stability

Emerging evidence suggests that WIF1 is at the nexus of an intricate crosstalk between WNT and NF-κB pathways [42,43]. The crosstalk exhibits at several molecular sites. In the cytoplasm, βTrCP links both pathways by targeting IκB and β-catenin for degradation, while GSK3β regulates β-catenin stability and modulates NF-κB activation. At the membrane level, TLR4 and IKK can influence both inflammatory and WNT signaling. Within the nucleus, β-catenin and NF-κB (p65) compete for transcriptional control through shared co-factors like CBP/p300, affecting gene expression for inflammation and cell differentiation [42]. According to our RT-qPCR results, the BCL2 and MMP9 showed prominent difference between P2 and P7 pellets under differentiation of IGF-1. Together with published studies, indicate that restoration of WIF1 not only interrupts WNT/β-catenin-driven dedifferentiation but also dampens the expression of NF-κB–related catabolic and survival factors (BCL2, MMP9), implying that WIF1 suppression can indirectly elevate pro-inflammatory and stress signaling programs typical of late-stage OA cartilage [44].

On the other hand, TGF-β1 pathway was also known to induce ACAN and COL2A1 expression while decreasing MMPs function by activation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) and Smad2/3 in chondrocytes [45,46]. However, TGF-β is not always protective for cartilage. Its signaling is both stimulatory and inhibitory for cartilage matrix synthesis, depending on the cellular context and maturation stage [47]. Low TGF-β levels help maintain healthy chondrocytes, but in aging and osteoarthritic joints, elevated TGF-β activates alternative pathways that drive cartilage degeneration, osteophyte formation, and synovial fibrosis, contributing to joint pathology [48]. In the research of cardiac fibrogenesis, canonical WNT/β-catenin pathway often works simultaneously with TGF-β signaling when tissues become damaged or diseased. In these settings, WNT activity can strengthen and stabilize TGF-β–driven fibrotic or degenerative effects. Canonical WNT enhances TGF-β/Smad responses, while noncanonical WNT can influence alternative TGF-β pathways through shared MAPK molecules such as JNK, ERK, and p38(49). When WNT signaling is blocked, for example by WIF1 or DKK1, these pathogenic TGF-β effects are usually reduced [21]. As a result, processes such as fibrosis, cartilage breakdown, and hypertrophic changes driven by TGF-β tend to diminish. Our data suggest that WIF1 overexpression leads to a decrease in p-SMAD2/3 levels, which may reflect inhibition of TGF-β/SMAD signaling via WNT–TGF-β crosstalk. WIF1 loss resulted in increased phosphorylation of SMAD2/3, suggesting improved TGF-β responsiveness. In contrast, WIF1 overexpression decreased TGF-β activation and enhanced matrix production, which may due to the cross talk of WNT canonical pathway via GSK-3β level [49].

Collectively, these data support a multi-pathway regulatory model in which WIF1 maintains chondrocyte identity by (i) suppressing excessive canonical WNT/β-catenin signaling, (ii) preventing SMAD-dependent catabolic signaling, and (iii) attenuating JNK-mediated catabolic stress responses. Importantly, the ability of WIF1 overexpression to partially reverse late-passage dedifferentiation challenges the long-held assumption that phenotypic drift during monolayer expansion is irreversible. Instead, our findings reveal a previously unrecognized plasticity governed by WIF1-centered signaling homeostasis.

Our integrative analysis offers first-in-field evidence for WIF1 as a molecular gatekeeper of chondrocyte homeostasis, not as a single-pathway inhibitor, but as a molecular axis governor modulating cross-regulation between WNT, TGF-β, and MAPK pathways—suppressing dedifferentiation and sustaining cartilage-specific ECM gene expression by coordinately repressing excessive WNT/β-catenin, inhibiting catabolic JNK, and SMAD signaling axes. These findings advance our mechanistic grasp of chondrocyte stability and may underpin novel strategies to stabilize expanded chondrocytes—enhancing engineered cartilage quality for regenerative medicine and cell therapy applications.

Study limitations and future perspectives

Despite these promising findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, our current gene/protein profiling may not fully capture the breadth of downstream networks regulated by WIF1. Future work leveraging genome-wide transcriptomics and proteomics could identify novel mediators such as microRNAs, epigenetic regulators, or metabolic enzymes. In vivo validation—such as transplantation of WIF1-modulated pellets into immunodeficient rodents—will be critical to confirm stability and functional restoration of cartilage. Additionally, dose-response and expression kinetics of WIF1 remain to be explored; differential effects of transient versus constitutive modulation may inform optimal protocols for cell therapy and regenerative engineering. Therefore, our study supports a unifying model wherein WIF1 governs the molecular equilibrium between WNT activation, stress signaling, and cartilage phenotype stability. By manipulating this axis, WIF1 emerges as a versatile and actionable target for stabilizing chondrocytes during mass expansion and restoring the integrity of cartilage in clinical applications.

Conclusions

WIF1 plays a critical role in maintaining chondrocyte stability by suppressing multiple signaling pathways linked to dedifferentiation. WIF1 overexpression preserves chondrogenic markers, enhances ECM synthesis, and maintains cartilage-like morphology in vitro and in vivo. Targeting WIF1 or its downstream signaling networks may serve as an effective strategy for improving cartilage repair outcomes.
