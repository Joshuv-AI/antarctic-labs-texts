Unraveling the complexity of the oilseed rape genome: from sequencing to gene discovery for trait improvement
Authors: Weiping Ma, Mingxun Chen, Lixi Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oilseed rape (Brassica napus) serves as a cornerstone of global vegetable oil production, yet its genetic improvement has historically been impeded by a complex allopolyploid genome. This review synthesizes the transformative evolution of rapeseed genomics, traversing from initial fragmented references to the modern era of gap-free Telomere-to-Telomere (T2T) assemblies and graph-based pan-genomes. We highlight how these advanced resources resolve previously inaccessible repetitive regions and centromeres, revealing how structural variations (SVs) and homoeologous exchanges (HEs) drive key adaptive traits and morphotype diversification. Furthermore, we examine the integration of large-scale resequencing with sophisticated multi-omics pipelines to bridge the gap between statistical associations and biological causality. Through case studies, such as the characterization of BnRRF for seed weight and BnA09MYB47a for seed coloration, we illustrate the power of combining transcriptomics with Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats-associated Protein 9 (CRISPR-Cas9) for functional validation. Finally, we explore the frontier of “Genomic Design,” where Artificial Intelligence (AI) algorithms like Target-Oriented Prioritization (TOP), combined with Speed Breeding 2.0 protocols, promise to accelerate the development of next-generation cultivars. This synthesis underscores the pivotal shift from descriptive genomics to the precision engineering of climate-resilient, high-yielding polyploid crops.

Introduction

Brassica napus L. (rapeseed/oilseed rape/canola) is a globally ubiquitous crop, valued for its high-quality vegetable oil, protein-rich meal for livestock, and its agronomic utility as a break crop in cereal rotations (Friedt et al. 2018; FAO 2023). As the world faces the dual challenges of a growing population and a rapidly changing climate, the demand for resilient, high-yielding B. napus varieties has never been more pressing. However, the path to genetic improvement is fraught with challenges inherent to the species’ genomic architecture.

The B. napus genome is a young, complex allopolyploid formed approximately 7500 years ago via spontaneous hybridization between the diploid progenitors Brassica rapa (A genome, 2n = 20) and Brassica oleracea (C genome, 2n = 18) (Chalhoub et al. 2014). This hybridization event, which likely occurred in the Mediterranean region where the two progenitors were co-cultivated, resulted in a large (~ 1.2 Gb) genome comprising 19 chromosome pairs (A01-A10 and C01-C09). This allopolyploidy confers significant adaptive plasticity but results in extensive gene duplication and subgenome dominance, where the C subgenome has historically been viewed as dominant in terms of gene retention and expression, although recent evidence suggests a more complex, “nuclear chimera” model of interaction (Sun et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2019).

The genomic complexity is further compounded by the extensive presence of transposable elements (TEs) and repetitive sequences, which constitute a significant portion of the genome (Chalhoub et al. 2014). These repetitive regions are often considered inaccessible and have historically confounded assembly efforts, resulting in collapsed duplications and fragmented references that obscure critical regulatory elements and centromeric functions. Additionally, the breeding history of B. napus involves a significant genetic bottleneck (Cowling 2007). Intensive selection for “double-low” traits (low erucic acid in oil and low glucosinolates in meal) during the late-twentieth century successfully improved nutritional quality but severely reduced genetic diversity. This narrowed germplasm base limits the crop’s resilience to biotic and abiotic stresses, necessitating the exploration of diverse global germplasm and wild relatives (Lu et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2022).

To address these hurdles, the genomics community has moved from early draft assemblies to sophisticated multi-omics approaches. The De novo assembly of the ‘Darmor-bzh’ reference genome in 2014 ushered in a new era of genomics-assisted breeding. Subsequent advances in high-throughput resequencing, functional genomics, and gene editing have enabled powerful tools like Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) and Genomic Selection (GS) to accelerate trait discovery (Lu et al. 2019; Tang et al. 2021). This review provides an exhaustive synthesis of these advancements, detailing the technological leap from short-read drafts to gap-free T2T genomes, the shift from linear to graph-based pan-genomics, and the integration of AI and synthetic biology into the breeding pipeline.

From draft assemblies to more continuous and complete references

The journey of B. napus genomics mirrors the broader evolution of DNA sequencing technologies. From the initial reliance on Transcriptome-derived markers to the current state-of-the-art gap-free assemblies, each technological leap has unveiled new layers of genomic complexity.

Prior to the availability of a physical reference genome, researchers relied heavily on transcriptome data (mRNAseq) and Expressed Sequence Tags (ESTs) to explore gene expression patterns and associate them with agronomic traits. mRNA sequencing allowed for the analysis of differential gene expression across homoeologous gene pairs, enabling the initial identification of genome dosage changes and homoeologous exchanges (HEs), non-reciprocal recombination events between the A and C subgenomes. These early studies linked HEs to phenotypic variations in traits such as seed glucosinolate content and oil biosynthesis, but the lack of a positional reference limited the ability to map these variations to specific genomic coordinates (Bancroft et al. 2011; Harper 2012; He et al. 2017).

The release of the ‘Darmor-bzh’ assembly marked a seminal milestone (Chalhoub et al. 2014). This genome was assembled into 849.7 Mb using a hybrid approach combining 454 and Illumina short-read sequencing. The final assembly achieved a contig N50 of 37.9 kb, with 84% of the sequence anchored to chromosomes. Its completeness and continuity were further supported by a Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) score of 97.6% and a Long Terminal Repeat (LTR) Assembly Index (LAI) score of < 10. It confirmed the allopolyploid nature of the species and highlighted the phenomenon of subgenome dominance (Cn > An). Crucially, it revealed that widespread HEs drive gene loss and expression divergence, particularly the retention of oil biosynthesis genes over glucosinolate genes during domestication. However, the inherent limitations of short-read sequencing meant that repetitive regions, particularly TEs and centromeres, were largely unresolved. These regions were often collapsed into single consensus sequences or omitted entirely, resulting in “gaps” that obscured the true structural complexity of the genome.

A transformative shift occurred with the advent of long-read sequencing technologies, specifically PacBio Single Molecule, Real-Time (SMRT) sequencing and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT). These platforms, capable of generating reads spanning tens of kilobases to megabases, allowed researchers to span complex repetitive regions that were previously unbridgeable. This technological leap was first applied to refine existing references (Table 1). Bayer et al. (2017) utilized long-read data to improve ‘Darmor-bzh’ and generate a De novo assembly for the winter-type cultivar ‘Tapidor’ (634 Mb). This study demonstrated that assembly methods significantly influence gene prediction counts and structural accuracy. By applying Oxford Nanopore (ONT) sequencing, Rousseau-Gueutin et al. (2020) achieved a dramatic refinement of the ‘Darmor-bzh’ assembly. The improved genome featured a contig N50 > 10 Mb, a BUSCO score of 98.6%, and an LAI of 15–18, which enabled the resolution of complex repeat regions previously unassailable with short reads. More efforts have expanded beyond winter types to include semi-winter and spring ecotypes, which are critical for Asian and Canadian breeding programs, respectively (Table 1). (1) ‘ZS11’ (Semi-Winter): Initially assembled using a BAC-to-BAC (Bacterial Artificial Chromosome to Bacterial Artificial Chromosome) strategy, this genome was upgraded using PacBio and Hi-C to create ‘ZS11_PB’ (921.5 Mb; contig N50 1.64 Mb, BUSCO 92%, LAI 12–14). This high-quality assembly provided clear evidence of genomic introgression from B. rapa, particularly in vernalization genes like BnFLC3, clarifying the genetic basis of the semi-winter ecotype’s adaptation to temperate climates (Sun et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2021). (2) 'Ningyou 7' (Semi-Winter): Assembled using a combination of Illumina, PacBio, and Hi-C, this genome (994 Mb, contig N50 1.27 Mb, BUSCO 98.4%, LAI 12) served as a resource for analyzing selection footprints and deleterious variation accumulated during the domestication of Asian rapeseed (Zou et al. 2019). (3) 'GH06' (Yellow-Seeded): The assembly of this yellow-seeded cultivar was instrumental in identifying the causal regulator of flavonoid biosynthesis, providing a genetic roadmap for breeding high-oil yellow-seeded cultivars (Qu et al. 2023). (4) 'Xiaoyun' (Spring type): This cultivar serves as an ideal model for functional genomics, akin to Arabidopsis thaliana, due to its short lifecycle. The final assembly is of high quality, demonstrating exceptional completeness (BUSCO: 98.26%) and proficient assembly of repetitive sequences (LAI > 20). It is accessible via the Brassica napus Genomics Database of Xiaoyun (BnGDXY) database (Wang et al. 2024a).

The most significant recent breakthrough has been the achievement of T2T and gap-free assemblies (Li et al. 2023). T2T assembly represents the gold stand of genomic completeness, offering a complete, continuous sequence of each chromosome from one telomere to the other, resolving all centromeric satellites, TEs, and ribosomal DNA arrays. The assembly of the cultivar 'Xiang5A' represents the pinnacle of this generation. Using a combination of PacBio HiFi (High Fidelity) reads, ONT ultra-long reads, and Hi-C chromatin interaction data, researchers generated a gap-free assembly of 1,004.95 Mb. This assembly achieved gap-free status for all 19 chromosomes and complete T2T coverage for 8 chromosomes, surpassing all previous references in contiguity and completeness (BUSCO: 99.6%, LAI: 20.03). The T2T assembly shed light on the previously inaccessible repetitive regions of the genome, particularly centromeric and pericentromeric regions. It revealed that these regions are populated by specific satellite repeats and retrotransposons that were previously collapsed. This resolution is critical for understanding chromosome segregation and stability in polyploids. The gap-free nature of the assembly allowed for the identification of structural variations (SVs) that interrupt gene function but are invisible to short-read mapping. The study identified 917 flowering-related genes affected by SVs, including BnaA03.VERNALIZATION INSENSITIVE 3 (BnaA03.VIN3) and BnaC04.HIGH EXPRESSION OF OSMOTICALLY RESPONSIVE GENES 1 (BnaC04.HOS1) (Fig. 1). These SVs include large insertions and copy-number variations that modulate flowering time, playing a crucial role in the adaptation of 'Xiang5A' to the specific photoperiod and temperature conditions of the Yangtze River Basin. The trajectory of B. napus genomics from 'Darmor-bzh' to 'Xiang5A' represents a shift from fragmented draft scaffolds to high-resolution, contiguous chromosome assemblies. The progressive improvement in LAI scores, from < 10 in drafts to > 20 in T2T assemblies, quantifies the successful conquest of the genome’s repetitive regions, including the centromeres. By leveraging T2T technologies, the community has overcome the historical artifact of homoeologous collapsing, revealing a genome that is structurally fluid yet genetically intact. T2T technology overcomes these challenges by resolving the inaccessible genomic regions, using ultra-long reads to span entire centromeres and ribosomal DNA arrays that were previously collapsed, and enabling precise subgenome discrimination. This allows for the identification of structurally variable, functionally important genes, such as 917 flowering-related genes (e.g., BnaA03.VIN3 and BnaC04.HOS1), that were invisible to short-read mapping (Li et al. 2023). Furthermore, it provides a high-resolution view of synteny and fractionation, revealing that 'Xiang5A' has undergone near-complete triplication and allotetraploidy relative to A. thaliana. Together, these advances move the field beyond descriptive genomics and toward the precision engineering of polyploid crops.

Unlike diploid genomics, polyploid research must uniquely navigate the complexities of subgenome dominance and HEs, where redundancy often obscures functional inference. While standard approaches suffice for diploid alleles, they frequently conflate highly similar homoeologs in B. napus. Emerging T2T assemblies and long-read sequencing now solve this by precisely distinguishing A and C subgenomes, even within repetitive centromeres. This resolution is uniquely critical for polyploids, enabling researchers to disentangle the specific dosage effects and asymmetric selection that drive evolution, rather than viewing the genome as a collapsed consensus.

Beyond a single genome: pan-genomes and the dispensable genome

The sequencing of multiple high-quality genomes has fundamentally shifted the paradigm of genomic research from a single reference genome to the pan-genome. This concept posits that the genome of a species is not a static entity but a dynamic collection of “core” genes (shared by all individuals) and “dispensable” genes (present only in a subset). In B. napus, the dispensable genome is substantial, comprising approximately 38–53% of the total gene content.

The dispensable genome: A reservoir for adaptation

This extensive variability is primarily manifested as Presence–Absence Variations (PAVs), driven by SVs such as insertions, deletions, and TE activity. Studies have shown that the dispensable genome is not merely “junk” DNA but a critical reservoir for adaptive traits.

Hurgobin et al. (2018) constructed a pan-genome from 53 accessions, revealing that HEs generate significant structural variation, particularly within the resistome (genes involved in disease resistance). Crucially, they found that 69.4% of identified disease resistance (R-) genes were variable (non-core), suggesting that the dispensable genome is the primary source of genetic variation for responding to biotic stress.

Furthermore, dispensable genes are often enriched for functions related to abiotic stress tolerance. For instance, in B. napus and related Brassica species, dispensable genes have been linked to responses to salt, drought, and heat stress, as well as defense against pathogens like Plasmodiophora brassicae (clubroot).

From linear to graph pan-genomes

Traditional linear pan-genomes, which list presence/absence against a single reference, often fail to represent complex nested variations and lose positional information for non-reference sequences (Vernikos 2020). To overcome this limitation, the research field is transitioning toward graph-based pan-genomes. A pan-genome graph encodes population-level diversity as a network of nodes (sequences) and edges (paths), allowing for the representation of complex topologies like inversions and nested insertions that linear references cannot capture (Bayer et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2023; Shi et al. 2023).

A landmark study by Afsharyan et al. (2025) constructed a high-resolution SV atlas and graph pan-genome using long-read sequencing from 94 eco-geographically diverse B. napus accessions. This cohort included winter, spring, and semi-winter oilseed types, as well as swede (rutabaga) and kale morphotypes, providing a comprehensive view of the species’ structural diversity.

This graph-based approach cataloged over 3.6 million SVs, a magnitude of variation previously invisible to Single-Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP)-based studies. This massive dataset allows for the precise genotyping of complex alleles. The study revealed a distinct bias in collective SV distribution toward the A subgenome, indicating it is more structurally fluid. However, an asymmetrical selection pattern favoring the C subgenome was observed. This suggests a functional dichotomy: The C subgenome is under tighter selective constraint, likely preserving essential housekeeping functions, while the A subgenome functions as a primary reservoir for structural plasticity and genetic diversification. Large-scale inversions were identified as critical drivers of diversification. Unlike other SVs, selection signatures for inversions did not show subgenome asymmetry. Instead, they were enriched in paracentric chromosome regions. These inversions likely act as recombination suppressors, maintaining adaptive haplotypes, or “supergenes,” intact during breeding, thereby preserving co-adapted gene complexes essential for survival. The graph pan-genome linked specific large chromosomal deletions and duplications to the divergence of morphotypes. For instance, the distinction between swede (enlarged hypocotyl/root) and oilseed rape is driven by copy-number variations (CNVs) in specific loci. The study pinpointed copy-number variation in flowering-time genes BnFLC.C09 and BnATX2.C08, and the cell wall development gene BnCEL2.C08 in the C subgenome as key differentiators. This highlights how structural changes in gene dosage can drive gross morphological evolution.

Historically dismissed as “junk DNA,” repetitive elements like TEs and satellite repeats are now recognized as essential architects of diversity in the B. napus genome. Their non-random distribution, concentrated in pericentromeric regions yet interspersed within gene-rich euchromatin, places them in positions to profoundly influence gene expression and structure. This dense TE landscape presents a double-edged sword: While providing the homology for nonallelic homologous recombination (NAHR) driven SVs (deletions, duplications, inversions), their mobilization can also disrupt essential genes. B. napus employs epigenetic silencing to suppress TE activity, but this control is “leaky” during events like polyploidization, permitting bursts of transposition that generate new variation. Consequently, TEs are fundamental drivers of genome evolution, acting through NAHR and retrotransposition to create a dynamic landscape of structural diversity. This is exemplified by their direct role in shaping inversions—recent pan-genome analyses show TEs, particularly Copia and Gypsy elements, comprise over 76% of inversion breakpoints. Beyond structure, TEs functionally reshape the regulatory code, acting as distal enhancers to boost yield (e.g., GTR2, CYP78A9) or as dosage regulators to fine-tune flowering time (e.g., FLC) (Afsharyan et al. 2025). More recently, Yang et al. (2026) constructed a comprehensive pan-TE map across 2311 B. napus accessions, demonstrating that TEs are key drivers of phenotypic diversity rather than “junk DNA.” They identified over 1400 TEs associated with agronomic traits, notably discovering a CACTA-like insertion that functions as a distal enhancer by recruiting MYB transcription factors to upregulate BnaA09.CYP78A9, thereby increasing seed weight and silique length. Thus, far from being genomic passengers, TEs are pivotal architects of evolutionary adaptation in B. napus.

These findings underscore that the “dispensable genome” and SVs are not merely redundant variation but are mechanistic drivers of evolution and domestication in B. napus. By utilizing graph pan-genomes, breeders can now visualize and select for these complex variants, unlocking a new dimension of genetic diversity for crop improvement.

Large-scale genome resequencing of rapeseed genetic populations

The advent of high-throughput sequencing has revolutionized plant genomics, enabling large-scale resequencing projects that comprehensively catalog genetic variation across global collections of B. napus cultivars, landraces, and synthetic lines. These efforts have generated millions of SNPs and InDels, providing an unprecedented resource for studying population structure, evolutionary history, and the genetic basis of key agronomic traits. To facilitate the use of these vast datasets, research institutions have developed specialized online databases. BnaSNPDB (Yan et al. 2020) and BnaGVD (Yan et al. 2021) allow for the efficient retrieval and analysis of genomic variations, offering modules for Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) visualization, phylogenetic analysis, and GWAS integration. Representing a significant expansion in scale and diversity, the BnlR database (Yang et al. 2023) integrates six distinct omics layers, i.e., genomics, transcriptomics, variomics, epigenetics, phenomics, and metabolomics, from over 2,300 accessions. These comprehensive resources provide an unparalleled platform for functional genomics and the acceleration of molecular breeding in rapeseed. While specialized repositories like BnaSNPDB and the multi-omics hub BnlR have centralized access to massive variation datasets, significant barriers to widespread adoption persist. The transition to T2T and graph-based models introduce computational complexities and data volumes that often exceed the infrastructure of practical breeding programs. Consequently, a “translation gap” remains; effectively converting millions of cataloged SNPs and complex structural variations into simple, cost-effective markers for field selection is the next critical challenge. Future databases must evolve from passive data repositories into active, breeder-centric decision support systems.

Origins and domestication history

To elucidate the origin of this allopolyploid species, Lu et al. (2019) resequenced 588 diverse accessions. Their analysis suggested the A subgenome evolved from the European turnip type of B. rapa, while the C subgenome originated from a common ancestor of several B. oleracea subspecies. Demographic modeling estimated that the hybridization event forming the original winter oilseed B. napus occurred approximately 1910–7180 years ago.

This study also identified distinct selection signals during two improvement stages: (1) Initial Domestication: Involved subgenome-specific selection on defense-response and oil accumulation genes, particularly in the A subgenome. (2) Modern Breeding: Targeted loci controlling seed quality, such as glucosinolate transport and fatty acid elongation, to meet industrial and nutritional standards.

Ecotype divergence and adaptation

Addressing the genetic basis of ecotype divergence, Wu et al. (2019) resequenced 991 global accessions to investigate the differentiation into winter, semi-winter, and spring types. Population structure analysis clearly separated these ecotypes, while LD decay patterns indicated stronger bottlenecks and lower diversity in the C subgenome compared to the A subgenome.

Selective sweep analysis identified regions enriched for genes regulating flowering time and hormone signaling. Notably, GWAS highlighted significant associations between flowering time variation and promoter polymorphisms in orthologs of key floral integrators, FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) and FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC). Ecotype-specific SNPs in the upstream regions of these genes were found to correlate with expression differences, underpinning adaptation to varying vernalization requirements and photoperiods.

More recently, Hu et al. (2022) significantly advanced rapeseed genomics by conducting deeper whole-genome resequencing of 418 diverse accessions (average depth 15.7x), identifying 7.5 million SNPs and 469,000 InDels. This high-resolution dataset enabled a comprehensive assessment that uncovered 628 associated loci for 56 agronomic traits. This study precisely characterized selective sweeps during modern breeding, identifying key genes under selection for adaptation (e.g., BnPIN2 for leaf architecture) and seed quality (e.g., BnRRF for seed weight).

Homoeologous exchanges as drivers of domestication

A critical insight from recent resequencing efforts is the pivotal role of HEs in domestication. HEs are non-reciprocal recombination events where a segment of one subgenome is replaced by its homoeolog from the other subgenome. Recent analysis of whole-genome resequencing data from diverse morphotypes revealed that HEs are not random errors but frequent, asymmetric events that have been positively selected during crop improvement (Wang et al. 2024c).

Specifically, there is a marked bias where the A subgenome more frequently replaces C genome segments (A > C replacement). These asymmetric HE events are concentrated in specific genomic regions and vary significantly among populations. For example, specific HEs have been linked to the loss of functional glucosinolate pathway genes in the C subgenome, which were replaced by non-functional or lower-expression alleles from the A subgenome. This mechanism was crucial in the breeding of “double-low” varieties, demonstrating that HEs are a major driver of rapid phenotypic evolution in polyploids.

Reference bias and the overcoming by graph-based pan-genomes

In the early days, rapeseed genomics relied on linear reference genomes like 'Darmor-bzh' or 'ZS11_PB' for resequencing studies. This single-reference approach, while foundational, introduced significant “reference bias,” where sequences absent from or highly divergent in the reference genome were systematically lost. This was a critical limitation for B. napus, a species where a substantial portion (38–53%) of its total gene content is “dispensable.” The shift to pan-genomics has overcome this by moving from a single linear sequence to a collective that includes both “core” and “dispensable” genes. The frontier of this effort is the graph-based pan-genome, which encodes population diversity as a network of sequences and paths. By integrating diverse, high-quality assemblies, including T2T genomes, this model fundamentally solves reference bias by ensuring every sequence from any accession can be represented in its correct genomic context, thereby capturing complex SVs like large inversions that are invisible to linear mapping.

The application of graph-based pan-genomes has highlighted the pivotal role of SVs as functional engines of diversity in rapeseed population analysis. Unlike SNPs, SVs are major drivers of significant phenotypic divergence. For instance, analyses of a 94-accession graph pan-genome revealed that CNVs, a type of SV, underpin the morphological differentiation between oilseed rape and swede morphotypes. Furthermore, the dispensable genome, rich in SVs, acts as a crucial reservoir for adaptation, with nearly 70% of disease resistance genes being variable. SV analysis also reveals subgenome asymmetry, showing the A subgenome functions as a primary reservoir for structural plasticity and genetic diversification, while the C subgenome is more constrained. Thus, by cataloging millions of SVs, graph pan-genomes provide the resolution needed to link structural diversity to agronomic traits, moving the field toward precision breeding (Afsharyan et al. 2025).

The integrated multi-omics pipeline for agronomic trait discovery in B. napus

To identify causal genes for important agronomic traits within the complex, polyploid genome of B. napus, researchers have developed an efficient strategy that integrates five distinct analytical steps. This pipeline is designed to overcome genetic redundancy and distinguish true biological causality from statistical association (Chen et al. 2012; Xuan et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2020, 2024b; Long et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2023, 2024; Tu et al. 2025) (Fig. 2). The foundation of this approach is the establishment of a core germplasm collection, typically comprising 200–300 rigorously selected accessions to capture most of the global genetic variation. For instance, the core collection established by Xuan et al. (2020) and improved by Guo et al. (2022) selected approximately 280–290 accessions from a pool of over 1,000. While smaller than earlier populations, such as the 991 accessions used by Wu et al. (2019), 588 accessions by Lu et al. (2019), and 378 accessions by Arifuzzaman et al. (2019), this optimized set captures > 95% of the SNPs and InDels present in the larger global panels. From statistical association to biological causation

While GWAS leverages natural genetic diversity to scan for variants correlated with phenotypic traits, a critical distinction must be drawn between statistical “association” and biological “causation.” Association refers to a measurable correlation, often identified through significance thresholds (e.g., P values), but does not inherently imply that a variant directly influences a trait. To bridge this gap, the pipeline integrates high-resolution genotyping with Bulk Segregant Analysis sequencing (BSA-seq) and tissue-specific transcriptomics.

BSA-seq is, particularly, effective for traits with extreme phenotypes. By pooling DNA from extreme individuals (e.g., highest vs. lowest seed weight) in a segregating population, researchers can rapidly identify genomic regions with skewed allele frequencies, pinpointing the causative loci with high precision (Geng et al. 2025). This is often coupled with RNA-seq to identify Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) within these mapped regions, drastically narrowing the candidate pool from hundreds to fewer than twenty.

Prioritization and functional validation

Following candidate filtration, the workflow utilizes allelic divergence mapping to pinpoint functional SNPs or InDels within coding or regulatory sequences. This process is further enhanced by epigenomic mapping techniques, such as ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq, which identify causal non-coding regulatory elements (CREs) and resolve complex regulatory logic between subgenomes. The final and most critical step is high-throughput functional validation. Given the genetic redundancy of B. napus, validation often requires multiplex CRISPR-Cas9 editing (e.g., creating quadruple mutants) to elicit a clear loss-of-function phenotype. Advanced systems, including CRISPR-TSKO (Tissue-Specific Knockout), are increasingly employed to generate stable mutants without pleiotropic defects (Gocal 2021). Once a causative gene is confirmed, techniques such as complementation cloning, where the wild-type phenotype is restored in a mutant line via transformation, provide definitive proof of function.

BnRRF: Regulating seed weight via ribosome recycling

Seed weight is a critical yield component, yet its molecular regulation in polyploids has been elusive. The discovery of BnRRF (Ribosome Recycling Factor) exemplifies the power of the integrated pipeline. Researchers utilized a combination of BSA-seq and RNA-seq on an F2 population derived from a cross between a large-seeded parent (ZS11, 4.9 g) and a small-seeded parent (R140, 2.6 g) (Geng et al. 2025). BSA-seq initially mapped the seed weight trait to major genomic regions on chromosomes A06, A08, A09, and C07/C08. By intersecting these loci with RNA-seq data from developing seeds, the candidate list was narrowed to DEGs. BnRRF, located on chromosome C08, was identified as a key positive regulator. Functional characterization revealed that BnRRF expression is positively correlated with seed weight. Interestingly, the functional difference between the parents was linked to promoter variations: the large-seeded parent contained specific MYC-motifs and Wound-Unresponsive (WUR)-motifs in the BnRRF promoter that were absent or modified in the small-seeded parent. These motifs are implicated in gene expression regulation and cell proliferation control. Validation via overexpression confirmed that BnRRF significantly increases seed weight, potentially by enhancing translational efficiency and ribosome recycling during the rapid cell division phase of seed development (Geng et al. 2025).

BnA09MYB47a: The switch for seed color and oil content

The yellow-seed trait in B. napus is highly prized because it is associated with a thinner seed coat, lower fiber content, and higher oil content. Using high-quality genome assemblies of yellow-seeded 'GH06' and black-seeded 'ZY821', researchers fine-mapped a major Quantitative Trait Locus (QTL) on chromosome A09 (Qu et al. 2023). The causal gene was identified as BnA09MYB47a, which encodes an R2R3-MYB transcription factor. Functional studies revealed a distinct regulatory mechanism: The black-seed allele (BnA09MYB47a_ZY821) actively promotes flavonoid biosynthesis by directly binding to the promoter of BnTT18 (an anthocyanidin synthase) and activating its expression. This leads to the accumulation of proanthocyanidins (pigments) in the seed coat. In contrast, the yellow-seed allele (BnA09MYB47a_GH06) carries structural variations that render it non-functional or unable to bind the BnTT18 promoter. This disruption breaks the flavonoid pathway, preventing pigment accumulation and resulting in the transparent, yellow seed coat. This discovery provides a precise molecular marker for breeding high-oil yellow-seeded varieties and elucidates the specific regulatory network controlling flavonoid flux in rapeseed (Qu et al. 2023).

BnPIN2: Shaping plant architecture via auxin transport

Plant architecture, particularly leaf angle, is a key determinant of planting density and photosynthetic efficiency. Through GWAS on a diversity panel, BnPIN2 (an auxin efflux carrier) on chromosome A03 was identified as a major regulator of leaf angle (Gao et al. 2018). Haplotype analysis showed that specific natural variants in BnPIN2 correlate with erect leaf architecture, a trait desirable for high-density planting. Mechanistically, BnPIN2 regulates the polar auxin transport. Variants that alter its expression or cellular localization lead to asymmetric auxin distribution in the leaf petiole and joint (pulvinus). This differential auxin gradient results in a more upright leaf angle. This gene likely interacts with other architecture regulators, such as BnLAZY1, to fine-tune the plant’s gravitropic response and optimize light capture in dense canopies (Gao et al. 2018).

BnaICS7: A haplotype-specific regulator of clubroot resistance

Clubroot disease, caused by Plasmodiophora brassicae, poses a severe threat to the yield and quality of B. napus. Through genome-wide association analysis of 418 rapeseed accessions, Xue et al. (2024) identified significant associations between clubroot resistance and variations in the ICS gene family, which is central to salicylic acid biosynthesis. A key causal gene, BnaICS7, was pinpointed on chromosome A06. Haplotype analysis revealed a distinct functional mechanism: The predominant haplotype HapA was significantly associated with a lower disease index, while alternative haplotypes (HapB and HapC) with non-synonymous mutations in exon 3 led to increased susceptibility. This suggests that specific allelic variations in BnaICS7 critically influence the plant’s defense response, likely by modulating the SA-mediated signaling pathway. The identification of the resistant HapA provides a valuable genetic marker for breeding clubroot-resistant rapeseed varieties and highlights the role of natural variation in a salicylic acid biosynthesis gene in pathogen defense.

BnaA01g23120D: a genetic bridge linking root microbiome to nitrogen acquisition

Nitrogen uptake efficiency is critical for crop productivity and environmental sustainability. Through large-scale multi-omics analyses of 175 B. napus accessions, Li et al. (2025) integrated genomic, transcriptomic, and rhizosphere microbiome data to identify host genetic factors regulating beneficial plant–microbe interactions. A key locus associated with nitrogen uptake and microbial abundance was fine-mapped, and the causal gene was identified as BnaA01g23120D, encoding a serine/threonine kinase involved in ABA signaling. Functional studies revealed a distinct mechanism: the expression of this gene is regulated by distant eQTL hotspots and is essential for the plant’s response to the beneficial bacterium Sphingopyxis. This bacterium enhances auxin biosynthesis, thereby promoting lateral root development and nitrogen acquisition. Crucially, mutation of BnaA01g23120D ortholog in A. thaliana abolished the bacterium’s ability to promote root growth, demonstrating its indispensable role in mediating this beneficial interaction. This discovery provides a genetic target for breeding rapeseed varieties that can better harness the root microbiome for improved nitrogen uptake efficiency.

Future perspectives for B. napus research: from genomic completeness to intelligent breeding

Despite remarkable progress in sequencing and functional genomics, the next decade of B. napus research must transcend simple gene discovery. Future endeavors will need to focus on resolving the inaccessible regions of the genome, deciphering the regulatory logic of polyploidy, and integrating artificial intelligence and synthetic biology to revolutionize breeding.

The era of telomere-to-telomere graph pan-genomes

The future of B. napus genomics is moving beyond simple, linear maps toward a comprehensive graph pan-genome. This transition is no longer theoretical; the recent “Xiang5A” assembly has explicitly demonstrated the feasibility of gap-free sequencing in this complex polyploid (Li et al. 2023). Building on this breakthrough, researchers can now integrate Telomere-to-Telomere (T2T) assemblies from hundreds of diverse accessions. This is essential for capturing the “dispensable” genome, which contains variable genes often missed in standard references that are crucial for disease resistance and stress adaptation. Crucially, these gap-free assemblies finally resolve complex regions like centromeres and telomeres. Deciphering centromeres is vital for understanding the stability of B. napus as an allotetraploid. By comparing the centromeres of the A and C subgenomes, researchers can explain how chromosomes pair and segregate correctly during meiosis. Furthermore, fully resolved centromere sequences enable the precise manipulation of the centromere-specific histone protein, CENH3. This could revolutionize breeding by facilitating haploid induction systems, allowing for the rapid creation of pure breeding lines. Similarly, complete telomere sequences offer insights into plant longevity. Telomere integrity is directly linked to aging and how plants respond to environmental stress. A graph pan-genome that accurately captures these repetitive regions not only explains morphological differences but also unlocks new strategies for breeding climate-resilient crops (Afsharyan et al. 2025).

AI-driven breeding and “Genomic Design”

The integration of multi-omics data marks a paradigm shift from descriptive genomics to AI-driven “Genomic Design.” Machine Learning and Deep Learning are now central to breeding, offering predictive capabilities that far surpass traditional statistics. Phenotypic Prediction has evolved significantly; Deep Learning models like convolutional neural networks (CNNs) analyze Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) imagery to predict yield and detect diseases with > 95% accuracy (Wan et al. 2021; Gan et al. 2025). However, the most transformative application is Genotype × Environment (G × E) prediction. Beyond static phenotyping, advanced AI models now integrate historical and real-time meteorological data with multi-omics profiles to decode G × E interactions. This allows breeders to forecast performance under erratic climate conditions, accelerating the development of “climate-resilient” crops. Simultaneously, Genomic Selection 2.0 is replacing older models with algorithms like Target-Oriented Prioritization (TOP). TOP learns correlations among thousands of traits, enabling breeders to simulate crosses in silico rather than in the field (Crossa et al. 2017). By comparing untested genotypes against a pre-defined “ideotype,” breeders can efficiently prioritize parents that balance yield, quality, and environmental adaptability (Wang et al. 2021). Combined with high-precision Trait Identification via hyperspectral imaging (Liu et al. 2025), these tools create a holistic, data-driven breeding pipeline.

Integrating insights across these genomic scales creates a unified framework for precision breeding. While T2T assemblies resolve the structurally complex regions essential for chromosomal stability, graph pan-genomes catalog the comprehensive reservoir of dispensable variations driving adaptation. By layering this structural resolution with multi-omics data, AI models can now transcend simple statistical associations to decode complex G × E interactions. This multi-scalar synergy allows breeders to trace phenotypes—from root morphology to stress resilience—directly back to specific structural variants and regulatory networks, transforming diverse genomic layers into a cohesive blueprint for the “Genomic Design” of future cultivars.

Speed breeding 2.0: accelerating the cycle

Ultimately, genomic success is reflected in its translation into field-ready varieties. Bridging the gap between gene cloning and cultivar release requires the integration of optimized protocols such as Speed Breeding 2.0, an optimized protocol for B. napus that utilizes 22-h photoperiods and specific temperature cycling (22 °C/17 °C), to achieve 4–5 generations per year (Song et al. 2021). This acceleration in breeding is, particularly, compatible with CRISPR-Cas9 applications, which enables breeders to quickly segregate out transgenes and achieve homozygosity for edited alleles, compressing years of stabilizing selection into months. This is especially crucial for ambitious targets like fixation of heterosis. Future possibilities include combining Speed Breeding with a CRISPR-based MiMe (Mitosis instead of Meiosis) system (Wang et al. 2024d), which can effectively turn sexual reproduction into apomixis and generate clonal seeds, thereby rapidly fixing heterosis and preventing the segregation of traits in subsequent generations.

By integrating these rapid-cycle approaches with AI-driven genomic selection, breeders can swiftly introgress beneficial alleles (e.g., BnRRF) into elite backgrounds. This integrated pipeline represents the fastest route to developing resilient, high-yielding B. napus varieties essential for global food security. To ensure concrete gains in yield stability and sustainability over the next decade, research must pivot to three near-term priorities: (1) operationalizing graph pan-genomes to harness structural variations for disease resistance; (2) deploying AI-driven G × E models to predict performance under erratic climates; and (3) scaling Speed Breeding pipelines to rapidly fix complex traits. Prioritizing these translational technologies over purely descriptive studies offers the most direct route to developing climate-resilient cultivars.

Conclusion

The field of Brassica genomics has undergone a revolutionary transformation. We have moved from fragmented, short-read draft assemblies to gap-free T2T genomes that illuminate the deepest recesses of the genome. We have transitioned from static linear references to dynamic graph pan-genomes that capture the full spectrum of structural diversity. And, we have evolved from simple marker-assisted selection to AI-driven “Genomic Design” and synthetic biology. These advances provide the toolkit necessary to unravel the complexities of this allopolyploid crop, enabling the precise engineering of traits for yield, quality, and climate resilience. It is crucial to distinguish between tools currently reshaping breeding and those still on the horizon. While marker-assisted selection and GWAS-derived markers are actively deployed for traits like disease resistance and oil quality, advanced strategies like Graph Pan-Genomes and AI-driven “Genomic Design” remain largely at the proof-of-concept stage. Their widespread adoption is currently limited by the high computational cost of processing T2T datasets and the need for user-friendly interfaces accessible to breeders. Similarly, while CRISPR-Cas9 and synthetic apomixis offer revolutionary potential for fixing heterosis, their deployment faces significant regulatory hurdles in many regions, alongside the technical challenge of ensuring stable, off-target-free editing in complex polyploids. Bridging this gap requires not just technological refinement, but regulatory alignment and the development of cost-effective, high-throughput genotyping platforms that can translate deep genomic insights into practical field selection. As we integrate these technologies, the complex/inaccessible regions of the rapeseed genome are no longer obstacles, but resources for the future of sustainable agriculture.
