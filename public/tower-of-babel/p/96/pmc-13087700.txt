Large pleuropulmonary blastoma mimicking congenital pulmonary airway malformation in an infant: A case report
Authors: Katherine Safsten Bekker, Alyssa M Gill, James M Decou, David J Hoogstra, Erica Michiels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPleuropulmonary blastoma (PPB) is a rare pediatric lung malignancy that primarily involves the lungs and pleura. PPB can be easily misdiagnosed as congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM) based on its cystic features. We report the case of an infant who presented to the emergency department with fever and respiratory distress. The patient tested positive for COVID-19, and imaging was initially suggestive of large CPAM in the right hemithorax causing compression of the right lung and midline shift to the left. Imaging also showed two hypodense lesions, one on the upper pole of the right kidney and the other on the right lobe of the thyroid. Prompt surgical removal of the lung lesion was indicated given the patient's worsening respiratory status and increasing mediastinal shift. Following thoracoscopic resection, histopathologic testing indicated PPB type 1r with DICER1 pathogenic variants, rather than CPAM. Introduction

Pleuropulmonary blastoma (PPB) is a rare pediatric neoplasm of the lung and pleura that primarily presents in the first 4-5 years of life [1]. There are multiple reports of PPB initially being misinterpreted as a congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM) on imaging [2,3]. The variable clinical presentations between PPB patients make it difficult to distinguish from CPAM in settings like an emergency department (ED). Even with few specific signs and symptoms, many PPB patients will present with coughing, shortness of breath, and fever [3]. This case report describes a COVID-positive infant diagnosed with a large cystic lung lesion and mediastinal shift requiring prompt surgical intervention. Differential diagnosis included tension pneumothorax and CPAM; however, histopathology analysis confirmed PPB. Further testing of tumor cells showed DICER1 somatic pathogenic variant, with concerns for germline changes. However, shortly after the lesion was found, the family moved locations, and management was transferred to a new oncology team.

Case presentation

An infant presented to the ED with upper respiratory infection symptoms of fever, irritability, cough, nasal congestion, and rhinorrhea for the past three days. The patient's mother reported breathing difficulties since birth, but a chest x-ray (CXR) eight months prior was read as unremarkable. However, retrospective reading shows a clear, small, ill-defined opacity in the right infrahilar region (Fig. 1). Prenatal history and ultrasounds were unremarkable, as was the birth history at 39w1d. Family history is pertinent for a benign lung nodule in the father, with additional family history of leukemia, colon cancer, lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, and nephrolithiasis.

Chest x-ray from 8 months prior noting a few ill-defined opacities overlying the right infrahilar region, thought to be related to atelectasis or acute airspace process.

Vital signs were remarkable for an elevated blood pressure of 110/94, tachycardia at 184 bpm, fever of 40.1 C (104.2 F, rectal), and SpO2 between mid-90's to high 80's. On physical examination, there were significant subcostal retractions with more chest wall movement on the left side than on the right. Breath sounds were decreased on the right side. The patient's chest and abdominal muscles were prominent. Complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel were normal while respiratory pathogen testing was positive for COVID-19. CXR showed a large multi-cystic lesion collapsing the right lung and causing a mediastinal shift to the left (Fig. 2). Initial concern was for a tension pneumothorax; however, CT angiogram of the thorax supported evidence of a 11 × 11 × 4 cm multi-cystic lung lesion, suggesting potential CPAM. Two hypodense lesions were also noted on the right kidney and thyroid, and the head circumference was 47.5 cm, in the 90-95th percentile for the patient's age.

Preoperative chest x-ray demonstrating a large cystic lesion in the right hemithorax compressing the right lung with a midline shift of the heart and mediastinum to the left.

Pediatric Surgery was consulted for removal of the lesion. Surgery was initially postponed 5-6 weeks, following COVID resolution; however, a repeat CXR showed exacerbated mediastinal shift and immediate surgery was recommended. For management of the acute COVID-19 infection, the patient was placed on remdesivir by Pediatric Infectious Disease. Two days after presenting to the ED, the patient underwent right thoracoscopic resection of the lesion with Pediatric Surgery. The patient recovered well and postoperative CXR (Fig. 3) confirmed re-expansion of the right lung and resolution of mediastinal shift. The patient was discharged home three days after surgery without complications.

Post-operative expansion of the right lung with resolution off mediastinal shift.

Grossly, the lesion showed a large, multiloculated, thin-walled cystic mass without solid areas or intervening lung parenchyma, characteristic of PPB rather than CPAM [4]. The International PPB/DICER1 Registry analyzed a specimen from the lesion, and found cysts lined by bland epithelium, further distinguishing the mass as a PPB rather than CPAM [5]. Furthermore, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis for p53 showed 15%, suggesting either type I or type Ir [6]. The mass lacked subepithelial primitive mesenchymal cells and showed patchy desmin staining and absent myogenin, suggesting an absence of rhabdomyoblastic differentiation. These findings support involution of neoplastic components of the lesion a PPB type Ir diagnosis [7]. Finally, the lesion was positive for somatic DICER1 pathogenic variants, further indicating PPB [8,9]. Tissue from the lesion was submitted to Mayo Clinic Laboratories for DICER1 analysis. The following clinically relevant variants were detected: NM177438.3(DICER1):c.5425G > A (p.Gly1809Arg) in Exon 25 with a variant allele frequency of 21.9%, representing a somatic missense hotspot mutation in the RNase IIIb domain; and NM177438.3(DICER1):c.2705dup (p.Ser903ValfsTer2) in Exon 17, representing a truncating frameshift mutation resulting in a premature stop codon. These variants, along with the father's benign lung mass, a germline DICER1 pathogenic germline variant was suspect. However, no further history on the father's mass was taken; a cystic lesion rather than a solid mass would be more indicative of a DICER1 variant.

Shortly after the procedure, the parents reported that the patient had returned to normal activities. Patient appeared alert and active during post-operative assessment, though physical exam showed occasional guarding of the surgical site. Parents further reported occasional acute periods of rapid breathing, lasting only a few seconds. Two months following the procedure, the patient's family relocated but will continue to be followed by Pediatric Hematology-Oncology for PPB, with recommendations to test for DICER1 pathogenic germline variant in both the patient and the parents. Recommendations were also given for a CT every three months for at least two years to monitor for recurrence, along with ultrasounds of the thyroid and kidneys to evaluate the lesions seen on initial imaging. A brain MRI was recommended, though likely not indicated given the patient's lack of CNS symptoms.

Discussion

PPB is a rare malignant lung lesion affecting only 25-50 children in the U.S. annually [10]. Type Ir, as seen in this patient, has an excellent prognosis with a near-100% [7] 5-year survival rate. Types I, II and III are associated with progressively worse prognoses, with 5-year survival rates of 91%, 71%, and 53% respectively [7,11]. Due to its cystic and multiseptal nature, it can be difficult to distinguish from CPAM on imaging and the sensitivity and specificity of CT for detecting PPB versus benign lung lesions is 58% and 83%, respectively [12,13]. Tension pneumothorax, as considered in this patient, and pleural effusion are other common misdiagnoses [14]. Thus, diagnosis and subtyping are typically confirmed by histopathology [11]. The mainstay treatment for Type Ir PPB is surgical resection and considering this patient's declining respiratory status and increasing mediastinal shift, urgent surgical resection was indicated regardless of acute illness [10].

This case is particularly interesting because it highlights the importance of distinguishing between type I and type Ir with pathology, requiring IHC and histological analysis. Nelson et al. [8] found that before histological analysis, age and cyst size together are more predictive of whether a lesion is type I or type Ir than either factor alone. Given that this patient was under 1 year old and the cyst was relatively large causing significant mass effect, the lesion had a high probability of being type I (between 78 and 89%). Thus, careful and thorough pathology is necessary, warranting consultation with the International PPB/DICER1 Registry.

DICER1-related tumor predisposition is autosomal dominant with reduced penetrance in some patients, predisposing individuals to various malignant neoplasms, including PPB, rhabdomyosarcoma, and rare CNS tumors [9,15,16]. Other common phenotypes with DICER1-related tumor predisposition include multinodular goiters, lung cysts, structural abnormalities of the kidney and collecting system, retinal abnormalities, and dental anomalies, none of which were reported in the patient at this time [9,17]. Patients may present with macrocephaly [9,18,17], and while this patient does not meet the criteria for macrocephaly, their head circumference was between the 90th and 95th percentile. A brain MRI was initially planned (but not ordered) to screen for rare CNS tumors, but the patient showed no CNS symptoms and screening was likely not necessary, especially before germline testing was done. The utility for CNS tumor screening for asymptomatic patients is unknown [9].

Germline DICER1 pathogenic variants are identified in approximately 66-70% of children with PPB [9] This high prevalence illustrates the central role of DICER1 disruption in PPB pathogenesis and supports the recommendation for DICER1 testing in all children diagnosed with PPB and their first-degree family members [9]. Testing allows care teams to determine the next steps in surveillance: a patient without a DICER1pathogenic variant need not be subject to unnecessary testing, while a patient with pathogenic variants can undergo surveillance for earlier neoplastic detection. It should be noted that some patients may have mosaic findings, with no germline mutation, but multiple tumors positive for DICER1 pathogenic variants. These patients may also require surveillance [9].

Conclusion

This case demonstrates an interesting presentation of PPB. Despite its rarity, PPB should be considered in the differential diagnosis of unusual lung lesions found on CXR in infants and children presenting to the ED. Surgical resection is the mainstay of treatment, and histopathology can confirm diagnosis and typing. Patients with PPB should be followed with Pediatric Hematology Oncology for recurrence with additional tumor surveillance in patients found to have a DICER1 pathogenic germline variant [15]. Additionally, genetic testing may be beneficial for first-degree family members of a child with a pathogenic germline variant in DICER1, particularly other children [19]. If a variant is identified, appropriate surveillance strategies can be implemented to manage their associated tumor risk. As the family moved and changed care providers, effective communication between care teams was essential to ensure continuity, with clear plans established for ongoing surveillance and any additional testing needed for both the child and the parents.
