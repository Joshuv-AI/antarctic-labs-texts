Nuclear Pirin promotes HCC by acting as a key inflammation-facilitating factor
Authors: Huanhuan Ma, Tingyan Cao, Fengqiong Zhang, Dachao Sun, Lili Chen, Yao Lin, Shuqing Lai, Bin Jiang, Yanming Zhou, Jun You, Xiyao Liu, Yue Wang, Furong Lin, Yuxin Liu, Jinyang Wang, Weiling He, Qinxi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic inflammation and elevated reactive oxygen species are key contributors to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression.ObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the role of the oxidative stress sensor protein Pirin (PIR) as a critical mediator of inflammation in HCC progression.DesignWe investigated PIR’s role in HCC tumourigenesis through RNA interference, genetic knockout and pharmaceutical inhibition in HCC cell lines and various mouse models. Furthermore, we used transcriptomics, quantitative reverse transcription PCR, western blot, immunofluorescence staining and immunohistochemistry analysis to elucidate the molecular details.ResultsThis study reveals a novel redox-dependent mechanism governing PIR’s nuclear shuttling, contributing to liver inflammation and HCC progression. We identified a positive feedback axis where nuclear PIR amplifies inflammatory responses, leading to hepatitis and HCC advancement. Cytokines in this loop are regulated by PIR-enhanced v-rel reticuloendotheliosis viral oncogene homolog A (RELA) transcription, promoting PIR’s nuclear translocation, increasing proinflammatory cytokine levels, and disrupting redox balance. We confirmed that liver parenchymal cells produce autocrine cytokines supporting their growth and malignancy. Notably, PIR’s redox-mediated nuclear shift can be inhibited by N-acetyl cysteine or PIR inhibitors, reducing HCC promotion in mice.ConclusionWe elucidate a novel redox-dependent regulatory mechanism governing the nuclear localisation of PIR and its role in promoting liver inflammation and HCC progression. Our findings underscore the significance of cellular redox status in regulating PIR’s activity and highlight the potential of targeting this pathway with antioxidants to mitigate HCC progression.Keywords: REACTIVE OXYGEN SPECIES, INFLAMMATION, HEPATOCELLULAR CARCINOMA

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a common and highly lethal liver cancer with a complex, multifactorial pathogenesis.1 Chronic inflammation and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels are critical drivers of its initiation and progression.2,7 Inflammation conditions in HCC are frequently triggered by factors such as HBV or HCV infection, long-term alcohol abuse or non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)8 9 (see online supplemental table 1 for a list of all abbreviations used in the text). Prolonged inflammatory states lead to the excessive release of proinflammatory factors and cytokines, such as tumour necrosis factor-α (TNFα) and interleukin 6 (IL-6),10 11 which in turn activate pathways like nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3).12 13 NF-κB promotes the expression of inflammatory genes and cytokine secretion, while STAT3 enhances tumour cell proliferation and survival and suppresses apoptosis.14 15 The resulting interplay between these pathways creates a vicious cycle where chronic inflammation triggers elevated ROS production, and ROS further exacerbate inflammation by activating pathways like NF-κB and STAT3.716,18 This cycle of inflammation and oxidative stress accelerates liver damage and tumour progression,19 20 underscoring the need to understand these mechanisms for improved HCC therapies.

Pirin (PIR), an iron-binding protein of the cupin superfamily, is highly expressed in several cancers and regulates processes including cell proliferation, migration and apoptosis.21,26 In vitro research indicates that under oxidative stress, PIR undergoes a redox-dependent transition from Fe²+ to Fe³+, enhancing its ability to regulate NF-κB transcriptional activity and suggesting a role as an oxidative sensor.27 However, these findings need further validation in vivo, particularly regarding PIR’s involvement in oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways in HCC.

In this study, we found that PIR is significantly upregulated in HCC and translocates between the cytoplasm and nucleus via redox-dependent signalling pathways, contributing to the regulation of liver inflammation and HCC progression. Specifically, we identified a cytokine-driven feedforward activation loop that enhances nuclear PIR function through redox mechanisms, which can be disrupted by treatment with N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) or anti-PIR agents. These findings underscore the critical role of PIR in mediating the crosstalk between ROS and inflammation, thereby promoting HCC progression and offering a potential therapeutic target for HCC treatment.

Results

Clinical relevance of PIR expression and cancer

We first analysed PIR expression data across various cancer types according to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and found that PIR is significantly upregulated in several solid tumours, including HCC (online supplemental figure S1A). This upregulation was confirmed in three external individual data sets with paired human HCC (T) and corresponding non-tumour (N) tissues (figure 1A, online supplemental figure S1B,C). Additionally, PIR mRNA expression is slightly upregulated in both NAFLD, viral hepatitis and liver cirrhosis, while it is strongly upregulated in HCC (figure 1B), suggesting a potential role for PIR in the progression of liver disease. Additionally, PIR upregulation in liver cancer cells was verified in an external single-cell atlas of 10 patients with HCC with paired tumour and normal tissues (figure 1C). It is important to note that upregulation of PIR is predominantly in hepatocyte-like cells rather than immune cells (online supplemental figure S1D–F). Hepatocellular gene enrichment analysis (online supplemental figure S1E) and the expression of albumin and alpha-fetoprotein expression (online supplemental figure S1F) were used to verify the major cell clustering. Immunohistochemical analysis further confirmed that the expression of PIR protein is significantly elevated in tumour tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues across three independent cases of HCC (figure 1D). Moreover, Patients with high PIR expression (≥ median expression) were found to be significantly associated with poor overall survival rates (figure 1E). Finally, clinical characteristic analysis based on The Cancer Genome Atlas Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (TCGA-LIHC) cohort showed that higher expression of PIR was significantly associated with more aggressive fibrosis (p=0.012, online supplemental figure S1G). These findings suggest that PIR has significant clinical implications and may play a critical role in the progression of HCC.

PIR facilitates HCC development

To investigate the functions of PIR in HCC, we knocked down PIR in the Huh-7 and HepG2 liver cancer cell lines, which resulted in a significant impairment of cell viability (figure 2A,B and online supplemental figure S2A,B). Reconstitution of PIR expression with wild type (Wt) PIR (rPIR-1) in PIR knockdown cells efficiently restored cell proliferation, confirming the functional specificity of PIR (figure 2C,D and online supplemental figure S2C,D). Mechanistic studies in these cell lines showed that PIR knockdown decreased the expression of the cell cycle regulators Cyclin D1 and E2F1 while increasing Fas expression (figure 2D and online supplemental figure S2D), consistent with our previous studies on other cancer types.26 28 Moreover, reconstitution of PIR expression in PIR knockdown cells reversed the expression of these molecules (figure 2D and online supplemental figure S2D), indicating that these molecules may also be regulated by PIR in human HCC cell lines.

To extend our findings in vivo, we generated two groups of mice, PIR transgenic (Tg) mice with Cre-activated alleles Rosa26-LSL-CAG-PIR and their Wt counterparts (figure 2E), and PIR conditional knockout mice (KO) and their Wt counterparts (online supplemental figure S2E). The successful establishment of these mouse models was confirmed through genotyping and the detection of PIR protein in liver tissues (figure 2F,G and online supplemental figure S2F,G). The mice were then subjected to a well-established HCC induction protocol using diethylnitrosamine and carbon tetrachloride (CCl4), which recapitulates the fibrosis, chronic inflammation and oxidative stress observed during human HCC progression.29 30 As anticipated, PIR overexpression (Tg mice) significantly accelerated HCC progression (figure 2H–K), whereas PIR KO markedly attenuated DEN-induced and CCl4-induced HCC in C57BL/6 mice (online supplemental figure S2H–K). To further validate the role of PIR in tumourigenesis of HCC, we analysed the expression of PIR, Cyclin D1, E2F1 and Fas in tumour, peritumour and normal liver tissues from our DEN/CCl4-induced HCC mice. In tumour tissues, PIR expression was significantly upregulated and positively correlated with Cyclin D1 and E2F1 levels (figure 2L), indicating that Cyclin D1 and E2F1 may also be involved in HCC promoted by PIR as in breast cancer. However, we did not observe a significant difference in Fas levels between tumour, peritumour and normal liver tissues. This may be attributed to the overall low levels of Fas expression in mouse liver tissue and no expression of Fas in poorly differentiated HCC.31 In addition, we observed that DEN or CCl4 treatment could mildly promote PIR expression (online supplemental figure S2L,M). Taken together, these observations confirm that PIR facilitates the development and progression of HCC.

Multiple-transcriptomic profiling reveals a potential role of PIR in activation of inflammatory pathways in hepatocytes

To gain further insight into the molecular signature underlying PIR-promoted HCC, we performed transcriptomic profiling in primary hepatocytes (PHCs) derived from PIR-Tg mice (online supplemental figure S3A,B). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis based on the hallmark of the Molecular Signatures Database32 was used to identify the enriched gene sets. The inflammatory signalling pathways, including the TNF-NF-κB pathway and the IL-6-STAT3 pathway, were significantly enriched (figure 3A). These pathways are known to orchestrate the activation of various immune cells and the production of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines such as TNFα and IL-6, which are critical for the initiation of inflammation (figure 3B). On the contrary, PIR KO in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) that had been transformed with SV40 Large T antigen and exhibited tumour-like characteristics resulted in the opposite gene expression profile (figure 3C,D and online supplemental figure S3C,D). Moreover, bioinformatics analysis of publicly available data sets from TCGA-LIHC and GSE1452033 reveals that PIR expression is indeed positively correlated with the activation of inflammatory pathways in clinical HCC samples (figure 3E,F), further supporting a role for PIR in mediating inflammatory signalling during HCC development.

PIR is involved in the activation of TNFα-NF-κB and IL-6-STAT3 inflammatory pathways

To confirm if PIR is involved in the activation of inflammatory pathways, we primarily detected several key inflammatory cytokines in mouse serum. As shown in figure 4A,B, higher and lower levels of TNFα and IL-6 were separately detected in the serum of PIR Tg mice and KO mice, as compared with their corresponding Wt mice. A similar result was obtained by detecting these two cytokines in the medium in which PHCs from Tg mice were cultured (online supplemental figure S4A), indicating that PIR stimulates the expression and secretion of these cytokines. Next, we evaluated the mRNA expression levels of these cytokines to further confirm the regulatory role of PIR on inflammatory factors. The results showed that the mRNA levels of these cytokines were significantly increased in PHCs from PIR Tg mice, whereas they were significantly decreased in the corresponding cells from PIR KO mice (figure 4C,D). Consistently, knockdown of PIR in human HCC cell lines Huh-7 and HepG2 led to a significant reduction of mRNA levels of these cytokines, which was completely rescued by reconstitution of PIR expression (online supplemental figure S4B,C). These data suggest that PIR functions to stimulate the expression and secretion of TNFα and IL-6 in hepatocytes.

Since TNFα-NF-κB and IL-6-STAT3 pathways are the core inflammatory pathways in promoting HCC progression, we next assessed whether PIR activates NF-κB and IL-6-STAT3 pathways by evaluating the phosphorylation status of their key components. As expected, the levels of p-STAT3 (an indicator of STAT3 pathway activity), p-RELA and p-NF-κB1 (indicators of NF-κB1 pathway activity) were remarkably increased in PHCs and liver tissue from PIR Tg mice (figure 4E,G), and decreased in the same cells and tissue from PIR KO mice (figure 4F,H). This observation was confirmed by the results from PIR Tg (online supplemental figure S4D) and KO (online supplemental figure S4E) mice that were induced with DEN and CCl4, and HepG2 (online supplemental figure S4F) and Huh-7 (online supplemental figure S4G) cell lines with PIR knockdown and further re-expression. In addition, the activity of NF-κB luciferase reporter was diminished by PIR knockdown and rescued by PIR reconstitution in Huh-7 cells (online supplemental figure S4H). In line with this evidence, PIR promoted NF-κB luciferase reporter in a dose-dependent manner, and such effect was dramatically inhibited by Tph A, a reported PIR inhibitor (online supplemental figure S4I). Collectively, these data suggest that PIR promotes inflammatory pathways by stimulating the expression of cytokines, which may contribute to inflammation-associated liver pathogenesis.

Overexpression of PIR in hepatocytes induces hepatitis and hepatic fibrosis signs

The liver is susceptible to both chronic inflammation and hepatic fibrosis, which can be mutually reinforced.34 35 Chronic inflammation can stimulate hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) to produce extracellular matrix proteins and contribute to fibrosis.36,39 Conversely, the fibrotic environment can exacerbate inflammation by promoting proinflammatory cytokine production.36 40 41 To assess the impact of PIR overexpression in promoting hepatitis and hepatic fibrosis, livers of 3-month-old, 6-month-old or 12-month-old Tg and Wt male mice were detected for leucocyte infiltration using H&E staining and collagen accumulation using Picrosirius red staining. As shown in figure 5A, we can observe obvious hepatitis (indicated by leucocyte infiltration) and liver fibrosis (indicated by collagen accumulation) in Tg mice, in particular 12-month-old mice. Consistently, we also found robust activation of HSCs, a major event during the development of liver fibrosis, in Tg mice by monitoring the expression of collagen A1 (figure 5B). Furthermore, the levels of serum glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase (figure 5C) and glutamate pyruvate transaminase (figure 5D), as well as hepatic ROS (indicated by dihydroethidium staining, figure 5E), were significantly elevated in 12-month-old Tg mice, demonstrating hepatocyte damage induced by PIR overexpression. Unexpectedly, clear nuclear localisation of PIR was observed in hepatocytes of 12-month-old Tg mice and seemed to show close correlation with ROS accumulation (figure 5E). In addition to these histological and biochemical analyses, quantitative proteomics of PHCs were analysed, revealing dysregulation of pathways closely associated with hepatic fibrosis pathogenesis, including oxidative phosphorylation, bile acid metabolism, xenobiotic metabolism, fatty acid metabolism and inflammatory pathways (online supplemental figure S5A–C). Taken together, our results demonstrate that PIR overexpression may initially activate proinflammatory signalling pathways which in turn leads to hepatic fibrosis and liver injury ultimately, suggesting that PIR-induced fibrosis may be a key pathogen during its promotion of HCC.

PIR nuclear accumulation synergises with RELA to enhance the transcription of inflammatory cytokines

We aim to further elucidate the detailed mechanism by which PIR regulates the TNFα-NF-κB and IL-6-STAT3 signalling pathways. In vitro studies have shown that PIR, as a redox sensor, can enhance the transcriptional activity of the NF-κB subunit RELA.27 However, our observations indicate that PIR predominantly localises in the cytoplasm in normal liver cells; it remains unclear how PIR functions as a coactivator in the nucleus. Based on the experimental results presented in figure 5E, there appears to be a positive correlation between ROS level and nuclear localisation of PIR protein. We therefore hypothesise that changes in PIR localisation may be related to ROS levels. To investigate this hypothesis, we treated MEF cells expressing RFP-PIR with 200 µM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and observed the real-time localisation of PIR. The results indicated that H2O2 significantly facilitated the translocation of PIR from the cytoplasm to the nucleus (figure 6A and online supplemental figure S6A) and MEFs derived from Tg mice (figure 6B,C). It was noteworthy that MEFs derived from Wt mice exhibited weak nuclear translocation. This may be attributed to the low basal expression of PIR in Wt MEFs, along with the potential loss of PIR during the purification process of nuclear proteins. On H2O2 treatment, both RELA and STAT3 translocated to the nucleus (figure 6D). In fact, we found that the colocalisation between PIR and RELA was significantly enhanced in the nucleus after H2O2 administration, and NAC can reverse this phenomenon (figure 6E and online supplemental figure S6B). In addition, endogenous immunoprecipitation confirmed interaction between PIR and RELA, and the interaction was further enhanced by H2O2 treatment (online supplemental figure S6C). However, there was no significant increase in the colocalisation between PIR and STAT3, despite the fact that they all translocated into the nucleus on H2O2 application (online supplemental figure S6D). The underlying reason may be that the activation of the STAT3 pathway is a consequence of the activation of the NF-κB pathway. Therefore, we suggest that ROS is a key factor regulating the translocation of PIR from cytoplasm to nucleus where it functions as a co-activator of RELA. Our chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments using an anti-RELA antibody in cells with altered PIR expression further supported that its presence modulates the recruitment of RELA to the TNF and IL-6 promoter regions (online supplemental figure S6E). Consistently, clear nuclear localisation of PIR was observed in various human and mouse liver tissues known to exhibit inflammation and high levels of ROS, including human HCC tissue, Tg mouse liver tissue and liver tissues of Wt mice subjected to DEN/CCI4 treatment or high fat diet feed (figure 6F). Hence, we suggest that H2O2 promotes the interaction of PIR and RELA in the nucleus where PIR synergises with RELA to elevate the transcription of inflammatory cytokines. Actually, we observed that H2O2 treatment dramatically stimulated NF-κB activity (online supplemental figure S6F) and elevated its downstream cytokines’ mRNA levels (online supplemental figure S6G,H). Moreover, the interactions between PIR and RELA were abolished by replacement of RELA with a previously reported mutant27 that impairs its interactions with PIR (online supplemental figure S6I). In addition, the activity of the NF-κB signalling pathway in PIR Tg MEFs was significantly suppressed by Tph A (figure 6G), and PIR-promoted cytokines’ expression was completely abrogated by RELA depletion with shRNAs (online supplemental figure S6J) or NF-κB pathway inhibition with N4-[2-(4-phenoxyphenyl)ethyl]-4,6-quinazolinediamine (QNZ) (online supplemental figure S6K).

We aimed to elucidate the mechanism underlying PIR nuclear translocation on oxidative stress. Previous studies have shown that methionine and cysteine residues in proteins are highly susceptible to oxidation by ROS,42 which can affect redox regulation and protein subcellular localisation.42,45 To investigate this, we generated cysteine mutants (C79A and C106A) and methionine mutants (M85A and M93A) of PIR. Cells transfected with Wt and mutant PIR were exposed to H2O2, and PIR localisation was measured by microscopy. We discovered that the two methionine mutants (M85A and M93A) completely suppressed PIR nuclear translocation on H2O2 administration, while the cysteine mutations did not (figure 6H). Consistently, mass spectrometry analysis of immunoprecipitated endogenous PIR from Huh7 cells pretreated with H2O2 revealed that the Met85 and Met93 residues of PIR were oxidised indeed (figure 6I). Furthermore, we demonstrated that on H2O2 treatment, knockdown of PIR significantly suppressed the transcriptional activity of NF-κB. This suppression could be restored by reconstituted expression of Wt PIR but not the PIR M85A/M93A mutant (online supplemental figure S6L).

Taken together, our results suggest that H2O2-promoted nuclear accumulation of PIR synergises with RELA to activate the transcription of inflammatory cytokines, contributing to the pathogenesis of the liver disease.

There exists a PIR-facilitated positive feedback loop in the progression of inflammation

The connection between inflammation and redox status is indeed a complex and intricate biological concern. Based on the above results, we propose that PIR, as a redox-sensitive protein, is activated by high levels of ROS and then facilitates the transcriptional activity of RELA towards inflammatory factors such as TNFα and IL-6, which in turn promote ROS production to further strengthen the synergistic function of PIR and RELA in activating inflammatory factors generation, establishing a positive feedback loop in the progression of inflammation. To substantiate this proposal, cells were treated with cytokines and observed for ROS production and PIR localisation. Predictably, cytokines’ treatment increased intracellular ROS level (figure 7A and online supplemental figure S7A) and promoted PIR translocation into the nucleus (figure 7B and online supplemental figure S7B), which were completely abolished by NAC administration. Furthermore, cytokines’ treatment also activated NF-κB transcriptional activity (figure 7C and online supplemental figure S7C). To explore the clinical implications of PIR and RELA in HCC progression, we detected PIR and p-RELA expression in the liver tissues of a cohort comprising two normal individuals, six patients with cirrhosis and eight patients with HCC. The immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining results indicate that the levels of PIR and p-RELA are obviously higher in cirrhosis and HCC tissues than in normal liver tissue and correlated positively (figure 7D). To recapitulate, our results provide detailed evidence on the intricate interplay between inflammation and ROS by establishing a PIR-facilitated positive feedback loop, shedding new light on the mechanism how liver inflammation is gradually exacerbated.

Disruption of this loop by PIR inhibitor and NAC alleviates HCC progression

Next, we investigated the impact of disruption of this loop by PIR inhibitor Tph A and ROS scavenger NAC on HCC progression. Notably, DEN and CCl4 induced a significantly higher number of tumours in Tg mice compared with Wt mice, and treatment with either Tph A or NAC induced an obvious reduction in tumour numbers (figure 8A,B). Consistently, Tph A or NAC treatment also led to decreased body weight (figure 8C), alleviated liver injury (figure 8D) and reduced PIR accumulation in the nucleus (online supplemental figure S8A,B). However, no significant effects on HCC progression were observed in the Wt mice. This may be due to an insufficient duration of tumour induction (figure 8B–D). In addition, in the orthotopic liver HCC mouse model with the injection of Hepa1-6 HCC cells (online supplemental figure S8C), pharmacological inhibition of PIR by Tph A suppressed tumour growth, and treatment with an anti-IL-6 monoclonal antibody further enhanced the anti-tumour efficacy of Tph A (online supplemental figure S8D,E). To investigate the activation of inflammatory pathways, we detected serum cytokine levels of these mice. As illustrated in online supplemental figure S8F,G, lower levels of TNFα and IL-6 were detected in the serum of Tph A and anti-IL-6 antibody treated groups, with even further reduction observed in the group with combined treatment. Furthermore, we employed a hydrodynamic tail vein injection (HDTVi) model to induce spontaneous HCC tumours in C57BL/6 mice, using clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats and CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9)-mediated loss-of-function genome editing of endogenous TP53 and Sleeping Beauty transposon-driven cellular myelocytomatosis oncogene (c-MYC) overexpression (TP53KO/c-MYCOE) with or without PIR KO (figure 8E). PIR KO resulted in a substantial decrease in tumour mass, and the combination with anti-IL-6 treatment led to an even more significant reduction of tumour mass (figure 8F). These findings suggest that targeting PIR either by pharmacological inhibition or genetic silencing suppresses tumour growth and enhances the therapeutic effects of anti-IL-6 in HCC mouse models. We observed similar inhibition of NF-κB and STAT3 signalling pathway (figure 8G), along with a reduction in TNFα and IL-6 levels in this HDTVi mouse model (figure 8H,I). Concerning the potential for spontaneous HCC formation in PIR-Tg mice over time, we investigated tumourigenesis associated with prolonged PIR expression. At 24 months of age, approximately 40% (4/10) of Tg mice developed visible tumour nodules, whereas age-matched C57BL/6 livers exhibited a significantly lower tumour frequency of only 8.3% (1/12) (p=0.011), indicating a pronounced susceptibility of PIR-Tg mice to HCC formation (figure 8J,K). Furthermore, IHC analysis revealed a marked increase in Ki67 staining in liver sections from PIR-Tg mice compared with Wt counterparts (online supplemental figure S8H,I). This finding indicates the presence of hyperproliferative lesions in PIR-Tg mice liver and strongly supports the notion that PIR overexpression contributes to dysregulated cell proliferation.

In summary, our findings confirm the role of elevated PIR expression in promoting HCC progression by participating in a positive feedback loop in the exacerbation of ROS-based liver inflammation and prove the potential of inhibition of PIR in the treatment of HCC with excessive PIR expression (online supplemental figure S8J).

Discussion

In the liver, approximately 80% of HCC develops in a microenvironment characterised by chronic injury, inflammation and fibrosis, in which relatively high levels of ROS are a main factor promoting inflammation and the pathogenesis of liver.46 47 Although the association between inflammation and HCC has been well established, the exact mediators responsible for the generation of gradually increasing inflammation in the progression of HCC have not been fully identified. In this study, we report that PIR is such a mediator and participates in the formation of a positive feedback loop in the progression of liver inflammation. Specifically, we found that high levels of ROS in the liver promote the translocation of PIR from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, where it functions as a transcriptional co-activator to promote classic NF-κB pathway by interacting with and facilitating RELA, a key component of the NF-κB complex. Activated NF-κB pathway then leads to robust transcription and secretion of inflammatory factors such as TNFα and IL-6. It is worth noting that, according to our investigation, these autocrine factors can in turn stimulate ROS generation of hepatocytes. Increased ROS levels further trigger PIR function in synergy with RELA to activate NF-κB and inflammatory factors generation, completing a positive feedback loop in boosting the progression of inflammation (online supplemental figure S8H). While the roles of ROS and inflammation in the promotion and progression of HCC have been well documented,48,51 the mechanism by which inflammation and ROS intrahepatocyte interplay to strengthen each other has not been well clarified. As we can see, PIR is a key factor in this positive feedback loop functioning to facilitate the interpromotion between inflammation and ROS. Given that PIR Tg mice are more susceptible and the PIR KO mice are more resistant than Wt mice to the induction of HCC by DEN and CCl4, genetically knocking out or pharmaceutically inhibiting PIR significantly reduced liver tumour burden in mice. Since PIR is excessively expressed in clinical HCC, we suggest PIR as a key inflammation-facilitating protein in the progression of HCC, providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the development and progression of HCC. In fact, primary liver injury and inflammation are frequently caused by a variety of risk factors such as infection of hepatitis virus, lipidosis, overloaded alcohol and other dietary toxins. In this scenario, excessive expression of PIR may serve as a strong facilitator to amplify the inflammation. However, we still don’t know whether these risk factors can also upregulate the expression of PIR.

Besides biological significance in HCC progression, this PIR-facilitated positive feedback loop also provides therapeutic and diagnostic opportunity to HCC. Given the critical role of ROS and inflammation in HCC promotion and progression, disruption of this loop may alleviate inflammation and block tumourigenesis and progression of HCC. This loop is composed of PIR, RELA, cytokines and ROS. It is reasonable that the inhibition of anyone of these components will disrupt the loop and show a therapeutic effect on HCC. As expected, treatment of HCC cells with ROS scavenger NAC can disable nuclear translocation of PIR, diminish RELA activity, and therefore impair cytokine production. Consistently, early and continuous administration of NAC could suppress the tumourigenesis of mouse HCC induced by DEN and CCl4 to some extent. Although there is a lack of adequate clinical evidence to support the routine use of anti-inflammatory drugs in improving the prognosis of patients with liver cancer, according to our investigation from mouse studies, anti-IL-6 antibody treatment combined with inhibition of PIR effectively prevents the induction of HCC by risk factors at a very early stage, indicating that targeting PIR is a promising strategy for HCC treatment. In this regard, effort should be made to develop more effective PIR inhibitors in fighting HCC. In recent years, recovery of antitumour immunity by immune checkpoint inhibitors is considered a potential therapeutic approach for HCC.52 It would be interesting to investigate whether the combination of a PIR inhibitor and an immune checkpoint inhibitor will show a synergistic effect on HCC treatment. Finally, our study reveals that PIR protein level may serve as a potential biomarker for HCC diagnosis. It is valuable to develop an approach to detect serum PIR protein level and evaluate its application in the assistance of HCC diagnosis.

Limitations of the study

In terms of limitations, our study suggests that the nuclear translocation of PIR may depend on the oxidation of two amino acid residues by H₂O₂. However, we were unable to determine the oxidisation of the residues in vivo at this stage due to the lack of proper antibodies. In addition, it remains unknown if assistance from any partner of PIR or importin proteins is involved in the event of PIR nucleus shuttling and whether cytosolic PIR has distinct functions from nuclear PIR in driving tumourigenesis.
