Birth weight and prematurity for predicting type 2 diabetes mellitus: a prognostic review
Authors: Ishanka Weerasekara, Lea Styrmisdóttir, Martin Ringsten, Nicola Orsini, Carol Mukiira, Shantanu Sharma, Michelle Fiander, Ola Andersson, Allan A Vaag, Peter M Nilsson, Matteo Bruschettini, supported by the Cochrane Neonatal Review Group and Cochrane Sweden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (prototype). The objectives are as follows:Primary objectiveTo determine the prognostic value of birth weight (BW) and prematurity in predicting the incidence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) later in life (among adults, adolescents, and children) compared to normal BW and full‐term birth.Secondary objectivesTo determine the prognostic value of BW and prematurity in predicting the incidence rates of diabetic neuropathy, diabetic retinopathy, and diabetic nephropathy, later in life (among adults, adolescents, and children) compared to normal BW and full‐term birth.

Background

Description of the health condition and context

The prevalence of diabetes is rapidly increasing worldwide, with the highest incidence in low‐ and middle‐income countries, particularly in Asia. India and China have reported the highest number of cases, imposing a significant burden on healthcare systems and disproportionately affecting the poorest economic groups due to high treatment costs [1, 2]. The two primary forms of diabetes are type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), which differ significantly in their causes, development, and treatment. T1DM is primarily caused by an autoimmune reaction to the insulin‐producing beta cells in the pancreas, resulting in no or little insulin production [3]. T2DM is a metabolic disorder that leads to hyperglycemia, often secondary to insulin resistance [3]. It is often referred to as adult‐onset diabetes, as it is commonly diagnosed later in life compared to T1DM. Diabetes can also be caused by specific conditions, such as monogenic diabetes (one form being maturity‐onset diabetes of the young), drug‐induced diabetes (e.g. due to glucocorticoid use), and gestational diabetes mellitus (diagnosed in the second or third trimester of pregnancy in women without prior overt diabetes) [4].

In the Global Burden of Disease study 2019, a greater increase in prevalence and disability was reported for people with T2DM over T1DM during the 30‐year period in Western European countries [5]. Global projections show that by 2045 approximately 783 million people will be living with diabetes, and T2DM will account for about 90% of all cases. An aging population, increased obesity, and decreased physical activity are some key contributors to the rise in T2DM [6]. An inherited genetic susceptibility is also an important risk factor for T2DM [7].

Long‐term problems affecting people with T2DM include macrovascular complications, such as ischemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, and cerebrovascular disease; and microvascular complications, such as retinopathy, neuropathy, and nephropathy [8]. Diabetic retinopathy, a significant microvascular complication of diabetes, leads to vision impairment and blindness [9]. Diabetic neuropathy, which affects up to half of all people with diabetes over time, is characterized by a loss of sensory function primarily in the lower extremities and is often accompanied by pain and significant morbidity [10]. Diabetic nephropathy involves structural and functional abnormalities in the kidneys, such as glomerular hyperfiltration, thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, and increased urinary albumin excretion. This condition typically results from chronic hyperglycemia and other metabolic factors associated with diabetes [11].

Description of the prognostic factor

Investigating potential risk factors, such as birth weight (BW) and gestational age, can provide an understanding of the origins and risk factors/markers associated with the development of T2DM.

Prematurity, low BW (LBW), or small for gestational age (SGA) appear to increase the susceptibility to developing chronic non‐communicable diseases in adulthood [12]. Furthermore, the association between BW and the risk of developing T2DM has been broadly examined [12]. Many studies have found that LBW increases the risk of T2DM, and there are different suggestions as to how this occurs. The thrifty phenotype hypothesis, also called the fetal programming hypothesis, suggests that fetal metabolic reprogramming occurs as a result of malnutrition within the uterus. If nutrition is improved after birth, rapid growth takes place and the body stores more fat than muscle, predisposing it to insulin resistance and other metabolic defects [13]. Another proposal is the fetal insulin hypothesis, which suggests that LBW and T2DM have a common genetic basis, being two phenotypes of the same genotype. Variations in genes influencing insulin secretion can lead to decreased fetal insulin and affect intrauterine growth, as well as a decreased capacity to secrete and respond to insulin, resulting in a higher risk of adult‐onset T2DM [14]. BW in relation to gestational age is categorized as SGA, appropriate for gestational age (AGA), or large for gestational age (LGA) infants.

Multiple confounding factors influence both the risk and progression of T2DM (Figure 1). The association between early life factors and incident disease manifestations, including T2DM, could be confounded by genetic traits [15], maternal health [16], and BW increase during pregnancy [17, 18]; which could also to a varying degree influence placental function and metabolic traits in the offspring [19, 20, 21]. These confounding factors may include age, sex, body mass index (BMI), parity, previous stillbirth, family history of diabetes, calorie intake, physical activity, ethnicity, social status, living condition, physical activity, education, preterm birth history, pubertal stage, fat mass, Mediterranean Diet index, type of delivery, breastfeeding, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, triacylglycerol, and per capita income [22, 23, 24]. Of note, male preterm infants often have poorer outcomes compared to females [25]. In previous similar meta‐analyses, review authors performed statistical adjustments on potential confounders such as age, sex, smoking, alcohol drinking, high cholesterol level, BMI, and socio‐economic status adjusted for in the analysis [26, 27]. Most other reviews recorded the confounding factors reported in the included studies, such as gestational diabetes, diet, smoking and drinking during pregnancy, breastfeeding, and mother's pregnancy BMI [13, 23, 24, 28].

Health outcomes

Prematurely born children or children with a LBW for their gestational age are at an increased risk of developing metabolic diseases, particularly if they rapidly gain weight during infancy [29]. BW associates more strongly with T2DM, and each kilogram increment in BW was associated with a 22% reduction in risk of later developing T2DM (odds ratio (OR) 0.78, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.70 to 0.87) [28]. Men are diagnosed with T2DM slightly more frequently compared to women [30]; however, women show a higher risk of developing T2DM at both low and high BWs compared to men [31]

We have not identified any systematic reviews that specifically address prematurity and T2DM. However, two national cohort studies from Sweden and the UK offer contrasting perspectives [32, 33]. The Swedish study found evidence supporting an association; preterm birth was associated with approximately 1.3‐fold risk of T2DM at age < 18 years and 1.5‐fold risk at age 18 to 43 years [32], whereas the UK study reported no independent effect of preterm delivery on subsequent development of T2DM [33].

Why it is important to do this review

The overall impact of an adverse fetal environment on later disease risk may have been grossly underestimated in previous studies, where the extent of association between BW and disease outcomes has not been adjusted for gestational age, or prematurity status and LBW [24, 27]. This prognostic Cochrane review with a dose‐response meta‐analysis aims to represent the total impact of an adverse fetal environment on T2DM disease risk. Given that T2DM is a prevalent and growing health concern globally, this is a highly clinically relevant and important question for patients, health professionals, policymakers, and communities. By better understanding the relationship between BW, prematurity, and T2DM, improved preventive strategies and interventions can be implemented for people who are at a higher risk. Investigating potential risk factors, such as BW and gestational age, can provide an understanding of the origins and risk factors associated with the development of T2DM. If a strong link is found between LBW, prematurity, and T2DM, this can inform policymakers who can potentially make changes to maternal and child health programs. Although many studies have shown that LBW increases the risk of developing T2DM, the shape of the association remains unknown. Previous systematic reviews have reported J‐shaped associations [28], a non‐linear and L‐shaped relationship [26], or U‐shaped associations [22, 27]. In addition, none of the systematic reviews to date performed an assessment of the risk of bias or certainty of the evidence [13, 24, 26, 27, 28, 34]. Therefore, a prognostic systematic review, including GRADE and risk of bias assessments, is warranted to address the uncertainty in the existing evidence.

Objectives

Primary objective

To determine the prognostic value of birth weight (BW) and prematurity in predicting the incidence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) later in life (among adults, adolescents, and children) compared to normal BW and full‐term birth.

Secondary objectives

To determine the prognostic value of BW and prematurity in predicting the incidence rates of diabetic neuropathy, diabetic retinopathy, and diabetic nephropathy, later in life (among adults, adolescents, and children) compared to normal BW and full‐term birth.

Methods

For this Cochrane protocol, we will follow the guide to systematic review and meta‐analysis of prognostic factor studies [35], the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, and reporting guidance per PRISMA‐P [36, 37, 38]. We will also use the tools developed by the Cochrane Prognosis Methods Group to perform a systematic review of prognosis studies [39].

Types of studies

We will include any longitudinal cohort studies (retrospective and prospective) reporting BW or prematurity and T2DM later in life. For this review, we will define a longitudinal study as a study that utilizes continuous or repeated measures to follow particular individuals over prolonged periods of time, and investigates the relationships between exposure to known or potential risk factors and the subsequent risk of development of disease or mortality [40, 41]. We will exclude randomized trials, as we do not expect to identify any matching our research question. We will primarily analyze the incidence of T2DM. Furthermore, we will identify publications on incidence data on complications of T2DM and analyze them in separate analyses.

Target population

The target population is infants with different BWs at different gestational ages. We will include adults with T2DM compared to adults without T2DM, with available data on BW, or gestational age, or both. We will include studies regardless of age or gender, published in any region of the world. We will exclude participants who have either incomplete records or have other types of diabetes other than T2DM.

If several population cohorts or subgroups are available within the same study, we will include cohorts enrolling infants of mothers without diabetes (i.e. mothers that do not have gestational diabetes, T1DM or T2DM (non‐gestational), or insulin‐dependent [42, 43]) to mitigate the impact of this heredity factor.

Types of prognostic factor

We will assess studies including infants within a range of LBWs and gestational age. We will use the World Health Organization (WHO) definition of LBW, which is weight at birth of less than 2500 g. This will be further classified into very low BW (VLBW; 1000 g to 2499 g) and extremely low BW (ELBW; < 999 g) [43, 44].

The delivery of infants before the completion of 37 weeks of gestation is defined as a preterm birth [45]. Preterm birth is subcategorized according to the gestational age at birth, as extremely preterm (< 28 weeks), very preterm (28 to < 32 weeks), or moderate to late preterm (32 to < 37 weeks) [46]. We will also assess studies in SGA (defined as a BW of less than 2 standard deviations (SDs) of the mean or 10th percentile for gestational age), AGA (defined as BW within 2 SDs of the mean or the 10th to 90th percentile), and LGA (defined as a BW above 2 SDs of the mean or the 90th percentile for gestational age) infants [42, 43]. We will include studies with BWs reported in health records and self‐reported BW. If studies from both classification approaches for preterm births are included, we will analyze and present the results separately to ensure clarity and inclusivity.

Types of outcomes to be predicted

Critical outcome

Incidence of T2DM. We will consider both medically diagnosed and self‐reported T2DM. We will prioritize medically diagnosed T2DM over self‐reported T2DM when a preference between methods is needed, because this provides greater reliability and accuracy in measuring the outcome of T2DM. There will be no restrictions to the age of onset of incidence/diagnosis. However, we will collect information on the age of diagnosis for meaningful interpretations; that is, onset of diagnosis before school (0 to 5 years), schooling age (6 to 15 years), working age (16 to 64 years), and retirement (≥ 65 years).

Time points for outcome assessments

The prognostic factors are intended to be measured at birth. The outcome, the development of T2DM, will be measured at any point in the future, recognizing that the condition can emerge at different stages across the lifespan.

We will consider exploring outcomes (onset of diagnosis of T2DM or diabetic neuropathy/retinopathy/ nephropathy) at any of these time points: before school (0 to 5 years), schooling age (6 to 15 years), working age (16 to 64 years), and retirement (≥ 65 years).

Search methods for identification of studies

Electronic searches

The Information Specialist (MF) drafted the search strategy for OVID MEDLINE (see Supplementary material 1). We tested the terminology in the search strategy using studies included in three systematic reviews on topics similar to the subject of this Cochrane review [24, 27, 28]. An Information Specialist from Cochrane Central Editorial Service peer reviewed the search strategy. The MEDLINE strategy will be translated for OVID Embase using corresponding controlled vocabulary and syntax.

We will conduct the searches without language or publication status restrictions. Searches for studies will use no date limit; searches for systematic reviews will be limited from 2020 forward. We will use a prognostic studies filter by Stallings et al in the MEDLINE search strategy [47]; the strategy has been adapted to include the keyword fields in OVID MEDLINE (kw, kf) to extend the "tw" syntax per the filter. This strategy may be translated for Embase given it is quite recent, but the ISSG Search Filters Resource will also be examined.

Searching other resources

We will conduct a gray literature search if we can identify organizations that produce white papers or other unindexed research on the topic of this review. We will check the reference lists of related systematic reviews identified by our search, and the reference lists of studies selected for inclusion in this review. We will contact experts in the field to inquire about unpublished or in‐process studies.

We will crosscheck the included studies in PubMed and the Retraction Watch Database for corrections, retractions, expressions of concern, errata, corrigenda, or notices associated with post‐publication changes. All of these may potentially lead to the exclusion of a study from a review or meta‐analysis [36].

Data collection and analysis

Review authors associated with potentially eligible studies will not assess the eligibility, extract data, evaluate the risk of bias, or assess the certainty of the evidence for those studies.

Selection of studies

Two review authors (IW, LS, or CM in working pairs) will independently screen the titles and abstracts identified by the search strategy using Covidence [48]. We will obtain the full‐text articles of any potentially eligible studies, and two review authors (IW, LS, or CM) will independently assess the full‐text articles for inclusion. We will resolve any disagreement at screening or full‐text assessment stages by discussion, or by consulting a third review author (SS or OA). We will document the study selection process in a PRISMA flow diagram [49, 50].

Data extraction and management

Two review authors (IW, LS, CM, MF, AAV, or PMN in working pairs) will independently extract data in Covidence using a predefined data extraction form [48]. We will pilot the data extraction forms by having all review authors involved in data extraction independently extract data from at least three included studies. We will collect information on participants (age, age of diagnosis, sex, diagnostic criteria used, severity of symptoms, duration of symptoms, recruitment method, inclusion/exclusion criteria, comorbid conditions, study setting, allocation procedure, blinding, number of participants), exposure (description of exposure), outcome measures (description of measures used, timing of administration, continuous/ dichotomous nature, psychometric properties, references provided), and results (point estimates and measures of variability, frequency counts for dichotomous variables, number of patients). We will also extract any within‐study dose‐response analyses. We will resolve any disagreements by discussion, or involve a third review author (SS or OA).

Dealing with duplicate and companion publications

In the case of multiple reports for a study, we will collate reports so that the study (not the reference) is the unit of analysis. We will consult all reports to ensure we have the most complete dataset. We will prioritize the publication that provides the longest follow‐up data related to our outcomes. We will contact study authors if we require additional information or clarification about data.

Risk of bias assessment in included studies

Two review authors (IW, LS, SS, or OA) will independently assess the risk of bias of each included study using the Quality In Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool [51] (see Supplementary material 2). We will resolve any disagreements by consensus, or in consultation with review authors who did not undertake the original risk of bias assessment (MR or CM). We will discuss complex assessments within the full review team to reach consensus. The responses to the signaling questions will be made available on request from the corresponding review author.

We will contact study authors to obtain any missing information required for our risk of bias assessments.

Measures of association

We will extract the risk of dichotomous outcomes using hazard ratio (HR), OR, risk ratio (RR), or proportions where possible, with 95% CIs. Whenever possible, we will extract estimates adjusted for potential confounding. If several adjusted estimates are presented, we will select the estimate most closely aligned with our prespecified list of potential confounding variables (see ‘Risk of bias assessment in included studies’ section). We will further analyze the interrelationship between the assessing variables (such as, age of the diagnosis, BW, and premature birth) for meaningful interpretation. We will exclude unadjusted estimates from our analysis.

Dealing with missing data

We will contact study authors if we require further information or clarification. If time‐to‐event analyses are performed and adjusted effect estimates and their uncertainty are unavailable, we plan to derive unadjusted estimates and their standard errors following guidance described by Tierney and colleagues if the summary statistics reported will permit [52]. We will perform sensitivity analyses to assess how sensitive results are to reasonable changes in the undertaken assumptions. We will address the potential impact of missing data on the findings of the review in the 'Discussion’ section.

Assessment of heterogeneity

Between‐study heterogeneity may result from clinical diversity, methodological diversity, and statistical heterogeneity [53]. Clinical diversity can arise from differences in study populations (e.g. age, sex, and comorbidities), and variations in the definition of exposure (e.g. birthweight and prematurity) within studies. Also, differences in outcome assessment (e.g. self‐diagnosed T2DM, medically diagnosed T2DM) can cause heterogeneity. Methodological diversity can arise due to the diversity of methods used and the robustness of the methods used (based on the risk of bias). Variations in the approach to analysis also contribute to methodological heterogeneity.

Statistical heterogeneity can be quantified using Tau². We will assess between‐study heterogeneity using the I² statistic as follows [53].

Data synthesis

Data synthesis and meta‐analysis approaches

We will use the aggregated data from the published papers, reports, and reviews to perform the two‐stage random effect dose‐response meta‐analysis. We will retrieve the effect estimates (relative risks, HR, or OR) with their 95% CIs that will have the highest degree of adjustment for the confounding variables from the selected studies. We will use the relative measure of association between high or low BW categories and T2DM onset as the normal BW category as a reference. We will follow this with a meta‐analysis using the two‐stage generalized least square trend method. We will use the mid‐point of the BW category assigned to the corresponding effect size to estimate the study‐specific slopes first, followed by an overall average slope. For each dose level, we will estimate the effect size (log relative risk) and its standard error. We will explore the potential non‐linear relationship by modeling BW levels using a restricted cubic spline random‐effects model with three knots at 10%, 50%, and 90% of the BW distribution. Between studies, we will assess heterogeneity (Q test) and express it using I2 and P values. We will perform all the analysis using dosresmeta R package [55]. We will include all eligible studies and conduct a sensitivity analysis according to the levels of risk of bias.

We will analyze studies with self‐reported BW and BWS reported in health records, separately. We have summarized the potential confounding factors that could affect the dose‐response relationships, in a directed acyclic graph chart [56] (Figure 1).

In this Cochrane review, we will also perform dose‐response meta‐analyses of T2DM in relation to BW, and to prematurity. In most studies and reviews there is a focus on BW per se. However, as gestational age is an important determinant of BW (i.e. higher BW correlates with higher gestational age), it is of interest to adjust BW accordingly in prospective risk analyses. On the other hand, prematurity (defined as gestational age < 37 weeks of gestational age) is an exposure on its own, and can be further subdivided into extremely preterm (less than 28 weeks); very preterm (28 to less than 32 weeks); and moderate to late preterm (32 to 37 weeks). Our main focus is on BW in relation to incident diabetes, and many previous studies have shown that LBW (< 2500 g, or below median, or lowest tertile/quartile) is associated with diabetes. However, high BW (U‐shaped risk relationship) is also of importance and this may be a marker of maternal obesity, pre‐diabetes, or gestational diabetes, and also a risk marker for future offspring diabetes onset. We also aim to assess papers addressing the role of prematurity and incident diabetes if such papers exist in the literature. Genetic studies have shown that a causal association exists between lower BW and adult risk of diabetes [57].

We will use weighted mixed‐effects models suitable for table of correlated estimates [58, 59]. We will assign a single exposure value to each category based on what has been reported (mean, median, midpoint) within each study. In case the typical exposure value within each exposure interval is not available from the publication, we will assign it according to its distribution. We will use regression splines of different degrees to answer specific questions about the dose‐response relationships [59, 60]. We will take the heterogeneity of dose‐response gradients across studies into account by using random‐effects for the regression coefficients of the exposure transformations. The main target of statistical inference (test of hypothesis, CIs) is the pointwise dose‐response relationship for the average study. To examine the magnitude of heterogeneity across studies, we will use the best linear unbiased predictions of the random‐effects. We will compare alternative candidate dose‐response models using the Akaike Information Criteria, balancing goodness of fit and the overall number of parameters. We will perform stratified analyses according to relevant design or scientific factors (e.g. gradient of susceptibility to systematic and differential exposure measurement errors) using a weighted mixed effects model. The dose‐response meta‐analysis will be conducted based on the studies presenting sufficiently detailed findings on the adjusted effect of BW on the risk of diabetes.

Where meta‐analysis is not possible, we will transparently report a summary of the synthesis findings as per the Synthesis Without Meta‐analysis (SWiM) guidelines [61].

Equity‐related assessment

We will conduct equity assessments by examining disparities in birth outcomes, including preterm births, with a particular focus on sex and socioeconomic status. Male preterm infants often have poorer outcomes compared to females [25]. By analyzing how socioeconomic status and gender intersect with BW and prematurity, we aim to understand their influence on the risk of T2DM. We will report on the characteristics of the populations according to relevant PROGRESS‐Plus factors, and also whether there are population subgroups with a higher risk of T2DM [63].

Certainty of the evidence assessment

Two review authors (IW, LS, MR, or MB) will independently assess the certainty of the evidence using the GRADE approach for prognostic reviews [66, 67,68], and guidance for observational studies and studies in the realm of public health, per the main GRADE guideline [69].

We will use the GRADEprofiler Guideline Development Tool software (GRADEpro) [70]. We will use the sub‐PICOTS framework to assess the development of complications in those who have diabetes [35].

We will assess the certainty of the body of evidence for each of the following outcomes: incidence of medically diagnosed and self‐reported T2DM, incidence rates of diabetic neuropathy, diabetic retinopathy, and diabetic nephropathy, as defined by the study authors. We will include two time points: working age (16 to 64 years) and retirement (≥ 65 years). There will be no restrictions to the age of onset of incidence/diagnosis, and we will include information about the duration of follow‐up or the scale used.

During the review, we will engage a representative from the Regional Association of Skåne (https://diabetesskane.se/), which is an organization of patients with diabetes, to gather their thoughts on the recommendations arising from the results.
