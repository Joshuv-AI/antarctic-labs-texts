A hybrid detection model for acute lymphocytic leukemia using support vector machine and particle swarm optimization (SVM-PSO)
Authors: Lama K Alsaykhan, Mashael S Maashi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeukemia, a hematological disease affecting the bone marrow and white blood cells (WBCs), ranks among the top ten causes of mortality worldwide. Delays in decision-making often hinder the timely application of suitable medical treatments. Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is one of the primary forms, constituting approximately 25% of childhood cancer cases. However, automated ALL diagnosis is challenging. Recently, machine learning (ML) has emerged as an important tool for building detection models. In this study, we present a hybrid detection model that improves the accuracy of the detection process by combining support vector machine (SVM) and particle swarm optimization (PSO) approaches to automatically identify ALL. We use SVM to represent a two-dimensional image and complete the classification process. PSO is employed to enhance the performance of the SVM model, reducing error rates and enhancing result accuracy. The input images are obtained from two public datasets (ALL-IDB1 and ALL-IDB2), and public online datasets are utilized for training and testing the proposed model. The results indicate that our hybrid SVM-PSO model has high accuracy, outperforming stand-alone algorithms and demonstrating superior performance, an enhanced confusion matrix, and a higher detection rate. This advancement holds promise for enhancing the quality of technical software in the medical field using machine learning.Keywords: Machine learning, Deep learning, Leukemia Cancer, Acute lymphocytic leukemia, Disease, White blood cellSubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Ecology

Introduction

Diagnostic challenges in leukemia—a fatal malignancy affecting blood cells—are immense due to its diverse manifestations and subtypes. Acute lymphoblastic leukemia is one of the most common types of leukemia prevalent among children and teenagers and is characterized by rapid and abnormal lymphoid cell growth in the bone marrow. Traditional acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) diagnoses include blood image tests and bone marrow biopsies. Figure 1 present the cellular conditions across different states, including healthy and ALL. These procedures are highly time- and labor-consuming and depend heavily on the expertise of the operator. Over the past few years, machine learning has been a very promising tool for automating the detection and classification of leukemia to enable faster and more accurate diagnoses.

Sample images of two different types of leukemia, chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and acute lymphocytic Leukemia (ALL), as well as healthy blood cells1.

In this research, we develop a new hybrid model that combines a support vector machine (SVM) algorithm with particle swarm optimization (PSO) to enable the automated identification of ALL from blood smear images. Although widely accepted because of its robust handling of classification problems, the performance of an SVM is heavily reliant on choosing appropriate hyperparameters. In this study, PSO—an optimization technique that imitates flocking birds and the social behavior of birds—is employed in tuning SVM parameters to enhance the accuracy of the detection model.

This research integrates SVM and PSO for ALL detection, a novelty in the current research landscape. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has adequately explored this combination. This approach gains advantages not only from the classification strength of SVMs but also from the efficiency of PSO. Thus, this is a state-of-the-art model, both in terms of accuracy and computational cost. The proposed model is further evaluated on various datasets for validation, including the ALL-IDB 1 and 2 datasets and another online dataset, to ensure its effectiveness and robustness.

Background and related works

An overview of machine learning methods used for leukemia detection

In the last two decades, several studies have used Machine Learning (ML) and computer-aided diagnostic techniques in laboratory image analyses in an attempt to diagnose leukemia earlier and identify its subgroups. In these studies, blood smear images were examined to identify, classify, and count the cells in different types of leukemia. Numerous ML and DL techniques have been utilized to detect leukemia, and each algorithm has different features and levels of accuracy. In this section, we present the most relevant studies on leukemia detection.

SVM is one of the most commonly used ML techniques for detecting leukemia. It has been used to identify ALL based on geometric and Local Binary Pattern (LBP) features indicating blast cells in blood images2. Several studies have demonstrated that SVM works well for medical image classification. For instance, Xie et al.3 demonstrated the effectiveness of SVM in categorizing retinal images with a greater accuracy rate, and Zhang et al.4 successfully applied SVM-based methods to breast cancer detection in 2020, proving the strength and power of this method when dealing with high-dimensional data. SVM is commonly combined with other methods. For example, in this study5 The SVM technique was utilized along with the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) method, which is a type of deep learning method mostly used for image classification, detection, and segmentation applications, with UNET segmentation to suggest predominant features for the detection of ALL. Compared with other state-of-the-art methods, this technique achieved an accuracy of 98.2%. Another study6 utilized a local dataset to detect ALL using a CNN-SVM model to automatically extract operation and granularity features. The model performs better than other methods, achieving an accuracy of 94%. Tuncer et al.7 proposed a hybrid model that combines convolutional neural networks with SVM for ALL classification and achieves high accuracy rates. However, most such methods are computationally intensive and require a huge amount of training data, which is difficult to acquire in many clinical settings. Umair Saeed in8 developed an automated feature extraction method that employs a CNN deep learning model and was trained for ALL disease classification on the ALL-IDB 1 dataset. Data augmentation techniques were used to generate additional images from the original dataset, and the model achieved high accuracy and precision, supported by qualitative analysis to verify its efficacy. Maíla et al. developed a CNN-based model for leukemia classification in their 2022 study9, in which they utilized a dataset of 3,536 images collected from 18 different sources and applied data augmentation techniques to enhance image quality. By employing multilevel and ensemble CNN architectures, they achieved accuracy rates of 94.73% and 94.59%, respectively. Furthermore, a computation technique employing Fuzzy C-means and SVM has been proposed10 for use in detecting cancerous and non-cancerous cells, as well as their subtypes, using principle component analysis (PCA) based features extracted from nucleus images. The proposed method was applied to a local dataset at Isfahan Al-Zahra Hospital and Omid Hospital and has achieved one of the highest accuracies reported in the literature, ranging between 97% and 98% for each image. These studies demonstrate that SVM has potential for use within medical diagnostics; however, full realization of this potential still faces one major challenge: hyperparameter tuning.

An overview of optimization methods used for leukemia detection

Optimization11 is crucial to get the best results with ML approaches. Thus, an efficient optimizer is important to ensure that optimal solutions are achieved. An optimizer is a search or optimization algorithm that is utilized to iteratively train a model, resulting in both maximum and minimum function evaluation12. The most frequent optimizer algorithms used for the leukemia diagnosis field are practical swarm optimization (PSO)13 and the generative adversarial optimizer (GOA)14. It has been proven that PSO can ultimately be employed successfully in the optimization of ML models—being an optimization algorithm inspired by the social behavior of flocking birds. In the ML field, algorithms are commonly combined for improved accuracy. Shi et al.15 employ PSO for improved model performance and the optimization of neural network parameters for heart disease prediction. Similarly, Liu et al.16 prove that PSO can effectively fine-tune the parameters of a convolutional neural network for improved skin lesion classification. A novel hybrid approach using a fuzzy logic technique and PSO was proposed to detect chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) using a local dataset17. The model consistently demonstrated an accuracy of around 96%. Another study uses an artificial neural network (ANN) algorithm to optimize multidimensional data classification, with a focus on acute leukemia samples18. The algorithm produced highly sensitive results, achieving an accuracy of 96.67% in ALL classification with a large dataset while employing augmentation techniques. These results show that PSO significantly improves the performance of the learned models by efficiently searching through the space of hyperparameters.

A recent novel technique for detecting ALL cells was presented by Tuba and Tuba19. The authors utilized GOA along with SVM, applying 11 extraction features (5 shape and 6 texture features). The ALL-IDB1 dataset was used for training and testing. In comparison to naïve Bayes classification, k-nearest neighbors, and a back propagation neural network, their method achieved the highest classification accuracy (93.84%). Consequently, the integration of SVM and PSO into a single system has been explored in various applications and is typically very promising. For instance, Chen et al.20 developed a hybrid SVM and PSO model for fault diagnosis in industrial machinery, which achieved higher accuracy than traditional methods. More recently, in 2023, Wang et al.21 formulated a PSO-optimized SVM for cardiovascular disease prediction that outperforms other optimization techniques in terms of both accuracy and computational efficiency. These studies establish a particularly strong foundation for the application of hybrid SVM and PSO models in medical diagnostics; however, applications specific to the field of leukemia detection are still open to research and development.

The proposed method

In this study, we propose a hybrid approach that includes two algorithms—ML SVM and PSO algorithms—to detect ALL cells in blood images. Based on the findings from the literature review presented in Sect. 2.1, the SVM stand-alone method has been utilized previously for ALL detection; however, improvements in accuracy are needed. Similarly, as discussed in Sect. 2.2, PSO has been combined with other ML methods in hybrid configurations; however, achieving high accuracy has proven challenging. Therefore, we chose to pursue a hybrid SVM-PSO approach to address these limitations. ALL is considered the deadliest of all leukemia subtypes. SVM has the ability to generate an internal representation of a two-dimensional image, allowing the model to determine the position and scale of variant structures in the data, which is important when working with images22. To minimize the error rate, PSO is used to tune the performance of the SVM model by optimizing the k parameter, thus generating more accurate results.

Our proposed SVM-PSO framework, which is shown in Fig. 2, consists of two stages: a classification process and an optimization process. In the first stage, blood images are obtained as inputs from the ALL-IDB1&2 dataset. Prior to experimentation, pre-processing of the datasets is conducted to ensure that there are no missing values in the data. The datasets are then split into two subsets: a training (70%) dataset and a testing (30%) dataset. In the SVM model, the feature selected, trained, and scaling techniques are used to make the classification easier. In the second stage, the PSO begins the tuning process for the k parameter in the SVM model, aiming to minimize the error rate of the model, thereby enhancing accuracy. This optimization process involves training and testing a model for each particle. The error rate serves as the metric for evaluating the model’s performance, with the objective of achieving the lowest error rate. If the minimum error rate or maximum iteration limit is reached, the optimization process terminates. Otherwise, the optimizer will continue to search for alternative values of the k parameter, repeating the training and evaluation steps until the optimal solution, characterized by the minimum error rate, is found. This iterative process refines the performance of the SVM model through parameter optimization, resulting in improved accuracy in ALL cell detection.

As shown in Table 2, the proposed SVM-PSO model involves several steps, the first of which is preprocessing. In the second step, the initial particle swarm parameters are set, including the particle number, learning factors, particle velocity, and particle position. In step 3, the SVM parameter is initialized by encoding the k parameter as the position of the particle. In step 4 the condition for stopping the process initiated which are minimum k value or achieve maximum iteration. In step 5, the SVM model is trained in 100 iterations. The parameter k changes along with the flying of particles. The error rate is evaluated in the sixth step and compared with the previous value. If it is better, the whole process terminates, and if it is not, the optimization process will run (stage 2).

In stage 2, the optimization process is performed using PSO. In step 7, the initial fitness value is determinate, and the fitness value of the particle is evaluated. The fitness value is used to evaluate the validity of the SVM model based on the k parameter. A better k parameter value leads to a lower error rate. In step 8, the particle parameters best Pbest, global best gbest, particle velocity, and particle position are updated. The SVM model is fed with the output of the PSO with the obtained optimal k parameter value. Returning to step 4, the SVM model is run with the obtained optimal k parameter and determined minimum error rate. Steps 5–8 are repeated until the termination conditions are met.

Experimental settings

Dataset description

In this study, we utilized two datasets. The first dataset is ALL-IDB, which is provided by the Department of Information Technology of Universitá degli Studi di Milano, and can be freely downloaded at23 This dataset contains microscopic images of blood samples captured with a Canon PowerShot G5 digital camera and is designed specifically for evaluating and comparing segmentation and image classification algorithms. All images in the dataset are in JPG format, with 24-bit color depth and varying resolutions. The magnification of the images ranges from 300x to 500x. The ALL-IDB comprises two distinct datasets: IDB1 and IDB2. We evaluated our algorithm using both ALL-IDB1 and ALL-IDB2, which are designed specifically for evaluating the effectiveness of classification systems. The ALL-IDB dataset contains 880 images, 50% of which are microscopic images of benign blood samples, while the other 50% are microscopic images of malignant blood samples. Hence, the dataset comprises two distinct image classes: benign cells and malignant cells. Figure 3 presents multiple image samples, including both benign and malignant image classes. The images capture the structure, shape, and cellular morphology of the blood cells, as well as the diversity in magnification levels within the same image class. All images show various other types of blood cells impeding a white blood cell. In addition, specific samples capture multiple white blood cells. The IDB1 and IDB2 datasets were split into training and test sets: 30% for testing and 70% for training, with no overlap between any of the two subsets. For the IDB1 and IDB2 dataset, these percentages correspond to 616 images for training and 264 images for testing.

ALL-IDB sample images showing benign (top) and malignant (bottom) lymphocytes.

The second dataset is a public online dataset24 containing images produced in the bone marrow laboratory of Taleqani Hospital in Tehran, Iran. It comprises 325 peripheral blood smear (PBS) images obtained from 89 individuals suspected of having ALL. The images were split into training and testing sets. The percentage splits were 70% for training (227 images) and 30% for testing (98 images). These images are of PBS slides that were prepared and stained by highly skilled laboratory personnel using blood samples from the 89 patients mentioned earlier. The dataset comprises two distinct categories: benign and malignant. The images were captured using a Zeiss camera in a microscope set at 100x magnification and saved in JPG format. Utilizing a flow cytometry tool, an expert conclusively identified the specific types and subtypes of the cells captured in the images. Sample images highlighting the different image classes (benign and malignant cells) are presented in Fig. 4.

Sample images from the online dataset showing benign (top) and malignant (bottom) lymphocytes.

Conclusion and future work

ALL is a type of cancer that originates in your blood, stemming from WBCs in the bone marrow, the spongy tissue in the bones. ML presents significant opportunities to enhance pathological diagnosis in the medical field, particularly with the growing use of digital microscopic images. Diagnosing leukemia can be time-consuming and challenging in many regions worldwide, prompting the increased adoption of ML approaches to expedite and streamline the diagnostic process. ML, a type of artificial intelligence (AI), enables computers to learn and adapt without needing to be explicitly programmed for each scenario. The central idea of ML involves creating models that can learn by themselves by training on a very large number of inputs. Iterative refinement aims to minimize errors while maximizing model accuracy.

In this study, we proposed a hybrid model based on SVM and PSO methods to diagnose ALL using ML algorithms automatically. Primarily, the model enables the identification of infected samples, which can aid laboratory specialists in the diagnosis process. Consequently, this approach promises to enhance diagnostic quality, bolster reliability, and increase efficiency. Experimental evaluations were conducted using two computing platforms: a supercomputer and a personal laptop. The ALL-IDB1 and ALL-IDB2 datasets were used for training and testing the proposed model. The results demonstrated the superiority of the proposed model. SVM-PSO demonstrated enhanced accuracy and detection rates compared to SVM alone. Future studies could extend these results by exploring other subtypes of leukemia. Moreover, future research could utilize additional datasets to comprehensively evaluate and optimize the model’s performance and efficiency.
