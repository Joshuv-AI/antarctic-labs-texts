Choroid plexus modulates subventricular zone adult neurogenesis and olfaction through secretion of small extracellular vesicles
Authors: Luke L. Liu, Jonathan Shannahan, Wei Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The choroid plexus (CP) in the brain ventricles secretes cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) that bathes the adjacent subventricular zone (SVZ). As the largest adult neurogenic region enriched with neural stem/progenitor cells (NSPCs), the SVZ supplies newborn neurons to the olfactory bulb (OB) for normal olfaction. This report depicts the presence of a CP-SVZ regulatory (CSR) axis, in which the CP regulates SVZ adult neurogenesis and olfaction via secretion of small extracellular vesicles (sEVs). The proposed CSR axis was supported by the evidence of (1) a direct effect of CP epithelial cells on the SVZ by in vivo transplantation and in vitro CP-SVZ co-culture assays, (2) differential OB neurogenesis following intracerebroventricular (ICV) infusion of sEVs derived from the CP of control or manganese (Mn)-poisoned mice, (3) progressively diminished SVZ adult neurogenesis after CP-selective inhibition of sEV secretion via AAV5-mediated SMPD3 knockdown, and (4) compromised olfactory performance following CP-selective SMPD3-knockdown. Collectively, our findings demonstrate the physiological, toxicological, and behavioral importance of this sEV-dependent CSR axis in the adult brain.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12987-026-00793-7.

Introduction

The choroid plexus (CP) produces and secretes the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to support the brain health and development by regulating the CSF proteome, ions, and other underdefined components [1, 2]. Immediately adjacent to the CP is the subventricular zone (SVZ), the largest adult neurogenic niche located in the surface layer of lateral ventricles. The SVZ harbors neural stem/progenitor cells (NSPCs) and supplies newborn neurons to the olfactory bulb (OB) for normal olfaction [3]. Recent evidence suggests CP plays a role in regulating adult SVZ activities [4–6]. However, the concept of a Choroid plexus-Subventricular zone Regulatory (CSR) axis, in which the regulatory signals delivered from the CP to the SVZ mediate NSPC proliferation, migration and differentiation in the SVZ-rostral migratory stream (RMS)-OB system, remains elusive. Even less is known regarding what factors secreted by the CP into the CSF may transmit the regulatory signals targeted towards the SVZ. Hence, a thorough understanding of this axis will help address the olfactory disorder observed in numerous neurological conditions and facilitate therapeutic interventions.

Studies from this lab [7] along with cumulative data in literature [8–10] have established the CP, which receives the most abundant blood flow within brain, serves as the target for insults from the circulation, such as peripheral inflammation [11, 12], viral infection [10, 13], and neurotoxicant exposure [7, 14–16]. Specifically, exposure to toxic metal manganese (Mn) is known to cause Parkinsonian disorders among Mn-exposed welders and smelters [17, 18], lead to extensive Mn accumulation in the CP and CSF [19], cause aberrant adult neurogenesis in the SVZ [20, 21], and induce olfactory dysfunction [18]. Thus, it seemed highly likely that the CP, in response to environmental insult in the systemic circulation, may mediate SVZ activities via this proposed CSR axis, leading to early signs and symptoms of Mn-associated neurodegeneration.

Small extracellular vesicles (sEVs, diameter < 200 nm) secreted by host cells carry cell type-specific cargo for signaling transduction [22–24]. The CP exhibits a robust expression of sEV-related markers as demonstrated by independent lines of evidence [25, 26]. Considering (1) the proximity between the CP and SVZ, (2) the expression of critical primary cilia structure in SVZ NSPCs for receiving CSF signals [27], and (3) the abundant sEV-related gene expression in the CP, we propose that the CP produces and secretes sEVs into the CSF; the SVZ senses the sEV-carried signals and thereby regulates neurogenesis in the SVZ-RMS-OB system. Overall, the data presented in this study through molecular, toxicological, and neurobehavioral characterizations support the existence of a viable sEV-dependent CP-SVZ regulatory (CSR) axis in brain.

Animals

Male CD-1 mice (3 months old) were purchased from Envigo (Indianapolis, IN); the rationale for choosing this sex is that males are more prone to developing olfactory disorders than females [28–32] and that female’s SVZ adult neurogenesis is influenced by fluctuating circulating hormones during menstrual cycles [33]. Upon arrival, mice were housed in a temperature-controlled, 12/12-h light/dark cycle room and allowed to acclimate for one week prior to experimentation. Animals were provided with deionized water and pellet rat chow (Teklad Dlobal 18% Protein Rodent Diet, 2018s; Envigo) ad libitum. This study was conducted in compliance with standard animal use practices and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Purdue University (PUCAC No. 1112000526).

Co-culture of SVZ neurospheres with CP tissues

To investigate the direct impact of CP on SVZ neurogenesis, we established an in vitro co-culture system using a two-chamber Transwell device [34]. Freshly isolated CP tissues (pooled from 2 brains) were placed in the upper Transwell insert (pore size of 0.4 µm at a density of 4x106 pores/cm2) and the neurospheres derived from adult mouse SVZ tissues were cultured on the glass surface of the lower chamber. To produce SVZ-derived neurospheres, the SVZ tissue, which was a thin layer lining the lateral ventricle, was carefully dissected using a pair of Dumont curved #5/45 forceps. The dissociated preparation, following filtration through a 40-µm cell strainer, was cultured in a neurosphere background medium (NBM: Neurobasal plus medium containing 2% B-27, 1% GlutaMAX, and 50 µg/mL gentamicin) supplemented with EGF (20 ng/mL), FGF (20 ng/mL), and heparin (2 µg/mL) for neurosphere induction. Primary neurospheres, which were formed 6 days after the initial seeding, were collected for Trypsin dissociation and the cell suspension was then reseeded into a 96-well U-shaped-bottom microplate (0.1 mL/well, 1.0 × 104 cells/mL in NBM enriched with growth factors and heparin) for secondary neurosphere formation. After 24 hrs, the secondary neurospheres were formed with nearly identical diameters [35]. CP tissues were dissected from brain ventricles of two PBS-perfused mouse brains. The collected CP tissues were resuspended in 0.6 mL NBM and then transferred to the Transwell inserts, which were placed above the seeded neurospheres. The control group had 0.6 mL NBM only in the insert. Following 24 hrs of co-culture, CP tissues along with the insert were removed. Neurosphere culture continued for an additional 6 days. All cell cultures, including this CP-SVZ co-culture, in this study were performed in 95% air/5% CO2 at 37 °C. Upon completion of the culture, neurospheres were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS for 10 min at room temperature and stored in PBS until further immunostaining for confocal microscopy analysis.

Immunocytochemistry

Immunocytochemistry (ICC) was performed as previously described [35] to characterize the neurosphere growth and the impact of co-culture with CP tissues. Fixed neurosphere samples were blocked/permeabilized in PBST containing 5% normal goat serum and 0.3% TritonX-100. Blocked neurospheres were further incubated with primary antibodies against MAP2 (marker for mature neuronal cells) and Nestin (marker for NSPCs) at 4 °C overnight. Following 3 washes with PBST, neurospheres were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) at room temperature for 1 h protected from light, followed by counterstaining with DAPI. After 3 PBST washes, samples were immediately analyzed utilizing Nikon A1Rsi confocal system.

Primary culture of choroid plexus epithelial cells (CPECs) and leptomeningeal fibroblasts

Primary culture of CPECs was performed as previously described [36] with modifications. Briefly, freshly isolated CP tissues from 2 mice were washed in Leibovitz’s L-15 medium and then pelleted by centrifugation at 300 g for 3 min. After aspirating L-15 medium, CP tissues were dissociated with pre-warmed pronase (2.5 mg/mL) in HBSS at 37 °C for 7 min. To terminate the digestion, 1 mL CPEC medium, which was prepared by supplementing DMEM/F12 medium with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), gentamycin (40 μg/mL) and EGF (100 ng/mL), was added, followed by centrifugation (500 g for 5 min) to pellet the dissociated CP tissues. After supernatant aspiration, CP samples were resuspended in 2 mL CPEC culture medium and further dissociated by pipetting up and down 10 times through a regular P1000 tip. Dissociated cells were then plated on a PLO-precoated 35-mm Petri dish and cultured for 3 days, followed by treatment with cis-HP (25 μg/mL) for 3 days to eliminate fibroblast contamination. After a 1-day continued culture with CPEC medium, the primary culture of CPECs was established and ready for the studies described below.

The leptomeninges, a structure in direct contact with the CSF, were processed for primary culture as previously described [37]. Following isolation, the tissue was minced and digested with 2.5 mg/mL pronase in HBSS at 37 °C for 10 minutes. The digest was then filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer for seeding. Leptomeningeal fibroblasts were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 µg/mL), and 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol.

Transplantation of primary CPECs

To test whether the CP modulates SVZ NSC activities, primary-cultured CPECs were stereotaxically injected to the SVZ niche, which actively supplies newborn neurons to the OB [38]. Since the growth factors such as EGF are known to influence SVZ NSCs [39], the CPECs were cultured in EGF- and FBS-free medium for an additional 2 days with daily medium replacement. For brain injections, a suspension of primary CPECs was prepared by incubating the primary culture with Trypsin at 37 °C for 3 min. Following 2 wash/centrifugation cycles, CPECs were resuspended in artificial CSF (aCSF) at a density of 1.5 × 103 cells/µL. These cells were then used either for direct transplantation or first labeled with the fluorescent Vybrant DiI cell-labeling solution (200x) for subsequent transplantations.

Upon intracranial injection, mice under isoflurane anesthesia received 1 µL CPEC suspension at the following coordinates: 0.0 mm, 1.5 mm, 2.0 mm (anterior, lateral, ventral distance relative to bregma); the control mice received 1 µL aCSF at the same coordinates. To ensure accurate graft, preparations were slowly injected within 10 min, followed by a 5-min stay before withdrawing the needle from the brain. The incised skin was closed by Gluture topical tissue adhesive alongside the cut, followed by triple antibiotic ointment application. Post-operative animals recovered on a heat pad and were then housed individually for 3 days.

To label proliferating NSCs, BrdU (50 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally (IP) injected 3 hrs prior to necroscopy. Under deep anesthesia by IP injection of ketamine (75 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), mice were sequentially perfused with ice-cold PBS and 4% PFA in PBS. Brain samples were extracted, and immersion fixed overnight with by 4% PFA. Following dehydration in 30% sucrose for 72 hrs, brain samples were mounted on a microtome and frozen by dry ice for slicing. Coronal slices containing the SVZ, which approximately span from +1.5 mm (anterior) to −1.0 mm (posterior) relative to the bregma, were collected from the injected hemisphere (serially into 3 wells for preservation). Slices were preserved in cryopreservation medium (30% sucrose, 1% polyvinylpyrrolidone, 30% ethylene glycol in 0.1 M phosphate buffer) at −20 °C until immunohistochemistry analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to characterize the SVZ adult neurogenesis as previously described [35]. First, brain slices preserved in cryopreservation medium were washed with PBS 3X to remove cryopreservation medium. In subsequent steps, if BrdU analysis was needed, slices were incubated in 2 N HCl for 30 min under 37 °C, followed by neutralization with 0.1 M borate buffer (pH 8.5) for 30 min; slices continued to be blocked/permeabilized with PBST containing 5% normal goat serum and 0.3% TritonX-100. Otherwise, PBS-washed slices were directly blocked/permeabilized. Blocked brain slices were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following 3 PBST washes, brain slices were further incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) for 1 h at room temperature and then with DAPI for counterstaining (protected from light). Following 3 PBST washes, brain slices were mounted onto microscope slides with mounting medium. Images were captured by Nikon A1Rsi Confocal system; Z-stack scanning and large-image stitching were coupled for 3D reconstruction and cell counting.

Differential centrifugation of conditioned medium for sEV collection

CP tissues were cultured for 24 hrs in sEV-free CPEC medium, which was prepared by mixing ultracentrifuged DMEM/F12 medium containing penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and gentamycin (40 μg/mL) with ultracentrifuged exosome-depleted FBS (10%). Conditioned medium from primary CPECs was collected at the end of the culture. To begin the differential centrifugation, conditioned medium was centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min to pellet live tissues/cells, followed by a 20-min centrifugation at 2000 g to remove dead cells/debris. Subsequently, the medium was further centrifuged at 4 °C at 12,000 g for 30 min, leaving micro-extracellular vesicles (mEVs) pelleted for collection. The supernatant medium was then ultracentrifuged at 4 °C at 120,000 g for 70 min, which separated the crude small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) (pellet) from soluble proteins (supernatant) (rotor model: Type 70.1 Ti; ultracentrifuge model: Optima L-90K). After collecting the soluble proteins, the crude sEV samples were purified by ultracentrifugation for a second time. Finally, sEVs were resuspended and stored in NBM (for testing neurosphere growth) or PBS (for Western blot, nanoparticle tracking analysis and electron microscopy) at − 80 °C. Western blot

Western blot was performed as previously described [40]. Briefly, proteins were extracted from samples with RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor. The protein concentrations in samples were determined by a BCA protein assay kit. After mixing with 2× Laemmli sample buffer, samples were heated to 95 °C for 5 min. Upon electrophoresis, 2 µg protein from sEV samples and CP lysates were loaded to verify expression of sEV-related marker (CD63 and TSG101), while the remaining samples analyzed in this study had 10 µg protein loaded into each well. Following electrophoresis under 120 V for 75 min, proteins on the gel were then transferred to PVDF membranes (under 100 V for 1 hr in an ice tank). The PVDF membranes were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies (1:3000 for α-tubulin and 1:500 for all the other primary antibodies). Following sufficient washes with TBST, PVDF membranes were further incubated with goat anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:3000) at room temperature for 1 h. The colorimetric signals on the membranes were developed using ECL Western Blotting substrate and captured by ChemiDoc XRS system. The band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and were reported as relative expression levels normalized to α-tubulin.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

NTA was performed as previously described [41]. Briefly, sEV samples diluted in ultracentrifuged sterile-filtered PBS were slowly injected to the imaging chamber of the NanoSight LM10 system. This system was equipped with a 405 nm laser and a sCMOS camera. By setting up the camera level at 10 and the detection threshold at 4, two 30-sec videos were captured at the “optimal imaging location”, an area just past the flare spot where the laser beam emerges into the chamber. The particle size distribution and concentration of sEV samples were analyzed using the NanoSight NTA software (v2.3).

Electron microscopy

Electron microscopic (EM) images of CP-secreted sEVs were acquired under technical guidance by Life Science Microscopy Facility (LSMF) at Purdue University. Briefly, sEV samples were pipetted onto a carbon-coated copper EM grid and incubated for 2 min. After blotting away excess liquid, grids were washed with diH2O to remove salts. The grids were then negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 1 min. Images were acquired through a Gatan US1000 2K CCD camera on a FEI Tecnai T20 transmission electron microscope equipped with a LaB6 source operated at 200kV.

Fluorescent labeling of sEVs

To study the distribution and cellular uptake of CP-secreted sEVs, isolated sEVs were fluorescently labeled with PKH67, a lipophilic membrane dye, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, sEVs were incubated with PKH67 dye diluted 25-fold in Diluent C. The reaction proceeded at 37 °C for 30 minutes and was stopped by adding 1% BSA in PBS. Unbound dye was removed by two rounds of differential centrifugation. A negative control was prepared by subjecting PBS supplemented with PKH67 (without sEVs) to the same dilution, incubation, and centrifugation procedure. Following the final wash, labeled sEVs were resuspended in NBM for in vitro neurosphere assays or in PBS for in vivo ICV infusion.

Successful labeling and complete removal of unbound dye were validated using a 6-hour neurosphere uptake assay. Neurospheres cultured in NBM containing PKH67-labeled sEVs or the negative control preparation for 6 hrs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 minutes. To assess sEV uptake by neural progenitor cells, fixed neurospheres were processed for immunofluorescence as described in section "Immunocytochemistry". This involved blocking with normal goat serum (NGS), incubation with a rat anti-Nestin primary antibody, and labeling with an appropriate fluorescent secondary antibody for subsequent confocal microscopy.

Manganese (Mn) exposure and atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS)

Mice were exposed to Mn as previously described [20, 21]. Briefly, MnCl2·4 H2O (dissolved in sterile saline) was IP injected to mice at the dose of 6 mg Mn/kg, once daily, 5 days/week for 4 consecutive weeks; controls received an equivalent volume of sterile saline. Upon completion of Mn treatment, mice were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (75:10 mg/kg, 1 mg/kg, IP) and transcardially perfused with 30 mL ice-cold PBS to remove blood. After extracting the brain, brain tissues (CP, SVZ, OB, hippocampus, striatum, and frontal cortex) were collected for Mn concentration measurements with AAS.

Following digestion with concentrated ultrapure HNO3 in a MARSXpress microwave-accelerated reaction system, Mn concentrations in brain samples were quantified by 200 Series SpectrAA with a GTA 120 graphite tube atomizer. The detection limit for Mn by this system was 0.09 µg/L. Throughout the measurements, AAS readings were kept in the linear range of the standard curve (0–20 µg/L) by proper dilutions with diH2O. Mn concentrations across tested brain regions were reported as “µg Mn/g tissue”.

Procedures for ICV infusion were detailed previously [35]. Briefly, loaded pumps were coupled with Alzet brain infusion kit 3 to establish a pump-tubing-cannulation system. Isoflurane-anesthetized mice were fixed onto a stereotaxic device. After exposing and clearing the bregma area on the skull, an extended incision was sagittally made to the neck area. Subsequently, a sterile curved hemostat was inserted subcutaneously (curved side up) and slowly advanced to the back area; the hemostat was gently opened and closed to create a space for pump implantation. A burr hole was then drilled at the following coordinates: 0.22 mm, 1.0 mm (posterior, lateral relative to bregma). The pump was then gently inserted into this newly created subcutaneous space, with the cannulation piece (connected with the tubing) available for “pick-up” by the cannula holder. Clamped by the holder, the cannula unit (with Loctite applied on its base) was slowly lowered down to let the steel cannula (2.5 mm) into the brain through the burr hole by 2.5 mm relative to the skull surface and was thus tightly glued. The incised skin was closed by Gluture topical tissue adhesive alongside the cut, followed by triple antibiotic ointment application. The postoperative animals were recovered on a heat pad and housed separately. Upon completion of the ICV infusion, mice were either sacrificed for sampling or had pump removed (under anesthesia) followed by closure of tubing’s free end and survived until the planned sampling date.

Upon completion of ICV infusion experiments, brain sampling (perfusion, fixation, and dehydration) and microtome slice preparation (serially into 6 wells) were performed as described before. Brain slices were stained through immunohistochemistry to characterize the SVZ adult neurogenesis as affected by ICV infusions.

CSF collection

The CSF samples were collected by cisterna magna puncture with a fire-pulled glass pipette. Briefly, under isoflurane anesthesia, the head of mice was fixed onto a stereotaxic device. By sequentially trimming the fur on the neck, sagittally incising the skin, removing the connective tissues, and severing 3 covering muscular layers, dura mater with a pale/tough texture was spotted. The fire-pulled pipette was carefully advanced, while avoiding evident blood vessels, for cisterna magna puncture. CSF samples in the pipette were carefully released into Eppendorf tubes. By a brief centrifugation at 2000 g for 5 min, CSF samples were inspected for any blood contamination (evident red spot on the bottom); contaminated CSF samples were discarded. From each mouse, 3–8 µL of clean CSF was collected. To use an equal amount of CSF for sEV NTA measurements (5 µL), samples yielding less than 5 µL were pooled.

Dissociation curves were examined to verify that the majority of detected fluorescence was derived from the labeling of specific PCR products. Each qPCR reaction was run in duplicate. Relative mRNA expressions were calculated using 2−∆∆Ct method with beta-actin (Actb) as the reference gene. siRNA knockdown

Primary CPECs were grown to a confluence of approximately 50% in CPEC culture medium. To transfect siRNA, culture medium was removed, and cells were washed with Opti-MEM medium for a serum-free environment. For SMPD3 knockdown, Opti-MEM medium containing Smpd3-targeting siRNA (10 nM) and 0.5% Lipofectamin RNAiMAX was prepared, mixed, and added to the culture; controls were cultured with medium containing nontargeting siRNA (otherwise the same). Cells were incubated with siRNA for 48 hrs. In the first experiment to assess the siRNA transfection efficacy, cells were then collected to quantify Smpd3 mRNA level by qPCR and SMPD3 protein expression by Western blot. In the second experiment to determine the role of SMPD3 in CPEC sEV secretion, following the 48-hr transfection, culture medium was replaced with sEV-free CPEC medium for an extended culture of 1 day. The culture medium was then collected for differential centrifugation for sEV concentration measurements by NTA.

Buried food test

The buried food test was performed as described with modifications [43]. Prior to testing, a 24-hr food deprivation was conducted by transferring the mice to new cages with water but without food pellets on the wire top. Testing cages were prepared by filling clean cages with 560 g Teklad laboratory animal corncob bedding, which, following smoothing to flat, creates a 3.5-cm layer. One piece of food pellet weighing 2 g was then hidden underneath the corncob at the center of the testing cage. Before the pellet-finding test, mice were habituated in clean cages (without a pellet) for 15 min. Subsequently, mice were transferred from habituation cages to testing cages: a digital stopwatch was used to count the latency time (s) of mice finding the hidden pellet. The “finding” event was defined by observing the pellet unearthed by mice from the 3.5-cm thick corncob bedding. Of note, the food pellet was hidden underneath at the center but not the corners of cages; this change from others [43] was based on our observation that CD-1 mice, upon transfer to a new cage, tend to explore the cage corners and edges by digging the bedding materials in the beginning seconds, which could confuse the experimenter in defining the “finding” event.

Olfactory preference test

This olfactory preference test was performed in the form of habituation/dishabituation as previously described [44]. Prior to testing, a mouse was placed in the testing cage to acclimate for 15 min. To start a trial, a piece of clean filter paper (1 cm × 1 cm) was adhered to the center of the testing cage, followed by 20 µL of water pipetted onto this paper. In the following 2 min, the cumulative time spent sniffing the filter paper (investigation time) was recorded with a digital stopwatch. This presentation of water was repeated twice with a 1-min interval (habituation). On the fourth trial (dishabituation), female urine (screened as the most preferred odorant) was introduced to the subject by pipetting 20 µL of urine sample onto the filter paper; this was also repeated twice with a 1-min interval. Investigation time in the six 2-minute sessions was plotted to assess the subjects’ preference toward female urine as affected by CP-targeted sEV secretion suppression.

Olfactory avoidance test

In the olfactory avoidance test, 2-MB acid (98.0% purity, 9.16 M), a known olfactory repellent, was used as previously reported [44]. Selection of this compound was also based on the results from our screening assay, where 2-MB acid was identified as the most aversive odorant. In this test, the testing cage was divided into two compartments (1:2) by a partition curtain, which was made by a print paper with one end cut into strips; animals can freely cross compartments through this curtain. Upon testing, a mouse was first habituated to this cage for 15 min. Subsequently, a filter paper pipetted with 20 µL diH2O was introduced into the smaller compartment. During the following 2-min session, the time spent (s) in the larger compartment was recorded as “avoidance time for diH2O”. Next, after removing the diH2O filter paper, a filter paper with 2-MB acid solution (1,000x diluted with diH2O, 20 µL) was placed into the smaller compartment. Likewise, the time spent in the larger compartment was recorded as “avoidance time for 2-MB acid”. Ultimately, the avoidance behavior was quantified as the “Avoidance Time,” calculated by subtracting the “avoidance time for 2-MB acid” from the “avoidance time for diH2O”. Data analysis

Neurosphere samples were analyzed for both migration and marker expression. For migration analysis, acquired images were contrast-adjusted in NIS-Elements software to enable clear visualization of all cells. The total area covered by cells from each neurosphere was selected using the freehand tool for quantification. For marker-based cell counting, because the markers used in this study (Nestin, MAP2, DCX) were all cytosolic or cytoskeletal, images were exported to FIJI for analysis using the “Cell Counter” plugin to identify specific cell types based on cytosolic expression around the nucleus.

For IHC-based cell counting to evaluate SVZ neurogenesis, proliferating SVZ NSPCs were identified according to established marker combinations [45], first by the co-expression of Nestin (red) and GFAP (blue), resulting in a purple signal, combined with a nuclear BrdU (green) signal. Newborn neurons were identified by nuclear co-localization of NeuN (green) and BrdU (red), resulting in a yellow nuclear signal.

For immunofluorescent intensity quantification, all images within a comparative group were acquired under identical optical parameters, including laser intensity and detector gain. Intensity was then quantified using the “Measure” function in FIJI. To quantify a non-marker signal localized to a specific cell type, its intensity was measured within the cytoplasmic contours defined by that cell’s marker expression.

For cellular morphology-based evaluations, we used both phase contrast images and molecular marker expression. For example, a CPEC was indicated by the presence of both a cuboidal shape and cytosolic TTR or membrane-localized Cadherin-3 expression. To quantify DCX(+) neuroblast neurite length, the longest process from a total of 80 cells was traced using FIJI’s segmented line tool (20 cells per mouse main OB slice).

For cell density quantifications, the area of interest was first selected, and then marker-positive cells within it were counted. The density was calculated as the number of identified cells divided by the selected area.

For statistical analysis, data in this study are presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests were used for comparisons between two groups; for comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was performed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparisons test. Graphs and statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism version 8.0. Statistical significance was designated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001 (for comparisons with controls), and #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, ###p < 0.001 (for comparisons between non-control groups). For clarity, p-values were provided directly on the relevant statistical panels.

Results

CP directly regulates SVZ adult neurogenesis: evidence from in vitro co-culture and in vivo transplantation assays

To establish the relationship between CP and adult neurogenesis activities in SVZ, a two-chamber co-culture system to model the CP and SVZ interaction was utilized, i.e., placing freshly dissected CP tissues from all brain ventricles in the insert above the SVZ neurospheres cultured in the lower chamber (Fig. 1A). Co-culture of CP with SVZ neurospheres for 24 hrs, as the isolated CP tissues remained largely viable within the first 24 hrs in vitro, resulted in a significantly enriched growth of SVZ neurospheres in comparison with controls (Fig. 1B). MAP2 (+) cells per neurosphere were increased by 51.1% when co-cultured with CP (68.4 ± 12.9 in controls vs.103.4 ± 14.1 with CP) (Fig. 1C). The presence of CP also promoted the neurosphere migration by 62.4% (Fig. 1E). However, the Nestin (+) cells did not differ between the two groups (Fig. 1D). Thus, our initial in vitro co-culture data demonstrated that CP promoted the SVZ adult neurogenesis by stimulating the neuronal differentiation and migration without evidently affecting the neural stem/progenitor cells.

To determine if the CP exerted a similar stimulating effect on SVZ NSCs in vivo, primary-cultured CPECs (Fig. 1F) were collected and intracranially injected into the SVZ niche area, where substantial newborn neurons are produced for OB [38] (Fig. 1G). Morphological and molecular characterization of primary cultured CPECs indicates a high purity of CPECs, evidenced by the dominant presence of a cuboidal shape, cytosolic TTR expression, and intercellular expression of Cadherin-3 proteins (Fig. 1F). As illustrated in Fig. 1G, the transplantation coordinate was located in the NSPC-active anterior portion of the lateral ventricle wall and slightly ventral to the RMS. Compared with aCSF-injected controls, injection of CPECs significantly elevated the proliferation of GFAP (+)/Nestin(+) NSPCs in the SVZ by 30.2% (Fig. 1H, I), suggesting that some yet-unidentified factors released from CPECs prompted SVZ NSPCs into a pro-neurogenesis status.

To further characterize the effects of CP on SVZ neurogenesis, we evaluated how this transplantation may affect newborn neurons in the OB (Fig. 1J). Quantification of BrdU (+)/NeuN(+) cells revealed that CPEC transplantation significantly decreased newborn neurons in the granule cell layer (but not glomerular layer) of the OB (Fig. 1K, L). Considering the earlier observation of CPEC-induced NSPC activation and the complex neuroanatomy of the SVZ-RMS-OB axis, we performed long-term tracking of transplanted CPECs with Dil staining for their migration, survival, and intercellular interactions (Fig. 1M). Histological analysis revealed that, 30 days following the procedure, transplanted CPECs had a good survival rate within the transplanted SVZ niche, moderately migrated into nearby areas, including the striatum, corpus callosum, and areas slightly anterior and posterior (Fig. 1N), and further into the RMS (Fig. 1O). Furthermore, despite a less pronounced presence in the RMS, observed live CPECs appeared to exhibit repulsive effects on neuroblast migration, evidenced by RMS disruption when CPECs were located either inside (top panel) or dorsal (middle panel)/ventral (bottom panel) to the RMS (Fig. 1O). Given the strong and specific expression of the secretory protein TTR by CPECs (Fig. 1P), we investigated whether this known CP-secreted molecule could be detected in the RMS. Colocalization of non-cellular TTR signals with DCX (+) neuroblasts in the distal RMS showed a clear uptake of CP-secreted TTR by neuroblasts (Fig. 1P). These observations suggest that CP, when directly introduced near the RMS, can change the predetermined trajectory of neuroblast migration, possibly underlying the decreased presence of newborn neurons in the OB (Fig. 1L).

Overall, our observations validated the direct effects of CP on the SVZ NSPCs and provide evidence for its regulation of subsequent RMS neuroblast migration and OB neurogenesis. Given the integration of secreted TTR by RMS cells, we sought to identify the critical fraction in the CP secretome that exerted these SVZ regulatory properties.

Small extracellular vesicle (sEV) is a critical CP secretome fraction for regulating SVZ activity

The sEVs are recognized as effective mediators for intercellular communication [22, 23, 46]. Thus, we assessed the ability of the CP to produce and secrete sEVs by comparatively analyzing canonical sEV marker expression across brain regions, including the CP, SVZ, striatum, hippocampus, and cerebellum (Fig. S1). Transcripts level of Cd9, Cd63, Cd81, and Tsg101 were significantly higher in the CP than those in other evaluated regions (Fig. S1A-S1D). For example, Cd9 mRNA and Cd63 mRNA in the CP were 77.0% and 90.3% higher than those in other tested regions, respectively (Fig. S1A, B). Although the enriched expressions in the CP were less pronounced for Cd81 and Tsg101, the CP still had the highest expressions of both markers (Fig. S1C and S1D). These qPCR data were further verified at the protein level. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated that CD63 (Fig. S1E) and TSG101 (Fig. S1G) were enriched in the CP, particularly within the CPECs, the cell type in direct contact with CSF for substance secretion. These observations suggest a robust capacity of the CP in sEV production and secretion, thus prompting the need for functional characterization of this secretome fraction.

Using differential centrifugation, we acquired the sEVs and other fractions from the CP secretome, including micro-extracellular vesicles (mEVs) and the soluble fraction (Fig. 2A). Supplementing SVZ neurosphere cultures with each fraction revealed a fraction-specific regulatory pattern on neurogenesis, with sEVs having the most prominent effect (Fig. S2A). Immunoblotting for the mature neuron marker MAP2 showed that only the sEV fraction significantly increased its expression (Fig. S2B, C). In contrast, Nestin expression was elevated by all fractions, indicating a high general responsiveness of NSPCs to CP secretome supplementation in vitro (Fig. S2B, D). Neurosphere migration was increased by both sEV and mEV supplementation, but not by the soluble fraction (Fig. S2A, E). Given their in vitro effects on NSPC proliferation, neuronal differentiation, and migration, sEVs emerged as the most promising CP secretome fraction for further investigation.

Consistent with the comparative analyses in Fig. S2, confocal analysis of sEV-supplemented neurospheres confirmed that sEVs significantly promoted neurosphere growth (Fig. 2B), increasing neuronal differentiation/maturation and migration by 21.3% and 65.6%, respectively (Fig. 2C, E). Nestin (+) cells were not altered by sEV supplementation (Fig. 2D). We then characterized these sEVs according to the Guidelines by the International Society of Extracellular Vesicles [47] using western blot, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and electron microscopy. Western blot analysis demonstrated these sEVs were rich in sEV-specific markers TSG101 and CD63 (Fig. 2F). Importantly, under electron microscopy, sEVs exhibited the typical “cup-shaped” morphology with a diameter less than 200 nm (Fig. 2G). In addition, NTA for size distribution analysis further confirmed the diameters of captured particles were primarily smaller than 200 nm (Fig. 2H). Hence, these data demonstrate the unique biological properties of sEVs within the CP secretome following their physical separation via differential centrifugation.

To track the cellular uptake, CP-sEVs were incubated with PKH67 for fluorescent labeling. Unbound dye was removed by two rounds of ultracentrifugation before the sEVs were applied to neurospheres (Fig. 2I). Confocal imaging showed robust PKH67 signals that largely overlapped with Nestin (+) cells in neurospheres, while control samples exhibited minimal signal (Fig. 2J, K). This assay confirmed both a direct interaction of CP-sEVs with SVZ NSPCs and the effectiveness of our labeling workflow.

We next performed ICV infusion of PKH67-labeled CP-sEVs to assess their biodistribution in vivo (Fig. 2L). Robust sEV signals were detected in infused brains, with the most evident accumulation within the SVZ. Signals in deeper parenchyma were lower and more punctate (Fig. 2M). Co-staining for sEVs (PKH67) and NeuN revealed greater sEV enrichment in the NeuN-dense gray matter striatum than in the dorsal white matter corpus callosum (Fig. 2N). Analysis of sEV signal intensity from the CSF-adjacent to the CSF-distal striatum confirmed that uptake was more pronounced by cells near the ventricle than by those in deeper parenchyma (Fig. 2N insets, O). Furthermore, co-staining with NSC markers Nestin and GFAP revealed robust sEV signals within the cytosol of Nestin (+)/GFAP(+) NSCs (Fig. 2P). CP-sEV uptake was also observed moderately in DCX+ neuroblasts (Fig. 2Q) and minimally in CD31+ vascular cells (Fig. 2R). Overall, these observations support a direct role of CP-sEVs in modulating SVZ NSPCs and neurogenesis.

CP-sEVs mediate Mn-induced dysregulation of SVZ adult neurogenesis

For understanding the in vivo function of CP-secreted sEVs in SVZ adult neurogenesis, we characterized the neurogenic process following ICV infusion of CP-derived sEVs in mice. Mn is known for its toxic effects on the SVZ/RMS/OB axis, as characterized by our group previously [20, 21]. Hence, with our Mn poisoning paradigm as a model, we probed whether sEVs collected from the CP of Mn-exposed mice differentially affected adult neurogenesis, compared to sEVs collected from healthy controls. A cohort of adult mice received daily IP injections of Mn at 6 mg/kg for 4 wks; controls received saline. The CP-derived sEV fractions were isolated after differential centrifugation (defined as “Mn-sEVs” and “Ctrl-sEVs” for Mn-exposed and control mice, respectively) and loaded into the osmotic pump for ICV infusion (Fig. 3A). Metal analysis by atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS) verified that subchronic Mn exposure increased Mn accumulation in the CP to a much greater extent than in other tested brain regions (Fig. 3B). We designed 3 experimental timelines to characterize the impact of CP-secreted sEVs (control vs. Mn-exposed) on NSPC proliferation in SVZ (Fig. 3C), newborn neuron generation in the OB (Fig. 3H), and neuroblast migration in RMS (Fig. 3K).

First, a 4-day infusion of CP-derived sEVs, regardless of the host’s exposure status, significantly increased the proliferating NSPCs in the SVZ (Fig. 3D, E). Compared to controls with PBS infusions, Ctrl-sEVs induced a greater proliferation of SVZ NSPCs (by 48.7%) than did Mn-sEVs (by 25.6%) (Fig. 3E). These data suggest that SVZ NSPCs were highly sensitive to CP-secreted sEVs. Although Mn is known to induce microgliosis in the SVZ [21], our current data showed that ICV infusion of Mn-sEVs did not significantly affect the microglial density in the SVZ (Fig. 3F, G).

Second, to study newborn neuron supply to the OB, Ctrl-sEVs and Mn-sEVs were infused for 4 days and, on day 30, OB tissues were dissected to quantify the newborn neuron density in both the granule cell layer (GCL) and the glomerular layer (GL) (Fig. 3H). Compared with PBS-only controls, infusion of Ctrl-sEVs significantly increased the newborn neuron density in the OB’s GCL by 65.4%, while Mn-sEVs significantly decreased this density by 33.1% (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3I, J). ICV infusion of CP sEVs, however, did not change the density of newborn neurons in GL (Fig. 3I, J). These findings indicated that normal CP-sEVs are critical for maintaining the newborn neuron supply to the OB; Mn accumulation in the CP, however, likely alters CP-secreted sEVs.

Finally, to understand the discrepancy between increased proliferation in SVZ and diminished newborn neurons in OB following Mn-sEV infusion, we investigated the neuroblast migration in the RMS (Fig. 3K). Our data revealed that, in the initial segment of RMS (the portion in proximity to brain ventricle), infusion of Mn-sEVs led to a greater density of microglial cells than did Ctrl-sEVs, as shown by an escalated ratio of Iba-1 (+)/DCX(+) cells (Fig. 3L, M). Notably, in Mn-sEV infusion group, Iba-1 (+) microglial cells displayed an enlarged body size in both SVZ and the initial RMS segment, indicative of an activated (phagocytotic) phenotype (Fig. 3L, M). In addition, we observed a significantly elevated rate of apoptosis along the RMS in Mn-sEV group, in which the apoptotic signal was mainly present in the RMS-supporting astrocytes but not in the DCX (+) neuroblasts (Fig. 3N, O). Thus, certain underdefined inflammatory factors/modulators carried by Mn-sEVs may diminish newborn neurons in OB. Taken together, these findings demonstrated that CP-secreted sEVs are potent modulators of the SVZ adult neurogenesis.

Considering the upregulated SVZ neurogenesis following ICV infusion of CP-sEVs (Fig. 3), we sought to determine if this effect was CP-specific. We thus established a primary culture of leptomeningeal fibroblasts, another CSF-contacting cell type (Fig. S3A). ICC staining validated typical fibroblast morphology and marker expression: elongated processes and strong vimentin expression, but no TTR (Fig. S3A). We then isolated sEVs from fibroblast conditioned medium via differential centrifugation and performed ICV infusion to assess their effect on SVZ neurogenesis in the OB (Fig. S3B). IHC staining revealed that fibroblast-sEVs significantly decreased newborn neurons in both the GCL (by 60.9%) and GL (by 38.6%) (Fig. S3C, D). This “opposite” effect supports that the pro-neurogenic activity of CP-sEVs is CP-specific. These findings suggest that sEVs from different source tissues may collectively fine-tune adult neurogenic activity through distinct regulatory roles.

SMPD3 determines sEV secretion by CPECs

We further investigated the molecular pathways in the CP that modulate sEV secretion. SMPD3 [sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase 3, also known as neutral sphingomyelinase 2 (nSMase2)] is an important rate-limiting sEV-related enzyme that catalyzes the biogenesis and release of sEVs into the extracellular space [48–51]. Our immunohistochemical staining data demonstrated the presence of SMPD3 in the CP (validated by minimal signals in negative controls) (Fig. 4A). Moreover, its expression was substantially higher in CPECs and SVZ NSPCs than in surrounding brain regions (Fig. 4A, B). Higher-magnification confocal imaging revealed the co-localization of SMPD3 with canonical sEV markers CD63, TSG101, and CD9 (Fig. S1). SMPD3 also showed substantial co-localization with CD63, while some non-overlapping CD63 signals were located more apically toward the CSF, suggesting a snapshot of active sEV secretion (Fig. S1F). A similar co-localization pattern was observed with TSG101 and CD9, to varying extents. TSG101 was more apically localized and exhibited a nanoscale punctate morphology, also indicative of ongoing sEV secretion (Fig. S1H). The CD9 pattern resembled that of CD63, with substantial overlap and a non-overlapping fraction appearing as nanoscale puncta protruding into the CSF (Fig. S1I). These observations suggest a critical role for SMPD3 in regulating sEV synthesis and secretion in the CP.

We then used GW4869, a noncompetitive inhibitor of SMPD3 enzymatic activity that disrupts sEV biogenesis and release, to examine its effect on CP sEV secretion [11, 42, 52]. Mice received 3 daily IP injections of GW4869 (2.5 µg/g) or equivalent volumes of DMSO (as controls) (Fig. 4C). Following the GW4869 administration, the concentration of sEVs in the CSF, determined by differential centrifugation and NTA, was significantly lower than that in controls (Fig. 4D). The qPCR data also showed that GW4869 treatment significantly diminished the level of Ttr transcripts in the SVZ (Fig. 4E), a gene/cargo highly enriched in the CP [26] and carried by sEVs [53].

To further define the role of SMPD3 in regulating sEV secretion by the CP, Smpd3 expression in primary cultured CPECs was knocked down via siRNA transfection (Fig. 4F). This transfection significantly reduced both Smpd3 mRNA (Fig. 4G) and SMPD3 protein levels (Fig. 4H, I) and suppressed sEV secretion (Fig. 4J), demonstrating that SMPD3 regulates sEV secretion by CPECs in vitro.

We then aimed for in vivo CP-selective Smpd3 knockdown. Adenovirus-associated virus type 5 (AAV5) can selectively target CP for genetic modifications through ICV injections [54, 55]. By ICV injection of AAV5 followed by histological analysis 2 weeks post-treatment (Fig. 4K), our IHC staining revealed that AAV5 possessed a unique tropism toward the CSF-contacting CPECs, evidenced by GFP (transgene carried by AAV5) selectively observed in the CPECs with minimal to no signal in CD31 (+) vascular cells within the CP or other parenchymal cells (Fig. 4L). Sagittal whole-brain staining further demonstrates the unique AAV5 tropism in the CP located in other brain ventricles, i.e., the third and fourth ventricles (Fig. 4L). Importantly, AAV5 did not transfect cells in the SVZ (Fig. 4L), validating its usefulness to study the CP-SVZ axis in later experiments (Figs. 5, 6).

With its unique tropism in the CP, we first confirmed that AAV5 alone did not alter SVZ adult neurogenesis. By characterizing adult neurogenesis in the SVZ and OB following ICV injection of AAV5 (PBS injections as controls) (Fig. S4A, G), we observed that at 30 days after the AAV5 injections, there was no significant alteration in the proliferating NSPCs in the SVZ (Fig. S4B, C). Although more Iba-1(+) cells were observed in the CP of the AAV5-injected group compared with controls (Fig. S4D, E), Iba-1(+) cells were not altered in the SVZ (Fig. S4D, F). Similarly, we did not observe any alterations in the density of BrdU(+)/NeuN(+) newborn neurons in GCL or GL (Fig. S4H, I) following AAV5 injections. Using longer time points up to 180 days post-injection (Fig. S4J), we observed strong, sustained GFP expression selectively in CPECs at 90 and 180 days (Fig. S4K, L) and no difference in body weight between groups (Fig. S4M), supporting the safety and long-term efficacy of the AAV5 vector for CP-SVZ studies.

Given the critical role of SMPD3 (Fig. 4A–J), we used AAV5 carrying shRNA targeting Smpd3 to test whether reducing SMPD3 expression in CPECs suppressed the sEV release into the CSF (Fig. 4M). Compared with the controls injected with control vector “AAV5-shRNA (Ctrl)” containing a non-targeting sequence, injection with AAV5-shRNA(Smpd3) significantly reduced the SMPD3 protein in the CP tissues 4 weeks following the ICV injection (Fig. 4N, O). This reduction was further supported by immunohistochemical staining, demonstrating significantly decreased SMPD3 expression in the CPECs (Fig. 4P, Q). CSF was collected from these mice through cisterna magna puncture. After obtaining sEV preparations through differential centrifugation as described previously, samples were analyzed with NTA. Our data reveal that a CPEC-targeted SMPD3 knockdown by AAV5 significantly reduced sEV concentrations in the CSF by 76.7%, compared to the controls (Fig. 4R). To further determine if sEV secretion suppression affects the secretome, we performed Smpd3 knockdown at an additional lower dose (Fig. 4S). Immunoblotting for TTR showed that, 2 weeks post AAV5 injection, AAV5-shRNA (Smpd3) dose-dependently diminished TTR levels in sEV fractions from CP conditioned medium compared to controls (Fig. 4T, U). Collectively, these data demonstrated that SMPD3 is critical for sEV secretion by the CPECs both in vitro and in vivo, providing a foundation to investigate how the CP modulates SVZ adult neurogenesis via sEVs.

Suppression of CP sEV secretion diminishes SVZ adult neurogenesis

To determine the impact of CP-secreted sEVs on SVZ adult neurogenesis, we quantified the proliferating NSPCs in SVZ one month post ICV injection of AAV5-shRNA (Smpd3) (Fig. 5A). Compared to controls with AAV5-shRNA(Ctrl), a suppressed sEV release by SMPD3 knockdown significantly decreased proliferating BrdU(+) NSPCs in SVZ by 21.3% (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5B, C). This observation provided direct evidence that CP-secreted sEVs sustain the proliferation of SVZ NSPCs.

Since SVZ NSPCs ultimately supply newborn neurons to the OB, we examined the OB newborn neuron density at short-term (90 days) and long-term (180 days) time points following CP-selective sEV suppression (Fig. 5D, G). In both experiments, BrdU pulse labeling was performed one month prior to tissue dissection. In the short-term study, suppression of sEV secretion by shRNA (Smpd3) significantly reduced BrdU(+)/NeuN(+) newborn neurons in GCL by 42.2% compared to the controls (Fig. 5E, F). Newborn neurons in GL, however, were not significantly affected (Fig. 5E, F). The long-term study yielded a comparable outcome: prolonged suppression of sEV secretion by CP further diminished newborn neurons in OB by 62.0% in GCL but not in the GL (Fig. 5H, I). These observations further support a critical role of CP-derived sEVs in maintaining the SVZ adult neurogenesis.

As precursors for newborn neurons, DCX(+) immature neurons are a marker of ongoing neurogenesis [56] and also an indicator of the synaptic plasticity of local neuronal networks [57, 58]. Hence, we quantified DCX (+) cells in the OB 180 days post the CP-targeted sEV modulation. Consistent with the diminished BrdU(+)/NeuN(+) cells in the OB, the density of DCX(+) cells was significantly reduced by 81.1% in shRNA(Smpd3)-treated animals (Fig. 5J, K). Moreover, the morphology of OB DCX (+) cells was altered: unlike the typical migratory phenotype with elongated leading and trailing processes seen in controls, DCX(+) cells in shRNA(Smpd3)-treated animals displayed shortened cellular processes (Fig. 5L). These findings further indicate that altered CP sEVs affected the composition of the OB neuronal network.

CP-selective sEV secretion suppression compromised olfaction

The primary function of the OB is to relay olfactory signals from the olfactory epithelium to specific cortical areas. To assess the olfactory function as modulated by CP sEV secretion, we used multiple olfactory testing protocols to assess animals’ olfaction [43, 44] (Fig. 6A), i.e., the buried food test (Fig. 6C), the olfactory preference test (Fig. 6F), and the olfactory avoidance test (Fig. 6I), following short-and long-term CP-targeted sEV suppression.

First, in tests conducted 3 months post-ICV injections, mice with AAV5-shRNA (Smpd3) spent an average of 66.0 ± 19.2 sec in finding the hidden pellet, which was 47.3% longer than animals in the control group (44.8 ± 18.7 sec) (Fig. 6D). This difference increased to 180.6% when the test was conducted at 6 months post ICV injections (Fig. 6E). Data from the pellet-finding test demonstrate that a normal CP sEV secretion is essential for olfaction-guided foraging.

Second, we conducted the olfactory preference test in male mice using female mouse urine, measuring male mouse’s investigation time for this preferred attractant odorant (Fig. 6B) in a dishabituation session (Fig. 6F) [44]. At 3 months following the AAV5 injections, male mice in the AAV5-shRNA (Smpd3) group investigated the female urine (and staying with it) for 8.8 ± 2.1 sec, which was 27.2% less than the controls (12.1 ± 2.2 sec) (Fig. 6G). This decrease in investigation time reoccurred 6 months post the AAV5 injections, with an even greater decrease by 61.0% in AAV5-shRNA (Smpd3)-treated animals compared to controls (Fig. 6H). This suggests that CP-secreted sEVs were critical for maintaining olfaction-based social behaviors.

Third, we tested animals’ avoidance behavior by introducing 2-MB acid (a proven olfactory repellent, also supported by Fig. 6B) and quantifying their avoidance time for the 2-MB acid compartment relative to the diH2O control compartment (Fig. 6I). At 3 months post AAV5 injections, control mice in AAV5-shRNA (Ctrl) group showed greater avoidance of the 2-MB acid compartment (28.4 ± 9.2 sec relative to water) compared to the AAV5-shRNA(Smpd3) group (17.5 ± 11.0 sec relative to water) (Fig. 6J). This pattern persisted at the 6-month time point: AAV5-shRNA (Smpd3)-treated mice were less likely to avoid the 2-MB acid space (22.2 ± 8.7 sec) than controls (35.5 ± 6.6 sec) (Fig. 6K). Thus, CP sEV secretion also contributes to olfaction-mediated avoidance behaviors. Taken together, these neurobehavioral experiments support our hypothesis that diminished newborn neuron supply to the OB, due to suppressed sEV secretion by the CP, impairs olfaction.

Finally, considering the cellular heterogeneity of OB and contributions of various OB cell types to olfaction [59], we characterized the microglia, astrocytes, and oligodendrocyte lineage cells following CP-selective sEV secretion (Fig. S5). Double-staining of Iba-1 and CD68 showed that neither total (Iba-1+) nor activated (Iba-1+-CD68+) microglia were significantly altered (Fig. S5A, B). Similarly, astrocytes in the OB were unaffected, as the density of S100β+-GFAP+ cells did not differ between groups (Fig. S5C, D). Given the importance of oligodendroglia cells in maintaining OB network, we analyzed the myelination status using myelin basic protein (MBP) staining with pan-oligodendroglia marker Olig2 and oligodendrocyte progenitor cell (OPCs) marker NG2 (Fig. S5E). MBP fluorescence intensity in the OB was unchanged (Fig. S5F). The Olig2(+) cell density was also unaltered (Fig. S5G). Moreover, we observed no significant change in NG2(+) OPCs (Fig. S5H); yet the OPC distribution seemed to shift toward the OB core of in the AAV5-shRNA(Smpd3) group, compared to a GCL-dominant presence in controls. Overall, a lack of major alteration of OB glial cell populations supports our hypothesis that the diminished SVZ adult neurogenesis due to suppressed CP sEV secretion is a primary driver of olfactory dysfunction.

Discussion

The data presented in this assessment provide direct evidence to support the existence of a CP-SVZ regulatory (CSR) axis, in which the CP regulates the SVZ adult neurogenesis through its production and secretion of sEVs. Further, the proposed CSR axis is significantly involved in maintaining normal olfactory function. The concept of the CSR axis is supported by several lines of evidence in this study. First, sEVs were identified to be the critical fraction in the CP secretome stimulating SVZ neurogenesis both in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, impaired newborn neuron supply to the OB following ICV infusion of sEVs collected from the CP of Mn-exposed animals further supports this concept. Second, by in vitro CPEC Smpd3 siRNA knockdown and in vivo CP-selective SMPD3 knockdown with AAV5-shRNA(Smpd3), we validated the SMPD3-dependent CP regulation of the CSF sEV concentration. Third, selective suppression of CP sEV secretion by SMPD3 knockdown progressively diminished adult neurogenesis in the SVZ-RMS-OB system. Finally, neurobehavioral tests demonstrated that suppressed CP secretion of sEVs ultimately led to impaired olfactory function. Collectively, our findings highlight the importance of the CP, through sEV secretion, in modulating the adult SVZ neurogenesis process, thereby influencing the OB/olfaction system.

The CP-associated secretome contains health- and growth-promoting factors that are critical to the well-being of developing and adult brains. Literature indicates substances released by the young CP are capable of “rejuvenating” old brains by upregulating brain plasticity [6, 60]. Our data are in agreement with these reports and further demonstrate that the sEVs secreted by the CP may mediate these beneficial effects. Interestingly, many “nourishing” molecules in the CP, such as Klotho [6162], microRNA-204 [52], SOD3 [8, 63], sonic hedgehog protein (SHH) [64, 65], likely exert their biological functions using sEVs as the dominant carrier. The CP’s rich blood supply makes it sensitive to systemic biological or chemical changes in the body; hence, it is possible the CP produces and utilizes sEVs as a primary mechanism to communicate with cells within the SVZ. This CP-SVZ signaling is promoted via the pinwheel architecture of the SVZ on the ventricular surface, which can directly contact the CSF [66]. The “antenna” or a subcellular structure known as the primary cilium [27] in SVZ NSPCs facilitates sEV uptake for subsequent signal reception/transduction [67, 68]. The exact interactions between CP-derived sEVs and cellular components of the SVZ remains unknown and requires extensive future investigation. However, the CP’s robust sEV secretion and the SVZ’s high sensitivity to changes in the CSF appear to collectively underpin the physiological existence of the CSR axis. Understandably, other mechanisms may also exist; for example, a recent transgenic mouse model with heterozygous SMPD3 knockout showed no alterations in adult CSF exosome concentration [69]. This suggests that SMPD3 expression in the adult CP is non-compensatory [evidenced by our observations from AAV5-shRNA (Smpd3) in Figs. 5, 6], unlike the scenario following an early developmental disruption. Orthodenticle homeobox 2 (OTX2), a critical neuronal lineage transcription factor enriched in the CP, likely enters the CSF directly as a soluble protein [5, 70]. Copper (Cu) in the CSF, regulated by the transporters in the CP, can also modulate SVZ adult neurogenesis not necessarily via CP-derived sEVs (Liu et al., 2022).

Clinical implications of the CSR axis remain to be elucidated. Significant, however, is the linkage of the proposed CSR axis to olfaction. Olfactory disorders are highly prevalent, early-onset symptoms in several neurodegenerative disorders, particularly Alzheimer’s disease [71–73] and Parkinson’s disease (PD) (both idiopathic and Mn-induced) [74, 75]. Disturbed olfaction is also one of the prominent leading symptoms associated with post-COVID conditions [76, 77]. Interestingly, severely disrupted function of the CP in humans has been associated with pathological progressions in AD, frontotemporal dementia, Huntington’s disease, and severe COVID-19 [78, 10, 79, 80]. Thus, our proposed CSR axis may provide a useful explanation to connect the CP’s sensitivity to changes in the circulation’s chemical environment to olfactory dysfunction. It is possible the CP may directly communicate with the OB through secreted CSF, which exchanges freely with the interstitial fluid between neurons and glial cells in brain structures [2, 81]. Alternatively, the CP may influence olfaction through the immediately adjacent SVZ NSPCs as proposed in this study. Noticeably, there was a debate in literature regarding the importance of the SVZ adult neurogenesis in human olfaction [82, 83]. Our findings, however, provide useful evidence to support the sEV-dependent CSR axis in modulating the olfactory sensation. Given the shared cellular compositions between adult human olfactory epithelium [84] and the SVZ, local application of CP-sEVs to the olfactory epithelium may also possess therapeutic potential.

The concept of the CSR axis has a significant implication in toxicology. The ability of the CP to effectively sequester toxicants, owing to its interactions with blood-borne extrinsic materials, renders the tissue vulnerable to chemical-induced injury not solely on its structure but also on its function [7, 14, 16, 85, 86]. The data presented in this study support that exposure to Mn leads to extensive accumulation of Mn in the CP, which in turn alters the signals carried by sEVs from the CP. At present, the evidence to support toxicant-induced dysfunction of sEV secretion by the CP with ensuing impaired olfaction remains scarce. Nevertheless, the CSR axis may become a new target for toxicological understanding of chemical-induced brain injury. In addition, the CSR axis may provide new mechanisms and new targets for pharmacological intervention to address multiple neurological diseases. Multiple clinical trials have shown promising therapeutic outcomes of sEV-based interventions [87–91]. Factors secreted by the youthful CP appear to improve brain function [6, 60, 92]; hence, health-promoting factors secreted by the CP derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) could be harnessed for future trials [93–95].

In conclusion, the data presented in this study provide first-hand evidence to support the existence of the Choroid plexus-Subventricular zone Regulatory (CSR) axis, which, through the yet-to-be-identified molecules carried by CP-secreted sEVs, regulates adult neurogenesis in the SVZ and ultimately modulates the olfactory function. Mn-caused anosmia/hyposmia may be due at least partly to its adverse effects on the CSR axis. Further molecular characterization of the CSR axis will have significant implications in neurobiological, neurodevelopmental, neuropharmacological and neurotoxicological research and application.

Limitations of the study

This report lacks cargo content analyses of CP-secreted sEVs beyond TTR. Analysis of CP-secreted sEV cargo shall further elucidate the mechanism by which Mn-sEVs differentially modulate neurogenesis to the OB, and in the meantime, help identify SMPD3-dependent secreted cargo. In addition, this study used male animals only to assess the olfaction as mediated by CP-secreted sEVs, taking into account the susceptibility of the males to olfactory disorders and the confounding influence of the female estrous cycle on SVZ activity. Future studies should include female mice to investigate potential sex differences. Ongoing and future studies will further elucidate this mechanism by examining specific sEV cargo components influencing neurogenesis, sEV targeting systems, and disease-/sex-mediated variations to establish clinical targets. Overall, the current study establishes foundational knowledge supporting the CSR axis and the regulatory contribution of CP-derived sEVs.
