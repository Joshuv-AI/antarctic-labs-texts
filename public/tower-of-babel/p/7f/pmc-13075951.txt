The Emergence of a Fatal Rash in a Renal Transplant Patient
Authors: Rebecca Unterborn, Thomas B Campbell, James Cooper, Shyam Raghavan, Edward Stenehjem, Chia-Yu Chiu
Published: 2026

QUESTION

A 63-year-old man with hypertensive nephropathy underwent kidney transplantation in 2021. Approximately 2 years posttransplantation, he presented with 1 week of dyspnea, dry cough, and rapidly progressive skin papules. His immunosuppressive regimen before admission included tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisone 5 mg daily. He was never diagnosed with or treated for rejection. He had no other relevant past medical or surgical history and no recent international travel; his only domestic travel was to Minnesota about 1 month prior.

Upon admission, he was afebrile and initially hemodynamically stable. Physical exam was notable for papular skin lesions involving the face, trunk, and extremities (Figure 1A and 1B). Laboratory studies demonstrated white blood cell count of 2.1 × 109/L with predominant lymphopenia (absolute lymphocyte count nadir of 0.3 × 109/L), hemoglobin 10.9 g/dL, platelets 26 × 109/L, and creatinine 5.72 mg/dL (baseline 2.5 mg/dL). Notably, his tacrolimus level was >30 ng/mL (unquantifiable). A urinalysis was collected and demonstrated proteinuria but no hematuria or pyuria. Blood cytomegalovirus, herpes simplex virus, and varicella zoster virus were undetectable by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Blood Epstein–Barr virus PCR was <1000 IU/mL. Chest X-ray and computed tomography scan of the chest showed a 2.2-cm solitary pulmonary nodule in the right upper lobe (Figure 1C and 1D), and abdominal ultrasound demonstrated hepatosplenomegaly. Biopsy of the pulmonary nodule was not feasible due to thrombocytopenia. Serum β-D-glucan, Blastomyces antibodies, and Coccidioides antibody panel were negative. Aspergillus galactomannan was positive (index 7.52). Histoplasma urine and serum antigens were positive at 4.62 ng/mL and 1.88 ng/mL, respectively. Standard bacterial blood cultures were negative. Fungal blood cultures were obtained and a skin biopsy was performed for pathology and cultures.

What is the diagnosis?

ANSWER: DISSEMINATED EMERGOMYCES CANADENSIS IN A KIDNEY TRANSPLANT RECIPIENT

Histopathological examination of the skin revealed abundant yeast forms morphologically resembling Histoplasma capsulatum (Figure 2A and 2B). Voriconazole was started on hospital day 2, and liposomal amphotericin was added on hospital day 4 for presumed disseminated histoplasmosis. He subsequently developed respiratory and renal failure, as well as encephalopathy and worsening shock requiring intubation and transfer to the intensive care unit on hospital day 6. His family elected to transition to comfort-focused care, and the patient passed away on hospital day 8. One week following collection, his skin biopsy culture grew mold and 2 weeks postcollection, his fungal blood culture grew mold. Postmortem cultures from the skin biopsy and fungal blood samples were positive for Emergomyces canadensis, identified by DNA sequencing. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was not performed in this case. This is a case of Emergomyces in a renal transplant recipient that was initially misdiagnosed as disseminated histoplasmosis. This is only the second documented clinical case of E canadensis in a transplant recipient in North America.

Emergomyces is a dimorphic fungus that exists in the soil in a mold phase, and its conidia can be aerosolized and inhaled to cause pulmonary or cutaneous infection in humans [1]. It was first described in the 1990s as causing widespread cutaneous lesions in a person with advanced human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [1]. It was initially classified as an Emmonsia species, but in 2013, a case of disseminated mycosis was reported in a person with HIV in South Africa, which led to reexamining this organism and the creation of a new genus: Emergomyces [1, 2]. There are 5 known Emergomyces species reported globally as causes of clinical disease in humans, each with its own geographic distribution [1]. In North America, E canadensis is the most common species and has been reported to cause disseminated disease mostly in immunocompromised hosts [1, 3]. Though cases are rare, the mortality rate is up to 50% [2, 3].

Prompt identification of emergomycosis is particularly challenging due to cross-reactivity with other fungal tests, its morphologic similarity to other fungi under microscopy, and its frequent absence from the clinician's differential diagnosis. More than 75% of emergomycosis cases have been misdiagnosed as other dimorphic fungal infections or tuberculosis, as it frequently presents as disseminated disease, including pulmonary and cutaneous manifestations [2].

Cross-reaction with Histoplasma antigens, β-D-glucan, and Aspergillus galactomannan have been reported [1, 4, 5]. Though Histoplasma urine antigens have reported sensitivities and specificities around 90% for detecting disseminated histoplasmosis in people with HIV [6], cross-reactivity of this assay with other dimorphic fungi is common and one study in South Africa noted cross-reactivity with the Histoplasma antigen in about 20% of culture-confirmed invasive Emergomyces africanus cases [5]. Aspergillus galactomannan has been known to cross-react with dimorphic fungi as well, such as Histoplasma, Coccidioides, and Emergomyces [6, 7]. Sequencing of DNA barcoding regions (ITS and D1/D2) accurately identifies E canadensis, distinguishing it from Emmonsia-like species and Blastomyces dermatitidis [3]. This supports the notion that diagnostic challenges likely lead to underdiagnosis, and therefore disease characteristics may not be fully understood.

Literature suggests that amphotericin may reduce mortality, and European/international guidance recommends liposomal amphotericin for disseminated emergomycosis [2, 8]. No Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute or European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing standard breakpoints have been established for antifungal agents against Emergomyces spp. Reported minimum inhibitory concentrations range from <0.016 to 0.25 μg/mL for amphotericin B and itraconazole, >64 μg/mL for fluconazole, 0.063 to 0.25 μg/mL for voriconazole, and <0.016 to 0.125 μg/mL for posaconazole [2–4, 9]. For patients who are stabilized on amphotericin, some experts recommend 12 months of itraconazole step-down therapy and potentially indefinite itraconazole if immunosuppression cannot be reduced [1, 2].

To date, including our case, a total of 8 cases of emergomycosis have been reported in North America (3 cases in Canada and 5 cases within the United States; 7 cases of E canadensis and 1 case of Emergomyces pastuerianus) (Table 1). The reason for clustering of E canadensis cases in the Mountain West region of the United States remains unclear, as most epidemiological studies have focused on E africanus, the species more commonly found in Africa [1]. Considering that E africanus DNA was extracted from 30% of soil samples, including diverse soil types, and 10% of air samples in South Africa [1], it is possible that E canadensis may thrive in the dry high-desert soils of the mountain states (4 of 8 cases summarized in Table 1) and can be easily aerosolized within this dusty climate. More research is needed to better understand the geographic distribution and to try to improve rapid diagnostics for E canadensis. With increasing prevalence of transplantation and expanded use of immunosuppressive medications, emergomycosis should be considered in the differential for disseminated fungal infection in an immunosuppressed host.
