Changing patterns of colorectal cancer in China between 2000 and 2016 based on tertiary hospital data
Authors: Junling Wu, Hanwen Zhang, Zhe Luan, Jun Chen, Zhizhuang Zhao, Yiming Zhao, Congyong Li, Wei Zhang, Yi Chen, Jiaqi Zhang, Jiwei Zhao, Shuhuang Peng, Hanfei Liu, Jing Wang, Shufang Wang, Gang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer ranks fourth globally in cancer-related deaths, causing about nine million annual fatalities. Developed countries show higher mortality rates due to factors like aging, diet, obesity, inactivity, and tobacco use. While colonoscopy initiatives reduce incidence, early-onset cases rise. China faces a significant colorectal cancer burden. This study examines post-2000 colorectal cancer trends in Chinese tertiary hospitals to guide future priorities.Materials and methodsThis study, spanning 2000–2016, gathered colorectal cancer data from five Chinese tertiary hospitals. Patient details, including age, gender, colonoscopy findings, and pathology reports, were anonymized with unique serial numbers to protect privacy.ResultsThe overall incidence rate in females is approximately 2.9%, which is lower than the 3.2% in males (p < 0.05). Female patients have a lower average age compared to males (59.7 vs. 61.5, p < 0.01). Additionally, after 2008, there is a significant increase in the average age of patients compared to before 2008 (p < 0.001), and the proportion of female patients has significantly risen (p < 0.05). The preferred location for colon cancer has shifted from the distal end to the proximal end, and there is also an increase in the malignancy level.ConclusionFrom the perspective of tertiary hospitals, the pattern of colorectal cancer in China has undergone significant changes. The focus of diagnosis and treatment in tertiary hospitals in China should gradually shift toward the elderly patients, which helps address healthcare needs and is of significant importance for resource allocation.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer stands as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, resulting in approximately nine million deaths annually [1]. Regional variations in mortality rates are evident, with developed countries experiencing markedly higher rates than their developing counterparts. This discrepancy is primarily attributed to factors such as aging populations, dietary patterns, obesity, sedentary lifestyles, and tobacco use, all contributing to an elevated risk of colorectal cancer. Notably, the implementation of nationwide colonoscopy screening initiatives in developed nations has led to a gradual decline in both incidence and mortality rates [2]. In contrast, the proportion of early-onset colorectal cancer is increasing, particularly in the distal colon and rectum [3]. In February 2022, the National Cancer Center of China released cancer statistics up to 2016, revealing that colorectal cancer has become the second most prevalent cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths, demonstrating a continuous upward trajectory. Irrespective of gender, both the incidence and mortality rates for colorectal cancer are increasing, with a more pronounced trend observed in males [4]. China grapples with a substantial burden of colorectal cancer.

This study focuses on tertiary hospitals in China, exploring the characteristics of colorectal cancer patients in these hospitals after the year 2000, aiming to provide insights for future diagnosis and treatment priorities in tertiary hospitals.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by The Ethics committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (S2022-110-01).

This study collected data from five tertiary hospitals in different regions of China, covering the period from 2000 to 2016 and focusing on patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer. The data included information on patients’ age, gender, colonoscopy findings, endoscopic diagnosis, and pathology reports. In the process of data extraction, measures were taken to safeguard patient privacy by removing identifiable information. Each patient was then assigned a randomly generated and unique serial number serving as an identification code. Incomplete patient data were also excluded. The location of cancer was extrapolated from colonoscopy findings, and the degree of cell differentiation in patients with adenocarcinoma was derived from pathology reports. The dataset was stratified into two temporal segments based on the examination period: 2000–2008 and 2009–2016. Cancer locations were classified into rectum and colon, with the colon further delineated into proximal colon and distal colon. The proximal colon encompassed the cecum/ascending colon and transverse colon, while the distal colon included the descending colon and sigmoid colon.

The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer through colonoscopy and pathology; (2) Age 18 years or older; (3) Availability of complete clinical records, including patient age, sex, colonoscopy findings, endoscopic diagnosis, and pathology reports.

The exclusion criteria as follows: (1) Patients who could not be diagnosed with colorectal cancer; (2) Patients younger than 18 years; (3) Patients lacking essential clinical information (such as colonoscopy results, pathology reports, etc.). For these cases, we opted to exclude the patients from the analysis rather than treating the missing values as NA; (4) Patients who have received treatment for other cancers or participated in other clinical trials.

Statistical analyses were conducted using the SPSS 26.0 program. Independent sample t-tests were employed to ascertain age differences, while other data underwent analysis using the χ2 test. Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05.

Results

Firstly, from an overall perspective, the detection rate of colorectal cancer has consistently hovered around 4%−5% since 2000. However, a discernible decline has been evident from 2012 to 2016 (Fig. 1). Examining the data through an age-centric lens reveals a positive correlation between age and the detection rate of colorectal cancer. A substantial uptick is discernible from the age of 40 onwards, with the age bracket spanning 50–70 identified as a high-incidence period, registering an almost 30% detection rate (Fig. 2).

The study included a total of 137,935 patients, with 80,844 males and 57,091 females. Among these, 4,240 patients were diagnosed with colorectal cancer, and 133,695 patients were diagnosed with conditions other than colorectal cancer. The number of female cancer patients accounts for 2.9% (1668) of the total female population, while the number of male cancer patients accounts for 3.2% (2572) of the total male population (Table 1). The results of the Pearson chi-square test show χ2 = 7.579, P = 0.006<0.05, indicating a difference between genders in susceptibility to colorectal cancer. The total number of male cancer patients is 2572, with an average age of 59.71, while the total number of female cancer patients is 1668, with an average age of 61.53. There is a significant difference in the age of onset between the two groups (independent sample t-test result with t-value of 4.384, P<0.001). The male-to-female ratio of affected individuals is 1.54:1.

The distribution of colorectal cancer also underwent changes between pre-2008 and post-2008. Rectal cancer had been the predominant type, but its proportion within colorectal cancer significantly decreased from 45.92% before 2008 to 41.37% after 2008 (P < 0.01). On the other hand, there were significant increases in the proportions of transverse colon (9.3% vs. 11.8%), and sigmoid colon cancers (18.5% vs. 21.8%). Simultaneously, there was a significant increase in the percentage of proximal colon cancer (26.4% vs. 30.3%) among colorectal cancer patients (P < 0.05). However, the data showed that the percentages of distal colon cancer were similar between the two groups (Table 3).

Discussion

The overall detection rate in China’s tertiary hospitals has exhibited a gradual decline, contrasting with the national overall detection rate, which has shown an upward trend [4]. This suggests that the majority of patients may not necessarily require comprehensive diagnosis and treatment in tertiary hospitals. Notably, endoscopic diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities in hospitals outside of tertiary settings, including primary care facilities, have witnessed significant improvement compared to the past [5–7]. This positive trend is linked to the widespread adoption of colonoscopy, advancements in colonoscopy technology, and the alleviation of pressure on healthcare resources [8].

The overall average age of patients seeking medical attention is notably high, with a discernible increase in the average age of patients after 2008 compared to the period before 2008. Moreover, there is a trend toward lower differentiation in cell morphology based on pathological findings. These indicate that the patients seeking medical care are predominantly elderly individuals, and there is a gradual increase in the malignancy level. Epidemiological data reveal that a majority of colorectal cancer patients are elderly, aged over 65 [9]. This demographic often presents with multiple underlying health conditions and compromised physical health. The complexity of their health conditions poses challenges for diagnosis and treatment, and surgery may not necessarily be a beneficial option [10, 11]. Currently, there is no standardized treatment protocol for such patients [12]. Therefore, the diagnosis and treatment of this population are more complex, and tertiary hospitals with higher medical levels may be a better choice for them.China is gradually entering an aging society. In 2000, the population aged 65 and above in China was 88.21 million, rising to 201 million in 2021, accounting for 14.20% of the total population [13], As the risk of colorectal cancer increases with age, the burden of colorectal cancer is expected to grow. It is projected that around 2030, colorectal cancer will become the most common gastrointestinal cancer in China [14]. Colorectal cancer has a relatively slow onset and development, allowing for a roughly ten-year golden screening window for standardized screening and treatment [15]. If colorectal cancer is detected early, the 5-year survival rate is approximately 90%. However, when the cancer progresses outside the colon or rectum, the survival rate is lower [16]. Therefore, it is essential to focus on colorectal cancer prevention, screening, diagnosis, and treatment in the elderly population.

Currently, there are five main types of screening methods (colonoscopy, fecal testing, colorectal cancer DNA screening kits, flexible sigmoidoscopy, and computed tomographic colonography). Colonoscopy, flexible sigmoidoscopy, and CT colonography belong to imaging examinations, capable of detecting lesions such as polyps. Fecal testing, especially fecal immunochemical testing (FIT) and fecal occult blood testing (FOBT), are more suitable for home screening [17], particularly for the elderly, circumventing the invasive damage associated with endoscopy and the radiation damage from CT imaging. However, their specificity and sensitivity fall notably short of those of endoscopy. Furthermore, there are numerous early monitoring biomarkers in genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, but their scalability poses a challenge [16], Globally, screening primarily relies on imaging and fecal monitoring. In the Chinese context and based on relevant domestic research, the screening value of techniques such as fecal DNA testing, colon capsule endoscopy, and plasma Septin9 methylation remains unclear and necessitates further investigation. Sigmoidoscopy and CT colonography, while holding certain value, are less prevalent in China or relatively costly, leading to their current non-recommendation. Consequently, colonoscopy is presently considered the most suitable screening method in China [18].

Before selecting a treatment plan for elderly patients, a comprehensive assessment of their physical condition is imperative to identify those who are suitable and likely to derive the greatest benefit from active treatment while maintaining an acceptable quality of life [19]. Currently, there is a relatively unanimous understanding that radiation therapy or combined radiochemotherapy is beneficial for patients unable to undergo surgery or refusing surgery due to health reasons [20, 21].

Nevertheless, several studies have identified a concerning trend of early-onset colorectal cancer, a phenomenon observed on a global scale [22, 23], Intriguingly, research findings have indicated that the majority of individuals with early-onset colorectal cancer lack a genetic predisposition. Consequently, factors such as obesity, antibiotic usage, low levels of physical activity, and dietary patterns are deemed substantial contributors to the development of early-onset colorectal cancer. Notably, these factors coincide with significant lifestyle and societal changes associated with economic prosperity [24], This emerging trend demands careful consideration and further investigation.

Among individuals seeking medical attention, females exhibit a higher average age than males (P < 0.001), yielding a male-to-female ratio of 1.54:1. This gender difference suggests a protective factor, consistent with research indicating that, on average, females have a 25% lower overall risk of developing colorectal cancer compared to males [1]. This discrepancy could be attributed to various factors such as higher red meat consumption, increased rates of alcohol consumption and smoking among males, and a greater likelihood of visceral fat deposition, all contributing to an elevated risk of colorectal cancer in males [25–27]. Furthermore, studies highlight the protective role of estrogen in females. The estrogen receptor ERβ, operating through the p53 signaling pathway, regulates DNA repair and apoptosis, inhibits oncogenes and metastasis [28].

Furthermore, there has been a shift in the predominant locations of colorectal cancer, marked by a reduction in the proportion of rectal cancer and a rise in the prevalence of colon cancer. Research suggests an increasing incidence of proximal colon cancer in both sexes [29], indicating a trend toward proximal migration. The right colon encompasses the cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, and transverse colon, while the left colon includes the splenic flexure, descending colon, and sigmoid colon. These two segments have distinct embryonic origins, with the right colon originating from the midgut and the left colon from the hindgut [30]. Additionally, differences in anatomical structures, such as blood supply and nerve distribution, exist between the left and right colon. These anatomical and embryonic distinctions contribute to unique characteristics between left-sided and right-sided colon cancers. Proximal colon cancer is often associated with genetic factors and may be triggered by mechanisms similar to those in hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer, while distal colon cancer is more influenced by acquired factors and may share mechanisms with polyp diseases [31]. Moreover, their tumor characteristics, molecular subtypes, and clinical symptoms differ, underscoring the necessity for distinct treatment strategies based on the location’s characteristics.

Conclusion

From the perspective of tertiary hospitals, the pattern of colorectal cancer in China has undergone significant changes, including aspects such as age, gender, location, and incidence rates. Due to the development of national healthcare, tertiary hospitals will increasingly need to handle patients who are older and more critically ill, the focus of diagnosis and treatment in tertiary hospitals in China should gradually shift toward the elderly patients, which helps address healthcare needs and is of significant importance for resource allocation.
