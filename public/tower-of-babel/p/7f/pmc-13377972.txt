LBH deficiency influences the therapeutic effects of regulatory B cells through the regulation of IL-10 production via the Rap1 pathway
Authors: Weijia Bao, Ke Hu, Yuan Qu, Yihong He, Xiaotong Zhu, Yuping Zhang, Yage Tian, Ligang Jie, Qinghong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegulatory B cells (Bregs) are immune cells that affect the autoimmune response by producing interleukin (IL)-10, but the molecular mechanism affecting Breg differentiation and IL-10 production is not clear. Our study found that mice with limb bud and limb-bud and heart (LBH) gene deficiency in dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis showed severe colitis but exhibited an increase in Bregs in mesenteric lymph nodes and the spleen. LBH deficiency increased IL-10 production and expression of the surface molecule CD24 in B cells. Mechanistically, Ras-proximate-1 (Rap1) signaling pathway activation in LBH-deleted B cells and simultaneous inhibition of Rap 1 activation or extracellular signal-related kinase (ERK) phosphorylation reduced Breg differentiation among B cells. Furthermore, LBH deficient B cells showed a more significant therapeutic effect on DSS-induced colitis. Taken together, the results of this study revealed the mechanism by which LBH affects IL-10 production during Breg differentiation and that LBH deficiency enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of B cells for DSS-induced colitis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01582-3.Keywords: Regulatory B cells, Limb-bud and heart, Interleukin-10, Ras-proximate-1 pathway, Inflammatory bowel disease

Introduction

B lymphocytes are critical in the pathogenesis and are often considered a driver of autoimmune diseases. In addition to pathogenic B cells, protective B cells that produce inflammatory inhibitors also play a key role in autoimmune diseases [1]. Regulatory B cells (Bregs) are immunosuppressive cells that support immune tolerance and inhibit the proliferation of pathogenic lymphocytes by producing cytokines such as IL-10 and IL-35, thus inhibiting immune damage [2]. Unlike regulatory T cells, Bregs are considered to originate from B cells at different stages of differentiation in an inflammatory environment. The most characterized population of Bregs is IL-10 producing Bregs. This population has no specific phenotype among B cell subsets, such as CD19+CD24hiCD38hi and CD19+CD24hiCD27int, and IL-10 production can be detected in B cell subsets such as CD5+CD1dhi, CD23hiCD24hi, and CD19+CD21hi in mice [3]. Although significant progress has been made on Bregs in the past decade, Bregs may differentiate and form through signaling pathways [4–6] and epigenetic regulation [7], and the molecular mechanism regulating IL-10 production remains to be explored.

The limb-bud and heart (LBH) gene is a highly conserved transcriptional cofactor in vertebrates and is expressed mainly in embryonic tissues and some adult tissues, including the intestine, spleen, kidneys, brain, and peripheral nervous system [8]. LBH has multiple functions in embryogenesis, and in addition to its involvement in the development of the limbs and heart, LBH can regulate angiogenesis [9] during fetal bone development through the VEGF signaling pathway. In recent years, several studies have shown that LBH is involved in the tumorigenesis of a variety of cancers [10–12] and may be involved in the development of autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [13–14]. The expression of LBH mRNA in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of SLE patients compared with that in healthy controls suggests that if may be involved in SLE pathogenesis [14]. However, how LBH participates in the occurrence and development of autoimmune diseases by regulating lymphocyte differentiation and function remains unclear.

Regarding inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), immune abnormalities present in the major effector cells of adaptive immunity, including T, NK, and B cells, induce invasive intestinal immune responses in genetically susceptible hosts [15]. Significant advances have been made in the medical treatment of this disease, but many patients become refractory to the available therapeutic options. Despite the remarkable effectiveness of biological and small molecule therapies in the treatment of IBD, there is still a substantial unmet need to control intestinal inflammation. In recent years, cellular immunotherapy has received increasing attention. It also shows a good safety profile and may trigger anti-inflammatory pathways to promote lasting resolution of inflammation, which may add to the limited current IBD treatments [16]. Some studies have confirmed that intraperitoneal injection of Bregs that have undergone in vitro-induced differentiation relieves the inflammatory damage of IBD [17–18].

In this study, we explored the influence of the LBH gene on IBD pathogenesis and found that LBH could affect Breg differentiation. Thus, we investigated the phenotypic changes and molecular mechanisms of LBH gene-induced differentiation of Bregs and verified the differential effect of the LBH gene in regulating differentiated B cells in the cellular immunotherapy of IBD.

Materials and methods

Animals

Animals

WT C57BL/6J mice (male, 8–12 weeks old) were obtained from Zhuhai BesTest Biological Technology Company, and LBH−/− mice were established by Cyagen Biosciences. All mice were housed in a sterile barrier system environment, and age-matched mice were used in all related experiments. The protocols were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee at Southern Medical University Zhujiang Hospital (No. LAEC-2020-238). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

2.Colitis induction and evaluation

Colitis induction and evaluation

Acute colitis was induced by administering 3% DSS (Meilunbio, China). Body weight was recorded daily. On day 7, the DSS solution was replaced with distilled water, and the mice were euthanized on day 8. Disease activity scores (DAI Score) were evaluated as previously described [19]. Colons were collected, measured for length, and evaluated for histological damage. Colon tissue was fixed in 4% buffered formaldehyde (Servicebio, China) and stained with H&E for histological analysis. The histological analysis included evaluating the extent of injury, regenerative capacity, crypt lesions, and percentage area of damaged tissue [20]. The severity of colitis was assessed according to the DAI score, colon length and histological damage.

We obtained spleen and mesenteric lymphnodes from WT and LBH−/− colitis mice and homogenized through a 40-µm cell strainer. Lymphocytes were separated by Mouse 1×Lymphocyte Separation Medium (DAKEWEI,7211012) density gradient centrifugation and used for flow cytometry.

3.B cell purification, culture, and stimulation in vitro

B cell purification, culture, and stimulation in vitro

We obtained splenocytes from both WT and LBH−/− mice in a sterile environment. B cells were isolated using a MojoSort Mouse Pan B Cell Isolation Kit II (Biolegend, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity of the isolated CD19 + B cells was confirmed to be > 95% using flow cytometry. The purified B cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, C11875500BT) supplemented with 10% FBS (Biological Industries, 04-001-1ACS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone, SV3001), and 2 mM glutamine (Gibco, 25030081), and treated with or without HJC0197 (GLPBIO, GC17072) or U0126 (MCE, HY-12031 A) at different concentrations in the presence of 3 µg/mL LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, L2880) for 24, 48, or 72 h.

4.Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry

For Breg detection, from spleen and mesenteric lymph nodes, lymphocytes were fixed and permeabilized (PMG Cytofix/Cytoperm Solution Kit, BD) after surface staining with anti-mouse CD19 FITC(6D5,BioLegend), followed by intracellularly staining with anti-mouse IL-10 PE(JES5-16E3, BioLegend). In order to detect Breg in vitro, cells were harvested in wash buffer (PBS,0.1% BSA), and incubated with Zombie NIR™ (BioLegend, USA) for 30 min in the dark, and IL-10 labeling was performed after fixation permeabilization. For surface staining, B cells were stained for 30 min with a combination of the following antibodies: anti-mouse CD1d APC (19G11, Elabscience), anti-mouse CD5 FITC(53 − 7.3, Elabscience), anti-mouse CD21 Brilliant Violet 421™ (7E9, BioLegend), anti-mouse CD23 PE/Cy7 (B3B4, Elabscience), anti-mouse CD24 APC (M1/69, Elabscience), anti-mouse/rat/human CD27 PE (LG.3A10, BioLegend), anti-mouse CD38 FITC (NIMR5, Elabscience), anti-mouse CD43 FITC (S11, BioLegend), anti-mouse IgD PerCP/Cy5.5 (11-26c.2a, Elabscience), and anti-mouse IgM PerCP/Cy5.5 (RMM-1, Elabscience). Images were acquired on a CytoFlex (Beckman Coulter, USA) or a FACSVerse(BD Bioscience, USA), and analysis was performed using FlowJo software (version X).

5.Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

After 24, 48, and 72 h of culture, supernatants from each group were collected and IL-10 levels were detected using a ELISA MAX™ Deluxe Set Mouse IL-10 (BioLegend, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance of each sample was measured at 450 nm using a Synergy H1 Microplate Reader (Bio-Tek Instruments, USA).

6.RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

RNA-seq data of blood from HCs and IBD patients was downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under the accession number, GSE126124 [21]. Ten, we compared LBH expression changes between 39 HCs and 59 IBD patients. Differentially expressed genes were identifed with adjusted P values < 0.05 and Log2FoldChange absolute values > 2.

B cells from WT or LBH−/− mice were stimulated with 3 µg/mL LPS for 48 h and collected. RNA was extracted using the RNAex Pro Reagent (Accurate Bio, AG21101). The RNA libraries were sequenced on an Illumina sequencing platform by Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). Genes were assembled using StringTie software and quantified by fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads. Genes that were differentially expressed between samples were analyzed using the R package edge. Differential genes were defined using a standard of a fold change > 1.5 and a false discovery rate < 0.05, and these genes underwent Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis.

7.Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

B cells from WT or LBH−/− mice were stimulated with 3 µg/mL LPS for 48–72 h and collected. RNA was extracted using the RNA CeLL Kit (BIOG,51021). Complementary DNA was synthesized with a reverse transcript kit (Novoprotein, E047). The protocol for SYBR Green (Novoprotein, E096) based qPCR was set at an initial denaturation of 30 s, 95℃, followed by 40 cycles of 5 s denaturation at 95℃ and 30 s annealing/extension at 60℃. All the target genes were relative to β-actin. All of the sequences for the primers used in the study are summarized in Supplemental Table S1.

8.Western blotting

Western blotting

B cells were activated using 3 µg/mL LPS, and lysed in RIPA buffer(Beyotime Biotech, China) with protease inhibitor cocktail (MCE, USA), phosphatase inhibitor (Beyotime Biotech, China) and PMSF (Beyotime Biotech, China). Equal amounts of proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE (Seven, Beijing, China) and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Following blocking in PBS supplemented with 5% nonfat dry milk for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight with RAP1A+1B (#ab187659, Abcam), EPAC1(#12572-1-AP, Proteintech), ERK(#4695T, Cell Signal Technology), p-ERK(#4307T, Cell Signal Technology) and β-Actin (#BS6007M, Bioworld). After washing in TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (#511203, Zenbio or #BS12478, Bioworld) for 1 h. Visualize labeled proteins using enhanced chemiluminescent fluid (ECL, NCM Biotech, China).The grey value of each protein band was quantified using ImageJ software.

9.Adoptive transfer of B cells

Adoptive transfer of B cells

Splenic B cells isolated from WT or LBH−/− mice were activated with LPS for 48 h. To explore the effect of LBH on B cell treatment in vivo, 1 × 106 active WT or LBH−/− B cells were intraperitoneally injected into WT mice on the second day after DSS-based induction. The mice were weighed daily, sacrificed on day 8, and evaluated for intestinal inflammation, which was determined by the DAI score, colon length, and histological damage.

10.Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 using unpaired t-tests and two-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 and represented as ns, no significance, *p < 0.05, **p < 0 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results

LBH deletion exacerbates DSS-induced colitis in mice

LBH deletion exacerbates DSS-induced colitis in mice

We compared the genetic differences in the blood of healthy controls (HCs) and IBD patients in the GEO database (No.GSE126124), and found that LBH expression was significantly reduced in IBD patients (Supplemental Fig S1), indicating that LBH may be involved in the development of IBD.

To verify the effect of LBH on colitis, we generated DSS-induced colitis models using Wild-type (WT) and LBH-knockout (LBH−/−) mice (n = 6/group). Two LBH−/−model mice died, on day 5 and day 7, with no death occurring in the other groups (Fig. 1B). The WT and LBH−/− model groups showed both colonic shortening and typical pathological changes of colitis compared with those in the control group without DSS induction. Furthermore, the LBH−/−model group exhibited greater disease activity, more weight loss, and a shorter colon at evaluation and experienced more severe pathological changes of colitis, including the destruction of the entire epithelium, disappearance of crypts, and severe inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 1C–H), all of which indicated that the deletion of LBH caused more severe colitis lesions.

LBH deletion exacerbates DSS-induced colitis in mice. Colotis model in WT or LBH−/− mice induced by DSS. A Experimental design (n = 6/group). B Survival curve. C Representative hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) staining of the colon from each group. Images were taken using 60× and 200× magnification. WT Control, LBH-/- Control, WT Model group (n = 6/group). Death in the course of DSS induced colitis in LBH-/- Model group at evaluation (n = 4/group). D Examined mice body weight daily and calculated as a percentage of initial body weight. E Representative plots of the colon of each group after sacrifice. F Compared the DAI score at evaluation in each group. G Colon length was compared among four groups of mice. H Histological score assessment of colon tissue. Statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ns, no significance, *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001)

2.LBH deletion promotes IL-10 production in B cells

LBH deletion promotes IL-10 production in B cells

To explore the differences in IL-10 production by B cells in colitis mice after LBH deletion, we extracted lymphocytes from mesenteric lymph nodes and spleens from WT and LBH−/− model mice during evaluations. Detection of Bregs among lymphocytes showed that the proportion of Bregs among B cells was increased in both mesenteric lymph nodes and the spleen in the LBH−/− model group, and the mean fluorescence intensity was significantly increased (Fig. 2A–F).

LBH deletion promote IL-10 production in B cells. Lymphocytes were extracted from mesenteric lymph nodes and spleen of WT model and LBH−/− model mice, A-F comparing the proportion of Bregs in B cells and the MFI of IL-10 in B cells. Spleen B cells from WT and LBH−/− mice were purified and cultured with LPS (3ug/mL) stimulation, and G, I collected at 0 h, 24 h, 48 h and 72 h to compare the proportion of Breg in B cells. H The RNA extracted at 72 h was used to detect gene expression levels. J Supernatants were harvested after 24 h, 48 h and 72 h. The IL-10 concentration in each supernatant was then determined using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ns, no significance, **p < 0.01;**p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001)

To confirm the effect of the LBH gene on IL-10 production in B cells, we purified splenic B cells from WT and LBH−/− mice and collected B cells at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h of lipopolysaccharide(LPS) stimulation to detect Bregs. The proportion of LBH−/− B cells was significantly increased at 48 and 72 h. IL-10 gene expression in LBH−/−B cells increased significantly at 72 h. Supernatants harvested at 24, 48, and 72 h showed the same trend as the Breg ratio, with significant increases in the IL-10 concentration in LBH−/−B cells (Fig. 2G–I), demonstrating that LBH deletion promoted IL-10 production in B cells.

3.LBH−/− B cells promote expression of the surface molecule CD24 after activation

LBH−/− B cells promote expression of the surface molecule CD24 after activation

The above studies confirmed that LBH deficiency can promote Breg differentiation, Bregs can be produced in various processes of B cell differentiation, and the phenotype is complex. To investigate the effect of LBH on the B cell phenotype after activation, we examined surface molecule expression, including CD1d, CD5, CD21, CD23, CD24, CD27, CD38, CD43, IgD, and IgM, in Bregs [17, 23–25]. We found that in Bregs, expression of the surface molecule CD24 was increased after activation of LBH−/− B cells compared with that in WT B cells (Fig. 3A), and the proportion of CD24-positive B cells was statistically significant (Fig. 3B–C).

4.LBH regulates the Ras-proximate-1 (Rap1) pathway in activated B cells

LBH regulates the Ras-proximate-1 (Rap1) pathway in activated B cells

To determine how LBH regulates Breg differentiation among B cells, we extracted RNA from WT and LBH−/− B cells stimulated with LPS for 48 h and assessed and compared their transcriptome profiles using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis revealed a significant difference in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and Rap1 signaling pathways between activated WT B cells and LBH−/− B cells (Fig. 4A). The Rap1-MAPK pathway regulates cell proliferation and activity [26], and IL-10 production by Bregs is regulated by the extracellular signal-related kinase (ERK) pathway [27]. Comparison of genes involved in Rap 1 signaling between activated WT B cells and LBH−/− B cells showed that expression of Rap guanylate exchange factor 3 (Rapgef3, Epac1), which activates Rap1, was increased in LBH−/− B cells as by RNA-seq (Fig. 4B), which was further confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) (Fig. 4C-D). Furthermore, we examined the protein expression of Epac1, Rap1, pERK, and ERK and found increases in Epac1 expression, the relative expression of p-ERK and ERK, and the p-ERK/ERK ratio in LBH−/− B cells, indicating Rap 1-ERK pathway activation in LBH−/− B cells (Fig. 4E–F).

5.Inhibition of Rap1 activity reduces IL-10 production in activated LBH−/− B cells

Inhibition of Rap1 activity reduces IL-10 production in activated LBH−/− B cells

Rap 1 usually binds to GDP and is in the inactive form in this complex. Rap 1 is activated when GTP displaces GDP in the Rap 1-GDP complex via Rapgefs [28–29]. Our results showed that Epac1 (Rapgef3) expression was increased in activated LBH−/− B cells. To verify the effect of the Rap 1 pathway on IL-10 production in LBH−/− B cells, we cultured LBH−/− B cells for 24 h, 48 h and 72 h with or without the Epac inhibitor HJC0197 and divided the B cells into four groups, including control, HJC0197 1µM, HJC0197 5µM, and HJC0197 10µM. The B cells and supernatants were collected after 72 h. Detection of IL-10 in B cells showed that the proportion of Bregs and mean fluorescence intensity of IL-10 decreased with increasing HJC0197 concentration at 24, 48, and 72 h (Fig. 5A–C). Measurement of the IL-10 concentration in the supernatant revealed that the trend was similar to that of IL-10 in B cells (Fig. 5D).

7.LBH deletion promotes the therapeutic effects of B cells in mice with DSS-induced colitis

LBH deletion promotes the therapeutic effects of B cells in mice with DSS-induced colitis

To validate the effect of LBH on B cell function, we compared the therapeutic effect of WT and LBH−/− B cells in mice with DSS-induced colitis. The DSS-induced colitis model mice showed severe weight loss, colon shortening, and pathological changes compared with the controls. Intraperitoneal injection of activated WT and LBH−/− B cells produced significant therapeutic effects. However, compared with the WT B cell treatment group, the colitis model mice treated with LBH−/− B cells showed less weight loss and greater colon growth and had less disease activity at the time of evaluation. Additionally, with LBH−/− B cell treatment, the colon tissue had a more complete mucosal epithelium, more crypts, and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 7.A–G). The results indicated that activated LBH−/− B cells play a therapeutic role in colitis model mice.

Discussion

As a highly conserved transcriptional cofactor involved in limb and heart development, LBH has recently been found to likely influence lymphocyte functions involved in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases such as RA and SLE [13–14]. By analyzing LBH expression in the whole blood of HCs and IBD patients, we found that the expression of LBH in IBD patients was significantly reduced, indicating that LBH may also participate in IBD by affecting the function of blood cells. Bregs are involved in autoimmune diseases such as SLE, RA, IBD, experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, and systemic sclerosis through the production of inflammatory inhibitory factors such as IL-10 and IL-35 [30–34]. Additionally, adoptive transfer of Bregs can play a therapeutic role in autoimmune disease [17–18, 35]. In this study, LBH−/− mice with DSS-induced colitis had a more severe disease onset, but they had an increased proportion of Bregs in the mesenteric lymph nodes and spleen. Meanwhile, LBH−/− B cells also produced more IL-10 than did WT B cells in in vitro culture, and the expression of the surface molecule CD24, which is expressed on Bregs, was elevated. Expression of Rap 1 signaling-related transcription factors was increased in the transcriptome of activated LBH−/− B cells, and production of IL-10 was reduced in LBH−/− B cells after inhibition of Rap1 activation and ERK phosphorylation. In addition, intraperitoneal injection of activated LBH−/− B cells produced better therapeutic efficacy against DSS-induced colitis. This is the first study to confirm that loss of LBH could promote IL-10 production in B cells by activating the Rap1 signaling pathway, thereby affecting the therapeutic efficacy of B cells.

As an important immunomodulatory factor, IL-10 plays a key role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis, which is compromised by excessive immune responses [36]. IL-10 is highly associated with IBD, and IL-10−/− and IL-10R−/− β mice develop spontaneous enterocolitis [37]. Additionally, among human polymorphisms of IL-10, IL-10R α and IL-10R β are associated with extremely early onset colitis [38–39]. Bregs are important immune cells that produce IL-10, and their induced differentiation process is regulated in many ways, such as by signaling pathways. Activation of the MAPK/ERK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways has been shown to promote IL-10 production in B cells [27, 40]. Although LBH has been reported to be involved in angiogenesis, embryonic development, tumors, and autoimmune diseases, the role of LBH in cell function is not clear. Our study showed that in B cells, LBH deficiency can activate the Rap1 signaling pathway, increase phosphorylation of ERK, and promote IL-10 production. The Rap1 pathway is involved in the development of many human diseases, such as suppress oncogenic Ras phenotype, antimicrobial killing, cytokine release, and control of cell death pathways in white blood cells [29, 41]. These findings suggest that LBH can regulate Breg differentiation by regulating the Rap 1/ERK signaling pathway.

Bregs have been reported to have some characteristic surface markers. Bregs were first identified as a population of B cells expressing CD5+CD1dhi [42], and the activity of Bregs was attributed to CD21+CD23+CD24+ transitional stage2-like B cells [43]. Examination of characteristic surface molecules on Bregs revealed that CD24+ cells were increased among LBH−/− B cells. LBH has different effects on cell function in various diseases and cells. Ekwall AK and colleagues reported that LBH deficiency increased fibroblast-like synoviocyte proliferation; conversely, LBH overexpression significantly inhibited cell growth [12]. Kilan AB et al. found that loss of LBH attenuated mammary hyperplasia and tumor development [44]. It is currently known that CD24 serves as a costimulatory factor of T cells and regulates their homeostasis and proliferation; while in B cells, CD24 is functionally involved in cell activation and differentiation [45]. Thus, our results suggest that the loss of LBH may promote IL-10 production by promoting the activation and differentiation of B cells.

Current studies suggest that, similar to IBD, the onset of autoimmune diseases stems from an immune imbalance. In many autoimmune diseases, Bregs produce inhibitory inflammatory factors, presenting as reduced or functional defects [33, 46]. However, regarding IBD, the colon tissue and peripheral blood of active ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease patients contained more Bregs than those of the control group and inactive group [31]. As we known, many cytokines involved in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases are at the same time capable of inducing Breg in mice and humans, such as IL-21, IL-6, IL1β, IFNα, IL-33, IL-35, BAFF and APRIL. Breg-inducing cytokines typically have a higher expression than in healthy individuals, suggesting their paradoxical nature. This finding was interpreted as immune tolerance, resulting from increased adaptation of Bregs with elevated release of inflammatory factors during the course of the disease. Additionally, the increase in Bregs was not sufficient to produce sufficient inhibitory factors to resist rapid disease progression. Therefore, the current challenge lies on understanding the balance of action of these cytokines and to efficiently modulate their effect. This will not only push forward our knowledge on the fundamental mechanism of Breg dysregulation in autoimmunity but would also enhance the design of more effective diagnostics and therapies [47]. In our study, LBH−/− mice exhibited more severe disease during DSS-induced colitis, while the mesenteric lymph nodes and spleen produced an increased proportion of Bregs. This finding may be related to the fact that LBH deficiency promotes IL-10 production in B cells or possibly related to the reported immune tolerance. Adoptive B cell therapy can effectively treat autoimmune diseases, and intraperitoneal injection of B cells can significantly treat colitis. We found that intraperitoneal injection of LBH−/− B cells was more effective, indicating that LBH can affect the therapeutic effect of B cells by promoting IL-10 production.

This study has some limitations. Although LBH deletion promote IL-10 production via the Rap1 pathway, the mechanism by whether LBH directly regulates Epac1 transcriptionally or through another intermediary was not determined. Although this study found elevated CD24 in LBH−/−B cells, which may represent enhancing the activation and differentiation of B cells, no further analysis was conducted on the phenotypic typing of LBH−/− Bregs, which requires further exploration in future studies. In addition, although mouse models are convincing, validation of key findings in human B cells or datasets would increase clinical relevance.

In conclusion, Our results suggest that LBH deletion enhances the therapeutic effect of B cells on DSS-induced colitis, and mechanistically, loss of LBH activates the Rap1 signaling pathway to promote IL-10 production. These findings provide a valuable molecular mechanistic basis to improve the effect of cell therapy and provide new targets and directions for the treatment of autoimmune diseases.
