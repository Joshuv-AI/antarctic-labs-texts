Inhibition of MCCC2 Impedes TNBC Progression by Downregulating Leucine Metabolism-Dependent mTOR Signaling
Authors: Rui Chi, Jin Zhang, Zheng Li, Yi Xie, Quanquan Zheng, Jiayi Chen, Yunxia Liu, Fang Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMethylcrotonoyl-CoA carboxylase 2 (MCCC2) has been linked to the progression of multiple tumors, whereas the mTOR has a well-documented role in TNBC. However, the contribution of MCCC2 to TNBC progression and its potential regulation of mTOR signaling remains unclear. This study seeks to assess the functional effect of MCCC2 silencing and tumorigenesis of TNBC, and to investigate whether these effects are mediated through modulation of the mTOR signaling pathway in a leucine-dependent manner.MethodsThe GEPIA database was utilized to assess MCCC2 expression and its prognostic significance in breast cancer. Functional assays were employed to evaluate the biological effects of MCCC2 knockdown. The CPTAC database was used to explore MCCC2-associated signaling pathways. The involvement of the mTOR pathway was further examined by combining MCCC2 knockdown with rapamycin treatment and leucine-deprivation experiments.ResultsMCCC2 expression was significantly elevated in breast cancer tissues and associated with poor prognosis. Knockdown of MCCC2 in TNBC cells inhibited proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumor growth. Bioinformatic analyses revealed a strong association between MCCC2 and several oncogenic pathways, including mTOR, MYC/MYCN, NRF2, and RTK. Notably, the inhibitory effects of MCCC2 knockdown were reversed by rapamycin and abolished under leucine-free culture conditions, suggesting a leucine-dependent mechanism of mTOR regulation.ConclusionMCCC2 promotes TNBC progression by activating the mTOR signaling pathway in a leucine-dependent manner. Targeting MCCC2 and its metabolic axis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for TNBC.

Introduction

Despite considerable progress in early screening and treatment approaches, breast cancer continues to be among the most frequently diagnosed cancers and a major contributor to cancer-associated deaths in women globally.1 Of all breast cancer subtypes, TNBC is the most aggressive, accounting for approximately 10–15% of all breast cancer cases.2,3 TNBC is characterized by the absence of ER, PR, and HER2 expression, which leads to limited treatment choices and unfavorable clinical outcomes.4 Due to its lack of responsiveness to hormonal therapies and HER2-directed drugs, chemotherapy continues to serve as the primary treatment for TNBC.5,6 However, chemoresistance and a high risk of recurrence significantly limit the efficacy of current therapeutic strategies,7 highlighting the urgent need for novel molecular targets and personalized treatment approaches.

Methylcrotonoyl-CoA carboxylase 2 (MCCC2), a subunit of the mitochondrial enzyme 3-methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase (MCC), plays a key role in leucine catabolism.8 Mutations in MCCC1 and MCCC2 genes are known to cause autosomal recessive organic aciduria, frequently associated with secondary carnitine deficiency.9 Recently, MCCC2 has emerged as a potential oncogenic factor in multiple cancers.10–12 Yet, the specific function of this factor in TNBC is still not well elucidated.13

Leucine is a critical regulator of cellular metabolism, mainly because it functions to activate the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway.14,15 The mTOR pathway governs key processes such as cell growth, metabolism, protein synthesis, and survival, and is frequently dysregulated in cancer.16–18 Aberrant activation of mTOR signaling drives tumor initiation and progression by promoting anabolic metabolism and uncontrolled cell proliferation.19

In this study, we demonstrate that MCCC2 promotes TNBC progression through leucine-dependent activation of mTOR signaling. Knockdown of MCCC2 impairs TNBC cell growth and invasiveness by attenuating mTOR pathway activity, thereby revealing a metabolic vulnerability that may serve as a promising therapeutic target in TNBC.

Materials and Methods

GEPIA and UALCAN

The Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA, https://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index) platform was employed to evaluate MCCC2 mRNA expression across pan-cancer datasets. This analysis was performed under the criteria of |Log2FC|≥1 and q-value ≤ 0.01, with box plots constructed to contrast MCCC2 expression patterns between breast cancer tissues and normal counterparts.

Representative immunohistochemical (IHC) images illustrating MCCC2 protein expression were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA).

Additionally, the UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) was utilized to assess MCCC2 protein expression levels and its associations with multiple signaling pathways in breast cancer versus normal tissues, leveraging data from the CPTAC dataset.

Kaplan-Meier Plotter

The prognostic relevance of genes involved in BCAA catabolism in TNBC was assessed via the Kaplan-Meier plotter (available at https://www.kmplot.com). Patient samples were stratified into high and low expression groups based on median MCCC2 mRNA expression. Relapse-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) were analyzed. MCCC2 (ID209623_at).

Western Blot

Cells were lysed in SDS buffer and boiled, after which proteins were fractionated via 8–12% SDS-PAGE (Yeasen Biotechnology, 21108ES50, Shanghai, China). The antibodies utilized included β-actin-HRP (Abways Technology, #AB2001, Shanghai, China), p70S6K (CST, #9202, Danvers, MA, USA), Phospho-p70S6K (CST, #9234, Danvers, MA, USA), MCCC2 (ABclonal, A15181, Wuhan, China), α/β tubulin (CST, #2148s), GAPDH (CST, #2118, Danvers, MA, USA), Anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (CST, #7074, Danvers, MA, USA), and Anti-mouse IgG-HRP (CST, #7076, Danvers, MA, USA).

RT-qPCR

RNA extraction and RT-PCR were carried out following the protocol described previously.20 All primers used in the study were obtained from Sangon (Shanghai, China). The sequences of the primers are listed below: MCCC2 forward: 5′-TTTGTCCAGTTATGCTGCCAAAGA-3′; MCCC2 reverse: 5′-GGCACCATCCTTGGCAATTC-3′; 18S forward:5′AGGCCCTGTAATTGGAATGAGTC-3′; 18S reverse: 5′-GCTCCCAAGATCCAACTACGAG-3′.

Cell Lines

The HEK293T cell line was purchased from National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China), the MDA-MB-231-LM2 cells (referred to as LM2, derivative of CVCL_0062, kindly provided by Dr. Qian Zhao (Department of Pathophysiology, Key Laboratory of Cell Differentiation and Apoptosis of National Ministry of Education, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (SJTU-SM), No. 280, Chong Qing South Rd, Shanghai, 200025, China). Although LM2 cells were not directly obtained from a commercial cell repository, their use in this study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Wuxi No.2 People’s Hospital. Prior to experimental use, the LM2 cell line was authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling to confirm its identity and exclude cross-contamination. The STR profile was consistent with that of the parental MDA-MB-231 cell line. In addition, all cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. The TNBC cell line LM2, along with HEK293T cells, were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Hyclone, Beijing, China). This medium was fortified with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco BRL, Gaithersburg, MD, USA).21 All cell lines utilized in the present study were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2. Knockdown efficiency was determined by qRT-PCR. A knockdown efficiency of ≥70% was set as the threshold for effective gene silencing, and all experiments were performed using cells that met this criterion.

Construction of MCCC2-Knockdown LM2 Cell Line

Lentivirus was packaged in 293T cells via a liposome-mediated transfection system. The liposome-based viral packaging mixture was gently mixed and incubated at room temperature, followed by its addition to 293T cell cultures for incubation. After culture, the viral supernatant was harvested and filtered to remove cell debris, which was then immediately used to infect LM2 cells. Subsequent to infection, puromycin selection was performed to obtain positive cells, ultimately generating MCCC2-knockdown LM2 cell lines with stably maintained knockdown efficiency of over 70% for subsequent experimental use.

CCK-8 Assay

Cells were plated at a density of 2×103 per well in 96-well plates, followed by incubation at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 6 hours to facilitate cell adhesion. After 24 h (1 d), 48 h (2 d) and 72 h (3 d), 10μL of CCK-8 reagent and 100 μL of fresh medium were added per well, followed by incubation for 1 h at 37°C. Subsequently, the optical density of each well was determined at 450 nm with a spectrophotometer. For leucine-deprivation experiments, leucine was omitted from the culture medium.

Wound Healing Assay

LM2 cells (2×105 per well) were cultured until 85–100% confluency. A scratch was made with a sterile 200 μL pipette tip and cells were rinsed with cold PBS. Cells were incubated in medium with rapamycin added or omitted. Migration was evaluated by capturing images at 0 h and 48 h, and leucine-free conditions were run in parallel.

Transwell Assay

Invasion assays were performed using Transwell chambers equipped with 8-µm pore membranes (#3422, Corning, Corning, NY, USA). Matrigel (#356234, BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was diluted and applied to the chambers prior to cell seeding. Cells were first cultured for 48 hours in medium either containing rapamycin or not. Subsequently, trypsinization was performed on the cells, which were then resuspended in medium without serum and plated into the upper sections of transwell inserts at a density of 2×104 cells per insert. The lower sections of the inserts received complete medium. Once 24 or 48 hours had elapsed, the cells that had migrated were fixed and stained using 0.1% crystal violet. The incubation time of the Transwell assay was strictly defined as 24 hours for cell migration detection and 48 hours for cell invasion detection, respectively. All cells were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 during the assay, and other experimental procedures remained consistent with the previously described protocol.

TNBC Xenograft Model

LM2 cells (1×105), either with or without MCCC2 knockdown, were suspended in a 3:1 volume ratio of Matrigel, with the total volume adjusted to 75 μL, and orthotopically implanted into the inguinal mammary fat pads of 6–7-week-old female NOD/SCID mice. Tumor growth was monitored regularly, and mice were sacrificed 24 days after injection for tissue collection and analysis. Tumor volume monitoring was initiated one week after the experiment started, and the length and short diameter of the tumor were measured every 3 days using a vernier caliper. Tumor volume was roughly calculated by the formula: Volume = length × width × width. At the end of the experiment, the tumors were dissected, and their weight was measured accurately using an electronic balance. Anesthesia was induced with 3% isoflurane in oxygen at 0.8 L/min and maintained at 1–1.5% isoflurane with oxygen at 0.5 L/min during procedures. At the endpoint, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation, in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020).

Statistical Analysis

Data in this paper were analyzed and displayed using GraphPad 9 (La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± SD, one-way ANOVA or a two-tailed Student’s t test was performed to analyze the statistically significance. *P<0.05, **P<0.01, ***P<0.001.

Results

Increased MCCC2 Expression Correlates with Clinicopathological Features and an Unfavorable Prognosis

To comprehensively study the role of MCCC2, we initially examined its expression across different tumors. Figure 1A showed that MCCC2 was upregulated in the majority of tumors compared to normal tissues. Specifically, its mRNA levels were notably increased in tumor compared to their matched breast tissue counterparts. (Figure 1B). Immunohistochemical analysis further confirmed that MCCC2 protein was overexpressed in breast cancer tissues under different dyeing conditions (Figure 1C). Notably, MCCC2 overexpression was strongly linked to a more advanced tumor (T) stage (Figure 1D). Among different breast cancer subtypes, MCCC2 expression was notably higher in HER2-enriched, Luminal A, and Luminal B subtypes compared to the basal-like subtype (Figure 1E). However, when comparing tumor and normal tissues within each subtype, a marked overexpression of MCCC2 was detected exclusively in Luminal A and Luminal B tumor subtypes. (Figure 1F). In line with these findings, MCCC2 expression was notably higher in breast cancer cell lines when compared to MCF10A (Figure 1G). Survival analyses indicated that high MCCC2 expression was strongly linked to reduced RFS in breast cancer patients, though no association was observed with OS (Figure 1H and I). Notably, in the TNBC subgroup, elevated MCCC2 expression strongly predicted poor clinical outcomes (Figure 1J and K). Collectively, MCCC2 exerts an oncogenic role in breast cancer and could function as an independent prognostic biomarker, particularly in TNBC. Knockdown of MCCC2 Inhibits the Proliferation, Colony-Forming Ability, Migration, and Invasive Capacity of TNBC Cells

To investigate the functional role of MCCC2 in TNBC, LM2 cells were transfected with MCCC2-specific shRNA. The efficiency of MCCC2 knockdown was verified (Figure 2A and B). Silencing of MCCC2 significantly impaired the proliferation of TNBC cells, as demonstrated by CCK-8 assay (Figure 2C). Consistently, colony formation assays revealed a marked decrease in clonogenic potential following MCCC2 knockdown (Figure 2D and E). Furthermore, it showed that MCCC2 knockdown significantly diminished the migratory and invasive abilities of LM2 cells (Figure 2F–I). Collectively, results suggest that MCCC2 is required for maintaining the malignant phenotype of TNBC cells, including proliferation, clonogenicity, migration, and invasion. MCCC2 Overexpression Correlates with Key Oncogenic Signaling Pathways

To further explore the oncogenic potential of MCCC2 in breast cancer, data from the CPTAC were examined via the UALCAN portal. The results revealed that elevated MCCC2 protein expression was significantly associated with multiple classical oncogenic signaling pathways, including p53/Rb, MYC/MYCN, HIPPO, WNT, mTOR, receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK), SWI/SNF complex, and NRF2 pathways (Figure 3A–H). Notably, the strongest associations were observed between MCCC2 overexpression and the mTOR, MYC/MYCN, NRF2, and RTK pathways, which are pivotal regulators of cell fate, metabolism, and survival. These findings suggest that MCCC2 may contribute to breast cancer progression by modulating multiple pro-tumorigenic signaling cascades, particularly the mTOR, MYC/MYCN, NRF2, and RTK pathways. These findings indicate that MCCC2 may drive breast cancer progression by regulating multiple pro-tumorigenic signaling cascades—most notably the mTOR, MYC/MYCN, NRF2, and RTK pathways. MCCC2 Knockdown Inhibits TNBC Progression by Downregulating the mTOR Signaling Pathway

MCCC2 encodes an essential enzyme involved in leucine catabolism. Given that leucine, a branched-chain amino acid (BCAA), is a well- documented activator of the mTOR.22,23 We investigated the potential regulatory impact of MCCC2 on mTOR activity in TNBC cells. As anticipated, MCCC2 knockdown in LM2 cells led to a significant reduction in mTOR pathway activation, evidenced by decreased phosphorylation levels of p70S6K (p-p70S6K) (Figure 4A). Notably, the impaired proliferation, migration, and invasion caused by MCCC2 silencing were partially rescued by rapamycin treatment, an mTOR pathway inhibitor (Figure 4B–F). MCCC2 Knockdown Inhibits the TNBC Tumors Growth

To evaluate the effect of MCCC2 knockdown on TNBC progression in vivo, a tumor model was established using LM2 cells with stable MCCC2 silencing (Figure 5A). Tumor growth was notably inhibited in the shMCCC2 group versus the control group, which was supported by decreased tumor volume and weight (Figure 5B and C). Notably, no significant difference in body weight was observed between mice in the shNC and shMCCC2 groups, indicating minimal systemic toxicity associated with MCCC2 knockdown (Figure 5D). Consistent with in vitro findings, Western blot analysis of xenograft tissues revealed decreased levels of phosphorylated p70S6K, indicating inhibition of mTOR signaling in the shMCCC2 group (Figure 5E). Collectively, these results confirm that MCCC2 knockdown effectively suppresses TNBC development, at least in part through downregulation of the mTOR signaling pathway. MCCC2 Regulates mTOR Signaling via Leucine Metabolism in TNBC

To elucidate the role of MCCC2 in leucine metabolism and its impact on mTOR signaling in TNBC, a series of mechanistic experiments were performed. ELISA analysis revealed that knockdown of MCCC2 significantly increased serum leucine levels in TNBC xenograft models (Figure 6A), suggesting a disruption in leucine catabolism. To further examine the functional relevance, LM2 cells with MCCC2 knockdown were cultured in leucine-free medium. Under these conditions, no significant differences in proliferation or migration were observed between the control and shMCCC2 groups (Figure 6B–D). Consistently, MCCC2 knockdown failed to suppress mTOR pathway activity in leucine-deprived conditions, as indicated by unchanged phosphorylation levels of p70S6K (Figure 6E). These findings suggest that MCCC2 regulates TNBC progression through modulation of the mTOR signaling pathway in a leucine-dependent manner. Discussion

The defining feature of triple-negative breast cancer is the lack of key receptors—consequently, both hormonal therapies and HER2-targeted therapies fail to work.24,25 Clinical management of TNBC primarily depends on surgical resection and chemotherapy. However, these approaches are often hampered by systemic toxicity, limited bioavailability, and the emergence of chemoresistance.7,26 Although recent advancements in targeted therapies have shown promise for both early-stage and metastatic TNBC,27,28 patient prognosis—especially in advanced disease—remains poor. While the incorporation of immune checkpoint inhibitors into first- and second-line therapies has conferred clinical benefit in several solid tumors, including TNBC,26,29 patients with metastatic TNBC continue to exhibit the shortest OS among all breast cancer subtypes.30,31 These hurdles highlight the pressing demand for new therapeutic approaches and more potent treatment combinations.

A growing body of evidence indicates that tumor cells exhibit increased uptake and metabolism of BCAAs—notably leucine—to sustain growth and proliferation[32–34].32–34 Our previous research has implicated BCAA metabolism in the progression of TNBC,21 suggesting that dysregulated leucine uptake and enhanced catabolism may contribute to tumor aggressiveness. In this context, MCCC2, a key enzyme in leucine catabolism, may serve as a critical node linking nutrient metabolism to oncogenic signaling pathways.

We demonstrated that MCCC2 expression is significantly upregulated in breast cancer tissues, particularly in TNBC. Mechanistically, we uncovered a new regulatory axis through which MCCC2 modulates the mTOR signaling pathway—this pathway serves as a central controller of cellular metabolism (Figure 7).22,35–39 Notably, knockdown of MCCC2 suppressed mTOR pathway activation, as evidenced by reduced p-p70S6K levels, and these effects were abolished under leucine-deprived conditions. Furthermore, we observed increased serum leucine levels following MCCC2 knockdown in TNBC xenografts, reinforcing the role of MCCC2 in leucine catabolism and downstream signaling. Together, our study is the first to pinpoint a direct association between MCCC2 and activated mTOR signaling within TNBC. These findings provide compelling evidence that MCCC2 promotes TNBC progression by modulating leucine metabolism and activating mTOR-dependent pathways. Although this study initially clarified the role and molecular mechanism of MCCC2 in tumor occurrence and development, providing a basic reference for related research, it still has some areas for further improvement due to the limitations of existing experimental conditions. First, a single cell line model was used in in vitro experiments. This model can effectively control interfering factors to clarify the basic functions of MCCC2, but it cannot fully simulate the complex characteristics of the clinical tumor microenvironment, which may lead to certain differences between the results observed in vitro and clinical actual scenarios. Immunodeficient mice were selected for animal experiments to facilitate the clear observation of tumor cell colonization and the regulatory effect of MCCC2. However, they cannot simulate the normal immune state of the body, making it difficult to fully reflect the regulatory effect of MCCC2 on tumors in the immune context, and the clinical transformation potential of the results needs to be further verified in subsequent studies.

Second, in terms of the exploration of molecular mechanisms, this study focused on the regulatory effect of MCCC2 on the mTOR pathway, providing a core basis for the interpretation of its function, and has not yet deeply explored its synergistic regulatory relationship with other oncogenic pathways. Bioinformatics analysis suggests that MCCC2 is associated with pathways such as MYC/MYCN and NRF2, implying that it may be involved in the regulation of tumor progression through the synergy of multiple pathways. However, this study has not yet clarified the specific interaction modes between the above pathways, leaving room for further deepening the interpretation of the molecular mechanism by which MCCC2 regulates tumor occurrence and development.

In summary, our data support a critical role for MCCC2 in TNBC pathogenesis and highlight it as a potential therapeutic target. Targeting MCCC2 or related components of leucine metabolism, particularly in combination with mTOR pathway inhibitors or dietary leucine restriction, may represent a promising strategy for the treatment of TNBC.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings reveal that MCCC2 drives the progression of TNBC through leucine-dependent activation of the mTOR signaling pathway. These results uncover a previously unrecognized link between leucine metabolism and oncogenic signaling in TNBC and suggest that targeting MCCC2 itself, or the metabolic pathway it regulates, could present a new therapeutic strategy for patients diagnosed with this aggressive breast cancer subtype.
