Thirty‐day mortality among patients admitted due to acute myocardial infarction during the dates of the Cardiac Society of Australia and New Zealand Annual Scientific Meeting
Authors: Timothy Nathan Kwan, Richa Malhotra, David Brieger, Vincent Chow, Leonard Kritharides, Jayant Ravindran, Noor Alsadat, Gregory Harvey, Austin Chin Chwan Ng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients admitted during major medical conferences may receive different care if there are significant staff shortages. We evaluated the mortality of patients presenting with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) according to whether they were admitted during the Cardiac Society of Australia and New Zealand (CSANZ) Annual Scientific Meeting (ASM). Admissions during meeting days were compared to matching non‐meeting days within 4 weeks before or after the conference. Among 24 761 patients, those admitted during the CSANZ ASM had the same age, sex and comorbidities compared to controls but had higher 30‐day mortality after multivariable adjustment (OR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.02–1.37). These findings raise concern that care delivery during major conference periods may be affected, although the mechanism could not be determined from these data.

It is common among Australian hospitals for departments to operate on a skeleton staff during major conferences. Little is known about the provision of healthcare services at these times; however, one American study did suggest that acute myocardial infarction (AMI) mortality may fall during national interventional cardiology meetings. 1 We describe mortality among patients admitted with AMI during the Cardiac Society of Australia and New Zealand (CSANZ) Annual Scientific Meeting (ASM). This is the largest cardiology meeting in Australia, with over 2500 attendees per year.

We retrospectively studied patients who presented with AMI during the CSANZ ASM from 2003 to 2021 using the New South Wales (NSW) Admitted‐Patient‐Data‐Collection database linked to the death registry. This database has been capturing ≥97% of NSW healthcare facility admission data since 2001, including public and private hospital encounters. AMI was defined as a principal diagnosis of AMI according to International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision, Australian Modified codes (I21, I21.0, I21.1, I21.2, I21.3, I21.4, I21.9, I22, I22.0, I22.1, I22.8 and I22.9). Patients admitted with AMI on identical days of the week up to 4 weeks before or after the conference were controls. Characteristics were compared numerically, with significance determined by non‐parametric techniques: the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi‐squared test for categorical variables. The primary outcome was 30‐day mortality. The adjusted risk of 30‐day mortality was calculated with logistic regression, which adjusted for the following prespecified covariates: age, sex, date, ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) capability, PCI during admission, coronary artery bypass grafting during admission, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, smoking, heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, stroke and chronic obstructive airways disease. PCI capability was classified into none, intermittent or 24/7 availability at the admitting hospital, although private hospitals were classified as a 24/7 facility as more granular data were unavailable for these hospitals. Standard errors from the multivariable logistic regression model were clustered by admitting hospital using a hospital‐level cluster‐robust sandwich estimator. Sensitivity analyses restricted patients to those presenting with STEMI and separately restricted days of interest to the weekend. A landmark analysis was performed excluding the first 30 days. Ethics approval was granted by NSW Population and Health Services Research Ethics Committee, reference number: 2019/ETH01790.

There were 2809 AMI presentations during meeting days and 21 952 control patients. The median age was 72.4 (interquartile range (IQR): 60.4–82.0) years, and 64% of the cohort were male. Revascularisation rates and all comorbidities in the multivariable regression were balanced across groups (Table 1).

In the study cohort, 318 (11.3%) patients admitted during the CSANZ ASM and 2134 (9.7%) control patients died within 30 days (P = 0.008). There was no significant difference in mortality by 6‐months (Table 1). The adjusted 30‐day mortality risk was higher during meeting days (odds ratio (OR) = 1.18, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.02–1.37, P = 0.02). This association did fluctuate over time, although there was insufficient power to demonstrate a significant effect of the CSANZ ASM during individual years (Fig. 1). In a sensitivity analysis, results were similar if PCI capability was excluded as a covariate (odds ratio (OR): 1.18, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.02–1.36, P = 0.03). Results were numerically but not significantly stronger in a sensitivity analysis restricting AMIs to only STEMIs. Unadjusted risk of 30‐day mortality after STEMI was higher in patients admitted during the CSANZ ASM (12.0% compared to 9.8%, P = 0.049), but this was not significant after adjustment (OR: 1.25, 95% CI: 0.97–1.59). Results were similar in a sensitivity analysis that restricted meeting days and their matching controls to only the weekend (adjusted OR: 1.27, 95% CI = 1.03–1.56, P = 0.02). A landmark analysis excluding the first 30 days after admission showed patients did not have significant mortality difference during follow‐up (OR: 0.91, 95% CI = 0.82–1.02, P = 0.10).

Discussion

Patients admitted during the CSANZ ASM with AMI have a significantly higher 30‐day mortality. This may be seen as comparable to the 'weekend effect' in which patients admitted with AMI on the weekend have mortality 5%–13% higher than weekdays. 2 , 3 These results, however, contrast with American data in which 30‐day mortality declined during cardiology meetings. 1 , 4 Data on other conditions are more variable, with conferences seemingly having no effect on survival after cardiac arrest 5 and being negatively associated with trauma mortality 6 and reduced medical staffing during periods of industrial action being associated with lower mortality. 7 These are observational data that do not prove a causal relationship between the CSANZ ASM and mortality after AMI, and we can only speculate on the possible reasons for the association. Potential mechanisms such as delay in coronary angiogram, lower prescription of prognostic therapy or misdiagnosis cannot be demonstrated from our data. Nevertheless, the findings do cause some concern that the decreased staffing during the CSANZ ASM may delay some critical treatments or that the absence of leaders in cardiology may reduce the application of optimal evidence‐based management strategies. We do believe that increased attention on staffing during meetings is warranted. Currently neither conference organisers nor hospitals present data on attendance, and greater transparency of attendance and the drain from certain hospitals may be the first step to exploring whether the spike in mortality can be addressed.

We note these findings may not be generalisable to other disciplines, regions outside of NSW or other meetings. Although 20 years of data were available, the study was underpowered to draw significant conclusions from subgroup analysis such as how the results differed during the COVID‐19 era. Importantly, the potential benefits of scientific meetings, such as education, research and consequent long‐term improvements in patient outcomes, are outside the scope of this study. Nevertheless, we believe this study does motivate further research and emphasises the importance of providing high‐quality care while many staff are away.
