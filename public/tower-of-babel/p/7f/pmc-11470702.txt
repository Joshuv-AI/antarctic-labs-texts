The effect of phosphorus, irradiance and competitor identity on the relative performance of invasive Chromolaena odorata
Authors: Yu-Long Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResource competition is an important factor affecting the invasion success of alien plants, and environmental factors influence the competition outcomes between invasive and native plants. In this study, we explore the competitive pattern between invasive Chromolaena odorata and two native plant species under different phosphorus and irradiance levels.ResultsThe final biomass of each plant was regulated by both morphological and physiological traits. Invasive C. odorata did not always perform better than both native plants, and the competitive pattern between C. odorata and native plants was dependent on native competitor identity and environmental conditions. With competition, invasive C. odorata showed higher biomass (over 60%) than native Xanthium sibiricum under all treatments, but only showed higher biomass (about 20%) than native Eupatorium lindleyanum in normal irradiance treatments. The effect of phosphorus on competition depended on the irradiance level. Under normal irradiance, phosphorus addition increased (almost 10 times) the competitive index of invasive C. odorata; however, under shade irradiance, phosphorus addition decreased (40%) the competitive index of C. odorata.ConclusionThese results suggest that phosphorus, irradiance and native plant competitor together influence the relative performance of invasive C. odorata. In shade environment, selecting E. lindleyanum as competitor and increasing phosphorus level is an effective method for controlling the invasion of C. odorata.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05684-3.Keywords: Invasive plants, Chromolaena odorata, Phosphorus, Irradiance, Competitor identity

Background

Alien plant invasion, which threatens the biodiversity, safety, and function of the native ecosystem and causes great damage to agriculture, forestry, and animal husbandry, is presently a hot topic of research [1, 2]. Importantly, identifying effective methods to control invasive plants is a big challenge for ecologists. Studies have shown that invasive plants have a higher competitive ability than native plants, facilitating the successful invasion of alien plants by repelling native competitors [3] and influencing the community composition, structure, and function. Some studies have indicated that resource conditions have great impacts on competition outcomes between invasive and native plants [4], and increased soil fertility has generally been shown to facilitate alien species invasion [5]. Resource fluctuation hypothesis proposed that resource fluctuation usually increases resource availability, which will provide invasion opportunities for non-native species, and the resident communities will be more susceptible to invasion [6]. Zheng et al. [6] indicated that nutrient conditions had important effects on the invasion success of alien Chromolaena odorata in artificial communities. Invasion success or failure of alien Erigeron canadensis depends on the amount and timing of nutrient application [7]. González-Muñoz et al. [8] reported that shade and drought stress in floodplains benefited the invasion of alien Acer negundo.

Resource conditions impacting the invasion status may be related to resource use efficiency differences between invasive and native plants. Most invaders exhibit a greater photosynthetic energy-use efficiency (PEUE) and photosynthetic nitrogen-use efficiency (PNUE) than native plants [9]. Feng et al. [10–12] proposed that invasive plants increase nitrogen allocation to photosynthesis but reduce nitrogen allocation to defense due to enemy release, contributing to the higher PNUE, PEUE, and photosynthetic water-use efficiency (PWUE) of the invader. Therefore, even in resource-poor conditions, some invasive plants in Hawaii still outperform native plants [13]. Wang et al. [14] showed that nitrogen-fixing bacteria can increase the invasiveness of alien Solidago canadensis in resource-poor environments. Invasive Mikania micrantha has different resource-use strategies at low and high nitrogen levels [15], facilitating its range expansion.

Competition plays an important role in structuring community composition and stability [16, 17]. Understanding competition mechanisms can increase the predictability of the results of interspecific interactions [16], which is beneficial for controlling invasive plants. The identity of native competitors significantly affects competition outcomes [18], and the invasion status of the alien plant varies in communities with different native species [19]. It might relate to functional traits of native species. Longo et al. [20] found that resident species with similarly functional traits were more resistant to the invasive plants in grassland of Argentina, because these species have similar niches with the invasive plants, and no more resources were left to the invader. However, Sheley and James [21] found that resident neighbors with distantly functional traits had better resistance, because the probability is higher for one species (among these neighbors) has higher fitness than the invader.

Chromolaena odorata (L.) R. M. King and H. Robinson (Compositae) is a perennial shrub, native to North, Central, and South America, and is now a noxious invasive species throughout most of tropical and subtropical Asia, Oceania, and Africa. It was first introduced into India as an ornamental plant in the mid-19th century and has become a noxious invasive species in southern China [19]. Chromolaena odorata threatens biodiversity, agriculture and forestry, which has been listed as one of main invasive species in China. Acidic soil is predominant in tropical and subtropical areas, and the deficiency of available phosphorus in the soil is common [22]. Phosphorus is the second most essential macronutrient in terms of limiting plant growth and is a key component of energy metabolism and nucleic acid and membrane biosynthesis. Many basic biochemical processes, such as photosynthesis and respiration, are energized by inorganic phosphate and its organic derivatives [23]. In addition, irradiance is an important resource regulating plant survival, growth, and distribution [24]. Therefore, in this study, we compare competitive outcomes between invasive C. odorata and two native plants under different phosphorus and irradiance levels to provide valuable information for understanding the invasion mechanism of C. odorata. We aim to determine (1) whether the invader performs better than native plants under all treatments; (2) the effects of phosphorus and irradiance on the relative performance of invasive C. odorata when it competes with native plants; and (3) whether invasive C. odorata responds similarly to competition from two native plants.

Materials and methods

Study site

This study was conducted at Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden, Chinese Academy of Sciences (XTBG-CAS) (21°56’ N, 101°15’ E), located in Menglun, Mengla, Yunnan Province, southwest China. The mean annual temperature is 21.7℃. The mean temperatures of the hottest and coolest months are 25.3℃ (July) and 15.6℃ (January), respectively. The average annual precipitation at this site is 1557 mm, with a dry period lasting from November to April.

Study materials

In this study, invasive Chromolaena odorata and two native plants (Eupatorium lindleyanum and Xanthium sibiricum) were selected as experimental materials. Both E. lindleyanum and X. sibiricum are common native species, and they have similar life form to invasive C. odorata (personal observation). From October 2020 to May 2021, the seeds of these three species were collected in our nursery garden of experimental plants. In June 2021, the seeds were germinated in the seedbed. In July 2021, similar size seedlings (about 8 cm tall) of three species were selected and transplanted into pots (15 L) in a greenhouse. The pots were filled with equal proportions of river sand and a top layer (0–15 cm) of forest soil as cultivation substrate (total N, 1.03 g / kg; total P, 0.65 g / Kg). There were five planting patterns: monoculture of C. odorata, E. lindleyanum, and X. sibiricum (one individual in each pot) and competition planting (C. odorata + E. lindleyanum; C. odorata + X. sibiricum) (Fig. S1).

Phosphorus and irradiance treatments

These seedlings were planted at two phosphorus and two irradiance levels. The two phosphorus levels were no phosphorus addition and phosphorus addition. Referring to phosphorus treatment in previous study [25], in this study, each pot received 0.5 g calcium magnesium phosphate per month from September to November as phosphorous addition. Chromolaena odorata can distribute from understory to open site (field observation). Therefore, we set two irradiance levels: normal irradiance and shade irradiance. Normal irradiance was 100% full sun exposure, and shade irradiance was about 50% of full sun exposure and was created by covering the plants with shade houses comprising one layer of black nylon mesh). There were four environmental treatments: normal irradiance and no phosphorus (Normal + No P), normal irradiance and phosphorus (Normal + P), shade irradiance and no phosphorus (Shade + No P), and shade irradiance and phosphorus (Shade + P). In each treatment, there were six replicates of each planting pattern (Fig. S1). There were total 120 pots. The seedlings were well watered every day, and the soil moisture is about 20%.

Measurements

At the end of November 2021, the effective quantum yield of PSII (Phi2) was measured on new fully expanded leaves using a MultispeQ device (PhotosynQ, USA) at 09:00–11:30 following the method of Kuhlgert et al. [26]. The net photosynthetic rate (Pn) was determined on new fully expanded leaves using a Li-6400 Portable Photosynthesis System (Li-Cor, Lincoln, NE, USA) at 09:30–11:30. The conditions in the reference chamber were as follows: relative humidity, 75%; illumination, 800 µmol m− 2 s− 1 photosynthetic photo flux density (PPFD); CO2 concentration, 380 µmol mol− 1; and block temperature, 25 °C.

After determining Phi2 and Pn, the aboveground parts of each individual were harvested and separated into leaves and support organs (including stems, branches, and petioles). Each part was oven dried at 80 °C for 48 h, and the biomass of each individual was the sum of leaves and support organs. The leaf area was determined using a Li-3000 A leaf area meter (Li-Cor, Lincoln, NE, USA). The leaf area ratio (LAR) was calculated as leaf area/biomass.

Statistical analysis

When the seedlings were grown in monoculture, the effects of species, irradiance, phosphorus, and all the interactions between factors were determined according to three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When grown in competition, the effects of species, irradiance, phosphorus, native competitor identity, and the interactions between factors on each variable were determined using four-way ANOVA. When the seedlings were grown in monoculture, the differences in each variable were determined among species using one-way ANOVA, and when the seedlings were grown in competition, a t test was used to detect the differences in each variable between invasive C. odorata and E. lindleyanum or X. sibiricum. All analyses were carried out using SPSS 18.0, and the Levene statistic was used to determine the homogeneity of variances.

Discussion

Phi2 is the effective quantum yield of of PSII, which represents the probability of light energy are used in photochemical reaction, and high value reflects the plant has good health condition or strong environment adaption ability [26]. Invasive C. odorata had a similar Phi2 but higher Pn than native E. lindleyanum and X. sibiricum in most treatments (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). This indicates that the photosynthetic carbon assimilation rate was higher but that the respiration cost was lower for invasive C. odorata than the two native plants [27], which could benefit the invader, allowing biomass to accumulate more quickly than that of native plants and facilitating the successful invasion of alien plants [28]. Some studies have also found that many invaders have a higher Pn and biomass than native competitors. Zheng et al. [29] found that the photosynthetic rate and total biomass were higher for invasive Eupatorium adenophorum than native congeners (E. heterophyllum and E. japonicum). Dai et al. [30] recently found that invasive Wedelia trilobata had a higher Pn and stem biomass than native W. chinensis under normal nitrogen conditions.

However, inconsistent with our prediction and the above research, in this study, the biomass of C. odorata was significantly higher than that of native X. sibiricum but lower than that of native E. lindleyanum except under “Normal + P” and “Normal + No P” treatments (Figs. 5 and 6). This indicates that in addition to Pn, other traits, such as LAR, also play an important role in the biomass accumulation process. LAR is a determinant of the plant growth rate. A higher LAR implies that the per unit biomass produces a larger leaf area, and the enlargement of the photosynthetic area is beneficial for biomass accumulation. It can give a competitive advantage to the plant [31]. Invasive C. odorata had a higher LAR than native X. sibiricum, but it was similar or lower than that of native E. lindleyanum (Figs. 7 and 8). According to the change trend similarity between Pn and biomass and between LAR and biomass, LAR plays a more important role than Pn in biomass accumulation under the “Shade + P” treatment, but Pn plays a more important role than LAR under the “Normal + P” and “Normal + No P” treatments (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8). It might due to in shade environment, photosynthetic electron transport rate and photosynthetic enzyme activity (physiological traits) are low, so, morphological traits (such as LAR) are more determinant in biomass accumulation even competition outcome. Dai et al. [30] reported that the invasion success of alien Wedelia trilobata was also regulated by both morphological and physiological traits.

The CI was positive when invasive C. odorata competed with native X. sibiricum (Fig. 9), suggesting that the relative performance of C. odorata to X. sibiricum was better when they grew in competition than in monoculture. However, CI was negative when C. odorata competed with native E. lindleyanum in the “Shade + P” and “Shade + No P” treatments (Fig. 9), suggesting that the relative performance of C. odorata to E. lindleyanum was poorer when they grew in competition than in monoculture under these conditions. These results indicated that the relative advantage of the invader depending on environment and competitor identity, and in shade environments, E. lindleyanum can be used to control the spread of invasive C. odorata. Arias et al. [3] also proposed that the negative effect of invasive Ligustrum lucidum on the performance of native Lithraea molleoides was only found under higher resource availability.

Phosphorus is the second most important macronutrient (after nitrogen) in limiting plant growth, and is involved in an array of physiological processes, such as photosynthesis, respiration and nucleic acid synthesis [32, 33]. Phosphorus had a significant effect on biomass (Table 1). Phosphorus addition significantly increased the biomass of each plant when grown in monoculture (Fig. 5), but in competition, phosphorus addition did not increase the biomass of native X. sibiricum under normal irradiance and did not increase the biomass of C. odorata in a shade environment (Fig. 6). It indicated that the effect of phosphorus on biomass depending on plant identity, irradiance and competition strength. It may due to phosphorus and irradiance together influencing chlorophyll synthesis [34]. Previous studies also showed that nutrient addition can increase the plants′ final biomass [35, 36], and different plants respond diversely to phosphorus addition or deficiency [37].

The effects of phosphorus addition on the competition between the invader and native plants depended on the irradiance level. In a normal irradiance environment, phosphorus addition increased the competitive advantage of C. odorata; however, in a shaded environment, phosphorus addition decreased the competitive advantage of C. odorata (Fig. 9). Similar results were also reported by Zhu et al. [38], who found that the effects of phosphorus on the biomass of macrophytes differed between light and shade environments.

Irradiance had no significant effects on biomass in monoculture or competition, but its interaction with species, phosphorus, and competitor identity had a significant effect on biomass under competition conditions (Tables 1 and 2). This indicates that when C. odorata invaded the native plant community, irradiance, phosphorus and competitor identity together influence the relative performance of C. odorata. In future, the effects of irradiance, phosphorus and their interaction on invasion of alien plants should be detected in community level.

Conclusion

In this study, it is found that invasive C. odorata does not always perform better than both native plants, and the competitive outcome between C. odorata and native plants is dependent on native competitor identity and environmental conditions (irradiance and phosphorus level). In order to prevent C. odorata from invading forest, E. lindleyanum can be planted in the understory with phosphorus addition. The results also give a cue that when controlling alien plants, biotic (e.g., native plant community composition, herbivores, and soil fauna) and abiotic factors (e.g., nutrient, water, and irradiance) should be considered at local sites.
