Targeting childhood malaria: insights from multi-country DHS data in Sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Benjamin Akwasi Konja, Nana Ama Frimpomaa Agyapong, Emmanuel Kweku Ocran, Gloria Anyimadu, Livingstone Agyapong, Kwame Kumi Asare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a major public health challenge in sub-Saharan Africa, disproportionately affecting children under five and pregnant women. Understanding the socio-demographic and maternal determinants of malaria across multiple countries is critical for designing targeted interventions such as Targeted Drug Administration (TDA), Perennial Malaria Chemoprevention (PMC), Seasonal Malaria Chemoprevention (SMC), Vector Control and Epidemiological Mapping. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and determinants of malaria infection among children under five across seven sub-Saharan African countries and to identify country-specific risk factors.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study used secondary data from the most recent Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS-8) for Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Kenya, Mozambique, Senegal, Tanzania, and for Cameroon, Malaria Indicator Surveys (MIS-8) was used. Two outcome measures were defined: malaria infection based on rapid diagnostic test (RDT) results (five countries: Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Mozambique, Tanzania and Cameroon) and maternal self-report (seven countries: Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Kenya, Mozambique, Senegal, Tanzania and Cameroon). The data were analyzed using Stata/SE 17.0 and the analytic samples included 20,323 mother–child pairs for RDT analysis and 12,457 for self-reported analysis. The study examined socio-demographic and maternal characteristics, including residence, wealth index, education, parity, body mass index (BMI), and pregnancy status. Analyses incorporated sampling weights and survey design. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and country-specific multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify independent predictors.ResultsMalaria prevalence was higher by self-report (33.5%) than by RDT diagnosis (25.2%), with consistently higher self-reported estimates across Tanzania (31.7% vs. 6.9%), Burkina Faso (58.3% vs. 27.4%), and Côte d’Ivoire (47.1% vs. 36.9%). RDT-confirmed malaria showed stronger and more consistent associations with key risk factors, including rural residence (in Burkina Faso aOR = 3.58, in Côte d’Ivoire aOR = 3.64, in Mozambique aOR = 5.74, in Tanzania aOR = 12.70; all p < 0.001), wealth status has lower malaria risk (richest quintile: Burkina Faso aOR = 0.43, Côte d’Ivoire aOR = 0.26, Mozambique aOR = 0.14, Tanzania aOR = 0.27), and while pregnancy status has significant risk (in Burkina Faso p = 0.008, in Côte d’Ivoire p = 0.005, in Cameroon p = 0.006, and in Mozambique p < 0.001), compared with weaker and sometimes inconsistent self-reported associations. The RDT- malaria diagnosis produced more plausible and statistically robust associations (in Mozambique age 25–34: aOR = 1.32, p = 0.040; in Burkina Faso parity two births: aOR = 1.33, p < 0.001), whereas self-reported malaria showed weaker or contradictory effects suggesting potential misclassification and reporting bias in self-reported diagnosis.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that important methodological and epidemiological differences exist between self-reported malaria and malaria RDT diagnosis across sub-Saharan African countries. Self-reported malaria showed overestimated malaria prevalence and produced weaker and less consistent associations with established risk factors, indicating limited reliability for identifying true malaria infection patterns. In contrast, malaria RDT diagnosis generated more coherent and epidemiologically plausible associations with rural residence, socioeconomic status, nutritional status, and pregnancy, reflecting more accurately the biological and environmental determinants of malaria transmission.Keywords: Malaria, Children under five, Sub-Saharan Africa, Socio-demographic determinants, DHS, Multi-country analysis

Introduction

Malaria remains one of the most significant public health challenges globally, with sub-Saharan Africa bearing a disproportionate share of the burden [1, 2]. Globally, malaria caused an estimated 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths across 80 endemic countries, with the WHO African Region accounting for 95% of cases (265 million) and deaths (579,000), while children under five years constituted 76% of malaria deaths. Despite substantial progress in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment over the past two decades, the region continues to account for the vast majority of global malaria cases and deaths [3, 4]. Young children and pregnant women remain particularly vulnerable due to biological susceptibility and socio-environmental exposures [5, 6]. Malaria in early childhood contributes to anemia, impaired growth, neurocognitive deficits, and increased mortality, while infection during pregnancy is associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including low birth weight and preterm delivery [7, 8].

Although regional malaria trends have improved in some countries, progress has been uneven [9]. Transmission intensity, ecological conditions, vector distribution, health system capacity, and implementation of control strategies vary widely across sub-Saharan African countries [10, 11]. As a result, malaria prevalence and its determinants differ substantially between and within countries. Understanding these contextual differences is essential for designing effective, targeted interventions that align with local epidemiological realities.

Socio-demographic and socioeconomic factors play a critical role in shaping malaria risk [12, 13]. Rural residence is often associated with higher exposure due to environmental conditions favorable for mosquito breeding and limited access to preventive services [14, 15]. Household wealth influences housing quality, ownership and utilization of insecticide-treated nets, healthcare-seeking behavior, and access to prompt treatment [16, 17]. Maternal characteristics such as educational attainment, parity, nutritional status, and pregnancy may also modify malaria risk through behavioral, biological, and immunological pathways [18, 19]. However, evidence on the consistency and magnitude of these associations across different African settings remains mixed [20].

Previous studies have frequently focused on single-country analyses, limiting cross-national comparability and generalizability [21, 22]. Multi-country assessments provide an opportunity to examine both shared and context-specific determinants of malaria infection, thereby informing regionally coordinated yet locally tailored malaria control strategies. Such comparative analyses are particularly relevant in the context of ongoing global malaria elimination efforts, which increasingly emphasize stratified and data-driven approaches.

This study therefore aimed to assess the prevalence and determinants of malaria infection among children across seven sub-Saharan African countries. Specifically, we examined socio-demographic characteristics, evaluated cross-country variation in malaria burden, and identified country-specific risk factors using multivariable logistic regression models. By highlighting patterns of heterogeneity and commonalities across settings, this study seeks to provide evidence to guide targeted interventions and strengthen malaria control policies in diverse endemic contexts.

Methodology

Study design and data source

This study employed a cross-sectional analytical design using secondary data obtained from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) or Malaria Indicator Surveys (MIS). The DHS Program conducts nationally representative household surveys in low- and middle-income countries using standardized questionnaires and rigorous sampling procedures [23]. The surveys collect data on population health, maternal and child health, nutrition, and infectious diseases, including malaria. For this analysis, the most recent DHS datasets from six sub-Saharan African countries were utilized; from Burkina Faso (2021), Côte d’Ivoire (2021), Mozambique (2022–2023), Kenya (2022), Senegal (2023), and Tanzania (2022) and MIS datasets from Cameroon (2022). The included countries were selected based on the availability of recent DHS-8 datasets with harmonized malaria outcome measures and complete covariate information required for the analysis. Only countries with comparable data structures allowing extraction of both rapid diagnostic test (RDT) results and/or standardized maternal reports of child malaria were retained to ensure consistency across the two analytic frameworks. In addition, inclusion required the availability of key independent variables, including socioeconomic, maternal, and child-level characteristics, without substantial missingness after merging the children’s (KR) and Household Member (Person) (PR) recode files. Countries were therefore retained only where data completeness and variable harmonisation permitted valid cross-country comparisons under both diagnostic approaches. This selection process also ensured representation across major sub-Saharan African regions while maintaining methodological comparability and minimizing bias introduced by incomplete or non-aligned survey modules.

RDT diagnosis

This analysis included DHS datasets from Burkina Faso (2021), Côte d’Ivoire (2021), Mozambique (2022–2023), Tanzania, (2022) and MIS datasets from Cameroon (2022). The analysis combined the household recode (PR) and children recode (KR) files through a one-to-one merge using cluster number, household number, the child’s line number, and the mother’s line number.

Self-reported malaria

This analysis included DHS datasets from Burkina Faso (2021), Côte d’Ivoire (2021), Mozambique (2022–2023), Kenya (2022), Senegal (2023), Tanzania (2022), and MIS datasets from Cameroon (2022). The analysis relied on the children recode (KR) file, focusing on children under five and their mothers.

Sampling procedure

The study applied a stratified two-stage cluster sampling framework inherent to DHS surveys, incorporating sampling weights, primary sampling units, and strata. In the first stage, enumeration areas (EAs) are selected from national sampling frames derived from recent population censuses using probability proportional to size. In the second stage, households are systematically selected within each EA. Eligible women aged 15–49 years residing in selected households are interviewed using standardized questionnaires. For this study, women with complete information on key variables of interest, including child malaria status and selected socio-demographic characteristics, were included in the analysis. Sampling weights provided by DHS were applied to account for the complex survey design and ensure nationally representative estimates.

Sample collection and laboratory procedures

Capillary blood samples were collected via finger prick by trained DHS field technicians following standardized DHS biomarker protocols. Malaria infection was assessed using rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) in the five countries that included biomarker testing in the survey. The tests were conducted in the field according to manufacturer and DHS protocol guidelines, and results were read within 15–20 min.

Data management and statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using Stata/SE 17.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, Texas 77845, USA). Before analysis, we applied dataset weighting while accounting for the primary sampling unit and sampling errors, and survey weights, clustering, and stratification were accounted for using the svyset command (v005, v021, and v022 variables). This adjustment was implemented to mitigate potential sampling bias. To account for the complex sampling design of the various DHS files, the survey design was handled by utilizing the complex survey command “svyset” in STATA, which incorporates information regarding strata and primary sampling units (PSUs). This approach ensures that the estimates derived from the analysis accurately reflect the population characteristics and account for the survey’s sampling methodology. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize socio-demographic characteristics and malaria prevalence. Weighted frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables. All covariates were selected a priori based on established literature and conceptual relevance to malaria risk, and no variable was included based on bivariate screening. Maternal and child characteristics, including maternal age (included as a standard confounder), residence, wealth index, education, parity, birth order, body mass index (BMI), and pregnancy status, were entered simultaneously into the multivariable logistic regression models. Maternal BMI was included as a proxy for nutritional status potentially influencing susceptibility to infection, while pregnancy status was included due to known immunological and exposure-related changes during pregnancy. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed, particularly for closely related variables such as parity and birth order, using standard diagnostic tests, and no significant collinearity was detected. Bivariate analyses were conducted using chi-square (χ²) tests to examine associations between malaria infection and independent variables within each country. Variables with statistical significance at p < 0.05 were considered associated at the bivariate level. Multivariable logistic regression models were then fitted separately for each country to identify independent predictors of malaria infection. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Throughout both bivariate and multivariate logistic regression, “svy” was used before each analysis (i.e., we used svy: logit variable, i. covariate, odd ratio and svy: tab variable covariate, row).

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

The analytic samples for self-reported malaria (n = 12,457) and RDT-confirmed malaria (n = 20,323). Age distribution differs by measurement: both self-reported and RDT data included younger children (0–4 years). Sex distribution remains balanced in both datasets with 50.8% and 50.3% male. Maternal characteristics show consistent patterns. Most women fall within 25–34 years, and rural residence dominates in both samples (63.3% self-reported; 66.7% RDT). Educational attainment skews toward lower levels, although the proportion with no education is higher in the self-reported group (54.7% vs. 44.9%). Wealth distribution remains relatively even, but the RDT sample shows a slight concentration in poorer quintiles. Fertility patterns differ: one birth dominates in self-reported data (58.8%), whereas the RDT sample shows a more even split between one (49.6%) and two births (43.8%). BMI distributions are comparable, with normal weight most common, though the RDT sample shows a higher proportion of overweight individuals. Malaria prevalence diverges substantially by measurement method. Self-reported diagnosis indicates a higher burden (33.5%), whereas RDT-confirmed prevalence is markedly lower (25.2%), suggesting potential overreporting or misclassification in self-reports. Pregnancy status remains similar across both groups (8–10% pregnant) (Table 1).

Distribution of malaria status by country

Malaria prevalence varies consistently across countries when comparing self-reported diagnosis with RDT confirmation. In all countries presented, self-reported malaria is higher than RDT-confirmed malaria, indicating systematic overestimation in caregiver reports. Tanzania reports substantially lower levels, with 31.7% self-reported and 6.9% RDT-positive, showing the largest discrepancy between perceived and confirmed infection. Burkina Faso shows the highest burden in both measures, with self-reported 58.3% and RDT-positive 27.4%, followed by Côte d’Ivoire with 47.1% and 36.9% (Fig. 1).

Determinants of malaria infection by Sub-Saharan African countries

Malaria-related associations differ depending on whether outcomes are based on self-reported diagnosis or RDT confirmation. Across countries, place of residence shows inconsistent patterns under self-reports, with significance only in Burkina Faso (χ²=24.09, p < 0.001), Kenya (p < 0.001), and Mozambique (p = 0.013), while Côte d’Ivoire (p = 0.278), Cameroon (p = 0.51), Tanzania (p = 0.772), and Senegal (p = 0.59) are not significant. In contrast, RDT-confirmed malaria shows a consistent rural disadvantage across all countries, with stronger test statistics (χ²=38–125, p < 0.001 in all cases), indicating a stable pattern not captured in several self-reported analyses. Wealth quintile shows a similar divergence. Self-reported malaria is significant in Burkina Faso (p = 0.006), Côte d’Ivoire (p = 0.002), Kenya (p = 0.028), and Mozambique (p = 0.021), but not in Cameroon (p = 0.335), Tanzania (p = 0.222), or Senegal (p = 0.381). RDT results show significance across all countries (p ≤ 0.003), with consistently higher positivity in the poorest quintile, confirming a stronger and more uniform socioeconomic gradient. Parity shows no association in self-reported data across all countries (p ≥ 0.054 in all cases; Burkina Faso borderline at p = 0.086). However, RDT-confirmed malaria shows significant associations in Burkina Faso (p = 0.03), Côte d’Ivoire (p = 0.006), Mozambique (p < 0.001), and Tanzania (p = 0.007), with no association in Cameroon (p = 0.628). This indicates that self-reports fail to detect reproductive risk differentials that emerge under biological testing. Body mass index shows no significant association under self-reported malaria in any country (Burkina Faso p = 0.054; Côte d’Ivoire p = 0.344; Kenya p = 0.231; Mozambique p = 0.231; Tanzania p = 0.431). In contrast, RDT-confirmed malaria shows significant associations in Burkina Faso (p < 0.001), Côte d’Ivoire (p < 0.001), Mozambique (p = 0.03), and Tanzania (p = 0.007), with consistently higher infection among underweight women. For current pregnancy, self-reported malaria is significant in Burkina Faso (p = 0.006), Côte d’Ivoire (p = 0.028), and Senegal (p = 0.001), but not in Cameroon (p = 0.564), Mozambique (p = 0.406), or Tanzania (p = 0.889). RDT results show significance in Burkina Faso (p = 0.007), Côte d’Ivoire (p = 0.005), Cameroon (p = 0.006), and Mozambique (p < 0.001), with no association in Tanzania (p = 0.677). In countries with only self-reported data, Kenya shows significant associations for residence (p < 0.001) and wealth (p = 0.028), while Senegal shows significance only for pregnancy (p = 0.001), with no other significant relationships. RDT-based analysis produces stronger and more consistent associations across socioeconomic, reproductive, and nutritional variables. Self-reported malaria underestimates key relationships, particularly for BMI and parity, and fails to detect several country-level patterns that emerge under parasitological confirmation (Table 2).

Determinants of malaria infection by Sub-Saharan African countries

Country-specific risk factors for malaria infection

Malaria-associated risk factors differ markedly between self-reported and RDT-confirmed analyses across Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Cameroon, Mozambique, and Tanzania. Maternal age shows no consistent pattern across either method. Self-reported models identify only a weak and non-replicable association in Kenya (25–34 years: cOR = 0.75, p = 0.022), while all other countries show null effects. RDT results remain similarly inconsistent: Cameroon shows reduced odds in crude analysis (cOR = 0.67, p = 0.001) with borderline adjustment (aOR = 0.78, p = 0.054), while Mozambique shows increased odds among women aged 25–34 (aOR = 1.32, p = 0.040). No stable age gradient emerges. Place of residence shows strong divergence between methods. Self-reported malaria is associated with rural residence in Burkina Faso (aOR = 1.88, p < 0.001), Kenya (aOR = 2.53, p < 0.001), and Mozambique (aOR = 1.65, p = 0.012), but not in Côte d’Ivoire, Cameroon, Tanzania, or Senegal. In contrast, RDT-confirmed malaria shows a consistent and strong rural effect across all countries, with markedly elevated odds (aOR range: 2.74 in Cameroon to 12.70 in Tanzania; p < 0.001 in all cases). This indicates a universal rural risk that self-reports partially miss. Wealth quintile demonstrates the clearest methodological contrast. Under self-reports, Burkina Faso and Côte d’Ivoire show higher odds in wealthier groups (in Burkina Faso richer: aOR = 1.66; Côte d’Ivoire richest: aOR = 2.08), whereas Kenya and Mozambique show protective effects (aOR = 0.46–0.56), and Cameroon, Tanzania, and Senegal show no significance. RDT results, however, show a consistent protective gradient in all countries, with progressively lower odds of malaria among higher wealth quintiles (richest aOR: Burkina Faso = 0.43, Côte d’Ivoire = 0.26, Cameroon = 0.36, Mozambique = 0.14, Tanzania = 0.27; all p < 0.05). This confirms a uniform socioeconomic gradient only under biological diagnosis. Parity shows inconsistent and often contradictory patterns. Self-reported data indicate sporadic effects, including protection in Burkina Faso for ≥ 3 births (aOR = 0.43, p = 0.007), protection in Cameroon for two births (aOR = 0.58, p = 0.014), and increased risk in Tanzania for ≥ 4 births (aOR = 6.73, p = 0.027), with no pattern elsewhere. RDT results instead show more coherent risk at moderate parity: Burkina Faso (two births: aOR = 1.33, p < 0.001), Mozambique (two births: aOR = 1.43; three births: aOR = 1.83), and Tanzania (two births: aOR = 1.41, p = 0.038). This suggests self-reports are unstable and directionally inconsistent. BMI shows a clear reversal between methods. In self-reported data, Burkina Faso shows higher odds among normal, overweight, and obese women compared with underweight (aOR = 1.67–2.83), while other countries show no consistent pattern. In RDT-confirmed data, the association reverses: higher BMI categories are protective in Burkina Faso and Côte d’Ivoire (aOR = 0.38–0.67), indicating higher risk among underweight women. Mozambique and Tanzania show no significant associations. Current pregnancy also differs by diagnostic method. Self-reported malaria shows increased odds in Burkina Faso (aOR = 1.51, p = 0.018) and Côte d’Ivoire (aOR = 1.66, p = 0.018), a protective effect in Senegal (aOR = 0.23, p = 0.002), and no association elsewhere. RDT results show a more consistent pattern, with higher odds in Burkina Faso (aOR = 1.70, p < 0.001), Côte d’Ivoire (aOR = 1.57, p = 0.001), Cameroon (aOR = 1.38, p = 0.034), and Mozambique (aOR = 2.22, p < 0.001), while Tanzania remains non-significant. In Kenya and Senegal, where only self-reported data are available, Kenya shows associations with rural residence, higher wealth (protective), and no stable effects for other variables, while Senegal shows a protective association for pregnancy only (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

This multi-country study demonstrates important methodological and epidemiological differences between self-reported malaria and malaria RDT diagnosis among children across sub-Saharan Africa. Although both measures identified a substantial malaria burden, self-reported malaria showed a higher prevalence estimates than malaria RDT diagnosis, suggesting systematic overestimation of infection when relying on caregiver or maternal reporting [24, 25]. This discrepancy likely reflects the common tendency in high-transmission settings to classify febrile illness as malaria in the absence of parasitological confirmation [26]. Consequently, self-reported malaria may capture a broader range of febrile illnesses, healthcare-seeking behaviours, and community perceptions of disease rather than true malaria infection [27].

Associations between self-reported malaria and key predictors such as residence, wealth, parity, body mass index, and pregnancy status were often inconsistent across countries and occasionally implausible. These inconsistencies likely arise from recall bias, differential symptom recognition, presumptive treatment practices, and variations in cultural understanding of malaria [28, 29]. As a result, self-reported measures may introduce substantial misclassification and distort the interpretation of epidemiological risk patterns [30].

In contrast, malaria RDT diagnosis produced stronger, more coherent, and epidemiologically plausible associations across countries. Rural residence emerged as one of the most consistent predictors of malaria infection, particularly in Burkina Faso, Kenya, and Mozambique. This finding aligns with established evidence that rural populations are often more exposed to mosquito breeding sites, agricultural activities, and environmental conditions favourable for malaria transmission, while simultaneously facing reduced access to healthcare and preventive interventions [12, 31, 32]. The absence of significant rural–urban differences in some countries may reflect improved intervention coverage in rural communities, increasing urban malaria transmission, or country-specific ecological differences [33].

Socioeconomic status also demonstrated clearer associations under RDT diagnosis. In several countries, children from wealthier households had lower odds of malaria infection, supporting evidence that improved housing conditions, access to insecticide-treated nets, healthcare utilisation, and preventive services reduce malaria exposure [34, 35]. However, the relationship between wealth and malaria was not entirely uniform. In Côte d’Ivoire, higher wealth status was associated with increased malaria prevalence, possibly reflecting ecological clustering, urban transmission dynamics, or differences in healthcare access and diagnostic testing [36]. These findings suggest that the relationship between socioeconomic status and malaria risk is context-specific and influenced by local environmental and health system factors.

The relationship between nutritional status and malaria further illustrates the superiority of confirmation. Malaria RDT diagnosis showed more plausible associations, particularly among underweight women, consistent with evidence linking malnutrition and impaired immune function to increased susceptibility to malaria infection [37, 38]. In contrast, self-reported malaria demonstrated inconsistent or contradictory relationships with body mass index, suggesting that subjective illness reporting may not accurately reflect meaningful risk patterns.

Similarly, reproductive factors such as pregnancy status and parity were more consistently associated with malaria under RDT diagnosis. Pregnancy remained independently associated with malaria in Burkina Faso, which is consistent with evidence that pregnancy-related immunological changes increase susceptibility to malaria infection in endemic settings [19, 39]. However, several factors may contribute to this relationship, including shared household and environmental exposures that increase malaria risk for both pregnant women and young children, as well as socioeconomic and demographic factors that may influence susceptibility to infection. The observed association may reflect overlapping risk environments and residual confounding rather than a direct effect of maternal pregnancy status on childhood malaria. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, causal inferences cannot be established, and further longitudinal research is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of this association. Furthermore, pregnancy-related physiological changes may be indicative of broader household vulnerabilities in malaria-endemic settings. The weaker or inconsistent associations observed in self-reported analyses suggest that subjective reporting may obscure important risk differentials. Although parity showed some associations at the bivariate level, these relationships were generally attenuated in multivariable analyses, indicating that parity may function more as a proxy for broader socioeconomic and demographic conditions than as an independent determinant of malaria risk.

These findings highlight the critical importance of diagnostic method selection in malaria research and surveillance. Self-reported malaria may be useful for understanding perceived illness burden, treatment-seeking behaviour, and community perceptions of disease, particularly in settings where diagnostic resources are limited [40]. However, it is a poor proxy for true malaria prevalence and may produce biased estimates of epidemiological associations. In contrast, malaria RDT diagnosis provides a more robust and valid measure of infection, yielding patterns that align more closely with established malaria transmission dynamics [24].

The study also highlights substantial heterogeneity in malaria prevalence and associated risk factors across countries, emphasizing the need for context-specific malaria control strategies. No single determinant consistently predicted malaria across all settings, indicating that interventions should be tailored to local ecological, socioeconomic, and health system realities rather than relying on generalized regional assumptions [41, 42].

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the use of large nationally representative DHS datasets from multiple sub-Saharan African countries, which enhanced statistical power and enabled cross-country comparisons across diverse epidemiological contexts. The inclusion of both self-reported and malaria RDT diagnosis measures provided a unique opportunity to evaluate how diagnostic approaches influence estimates of prevalence and associated risk factors. The application of survey-adjusted multivariable regression models also strengthened the validity of the findings by accounting for complex sampling designs and potential confounding factors.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional nature of the DHS data limits causal inference and prevents assessment of temporal relationships between predictors and malaria infection. Second, self-reported malaria is vulnerable to recall bias and misclassification, although this limitation was partly mitigated through comparison with malaria RDT diagnosis outcomes [27]. Third, differences in survey years across countries may have introduced temporal heterogeneity in malaria transmission patterns and intervention coverage. Fourth, restricting analyses to complete cases reduced sample size and may have introduced selection bias. Fifth, a major limitation of malaria RDT diagnosis; its accuracy can be affected by persistent parasite antigens after treatment, low parasite density, and pfhrp2/pfhrp3 gene deletions, which may result in false-positive or false-negative findings and consequently misclassification of true malaria infection status.

A further limitation is that the two analytical samples were not fully comparable. The RDT-diagnosis analysis included data from only five countries, whereas the self-reported malaria analysis included seven countries, with Kenya and Senegal represented exclusively in the latter. As a result, differences observed between self-reported malaria and malaria RDT diagnosis may not be attributable solely to the diagnostic method. Instead, they may partly reflect differences in the countries and populations included in each analysis, including variations in malaria epidemiology, intervention coverage, healthcare access, and socioeconomic conditions. Therefore, direct comparisons between the two analytical approaches should be interpreted with caution, as the lack of identical country representation may have introduced geographic and contextual heterogeneity that influenced estimates of malaria prevalence and associated risk factors. Finally, several important contextual factors, including insecticide-treated net use, indoor residual spraying, seasonal variation, local transmission intensity, and intermittent preventive treatment during pregnancy, were unavailable or inconsistently measured across surveys and therefore could not be incorporated into the analyses.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that important methodological and epidemiological differences exist between self-reported malaria and malaria RDT diagnosis across sub-Saharan African countries. Self-reported malaria higher malaria prevalence and produced weaker and less consistent associations with established risk factors, indicating limited reliability for identifying true malaria infection patterns. In contrast, malaria RDT diagnosis generated more coherent and epidemiologically plausible associations with rural residence, socioeconomic status, nutritional status, reflecting more accurately the biological and environmental determinants of malaria transmission.

The maternal pregnancy status was associated with malaria among children under five years of age. The findings highlight that reliance on self-reported malaria alone may lead to biased prevalence estimates and misleading epidemiological inferences, particularly in studies assessing determinants of infection and evaluating malaria control interventions. Although self-reported malaria remains useful for understanding perceived illness burden and healthcare-seeking behaviour, parasitological confirmation through RDTs provides a more robust measure for surveillance and research purposes.

The study also underscores the substantial heterogeneity in malaria burden and associated risk factors across countries, emphasizing the need for context-specific malaria control strategies rather than uniform regional approaches. Strengthening access to accurate malaria diagnostics and integrating parasitological confirmation into large-scale population surveys will be essential for improving malaria surveillance, guiding targeted interventions, and supporting evidence-based malaria control policies across sub-Saharan Africa.
