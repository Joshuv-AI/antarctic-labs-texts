24-hour movement behaviors patterns and their influencing factors in patients with coronary heart disease: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Xiaofeng Peng, Lingli Zhang, Ting Guo, Yongqi Dan, Ying Peng, Ting Cheng, Siyue Tang, Guanggao Zhao, Hui Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAgainst the backdrop of comprehensive health benefits associated with 24-hour movement behaviors for cardiac rehabilitation in patients with coronary heart disease, and utilizing objective accelerometer data, this study aims to identify the potential classes of 24-hour movement behaviors (encompassing moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, light physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep) among patients with coronary heart diseas. Furthermore, it seeks to preliminarily clarify which factors are associated with different classes of activity behavior patterns, in order to inform the development of personalized daily activity management strategies.DesignA cross-sectional study design.MethodsFrom March to September 2025, patients with coronary artery disease who had been discharged for more than one month were recruited from the cardiology outpatient department of a tertiary hospital in Nanchang, China, using convenience sampling. A total of 308 participants were enrolled, all of whom provided written informed consent. Data collection involved questionnaire surveys using a general information form, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia for Heart Disease. Objective movement behavior data, including time spent in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, light physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep, were continuously collected over 7 days using a triaxial accelerometer (ActiGraph GT3X-BT, Pensacola, Florida, USA). Latent class analysis was employed to identify subtypes of movement behavior patterns, and multivariable logistic regression models were used to analyze the associated influencing factors.ResultsThis study identified three latent subtypes of 24-hour movement behaviors in patients with coronary artery disease: a Sedentary-Low Activity subtype (49.35%), a Low-Intensity Active subtype (28.25%), and an Active subtype (22.40%). The Sedentary-Low Activity subtype was characterized by the longest sedentary time and the shortest durations of physical activity among the groups. In contrast, the Active subtype exhibited the highest level of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. The Low-Intensity Active subtype was distinguished by the highest amount of light-intensity physical activity, presenting a relatively balanced profile across all behavioral dimensions.Using the Sedentary-Low Activity subtype as the reference, patients with better cardiac function (OR = 13.007, P < 0.001) and lower levels of kinesiophobia (OR = 0.902, P = 0.001) were more likely to be classified into the Active subtype. Gender and smoking history were not significant predictors for this subtype (P > 0.05). Female patients were most likely to be classified into the Low-Intensity Active subtype (OR = 2.375, P = 0.020). Compared to patients with NYHA class III, those with NYHA class 0 also had a higher probability of belonging to the Low-Intensity Active subtype (OR = 3.756, P = 0.034). The kinesiophobia score and smoking history did not show independent predictive effects for this subtype (P > 0.05).ConclusionThe 24-hour movement behaviors of coronary heart disease patients exhibit heterogeneity. Healthcare providers should conduct comprehensive 24-hour movement behavior assessments for coronary heart disease patients, identify their activity patterns, and develop personalized management and intervention strategies based on these patterns. This approach may improve patients’ daily activity behavior and reduce the risk of readmission.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06179-9.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases are one of the leading causes of death worldwide. According to the World Health Organization, cardiovascular dFit indices and category probabilities of models with different number of categoriesModeliseases account for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually, representing over 31% of global deaths, making them a significant public health threat worldwide [1]. In response to this challenge, relying solely on the traditional “disease treatment” model is insufficient. Research indicates that by actively controlling all behavioral and metabolic risk factors, it is theoretically possible to prevent 82.1% of deaths from atherosclerotic heart disease, potentially saving approximately 8.7 million lives each year [2]. Although current standard exercise rehabilitation programs for coronary heart disease (CAD) can personalize treatment based on disease severity, physical fitness, and exercise capacity [3], existing cardiac rehabilitation research has yet to fully explore the synergistic and cumulative effects of physical activity (PA), sedentary behavior (SB), and sleep (SP).

In recent years, multiple studies have emphasized the need to expand the perspective when examining the relationship between physical activity and health outcomes, extending it to the entire 24-hour movement cycle [4–6]. In this context, scientifically intervening in 24-hour movement behavior has been considered a key strategy. The 24-hour movement behavior encompasses the overall time allocation of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), LPA, SB and SP throughout the day [7], with these three behaviors being interconnected and mutually influencing each other, thereby jointly affecting health outcomes [8]. A review of the literature reveals that current research on the activity behaviors of CAD patients has primarily focused on examining the influencing factors of single or dual behavioral variables, often neglecting the integrated nature of 24-hour movement behaviors [9–11].

Latent profile analysis is a person-centered statistical method [12]. Unlike variable-centered approaches, it clusters individuals based on their response patterns across multiple behavioral dimensions (such as time spent in different intensity PA, SB, and SP duration), thereby systematically identifying subgroups with similar behavioral profiles. This method has been applied to identify 24-hour movement behavior patterns in various populations [13, 14]. Therefore, this study aim to preliminarily clarify which factors are associated with different classes of activity behavior patterns, in order to inform the development of personalized daily activity management strategies.

Methods

Study population

This study is a cross-sectional investigation conducted between March and September 2025. Participants were continuously recruited from the cardiology outpatient department of a tertiary hospital in Nanchang. The inclusion criteria for participants were: (1) age over 18 years; (2) meeting the diagnostic criteria for coronary heart disease as outlined in the 2024 ESC Chronic Coronary Syndromes Management Guidelines [15]; (3) clear consciousness, normal cognitive ability, hearing, and language expression; (4) voluntary participation in the study with signed informed consent; (5) willingness to wear a triaxial accelerometer. The exclusion criteria were: (1) heart function grade IV; (2) concomitant severe organic diseases; (3) concomitant fractures, joint disorders, or other diseases severely affecting physical activity; (4) inability to perform activities of daily living independently; (5) withdrawal from the study or significant missing data during the study.In addition, participants who withdrew from the study or had substantial missing data were classified as dropouts and excluded from the final analysis according to predefined data completeness criteria.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was determined based on general recommendations from methodological literature on Latent Profile Analysis. The literature suggests that to ensure model stability and the identifiability of latent classes, each identified class should typically include at least 50 participants. Based on a preliminary assessment of existing 24-hour movement behavior profiles, it was anticipated that 2–4 latent classes might be identified. The theoretical minimum sample size was therefore calculated as 4 × 50 = 200 participants. Accounting for an anticipated 20% attrition rate, the adjusted minimum target sample size was 200 / (1 − 0.20) = 250 participants. Ultimately, 308 patients with CAD were successfully enrolled in this study [16].

Measurement

Activity behavior measurement

A triaxial accelerometer (ActiGraph GT3X-BT, Pensacola, Florida, USA) was used to measure 24-hour movement behaviors in patients with CAD. The device was typically worn on the hip and removed only during water‑based activities (e.g., bathing or swimming). The ActiGraph GT3X‑BT records body movement intensity at a specified sampling frequency, generating activity counts that were subsequently classified into MVPA, LPA, and SB using validated count cut‑points. SP was estimated by combining visual inspection of the accelerometry data—performed directly within the “Sleep” module of the ActiLife software, which is regarded as the optimal reference method for accelerometer‑based sleep analysis—with participant‑completed sleep diaries to improve the accuracy of identifying bedtime and wake time. Total time spent in each behavior was then calculated.

Covariates

A self-administered questionnaire designed for this study was distributed in person to collect general information. The questionnaire included the following items: age, gender, education level, employment status, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, primary caregiver, body mass index (BMI), number of comorbidities, number of stents implanted, and cardiac function classification. Cardiac function was assessed by the investigators according to the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification criteria [17]: class I indicates no limitation of physical activity; class II indicates slight limitation during ordinary activity, with symptoms provoked by normal exertion and relieved quickly with rest; class III indicates marked limitation during less-than-ordinary activity, with symptoms appearing below normal activity levels and requiring prolonged rest for relief; and class IV indicates symptoms at rest, with inability to carry out any physical activity without discomfort. A higher class denotes worse cardiac function and exercise tolerance.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

The PSQI was developed by Buysse et al. at the University of Pittsburgh [18, 19]. Formal permission to use the PSQI for this study was obtained from the Office of Research Protections at the University of Pittsburgh. It is widely used due to its simplicity and the high correlation with results from polysomnography tests, making it one of the most commonly applied sleep self-assessment scales both domestically and internationally. The scale includes seven dimensions: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medications, and daytime dysfunction. The total score ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. Xianchen Liu et al. [20]. tested the reliability and validity of the scale, and the overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the PSQI was found to be 0.84.

The tampa scale for kinesiophobia - heart (TSK-SV Heart)

The TSK-SV Heart was developed by Bäck et al. [21], adapted from the Pain Kinesiophobia Scale. It consists of four dimensions—perceived danger, kinesiophobia, exercise avoidance, and functional impairment—and includes 17 items. The scale uses a 4-point Likert scale, with scores of 1 representing “strongly disagree,” 2 representing “disagree,” 3 representing “agree,” and 4 representing “strongly agree.” Items 2, 8, 12, and 16 are reverse-scored. The total score ranges from 17 to 68, with higher scores indicating a higher level of kinesiophobia in patients. The Chinese version of the scale was translated by Lei Mengjie et al. [22], was obtained from the original authors via email correspondence. This version demonstrated good reliability, with an overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.859.

Data collection

Study protocol

Preparatory work before data collection

Prior to data collection, all investigators involved in the study received standardized training. Preparations included finalizing the questionnaires and initializing the ActiGraph GT3X-BT tri-axial accelerometer sensors.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted during follow-up visits at the cardiovascular outpatient clinic at least one month after patient discharge. Upon the cardiology department director’s assessment confirming that a patient met the inclusion criteria, researchers collaborated with the outpatient clinic director to explain the purpose of the tests. After obtaining informed consent, a WeChat group was established. For patients unwilling to join the group, the researcher’s work WeChat account was added as an alternative contact.

Researchers contacted patients by phone in order of their appointment sequence, after their examinations were completed but before the results were available. At the cardiovascular nursing clinic, the following data and procedures were completed: Collection of NYHA class, kinesiophobia score, and sleep quality score.

Device wearing instructions: Patients were instructed to wear the ActiGraph GT3X-BT tri-axial accelerometer for one continuous week. The device was to be fixed on the waist, positioned above the right iliac crest, and worn at all times except while swimming or bathing. Patients were asked to begin wearing it upon waking the next morning and to maintain their usual daily activities without undertaking additional exercise.Following the instruction session, and after the patient’s blood pressure stabilized at rest, researchers used standardized methods with calibrated equipment to measure blood pressure and BMI.

For participants with reading or writing difficulties, researchers read the questionnaire items aloud, explained their content, and recorded the responses based on the patient’s answers. Upon survey completion, researchers checked the questionnaires on-site to ensure no omissions or errors.After the clinic visit, researchers accessed the hospital system to record the patient’s LDL and HDL levels. On the same evening, an instructional PowerPoint and a video demonstration on device wearing were sent to patients to enhance engagement and trust. Researchers confirmed device wearing compliance with patients daily.

Device recovery

On Day 8 after the outpatient visit, the researcher contacted the participants via WeChat to confirm the time and location for device return. The participants could either return the device themselves or have the researcher collect it in person. Subsequently, upon retrieval of the device, the test data was downloaded and analyzed using the ActiLife software (version 7.0.0).

Data processing

24-hour movement behaviors data processing

Acceleration data were downloaded and processed using ActiLife software (version 7.0.0) with a sampling interval of 15 s [22]. LPA, MVPA, SB, SP were classified according to validated cut-points established by Butte et al. [22, 23]. (SB: counts ≤ 239; LPA: 240 ≤ counts ≤ 2119; MVPA: counts ≥ 2120). A valid monitoring day was defined as a day with at least 10 h of wear time (i.e., > 10 h of awake time in the software-derived data). In accordance with the widely used physical activity data screening criteria proposed by Anderson et al. [24], participants providing at least 3 valid days (including 2 weekdays and 1 weekend day) were included in the final analysis.

Handling of missing data

Outliers were initially identified using the 3σ principle based on the normal distribution. Following outlier detection, appropriate imputation methods were selected according to the distribution characteristics of the data. For approximately symmetric distributions, missing values were imputed using the mean. For skewed distributions, suspected outliers were verified to determine if they represented valid observations. Valid observations were retained, while confirmed abnormal values were replaced with the median. Concurrently, the proportion of missing values was calculated for each record or field. Any record with a missing data rate exceeding 30% was flagged for manual review. Records confirmed to be invalid upon audit were subsequently deleted from the analysis dataset.

Quality control

① Researchers contacted patients and their family members daily at 8:00 to confirm the wearing status of the tri-axial accelerometer sensors and reminded the patients to complete their sleep diaries. Weekly telephone follow-ups were conducted to assess medication adherence.② Based on the specific wearing conditions of the participants, if invalid data occurred due to non-wearing, incorrect wearing, or device malfunction, the participant was contacted for a retest after obtaining their consent. If consent was not given, the participant was excluded from the study.③ Face-to-face surveys were conducted. Investigators used a unified script to ask questions and then filled out the questionnaires based on the respondents’ answers. Before the survey, investigators received training that explained the questionnaire content, helped them master inquiry techniques, standardized the survey procedures, and unified the survey criteria. After collection, the questionnaires were reviewed, and those with missing content or responses inconsistent with the actual situation were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Determination of profile types

After data validation by two independent researchers and double data entry, latent profile analysis was conducted using Mplus 8.11, with 24-hour movement behaviors time of coronary heart disease patients as the indicator variable. The model selection process began with a single-class baseline model, and the number of latent classes was gradually increased. The optimal model was determined based on the criteria proposed by Sinha et al. [25]: (1) Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted BIC (aBIC), with lower values indicating better model fit; (2) entropy (range: 0–1), with values ≥ 0.80 suggesting good class separation; (3) Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test(LMR) and Bootstrap likelihood ratio test(BLRT), where P < 0.05 indicated that the k-class model was superior to the k-1 class model.

Determination of factors influencing profile types

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0. For continuous variables, normality tests were conducted first. Variables that followed a normal distribution were expressed as means ± standard deviation, and group comparisons were made using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For non-normally distributed variables, data were presented as medians (interquartile range), and group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages), with group comparisons made using the chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test. Variables that showed statistical significance in univariate analysis were included in multivariable logistic regression analysis to explore the factors influencing different behavior categories.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 341 questionnaires were distributed, with 308 valid questionnaires recovered, resulting in an effective response rate of 90.32%. Table 1 presents the distribution of general characteristics of the 308 participants. The mean age of the participating patients was 68.62 ± 9.47 years. The majority were male (n = 232, 75.3%), 177 (57.5%) had a history of smoking, and 139 (45.1%) had a history of alcohol consumption. Most participants had a high school/secondary vocational education (n = 87, 28.2%). For daily living, self-care was predominant (n = 264, 85.7%). Most patients were unemployed (n = 237, 76.9%). Nearly half of the patients (n = 146, 47.4%) had one comorbidity. NYHA Class I and Class II were most common, with 116 (37.7%) and 109 (35.4%) cases, respectively. Regarding coronary stents, 161 patients (52.3%) had 1–2 implanted. The mean BMI was 23.72 ± 3.06 kg/m². The mean score for kinesiophobia was 37.97 ± 5.92 points, and the mean score for sleep quality was 6.55 ± 3.57 points. More details can be found in Table 1.

Univariate analysis of factors associated with 24-hour movement behavior categories in patients with CAD

As shown in Table 4, the latent classes of activity behavior patterns were compared in terms of demographic, clinical, and psychological characteristics. The distribution of gender differed significantly among the classes (P < 0.05). The proportion of females was highest in the Low-Intensity Active subtype (34.5%), while males predominated in the Active subtype (87.0%). A significant difference was also found in smoking history (P < 0.05), with the Active subtype having the highest proportion of current smokers (71.0%). However, no significant differences were observed across classes for age, alcohol consumption history, education level, primary caregiver, or occupational status (P > 0.05).The distribution of NYHA class differed significantly among the subtypes (P < 0.001). NYHA class II was most prevalent in the Sedentary-Low Activity subtype (48.7%), whereas the Active subtype had the highest proportion of patients in NYHA class 0 (43.5%). No significant differences were found among classes regarding the number of comorbidities, number of stents implanted, body mass index, blood pressure, blood lipid levels, or left ventricular ejection fraction. Kinesiophobia levels differed significantly across classes (P < 0.001). Patients in the Sedentary-Low Activity subtype reported the highest kinesiophobia scores (39.27 ± 5.73), while those in the Active subtype reported the lowest (35.36 ± 6.34). Sleep quality scores did not differ significantly among the groups.

Discussion

Patients with coronary heart disease exhibit significant group heterogeneity in their 24-hour movement behaviors. This study identified three latent categories: the active type, the sedentary type, and the low-intensity active type. NYHA functional class, kinesiophobia, and gender were further established as key factors distinguishing these subtypes.

The 24-hour movement behaviors of patients with coronary heart disease can be categorized into three latent classes

The Sedentary-Low Activity subtype was the most prevalent pattern (49.35%) and presented the highest behavioral risk profile, characterized by the relatively longest average daily sedentary time (729.97 ± 62.40 min), the lowest moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (14.63 ± 12.34 min), and low light physical activity (189.31 ± 4.28 min). The Active subtype accounted for 22.4%, demonstrating the highest level of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (47.85 ± 27.25 min), lower sedentary time (605.18 ± 91.86 min), and the relatively longest sleep duration. The Low-Intensity Active subtype, an intermediate pattern (28.25%), was primarily characterized by the highest duration of light physical activity (324.41 ± 7.06 min). Further comparison between the Sedentary-Low Activity and Active subtypes revealed that the Active subtype maintained a higher level of physical activity and the longest sleep duration among all groups. In contrast, the Sedentary-Low Activity subtype had the relatively longest sedentary time and a relatively shorter sleep duration. These findings are consistent with research conducted by You et al. [25] in a general population, which indicated that sedentary behavior significantly increases the risk of sleep disturbances, whereas regular exercise has a clear beneficial effect on improving sleep.

Moreover, the behavioral pattern of the Sedentary-Low Activity subtype is inconsistent with the recommendations of the 24-hour movement behavior guidelines for adults [8]. The guidelines recommend that adults achieve 75–150 min of MVPA per week, maintain sleep duration of 7–9 h per day, and limit SB to less than 8 h per day. Patients in the Sedentary-Low Activity subtype exhibit very low levels of MVPA and SP, along with very high SB, constituting a clear multi-faceted health risk. Evidence indicate [26, 27]that compared to inactive patients, participation in any physical activity can significantly reduce the risk of cardiac and all-cause mortality. Furthermore, exceeding the guideline-recommended levels of MVPA provides additional benefits in reducing all-cause mortality risk. Conversely, longer sedentary time is associated with a higher mortality risk in patients with coronary heart disease. Even if patients’ physical activity levels temporarily fail to meet the targets, actively reducing sedentary time itself confers clear benefits. Inadequate sleep can increase the risk of coronary artery calcification, leading to worsening of the patient’s condition [28]. A study published in Nature also suggests that increasing sleep duration and improving sleep quality after myocardial infarction may help reduce cardiac inflammation and injury, promoting heart recovery [29].

In summary, the behavioral pattern of the “Active subtype” is more aligned with the recommendations of the 24-hour activity guidelines. Theoretically, it can, through synergistic effects, provide optimal cardiovascular protection and health outcomes for patients with coronary heart disease and should be considered the ideal target for clinical behavioral interventions.

Patients with the sedentary-low activity subtype were associated with higher levels of kinesiophobia and poorer cardiac function, while the opposite was true for the active subtype

Research indicates that kinesiophobia and NYHA classification are significant predictors of Sedentary-Low Activity subtype and Active subtype physical activity behaviors. A statistically significant association was observed between TSK scores and 24-hour activity behavior patterns (P < 0.05). Using the Sedentary-Low Activity subtype as the reference, each 1-point increase in TSK score reduced the probability of being classified into the Active subtype (OR = 0.902 < 1). This finding aligns with previous studies [30, 31], indicating that higher levels of kinesiophobia in coronary heart disease patients are associated with lower physical activity levels. Goubran et al. [32] also reported a negative correlation between kinesiophobia and physical activity that is prevalent among patients with cardiac, neurological, pulmonary, arthritic conditions, and the elderly; moreover, the incidence of kinesiophobia in cardiac patients is significantly higher than in those with other diseases. Using cardiac function class III as the reference, individuals with normal cardiac function had 13 times the odds (OR = 13.007) of exhibiting an Active subtype over a Sedentary-Low Activity subtype in their 24-hour activity behavior. This may be because cardiac function limitations lead to reduced activity and increased sedentary time. Previous studies have also shown [33] that patients with poorer cardiac function have lower physical activity levels and longer sedentary time, likely due to the close relationship between cardiac function classification and peak VO2 [34]. When cardiac function is poor, insufficient oxygen uptake can easily cause symptoms such as dyspnea, chest tightness, and fatigue, directly leading to decreased exercise tolerance and thereby limiting the ability to engage in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. Wang et al. [35] found that high-intensity interval training is an effective method to improve peak VO2 in coronary heart disease patients. Therefore, healthcare professionals should enhance psychological counseling for patients with kinesiophobia, assisting them in establishing effective psychological defense mechanisms to reduce their fear of movement. Meanwhile, improving patients’ cardiopulmonary capacity and exercise tolerance through standardized medication, exercise rehabilitation, and other comprehensive approaches is key to fundamentally breaking the sedentary cycle and increasing the potential for activity, thereby reducing sedentary behavior in patients.

Female patients tended to be classified into the low-intensity active subtype

Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that gender was a significant influencing factor for the Low-Intensity Active subtype activity behavior classification. Female patients with coronary heart disease had 2.375 times the odds (OR = 2.375 > 1, 95% CI: 1.144 ~ 5.933) of exhibiting the Low-Intensity Active subtype.24-hour activity behavior compared to male patients. This indicates that female patients with coronary heart disease are more inclined towards a behavior pattern primarily characterized by low-intensity activity. In a study by Chen et al. [36]on physical activity patterns in Chinese adults aged 40–75, significant gender differences were found in the composition of physical activity patterns. This difference also exists within the coronary heart disease population, where female patients generally exhibit lower overall activity levels than male patients, and males are more likely to engage in high-intensity physical activities compared to females [37–39].

These differences may result from the fact that, compared to men, women generally have lower lean body mass and muscle mass, as well as smaller muscle fibers [40, 41]. Research has also found [42] that during childhood, boys and girls may be influenced by narrow gender norms and may fail to engage in the types of physical activities offered, which leads to a lack of enjoyment and physical confidence in adulthood. It is important to note that this tendency for women to prefer a low-intensity activity pattern is not an indication of unhealthy or low-benefit activity. Current studies worldwide confirm [43–45] that even low levels of activity can significantly reduce mortality and cardiovascular disease event risks, with these health benefits further strengthened as activity levels increase. Ji et al. [41]found that, regardless of the type of activity or measurement dimension (frequency, duration, intensity, or muscle-strengthening activity frequency), women experience greater health benefits from physical activity compared to men. Further research on coronary heart disease patients [46] confirmed that, when performing equal amounts of aerobic exercise and resistance training, female patients show a significantly greater reduction in mortality risk compared to male patients. Therefore, considering both women’s tendency toward low-intensity physical activity and the differential benefits they gain from physical activity, clinical practice should encourage female coronary heart disease patients to actively participate in various suitable physical activities to improve their overall health.

Implications for policy and practice

The identification of three distinct 24-hour movement behavior profiles offers a granular perspective on patient heterogeneity. This approach enables the stratification of high-risk populations who are most likely to benefit from 24 h movement behavioral therapies, which may inform the refinement of clinical practice and public health policy. For the “Sedentary-Low Activity” subtype, the high prevalence of kinesiophobia and behavioral patterns inconsistent with health guidelines suggest that interventions should prioritize addressing safety concerns and reducing sedentary time, aligning with foundational cardiac rehabilitation principles. For the “Low-Intensity Active” subtype, the focus may remain on sustaining current activity levels while exploring opportunities to incrementally increase intensity, as tolerated.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross‑sectional design cannot establish causal relationships between influencing factors and behavioral patterns, nor can it assess the predictive value of different behavioral patterns for long‑term clinical outcomes such as rehospitalization and mortality. This is the most critical limitation of the present study, implying that the clinical management pathways proposed based on these findings still require validation through prospective research. Second, although we combined objective devices and subjective reports, the assessment of sleep was not equivalent to polysomnography, and patients’ compliance in wearing accelerometers was not perfect, which may have introduced measurement errors. Third, the sample was drawn from a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the results.

Future research priorities

Future investigations should employ large-scale longitudinal cohorts to elucidate the causal pathways linking specific behavioral phenotypes to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Subsequent mechanistic inquiries are warranted to delineate which dimensions of physical activity—including timing, intensity, and fragmentation—mediate optimal health outcomes. Finally, rigorously designed randomized controlled trials are imperative to validate whether interventions tailored to these 24-hour movement behavior profiles confer superior clinical benefits compared to standardized cardiac rehabilitation.

Conclusion

Patients with coronary heart disease exhibit significant group heterogeneity in their 24-hour moveme behaviors. This study identified three latent categories: the active type, the sedentary type, and the low-intensity active type. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that gender, smoking history, NYHA functional class, and kinesiophobia are important influencing factors for distinguishing different behavioral categories. Based on the above findings and referring to the concepts of relevant activity behavior guidelines, future clinical practice may consider comprehensively assessing patients’ 24-hour behavioral patterns and exploring integrated management pathways that address both physiological and psychological factors tailored to different behavioral types. This may provide potential directions for optimizing personalized behavioral interventions and improving long-term health outcomes in patients with coronary heart disease.
