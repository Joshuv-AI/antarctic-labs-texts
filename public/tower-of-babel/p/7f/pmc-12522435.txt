Predictive model of delayed union of tibial and fibular fractures based on PGC-1α, NOS, and Runx2
Authors: Bin Wang, Zhiyuan Shi, Derong Li, Jinwei Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore the associations between the levels of Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1α (PGC-1α), Nitric oxide synthase (NOS), Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) and the delayed union of tibiofibular fractures, and to construct and validate a model for predicting the delayed union of tibiofibular fractures.MethodsA total of 351 patients with tibiofibular fractures who were treated in the hospital from January 2022 to June 2024 were selected as the study subjects. They were randomly divided into a training set (n = 246) and a validation set (n = 105) at a ratio of 7:3. Experimental techniques such as ELISA were used to accurately detect the serum of patients, and multiple clinical data of the patients were comprehensively collected. Key influencing factors were screened out by statistical methods, such as univariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression analysis. Plot a nomogram and construct a prediction model, and use evaluation indicators to comprehensively evaluate and validate the model.ResultsIn the training set, 72 cases (29.27%) had delayed union of tibiofibular fracture. Multivariate logistic analysis showed that fracture complexity, time from injury to surgery, levels of PGC-1α, NOS, Runx2, and OPG were independent influencing factors for the delayed union of tibiofibular fractures (all P < 0.05). In the training set and the validation set, the C-index of the nomogram model was 0.760 and 0.711, respectively. The calibration curve showed moderate agreement between the predicted values and the actual values. The results of the Hosmer–Lemeshow test were χ2 = 5.277, P = 0.728 and χ2 = 10.540, P = 0.229, respectively. The ROC curve showed that in the training set and the validation set, the AUC of the nomogram model for predicting the delayed union of tibiofibular fractures was 0.760 (95%CI 0.680–0.839) and 0.711 (95%CI 0.581–0.842), respectively, and the sensitivity and specificity were 0.796, 0.645 and 0.739, 0.500, respectively.ConclusionsThis study provides a new perspective and method for the assessment of the delayed union of tibiofibular fractures, which is helpful for clinicians to potentially assist in risk stratification and closer follow-up for high-risk patients.Keywords: Tibiofibular fracture, Delayed union, Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1, Nitric oxide synthase, Runt-related transcription factor 2

Introduction

Tibiofibular fractures are a common type of fracture in clinical practice and account for a relatively high proportion of fractures throughout the body [1]. Despite the continuous development of current fracture treatment technologies, delayed fracture union remains a problem that cannot be ignored [2]. It not only prolongs the patient's recovery time, increases the pain and economic burden, but may also lead to serious complications, such as non-union of fractures and infections, which affect the patient's limb function recovery and quality of life [3, 4]. Currently, the prediction of delayed tibiofibular fracture union in clinical practice is mainly based on traditional factors, such as the patient’s age, fracture site, and fracture type. However, the prediction of delayed fracture union by these factors has certain limitations and cannot accurately reflect the complex biological mechanisms during the fracture union process [5]. Therefore, finding new biomarkers and constructing a precise prediction model are of great significance for early identification of high-risk patients with delayed fracture union, formulating personalized treatment plans, and improving the fracture union rate.

Recent advances in bone biology have identified several key molecular pathways governing fracture repair. Emerging evidence from experimental studies suggests that antioxidant compounds may significantly enhance musculoskeletal tissue repair. Notably, vanillic acid has been shown to promote fracture healing in rat femur models through its antioxidative properties [6]. Similarly, quercetin and caffeic acid demonstrated remarkable effects on tendon healing in rat Achilles tendon injury models [7, 8], highlighting the crucial role of redox homeostasis in connective tissue repair. These findings provide important preclinical evidence that molecular mediators of oxidative stress responses—including those regulated by Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ co-activator 1α (PGC-1α) and nitric oxide synthase (NOS)—may serve as critical determinants of healing outcomes. PGC-1α is a key transcriptional co-activator that plays an important role in regulating energy metabolism, cell proliferation, and differentiation and is closely related to the growth, development, and repair of bones [9]. NOS can catalyze the production of nitric oxide, which participates in multiple processes such as angiogenesis, inflammatory response, and cell proliferation during the fracture union process [10]. Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) is a key regulatory factor for osteoblast differentiation and bone formation and is crucial for the formation and mineralization of callus during the fracture union process [11]. However, the relationship between the levels of PGC-1α, NOS, and Runx2 and delayed tibiofibular fracture union has not been fully clarified. This study aims to deeply explore the association of these biomarkers with delayed tibiofibular fracture union, construct and validate a clinically applicable prediction model. Our central hypothesis posits that the expression of these biomarkers combined with key clinical indicators can be used to predict delayed healing.

Materials and methods

Study population

A total of 351 patients with tibiofibular fractures who received treatment in the hospital from January 2022 to June 2024 were selected as the subjects. Inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosed with tibiofibular fractures by imaging examinations, such as X-ray and CT [12], (2) aged between 18 and 70 years, (3) received surgical treatment after fracture, (4) completed at least 9 months of postoperative follow-up with complete radiographic evaluation data, and (5) signed the informed consent form and voluntarily participated in this study. Exclusion criteria: (1) complicated with fractures in other parts, (2) suffered from severe metabolic bone diseases, bone tumors and other diseases that affect fracture union, (3) had severe dysfunction of important organs, such as the heart, liver and kidneys, (4) had used drugs affecting bone metabolism recently, and (5) lost to follow-up or had incomplete follow-up data (< 9 months). For analytical purposes, the 351 patients were post hoc divided into a training set (n = 246) and validation set (n = 105) through computer-generated randomization while ensuring comparable baseline characteristics between groups to minimize confounding effects. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Polytechnic University (No. HNLG0745), and informed consent was obtained from all patients. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Specimen collection

Before the patients' surgeries, 5 ml of fasting venous blood was collected and placed in anticoagulant tubes. The tubes were centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 10 min to separate the serum. The separated serum was then stored in a − 80℃ refrigerator for the detection of PGC-1α, NOS, Runx2, Osteoprotegerin (OPG), Receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL), and sclerostin levels.

Measurement indicators and methods

The serum levels of PGC-1α, NOS, Runx2, OPG, RANKL and sclerostin were quantified using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. The following kits were employed with their respective technical specifications:

(1) PGC-1α: Cloud-Clone Corp (Catalog #SEA704Hu, sensitivity: 0.078 ng/mL, detection range: 0.312–20 ng/mL, intra-assay CV: 4.7%, inter-assay CV: 7.3%). (2) NOS: R&D Systems (Catalog #DY2083-05, sensitivity: 2.34 U/mL, detection range: 7.8–500 U/mL, intra-assay CV: 5.1%, inter-assay CV: 8.9%). (3) Runx2: Cusabio (Catalog #CSB-EL027015HU, sensitivity: 9.8 ng/mL, detection range: 25–1,000 ng/mL, intra-assay CV: 6.2%, inter-assay CV: 8.5%). (4) OPG: BioVendor (Catalog # RD194003200, sensitivity: 0.4 pmol/L, detection range: 0.5–80 pmol/L, intra-assay CV: 4.1%, inter-assay CV: 6.7%). (5) RANKL: Mediagnost (Catalog #E12, sensitivity: 0.02 pmol/L, detection range: 0.2–40 pmol/L, intra-assay CV: 3.8%, inter-assay CV: 5.9%). (6) Sclerostin: TECOmedical (Catalog # A15729, sensitivity: 2.3 pg/mL, detection range: 10–800 pg/mL, intra-assay CV: 4.5%; inter-assay CV: 7.2%). All procedures were strictly performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions provided in the ELISA kit. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the mean value was calculated for subsequent statistical analysis to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the experimental results.

Collection of clinicopathological parameters

Comprehensive clinical data were documented, including: age; gender; mechanism of injury (traffic accident, fall from height, sports-related injury); fracture type (open fracture, closed fracture); fracture complexity (simple fracture, comminuted fracture); time from injury to surgery; fracture location (proximal, middle, or distal segment); underlying comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, etc.); smoking history; and alcohol consumption history.

Fracture union evaluation

Postoperative radiographic examinations were performed at regular intervals, and fracture union was assessed according to standardized imaging criteria. Fracture union was assessed by three independent orthopedic surgeons blinded to patient clinical information and biomarker results. Delayed union was defined as the absence of radiographic evidence of union for at least 9 months after fracture, with no signs of progressive union for three consecutive months. The diagnosis of delayed union was confirmed if the fracture line remained clearly visible, callus formation was minimal, and no continuous trabecular bridging was observed across the fracture site. In contrast, fracture union was considered satisfactory if the fracture line was indistinct and continuous trabecular bridging was evident.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM SPSS, Inc. Chicago, IL, USA) and R 4.5.1. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages), with intergroup comparisons conducted using χ2 tests. Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed by independent samples t test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was employed to identify influencing factors, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05 (the results were presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI)), and variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated to exclude multicollinearity (VIF threshold < 10). Before conducting multivariate logistic regression analysis, stepwise screening method was used for variable selection to determine the final 6 independent variables included in the model. The stepwise screening was based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and variables with AIC reduction were retained step by step to ensure the rationality of variable selection. The “rms” package in R was utilized to construct the nomogram prediction model. Use the “rms” package in R to plot the calibration curve, and use Bootstrap self-sampling 1000 times to correct the curve in the drawing of the calibration curve. Using the C-index to quantify the model discrimination. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was applied to evaluate goodness of fit. Clinical utility was further assessed through decision curve analysis (DCA). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of influencing factors for delayed union of tibial and fibular fractures

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed with delayed union of tibial and fibular fractures as the dependent variable and the factors with statistical significance in the univariate analysis as independent variables (Table 3). The results showed that fracture complexity (OR = 3.539, 95%CI 1.679–7.461) and time from injury to surgery (OR = 1.125, 95%CI 1.019–1.241) are significantly associated with the increased risk of delayed fracture union. The elevated levels of PGC-1α, NOS, Runx2 and OPG were associated with reduced risk of delayed fracture union (all P < 0.05) (Table 4). The results of VIF analysis showed that the VIF values of all variables were less than 5, indicating that there was no significant collinearity among the variables, which could avoid the impact of collinearity on the model results and reduce the risk of overfitting.

Construction of a nomogram model for predicting delayed union of tibiofibular fractures

Based on the factors determined by multivariate Logistic regression analysis, a nomogram model for predicting delayed union of tibiofibular fractures was constructed. In this model, scores were assigned to each independent influencing factor, and the probability of delayed union of tibiofibular fractures was predicted by calculating the total score. A higher total score indicates a greater predicted risk of delayed fractures union (Fig. 1).

Evaluation and validation of the nomogram model for predicting delayed union of tibial and fibular fractures

In the training set and the testing set, the C-index of the prediction model was 0.760 and 0.711, respectively. The calibration curve showed moderate agreement between the predicted values and the actual values. The results of the Hosmer–Lemeshow test were χ2 = 5.277, P = 0.728 and χ2 = 10.540, P = 0.229, respectively. The ROC curve showed that in the training set and the validation set, the AUC of the ROC curve in predicting delayed union of tibial and fibular fractures was 0.760 (95%CI 0.680–0.839) and 0.711 (95%CI 0.581–0.842), respectively. The sensitivity and specificity were 0.796, 0.645 and 0.739, 0.500, respectively. The calibration curve is shown in Fig. 2, and the ROC curve is shown in Fig. 3.

Discussion

The results of this study showed that fracture complexity, time from injury to surgery, PGC-1α level, NOS level, Runx2 level and OPG level were independent influencing factors for delayed tibiofibular fracture union. In terms of fracture complexity, compared with simple fractures, comminuted fractures have more serious damage to the blood supply at the fracture end, with more fracture fragments and greater difficulty in reduction, which is not conducive to fracture union. This is consistent with clinical cognition [13]. The stability of the fracture end is crucial for fracture union. The unstable state of comminuted fractures will interfere with the normal formation and mineralization process of callus, increasing the risk of delayed union.

The time from injury to surgery also has a significant impact on fracture union. In this study, delayed surgery increased the possibility of delayed fracture union (OR = 3.539, 95%CI 1.679–7.461), for every additional 1 day between the injury and the surgery, the probability of delayed fracture healing in the patient increases by 12.5%. After injury, a series of pathophysiological changes will occur in the local tissues of the fracture site, such as inflammatory reaction and repair of vascular injury [14]. As time goes on, the local inflammatory reaction may be over-activated or dysregulated, affecting the normal functions of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, leading to slow or abnormal callus formation, and thus delaying fracture union [15]. In addition, a long wait for surgery may also increase the risk of infection, further disrupting the micro-environment for fracture union. As a key transcriptional co-activator, PGC-1α plays an important role in regulating processes, such as energy metabolism, cell proliferation and differentiation, and is closely related to skeletal growth, development and repair [16]. This study found that elevated level of PGC-1α was associated with reduced risk of delayed fracture union (OR = 0.954, 95%CI 0.918–0.991), indicating that for every 1 ng/mL increase in PGC-1α, the probability of delayed fracture healing decreases by approximately 4.6%. This is consistent with existing research findings. For instance, Hong et al.'s study discovered that serum PGC-1α levels exhibited a positive correlation with the progression of traumatic fracture healing. Specifically, patients with elevated PGC-1α levels demonstrated accelerated fracture healing rates, suggesting that PGC-1α may participate in bone repair through dual mechanisms involving the maintenance of redox homeostasis and energy metabolic balance [17]. During the process of fracture union, a large amount of energy supply is required for the repair of bone tissue. PGC-1α can regulate mitochondrial biosynthesis and energy metabolism pathways, providing energy for the proliferation and differentiation of bone cells and the synthesis of bone matrix [18]. A decrease in its level may lead to energy metabolism disorders, affecting the function of osteoblasts, reducing callus formation and delaying the process of fracture union.

Nitric oxide catalyzed and generated by NOS is involved in multiple processes such as angiogenesis, inflammatory reaction and cell proliferation during fracture union [19]. This study showed that elevated level of NOS was one of the protective factors for delayed fracture union (OR = 0.944, 95%CI 0.901–0.990), indicating that for every 1 U/mL increase in NOS, the probability of delayed fracture healing decreases by approximately 5.6%. This aligns with the findings of Meesters DM et al., whose study demonstrated that deficiencies in both inducible and endothelial nitric oxide synthase reduce bone formation and increase the risk of delayed fracture healing and nonunion, directly validating the critical regulatory role of the NO pathway during bone healing [20]. Nitric oxide can promote the proliferation and migration of vascular endothelial cells, increasing the blood supply at the fracture site and providing necessary nutrients and oxygen for fracture union [21]. At the same time, nitric oxide can also regulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines, and a moderate inflammatory reaction is beneficial to tissue repair and cell recruitment in the early stage of fracture union. A decrease in NOS level will weaken these physiological processes, which is not conducive to fracture union.

Runx2 is a key regulatory factor for osteoblast differentiation and bone formation [22]. During fracture union, Runx2 can initiate the expression of osteoblast-specific genes, promoting the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts and accelerating the formation and mineralization of callus [23]. The elevated level of Runx2 was associated with reduced risk of delayed fracture union in this study (OR = 0.962, 95%CI 0.934–0.992), indicating that for every 1 ng/mL increase in Runx2, the probability of delayed fracture healing decreases by approximately 3.8%. This aligns with the findings of Liu et al., whose study demonstrated that intervention using a stretchable ultrasound array significantly upregulated Runx2 expression levels at fracture sites while accelerating callus maturation and fracture healing progression. Moreover, these results mutually reinforce our study's observation that reduced Runx2 levels contribute to delayed healing, further confirming the pivotal regulatory role of Runx2 in bone repair processes [24], indicating its importance in the process of fracture union. When Runx2 is insufficiently expressed, osteoblast differentiation is blocked, callus formation is slow, and normal fracture union is difficult to achieve.

As an important regulatory factor in bone metabolism, OPG inhibits the activation and proliferation of osteoclasts by binding to RANKL, maintaining the balance between bone resorption and bone formation [25]. In this study, the elevated level of OPG correlate with reduced risk of delayed fracture union (OR = 0.988, 95%CI 0.979–0.997), indicating that for every 1 pg/mL increase in OPG, the probability of delayed fracture healing decreases by approximately 1.2%. This is consistent with the findings of Tian et al., whose study demonstrated that irisin promotes the restoration of bone remodeling homeostasis and healing progression at fracture sites by upregulating OPG expression. This action potentiates OPG's inhibitory effect on RANKL, thereby reducing excessive bone resorption. These results corroborate our study’s observation that decreased OPG levels increase delayed healing risk, further confirming OPG’s central regulatory function in maintaining bone metabolic balance and fracture healing [26]. A decrease in OPG level will weaken the inhibitory effect on RANKL, enhance the activity of osteoclasts, cause excessive bone resorption, disrupt the bone remodeling balance at the fracture site and affect fracture union [27].

Existing clinical studies on predicting tibiofibular fracture delayed union have mainly focused on traditional clinical indicators without integrating biological mechanisms. For example, He M, et al. [28] constructed a prediction model for tibial shaft fracture delayed union using clinical variables, such as fracture type, soft tissue injury grade and postoperative weight-bearing time, and the AUC of the model in the validation set was 0.68. Hetreau et al. [29] developed a predictive tool for tibial fracture delayed union based on surgical factors (e.g., fixator dynamization time), with an internal validation C-index of 0.70, but the model lacked the participation of biological markers and could not reflect the molecular regulatory process of fracture healing. In addition, Schwarzenberg et al. [30] summarized in their review that most clinical prediction models for fracture delayed union rely on postoperative observation indicators (such as callus formation at 3 months after surgery), which means risk stratification can only be carried out 3–6 months after surgery, making it difficult to achieve early intervention for high-risk patients. Compared with these previous clinical prediction models, this study has three core differences and advantages: first, in variable composition, we innovatively integrated clinical indicators (fracture complexity, time from injury to surgery) and molecular biomarkers (PGC-1α, NOS, Runx2, OPG). This not only retains the clinical operability of traditional models but also connects the macroscopic clinical manifestations with the microscopic biological mechanisms (such as PGC-1α regulating energy metabolism for bone repair, NOS promoting angiogenesis at the fracture site), making the predictive results have clear biological interpretability—this is a key advantage that models relying solely on clinical factors do not have. Second, in terms of predictive performance, the AUC of our model in the validation set is 0.711 (95%CI 0.581–0.842), which is slightly higher than the 0.68–0.70 of the above-mentioned models, indicating that our model has better ability to distinguish high-risk patients. Third, in clinical application timing, the biomarkers in our model can be detected through preoperative fasting venous blood sampling, which enables clinicians to assess the risk of delayed union before surgery and formulate personalized follow-up plans in advance. In contrast, traditional models based on postoperative indicators can only start risk assessment after several months of follow-up, which may miss the best opportunity for early intervention. It should be noted that some of the above-mentioned studies Schwarzenberg P have the advantage of multicenter large samples, while this study is a single-center retrospective design. This limitation may affect the external generalization of the model, and we plan to carry out multicenter prospective verification in the future to further improve the model's stability and applicability.

The developed nomogram provides clinicians with a practical tool for individualized risk assessment. To operationalize this model: (1) for each predictor (× 1– × 6), locate the patient's value on the corresponding axis and draw a vertical line to the 'Points' axis to determine the partial score. (2) Sum all partial scores to obtain the total points. (3) Locate the total points on the bottom axis and draw a vertical line to the 'Disease Risk' axis to read the predicted probability. For illustration, consider a patient with the following profile: fracture complexity score (× 1) = 1 (40 points), time from injury to surgery (× 2) = 14 days (40 points), PGC-1α level (× 3) = 50 ng/mL (20 points), NOS (× 4) = 10 U/mL (50points), Runx2 (× 5) = 55 ng/mL (45 points) and OPG (× 6) = 210 pg/mL (25 points). The cumulative score of 220 points corresponds to an estimated 30% probability of delayed fracture union. This threshold-based stratification enables clinicians to identify high-risk patients who may benefit from enhanced surveillance or early intervention, and optimize resource allocation while maintaining prognostic accuracy. However, it is important to note that the validation set of this study showed a relatively low specificity (50%) for the prediction model. This indicates that the model may have a risk of misclassifying patients with normal fracture union as high-risk individuals for delayed union. In clinical practice, such misclassification may lead to unnecessary follow-up examinations, repeated imaging evaluations, or even excessive intervention measures for patients with normal healing potential, which not only increases the physical and psychological burden of patients but also raises the overall medical costs of the healthcare system.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the single-center design and retrospective nature may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability to broader populations. Second, we lacked functional outcome measures and did not assess interobserver variability in radiographic evaluations. Most critically, external validation was not performed due to constraints in time and resources—such validation would require multicenter collaboration with larger, geographically diverse cohorts to ensure model robustness. While internal validation demonstrated satisfactory performance, future studies must address these limitations through prospective multicenter designs with standardized outcome assessments. Third, this study did not employ multiple comparison corrections (e.g., Bonferroni or FDR methods) for the statistical analyses. Although our hypotheses were formulated a priori and the number of primary comparisons was limited, some findings may be susceptible to Type I error inflation. In addition, the routine clinical measurement of molecular biomarkers involved in this study, such as PGC-1α and Runx2, has not been standardized yet. The detection methods and procedures may vary among different medical institutions, which increases the difficulty in popularizing the application of this prediction model. Therefore, the wide adoption of this model in clinical practice depends on the realization of standardized and convenient measurement of these biomarkers in the future. Future studies with larger sample sizes should incorporate appropriate multiplicity adjustments, particularly when exploring secondary outcomes or performing post-hoc analyses.

Conclusion

This study found that low complexity of fractures, short time from injury to surgery, high levels of PGC-1 α, NOS, Runx2 and OPG were associated with reduced risk of delayed union of tibial and fibular fractures. Based on these factors, a nomogram predictive model was constructed. The model shows moderate ability to predict delayed union. It could potentially assist in risk stratification and closer follow-up for high-risk patients. Further external validation in multi-center cohorts is needed before clinical adoption.
