The PAthways to Resilience And Mental health (PARAM) project: protocol for a multi-site developmental cohort in India
Authors: Bharath Holla, Eesha Sharma, Shradha Venkataramanan, Syeda Zoya, Kruthika Shankar, Amarjeet Kumar, Arshitha Basavaraj, Bhagyalakshmi Shankarappa, Jayant Mahadevan, Vanteemar S Sreeraj, Shoba S Meera, Geetha Desai, Amit Chakrabarti, Abhishek Ghosh, Shalini Naik, Debashish Basu, Kamakshi Kartik, Kartik Kalyanram, Padmalatha Venkatram, Srimathy Raman, Ashwini J Authreya, Senjam Gojendra Singh, Aniruddha Basu, Naveen Kikkeri Hanumantha Setty, Naresh Nebhinani, Onkar Prasad Khandwal, Neha Singh, VS Binu, Biju Viswanath, Meera Purushottam, Ganesan Venkatasubramanian, Rose Dawn Bharath, Ramesh Naidu Laveti, SD Sudarsan, TK Srikanth, D Dinakaran, Keshav Kumar, Yogesh Shouche, Raja Sriswan Mamidi, Neha Dahiya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychiatric disorders are increasingly conceptualised within a neurodevelopmental framework, in which genetic liability interacts with environmental exposures across the lifespan to shape brain and behavioural trajectories. Deviations in these trajectories may confer vulnerability or resilience to later psychopathology. Longitudinal cohorts spanning critical developmental windows are required to map these pathways and their associated risk and protective factors.MethodsThe PAthways to Resilience And Mental health (PARAM) project is a multi-site longitudinal cohort in India, enrolling approximately 9,000 participants from the antenatal period to 30 years of age. PARAM extends the Consortium on the Vulnerability to Externalizing Disorders and Addictions (cVEDA) cohort, which recruited more than 9,000 individuals aged 6–23 years, by adding intensive follow-up from the fetal period to early childhood. Eight sites across India participate, with five recontacting their original cVEDA participants for repeated assessments. Repeated measures include questionnaires on development, temperament, and mental health; family medical and psychiatric history; assessments of environmental exposures such as adverse experiences, diet, maternal stress, toxic exposures, and screen use; and satellite-derived indicators of urbanisation and air pollution over time. Detailed examinations comprise anthropometry and body composition, neurocognitive testing, neurophysiological measures including heart rate variability and body sway, neuroimaging with 3T MRI and fNIRS, and biospecimen collection (blood, buccal swabs, hair, nails, urine, stool) for genomic, toxicological, and metabolic assays. Data are stored in a harmonised digital archive linked to a barcoded biorepository. Follow-up employs a closed cohort design for children younger than two years and an accelerated longitudinal design for older participants. Planned analyses include mixed-effects and generalized additive models for trajectories, multi-site imaging harmonisation and normative modelling, multivariate integration of multi-omic and imaging features, and predictive modelling with nested cross-validation and external validation where feasible. Missing data and attrition will be addressed using multiple imputation and inverse probability weighting.DiscussionPARAM will offer insights into population-based developmental trajectories, identify modifiable risk and protective factors, and support predictive models for psychopathology. The findings are expected to inform precision interventions and public health strategies, while contributing a socio-culturally diverse resource to global mental health research.Keywords: Cohort study, Developmental trajectories, Environmental exposures, Genetic risk, Precision medicine, Global mental health

Introduction

Psychiatric disorders as deviations or disruptions of the neurodevelopmental continuum

Psychiatric disorders are increasingly recognized as arising from deviations of the neurodevelopmental continuum, which reflect variations in brain growth trajectories arising from interactions between inherited genetic predispositions and environmental contexts. Subtle deviations in these trajectories, critically during prenatal life, infancy, childhood, and adolescence, confer risk for psychopathology over the life course [1, 2]. This neurodevelopmental framing posits that while genetic factors may set initial boundaries for these trajectories, the exposome continually modifies developmental course. Developmental deviations in cognitive, emotional, and behavioral growth can serve as markers of heightened vulnerability (or resilience) to later psychiatric disorders [3]. As with pediatric growth charts, neurodevelopmental models could benchmark brain and behavioral development, enabling early detection of atypical patterns and guiding prevention strategies.

Early neurodevelopmental processes such as neuronal migration, proliferation, synaptic pruning, and myelination are shaped by genetic programs and experience. Stem cell–derived models can now reproduce abnormalities observed in psychiatric disorders, implicating disrupted neuronal differentiation, synaptic maturation, mitochondrial function, and immune signalling [4]. Converging human evidence shows that disruptions during prenatal or early postnatal periods alter brain connectivity years before syndromes emerge: early deprivation leaves enduring cognitive, social, and emotional effects despite later enrichment [3], while atypical structural and functional trajectories during early development predict neurodevelopmental, psychotic, and mood disorders [2, 5]. Such deviations may first appear as subtle motor, social, or cognitive differences that cumulatively signal vulnerability amenable to early intervention. These observations point upstream to the exposures that shape neurodevelopment and require systematic characterization.

The developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) model proposes that adverse maternal, prenatal, and early childhood exposures can “program” long-term trajectories through epigenetic, endocrine, immune, and neural pathways [6, 7]. Beyond physical growth restriction, evidence links maternal stress [8], inflammation [9], nutritional deficits, oxidative stress [10], and toxic exposures [11] to enduring neurodevelopmental alterations. These influences may act not only by constraining foetal growth but also by priming immune and neuroendocrine systems for exaggerated responses to stress later in life [12]. This priming aligns with stress–diathesis models that emphasize gene–environment interactions (GxE) wherein early vulnerabilities of genetic or neurodevelopmental origin manifest when compounded by later adversity. For example, polygenic risk for schizophrenia or mood disorders is more likely to translate into illness in the presence of childhood trauma [13]; children exposed to more adversity display blunted cortisol responses to stress, particularly when familial risk is present [14].

Timing of early-life stress is critical in shaping neurodevelopmental vulnerability

Recent neurobiological models show that early-life stress, depending on timing and context, can recalibrate brain development [15], including disruption of default-mode network maturation [16]. The early-life “exposome” framework integrates environmental influences starting from conception [17], and spanning internal (metabolic, immune, microbial) and external (stress, nutrition, pollutants, lifestyle) domains [17–21]. These exposures become biologically embedded through epigenetic regulation [22] and microglial processes [23], altering trajectories and shaping vulnerability. It follows that resilience and vulnerability arise from cumulative G×E interactions across sensitive periods, underscoring the need for longitudinal cohorts to capture exposures and outcomes across the life course. More importantly, these insights reinforce the importance of targeting modifiable exposures during prenatal and early childhood periods, when exposures are most consequential and trajectories most malleable, to mitigate vulnerability and bolster resilience.

Transdiagnostic, dimensional conceptualizations of psychopathology better inform risk stratification and precision interventions

Emerging evidence challenges the assumptions of conventional categorical psychiatric diagnoses (e.g., DSM or ICD) as “outcomes”, highlighting substantial overlap in genetic, neurobiological, and symptomatic presentations across traditional diagnostic categories [1, 24]. From a transdiagnostic and dimensional perspective, core neurodevelopmental processes and shared pathophysiological mechanisms cut across disorders [24]. For example, alterations in cognitive control, threat-processing networks, and reward circuitry recur in anxiety, depressive, and externalizing conditions, suggesting that these phenotypes reflect varied manifestations of common neurodevelopmental disruptions [25]. This reframing shifts emphasis from categories to mechanisms, directing research toward trajectories, circuits, and dimensions. Further, moving beyond group-level contrasts to specific deviation scores across multimodal descriptors enables outcome prediction at the level of the individual, thereby supporting personalized risk stratification and precision interventions.

India’s high-variance context: Implications formechanistic studies of neurodevelopmental psychopathology

Against the above backdrop, India provides a high-variance context to test and extend neurodevelopmental models, given its demographic and socio-cultural diversity and rapid urbanization and digitization. Indian cohorts such as the Consortium on Vulnerability to Externalizing Disorders and Addictions (cVEDA) [26] and the Bangalore Child Health and Development Study (BCHADS) have demonstrated how socioeconomic status [27], adverse childhood experiences [28], maternal mental health [29], caregiving practices [30], and environmental toxins [31] shape cognitive and emotional outcomes. Yet, important gaps remain. First, most existing cohorts in India commence in late childhood or adolescence, leaving antenatal and early childhood windows underrepresented. Second, there are no large-scale, age-referenced growth charts for brain, cognition, and mental health calibrated to India’s socio-cultural and ecological diversity. Third, exposure–outcome maps integrating nutrition, adversity, pollutants, and digital environments across the life course are limited. Fourth, much of the mechanistic evidence on DOHaD and stress–diathesis models derives from high-income settings, with uncertain generalizability to low- and middle-income contexts. Finally, transdiagnostic biomarkers are lacking, despite their importance for early risk stratification.

To address these gaps and extend insights from prior Indian cohorts, a large, developmentally anchored observational study is needed that follows individuals from the prenatal period into early adulthood within diverse socio-ecological contexts. The Pathways to Resilience And Mental Health (PARAM) project is designed to meet this need by generating neurodevelopmental trajectories, mapping exposures to these trajectories, and identifying modifiable risks and resilience factors in the high-variance Indian context. This paper describes the study objectives, design, methods, and planned analyses of PARAM. PARAM is a multi-site longitudinal cohort that integrates multimodal phenotyping, harmonized digital infrastructure for data acquisition/archival, and open-science governance to advance understanding of neurodevelopmental risk and resilience.

Methods

Study objectives

The PARAM project aims to generate neurodevelopmental trajectories and identify pathways to risk and resilience for mental health across the life course in India. Its specific objectives are: (i) To chart neurodevelopmental trajectories of brain, cognition, behaviour, and mental health across prenatal, childhood, adolescence, and early adulthood. (ii) To examine the impact of early-life exposures (maternal stress, nutrition, inflammation, toxins, digital media, and socio-environmental adversity) on developmental trajectories, with a focus on sensitive and critical periods. (iii) To integrate genetic and environmental data using G×E models and normative modelling approaches, deriving individual-level deviation scores that support subject-specific inference. (iv) To identify risk and protective factors for the onset and course of psychiatric symptoms, including biological markers, neuroimaging indices, neurocognitive functions, and psychosocial determinants. (v) To build open, harmonized infrastructure for multimodal data (phenotypic, imaging, biospecimen, geospatial), enabling reproducibility, data sharing, and participation in global consortia.

Study area and setting

PARAM is an Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) task-force study coordinated by the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS), Bengaluru (nodal centre), with seven additional recruitment sites across India (Fig. 1): Rishi Valley Rural Health Centre, Chittoor, Andhra Pradesh; Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh; Regional Institute of Medical Sciences (RIMS), Imphal, Manipur; ICMR–Centre for Ageing and Mental Health (ICMR-CAMH), Kolkata, West Bengal; Pt. Jawahar Lal Nehru Memorial Medical College (JNMMC), Raipur, Chhattisgarh; All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Kalyani (AIIMS-K), West Bengal; and All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Jodhpur (AIIMS-J), Rajasthan. The study has representation spanning North, South, West, East and North-East regions of India, capturing heterogeneous socio-cultural contexts and exposure profiles (e.g., urban density, informal settlements, mining-adjacent areas, and regions with socio-political stressors). Recruitment occurs in urban and rural settings, clinics, community clusters, schools/colleges and in risk-enriched contexts (e.g., socio-economic deprivation, high pollution), to support mechanistic enquiries into developmental deviations and psychopathology. Three other sites provide technical support for data acquisition and management – the NIMHANS Database and Biorepository (Database), Centre for Development of Advanced Computing, Bengaluru (C-DAC) and International Institute of Information Technology, Bengaluru (IIIT-B).

Study design

PARAM is an observational, multi-site cohort spanning the antenatal period to 30 years, implemented as two coordinated components to maximize developmental coverage while limiting respondent burden. The closed cohort component enrols pregnant mothers and infants, following mother–infant dyads from enrolment or birth to the child’s second birthday, with assessments approximately every six months (e.g., ~6, 12, 18, and 24 months) to capture change in the “first 1,000 days”. The accelerated cohort component (Fig. 1) covers the age span from 2 to 30 years using overlapping single-age cohorts, enabling reconstruction of age-graded trajectories within a shorter calendar window than a single-birth cohort. Follow-up uses a planned-missingness (matrix-sampling) design in which ~50% of participants are reassessed at 12 months and the remainder at 24 months; each participant contributes data at two of three planned waves. Participants are drawn from three streams: (i) re-consented cVEDA participants (6–23 years at cVEDA baseline) last evaluated 7–9 years ago; (ii) newly enrolled antenatal, infancy, toddler, and preschool participants; and (iii) newly enrolled children, adolescents, and young adults aged 6–30 years to offset cVEDA attrition and ensure age-band coverage.

Study eligibility and exclusions

The recruitment approach samples multiple segments of society across India to build a comprehensive cohort intended to be broadly representative of the Indian population and diverse risk experiences (Table 2); accordingly, the selection criteria are intentionally broad and pragmatic. Inclusion: Residence within the site catchment; age from antenatal period to 30 years; any gender. Exclusion: Conditions that preclude completion of core protocol (e.g., severe mental/physical illness or profound sensory/motor impairment). Imaging-specific: standard MRI contraindications at imaging visits (e.g., ferromagnetic implants, non-removable metal piercings, incompatible dental implants, or claustrophobia).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The PARAM study received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committees at all participating sites. Recruitment proceeds only after informed consent/assent. For minors (<18 years), written consent is obtained from a parent/legal guardian and assent from the child/adolescent (written for ≥12 years; verbal for 7–11 years). Consent to be recontacted for follow-up is obtained at baseline; renewed consent is sought prior to each follow-up assessment. Component-level consent is used for major modalities (e.g., questionnaires, neuroimaging, and each biospecimen type). Each participant is assigned a unique study ID, and all study data are de-identified and stored separately from personal identifiers. If medical or psychosocial concerns are identified during assessment, the team arranges appropriate referrals after discussion with the participant/guardian. All MRI scans are reviewed by a neuroradiologist; reports are provided to participants/guardians and, when indicated, referrals to neurology/neurosurgery are facilitated.

Sample size and sampling approach

PARAM uses pragmatic, non-probability sampling across clinics, communities, and schools to assemble an exposure-diverse cohort. The overall target is ≈9,000 participants from the antenatal period to 30 years, sized to cover sensitive developmental windows and to support estimation of age-referenced trajectories and deviation scores (Table 1). Under a planned-missingness follow-up (each participant contributes two of three waves), a modified Lehr heuristic for repeated-measures/mixed-effects endpoints was used to size broad age bands. Working allocations total N = 9,000 and are distributed as: antenatal/maternal ≈1,650; 0–5 years ≈2,200; 6–10 years ≈1,660; 11–13 years ≈1,165; 14–17 years ≈1,165; and ≥18 years ≈1,160. These targets balance precision and feasibility across bands, stabilize trajectory estimation in early childhood and school-age periods, and align with site-level recruitment capacity, while maintaining efficiency and limiting respondent burden under the planned-missingness design.

Approximately 15% of participants will undergo MRI/fNIRS (~1,350 at baseline), proportionally distributed across the accelerated cohort. Based on cVEDA coefficients of variation (~5–30% for T1-weighted brain volumes), the modified Lehr heuristic indicates ~20–50 participants per single-year age cohort with ≥2 scans to detect ~5–10% annual percent change, supporting longitudinal imaging analyses embedded within the broader cohort.

PARAM is not designed for probability-based, population prevalence estimates at the national level; instead, it is powered for trajectory estimation and association testing (e.g., links between trajectory deviations and mental-health outcomes) in pooled models with site as a stratification or random-effects factor. To optimize efficiency and sensitivity, covariate-aware sampling is implemented, following a U-shaped strategy that oversamples individuals at developmental extremes and across key exposure gradients (e.g., urbanisation, pollution, adversity). This increases variance in covariates of interest and enhances power for modelling nonlinear trajectories and interaction effects, as demonstrated in recent large-scale cohorts [32].

Timeline

PARAM commenced in May 2023. The first nine months (May 2023–January 2024) focused on start-up activities: staff recruitment; translation and cultural adaptation of the assessment battery into seven Indian languages; deployment of data-entry platforms and servers; centralized training and certification of project staff; and multi-site quality-control drills. Participant recruitment began in February 2024 and was rolled out in a staggered manner across sites due to local administrative timelines. Baseline recruitment and assessments will be conducted over an 18-month period at each site. Baseline assessments are completed within three months of a participant’s recruitment date. Recruitment will continue until April 2026, with the final baseline assessments completed by July 2026.

Data collection and storage

Psychological and behavioural measures are captured in Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) via secure web forms [33]. Data are encrypted in transit (HTTPS) to a centrally hosted REDCap server at NIMHANS; role-based user rights, Data Access Groups, and audit logs control and track access. Branching logic, field validations/required fields, and the Data Quality module support real-time checks. Monitoring and curation use scheduled exports and role-scoped API pulls. Neuropsychological tasks are administered in the Psychology Experiment Building Language (PEBL) [34], with summary scores written back to REDCap.

Neurophysiological data are acquired with Polar H10 chest straps paired via Bluetooth Low Energy to a custom study app that records inter-beat intervals, tri-axial accelerometer, and session metadata (study ID, site, timestamps) under a standardized SOP. Data stream via TLS to the central server and are linked to REDCap by study ID.

Imaging data is managed in XNAT. Sites upload DICOM via the web uploader, where automated de-identification is applied before archival. Protocol-conformance checks validate key parameters (e.g., TR/TE, flip angle, voxel size, sequence naming) against site-specific templates and flag deviations for review. On successful ingest, datasets are converted to BIDS generating NIfTI images with JSON sidecars. Raw DICOMs are retained in XNAT; BIDS datasets are stored in project archives with checksums for integrity. QC dashboards combine automated metrics (e.g., motion/signal summaries) with visual review, and scheduled phantom scans support longitudinal QA. For fNIRS, vendor exports in SNIRF are retained and organized per BIDS-fNIRS conventions, with linkage to phenotypic records. Biospecimens are barcode-tracked in the biorepository Laboratory Information Management System (LIMS) and cross-referenced in REDCap for chain-of-custody and assay metadata.

The primary data is hosted at the NIMHANS Database with mirrored storage at C-DAC-Bengaluru. Central data management runs routine range/logic audits and provides feedback to sites. De-identified datasets and biospecimens will be shared under governed access for secondary analyses.

Development

Intrauterine growth is documented from ultrasound records, using routine 2D obstetric scans and, where feasible, 3D neurosonograms of fetal brain and structure [35]. Postnatal development is assessed with the Developmental Screening Test (DST) [36] and the Vineland Social Maturity Scale (VSMS) [37] for ages 0–15 years; the Global Scales for Early Development (GSED v1.0) are used from 0–36 months [38]. Pubertal status is measured with the Pubertal Development Scale (PDS) [39] and lateralization with the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory–Short Form [40]. Parent-recorded videos, assessed with the Paediatric Infant-Parent Exam (PIPE) framework [41], index early communication and caregiver–child interaction.

Temperament

Age-appropriate Rothbart instruments (Infant Behaviour Questionnaire [IBQ] [42], Early Childhood Behaviour Questionnaire [ECBQ] [43], Children’s Behaviour Questionnaire [CBQ] [44], Early Adolescent Temperament Questionnaire–Revised [EATQ-R] [45], and Adult Temperament Questionnaire [ATQ] [46]) are used to assess negative affectivity, surgency/extraversion, effortful control, and affiliativeness through parent- or self-report. The Inventory of Callous–Unemotional Traits (ICU) is administered in both youth and parent-report versions [47].

Psychopathology

The WHO’s Flexible and Light Interview for ICD-11 (FLII-11) [48] screens common syndromes (including suicidality). The Child and Adolescent Behaviour Inventory - Revised (CABI-R) [49] provides dimensional internalizing/externalizing scores (6–17 y). Depressive symptoms are also captured with PHQ-9 child/adult forms [50]. Additional scales include the Brief Infant Sleep Questionnaire (BISQ) [51], Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10) [52], Premenstrual Symptoms Screening Tool (PSST) [53], and the Problematic Mobile Phone Use Questionnaire (PMPU-Q) [54].

Neuropsychological battery

Age-appropriate tasks (6–30 years) assess attention and working memory (Digit Span, Corsi), processing speed and flexibility (Trail Making Test A/B), set shifting (Wisconsin Card Sorting Test), response inhibition (Stop-Signal), learning and memory (Auditory Verbal Learning Test), and affect/social cognition [Emotion Recognition [55], Social Cognition Rating Tools in Indian Setting (SOCRATIS) [56]]. Most tasks are implemented in PEBL, with the Stop-Signal task in jsPsych and SOCRATIS/AVLT in REDCap. Batteries are standardized for longitudinal administration.

Neurophysiology Tasks

(i) BodySway (mBESS-adapted): firm surface with a safety observer; five eyes-closed stances—feet together, tandem (right/left), single-leg (right/left). Each stance is performed as three attempts, each a 10-second hold, hands on hips, with brief rest between attempts. Sway is quantified objectively from Polar H10 triaxial accelerometer data (e.g., resultant acceleration, sway path, root-mean-square path). (ii) Seated HRV with isometric handgrip: 5-min preparation, 5-min baseline, 3 maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) trials, sustained 30% MVC to exhaustion, then 5-min post-exhaustion and 5-min recovery.

Family history capture

A REDCap-integrated pedigree editor records three-generation family histories of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including mental illness; pedigrees are stored with the REDCap record and are exportable in multiple formats, including the GA4GH pedigree standard, enabling interoperable analyses.

Exposome and context

Socio-demographics and migration draw on NFHS-4/5 items [57]. Perinatal exposures are captured with the Pregnancy History Questionnaire–Revised (PHQ-R) [58]; Early caregiving is measured with the Postpartum Bonding Questionnaire (PBQ) [59], HOME-SF, and PIPE; parenting practices with the Alabama Parenting Questionnaire (APQ) [60]. School context is assessed with the School Climate Questionnaire [61]. Adversity is measured using the Indian Family Violence and Control Scale (IFVCS) [62] in mothers and the WHO Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ) [63] in participants; a brief positive-experiences addendum supplements that ACE-IQ. A COVID-19 module records infection history, vaccination/boosters, and (where applicable) antibody titres. Residential geocodes are captured in REDCap and stored with privacy safeguards; satellite-derived indicators of urbanisation and pollution are linked post hoc for exposure estimation.

Nutrition

Anthropometry (length/height, weight, mid-upper arm and head circumference), hand grip strength and bioelectrical impedance analysis provide body size/composition. Clinical signs of nutrient deficiency are recorded as per ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition, India guidance. Biochemistry includes hemogram and markers such as hemoglobin, transferrin, albumin, vitamins B1/B6/B12, thyroid function, lipids, liver enzymes, glucose, and DHA. Diet is captured with infant and young child feeding (IYCF) indicators, and food-frequency questionnaire.

Neuroimaging (deep subset)

Structural MRI (sMRI), resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI), and diffusion MRI (dMRI) are acquired at baseline and at follow-up for consenting randomized participants using harmonized 3 T protocols (Siemens and Philips scanners). Acquisition parameters are adapted from the ADNI protocol and refined based on experience from the cVEDA study to ensure cross-site consistency. Key parameters influencing image contrast and signal-to-noise are held constant to enable pooled analysis across sites. DICOMs are stored in XNAT, de-identified, converted to BIDS using dcm2bids, and undergo systematic validation and quality control, including phantom-based monitoring and motion/signal metrics. Resting-state fNIRS is recorded using the Cortivision Photon Cap C20, a lightweight, wireless, continuous-wave system with 16 LED emitters and 10 SiPM detectors. Optodes target the bilateral prefrontal cortex with both long and short separations to separate cortical signals from superficial artifacts. Up to 15 minutes of resting-state data is collected, allowing retention of at least 10 minutes of artifact-free signals post quality control. Preprocessing follows a reproducible pipeline, with steps including channel-level QC, short-channel regression, physiological noise rejection, and functional connectivity estimation.

Biospecimens

Blood, saliva/buccal, nail, hair, urine, stool, and cord blood (where available) are collected for DNA/RNA and biochemical assays. DNA supports genotyping and DNA-methylation studies; RNA enables targeted expression assays. Plasma/serum are profiled for metabolic and inflammatory markers (e.g., cytokines, hs-CRP, DHA); urine for neurotoxic metabolites (e.g., hydrocarbons, cotinine, arsenic, fluoride, pesticides); nails for chronic pollutants; hair for cortisol and DHEA. All biospecimens are barcode-tracked and linked to phenotypic records.

Procedure

Participants recruited from clinics, communities, and schools are assessed over one or two visits. Written consent is obtained from adults and parents/guardians of minors; minors ≥12 years provide written assent, and those <12 years provide verbal assent. Depending on age and instrument, the child/adult participant and at least one parent are interviewed. Total contact time is typically 4–5 hours with scheduled meals and comfort breaks. Any pending components are completed within 3 months of the recruitment date. Retention procedures include structured reminders and flexible scheduling for subsequent closed- or accelerated-cohort follow-ups. Follow-up assessments are conducted within a −1 to +3-month window around the scheduled date.

Quality control

SOPs govern all recruitment and assessment procedures. Site teams underwent centralized training (in-person/online), mock interviews with multi-rater feedback, and device/software standardization. Ongoing oversight includes weekly coordination calls to review enrolment, completeness, and data quality, plus periodic site visits for protocol adherence and troubleshooting. A centralized, automated QC pipeline monitors REDCap and PEBL data to: (a) flag duplicate IDs; (b) track site-wise accrual rates; (c) report completion by cohort and age band; (d) detect inconsistencies (e) identify administration deviations; and (f) flag questionnaire outliers for review against collateral measures. Findings trigger SOP updates and targeted retraining.

For MRI, each site conducts (i) regular phantom scans for calibration/stability; (ii) periodic healthy-volunteer scans to detect artifacts not seen on phantoms; and (iii) same-day automated MRI QC (~2–3-min HTML reports summarizing sequence parameters, anatomical montages, DWI motion/ghosting/distortion, and fMRI TSNR/motion/outliers) with OK/flag/reacquire disposition and immediate re-acquisition when feasible (https://github.com/hollabharath/fastMRIQC). For fNIRS, QC comprises (i) routine device/optode/software checks; (ii) cap sizing by head circumference/age; (iii) standardized prep (black cap, hair parting) to optimize optode–scalp contact and reduce ambient light; (iv) real-time montage/contact verification in Cortivision; and (v) signal-quality screening using the Scalp Coupling Index with per-subject QC reports and site-level summaries (https://github.com/hollabharath/fNIRS_PARAM); flagged sessions are repeated when possible.

Analysis plan

Handling missing data and design features

PARAM employs planned missingness (matrix sampling: two of three waves per participant). Primary analyses will assume Missing At Random (MAR) and use likelihood-based estimators (full-information maximum likelihood in mixed/SEM models) for unbiased inference. We will complement this with multiple imputation (chained equations), incorporating site, age, mode (in-person/remote), and key outcomes/exposures, and with inverse-probability weighting for attrition. Sensitivity analyses will probe MNAR mechanisms (pattern-mixture/selection models). Mode-related differences (e.g., online vs offline) and language versions will be addressed via measurement invariance checks (multi-group CFA/IRT) and, where needed, explicit covariates. For recontacted cVEDA members, analyses will index time since last assessment and account for left truncation (delayed entry).

Statistical analysis and modelling

We will first describe site- and age-band distributions and exposure gradients; multi-site differences will be addressed through site random effects and batch harmonization (e.g., ComBat), alongside acquisition/QC controls. Developmental trajectories for multimodal markers (neurocognition, symptoms, MRI/fNIRS features, anthropometry) will be modelled with generalized additive mixed models (GAMM) and GAMLSS to capture age-varying mean, scale, and shape; latent growth and growth-mixture/latent class models will identify sub-trajectory patterns. A normative-modelling framework will generate age–sex–site referenced deviation scores (z/centiles) for image-derived and cognitive measures, enabling subject-level inference [64]; implementations will include uncertainty-aware prediction and hierarchical/federated variants to accommodate multi-site effects and incremental model updates. Associations between trajectories (or deviation scores) and time-varying exposures (socio-environmental, geospatial, nutritional, biospecimen biomarkers) will be estimated with mixed-effects regression, testing Polygenic x Environment interactions and effect modification by sex/socioeconomic status; repeated-measures correlation will summarize within-person coupling of paired longitudinal measures. Cross-modal integration will use CCA, PLS/sPLS, and other regularized multiblock methods; brain–behaviour mappings will incorporate connectivity-based metrics (e.g., network strength, modularity). Predictive models (elastic net, gradient boosting, random forests) will use nested, site-stratified cross-validation and, where feasible, external validation; calibration, discrimination, and decision-curve analyses will be reported. Geospatial exposures (urbanisation/greenness/pollution) linked via residential geocodes will be analysed with spatiotemporal mixed models and distributed-lag non-linear models to estimate exposure–lag–response functions. Multiple testing will be controlled (e.g. False Discovery Rate corrections), robust/clustered standard errors used as appropriate, and prespecified analysis plans and code version-controlled for reproducibility. Mixed-effects and GAMM/SEM approaches will provide the primary framework for longitudinal inference.

Discussion

This protocol outlines PARAM, a multi-site observational cohort in India, designed to characterize neurodevelopment from the antenatal period through early adulthood, and to relate developmental trajectories to environmental and genomic contexts. The study extends learning from prior Indian cohorts by adding dense measurement in the first 1,000 days, recontacting a legacy adolescent/young-adult cohort, and incorporating multimodal phenotyping with harmonized digital infrastructure. Its primary contribution will be age-referenced trajectories and deviation scores across brain, cognition, and behaviour, situated within diverse socio-ecological exposures.

The scientific rationale rests on three premises. First, psychiatric risk is often detectable as deviations in developmental trajectories that precede clinical syndromes [1, 5, 65]. Second, the exposome—ranging from perinatal stress and nutrition to urbanisation and pollution—interacts with genetic liabilities to shape neurodevelopment [66]. Third, much of the current evidence originates from high-income settings [67]; India’s heterogeneity enables tests of generalisability and discovery of context-specific risk and resilience. By embedding developmental framing and transdiagnostic measurement, PARAM prioritizes transdiagnostic mechanisms over categories and emphasizes subject-level inference.

Several design features strengthen inference. The age span is covered through a closed cohort in early life and an accelerated cohort thereafter, enabling reconstruction of age-related trajectories faster than a single birth cohort alone, which would otherwise take decades to span infancy to adulthood [68]. Planned missingness is deliberately built into the design to reduce participant burden without sacrificing inferential power for longitudinal models [69]. Another hallmark is multimodal phenotyping on a common cohort, integrating questionnaires, neurocognition, neurophysiology, neuroimaging (MRI, fNIRS), biospecimens, and geospatial indicators. Field operations are standardized with SOPs, centralized training, and quality control, including REDCap-based data capture, XNAT-to-BIDS imaging workflows, automated QC, and barcode-tracked biorepository procedures.

Analytically, PARAM will estimate smooth age functions and dispersion using generalized additive mixed models and GAMLSS [70], and derive deviation scores [64] that support subject-level interpretation analogous to paediatric growth charts. Hierarchical/federated variants of normative modelling and harmonization strategies accommodate multi-site acquisition and will allow updating as the cohort grows. Deviation scores provide a common effect-size metric across modalities, facilitate outlier detection and stratification, and can be used as predictors of incident outcomes in survival and joint models. Multivariate approaches (CCA/PLS and related methods) can uncover latent dimensions of covariation linking different domains [71, 72]. These data-driven methods will integrate imaging, omics, and exposome features; predictive models will be evaluated with nested, site-stratified validation and, where feasible, external validation.

Expected outcomes

The cohort’s scope enables policy-relevant analyses. Repeated measures of nutrition, caregiving, school context, and geospatial exposures will support spatiotemporal models of exposure–lag–response across development [73, 74]. The antenatal and early-childhood focus can generate hypotheses about sensitive periods for future trials, while adolescent and young-adult follow-up will clarify transition risks. Outputs are expected to include developmental reference charts, exposure–trajectory maps, and risk stratifiers that can inform maternal–child programs, environmental regulation, and digital well-being policies [75]. The open, governed-access databank and biobank will support secondary analyses and participation in international consortia.

Limitations

These are inherent to the design and setting. Non-probability sampling improves feasibility and exposure diversity but limits estimation of national prevalence; inference is oriented to trajectories and associations rather than population rates. Attrition and mobility (including migration and sociopolitical disruptions) threaten follow-up; planned missingness, active tracing, flexible scheduling, and inverse-probability weighting mitigate but do not eliminate bias. Mode shifts (clinic vs. phone/online) and language versions may affect measurement; measurement-invariance checks and covariate adjustments are planned. Self-report instruments carry recall and social-desirability biases; triangulation with physiological, imaging, and biospecimen markers reduces sole reliance on self-report. Imaging is a subset, and site differences in hardware and workload can introduce heterogeneity; harmonized protocols, phantom/volunteer QC, and statistical harmonization will be applied, yet residual site effects may persist. As an observational cohort, PARAM cannot establish causal effects; analyses will emphasize temporality, dose–response, negative-control checks where possible, and sensitivity analyses for unmeasured confounding.

Data governance and privacy require particular attention. Residential geocodes enable satellite linkages but are handled with privacy safeguards and transformed for analysis. Consent/assent procedures are age-calibrated, with parental and spouse modules where applicable, and cultural adaptation of instruments has been undertaken across multiple Indian languages. Open-science aims are balanced with governed access to protect participants while enabling reproducibility.

Operationally, multi-site logistics in a large country create risks for uneven accrual and protocol drift; these are addressed through centralized coordination, routine site audits, automated data-quality dashboards, and feedback loops. Capacity building—training staff in standardized assessment, data entry, imaging acquisition, and QC—is an integral outcome, strengthening local research ecosystems.

In conclusion, PARAM is an ambitious response to the need for culturally inclusive, mechanistically oriented developmental research. PARAM provides an infrastructure to quantify developmental trajectories in a socio-culturally diverse population, integrate Genome × Exposome information with multimodal neurodevelopmental measures, and relate individual deviation profiles to incident psychopathology. While constrained by observational design and non-probability sampling, the study’s breadth, harmonization, QC, and analysis plan position it to deliver developmental models, exposure–trajectory insights, and testable hypotheses for future interventions. As data accrue, iterative public releases, reproducible pipelines, and alignment with global standards will maximize scientific value and accelerate translation to clinical and public health practice.

Data availability

The PARAM Database houses de-identified phenotypic, neurocognitive, neurophysiological, imaging (BIDS derivatives), geospatial indicators (derived), and laboratory datasets with linked metadata and data dictionaries. Annual frozen releases will be generated after centralized QC; the first data release is planned following completion of baseline assessments (target May 2026), with subsequent longitudinal updates. Individual-level data will be shared under governed, controlled access to protect confidentiality (e.g., restricted access to precise geocodes and raw DICOM); summary aggregates and codebooks will be openly available.

Data access is via a Project Executive Committee review and a Data Use Agreement that specifies scope, security, reidentification prohibitions, and publication/reporting terms. Requests should include a brief analysis plan and ethics compliance; approved users receive role-scoped credentials. Imaging will be shared as BIDS-formatted derivatives (with accompanying JSON sidecars and validator reports); raw DICOM are available under enhanced controls. Biospecimens (biorepository) may be requested through a Material Transfer Agreement and Biorepository Access Committee review, contingent on assay feasibility and sample stewardship.

All analysis code and pipelines used for curation/QC will be version-controlled and publicly released, with containerized environments to ensure reproducibility. Data and code will follow FAIR principles (findable, accessible under governance, interoperable, reusable), and persistent identifiers (DOIs) will be assigned to major releases. Researchers may get interim access to specific datasets on reasonable request during data collection, subject to the same governance.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The PARAM study received clearance from the Institutional Ethics Committees at all participating sites. Participants are recruited after written informed consent or assent, as applicable. In the case of minors (<18 years of age), informed consent is taken from the legal guardian (usually parents) and assent from the child/adolescent. Consent for being contacted for follow-up is taken at the time of baseline recruitment. Consent will be taken again before initiating follow-up assessments. Participants indicate their willingness for the various assessments (e.g. questionnaires, neuroimaging, each biological sample, etc.), individually, on the consent forms. Each participant recruited into the study is assigned a unique study identification number. All study data is thereby delinked from any personal identifying information. If any medical/psychosocial concerns are identified in a participant, appropriate referrals are arranged after discussion with the participant/legal guardian. All MRI scans conducted as part of the study are reviewed by a neuroradiologist. The reports are handed over to the participants/legal guardians and, when needed, appropriate neurological/neurosurgical referrals made.
