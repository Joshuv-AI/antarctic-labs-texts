Associations between sarcopenic obesity and risk of falls: a population-based cohort study among middle-aged and older adults using the CHARLS
Authors: Chuanwen Yu, Zhanpeng Meng, Xiangying Wang, Jiaqiang Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesAlthough previous research has established a strong link between sarcopenia and fall risk in middle-aged and older adults, the effects of sarcopenic obesity and possible sarcopenic obesity on fall risk remain underexplored in large-scale studies. This longitudinal analysis aimed to examine these associations using nationally representative cohort data.MethodsWe analyzed data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), including 9,996 participants aged 45 and older with available body composition measurements. Participants were categorized into four groups based on Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) criteria and median waist circumference (WC): (1) normal WC without sarcopenia (control group), (2) normal WC with sarcopenia, (3) obesity without sarcopenia, and (4) obesity with sarcopenia. Cox proportional hazards model was used to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and P-values, with the control group as the reference. The model was sequentially adjusted for demographic (age, sex, residence), socioeconomic (education), and behavioral (smoking, alcohol) variables.ResultsCompared to participants with normal WC and no sarcopenia (reference group), those with sarcopenic obesity had a significantly higher risk of falls (HR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.00–1.56; P = 0.048), even after adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral factors. Similarly, possible sarcopenic obesity was linked to a modest but statistically significant increase in fall risk (HR = 1.12; 95% CI: 1.02–1.24; P = 0.021). Subgroup analyses revealed that the fourth quartile showed a trend toward higher fall risk across all subgroups, especially among individuals aged ≥ 60 years and males. However, in most Subgroups, the 95% CIs cross 1, suggesting limited statistical significance in some strata.ConclusionsBoth sarcopenic obesity and possible sarcopenic obesity are associated with an elevated risk of falls. Middle-aged and older adults should adopt preventive strategies, such as maintaining regular physical activity, to preserve muscle mass and reduce the risk of falls.

Introduction

As global populations continue to age, the dual burden of sarcopenia and obesity has emerged as a significant public health concern due to their combined detrimental effects on physical function, mobility, and overall health outcomes in older adults [1]– [2]. Sarcopenia, characterized by age-related decline in muscle mass, strength, and/or physical performance, and obesity, particularly central adiposity, are each independently associated with increased risks of frailty, falls, and chronic disease [3]– [4]. Their coexistence, commonly referred to as sarcopenic obesity, represents a complex clinical phenotype that merges the musculoskeletal impairments of sarcopenia with the metabolic and biomechanical complications of obesity, leading to synergistically worse health outcomes [5]. Although there is no universally accepted definition of sarcopenic obesity, it generally refers to a decrease in muscle strength and mass combined with a high level of obesity [6, 7]. This evolving definition underscores the importance of integrating assessments of both adiposity (e.g., body mass index, BMI, and WC) and muscle status (e.g., strength, performance, or mass). In light of its growing prevalence and multifactorial impact, sarcopenic obesity is increasingly recognized as a critical target for early screening, prevention, and intervention in aging populations.

The relatively new concept of sarcopenic obesity is currently being studied by a variety of teams [8]. For example, Gandham et al. [9] found that individuals with sarcopenic obesity have a significantly higher risk of falls than those without sarcopenia. Similarly, Wang and colleagues identified a negative association between handgrip strength and fall risk, underscoring the protective role of muscle strength in fall prevention. Mechanistically, the co-existence of sarcopenia and obesity is hypothesized to have synergistic adverse effects on balance, postural control, and physical performance, leading to a greater fall risk than either condition alone [10]– [11]. Reduced muscle strength and impaired neuromuscular coordination compromise gait and stability, while excess fat mass alters biomechanics and contributes to systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and functional decline [12]– [13]. Despite the clinical significance of sarcopenic obesity, it remains underdiagnosed and underrecognized, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, due to limited diagnostic infrastructure and the absence of population-specific criteria. The shared pathophysiological mechanisms between sarcopenia and fall risk, including muscle degradation and neuromuscular impairment [14], have prompted increased academic interest in their relationship [15]– [16, 17]. However, most existing studies on sarcopenic obesity and fall risk are cross-sectional in design, restricting causal inference. Moreover, inconsistent definitions of sarcopenic obesity, ethnic differences in body composition, and heterogeneity in fall assessment methods contribute to conflicting findings across studies.

Notably, longitudinal studies exploring the relationship between sarcopenic obesity and fall risk remain scarce in Asian populations, where individuals often exhibit higher fat mass at lower BMI thresholds compared to Western populations. This context is particularly relevant in China, where middle-aged and older adults face progressive muscle loss and fat accumulation with age, trends that may synergistically elevate fall risk. In parallel, the concept of possible sarcopenia—a preclinical stage characterized by reduced muscle strength or impaired physical performance, but without confirmed low muscle mass—has gained increasing attention in aging research. Building on this concept, we define possible sarcopenic obesity as individuals who display low muscle strength or poor physical performance (typically assessed by the Short Physical Performance Battery, SPPB), combined with general or abdominal obesity (based on BMI or waist circumference), but without confirmed low appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (SMI). This operational definition represents a conceptual integration of the AWGS 2019 definition of possible sarcopenia and standard obesity criteria, consistent with emerging Literature that has started to apply this term to characterize transitional or early-risk phenotypes in aging populations. Notably, although the AWGS 2019 consensus does not explicitly define possible sarcopenic obesity, a growing number of studies have used this construct in recent years [18]– [19].

To address the limitations of previous studies and extend the current body of knowledge, the present study utilized data from the CHARLS to conduct a longitudinal analysis of the association between sarcopenic obesity and fall risk among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Furthermore, by including possible sarcopenic obesity in the analysis, this study aimed to determine whether this transitional state is similarly associated with increased fall risk. Such findings could enhance early risk identification and inform targeted intervention strategies to prevent the progression to sarcopenic obesity and its associated adverse outcomes.

Methods

Study population

The CHARLS was designed to gather high-quality microdata that represent Chinese individuals aged 45 and older, with the goal of analyzing population aging trends and Supporting interdisciplinary research on aging-related issues. The national baseline Survey, conducted in 2011, used a multistage probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling method, covering 28 provinces, 150 counties, and 450 villages. It included about 10,000 households and 17,707 participants. CHARLS is an ongoing study, with follow-up surveys carried out every two to three years. Data collection was done through computer-assisted personal interviews (CAPI), where participants were interviewed face-to-face in their homes. The Survey collected detailed information on demographic characteristics, intergenerational financial transfers, health status, healthcare and insurance, employment, income, expenditures, and assets. Additionally, CHARLS includes 13 physical measurements and the collection of blood samples. To date, five waves of CHARLS data have been released: the initial baseline survey (Wave 1, 2011), followed by updates in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. More details about CHARLS can be found in the study by Tang et al. [20], and all datasets are publicly available through the project’s official website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en). The CHARLS study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Peking University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The data from 2011 to 2020 were used in this research. The exclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) exclude those who did not conduct sarcopenia and obesity measurements in W1 (N = 4692); (2) exclude individuals under 45 and those with no age data in W1 (N = 393), as well as individuals missing relevant data or who experienced falls in W1 (N = 2171); (3) exclude participants without a successful follow-up measurement of falls (N = 455). This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Based on the final inclusion criteria, a total of 9,996 participants (aged 45–90 years) were included in the primary analysis. A detailed flowchart illustrating the sample selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Fall assessment

A fall is defined as an event in which an individual trips, slips, or loses balance, resulting in a descent to a lower level [21]. The primary outcome of this study is the occurrence of falls, with all fall incidents being self-reported by participants. In the four Survey waves conducted between 2013 and 2020, falls were assessed using a binary response format, where participants answered either “Yes” or “No.”

Ascertainment of obesity and sarcopenia status

In obesity assessment, BMI and WC are two primary indicators representing overall and abdominal obesity, respectively [22]. While BMI is widely used for its simplicity, its inability to differentiate fat distribution—such as visceral versus subcutaneous fat—is a well-recognized limitation [23]. In contrast, WC serves as a more direct measure of visceral fat accumulation and is strongly associated with metabolic syndrome risk [24]. This study defines obesity as a binary variable based on Asian-specific thresholds: WC ≥ 85 cm for men and WC ≥ 80 cm for women [18, 25, 26].

Sarcopenia was diagnosed based on the AWGS 2019 consensus, which defines sarcopenia as low muscle mass in combination with either low muscle strength or low physical performance. Incorporating three key criteria: muscle strength, appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM), and physical performance [17]. Grip strength was measured using a Yuejian WL-1000 hand dynamometer, with participants seated, their elbows at 90°, and both hands tested twice, recording the highest value obtained. According to the AWGS 2019 consensus, sarcopenia is diagnosed when low muscle mass is accompanied by either low muscle strength or low physical performance. In our study, participants were first screened for low muscle strength or low physical performance, and those meeting either criterion Subsequently underwent muscle mass estimation. Those with confirmed low muscle mass were classified as having sarcopenia, consistent with the AWGS 2019 definition. If one Limb was impaired, the unaffected side was used. Low grip strength was defined according to AWGS 2019 thresholds (< 28 kg for men, < 18 kg for women). Height and weight were measured using a Seca™ 213 stadiometer and an Omron™ HN-286 scale, with 0.1 cm/kg precision. In Line with prior research findings, low muscle mass was characterized by being in the lowest 20% of height-adjusted muscle mass (ASM/height²), specifically < 4.89 kg/m² in women and < 6.79 kg/m² in men [28]. This approach aligns well with gold-standard DXA/BIA measurements [29]– [30]. Physical performance was evaluated using gait speed (2.5 m/s, equivalent to a usual walking pace), a five-time chair stand test (performed with a 47 cm standard chair), and the SPPB test. The SPPB consists of three components: balance (side-by-side, semi-tandem, and tandem stance for 10 s each), gait speed, and chair stand performance, with a maximum score of 12. Impaired physical function was defined as gait speed < 1.0 m/s, chair stand time ≥ 12 s, or an SPPB score < 9 [31]. Subjects were diagnosed with sarcopenia when low muscle mass was combined with low muscle strength or low physical performance. Based on AWGS2019 [32], “possible sarcopenia” is defined as having either low muscle strength or low physical performance only.

We identified 2 dichotomous variables, sarcopenia and possible sarcopenia. Sarcopenia and obesity developed into 4 combinations of (1) normal waist circumference and non-sarcopenia, (2) normal waist circumference and sarcopenia, (3) obesity and non-sarcopenia, and (4) obesity and sarcopenia. We conducted sensitivity analyses by combining different definitions of obesity with sarcopenia, including potential sarcopenia and alternative obesity criteria, to assess the robustness of our findings.

Potential covariates

Skilled investigators gathered information on sociodemographic traits and health behaviors to account for potential confounding factors. Sociodemographic variables included age (continuous, in full years), gender (male = 1, female = 0), place of residence (urban = 1, rural = 0), and education level. Education was categorized according to the Chinese Population Census: illiterate (1), primary school (2), junior high school (3), senior high school/vocational high school (4), and college or higher (5). Additionally, health behavior factors included smoking (current smoker = 1, non-smoker = 0) and alcohol consumption status (drinker = 1, non-drinker = 0). These covariates were chosen based on prior research indicating their significant associations with both obesity and sarcopenia [33]– [34].

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables with a normal distribution, data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD); for non-normally distributed continuous variables, medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) are used. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. To compare baseline characteristics across the four sarcopenic obesity phenotype groups, appropriate statistical tests were applied based on variable type and distribution: one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables, Kruskal–Wallis tests for non-normally distributed variables, and Pearson’s chi-square tests for categorical variables. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the association between sarcopenic obesity/possible sarcopenic obesity and incident falls. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption for the Cox models was tested using Schoenfeld residuals and log-minus-log plots. No significant violations of the PH assumption were detected. The reference group was defined as individuals with normal WC and no sarcopenia. Three hierarchical models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 further adjusted for potential confounders identified through univariate analysis, including residential area, educational level, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. To test the robustness of the associations, sensitivity analyses were conducted using an alternative definition of obesity based on WC ≥ 90 cm for men, ≥ 85 cm for women, per Chinese criteria), and subgroup analyses were performed across strata of age, sex, residence, education, smoking, and alcohol use. To assess whether the association between sarcopenic obesity/possible sarcopenic obesity and fall risk differed by sex or age group, interaction terms were added to the Cox proportional hazards models. Stratified analyses were also performed to examine effect modification. Statistical significance for interaction was evaluated using the Likelihood ratio test. A three-dimensional bar chart was also generated to visualize the trend in fall risk across varying levels of WC in relation to sarcopenic status. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2 and STATA version 18.0. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 9,996 participants, with males comprising 48.61% of the sample. The mean age at baseline was 58.54 ± 9.22 years (range: 45–90 years). There were statistically significant differences in sociodemographic characteristics and health behaviors among the participants. Baseline characteristics stratified by sarcopenia and obesity status are summarized in Table 1. According to the AWGS diagnostic criteria, the prevalence of sarcopenic obesity in this study was 1.75% (n = 175), while possible sarcopenic obesity was found in 17.87% (n = 1,786). This prevalence seems significantly higher than figures reported in Western populations for related conditions such as probable sarcopenia, confirmed sarcopenia, and sarcopenic obesity based on partial diagnostic criteria [35]. Age-stratified analysis revealed that participants with sarcopenic obesity were the oldest, with a mean age of 72.68 ± 8.73 years. In comparison, those with possible sarcopenic obesity (67.31 ± 9.48 years) and obesity without sarcopenia (57.69 ± 8.73 years) were 5.37 and 15.0 years younger, respectively (P < 0.001). Moreover, sarcopenia phenotypes were strongly associated with lower socioeconomic status. The proportion of rural residents in the sarcopenic obesity group was 69.14%, which was 11.81% points higher than in the obesity without sarcopenia group (P < 0.001). A clear dose-response relationship was observed in illiteracy rates, with the highest prevalence in the sarcopenic obesity group (75.43%), followed by the possible sarcopenic obesity group (66.16%) and the obesity without sarcopenia group (41.85%). Health behaviors also varied across groups. The current smoking rate in the sarcopenic obesity group (21.14%) was 10.81% points lower than in the obesity without sarcopenia group, while high-risk alcohol consumption was markedly lower (14.86% vs. 28.97%). Regarding anthropometric measurements, the mean BMI of the study population (20.33 ± 2.35 kg/m²) fell within the normal range. However, the mean waist circumference (86.27 ± 4.62 cm) exceeded the threshold for abdominal obesity in Asians (≥ 85 cm for men and ≥ 80 cm for women).

Associations between sarcopenic obesity and fall events

The Cox proportional hazards model was used to evaluate the influence of confounding factors through a stepwise adjustment approach (see Table 2). Model 1 was the unadjusted crude model, Model 2 accounted for demographic factors (age, sex), and Model 3 further incorporated socioeconomic variables (residence, education level) and behavioral factors (smoking, alcohol consumption). Results showed that the sarcopenic obesity group (Group 4) had the highest risk of falls in the unadjusted model (HR = 1.98, 95% CI = 1.59–2.43). Further analysis indicated that individuals with both abdominal obesity and sarcopenia (Group 4) had a 25% greater fall risk compared to those with normal weight and no sarcopenia (Group 1) (HR = 1.25, 95% CI = 1.00–1.56). As adjustments were made for additional factors, the hazard ratio for falls showed a gradual decline. In Model 2 (adjusting for age and sex), the risk estimate dropped to HR = 1.24 (95% CI = 1.00–1.55). After further adjusting for socioeconomic and behavioral factors in Model 3, the risk remained at HR = 1.25 (95% CI = 1.00–1.56). However, even after full adjustment, sarcopenic obesity still posed a statistically significant independent risk for falls (HR = 1.25, with the lower bound of the 95% CI precisely at 1.00). Additionally, the three-dimensional bar chart shows that fall risk increases with rising WC among patients with sarcopenic obesity (Fig. 2-A and -B). Diagnostic tests confirmed that the proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for all models (p > 0.05).

Associations between possibly sarcopenic obesity and fall events

Similar to the above findings, we identified a significant positive association between possible sarcopenic obesity and an increased risk of falls (HR = 1.33, 95% CI = 1.21–1.46). Table 3 details the adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) across different models. After controlling for confounding factors, the independent pathological contribution of possible sarcopenic obesity was relatively modest (HR = 1.12, 95% CI = 1.02–1.24). Among patients possibly affected by sarcopenia, the 3D histogram reveals a positive correlation between WC and fall risk (Fig. 3-C and -D). Diagnostic tests confirmed that the proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for all models (p > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis

To assess the robustness of the observed associations, we conducted sensitivity analyses using three multivariable logistic regression models with incremental adjustments for potential confounding variables (Table 4). In the crude model (Model 1), individuals with sarcopenic obesity showed a significantly higher risk of falls (OR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.18–1.44) compared to those with possible sarcopenic obesity. After adjusting for age and sex (Model 2), the association remained statistically significant (OR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.01–1.23). Further adjustments for residential area, educational level, smoking status, and drinking status (Model 3) yielded consistent results (OR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.02–1.24), indicating that the relationship between sarcopenic obesity and fall risk is stable and not driven by measured confounders.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine whether the associations between sarcopenic obesity and possible sarcopenic obesity and fall risk varied across demographic and behavioral factors, including age, sex, residential area, education level, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. The results showed that the associations were generally consistent in direction across most subgroups. However, significant interaction effects were observed for age, sex, and education level, particularly in the sarcopenic obesity model (p < 0.05 for interaction). Specifically, the association between sarcopenic obesity and fall risk was stronger among individuals aged 60 years or older (HR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.09–1.76) and males (HR = 1.65, 95% CI: 0.96–2.82), compared to their younger or female counterparts. For possible sarcopenic obesity, a significant association with fall risk was also more evident in older adults (HR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.08–1.42) and in males (HR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.14–1.57), with interaction p-values < 0.001. Among different education levels, the association remained statistically significant or borderline significant in the primary education and illiterate groups, while becoming less consistent in higher-educated strata (e.g., college and above). Discussion

This large-scale prospective cohort study investigated the relationship between sarcopenic obesity and possible sarcopenic obesity, and the risk of falls in middle-aged and older adults. Our findings show that individuals with sarcopenic obesity faced a 25% higher risk of falls, whereas those with possible sarcopenic obesity encountered a 12% increased risk compared to their counterparts without sarcopenia. These associations persisted after adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral factors, highlighting sarcopenic obesity as an independent and clinically significant risk factor for falls. Previous studies have established a link between sarcopenia and fall risk, mainly through cross-sectional designs [9]. While these studies support the connection, they often used different definitions of sarcopenia and couldn’t determine causality. Our study estimated muscle mass using a regression equation validated for the Chinese population (ASM)—a method especially relevant for Asian populations. Additionally, this study offers new insights by systematically assessing the intermediate phenotype of potential sarcopenic obesity. The observed 12% increase in fall risk among individuals in this group highlights the importance of early intervention even before full sarcopenia develops.

The co-occurrence of muscle loss and fat accumulation reflects the complex physiological changes associated with aging [36]. While obesity alone is known to increase the risk of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and other chronic conditions, the synergy between sarcopenia and obesity—sarcopenic obesity—presents a dual burden with compounded health consequences. Our findings align with previous evidence linking sarcopenia to functional decline, cognitive impairment, depression, and mortality [37]. Notably, we used WC instead of BMI to define obesity, as BMI does not differentiate between lean and fat mass. WC, a proxy for visceral adiposity, was significantly Linked to increased fall risk, with sarcopenic individuals with abdominal obesity facing a 25% greater risk of falls. This highlights the importance of utilizing anthropometric measures that better capture fat distribution, particularly in older adults. Furthermore, the results of the sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the observed association between sarcopenic obesity and fall risk. Across three multivariable logistic regression models with incremental adjustments for potential confounders—including age, sex, residential area, educational attainment, smoking, and alcohol use—the association remained statistically significant and consistent in direction. This stability suggests that the relationship is unlikely to be explained solely by demographic or behavioral factors, reinforcing the potential clinical importance of sarcopenic obesity as an independent risk factor for falls.

The public health implications of these findings are significant. Falls are a leading cause of disability and mortality in aging populations [38–40], and sarcopenic obesity may accelerate functional decline through both metabolic and biomechanical mechanisms. Three practical insights arise from our results: (1) the category of “possible sarcopenic obesity” allows for earlier identification of at-risk individuals; (2) WC is a simple, low-cost tool that can enhance fall risk screening in community and clinical settings; and (3) Middle-aged and older adults should adopt preventive measures, like maintaining regular physical activity, to preserve muscle mass and reduce the risk of falls.

Mechanistically, the elevated fall risk associated with sarcopenic obesity is likely multifactorial. Muscle loss and excess fat deposition compromise physical performance, mobility, and neuromuscular coordination. Chronic low-grade inflammation, characterized by elevated C-reactive protein and interleukin-6, has been shown to impair muscle regeneration and accelerate atrophy [41]. Intramuscular fat infiltration disrupts energy metabolism, further reducing strength and balance [42]. Behaviorally, individuals with sarcopenic obesity often exhibit decreased physical activity, abnormal gait, and impaired balance control [43], while joint pain and low bone mineral density associated with obesity exacerbate mobility limitations [44]– [45]. Collectively, these factors contribute to increased fall susceptibility and support the need for integrated clinical management strategies.

Several strengths enhance the credibility of this study. First, the use of a large, nationally representative cohort provides robust evidence for temporal associations. Second, the application of AWGS 2019 criteria allows for accurate classification of sarcopenia and enhances comparability across Asian studies. Third, the use of WC instead of BMI improves the validity of obesity assessment in aging populations. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, fall events were self-reported, which may introduce recall bias and underreporting. Second, muscle mass was estimated using the ASM formula instead of dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which may have led to measurement error. Third, while SARC-F is a valuable clinical screening tool, especially for older adults, its sensitivity in middle-aged populations remains limited. We agree that incorporating such tools in future work could enhance early identification of individuals at risk for sarcopenic obesity. Lastly, although factors such as osteoporosis, bone-related diseases, and a history of fractures are relevant to fall risk, this information was not consistently collected across all waves of the CHARLS dataset. Therefore, it could not be included as a covariate in our analysis. Future research should consider integrating these critical factors when comprehensive data becomes available. Longitudinal studies incorporating DXA-based assessments, objective fall monitoring, and randomized interventions are necessary to confirm causal pathways and refine prevention strategies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a significant association between sarcopenic obesity and an increased risk of falls among middle-aged and older adults in China. The findings highlight the importance of early identification and intervention, particularly in individuals with potential sarcopenia and abdominal obesity. Incorporating routine screenings for muscle function and waist circumference in clinical and community settings may assist in fall prevention strategies. While the results suggest potential clinical relevance, further research, especially longitudinal and interventional studies, is needed to clarify causal pathways and optimize prevention approaches for this at-risk population.
