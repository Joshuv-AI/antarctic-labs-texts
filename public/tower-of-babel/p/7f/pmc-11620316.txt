Teprotumumab versus intravenous methylprednisolone in thyroid eye disease: A systematic review
Authors: Faizan Mehmood, Syed Ali Raza Rizvi, Sarah Alam, Benazir Ansari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Thyroid eye disease (TED), also known as thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy, is an autoimmune disorder caused due to a complex interplay between autoantigens including the thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor and the insulin-like growth factor-I receptor. TED is characterized by progressive proptosis or diplopia. This systematic review aimed to compare the efficacy of the newer monoclonal antibody – teprotumumab and intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) in TED patients. We performed a systematic review of previously published studies from 2013 to June 2023. A total of 329 articles were screened; among them, 111 non-duplicate publications were identified. After the screening of titles and abstracts, 156 publications were excluded; then, another 47 published papers were excluded after the full-text screening. The remaining 15 eligible studies were included in this systematic review. The majority of studies used either teprotumumab alone or in combination with others. Among 15 studies, eight studies used teprotumumab in TED patients, whereas remaining 7 studies used a standard treatment regimen. This systematic review provides an overview of the existing treatment options using monoclonal antibody – teprotumumab and IVMP in TED patients. The overall assessment provides a finding that antibody – teprotumumab is is a good choice compared to conventional IVMP for providing better outcomes in patients with TED.

Introduction

Thyroid eye disease (TED), also known as thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy, is an autoimmune disorder caused as a result of a complex interplay between autoantigens such as the thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor (TSHR) and the insulin-like growth factor-I receptor (IGF-IR). In return, they generate autoantibodies, B- and T-lymphocytes, and various cytokines that upregulate orbital fibroblast activity causing the characteristic soft-tissue expansion in TED. This may cause the patient to develop redness, dry eyes, increased lacrimation, lid retraction of variable degrees in mild-to-moderate cases, and proptosis, diplopia, facial disfigurement, and vision loss in severe cases[12] Various medical treatment modalities presently available for TED include oral glucocorticoids, intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP), azathioprine, rituximab, orbital radiotherapy, mycophenolate, mycophenolate plus IVMP, and teprotumumab.[34] In case of unresponsive active TED or residual inactive disease, surgical treatment can be done.[5] The European Group on Graves’s Orbitopathy (EUGOGO) guidelines recommend an additive dose of 4.5–5 g of IVMP over 12 weeks for moderate-to-severe TED patients. The data show an overall benefit to the patients, and it has been considered as first-line medical treatment in cases of moderate-to-severe and active TED.[4] Teprotumumab, a human monoclonal antibody that inhibits IGF-IR, has been reported to be effective in two different placebo-controlled, double-masked, randomized clinical trials (RCT) with improvement in proptosis and diplopia as well as reduction of inflammation when administered over a period of 24 weeks. It became the first USFDA-approved treatment for TED on January 21, 2021.[67]

TED is also called Graves ophthalmopathy, an autoimmune disorder that is also characterized by progressive inflammation and damage to orbital and ocular tissues.[89] In the US, the age-adjusted prevalence of TED is estimated at 0.25%.[89] With the help of IGF-IR on the orbital fibroblasts, TED causes the expansion of the extraocular muscle and retro-orbital fat.[49] Significant disfiguring facial changes including vision loss in severe cases could occur due to proptosis and disabling diplopia. At present, the noninvasive treatment options that could improve proptosis and diplopia are limited.[9] Although data demonstrate that IVMP reduces inflammation, the dose, time of administration, and therapy duration vary in the literature, introducing challenges to compare the clinical results, especially on the progressive outcomes of proptosis and diplopia. In previous clinical trials, a reduction in proptosis by 2 mm and an improvement of diplopia by 1 grade were considered clinically valid.[9]

During the active stage of TED, orbital inflammatory lymphocytic and macrophage infiltration occurs.[1011] Lubricating eye drops relieve eye symptoms in mild TED cases, and selenium supplements improve soft-tissue signs and TED-related living quality and can also slow down disease progression.[1012] For patients with moderate-to-severe active TED, intravenous glucocorticoid is the first-line treatment due to its anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive actions.[1013] However, the oral route of glucocorticoid is also effective, despite being less tolerated than the intravenous route.[1014]

A varying range of IVMP doses and regimens have been put forth by different studies for the treatment of TED. However, as per the EUGOGO study, a dose of 4.5–5 g is recommended for moderate-to-severe active patients and 7.5 g for more severe TED and for patients with constant/inconstant diplopia. The EUGOGO multicenter RCT used three doses of IVMP, namely 2.25, 4.98, and 7.47 g for a duration of 12 weeks on moderate-to-severe active TED patients. While the 7.47 g dose improved the inflammation, composite ophthalmic index, and the quality of life, there was no improvement in proptosis or diplopia using any of these doses. Similarly, a 7.5 g dose in two other trials also failed to show any clinically significant improvement in proptosis or diplopia, making a 4.5–5 g dose the most recommended for moderate-to-severe TED.[49] This variability across studies is reportedly due to study differences with respect to patient populations and other factors including disease severity, smoking, and sex, influencing treatment response. A comprehensive meta-analysis conducted by Douglas et al.[9] compared IVMP and teprotumumab by adjusting for a few of these differences. As the studies did not have a common comparator arm, they conducted unanchored matching-adjusted indirect comparisons. Second, they compared IVMP and placebo by reducing the effective sample size adjusting for characteristics that increased the likelihood of study differences being compared, resulting in broad confidence intervals and probably confounding the results. Third, since there were not many long-term outcomes studied for both IVMP and teprotumumab the analyses were limited to their respective study times. In addition, they also did not review studies that included compressive optic neuropathy patients, nor other TED therapies such as orbital radiotherapy, rituximab, or tocilizumab.[9] High-dose IVMP is administered under adequate monitoring and medical support. Cardiovascular, liver failures, and similar other severe adverse events were reported in TED patients who received high-dose IVMP making the optimal IVMP regimen still debatable. The complications of IVMP prescription, the importance of balancing the indications, and the dose-related efficacy of IVMP need to be studied extensively. This systematic review aimed to compare the effectiveness of the newer monoclonal antibody – Teprotumumab and IVMP in TED patients.

Materials and Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This systematic review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis criteria. A systematic search of the published literature was conducted from inception to 2013; search results were updated in June 2023 after identifying eligible studies using EMBASE, MEDLINE (via PubMed), and CENTRAL (via Cochrane Library) databases. The search items will consist of terms: “thyroid eye disease,” “thyroid-associated orbitopathy,” “Graves’ orbitopathy,” glucocorticoids,” “methylprednisolone,” and “teprotumumab.”

Inclusion criteria for the systematic review were only studies that include moderate-to-severe active TED patients receiving the recommended dosage of IVMP (4.5 g to 5 g for 12 weeks) with either of the two outcomes (proptosis in millimeters and diplopia according to Bahn-Gorman diplopia score).[8] Data from all published teprotumumab trials for TED as well as Phase 2 or Phase 3 trials were included in this study. Excluded studies were with nonrecommended regimens in terms of dose or duration of IVMP or teprotumumab. Studies without a measurable outcome like improvement in proptosis or diplopia will be excluded. Studies with patients receiving both IVMP and teprotumumab will be excluded. Studies with patients with an orbital surgery history receiving IVMP or teprotumumab were excluded.

The quality of the systematic reviews implicated in the present systematic review was evaluated using the GRADE system. Briefly, the GRADE scoring system is said to have four levels of evidence including very low, low, moderate, and high. Additional characteristics such as imprecision, risk of bias, inconsistencies, and publication bias were assessed for all included studies.[15] Two independent reviewers evaluated the validity of the included using a specific standard checklist. The items relevant for the purpose of review were assessed by the index of inter and intrarater agreement that was assessed using Kappa statistic.

The author’s names, year of publication, country of origin, intervention, and observations were extracted. Outcomes were noted as the majority of articles that the common intervention for the TED.

Results

We performed a systematic review of published studies on the effectiveness of the novel monoclonal antibody teprotumumab and IVMP in TED (2013 to June 2023). A total of 329 articles were screened, and 111 unique publications were identified. One hundred and fifty-six articles were excluded after screening the titles and abstracts and 47 after the full-text screening. The systematic review included the remaining 15 studies [Figure 1].

Systematic analysis of studies

This systematic review had a consistent validity appraisal among the two raters as given by the Kappa statistic of 0.886. Overall, the findings suggest that antibody – teprotumumab is a good choice compared to conventional IVMP for providing better outcomes in patients with TED.

Discussion

In this systematic review, recent therapeutic approaches were focused on treating TED. Patients with this condition mostly present with diplopia, optic neuropathy, and disfiguring proptosis that pose a challenge to physicians. Conventional treatment approaches including steroids and orbital radiotherapy were now successfully replaced with the administration of rituximab or tocilizumab immunotherapies. Nowadays, IVMP is among the preferred treatment candidates for the moderate-to-severe form of this disease.[29]

TED, despite being diagnosed by both clinical examination and radiological testing, different signs of TED could be overlooked, resulting in a delayed diagnosis in at least 25% of the patients.[1030] The management of TED involves evaluation of the inflammation of the eyelid, redness, swelling, and chemosis, and the degree of pain on spontaneous and gaze-evoked eye movement.[1031] In magnetic resonance imaging, a short-tau inversion recovery sequence present on the coronal plane is also considered a sensitive radiological marker for the activity of the disease that provides the signal intensity ratio by comparing individual extraocular and the ipsilateral temporalis muscle signal intensities.[1032] The typical biphasic course run by TED, begins with a rapid progressive inflammation peaking after 6–24 months, before a chronic fibrotic phase of inconsistent duration.[33] Early treatment is recommended during the inflammatory phase to control the disease activity.[10]

Glucocorticoid reduces inflammation in TED and was the common gold standard treatment of active TED.[30] In patients with moderate-to-severe and active TED, high-dose intravenous corticosteroid therapy remained the primary treatment.[4] Increased infiltrations of inflammatory cells occur during the disease’s active phase. Bartalena et al. previously reported a 7.47 g IVMP regimen provided an interim advantage over a lower dose but with increased toxicity.[34] He et al. reported a 4.5 g regimen for 12 weeks to be less effective than a 1.5 g IVMP for 3 months for ocular symptoms and disease activity reduction with far less recurrence.[35] Zhu et al. discovered that the 4.5 g IVMP for 12 weeks was effective and safer than the 4.5 g IVMP daily regimen.[36] Mu et al. recently reported that both daily and weekly IVMP protocols with a cumulative dose of 4.5 g produced comparable response rates.[37]

TED pathogenesis is known to be initiated by IGF-I. IGF-IR showed overexpression by the orbital fibroblasts along with B- and T-cells and played a central role in the pathogenesis.[163839] Thyrotropin receptors and IGF-IRs form physical and functional complexes that cause TED, additionally accumulate hyaluronan and express cytokines, causing edema, inflammation, and extraocular muscle and adipose tissue expansion.[404142] Teprotumumab blocks the signaling at either of the receptors, further preventing pathologic immune responses in active TED.[404142] Teprotumumab for patients with active TED demonstrated the interactions with the antibodies against the thyrotropin receptor and IGF-1R forming a functional signaling complex presented on the orbital fibroblasts.[43] Previous findings also showed that teprotumumab controls extraocular muscle volume and fat reduction that involves IGF-1 and subsequently reduced proptosis and diplopia.[43] In our systematic review, the majority of the studies reported the use of teprotumumab for TED.

Conventional systemic corticosteroids treat moderate-to-severe TED.[32] It was found the administration of intravenous corticosteroids was better than oral corticosteroids.[4445] A previous study found that IVMP is 26% more effective in treating Graves’ orbitopathy compared to oral prednisone.[44] It was reported that IV corticosteroids exhibit hepatotoxicity if given more than 8 g of methylprednisolone.[1331] Studies have reported that steroid therapy is associated with severe hypertension, hepatitis, liver, and cardiovascular dysfunction, undetected or uncontrolled diabetes, and steroid-responsive glaucoma.[313446]

High-dose intravenous glucocorticoid is the mainstay of treatment in moderate-to-severe and active TED patients. The infiltration of inflammatory cells occurs at the active TED phase lasting from 6 months to 2 years, with the disease correlating with the amount of infiltration.[11] The guidelines by the EUGOGO recommended two IVMP regimens in combination with mycophenolate sodium as first-line treatment for moderate-to-severe active TED.[3] However, to date, there has been no severity- or activity-driven IVMP regimen for treating TED. Recent advances include biological agents such as tocilizumab, a recombinant humanized immunoglobulin G1 anti-interleukin 6 monoclonal antibody, and interleukin 6 is known to increase TSHR expression in fibroblasts. Tocilizumab is considered a better treatment option for TED patients intolerant to IVMP.[47]

Teprotumumab, an all-human monoclonal IGF-1R inhibitory antibody, blocks signaling through this receptor complex, thus attenuating inflammation and ultimately reducing retro-orbital tissue expansion. The objective of our analysis was to systematically evaluate the aggregate response to teprotumumab from the two pivotal trials, by specifically focusing on responses for the major severity components of TED, namely proptosis and diplopia. These integrated analyses have allowed us to investigate patient subgroups, including those recognized to be more difficult to treat (e.g., men, tobacco users, higher antithyrotropin receptor antibodies [thyrotropin-binding inhibitory immunoglobulin; TBII], older age, longer disease duration, and patients with more progressive disease).

Large doses of IVMP were utilized in conditions such as multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury, and myasthenia gravis.[48] Usual side effects encompass alterations in taste perception, facial redness, stomach discomfort or ulcers, sleep disturbances, and changes in appetite.[49] According to Marinó et al., acute liver injury was observed in 0.3% of patients undergoing IVMP treatment for TED. Although this difference was not statistically significant, the dosage administered to patients who passed away was higher compared to those who recovered.[46] Understanding TED pathophysiology remains intricate and not entirely elucidated. A recent study indicated that within the orbit, there exists a subgroup of orbital fibrocytes that exhibit heightened expression of TSHR. These specific cells have the capacity to transform into myofibroblasts upon stimulation, enlarging soft tissues that is observed in TED.[50] In individuals with TED, orbital fibroblasts were identified to exhibit a significant expression of IGF-1 receptors. These receptors play a pivotal role in activating fibroblasts and prompting the production of glycosaminoglycans and hyaluronic acid which are elements of the extracellular matrix.[51] Both TSHR and IGF-1R are found in the orbital fibroblasts. The cross-talk between these mutually increased the secretion of hyaluronic acid in the orbit. In addition increasing the efficacy and potency of TSH while IGF-1 is present.[52] The actual role of autoimmunity in the pathogenesis of TED is still being determined. Although there are few studies that compared different TED treatments, these results were supported by previous studies conducted using other steroid regimens. A meta-analysis reported glucocorticoids ineffective in proptosis improvement compared to other monotherapies (like mycophenolate mofetil) in TED patients.[9] Moreover, only one published study[41] has conducted a comparison between IVMP (with 6 participants randomized to receive 6 g over 4 months) and a placebo group (comprising 9 participants); however, this study fell short of meeting its intended recruitment numbers. A higher response rate in the primary outcome (a combined measure) for IVMP in comparison to the placebo among individuals with active moderate-to-severe TED was observed upon analysis after 2.5 years of initial enrolment. Nevertheless, the change in proptosis did not display a statistically significant difference from the placebo, showing an average shift of 0.6 mm. Improvements in diplopia also did not exhibit statistical significance (with 2 out of 4 IVMP-treated patients improving compared to none out of 5 in the placebo group).[9]

Therefore, the associated fatal complications with the IV methylprednisolone make it as second choice of treatment candidate for TED. Our systematic review also showed that most of the studies use monoclonal immunotherapy compared to IV methylprednisolone therapy.
