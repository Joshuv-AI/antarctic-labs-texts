Oral pathogens reprogram endothelial barrier and angiotensin-converting enzyme2/endothelial nitric oxide synthase-nitric oxide signaling to promote blood pressure dysregulation
Authors: Ming-Shou Hsieh, Tung-Nien Hsu, Chin-Sheng Huang, Ting-Yi Renn, Kuang-Tai Kuo, Kuo-Yao Lin, Wei Jen Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeAlthough periodontal disease is associated with an increased risk of hypertension, the endothelial mechanisms linking oral inflammation to vascular dysfunction remain unclear. We investigated whether Porphyromonas gingivalis lipopolysaccharide (Pg-LPS) can directly disturb endothelial homeostasis and whether macrophage –endothelial crosstalk further worsens endothelial injury.Materials and methodsHuman umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were exposed to Pg-LPS alone or co-cultured (transwell system) with Pg-LPS-primed human monocytic cells -derived macrophages. Endothelial dysfunction was assessed through nitric oxide (NO) production, endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) phosphorylation, inflammatory and adhesion molecule expression, as well as junctional and cytoskeletal changes. Macrophage activation was evaluated by nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling and M1-like inflammatory polarization. Mechanistic studies were further carried out with inhibitors against toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), rho-associated kinase (ROCK), NOS, and the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) signaling axis.ResultsPg-LPS directly triggered endothelial inflammation, with increased interleukin-6 and -8, vascular cell adhesion molecule-1/intercellular adhesion moldcule-1 expression, enhanced reactive oxygen species and endothelial permeability. Pg-LPS also suppressed eNOS activation and NO production, and disturbed ACE/ACE2 balance. In co-culture, Pg-LPS-stimulated macrophages further aggravated endothelial inflammation, barrier dysfunction, and NO-related signaling impairment. TLR4 inhibition attenuated inflammatory and barrier-disruptive responses, while modulation of the ACE/ACE2–eNOS pathway altered endothelial NO signaling.ConclusionPg-LPS disrupts endothelial barrier integrity and NO signaling both directly and indirectly through inflammatory macrophage–endothelial crosstalk, involving TLR2/4–NF- κB, Rho/ROCK signaling and ACE2/eNOS imbalance. Introduction

Periodontal disease is no longer viewed as a purely localized oral condition. Increasing evidence suggests that it may contribute to systemic disorders, particularly hypertension and other cardiovascular complications.1 Among the mechanisms proposed to explain this link, endothelial dysfunction is especially compelling, as the endothelium sits at the interface between inflammatory challenge and vascular regulation. Once endothelial homeostasis is disturbed, changes in vascular tone, permeability, and inflammatory responsiveness may follow, creating conditions favorable for blood pressure dysregulation.2

A central component of endothelial homeostasis is nitric oxide (NO), which is generated mainly by endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS).3 Adequate NO production supports vasodilation, limits leukocyte–endothelial interactions, suppresses inflammatory activation, and helps maintain barrier integrity. By contrast, reduced eNOS activity and loss of NO bioavailability are well-recognized features of dysfunctional endothelium and are closely tied to heightened vascular resistance.4 Endothelial health is also influenced by the balance between the angiotensin-converting enzyme/angiotensin II (ACE/Ang II) and ACE2-associated pathways. While the former promotes vasoconstrictive and pro-inflammatory signaling, the latter acts in a counter-regulatory manner to preserve endothelial function and NO-related vascular protection.5 Under inflammatory conditions, disruption of this balance may further intensify vascular injury. Endothelial dysfunction also involves structural deterioration of the barrier itself. Intercellular junctions and the actin cytoskeleton work together to preserve endothelial integrity, but these structures are highly sensitive to inflammatory stress.6 Junctional breakdown, stress fiber formation, and increased permeability are common consequences of endothelial activation. The Ras homolog gene family member A (RhoA)/Rho associated coiled-coil forming protein kinase (ROCK) pathway is a major regulator of these changes and has been strongly linked to cytoskeletal tension, endothelial contraction, and vascular leak.

Among periodontal pathogens, Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg) is considered a major driver of chronic inflammatory injury.7 Its lipopolysaccharide, Pg-LPS, can activate innate immune pathways in both vascular and immune cells. In endothelial cells, this may trigger Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) activation, cytokine release, adhesion molecule expression, oxidative stress, and barrier disruption.8 Although these effects are consistent with endothelial activation, whether Pg-LPS coordinately alters inflammatory signaling, cytoskeletal remodeling, and the ACE2/eNOS/NO regulatory network remains insufficiently resolved. Another unresolved issue is the contribution of macrophage–endothelial communication. Inflammatory macrophages exposed to periodontal stimuli may release soluble mediators that amplify endothelial injury beyond the effect of direct bacterial stimulation alone. Such crosstalk could provide an important mechanistic bridge between local periodontal inflammation and systemic vascular dysfunction.9

Based on this rationale, we examined whether Pg-LPS directly impaired endothelial homeostasis and whether Pg- LPS-stimulated macrophages further worsened endothelial injury through indirect crosstalk. Using HUVECs and THP-1-derived M0 macrophages, we evaluated inflammatory activation, adhesion molecule expression, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, endothelial permeability, and NO-related signaling. We further applied pharmacologic inhibitors to probe the roles of TLR, Rho/ROCK, and ACE2/eNOS-associated pathways in this process.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

Primary human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; PCS-100-010, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were maintained in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 (EGM-2; CC-3162, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) containing the manufacturer-supplied BulletKit supplements. Cultures were kept in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and cells from passages 3 to 8 were used in all experiments. Human monocytic cells (THP-1; TIB-202, ATCC) were propagated in RPMI-1640 medium (11875-093, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10099- 141, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (15140-122, Gibco). For periodontal inflammatory stimulation, Porphyromonas gingivalis lipopolysaccharide (Pg-LPS; tlrlpglps, InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA) was applied at the indicated concentrations. THP-1 differentiation was induced using phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; P1585, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Macrophage polarization experiments further employed recombinant human interferon-γ (IFN-γ; 300-02, PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA), IL-4 (200-04, PeproTech), and IL-10 (200-10, PeproTech).

Differentiation and polarization of THP-1-derived macrophages

To generate macrophage-like cells, THP-1 monocytes were exposed to PMA (100 nM) for 24 h, after which the medium was replaced and the cells were allowed to recover for an additional 24 h. This procedure yielded adherent M0-like macrophages.

For phenotype induction, M0 cells were stimulated either with LPS (100 ng/mL; L4391, Sigma–Aldrich) together with IFN-γ (20 ng/mL) to obtain an M1-like state, or with IL-4 (20 ng/mL) plus IL-10 (20 ng/mL) to induce an M2-like phenotype. In experiments evaluating the effect of periodontal endotoxin, M0 macrophages were incubated with Pg-LPS (1 μg/mL) for 24 h. After stimulation, cultures were rinsed thoroughly with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to reduce carryover of residual LPS before subsequent assays and co-culture procedures.

Indirect macrophage–endothelial co-culture

To investigate macrophage-dependent endothelial responses without direct cell-to-cell contact, an indirect transwell system was used. Pg-LPS-treated THP-1-derived macrophages were placed in the upper compartment of inserts (0.4 μm pore size, 3422, Corning, Corning, NY, USA), whereas HUVECs were seeded in the lower compartment. The two cell populations were maintained together for 24 h. This setup allowed soluble mediators released from activated macrophages to influence endothelial cells while limiting direct Pg-LPS exposure to the HUVEC layer.

Pg-LPS treatment and viability assessment

For direct stimulation experiments, HUVECs were incubated with graded concentrations of Pg-LPS ranging from 0 to 1 μg/mL. Cell metabolic activity, used here as an indicator of viability, was determined by MTT assay (M5655, Sigma–Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was recorded at 570 nm on a Synergy HTX microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

Total cellular RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Reverse transcription was carried out using a cDNA synthesis kit (K1622, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was then performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (A25742, Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Relative transcript abundance was normalized against GAPDH and expressed using the 2−ΔΔCt calculation method. PCR primer sequences are shown in Table 1.

Western blotting

Whole-cell proteins were extracted in RIPA lysis buffer (89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (04693159001, Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equivalent amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Merck Millipore, St. Louis, MA, USA). After blocking, membranes were probed with antibodies against eNOS, phospho-eNOS, VCAM-1, ICAM-1, and β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA). Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (32106, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and band intensity was analyzed using ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence analysis

For fluorescence staining, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma–Aldrich), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin. Samples were then incubated with primary antibodies recognizing inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) (Cell Signaling Technology), followed by Alexa Fluor-labeled secondary antibodies (Invitrogen). Nuclear counterstaining was performed with DAPI (D1306, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were obtained using a confocal microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss, Germany), and fluorescence signal intensity was quantified in ImageJ.

Endothelial migration assay

The migratory behavior of HUVECs was examined using a wound closure assay. Endothelial cells were grown to a confluent monolayer in 6-well plates, and a straight scratch was created with a sterile pipette tip. Detached cells were removed by washing, after which the cultures were exposed either to Pg-LPS or to the indicated co-culture conditions. Images were collected immediately after scratching and again at 24 h with an inverted microscope (IX73, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Migration capacity was expressed as the percentage of wound closure relative to the initial wound area.

Public transcriptomic data mining and bioinformatic analysis

Gene expression profiles were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), including datasets GSE184085, GSE16134, and GSE10334. Data handling and normalization were performed in R (version 4.2.0), primarily using the limma package. Differentially expressed genes were screened with cutoffs of |log2 fold change|> 1 and adjusted P < 0.05.

Expression patterns were visualized by heatmaps generated with pheatmap, while dimensionality reduction and clustering analyses were conducted using FactoMineR and Seurat for transcriptome component analysis and UMAP projection, respectively. Differential expression distributions were displayed with volcano plots created in ggplot2. Functional interpretation of altered genes was further explored by DAVID-based annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment. Gene interaction networks were assembled and rendered in Cytoscape (version 3.9.1).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Quantitative results are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical testing was carried out using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). For comparisons involving two groups, Student’s t-test was applied. Multi-group analyses were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Transcriptomic profiling reveals P. gingivalis–induced inflammatory signatures in endothelial and gingival tissues across multiple GEO datasets

Transcriptomic analysis across multiple GEO datasets demonstrated a consistent inflammation-associated gene expression pattern in both endothelial and gingival tissues following P. gingivalis exposure. Heatmap analysis of GSE184085 showed that P. gingivalis infection markedly altered the HUVEC transcriptomic profile, with infected samples clearly separating from uninfected controls and exhibiting broad gene expression changes (Fig. 1A). This distinction was further supported by principal component analysis, in which the two groups formed discrete clusters (Fig. 1B). Gene network analysis further indicated that these transcriptional alterations were organized around a closely connected regulatory network associated with cellular stress and inflammatory signaling (Fig. 1C). Similar infection-related transcriptomic remodeling was also observed in gingival tissue datasets. In both GSE16134 and GSE10334, volcano plot analysis revealed a large number of differentially expressed genes enriched in infection- and inflammation-related responses (Fig. 1D). Collectively, these findings indicate that P. gingivalis elicits a shared inflammatory transcriptional program across both endothelial and gingival tissue contexts.

Pg-LPS activates inflammatory signaling and promotes a migratory endothelial phenotype in HUVECs

To further characterize the endothelial response to Pg-LPS, we first examined the regulatory and functional changes induced by stimulation in HUVECs. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the predicted regulatory network links Pg-LPS-responsive microRNAs to PI3K/AKT–eNOS and NF-κB-related signaling, suggesting coordinated effects on inflammatory activation, oxidative stress, and endothelial homeostasis. Fig. 2B shows that the selected Pg-LPS exposure condition did not cause overt cytotoxicity, supporting its use for subsequent mechanistic experiments. Pg-LPS increased the expression of inflammatory and endothelial activation markers, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-8, vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), ROS-related responses, and von Willebrand factor (VWF), indicating that Pg-LPS drives a clear activated endothelial phenotype (Fig. 2C). At the protein level, Western blot analysis further showed that Pg-LPS enhanced inflammatory signaling while altering endothelial protective pathways, consistent with activation of a pro-inflammatory state in HUVECs (Fig. 2D). Functionally, scratch assay results demonstrated that Pg-LPS-treated HUVECs exhibited increased migratory activity compared with control cells, suggesting a repair-like but dysregulated migratory response under inflammatory stress (Fig. 2E). In parallel, pathway enrichment analysis identified significant activation of IL-6, NF-κB, and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 2F), and UMAP-based clustering further supported the presence of distinct Pg-LPS-associated transcriptional states enriched for inflammatory signaling programs (Fig. 2G). Based on these findings, the proposed model in Fig. 2H suggests that Pg-LPS activates TLR4-driven NF-κB/MAPK signaling, leading to cytokine production, cytoskeletal remodeling, and enhanced endothelial migration, collectively contributing to endothelial dysfunction.

Pg-LPS induces pro-inflammatory polarization of THP-1-derived macrophages and supports two complementary macrophage–endothelial interaction models

To further investigate macrophage–endothelial crosstalk under periodontal inflammatory conditions, we established an indirect co-culture system using THP-1-derived macrophages and HUVECs (Fig. 3A). THP-1 cells were first differentiated into M0-like macrophages with PMA, then exposed to Pg-LPS, followed by washout before indirect transwell co-culture with HUVECs. This approach was intended to isolate macrophage-derived paracrine effects from any direct action of Pg-LPS on endothelial cells. Before moving to the co-culture experiments, we verified that the THP-1-derived macrophages responded normally to conventional polarization stimuli. As shown in Fig. 3B and C, LPS/IFN-γ induced M1-associated markers, whereas IL-4/IL-10 favored M2-related marker expression, supporting the suitability of this system for inflammatory modeling. We next tested whether Pg-LPS itself could push THP-1-derived macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory state. As outlined in Fig. 3D, Pg-LPS directly increased the expression of inflammatory mediators and macrophage activation-related genes, indicating successful priming of the cells. These Pg- LPS-treated macrophages were then used in the indirect THP-1–HUVEC co-culture model to assess whether activated macrophages could transmit inflammatory signals to endothelial cells without direct Pg-LPS exposure. In line with the gene expression data, immunofluorescence analysis showed stronger iNOS and COX-2 staining after Pg-LPS treatment (Fig. 3E), and this increase was confirmed quantitatively (Fig. 3F). These findings support that Pg-LPS drives THP-1-derived macrophages into an inflammatory, M1-like state and establishes a useful platform for studying macrophage-mediated endothelial injury.

Discussion

The present study provides mechanistic support for how periodontal inflammatory stimuli may contribute to vascular dysfunction through both direct endothelial injury and indirect immune-mediated amplification.10 Using Pg- LPS-based in vitro models, we found that endothelial dysfunction could be initiated by direct exposure of HUVECs to Pg-LPS and further intensified by Pg-LPS-activated macrophages through paracrine signaling. Across these complementary models, Pg-LPS promoted endothelial inflammatory activation, increased oxidative stress, disrupted barrier-related properties, and impaired NO-associated signaling. Our findings support the idea that periodontal inflammation can disturb vascular homeostasis not only through direct pathogen-derived signals, but also through inflammatory crosstalk involving immune cells. Although clinical and population-based studies have consistently reported associations between periodontal disease and elevated blood pressure, the endothelial mechanisms connecting oral inflammation to vascular dysregulation have remained insufficiently defined.11,12 In our direct endothelial model, Pg-LPS induced a clear inflammatory response in HUVECs, with increased expression of IL-6, IL-8, VCAM-1, ICAM-1, and VWF, along with enhanced ROS-related responses and altered migratory behavior. These changes are consistent with a dysfunctional endothelial phenotype marked by inflammatory activation, barrier instability, and impaired vascular regulatory capacity. A major finding of this study is that Pg-LPS interfered with NO-related endothelial signaling. Endothelial NO is central to the maintenance of vascular tone, anti-inflammatory balance, and barrier integrity, and loss of eNOS activity with reduced NO bioavailability is a well-recognized feature of endothelial dysfunction and hypertension. 13,14 Pg-LPS suppressed eNOS-associated signaling and reduced NO-related responses, indicating a loss of endothelial vasoprotective function.15 ACE/ACE2-associated signaling was also disturbed, suggesting that periodontal inflammatory stimuli may shift endothelial regulation away from protective, NO-supportive pathways and toward a more vasoconstrictive and pro-inflammatory state.16 This is biologically relevant because the ACE2 axis normally counterbalances vascular inflammation and supports endothelial function, whereas disruption of this pathway may further aggravate oxidative stress and NO deficiency.17 Pg-LPS also promoted barrier-disruptive endothelial responses. Increased permeability, junctional instability, and cytoskeletal remodeling are characteristic features of inflamed endothelium and are closely linked to vascular dysfunction.18 Our data point to the involvement of Rho/ROCK-associated signaling in this process, which is consistent with the known role of this pathway in endothelial contraction, actin stress fiber formation, and intercellular gap formation. The induction of adhesion molecules such as VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 further indicates that Pg-LPS not only weakens barrier integrity, but also drives the endothelium toward a more pro-adhesive state that may facilitate leukocyte recruitment and sustain vascular inflammation.19 Another important observation was that endothelial dysfunction was not confined to direct Pg-LPS exposure. Pg-LPS also pushed THP-1-derived macrophages toward an inflammatory, M1-like phenotype, accompanied by increased inflammatory mediator expression and elevated iNOS and COX-2 signals.20 When these macrophages were introduced into the indirect transwell co-culture system, they provided a model to examine macrophage–endothelial crosstalk without direct Pg-LPS stimulation of HUVECs. This distinction is important because it separates primary endothelial sensing of bacterial stimuli from secondary injury mediated by immune cell-derived soluble factors. Our results therefore suggest that periodontal inflammatory signals may damage the endothelium through two connected routes: direct pathogen-triggered activation and macrophage-derived paracrine amplification.

The vascular endothelium is unlikely to encounter circulating bacterial products alone; it may also be exposed to cytokines and other mediators released from activated monocytes and macrophages.21 Endothelial dysfunction in the setting of periodontitis is therefore unlikely to arise from a single linear mechanism. More plausibly, it reflects the combined action of direct Pg-LPS-triggered signaling and a secondary inflammatory amplification loop. Such a framework may help explain how a chronic inflammatory condition localized in the oral cavity can nonetheless contribute to broader vascular injury and blood pressure dysregulation. The inhibitor experiments further support the mechanistic involvement of these pathways. Blocking TLR-related signaling attenuated inflammatory activation and barrier-disruptive responses in the direct endothelial model, whereas modulation of the ACE/ACE2–eNOS axis altered NO-related signaling.22 These findings argue against a purely nonspecific toxic effect and instead indicate that the endothelial responses to periodontal inflammatory stimuli are mediated through identifiable signaling networks that are at least partly reversible. At the same time, the co-culture experiments suggest that once macrophage–endothelial crosstalk is established, endothelial dysfunction may no longer depend solely on direct LPS receptor signaling. Under those conditions, macrophage-derived cytokine networks may become sufficient to sustain endothelial injury, which could limit the effectiveness of targeting only a single upstream pathway.

Several limitations should also be considered. The experiments were performed in reductionist in vitro systems, which are well suited for mechanistic analysis but cannot fully capture vascular heterogeneity, immune complexity, hemodynamic stress, or systemic neurohumoral regulation in vivo. The indirect co-culture model was intentionally simplified to isolate macrophage-mediated paracrine effects after Pg-LPS priming, whereas endothelial cells in living organisms are likely exposed simultaneously to microbial products and immune-derived mediators. In addition, although our data implicate TLR2/4-, Rho/ROCK-, and ACE2/eNOS-associated pathways, the temporal hierarchy and functional interplay among these signals remain to be clarified.

Our data indicate that Pg-LPS disrupts endothelial homeostasis through both direct and macrophage-mediated mechanisms. In endothelial cells, Pg-LPS activated TLR2/4–NF-κB inflammatory signaling, increased oxidative stress, impaired barrier integrity, and disturbed ACE2/eNOS/NOassociated regulation. In parallel, Pg-LPS-primed macrophages amplified endothelial injury through paracrine signaling in the indirect co-culture system. Together, these effects provide a mechanistic link between periodontal inflammation and vascular dysregulation relevant to hypertension. Accordingly, periodontal disease may be better regarded not only as an oral inflammatory disorder, but also as a potential contributor to systemic vascular risk and chronic vascular stress (Fig. 4). Further studies in animal models and clinically relevant settings integrating periodontal status with vascular functional assessment are needed.

Proposed mechanism linking periodontal pathogen-derived lipopolysaccharide to endothelial dysfunction and vascular dysregulation through direct endothelial signaling and macrophage-mediated inflammatory amplification. LPS released from oral pathogens enters the systemic circulation and directly activates endothelial toll-like receptors (TLR2/TLR4), MyD88-NF-κB–mediated inflammatory responses, inducing inflammation. In parallel, LPS stimulates monocytes/macrophages to release pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and MCP-1), thereby amplifying vascular inflammatory microenvironment. The combined effects of direct endothelial activation and macrophage-derived paracrine signaling result in endothelial barrier disruption, increase vascular permeability, and impair NO signaling through reduced eNOS activation, increased iNOS expression and ACE/ACE2 imbalance. Together, these this integrated model highlights the dual contribution of direct pathogen-derived signaling and immune cell–mediated amplification in linking periodontal inflammation to systemic vascular disease.
