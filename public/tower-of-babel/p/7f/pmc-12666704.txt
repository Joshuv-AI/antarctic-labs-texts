Understanding microbiota-driven oncogenesis: The role of metabolites in tumorigenesis
Authors: Sadeeq Ullah, Chunyan Wu, Xiao Zou, Yangqing Zhong, Aftab Ahmad, Amin Ullah Jan, Yanguang Cong, Yong Chen, Lu Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe gut microbiota profoundly influence human health through its composition and metabolic activity, which are shaped by diet and host factors. Disruption of this equilibrium, known as dysbiosis, has been implicated in cancer development. Microbial metabolites act as key mediators connecting the gut microbiome to tumor progression by altering the tumor microenvironment and modulating signaling pathways in tumor and immune cells. This review examines the mechanistic links between the gut microbiota, its metabolites, and tumorigenesis, emphasizing how microbial metabolism of host- and diet-derived compounds affects oncogenic and genotoxic processes in colorectal cancer (CRC). It also explores how dietary and host factors modulate microbial composition and function, influencing cancer susceptibility. Understanding these interconnected processes provides conceptual insight into the microbiota-cancer axis and highlights opportunities for microbiome-targeted interventions in cancer prevention and therapy.

Introduction

The human body is home to a vast and diverse community of microbiota, with hundreds to thousands of distinct bacterial species often coexisting in the gut simultaneously.1 This shows a significantly greater genetic variety than that of the human genome.2 The long-standing symbiosis between bacteria and the host has evolved into a highly integrated system characterized by complex molecular crosstalk. This interaction supports important physiological processes, including metabolic regulation, nutrient absorption, and energy homeostasis, underscoring the reciprocal interdependence between host and microbes.3 The gut microbiota display noticeable inter-individual variability and are strongly influenced by lifestyle factors such as diet, smoking, and physical activity.4 Repeated antibiotic exposure, even during early development, is a well-established cause of dysbiosis, a destructive microbial imbalance linked to various cancers.5,6 Approximately 99% of the microbial biomass exists in the gastrointestinal tract, which exerts significant effects on both local and systemic physiological functions. Therefore, the gut microbiome plays a dominant role in maintaining overall health and metabolic stability.7 Moreover, the microbial community and its relative abundance differ across various locations within organs.8,9 These spatial differences may contribute to site-specific tumor development. The large intestine harbors a denser microbial population than the small intestine, which may help explain its higher incidence of cancer.10 In a healthy state, the gut microbiome functions symbiotically, conferring protection against pathogens and tumor formation.11 However, when this delicate equilibrium is disturbed, dysbiosis can emerge, driving diverse pathologies, including cancer.12 Recent studies indicate that gut microbes contribute to the advancement of tumorigenesis by producing carcinogenic microbial metabolites.13,14 Dysbiosis is now recognized as a mark of cancer; however, only a few microorganisms have been shown to directly initiate tumors or modulate host immunity to favor tumor progression.15,16 The genetic richness of the gut microbiota is remarkably more than 1,000 bacterial species collectively possessing a gene pool over 150 times larger than that of the human genome, highlighting their profound biological influence. The gut microbiota is dominated by a diverse group of bacterial genera, including Escherichia, Fusobacterium, Enterococcus, Bacteroides, Streptococcus, and Peptostreptococcus, with notable species including Escherichia coli, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Bacteroides fragilis, Streptococcus gallolyticus, and Enterococcus faecalis.17 These bacteria generate a wide range of metabolites from both undigested dietary components and compounds produced by the host.18 Many of these metabolites exhibit carcinogenic properties, implicating them in cancer pathogenesis.19,20 Colorectal cancer (CRC) poses a major healthcare challenge globally, ranking third in prevalence among all malignancies and stands as the second most common cause of death, responsible for approximately 500,000 deaths annually, posing a significant healthcare challenge worldwide.21 In China, CRC ranks as the second most prevalent cancer, accounting for nearly 30% of global cases. The economic burden is substantial, with per capita medical costs exceeding 61,000 CNY in 2014 and doubling within a decade.22 The gut microbiota influence several aspects of tumor biology, including transformation, progression, and responsiveness to therapies such as immunotherapy.23,24 Infectious agents account for over 16% of cancer cases worldwide.25 Although bacteria have been identified within multiple tumor types,26,27,28 their specific roles remain insufficiently defined.7 The gut microbiota produces a wide array of metabolites from both dietary and host-derived substrates, many of which contribute to carcinogenesis.18,29,30,31 The exact mechanisms underlying the modulation of tumorigenesis by gut microbial metabolites are not yet fully understood and require further investigation. In this review, we summarize the emerging evidence linking microbial metabolites to cancer progression, elucidate potential mechanisms of metabolite-driven tumorigenesis, and discuss recent insights into host-microbe interactions that influence cancer development.

The host-microbiota interface and homeostasis

The gut microbiota, often referred to as the “second brain,” exercises a profound impact on diverse physiological and pathological processes.32 Microorganisms have shaped the evolution of life on Earth and those colonizing the human body significantly modulate physiological function, health status, and disease susceptibility.33,34 The colon harbors the highest density of microorganisms within the gastrointestinal tract. These microbes engage in dynamic reciprocal crosstalk with the host, regulating a wide spectrum of biological processes and disease outcomes.34,35 Microbial cells inhabiting the human body, including those on mucosal surfaces and the skin, are at least as numerous as somatic cells and collectively encode far more genes than the human genome.36,37 It is estimated that 500 to 1,000 bacterial species coexist within the human body at any given time,1 although the number of distinct genotypes or subspecies is likely much higher. The composition of the gut microbiota varies markedly between individuals and is highly sensitive to lifestyle factors such as diet, smoking, and physical activity.2,38 The gut microbial gene pool is particularly rich, containing at least 100 times more genes than the human genome, which encode diverse enzymes and proteins that support energy extraction and metabolic adaptation to the intestinal niche. Approximately half of the metabolites found in feces and urine are of microbial origin, underscoring the microbiota’s central role in shaping host metabolism.39 Gut-derived metabolites strongly modulate host physiology and immunity, forming the biochemical foundation of the host-microbe axis and sustaining symbiotic equilibrium. The gut microbiota contributes to digestion, nutrient absorption, vitamin synthesis, and immune regulation.2 Resident microbes ferment indigestible dietary fibers to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), principally acetate, propionate, and butyrate, which are important for immune system development40 and for upholding intestinal homeostasis.41 Butyrate activates peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), promoting the proliferation of regulatory T cells (Tregs). These cells enhance β-oxidation and oxygen consumption in intestinal epithelial cells, preserving an anaerobic environment within the gut lumen (Figure 1). This creates a positive feedback loop; anaerobic bacteria generate SCFAs, which support epithelial hypoxia and in turn stabilize microbial community structure and gut homeostasis.42 Beyond local intestinal effects, the microbiota communicates with distant organs through a network of signaling molecules. These include structural components such as lipopolysaccharides (LPSs), peptidoglycans, and flagellin, as well as small microbial metabolites like SCFAs and amino acid derivatives (e.g., phenols and indoles). These molecules interact with host G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), mediating systemic metabolic and immune signaling.43 Microbiota-derived metabolites also influence host epigenetic regulation by modulating chromatin remodeling and gene expression. Microbial modulation of folate biosynthesis can alter DNA methylation, a key mechanism in epigenetic gene regulation in mammals.44,45 Revealing the metabolomic profile of the gut microbiota and its interactions with host pathways is crucial for understanding the mechanisms of host-microbe crosstalk and its systemic effects on distant organs and tissues.Figure 1Gut microbiota and homeostasisThe colonocytes sustain a state of hypoxia, which supports the proliferation of beneficial obligate anaerobes. Functional activity of the gut microbiota

The role of the gut microbiota in health and disease has usually been explored through DNA-based analyses that quantify microbial abundance. However, recent research highlights the limitations of this approach, as it assumes that taxonomic abundance directly reflects functional activity. Studies such as Schirmer et al.46 have demonstrated that microbial activity, rather than abundance, provides a more accurate representation of the microbiota’s contribution to CRC. For example, taxa with low abundance may exhibit high metabolic activity, playing central roles in cancer progression through their functional outputs. This realization has focused the integration of advanced multi-omics techniques, including metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, to better characterize the activity of gut microbes. Metabolomics permits the identification of small molecules derived from microbial metabolism, such as secondary bile acids (SBAs) and SCFAs, and links these to CRC pathogenesis. Transcriptomics elucidates actively expressed microbial genes and pathways involved in host-microbe interactions, while proteomics reveals microbial proteins that modulate host signaling cascades, including the Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT pathways, both central to CRC development.47,48 By focusing on activity, researchers have identified microbial metabolites that drive tumorigenesis, including genotoxic compounds and pro-inflammatory molecules, highlighting the disconnect between abundance and functional relevance. Moreover, this shift opens new paths for clinical applications. Functional insights can guide the development of biomarkers for CRC detection, such as metabolite profiles, and inform therapeutic strategies that target harmful microbial activity or enhance beneficial metabolites like butyrate. Collectively, focusing on microbial activity rather than abundance represents a paradigm shift that not only deepens understanding of CRC pathogenesis but also lays the origin for microbiota-based precision medicine.

The host selects the gut microbiota

The host has developed mechanisms to regulate the gut microbiota, developing the growth of beneficial bacteria capable of breaking down complex macromolecules into valuable products. The dominant bacterial groups in the adult colon are Clostridia (Firmicutes) and Bacteroidia (Bacteroidetes), both strictly anaerobic taxa that confer metabolic assistance through the production of diverse carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzymes. While facultative anaerobes such as Proteobacteria lack the specialized enzymatic machinery required for fiber degradation, they may compete with the host for energy substrates by oxidizing fermentation products to carbon dioxide in the presence of oxygen.49 Host regulatory mechanisms that maintain a beneficial colonic microbiota highlight the pivotal role of colonocytes (colonic epithelial cells) in shaping microbial composition.50 Based on energy metabolism, gut microbes can be mostly categorized into two groups. The first includes facultative anaerobes that generate ATP through oxidative phosphorylation, using electron donors such as glucose and acceptors such as O2 or nitrate (NO3−), but can switch to fermentation under anaerobic conditions. The second group consists of obligate anaerobes that cannot utilize oxygen, instead they rely on internal electron acceptors such as pyruvate or phosphoenolpyruvate to generate ATP via fermentation. Microbes that most efficiently produce ATP under given environmental conditions outcompete others, becoming dominant within microbial communities.51 Thus, redox reactions that yield the highest energy gains are key factors of microbial structure in both natural and gut ecosystems.41,52 The oxygen gradient along the intestinal tract further influences microbial distribution. Partial oxygen pressure decreases progressively from the duodenum to the colon,53 creating distinct ecological niches. Therefore, the sparsely populated small intestine is dominated by facultative anaerobes, as the densely colonized large intestine harbors obligate anaerobic fermenters adapted to this hypoxic environment.54,55 Despite limited oxygen availability, epithelial cells in the ileum continuously produce reactive oxygen and nitrogen species; for example, NADPH oxidase 1 (NOX1) generates superoxide,54,56 while inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) produces nitric oxide (NO⋅).57,58 Superoxide reacts with NO⋅ to form peroxynitrite, which subsequently decomposes into NO3− within the intestinal lumen.59 Unlike the ileum, the duodenum and caecum lack iNOS activity and therefore do not produce host-derived NO⋅.60 The host thus maintains distinct redox conditions along the gut with high oxygen levels in the proximal small intestine and NO3− availability in distal regions (Figure 2). The selective use of O2 or NO3− as terminal electron acceptors shapes microbial composition, favoring facultative anaerobes in the large intestine.51 The presence of a dense microbial population in the colon enables the efficient processing of undigested dietary residues that are resistant to host digestive enzymes. Through microbial fermentation, these refractory compounds are transformed into bioavailable metabolites that are absorbed and utilized by the host as a complementary source of nutrition.33,61 Maintaining a relatively sparse microbial population in the small intestine is equally beneficial, as excessive bacterial growth can lead to bile acid deconjugation, malabsorption, diarrhea, and weight loss.62 These findings suggest that the host, rather than microbial activity, is responsible for generating and maintaining the oxygen gradient that exists along the length of the intestinal lumen. Microbiota dysbiosis and its detrimental impact

Disruption of intestinal microbial equilibrium from eubiosis to dysbiosis can result from several factors, including chronic stress, diet, tobacco use, and prolonged antibiotic exposure. Among these, diet exerts the greatest influence. Energy-dense, fiber-deficient diets disrupt gut homeostasis by damaging host regulatory mechanisms and altering microbial composition. Reduced intake of plant polysaccharides depletes Prevotella species, key degraders of dietary fiber, while enriching Bacteroides species that metabolize host-derived mucin glycans, hence compromising the mucus barrier and promoting microbial translocation, inflammation, and disease.63,64,65 The degradation of mucin glycans can cause the colonic mucus barrier to deteriorate, leading to a loss of host control over the colonic microbiota, enabling microbes to penetrate the epithelial layer and potentially triggering inflammation and disease.66,67 Excessive intake of simple sugars exceeds intestinal absorptive capacity, allowing unabsorbed carbohydrates to reach the colon and act as electron donors that favor Firmicutes species adept at metabolizing sucrose and fructose.68,69 Prolonged fructose consumption further enriches bacterial taxa expressing mucin-degrading enzymes, exacerbating barrier disruption.70 Similarly, chronic high-fat consumption, particularly of saturated fatty acids, induces low-grade colonic inflammation and impairs epithelial mitochondrial bioenergetics. Dysbiosis has been strongly linked to an increased risk of CRC, one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality globally.3,72,73 Recent investigations have elucidated a crucial role for the gut microbiota in the tumorigenic process, with evidence indicating that microbial-derived metabolites, including carcinogenic compounds, contribute significantly to the onset and advancement of tumorigenesis.74,75 By analyzing data from several studies, researchers have identified a specific set of disease-causing microorganisms that are consistently enriched in patients with colorectal cancer, providing valuable insights into the association between the microbiota and CRC development and progression.76,77 Oral pathogens feature prominently among these enriched species, including F. nucleatum, Peptostreptococcus anaerobius, Parvimonas micra, Porphyromonas asaccharolytica, Peptostreptococcus stomatis, Solobacterium moorei, and Prevotella intermedia.78,79,80,81,82 Beyond CRC, a high richness of several oral pathogens, such as F. nucleatum, Lachnoclostridium species, and S. moorei, is also perceived in the microbiota of the gut of people who have colorectal adenomas.83,84,85 Beyond these oral taxa, colonic pathogens such as enterotoxigenic B. fragilis, Morganella morganii, S. gallolyticus, and pks+ E. coli are also prevalent in CRC.86,87,88 Although these microbes are mechanistically linked to CRC, it remains uncertain whether they act independently or synergistically. Equally important is the loss of protective commensals. Probiotic species including Streptococcus salivarius, Streptococcus thermophilus, Lactobacillus gallinarum, Carnobacterium maltaromaticum, and Clostridium butyricum are consistently depleted in CRC.89,90,91,92 Their absence weakens colonization resistance and boosts pathobiont expansion, shifting microbial equilibrium toward tumor-promoting groups. Restoring this balance presents openings for therapeutic modulation and biomarker development. Beyond compositional alterations, bacteria have been shown to penetrate tumor tissues.93,94,95 F. nucleatum, in particular, persists within primary and metastatic CRC injuries, underscoring its role in disease progression.96,97 Cumulative evidence indicates that dysbiosis not only accompanies but also actively promotes tumorigenesis through altered community composition and metabolic output.13 Moreover, gut microbes influence responses to chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors, highlighting their role in modulating treatment efficacy.98,99 The influence of microbes and the microbiota on carcinogenesis, which amplifying or mitigating the host’s vulnerability, can be categorized into three main groups: (1) regulating the equilibrium between host cell proliferation and apoptosis; (2) affecting the function and response of the immune system; and (3) changing the metabolism of endogenous factors, dietary elements, and pharmaceuticals, as described in Figure 3B.71 These findings demonstrate strong associations between dysbiosis and CRC. Emerging studies suggest that these links are more nuanced and occasionally contradictory, justifying closer evaluation. Although microbial dysbiosis and inflammation are consistently linked to CRC, the underlying mechanisms remain contentious. For instance, the F. nucleatum is broadly associated with pro-tumorigenic immune modulation; however, conflicting findings suggest that its abundance varies by tumor stage, functional site, and host immune tone.100 Similarly, metabolites such as deoxycholic acid and succinate can exhibit both genotoxic and signaling-regulatory roles, depending on concentration and host context.101,102 Protective SCFAs like butyrate may shift toward pro-oncogenic activity under hypoxic or dysbiotic conditions.103 Moreover, while the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is often activated by microbiota-derived signals, recent studies suggest microbial influence may be secondary to tumor-intrinsic signaling dysregulation.104,105 To enhance clarity, repetitive discussions on dysbiosis and inflammation across sections have been combined into unified mechanistic overviews, with improved focus on evidence hierarchy and unresolved controversies.

Microbiota-driven carcinogenic mechanisms

DNA-damaging toxins

The gut microbiota produce numerous metabolites capable of inducing DNA damage and driving CRC (Figure 4). Active microbial communities are frequently detected within CRC tissues, and the intratumoral presence of specific bacterial strains such as pks+ E. coli, F. nucleatum, and enterotoxigenic B. fragilis is strongly correlated with mutational burden and disease progression (Figure 4A).71,106,107,108 Experimental models confirm a contributing role for these microbes, which promote tumorigenesis through the secretion of genotoxic molecules that compromise genomic integrity.109,110 A well-characterized mechanism involves the pks pathogenicity key in E. coli, which encodes the polyketide synthase-nonribosomal peptide synthetase hybrid system responsible for synthesizing colibactin, a potent genotoxin.111 This toxin exhibits genotoxic properties, causing DNA alkylation and enhancing CRC development in mouse models, therefore demonstrating its tumorigenic potential.112,113,114 DNA alkylation by colibactin is attributed to its reactive cyclopropane bioactive core, which leads to the creation of DNA adducts that are assumed to be responsible for its mutational signature.113,115 Mouse models demonstrate that colibactin exposure enhances tumor formation, underscoring its oncogenic potential. Beyond colibactin, a recently identified class of genotoxins termed indolimines has been detected in Clostridium ramosum, Clostridium perfringens, and M. morganii.116 Cell-free assays revealed that indolimines induce double-strand breaks (DSBs) in host DNA, evidenced by γ-H2AX activation and cell-cycle arrest in epithelial cells. Unlike colibactin, indolimines produce unique DNA damage patterns and do not require the pks gene cluster. Importantly, indolimine-producing M. morganii, which is enriched in the gut of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and CRC, increases intestinal permeability and exacerbates tumor development in gnotobiotic mouse models. Another prominent bacterial genotoxin is the cytolethal distending toxin (CDT), composed of CdtA, CdtB, and CdtC subunits. CdtA and CdtC facilitate host-cell binding and internalization, while CdtB, a DNase I-like enzyme, induces DNA DSBs upon nuclear entry, leading to cell-cycle arrest and genomic instability.117,118 For example, Campylobacter jejuni-derived CDT promotes intestinal tumorigenesis by inducing DNA breaks and epithelial damage.108,119 Enterotoxigenic B. fragilis (ETBF) secretes B. fragilis toxin (BFT), which causes genotoxicity via reactive oxygen species (ROS) production (Figure 4B), leading to oxidative stress, disruption of cellular homeostasis, and enhanced tumorigenic potential.120,121 BFT also triggers proinflammatory cascades implicated in both CRC and breast cancer.122 A study by Maiuri et al.123 demonstrated that EBF-infected mice experience DNA methylation during inflammation-driven tumorigenesis. Oxidative stress also represents a critical route to DNA damage.124 E. faecalis has been shown to produce extracellular superoxide and related ROS through an NOX2-dependent pathway. These oxidants permeate host epithelial cells, disrupt redox homeostasis, and increase mutational susceptibility.125 These findings underscore the multifaceted ways in which gut microbes and their genotoxins compromise genomic stability, driving CRC initiation and progression through both direct DNA damage and oxidative or epigenetic mechanisms (Figure 4C).Figure 4Mechanisms of genotoxicity by oncogenic gut microbes(A) Oncogenic microbes exert genotoxicity through bacterial toxins, which damage DNA directly and indirectly via host-derived ROS/RNS, potentially resulting in cell death or cancer-promoting mutations if repair mechanisms are overcome. Affecting host signaling pathways

CRC, the WNT/β-catenin, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways are frequently dysregulated, and their activation can be further potentiated by specific pathogenic bacteria (Figure 5). The direct interaction between pathobionts and colonic epithelial cells, via specific receptors, transmits signals that modulate cellular behavior, significantly contributing to disease progression. The WNT/β-catenin pathway is central to cell differentiation, proliferation, and tissue homeostasis.126,127 In normal cells, β-catenin levels are tightly regulated by proteasomal degradation in the absence of Wnt ligands.128 In CRC, this pathway is disturbed, resulting in the buildup of β-catenin, a key transcriptional controller that governs the expression of numerous genes involved in cancer development and progression. Such perturbation is run by the presence of a diverse range of bacterial species, which collectively disrupt the homeostatic regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin axis, thereby promoting tumorigenicity.127 As an example, F. nucleatum produces two key adhesins, FadA and Fap2, that directly modulate host signaling. FadA binds to E-cadherin, activating β-catenin signaling and upregulating Wnt target genes such as MYC and cyclin D1, which drive tumor proliferation,82,129 Fap2 binds to Gal-GalNAc residues on CRC cells, promoting bacterial adhesion and colonization.130,131,132 FadA expression is consistently elevated in adenoma and CRC tissues and positively correlates with WNT pathway activation and proinflammatory gene expression, indicating its oncogenic potential. While most studies focus on single pathogenic species, microbial communities in the gut act as dynamic groups. Cooperative interactions within these groups can amplify the production of genotoxic and proinflammatory metabolites, including SBAs and hydrogen sulfide (H2S), whereas competitive interactions may suppress pathogenic taxa.133 These community dynamics influence the intestinal metabolite landscape, shaping the tumor microenvironment. Certain probiotic taxa can stabilize these effects by producing anti-inflammatory compounds or by competitively excluding pathogens, suggesting that targeting microbial community structure may provide therapeutic benefit through tailored probiotics or diet-based interventions. Beyond F. nucleatum, Helicobacter pylori demonstrates another bacterium that manipulates β-catenin signaling. Its virulence factor CagA disrupts the E-cadherin/β-catenin complex, releasing β-catenin to the nucleus and activating the WNT axis. This phenomenon is compounded by chronic inflammation via nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) activation mediated by LPS, peptidoglycan, and CagA-dependent mechanisms.108,134,135 In CRC-associated microbiota, enterotoxigenic ETBF secretes BFT, which cleaves E-cadherin and activates both β-catenin/MYC and NF-κB signaling pathways, promoting chronic inflammation and colorectal tumorigenesis.136,137 ETBF also induces TH17-mediated inflammation in the distal colon, enhancing tumor growth. Salmonella species secrete the effector protein AvrA to manipulate host signaling by inhibiting β-catenin ubiquitination and promoting its phosphorylation and nuclear translocation.138,139,140 Similarly, the oral bacterium P. anaerobius, overrepresented in CRC microbiota, employs its putative cell wall binding repeat 2 (PCWBR2) protein to engage integrin α2β1 on CRC cells, triggering the PI3K/AKT cascade via focal adhesion kinase (FAK), hence promoting tumorigenesis.13 In addition, P. anaerobius suppresses anti-tumor immunity by activating the integrin α2β1-NF-κB axis, which induces CXCL1 secretion and recruits CXCR2+ myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), facilitating immune evasion and disease progression.98 Another pathogen, Porphyromonas gingivalis, invades CRC cells and activates the MAPK-ERK pathway through its surface protease, gingipain, a mechanism that enhances epithelial transformation and tumor progression.141 These findings demonstrate that pathogenic bacteria modulate oncogenic signaling through multiple host receptors and pathways, including WNT/β-catenin, NF-κB, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK, therefore compromising epithelial integrity, sustaining inflammation, and driving colorectal tumorigenesis. Inflammation

Specific bacterial taxa, such as F. nucleatum and Bacteroides fragilis, drive chronic inflammation in CRC. F. nucleatum activates immune cells via its FadA adhesin, triggering β-catenin signaling and promoting pro-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin (IL)-8 and CXCL1. Similarly, B. fragilis produces a toxin that disrupts the epithelial barrier, activating NF-κB and inducing IL-17-mediated inflammation, thus increasing tumorigenesis.142 Microbial metabolites critically influence inflammatory signaling. SBAs such as deoxycholic acid activate NF-κB, elevating pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) and promoting epithelial proliferation. F. nucleatum strengthens inflammation by inducing TH17 differentiation and IL-17 secretion, raising tumor growth and immune evasion. Persistent microbiota-driven inflammation recruits myeloid-derived suppressor cells and Tregs, suppressing cytotoxic immunity, while metabolites like trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) impair CD8+ T cell infiltration, weakening tumor surveillance. The resulting inflammatory tumor microenvironment continues dysbiosis, sustaining a feedback loop that strengthens inflammation and promotes colorectal tumorigenesis.143 The gastrointestinal tract serves as a major interface for interactions between the microbiota and host immunity,144,145 and persistent inflammation remains a defining feature of this relationship.72,146 Patients with IBD exhibit a significantly higher risk of developing CRC than the general population.147 The immune system microbiota equilibrium regulates inflammatory nature and acts as a critical nexus between dysbiosis and cancer. Disturbances in this balance raise a tumor-promoting environment characterized by sustained inflammation and immunosuppression. Colonization of the mucosa by species such as P. anaerobius, F. nucleatum, E. faecalis, P. micra, enterotoxigenic B. fragilis, and pks+ E. coli triggers pro-inflammatory cascades that initiate colonic inflammation, a key precursor to CRC.82 Even in the absence of obvious inflammation, dysregulated inflammatory signaling plays a central role in CRC development. The host inflammatory response to gut pathobionts involves complex signaling networks, notably the IL-17 and NF-κB pathways, the activation of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), and the disruption of the epithelial barrier. PRRs, including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), RIG-I-like receptors, and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptors (NLRs), act as surviving that detect microbial antigens and initiate immune responses.148,149,150,151 These receptors recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns such as lipoteichoic acid (LTA), LPS, and bacterial DNA, as well as damage-associated molecular patterns released from injured host cells.152,153 Recognition of these ligands activates complex signaling cascades that stimulate cytokine and chemokine production, establishing a chronic pro-inflammatory state.154,155,156 Key signaling pathways that regulate lymphoid and myeloid cell function, particularly STAT3 and NF-κB, are frequently dysregulated in intestinal epithelial cells, promoting tumor initiation and progression (Figures 6A and 6D).157,158,159,160 NF-κB, a main transcriptional regulator, plays a central role in coordinating the inflammatory response by guiding the expression of a network of genes that participate in inflammatory and immune processes.161,162 NF-κB, a leading transcriptional regulator, controls inflammatory gene expression, including COX-2, iNOS, and cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6.163,164 Persistent activation of IL-6/STAT3, NF-κB, and COX-2 pathways promotes genomic instability, enhances oncogene expression, and confers survival, proliferation, and chemoresistance advantages to CRC cells, thus encouraging invasion and metastasis.165 Pathogenic bacteria exploit these pathways to sustain inflammation and tumor progression. H. pylori’s CagA protein activates STAT3, promoting proliferation and suppressing apoptosis in gastric epithelial cells, ultimately driving tumorigenesis.166,167,168 Similarly, F. nucleatum activates NF-κB signaling, recruiting myeloid cells and adopting pro-inflammatory tumor conditions.169 Casasanta et al. demonstrated that the binding of F. nucleatum’s Fap2 adhesin to CRC cells triggers IL-8 and CXCL1 secretion, establishing a metastatic microenvironment (Figure 6B).170 Udayasuryan et al. showed that F. nucleatum invasion induces cytokines including GM-CSF, CXCL1, IL-8, and macrophage inflammatory protein 3 alpha (MIP-3α), which collectively initiate tumor progression through enhanced proliferation, angiogenesis, and migration.171 Fap2 also binds to Gal-GalNAc residues, overexpressed on CRC cells, facilitating bacterial adhesion, intracellular colonization, and metastasis (Figure 6C).172 Similarly, ETBF, which is enriched in CRC microbiota, provokes strong inflammatory responses via its virulence factor BFT, activating IL-17, STAT3, and NF-κB pathways in epithelial cells.130,154,169,173 These pathways together maintain a pro-tumor microenvironment that supports CRC progression. Host- and microbiota-derived metabolites in carcinogenesis

Beyond their direct carcinogenic properties, bacterial strains colonizing the tumor microenvironment have evolved mechanisms to metabolically reprogram host- and diet-derived factors, which are critical regulators of molecular pathways and genotoxic stress, therefore potentiating the development of diverse cancer types through intricate host-microbe interactions. This section will elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which bioactive molecules derived from microbial metabolism influence key biochemical pathways and induce genetic alterations, terminating the initiation and progression of CRC.

Genotoxic metabolites

Microbial metabolites play pivotal roles in CRC progression through complex interactions with host signaling pathways. Key compounds, including SCFAs, SBAs, TMAO, H2S, and tryptophan derivatives, modulate inflammation, genomic stability, and cell proliferation. SBAs such as deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA) induce DNA damage via reactive oxygen and nitrogen species and inhibit the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), promoting cancer stem cell survival.174 Tryptophan metabolites like indole-3-acetate (IAA) and indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) interact with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) to regulate immunity and ferroptosis, influencing tumor growth.175 TMAO stimulates ROS production and epigenetic modifications and suppresses CD8+ T cell infiltration, while H2S from sulphate-reducing bacteria induces mitochondrial dysfunction and activates oncogenic Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT pathways. Collectively, these metabolites coordinate a balance between tumor-suppressive (e.g., butyrate-mediated PPARγ activation) and tumor-promoting (e.g., NF-κB and Wnt/β-catenin) signaling, with outcomes determined by microbial composition, diet, and host genetics (Figure 7A).176 High red meat intake is correlated with an increased incidence of CRC and other neoplasms, with the gut microbiome playing a related role through diverse mechanisms, including alterations in metabolic pathways and the production of mutagenic compounds.177 Charred meat, in particular, is irritating, as it generates carcinogenic heterocyclic amines that are processed by colonic bacteria, producing electrophilic metabolites believed to cause DNA damage.177 High consumption of red and processed meat increases exposure to precursors such as nitrosamines, heterocyclic amines, and heme iron.178 The porphyrin moiety of heme catalyzes endogenous N-nitrosation and lipid peroxidation, generating reactive aldehydes (e.g., malondialdehyde, 4-hydroxynonenal) that form DNA adducts and induce apoptosis of healthy epithelial cells. The detection of these aldehydes in feces highlights the microbial contribution to lipid oxidation and genotoxic stress. Gut microbes also ferment aromatic amino acids to yield indoles, phenols, phenylacetic acid, and p-cresol, bioactive compounds capable of disrupting DNA integrity and epithelial signaling.29,179 NOCs are produced endogenously in the large intestine through the nitrosation of amines derived from protein fermentation.180 Proteobacteria likely contribute to these nitrosation reactions through the enzymes nitroreductases and NO3− reductases.181 Apart from NOCs, ammonia, another byproduct of protein fermentation, is believed to induce carcinogenesis by causing damage to the mucosa and promoting colonic adenocarcinoma. Likewise, oxidative damage represents another significant type of DNA damage resulting from exposure to nitrosamines.182 TMAO, a microbial metabolite produced from the breakdown of dietary choline, phosphatidylcholine, and carnitine,183 is believed to have carcinogenic effects in CRC.184,185 The production of TMAO occurs through a two-step mechanism. First, gut microbes enzymatically convert dietary compounds like L-carnitine and choline into trimethylamine (TMA). Next, the liver oxidizes TMA into TMAO via the action of flavin-containing monooxygenases FMO3 and FMO1.184 Studies have shown that elevated levels of TMAO in the bloodstream are associated with oxidative stress, as they stimulate the production of superoxide, such as ROS.186,187 The involvement of TMAO in NOC formation, which is a recognized DNA-damaging agent, suggests a potential carcinogenic pathway linking TMAO exposure to DNA damage and epigenetic changes. Alterations in bile acid composition and enterohepatic circulation, focused by microbial hydrolase activity, promote CRC by disrupting bile acid homeostasis and lipid metabolism, facilitating SBA-mediated tumorigenesis.7 SBAs, produced by certain Clostridium species (Clostridium scindens, Clostridium hiranonis, and Clostridium hylemonae) through the transformation of primary bile acids (cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid), initiate CRC development by modulating the complex relationships between the host metabolic pathways and its microbiota. This process positions microbial-derived SBAs as crucial contributors to CRC pathogenesis.189,190 Through their binding to FXRs, SBAs disrupt FXR-mediated regulatory pathways, leading to the inhibition of FXR and subsequent promotion of cancer stem cell proliferation and DNA injury (Figure 7B). Reduced FXR activation has been widely observed in human CRC,188,191,192 and mice lacking Fxr genes are more prone to developing colon and liver tumors.193,194,195 DCA functions as a surfactant, disrupting cell membranes and releasing arachidonic upon penetration into cells. Arachidonic acid is then enzymatically (phospholipase A2; PLA2) converted into ROS, which promote inflammation and induce DNA damage.188,196 An alternative mechanism suggests that bacteria contribute to cancer development by hydrolyzing taurine-conjugated bile acids, leading to the release of taurine, which is then metabolized into H2S, a well-established carcinogen.197 The widespread deconjugation activity of the microbiota means that consuming taurine-rich foods, such as meat products, dairy, and energy drinks, increases the risk of H2S production in the colon, as the host conjugates more bile acids with taurine188 (Figure 7C). The prevalent deconjugation activity of the microbiota in the gut implies that a diet rich in taurine-containing foods, such as meat products, dairy, and energy drinks, enhances the likelihood of H2S production in the colon, as the host conjugates a greater amount of bile acids with taurine. The critical role of H2S in gut health, produced by the gut microbiota’s fermentation of sulfur-containing molecules, is increasingly recognized, particularly in the context of CRC and IBD, as it influences disease pathogenesis.198,199 It has been demonstrated that a high prevalence of sulfur-reducing bacteria in the colon is positively correlated with increased H2S production and the development of CRC.200,201,202,203 H2S has been reported to cause genomic DNA damage, potentially through radical-mediated mechanisms, which directly contributes to the accumulation of mutations associated with CRC. Similarly, activation of spermine oxidase by H. pylori results in the increased generation of H2S and other ROS species, which can lead to oxidative stress and potentially strengthen gastric pathology. These oxygen species can induce DNA mutations and contribute to cancer development.204,205,206 Besides causing DNA damage, various microorganisms possess proteins that affect host cellular processes, modifying cell signaling and promoting cancer development. These genotoxic metabolites, DCA, LCA, TMAO, H2S, and NOCs, form an interlinked metabolic network shaped by diet, microbial ecology, and host genetics. They sustain a pro-inflammatory and mutagenic microenvironment that participates in CRC via oxidative, genotoxic, and epigenetic mechanisms.

Microbial-derived metabolites and modulation of signaling pathways

Accumulating evidence reveals that tumor cells possess distinct bacterial populations, which produce metabolites that can drive tumorigenesis.16 Notably, P. anaerobius is significantly dominant in CRC patients, and recent research has shown that this bacterium metabolizes tryptophan to produce trans-3-indoleacrylic acid (IDA), a metabolite that promotes CRC development by inhibiting ferroptosis, a critical cell death mechanism.20 IDA has been shown to bind to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), leading to the upregulation of aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member A3 (ALDH1A3), which is involved in producing NADH. This process is essential for the ferroptosis suppressor protein to synthesize reduced coenzyme Q10. The absence of AHR or ALDH1A3 significantly reduces IDA-induced tumor development both in vitro and in vivo. It was proposed that IDA enhances cancer cell resistance to ferroptosis via the AHR-ALDH1A3-FSP1 axis (Figure 8A).207 The authors noted that supplementing with IDA or introducing P. anaerobius into the system accelerates tumor progression, highlighting the importance of the IDA-AHR-ALDH1A3-FSP1 pathway. Targeting the IDA-AHR-ALDH1A3 axis is proposed as a promising approach for treating CRC through ferroptosis-related mechanisms. A recent study highlighted a significant link between the high prevalence of ETBF and breast tumors. Such bacterium secretes a toxin known as BFT-1, which interacts with and stabilizes the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein 1 (NOD1) receptor protein. Accordingly, this interaction leads to increased phosphorylation of NUMB by the cyclin G-associated kinase (GAK). Phosphorylation of NUMB prevents its interaction with targets in vivo, ultimately activating the oncogenic NOTCH1 pathway.208 The simultaneous presence of ETBF and NOD1 expression within tumors signaled a diminished response to chemotherapy in breast cancer patients. It was reported that inhibiting NOD1 and clearing the bacteria enhanced the effectiveness of chemotherapy in breast cancer cells. The immune system produces effector CD8+ T cells crucial for combating cancers. Through the regulation of nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) 2, activated CD8+ T cells execute their effector function, releasing cytokines like TNF-α and IFN-γ, which enables them to target and eliminate tumor cells.209,210 Interfering with or inhibiting the NFAT2 signaling pathway by microbial metabolites could disrupt the effector functions of CD8+ T cells, potentially leading to the development of tumorigenesis. A recent study investigated the mechanistic understanding of the role played by the SBA metabolite DCA in the growth of CRC.211 It was discovered that among the analyzed metabolites, DCA significantly inhibited the effector functions of CD8+ T cells (cytokines production) and enhanced tumor growth in mice. DCA exerts its suppressive effects on CD8+ T cell responses by specifically targeting plasma membrane Ca2+ ATPase (PMCA), leading to the inhibition of Ca2+ influx and subsequent disruption of the NFAT2 signaling cascade, a critical pathway governing T cell function. Immunotherapy with monoclonal antibodies targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) is regarded as a successful treatment for various malignancies.214,215 However, this therapy has proven ineffective in some patients. Emerging data indicate that the gut microbiome exerts a modulatory effect on the efficacy of immunotherapeutic interventions in diverse cancer types, including CRC and melanoma, underscoring the need for further research into the gut microbiota’s influence on cancer immunology.216,217,218 Jiang et al. recently reported a notable association between high levels of F. nucleatum colonization in patients with metastatic CRC and a reduced likelihood of responding to immunotherapy, implicating this microorganism in immunotherapy resistance.213 In vivo studies revealed that succinic acid, a metabolite produced by F. nucleatum, impairs the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-interferon-β signaling axis, making the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the tumor site and ultimately compromising antitumor immunity (Figure 8C).213 These results suggest that metabolic byproducts of tumor-resident bacteria play a pivotal role in CRC pathogenesis, revealing novel insights into the complex link between the tumor microbiota, metabolomic profiles, and immune responses that influence immune suppression, tumor growth, and disease outcome. Similarly, formate, a metabolic byproduct of F. nucleatum, has been identified as an oncometabolite that plays a key role in the pathogenesis and progression of CRC.212 Formate has been shown to enhance CRC tumor invasiveness by activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling cascade and augmenting cancer stemness (Figure 8B).212 Furthermore, in vivo studies utilizing mouse models have demonstrated that treatment with F. nucleatum or formate leads to increased tumor burden and expansion of Th17 cells, establishing a pro-inflammatory microenvironment that fosters tumor growth, progression, and metastasis. Although colorectal cancer remains the most extensively characterized model for microbiota-metabolite interactions, emerging evidence suggests analogous mechanisms across several other tumor types. In gastric cancer, H. pylori has long been implicated in tumorigenesis through chronic inflammation, yet recent insights reveal that its CagA oncoprotein directly disturbs epithelial polarity and induces nuclear translocation of β-catenin, activating proliferative Wnt signaling and promoting genomic instability independent of inflammation.219 In breast cancer, ETBF secretes BFT-1, which stabilizes the NOD1 receptor, facilitating GAK-mediated phosphorylation of NUMB and subsequent activation of the NOTCH1 oncogenic axis, a pathway implicated in both tumor progression and resistance to chemotherapeutic agents.102 These findings highlight a novel paradigm in which microbial toxins modulate stemness-associated transcriptional programs in hormone-sensitive cancers. Furthermore, F. nucleatum metabolites are increasingly recognized as systemic modulators beyond the colon. In pancreatic cancer, F. nucleatum-derived succinate has been shown to impair cGAS-STING signaling, reducing type I interferon production and facilitating immune escape, particularly by reducing CD8+ T cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment.102 Similarly, formate, another metabolite produced by F. nucleatum, enhances aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling and promotes the expansion of Th17 cells, which in turn raises an immunosuppressive and pro-metastatic niche.105 These findings collectively indicate that microbe-derived metabolites contribute to tumor-specific immune modulation and metabolic reprogramming in a tissue-dependent manner. Expanding microbiome-oncology research to encompass extracolonic malignancies is therefore important to delineate context-specific host-microbe signaling axes and identify novel therapeutic targets.

Variations in gut microbiota and their metabolites have been identified as pivotal factors in the development of various cancers, particularly CRC.18 Certain gut microbes, including Bacillus and Lactobacillus species, produce gallic acid, a polyphenol metabolite that can induce CRC by influencing the activity of the P53 tumor suppressor gene, leading to tumor formation and progression.220 Evidence suggests that gut microorganisms or gallic acid can counter the tumor suppressive effects of TP53 gain-of-function mutations, leading to sensitive Wnt signaling and enhanced tumor formation in mouse models of colon cancer, including Csnk1a1-deficient and Apc-Min/+ mice.221 An in-depth examination of the existing literature suggests that microbial-derived metabolites significantly influence host metabolic pathways, with both primary and secondary metabolites exerting a profound influence on the progression and development of CRC.

Recent advances in microbiome-oncology have catalyzed a shift toward mechanistically grounded therapeutic strategies, moving beyond taxonomic profiling to functional modulation of host-microbe metabolic networks. Untargeted and targeted metabolomics have enabled high-resolution mapping of microbiota-derived metabolites such as butyrate, succinate, indole-3-propionate, and SBAs that exhibit context-dependent roles in tumorigenesis, immune regulation, and therapy response. These metabolites are now being investigated as dynamic biomarkers for early detection, tumor staging, and immunotherapy stratification.102 Similarly, microbiome engineering has advanced from empirical probiotic use to precision-guided interventions. Selective administration of strains such as L. gallinarum and C. butyricum has been shown to restore intestinal epithelial integrity, suppress IL-6/STAT3 signaling, and enhance Treg differentiation via SCFA production.100,103 In contrast, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) operates through compositional and functional reprogramming of the gut microbiome, via recent clinical trials demonstrating improved responses to PD-1 blockade in resistant CRC and melanoma patients, likely mediated through shifts in microbial metabolic outputs and antigen presentation landscapes. Furthermore, synthetic biology approaches have yielded programmable microbial chassis (e.g., E. coli Nissle 1917) engineered to secrete therapeutic payloads such as pro-apoptotic peptides, quorum-sensing inhibitors, or CRISPR-based transcriptional repressors within hypoxic tumor microenvironments.222 Emerging tools such as metabolite-responsive promoters, synthetic riboregulators, and bacteria-encapsulated nanocarriers are extending the reach of microbiome-derived therapeutics beyond local immunomodulation toward integrated systems-level intervention. Together, these innovations reflect a growing convergence of microbial ecology, systems biology, and synthetic engineering in the development of next-generation cancer therapeutics.

Microbiota-based therapeutic strategies

The growing recognition of microbial metabolites as active oncogenic mediators has led to the development of targeted microbiota-based therapeutic strategies. One promising approach involves the use of genetically engineered probiotic strains, such as E. coli Nissle 1917, which can be modified to deliver tumor suppressor genes (e.g., p53 and Tum-5) selectively into the hypoxic tumor microenvironment. These strains exploit the natural tropism of facultative anaerobes for tumor cores, enabling site-specific release of therapeutic payloads with minimal systemic exposure.222 Similar efforts are underway to develop selective inhibitors of microbiota-derived co-metabolites, such as H2S, TMAO, and SBAs. These metabolites contribute to DNA damage, inflammation, and immune suppression through epigenetic and receptor-mediated mechanisms.102 Additionally, synthetic biology platforms now enable the design of programmable bacterial vectors capable of biosensing tumor-specific cues and releasing immunomodulators or cytotoxins through quorum-regulated lysis systems.223 These therapeutic innovations represent a paradigm shift toward precision microbiome engineering, offering highly localized, metabolism-informed strategies for oncological intervention. Emerging frameworks: Functional redundancy and microbial flexibility in cancer

Traditional approaches to studying the gut microbiota often focus on the presence or abundance of specific taxa, underestimating the complexity and adaptability of microbial communities. Emerging frameworks such as functional redundancy and microbial resilience provide a more nuanced understanding of the microbiota’s role in health and disease, including CRC. Functional redundancy refers to the ability of multiple microbial taxa to perform similar biochemical functions, ensuring that critical processes, such as metabolite production or immune modulation, persist even when specific taxa are depleted, underscoring the limited utility of abundance metrics alone for understanding microbiota functions in CRC.224 For instance, different microbial species can produce SCFAs, such as butyrate, which contribute to gut homeostasis and immune modulation, emphasizing that microbial function is more critical than taxonomy. However, the biological effects of butyrate are not universally protective and appear to be context-dependent. Under normal physiological conditions, butyrate promotes epithelial differentiation, regulates inflammation, and suppresses oncogenic transformation. Yet, in cancerous colonocytes exhibiting Warburg-type metabolic reprogramming or Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, impaired mitochondrial oxidation of butyrate leads to its nuclear accumulation and excessive HDAC inhibition. This can result in epigenetic activation of proliferative and survival pathways, suggesting that butyrate may exert tumor-promoting effects in specific oncogenic contexts.102,105,225

Microbial flexibility, another key concept, describes the ability of gut communities to recover or adapt to perturbations such as antibiotic use, dietary shifts, or inflammatory damage. Zhou et al.226 emphasize that this flexibility is critical for maintaining a stable gut ecosystem and preventing the shift to dysbiosis, a state associated with CRC. Robust microbial communities can buffer external stressors, preserving beneficial functions and suppressing harmful processes like the overproduction of genotoxic metabolites or chronic inflammation. However, in vulnerable or dysbiotic states, microbial resilience may weaken, allowing opportunistic pathogens or harmful groups to dominate and contribute to cancer progression.

These frameworks highlight that therapeutic strategies should aim to restore microbial functionality and ecosystem stability, for example, by targeting shared metabolic pathways among redundant taxa or enhancing flexibility through dietary modulation. Integrating functional redundancy and microbial resilience into the study of gut microbiota represents a paradigm shift, offering deeper insights into the complex interplay between microbes and their host in CRC development and progression. Furthermore, H. pylori is a well-established bacterial driver of gastric cancer, where its virulence factor CagA disrupts the E-cadherin/β-catenin complex, leading to abnormal cell proliferation and survival. H. pylori also induces oxidative stress and DNA damage through ROS and nitric oxide production, activating inflammatory pathways like NF-κB and STAT3 to create a tumor-promoting microenvironment. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), microbial metabolites such as deoxycholic acid (DCA) and other SCFAs modulate liver inflammation and carcinogenic signaling through the gut-liver axis. Dysbiosis in liver cancer is marked by increased genotoxic bile acids and decreased beneficial SCFAs. In pancreatic cancer, F. nucleatum, commonly associated with CRC, has been detected in pancreatic tumor tissues. It induces immunosuppressive conditions, recruits myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and suppresses anti-tumor T cell responses, thus enabling immune evasion. F. nucleatum also activates oncogenic pathways like Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT, supporting tumor growth and metastasis. Shared mechanisms across cancers include microbial metabolites like TMAO, H2S, and tryptophan derivatives. TMAO promotes chronic inflammation and epigenetic modifications that favor tumor progression, H2S induces DNA damage and oxidative stress contributing to genotoxicity, and tryptophan derivatives modulate immune responses and inflammation through interactions with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), influencing cancer progression in various organs. Microbial metabolites serve as central mediators connecting gut microbial activity with oncogenic and tumor-suppressive mechanisms. As summarized in Table 2, SCFAs, bile acids, TMAO, H2S, tryptophan derivatives, and fermentation intermediates such as formate and succinate participate in multiple molecular pathways regulating inflammation, DNA stability, immune signaling, and cellular metabolism. Their actions are context dependent, shifting between protective and oncogenic roles based on metabolic, epigenetic, and inflammatory microenvironments. Conclusions and perspectives

Accumulating evidence suggests that gut microorganisms play a causal role in tumor development by producing harmful metabolites that contribute to carcinogenesis. This comprehensive review seeks to elucidate the intricate connections between gut-microbiota-derived metabolites and cancer, with a specific emphasis on decoding the molecular mechanisms by which these metabolites modulate cancer progression and tumorigenesis. The gut microbiota catalyze the biotransformation of dietary and host-derived substrates, including bile acids and glycoconjugates, into bioactive metabolites that modulate microbial community structure and participate in host cellular pathways, influencing immunological homeostasis, metabolic regulation, and systemic physiological functions.7,227 This review highlights the pivotal role of host- and microbiota-derived metabolites, as evidenced by recent studies, in the activation of oncogenic signaling cascades and induction of genotoxic stress. Furthermore, it underlines the critical influence of dietary factors and intestinal epithelial cell dynamics in modulating gut microbial composition and function, contributing to the pathogenesis and progression of colorectal cancer. The host has evolved mechanisms to maintain a healthy microbiota, and a fiber-rich diet supports this effort by promoting a balanced microbiome. However, any disruption to this balance can lead to changes in the microbial community, making it susceptible to invasion by harmful pathogens. Upon successful colonization, these pathogens can trigger a state of dysbiosis, characterized by disrupted microbial balance, leading to increased inflammation and production of genotoxins and other carcinogenic metabolites. Consequently, the imbalance of metabolites caused by the disturbed microbiome can contribute to the initiation and advancement of CRC. The combination of metabolomics and gut microbiome association studies presents new opportunities for the development of novel clinical strategies for CRC diagnosis, prevention, and therapy. Despite the acknowledged correlation between cancer and the microbiota, numerous critical knowledge gaps and uncertainties remain, necessitating continued exploration and investigation in this rapidly advancing field of research. A long-standing question in research on the microbiota associated with CRC is whether alterations in the microbiota drive cancer development or if cancer itself triggers changes in the microbiota. While the involvement of commensals and pathobionts in tumorigenesis and disease progression has been identified, the defined impact of the gut microbiota on CRC development remains uncertain.82 A more reasonable scenario suggests that genetic and epigenetic modifications in the colonic epithelium work in concert with gut dysbiosis to initiate and promote tumorigenesis, leading to tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis. Elucidating the mechanistic relationships between specific bacteria and cancer development is vital to the development of effective therapeutic approaches,228 necessitating research that transcends the mere identification of bacterial species involved in carcinogenesis. The comprehensive characterization of bacterial-derived genotoxins and metabolites in mouse models has revealed their pivotal role in driving cancer growth, offering a groundbreaking understanding of the intricate molecular mechanisms by which these small molecules activate and spread tumorigenesis.29 Further research using cutting-edge sequencing technologies, metabolomics, and proteomics is expected to uncover additional genotoxic elements and cancer-promoting factors in clinical isolates, paving the way for novel therapeutic approaches.229,230 Although numerous studies have offered a snapshot of the microbiome’s composition, additional investigation is essential to elucidate the temporal dynamics of the intestinal microbiome, encompassing both short-term fluctuations and long-term shifts.34 While inflammation plays a significant role in shaping the microbial community, the exact mechanisms remain unclear; it is uncertain whether the progression of inflammation drives dysbiosis or if other factors, such as host genetics or diet, initially disrupt the microbiome, leading to subsequent inflammation. To elucidate this complex cause-and-effect association, longitudinal studies combining microbiome analysis with inflammatory marker measurements are necessary. Advancing research in these domains will reveal the complex and multifaceted interactions between the microbiota and carcinogenesis, providing critical mechanistic insights that will inform the development of innovative, evidence-based therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing cancer therapy and improving clinical outcomes.
