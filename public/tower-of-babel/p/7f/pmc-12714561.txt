Stage-dependent dual mechanisms of MORC2 in retrotransposon silencing and sex chromosome inactivation in germ cells
Authors: Zhenlong Kang, Rui Guo, Shenglin Han, Fang Yang, Jinwen Zhang, Yuanyuan Wang, Kara A Nelson, Vamshidhar C Nallamala, N Adrian Leu, Zhengyi Li, Jialong Liu, Hongqing Liang, Yemin Lan, Anneliese Faustino, Hsin-Yao Tang, Andrew J Modzelewski, Lan Ye, P Jeremy Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetrotransposon silencing and meiotic sex chromosome inactivation (MSCI) are critical for male germ cell development. MORC2, a DNA-binding ATPase, represses retrotransposons in mammalian somatic cells through association with the HUSH complex. However, the in vivo roles for MORC2 in germ cells remain unknown, as its loss leads to embryonic lethality. Here, we report two distinct functions of MORC2 during spermatogenesis. Embryonic germ cell-specific inactivation of MORC2 results in a failure in silencing of LINE1 and IAP retrotransposons in male germ cells, meiotic arrest, and male sterility. LINE1 and IAP elements are hypomethylated in MORC2-deficient testes, providing a molecular explanation for their activation. In contrast, postnatal loss of MORC2 prior to meiosis causes a failure in MSCI. Mechanistically, MORC2 binds to gene promoters and represses transcription of both autosomal and sex chromosome-linked genes through H3K9me3 deposition in meiotic cells with a preferential effect on sex chromosome-linked genes. Biochemically, MORC2 interacts with MORC1 and SETDB1 in testis. Our results demonstrate that MORC2 plays stage-dependent dual functions in male germ cells: retrotransposon silencing in pre-meiotic cells and MSCI in meiotic cells, through association with MORC1 and SETDB1, respectively.

Introduction

Germ cells undergo dramatic chromatin remodeling during development. Specifically, epigenetic reprogramming in primordial germ cells results in genome-wide demethylation [1]. Shortly after sex determination, prospermatogonia are subject to de novo DNA re-methylation [2]. During this critical period, retrotransposons are activated and multiple silencing mechanisms safeguard germline genome integrity from potential damage by retrotransposon insertions [3]. During male meiosis, sex chromosomes (X and Y) are partially synapsed, form sex body, and are transcriptionally silenced, in a phenomenon known as meiotic sex chromosome inactivation (MSCI) [4–6]. During spermiogenesis, haploid spermatids undergo extensive histone to protamine exchange [7]. These chromatin remodeling events are essential for genome integrity and germ cell development.

The MORC genes are a family of chromatin-associated ATPases conserved from Arabidopsis to humans [8]. MORC proteins contain a GHKL-type ATPase domain and CW-type zinc fingers. In mammals, there are four Morc genes: Morc1-4. Morc1, the founding member of this family, was identified in an infertile mouse mutant with small testes (thus the name MORC, microorchidia) [9]. Morc1 is testis-specific and is essential for repression of retrotransposons in germ cells [10]. MORC1 silences retrotransposons in early germ cells through two distinct epigenetic marks: DNA methylation and H3K9me3 [11]. However, the mechanism of MORC1 in regulation of these two epigenetic marks remains unknown. In mouse, there are two Morc2 paralogs: Morc2 and Morc2b. Morc2b is only present in rodents, is testis-specific, and is essential for meiosis, but is dispensable for repression of retrotransposons [12]. While Morc1 and Morc2b are germ cell-specific, other members (Morc2, Morc3, and Morc4) are broadly expressed. Humans have only MORC2, the ortholog of mouse MORC2. Human MORC2 mutations cause axonal Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT), a neural disorder [13]. In somatic cells, MORC2 is recruited to chromatin by the HUSH (human silencing hub) complex and causes heterochromatin compaction [14]. In CRISPR-based genome-wide screens for retrotransposon repressors in mouse ES cells and human somatic cells, MORC2 were identified as one of the most potent repressors and were shown to bind to LINE1 elements [15, 16]. Human MORC2 recruits histone deacetylases such as HDAC1 and HDAC4 to gene promoters, resulting in transcriptional silencing [17, 18]. Human MORC2 has multiple DNA-binding sites and compacts chromatin through binding to multiple DNA substrates [19, 20]. These studies demonstrate that MORC proteins, particularly MORC2, play critical roles in chromatin remodeling in heterochromatin, gene promoters, and retrotransposons.

The HUSH complex consists of three subunits: TASOR, MPP8, and PPHLN1 [21]. In somatic cells, the HUSH complex binds to H3K9me3 through the chromodomain of MPP8 [22, 23] and recruits SETDB1, a H3K9 methyltransferase, to maintain the H3K9me3 silencing modification at heterochromatin [21]. SETDB1 silences retrotransposons in embryonic stem cells, primordial germ cells, and oocytes [15, 24–26]. The HUSH complex also recruits MORC2 to heterochromatin through TASOR and MPP8 [14]. While both SETDB1 and MORC2 are effector proteins for the HUSH complex, all studies of HUSH and MORC2 have been performed in human and mouse somatic cells.

The functions of most chromatin-remodeling proteins in germ cells including TASOR and MORC2 remain enigmatic, partly due to embryonic lethality caused by their mutations. Mouse mutants with Tasor point mutations were identified in an ENU mutagenesis screen but Tasor homozygous mutant embryos failed to develop beyond gastrulation [27]. Global loss of MORC2 causes embryonic lethality around E13.5 [28]. Genetic inactivation of Morc2 in the nervous system leads to increased brain size, altered brain structure, and increased expression of the protocadherin gene cluster [28]. Here, we find that MORC2 is abundantly expressed in male germ cells. MORC2 binds to gene promoters in meiotic cells. MORC2 interacts with MORC1 and SETDB1 in testes. We inactivated Morc2 specifically in germ cells using Ddx4-Cre and Stra8-Cre respectively and identified two distinct functions of MORC2 in male germ cell development: retrotransposon silencing in early germ cells and transcriptional repression in meiotic spermatocytes.

Materials and methods

Generation of Morc2 mutant mice

All animal experimental procedures were performed according to the protocol (IACUC 804421) approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of University of Pennsylvania. Morc2tm1a(KOMP)Wtsi mice (UC Davis, KOMP Repository) were crossed with Rosa26-FLPo mice (Stock number: 007844, Jackson Laboratory) to generate the Morc2 exon 4 floxed (Morc2fl) allele (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Morc2fl/fl females were crossed with Ddx4-Cre (Stock number: 006954, Jackson Laboratory) to generate Morc2fl/+ Ddx4-Cre mice [29]. Morc2fl/+ Ddx4-Cre males were crossed with Morc2fl/fl females to obtain Morc2fl/− Ddx4-Cre conditional knockout (Morc2DcKO) mice. Morc2fl/fl females were crossed with Stra8-GFPCre males to generate Morc2fl/+ Stra8-GFPCre mice [30]. Morc2fl/+ Stra8-GFPCre males were then crossed with Morc2fl/fl females to produce Morc2fl/− Stra8-GFPCre conditional knockout (referred to as Morc2ScKO) mice. All mice were genotyped by PCR (polymerase chain reaction) analysis of tail genomic DNA. Morc2 wild-type (288 bp) and floxed (485 bp) alleles were assayed by PCR with primers 5′AGAGGACATTGGTCTTCACATGCAT3′ and 5′ATCTTCCTGCTTCAACCTCCTAAGC3′. The Morc2 deletion allele (568 bp) was assayed by PCR with primers 5′AGAGGACATTGGTCTTCACATGCAT3′ and 5′GACTTTTCTTCCACTCACAGCCAGC3′.

Generation of 3 × FLAG-Morc2 mice

The 3 × FLAG-Morc2 mice were produced using CRISPR–Cas9-mediated genome editing approach as previously described [31]. A guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting the first exon of the mouse Morc2 gene was designed. The DNA oligo for the guide RNA (sgRNA) was cloned into the px330 vector, in vitro transcribed and purified. The single-stranded oligodeoxyribonucleotide (ssODN) template contains the 3 × FLAG-encoding sequence (Supplementary Fig. S2). A mixture of Cas9 mRNA, sgRNA, and ssODN DNA was injected into zygotes. The injected zygotes were cultured to the 2-cell stage and then transferred to uteri of pseudopregnant recipient females. Out of the 14 founder pups (7 males and 7 females), only one female had the 3 × FLAG insertion. The 3 × FLAG-Morc2 allele was shown to be correct by PCR amplification and sequencing. This founder female transmitted the 3 × FLAG-Morc2 allele through the germline. Wild-type (350 bp) and the 3 × FLAG-Morc2 allele (434 bp) were genotyped by PCR with these primers: 5′CCGGAGCACTTAAGGACAGT3′ and 5′TGGTTACCTCAGTGGCACCT3′.

Histological and nuclear spread analysis

Fresh testes and epididymides were fixed in Bouin’s solution (Sigma, SLBJ3855V) for 24–48 h, dehydrated sequentially in solutions with increasing concentrations of ethanol (50%, 70%, 95%, and 100%), and embedded in paraffin overnight at 55°C. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Nuclear spread analysis of spermatocytes was performed as previously described [32]. The following primary antibodies were used for immunofluorescence: anti-MORC2, anti-SETDB1, anti-γH2AX, anti-FLAG, anti-SYCP1, and anti-SYCP3 (Supplementary Table S7). FITC- or Texas red-conjugated secondary antibodies were used (Vector Laboratories). Images were captured on a Leica fluorescence microscope with digital cameras (Leica Microsystems).

Immunofluorescence and western blotting analyses

Fresh testes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) with 1× PBS (phosphate buffered saline) buffer at 4°C overnight. Fixed tissues were dehydrated with ethanol and washed in xylene. Testes were embedded in paraffin and cut into 4-μm-thick sections. All sections were rehydrated in decreasing concentrations of ethanol (100%, 95%, 70%, and 50%) and distilled water. Slides were treated in 10 mM sodium citrate (pH 6.0) at 100°C for 25 min and washed in 1× TBST twice after cooling to room temperature. Slides were blocked with the blocking buffer [10% FBS (fetal bovine serum), 1% BSA (bovine serum albumin), and 1% Triton X-100 in 1× TBST] at room temperature for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies diluted with blocking buffer at 4°C overnight. Primary antibodies are as follows: anti-MORC2, anti-SETDB1, anti-γH2AX, anti-LINE1 ORF1, anti-IAP GAG, anti-MIWI2, anti-MILI, and anti-TRIM28 (Supplementary Table S7). After three washes for 5 min each in 1× TBST buffer, slides were incubated at 37°C for 1 h in the dark with the secondary antibodies. After secondary antibody incubation, slides were washed three times with 1× TBST and mounted with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole). All slides were visualized under the Leica fluorescence microscope. For western blotting, tissue protein extracts were prepared in protein extraction buffer (62.5 mM Tris–HCl pH 6.8, 3% SDS, 10% glycerol, and 0.71 M 2-mercaptoethanol). 25 μg of total protein was run on 10% SDS–PAGE gel. The following primary antibodies were used for western blotting analysis: anti-MORC2, anti-SETDB1, anti-MORC2B [12], anti-MORC1 [12], anti-H3K9me3, anti-SYCP3, anti-TEX19, anti-LINE1 ORF1, anti-FLAG, anti-c-Myc (9E10), anti-V5, anti-GAPDH, anti-Histone H3, and anti-ACTB (Supplementary Table S7).

Bisulfite sequencing

For bisulfite sequencing, genomic DNA from P10 testes were treated with bisulfite. The sequence from the promoter of the L1MdA_I family was amplified by PCR (312 bp) with primers (sequences after bisulfite treatment: 5′TTATTTTGATAGTAGAGTT3′ and 5′CAGAACCAAACTCCTAACAA3′). The sequence of LTR (IAP) was amplified by PCR (257 bp) with nested PCR: the first round PCR primers 5′GTTTGTAATGGTGGGAGA3′ and 5′AAATAAAATATCCCTCC3′; the second round PCR primers: 5′TTGTGTTTTAAGTGGTAAATAAATAATTTG3′ and 5′CAAAAAAAACACACAAACCAAAAT3′. All the primer sequences were previously described [33]. PCR products were TA cloned. Individual clones were sequenced.

qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from P10, P14, and P60 Morc2DcKO testes and wild-type testes (biological triplicates for each genotype) using TRIzol (Invitrogen). 1 μg RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, 28025-013). Primers and SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied biosystems, A25742) were mixed thoroughly, and qPCR was performed in a QuantStudio 6 Flex qPCR instrument (Applied biosystems). LINE1 primers: 5′GAGAACATCGGCACAACAATC3′ and 5′TTTATTGGCGAGTTGAGACCA3′. IAP primers: 5′GCACCCTCAAAGCCTATCTTA3′ and 5′TCCCTTGGTCAGTCTGGATTT3′. 36B4 primers: 5′CATCATCAATGGGTACAAGCG3′ and 5′GCCTTGACCTTTTCAGTAAGTG3′. The transcript levels of LINE1 and IAP were normalized to that of 36B4.

STA-PUT isolation of mouse pachytene spermatocytes

Mouse pachytene spermatocytes were isolated as previously described [34]. Briefly, fresh testes were collected from Morc2fl/+ Stra8-GFPCre and Morc2ScKO mice at 8–12 weeks. The tunica albuginea was removed in 1× Krebs medium (1.2 mM KH2PO4, 120 mM NaCl, 1.2 mM MgSO4⋅7H2O, 1.3 mM CaCl2⋅2H2O, 4.8 mM KCl, 25 mM NaHCO3, and 0.2% Dextrose). Seminiferous tubules were digested in collagenase type IV (1 mg/ml) at 37°C, washed twice with preheated 1× Krebs medium, and digested with 0.25% trypsin with DNase I to form a single cell suspension at 37°C. The testicular cells were loaded to the STA-PUT chamber for sedimentation in the BSA gradient at 4°C for 2.5 h. Fractions containing pachytene spermatocytes were collected and purity was assessed by immunofluorescence using the anti-γH2AX antibody. Fractionated cells were stored at −80°C.

Cleavage under targets and release using nuclease (CUT&RUN)

Pachytene spermatocytes were isolated from 8- to 12-week 3 × FLAG-Morc2 and wild-type testes (n = 2 mice per genotype per preparation) using the STA-PUT method. Testes from P12 wild-type and 3 × FLAG-Morc2 mice (n = 2 mice per genotype per preparation) were digested with 1 mg/ml collagenase type IV and 0.25% trypsin to obtain single-cell suspensions for subsequent use. CUT&RUN experiments were performed in biological triplicates according to the protocol in the CUTANA™ ChIC/CUT&RUN Kit (Cat. No. 14-1048-24, EpiCypher). Briefly, 500 000 cells were collected and incubated with Concanavalin A-coated beads for 10 min at room temperature. 0.5 μg anti-FLAG primary antibody with 0.01% digitonin buffer was added to the cell-beads mix and incubated at 4°C overnight. After washing, Ca2+-activated pAG-MNase was added and incubated at 4°C for 2 h to digest targeted genomic regions. Then, 0.5 μg of E. coli spike-in DNA was added and incubated at 37°C for 10 min. CUT&RUN DNA was extracted and purified using SPRIselect beads. Fragmented DNA was then processed with end repair, adaptor ligation, U excision, and PCR amplification using the CUTANA™ CUT&RUN Library Prep Kit (Cat. No. 14-1001, EpiCypher). Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (2× 150 bp PE reads) at Azenta Life Sciences.

ChIP-seq

Pachytene spermatocytes were isolated from 8- to 12-week testes from control and Morc2ScKO mice (n = 10 control or n = 18 Morc2ScKO mice per preparation) using the STA-PUT method. All samples were crosslinked in 1% formaldehyde solution. DNA was fragmented using the Covaris-S220 sonicator in ChIP SDS lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0; 10 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; 1% SDS; 1× protease inhibitor). 0.5% of the sonicated sample was saved as input. Pachytene spermatocyte samples (two replicates) were incubated with anti-H3K9me3 antibody in the ChIP dilution buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5; 155 mM NaCl; 1.1% Triton X-100; 0.11% Na-deoxycholate; 1 mM EDTA; 1× Protease inhibitor) overnight, and then incubated with Protein A/G beads. Crosslinking was reversed by overnight incubation at 65°C. DNA was extracted with a mixture of phenol, chloroform, and isoamyl alcohol. The ChIP DNA libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB# E7645S/L). Fragmented DNA was processed with end repair for 5′ phosphorylation and 3′ dA-Tailing, followed by adaptor ligation. Adaptor-ligated DNA was subjected to U excision, size selection, and PCR amplification. Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (2× 150 bp PE reads).

ChIP-seq and CUT&RUN data analysis

Raw FASTQ data were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.32) and paired-end reads were aligned to the mouse genome (mm10) using Bowtie2 (v2.3.4.3). Peaks were called using MACS2 (v2.1.2) with a threshold of P < 0.0001. Peaks were annotated to the nearest TSS using ChIPSeeker (v1.16.1). H3K9me3 ChIP-seq data are available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under PRJNA1109850. CUT&RUN data are available under PRJNA1304570.

RNA-seq and data analysis

Pachytene spermatocytes were isolated from 8- to 12-week-old Morc2fl/+ Stra8-GFPCre and Morc2ScKO testes (biological triplicates for each genotype; two mice per replicate) using the STA-PUT method. P10 Morc2DcKO testes and wild-type testes (biological triplicates for each genotype) were also used. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen). RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep kit (Illumina, 20040532), pooled, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at the UPenn Next-Generation Sequencing Core. After adaptor trimming, low-quality reads were removed. Sequence reads were mapped to the mouse mm10 reference genome either in unique or multiple mapping mode with HISAT2 (v.2.1.0; Johns Hopkins University). The unique mapped reads of each gene were counted using HTSeq. DESeq2 was performed to evaluate gene expression as normalized counts per million (CPM). Alignments in the multiple mapping mode were used to evaluate transposable elements as previously described [35]. Differentially expressed (DE) genes were identified according to the cutoff (False Discovery Rate < 0.05; CPM > 1) using DESeq2 (P10 testes: Supplementary Table S1; Pachytene spermatocytes: Supplementary Table S6). The P10 RNA-seq data and pachytene RNA-seq data are available in NCBI SRA under PRJNA1109443.

Co-immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry

100 mg of P18 mouse testes per genotype (3 × FLAG-Morc2 and wild-type) was homogenized for protein extraction. Immunoprecipitation was performed with anti-FLAG M2 monoclonal antibody. Proteins eluted with FLAG peptides were subjected to mass spectrometry for protein identification at Wistar Institute Proteomics Core. The list of proteins identified in P18 testis is shown in Supplementary Table S2. Likewise, ∼70 mg of P0 mouse testes per genotype from 65 P0 3 × FLAG-Morc2 pups and 66 P0 wild-type pups were homogenized for protein extraction, immunoprecipitation with anti-FLAG M2 monoclonal antibody, and mass spectrometry. The list of proteins identified in P0 testis is shown in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

For nongenomic data analysis (testis/body weight ratio, sperm count, TUNEL assay, and qPCR relative expression), statistics were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1. The data were presented as mean ± s.d., with the number of biological replicates (n) specified in the figure legends. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were applied and P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Differential gene expression analysis for RNA-seq was performed with DESeq2, and significance was assessed by negative binomial test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction (FDR < 0.05). For CUT&RUN and ChIP-seq, peaks were called with MACS2 (Poisson distribution P < 0.0001). Peak annotation used ChIPseeker; motif enrichment was performed using HOMER (binomial test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction, FDR < 0.05). Hypergeometric tests were used for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis.

Results

Association of MORC2 with chromatin in germ cells

To investigate the role of MORC2 in germ cells, we first examined its expression and found that MORC2 was ubiquitously expressed in mouse tissues with the highest abundance in testis and muscle (Fig. 1A). Western blot analysis showed a dynamic expression pattern of MORC2 in postnatal testes at different ages. MORC2 was expressed in postnatal day 7 (P7) testis, which contained spermatogonia, but its abundance was sharply increased in testis at P14, when pachytene spermatocytes first appear (Fig. 1B). MORC2 was present in both soluble nuclear fractions and insoluble chromatin fractions but absent in cytoplasmic fractions of testis (Fig. 1C). MORC2 localized to the nuclei of both germ cells and Sertoli cells in the testis (Fig. 1D). Immunofluorescence analysis of MORC2 in staged seminiferous tubules showed that the MORC2 signal intensity decreased gradually as germ cells developed from spermatogonia to spermatozoa, with high expression in spermatogonia through zygotene spermatocytes and moderate expression in pachytene cells, diplotene cells, and round spermatids (Fig. 1E). MORC2 was present at a low level in elongating and condensing spermatids, but not detected in elongated spermatids (Fig. 1E). Immunofluorescence analysis of MORC2 in embryonic, P0, and P7 testes showed that MORC2 was expressed in gonocytes and spermatogonia (Supplementary Fig. S3). Together, these results suggest that MORC2 could function in nearly all male germ cells.

MORC2 is essential for male meiosis

Global loss of MORC2 leads to embryonic lethality [28]. Therefore, to determine the role of MORC2 in germ cells, we generated Morc2fl/− Ddx4-Cre conditional knockout (referred to as Morc2DcKO) mice (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Cre-mediated deletion of the floxed exon 4 leads to a frame shift in the resulting mutant transcript. Ddx4-Cre begins to express in germ cells at embryonic day 15 (E15) (Supplementary Fig. S1B) [29]. The Morc2DcKO testis was substantially smaller than the control (Morc2fl/+ Ddx4-Cre) (Fig. 2A). The Morc2DcKO testis weight was significantly lower (Fig. 2B). The MORC2 protein was lower in abundance in Morc2DcKO testis but still detectable, because Morc2 was still expressed in somatic cells of the testis (Fig. 2C). Consistently, immunofluorescence showed that MORC2 was absent in all germ cells but still present in Sertoli cells in Morc2DcKO testis, confirming its germline specific depletion in Morc2DcKO testis (Fig. 1D).

Histological analysis showed that the control testis contained the full spectrum of germ cells from spermatogonia to spermatozoa, whereas Morc2DcKO testis displayed meiotic arrest in all seminiferous tubules, with zygotene-like spermatocytes being the most advanced germ cells (Fig. 2D). As expected, the Morc2DcKO epididymis lacked sperm and contained only cellular debris (Fig. 2D). Morc2DcKO males were sterile but females were fertile. Histology of Morc2DcKO ovary appeared normal (Supplementary Fig. S1C). The litter size of Morc2DcKO females was comparable with that of control females (Supplementary Fig. S1D). To further characterize the meiotic defects in males, we examined the synaptonemal complex (SC) by immunofluorescence analysis of spread nuclei with antibodies against SYCP1 (SC transverse filaments) and SYCP3 (chromosomal axial elements) (Fig. 2E). In Morc2-deficient spermatocytes, axial elements (SYCP3) were assembled but failed to form synapsis, evidenced by the absence of SYCP1. In addition, SYCP3 formed large polycomplexes (condensates) in Morc2-deficient leptotene and zygotene spermatocytes. Such SC protein condensates were previously observed in Sycp2 and Sycp3 mutant spermatocytes [36, 37]. In addition, TUNEL analysis showed a significant increase in apoptosis in the seminiferous tubules of Morc2DcKO testes (Fig. 2F). These results demonstrate that loss of Morc2 leads to meiotic arrest at the zygotene-like stage in males.

Activation of LINE1 and IAP retrotransposons in Morc2-deficient germ cells

MORC2 silences LINE1 in human somatic cells and provirus in mouse ES cells [15, 16]. MORC1, another member of this gene family, represses LINE1 and IAP in mouse germ cells [10]. To determine whether MORC2 plays a similar role in germ cells, we examined retrotransposons by immunofluorescence (Fig. 3). LINE1 (ORF1) and IAP (GAG) retrotransposons were not readily detectable in control (Morc2fl/+ Ddx4-Cre) testes. However, we found that LINE1 was sharply upregulated in spermatocytes but not in spermatogonia in P18 Morc2DcKO testes (Fig. 3A). In contrast, IAP was upregulated in both spermatogonia and spermatocytes in P18 Morc2DcKO testes (Fig. 3B). The newborn (P0) testis contains gonocytes, which are not proliferating due to mitotic arrest. We found that LINE1 and IAP were upregulated in gonocytes from P0 Morc2DcKO testes (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S4A). qRT-PCR analysis showed that both LINE1 and IAP were upregulated in Morc2DcKO testes at P10, P14, and P60 (Fig. 3F). The abundance of LINE1 ORF1 protein was sharply increased in Morc2DcKO testes at P10 and P14 (Fig. 3G). These results demonstrate that MORC2 is essential for silencing of LINE1 and IAP retrotransposons in male germ cells.

piRNAs, a class of Piwi-interacting small noncoding RNAs, are essential for retrotransposon silencing in male germ cells. Piwi proteins MIWI2 and MILI bind to piRNAs and are expressed in gonocytes in newborn testes. MIWI2 localizes exclusively to the nucleus but is restricted to the cytoplasm when piRNA production is perturbed [38]. MIWI2 was still localized to the nucleus of gonocytes from P0 Morc2DcKO testis, suggesting that piRNA production might be intact in Morc2-deficient germ cells (Fig. 3D). The subcellular localization of MILI also remained the same between Morc2 mutant and control gonocytes (Fig. 3E). These results imply that activation of retrotransposons in Morc2-deficient germ cells appears to be piRNA-independent and that MORC2 silences retrotransposons through a different mechanism. Alternatively, MORC2 may act downstream of the MIWI2-mediated retrotransposon silencing.

DNA methylation is a key silencing mechanism of retrotransposons. To test this possibility, we examined the methylation of L1MdA_I, a young LINE1 element, and IAP in P10 testes by bisulfite sequencing. P10 testes contained spermatogonia and leptotene spermatocytes but lacked other meiotic and post-meiotic germ cells. The level of CpG methylation in L1MdA_I decreased from 92% in control testis to 41% in Morc2DcKO testis (Fig. 3H). Likewise, the level of CpG methylation in IAP decreased substantially from 85% in control testis to 43% in Morc2DcKO testis (Supplementary Fig. S4B). These results show that MORC2 is essential for DNA methylation and thus silencing of LINE1 and IAP in postnatal germ cells.

Histology confirmed that the cellular composition of Morc2DcKO testis at P10 was similar to that of controls (Supplementary Fig. S1E) and thus P10 testes were used for transcriptome profiling. RNA-seq analysis showed that a number of evolutionarily young TE subfamilies (L1Md_A, L1Md_T, L1Md-Gf, IAPEz-int, IAPLTR2_Mm, etc.) were upregulated in P10 Morc2DcKO testis (Fig. 3I and Supplementary Table S1). A number of protein-coding genes were DE (89 up-regulated and 43 down-regulated) (Supplementary Fig. S5 and Supplementary Table S1). The only substantially enriched biological process was homophilic cell adhesion for up-regulated genes that include 12 protocadherin gamma subfamily genes. These results demonstrate that MORC2 silences TEs in early male germ cells.

MORC2 is associated with MORC1 in testis

To systematically identify MORC2-associated proteins, we used 3 × FLAG-Morc2 testes for immunoprecipitation (Supplementary Fig. S2). Proteins immunoprecipitated from extracts of P18 3 × FLAG-Morc2 testes and non-tagged testes using anti-FLAG antibody were eluted and subjected to mass spectrometry for protein identification (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Table S2). MORC1 and MORC2B were identified in the immunoprecipitated proteins. MORC1 but not MORC2B is required for retrotransposon silencing in testis [10, 12]. Bromodomain protein family members, BRDT, BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4, were also identified in this screen. Of these bromodomain proteins, BRDT is known to have a role in spermatogenesis [39, 40].

(B) Co-immunoprecipitations of MORC2 and MORC1 in P18 wild-type and 3 × FLAG-MORC2 testes. (C) Co-immunoprecipitation of MORC2 and MORC1 in transfected HEK293T cells.

P0 (newborn) testis contains only diploid germ cells (gonocytes). Likewise, we identified MORC2-associated proteins in P0 testes by immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry. The most abundant proteins in the FLAG immunoprecipitated proteins in P0 FLAG-MORC2 testes were MORC1 and MORC2 with 78 and 134 peptides, respectively (Supplementary Table S3). Collectively, these results show that MORC2 is associated with the MORC proteins and bromodomain proteins in testes.

Co-IP analysis confirmed that MORC1 was enriched in immunoprecipitated proteins with anti-FLAG antibody from 3 × FLAG-MORC2 testes but not in untagged wild-type testes (Fig. 4B). Reciprocal co-IP confirmed the MORC2-MORC1 association in testis (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, co-IP analysis confirmed the interaction between MORC2 and MORC1 in transfected HEK293T cells (Fig. 4C). These results demonstrate that MORC2 forms a complex with MORC1 in testis.

MORC2 accumulates on the XY chromatin in meiotic germ cells

MORC2, a nuclear protein, is expressed in meiotic germ cells. To determine its subcellular localization, we performed immunofluorescent analysis of FLAG-MORC2 in spread nuclei of spermatocytes using anti-FLAG antibody (Supplementary Fig. S6A). While MORC2 was present throughout the nucleus in leptotene through diplotene spermatocytes, its abundance increased in the XY chromatin in pachytene and diplotene spermatocytes. Immunofluorescent analysis of MORC2 with anti-MORC2 antibody revealed similar diffuse nuclear distribution in prophase I spermatocytes and increased accumulation on the sex chromosomes in pachytene spermatocytes (Supplementary Fig. S6B). Sex chromosomes are silenced during male meiosis in a phenomenon known as MSCI [4–6]. This result suggests that MORC2 may play a role in MSCI.

MORC2 binds to gene promoters in pachytene spermatocytes

As MORC2 is associated with chromatin, 3 × FLAG-Morc2 mice were used to facilitate identification of MORC2-bound genomic sites (Supplementary Fig. S2). Pachytene spermatocytes were purified from adult testes using STA-PUT and used for CUT&RUN with anti-FLAG antibody followed by sequencing (triplicates) (Supplementary Fig. S7A). CUT&RUN experiments identified 6599 peaks (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table S4). 47.5% of peaks were in the promoter regions within 1 kb of TSS, whereas the other major MORC2-bound regions were distal intergenic (21.9%) and first exon (10.7%) (Fig. 5A and B). MORC2 bound to promoters of both autosomal and sex chromosome-linked genes (Fig. 5B). GO analysis of MORC2 target genes revealed enrichment in key biological processes such as chromatin remodeling, spermatogenesis, and regulation of transcription (Fig. 5C). Notably, 230 MORC2 target genes were single exon genes (Fig. 5D). MORC2 targets seemed to be evenly distributed among chromosomes (Fig. 5E). The top enriched sequence motifs in MORC2-bound genomic targets matched the consensus for MYBL2, MYBL1, and MYB transcription factors (Fig. 5F). MYBL1 (A-myb) is known to be essential for transcription during male meiosis [41, 42]. Our CUT&RUN results suggest that MORC2 regulates transcription in pachytene spermatocytes through binding at the promoters.

To determine the MORC2 binding profile in early germ cells, we performed FLAG × MORC2 CUT&RUN sequencing in P12 testes with anti-FLAG antibodies (Supplementary Fig. S8A and Supplementary Table S5). P12 testes contained spermatogonia and early meiotic germ cells up to zygotene spermatocytes. MORC2 also bound to the promoter regions of protein-coding genes in P12 testes (Supplementary Fig. S8B) but the binding was relatively weak (Supplementary Fig. S8C). The top GO terms of MORC2 target genes in P12 testes are chromatin organization and regulation of transcription (Supplementary Fig. S8D).

Postnatal loss of Morc2 causes defective meiosis and post-meiotic arrest

In addition to its expression in spermatogonia, MORC2 is also expressed in spermatocytes and spermatids (Fig. 1). To determine its potential role in these germ cells, we generated Morc2fl/− Stra8-GFPCre conditional knockout (referred to as Morc2ScKO) mice. Stra8-GFPCre is expressed in pre-meiotic spermatogonia but not in prospermatogonia [30]. In females, Stra8-GFPCre is expressed in embryonic oogonia prior to meiotic entry. Morc2ScKO males were sterile but females were fertile. The histology of ovaries from adult Morc2ScKO and control females was similar (Supplementary Fig. S9A). The size of litters from Morc2ScKO females was comparable with control females (Supplementary Fig. S9B). In contrast, Morc2ScKO testis was noticeably smaller than the control (Fig. 6A). Morc2ScKO testis weight was significantly lower than control (Fig. 6B). Morc2ScKO males lacked epididymal sperm (Fig. 6C). The MORC2 protein was nearly absent in Morc2ScKO testis (Fig. 6D). Histology revealed that the Morc2ScKO testis exhibited spermiogenic arrest (Fig. 6E). The most advanced germ cells in the Morc2ScKO testis were round spermatids and the Morc2ScKO epididymis contained sloughed round spermatids instead of sperm (Fig. 6E). There was a notable loss of spermatocytes in the Morc2ScKO testis (Fig. 6E). We found that early pachytene cells were present but mid to late pachytene and diplotene cells were severely depleted in the Morc2ScKO testis (Fig. 6F). Precise staging of seminiferous tubules showed that round spermatids were arrested at step 4 in the Morc2ScKO testis (Fig. 6G and Supplementary Fig. S10). These results show that inactivation of Morc2 prior to meiotic entry leads to a partial meiotic block at the early to mid pachytene transition and a complete spermiogenic block at step 4 of round spermatids.

We next monitored chromosomal synapsis and meiotic recombination in Morc2-deficient pachytene spermatocytes. Chromosomal synapsis appeared to be normal in Morc2ScKO pachytene spermatocytes (Fig. 6H). In early pachytene cells from both control and Morc2ScKO testes, γH2AX, a marker of DNA damage response, localized mainly to the XY body and also to autosomes as flares (Fig. 6H). In contrast, γH2AX autosomal flares disappeared in control mid pachytene cells but were still present in 70% of Morc2ScKO mid pachytene cells, suggesting a defect in meiotic double strand break repair in Morc2-deficient pachytene cells (Fig. 6H).

We examined LINE1 and IAP retrotransposons in adult Morc2ScKO testes by immunofluorescence of LINE1 ORF1 and IAP GAG. In contrast with de-repression of retrotransposons in Morc2DcKO testes, we did not observe de-repression of LINE1 and IAP retrotransposons in Morc2ScKO testes. In consistence, RNA-seq analysis did not show upregulation of evolutionarily young LINE1 retrotransposons (such as L1Md_A, L1_Md_T, and L1_Md_Gf) or IAPEz-int in Morc2-deficient pachytene spermatocytes from Morc2ScKO testes (Supplementary Table S6). These results show that MORC2 is dispensable for retrotransposon silencing in meiotic and post-meiotic germ cells in males.

MORC2 represses transcription with a preference to X-linked genes

To assess the effect of MORC2 inactivation on the transcriptome of meiotic cells, we performed RNA-seq analysis of control and Morc2ScKO pachytene cells purified using the STA-PUT method (Fig. 7A). As expected, the deleted exon 4 lacked sequencing reads in Morc2ScKO pachytene cells (Supplementary Fig. S9C). With the cutoff (fold change > 2, P < 0.01), we identified 3574 up-regulated DE reference genes but only 312 down-regulated reference genes in Morc2ScKO pachytene cells (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Table S6). While down-regulated protein-coding genes were not enriched in particular biological processes, the up-regulated protein-coding genes were highly enriched in the regulation of transcription (Fig. 7B). Down-regulated DE genes were randomly distributed among the chromosomes but an excess of up-regulated protein-coding DE genes mapped to the X chromosome [598 DE genes (16.7%) versus 214 genes expected (6%)] (Fig. 7C). GO analysis showed that the up-regulated X-linked genes were enriched in meiotic cell cycle, spermatid development, and negative regulation of transcription (Fig. 7D). Among the DE reference genes, there were 215 DE noncoding genes in Morc2ScKO pachytene cells: 115 up-regulated and 100 down-regulated (Supplementary Table S6). 29% of (33 out of 115) up-regulated noncoding genes were X-linked, showing an excess on the X chromosome, but none of down-regulated noncoding genes were X-linked.

There were two possible causes for the biased excess of up-regulated genes on the X chromosome. First, XY chromosomes undergo meiotic silencing (MSCI) [4–6]. As the Morc2ScKO pachytene population was enriched in early pachytene cells in comparison with the control population (Fig. 6F), incomplete MSCI in early pachytene cells would result in an apparent up-regulation of X-linked gene expression. Second, MORC2 could be directly involved in MSCI. To distinguish between these possibilities, we analyzed the overlap between MORC2 CUT&RUN target genes and DE genes. Only 37 MORC2 ChIP target genes (0.8%) were down-regulated, whereas 383 of them (8.7%) were up-regulated, suggesting that MORC2 may directly repress the transcription of these 383 genes (Fig. 7E). While the MORC2 CUT&RUN target genes were randomly distributed among the chromosomes (Fig. 5E), 129 (34%) out of the 383 MORC2-bound up-regulated genes were X- or Y-linked (Fig. 7F). GO analysis of the overlapped sex chromosome-linked genes showed enrichment in meiotic cell cycle, spermatid development, regulation of transcription, and chromatin organization (Fig. 7G). The genomic track view of a number of autosomal (Ccm2 and H1f0) and sex chromosome-linked (Suv39h1, Zfy1, and Zfy2) MORC2 target genes was shown (Supplementary Fig. S11). These results suggest that MORC2 represses transcription, particularly of X-linked genes, in pachytene spermatocytes.

Reduction of sex chromosome-wide H3K9me3 in Morc2-deficient pachytene cells

To investigate the mechanism of transcription repression by MORC2 in pachytene cells, we examined the repression histone modification—H3K9me3. Western blot showed that the abundance of H3K9me3 was comparable between control and Morc2ScKO pachytene cells as well as testes (Fig. 8A). We then performed H3K9me3 ChIP-seq using purified pachytene cells from control and Morc2ScKO testes (Supplementary Fig. S7B). In control cells, H3K9me3 signal peaked around TSS but also accumulated along gene bodies (Fig. 8B). In Morc2-deficient cells, H3K9me3 was substantially reduced along gene bodies and even more reduced around TSS (Fig. 8B). Strikingly, plotting all coding genes revealed that the decrease in the H3K9me3 levels of sex chromosomes was much more pronounced than autosomes in Morc2-deficient cells (Fig. 8C). While the decrease of H3K9me3 centered around TSS for both autosomal genes and X-linked genes, there was an additional chromosome-wide decrease of H3K9me3 for the X and Y chromosomes in Morc2-deficient pachytene cells (Fig. 8C). Consistently, mainly promoters had decreased H3K9me3 marks in Morc2-deficient cells (Fig. 8D). A total of 1458 genes had reduced H3K9me3 and upregulated expression in Morc2-deficient pachytene spermatocytes (Fig. 8E). Among these genes, 279 (19%) were X-linked and 8 were Y-linked (Fig. 8E). The most enriched GO term among 287 sex chromosome-linked genes was “negative regulation of transcription” (Fig. 8F). The genomic track view showed the gene-wide H3K9me3 reduction in sex-linked genes (Suv39h1, Zfy1, and Zfy2) (Supplementary Fig. S11). These results demonstrate that loss of H3K9me3 on sex chromosomes causes increased expression of sex chromosome-linked genes in Morc2-deficient pachytene cells.

Deficiency of MORC2 causes nuclear to cytoplasmic translocation of SETDB1

A previous study showed that inactivation of SETDB1, a H3K9 methyltransferase, led to more upregulated genes than downregulated genes in pachytene spermatocytes (Fig. 9A) [43]. The upregulated genes in SETDB1-deficient germ cells were preferentially X-linked (Supplementary Fig. S12A). Notably there was a substantial overlap of all up-regulated genes and an even larger overlap of X or Y-linked upregulated genes in Morc2ScKO and Setdb1cKO pachytene cells (Fig. 9B and C). Co-immunoprecipitation showed that MORC2 was associated with SETDB1 in testis (Fig. 9D). Co-transfection and co-immunoprecipitation in HEK293T cells confirmed the association between MORC2 and SETDB1 (Fig. 9E). In wild-type pachytene cells, SETDB1 accumulates on the XY chromatin (Supplementary Fig. S12B) [43]. In Morc2-deficient pachytene cells, SETDB1 was substantially reduced in the XY chromatin and also reduced throughout the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. S12B). SETDB1 was nuclear in wild-type pachytene cells, but, strikingly, mislocalized to the cytoplasm in Morc2-deficient pachytene cells (Fig. 9F). TRIM28 is a known SETDB1-associated protein. Unlike SETDB1, TRIM28 remained nuclear in both control and Morc2ScKO pachytene cells (Supplementary Fig. S12C). These results demonstrate that MORC2 forms a complex with SETDB1 in testes and is required for its nuclear localization in pachytene spermatocytes.

Discussion

Here we have demonstrated two essential roles for MORC2 in spermatogenesis: retrotransposon silencing and MSCI. Our data support that MORC2 performs these functions through distinct binding partners: MORC1 in retrotransposon silencing in gonocytes (Fig. 9G) and SETDB1 in sex chromosome inactivation in meiotic cells (Fig. 9H).

Our Morc2DcKO mice display similar phenotypes with the Morc1-deficient mice: de-repression of retrotransposons, meiotic arrest in males, and male only sterility [10, 11]. In testes from both mutants, retrotransposons exhibit DNA hypomethylation, which might be the cause of their de-repression. However, the molecular mechanism of MORC1 in retrotransposon de-repression remains elusive [10, 11]. Here, we find that MORC2 and MORC1 form a complex in testis and in co-transfected cells. Therefore, these results support a unifying model in which MORC2 and MORC1 function as a complex in repression of transposable elements in male germ cells (Fig. 9G). Loss of either MORC1 or MORC2 leads to hypomethylation of retrotransposons in pre-meiotic male germ cells (Fig. 9G). Interestingly, de novo DNA methyltransferases DNMT3L and DNMT3C were among the MORC2-associated proteins from newborn testes (Supplementary Table S3). Loss of DNMT3L or DNMT3C leads to hypomethylation and activation of retrotransposons in male germ cells [44–46]. Therefore, it is possible that MORC1/MORC2 recruits DNMT3L/DNMT3C to methylate retrotransposon DNA for silencing. In addition, MORC1 regulates SETDB1-mediated H3K9me3 deposition in retrotransposon loci through an unknown mechanism (Fig. 9G) [11]. In Morc1-deficient mice, the piRNA pathway remains intact [10]. In Morc2DcKO testes, MIWI2 remains nuclear, indicating that the piRNA pathway is functional. These results suggest that the MORC1/MORC2 complex functions in retrotransposon silencing either downstream or independent of the piRNA pathway.

In human somatic cells, MORC2 interacts with the HUSH complex and is critical for HUSH-mediated LINE1 transcriptional silencing [14, 16]. Mutations in MORC2 causes axonal CMT disease through hyperactivation of the HUSH complex [14]. The functions of the HUSH complex in germ cells have not been reported. Surprisingly, components of the HUSH complex were not identified in MORC2-associated proteins from testes, indicating that MORC2 may function independently of the HUSH complex in germ cells.

SETDB1 is known to silence retrotransposons in mouse embryonic stem cells, primordial germ cells, and oocytes [15, 24–26]. The retrotransposons silenced by SETDB1 in these cells belong to the endogenous retrovirus (ERV) families, which account for ∼10% of the genome, but not LINE1, which accounts for ∼20% of the genome. In addition, inactivation of Setdb1 with the Ddx4-Cre causes only mild de-silencing of retrotransposons in germ cells and did not result in early meiotic arrest, expected of mouse mutants with activated retrotransposons in male germ cells [47]. In contrast, MORC2, like MORC1, represses both LINE1 and IAP (ERVK family) in germ cells and Morc2DcKO testis exhibits pre-pachytene meiotic arrest. Therefore, MORC2 represses LINE1 retrotransposon silencing in germ cells through interaction mainly with MORC1, but not SETDB1.

We find that MORC2 is associated with SETDB1 in postnatal testis. Both MORC2 and SETDB1 accumulates in the XY body in pachytene spermatocytes. Morc2ScKO testis displays defects in meiotic progression through the mid-pachytene stage and round spermatid arrest but does not show activation of retrotransposons. In meiotic cells, MORC2 binds to gene promoters. Inactivation of MORC2 leads to transcriptional upregulation of preferentially X chromosome-linked genes and depletion of H3K9me3 marks from sex chromosomes. Morc2ScKO testis showed a depletion of mid-to-late pachytene spermatocytes. This could be explained by the upregulation of two Y-linked genes Zfy1 and Zfy2 in Morc2ScKO testis, as their upregulation is toxic to germ cells [48]. Notably, inactivation of Setdb1 causes similar defects in MSCI and H3K9me3 depletion from sex chromosomes [43]. ATF7IP2, a testis-specific binding partner of SETDB1, also localizes to the XY body in pachytene spermatocytes and regulates MSCI through deposition of H3K9me3 marks on sex chromosomes [49, 50]. Importantly, SETDB1 is nuclear in wild-type pachytene spermatocytes but is mainly cytoplasmic in Morc2-deficient pachytene spermatocytes, showing that MORC2 is required for SETDB1 localization to the nucleus. The fact that MORC2, ATF7IP2, and SETDB1 all regulate H3K9me3 on sex chromosomes supports the possibility that they work together in spermatocytes. The HUSH complex recruits both MORC2 and SETDB1 in somatic cells [14, 21]. Although the role of HUSH complex in spermatogenesis is unknown, our data support that MORC2 could directly recruit SETDB1 in transcriptional silencing of sex chromosome-linked genes in male meiotic cells.

While Morc2 is conserved in many species including vertebrates and broadly expressed, Morc2b is only found in rodents and is testis-specific. Loss of MORC2B in mice leads to meiotic arrest but not de-repression of retrotransposons [12]. In addition, deficiency of MORC2B, unlike MORC2, did not cause preferential up-regulation of sex chromosome-linked genes, suggesting functional specialization of the newly evolved Morc2b gene.

Protocadherin gene clusters are expressed as cell surface molecules in the vertebrate nervous system. Brain-specific inactivation of Morc2 causes upregulation of protocadherin (Pcdh) gene clusters in neurons [28]. Upregulation of protocadherin genes provides an explanation for the neuronal defects in Morc2 brain-specific knockout mice. Here, we also find upregulation of protocadherin gene cluster in Morc2-deficient male germ cells. However, the biological consequences of upregulated protocadherin genes in Morc2-deficient germ cells is not clear. Interestingly, most upregulated protocadherin genes in both Morc2-deficient neurons and germ cells belong to the protocadherin gamma cluster on chromosome 18, suggesting a conserved mechanism of protocadherin repression by MORC2 in both cell types. Each protocadherin cluster gene has one large coding exon and thus resembles single-exon transposable elements [51]. The TE-like genomic nature of protocadherin gene cluster may explain their recognition and repression by MORC2.

The BET (Bromodomain and extra terminal) family of proteins are evolutionarily conserved from yeast to humans [52]. The N-terminal double bromodomains of BET proteins bind to acetylated lysine in histones and non-histone proteins. The C-terminal extra-terminal (ET) domain is involved in protein-protein interactions. Budding yeast has only one BET protein, Bdf1, which is essential for meiosis and thus sporulation [53, 54]. In mammals, there are four BET proteins: BRDT, BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4. BRDT is testis-specific, whereas the other three BET proteins (BRD 2, 3, 4) are broadly expressed. BRDT binds to hyperacetylated histone H4 prior to histone replacement by sperm-specific transition proteins and protamines and is essential for chromatin reorganization during spermiogenesis [39, 40, 55]. Interestingly, injection of JQ1, a bromodomain inhibitor, causes reversible male contraception in mice [56]. All four BET proteins (BRDT, BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4) were identified in MORC2-associated proteins from testis. However, BRDT does not appear to be required for retrotransposon silencing [39, 40, 55]. The genetic studies of BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4 in germ cells have not been reported. Future studies are warranted to examine the implications of the association of BET family proteins with MORC2 in retrotransposon silencing in germ cells and meiotic progression.
