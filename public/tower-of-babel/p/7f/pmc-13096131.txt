Performance improvements of recycled concrete and ceramic aggregates using graphene oxide nanocoating
Authors: Andrea Antolín-Rodríguez, Julia García-González, Manuel Ignacio Guerra-Romero, Andrés Juan-Valdés
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Construction and demolition waste (CDW) represents—35% of global waste and—40% of related emissions. Recycled aggregates from CDW support sustainability and circular economy strategies. This study investigates the enhancement of recycled concrete and ceramic aggregates using graphene oxide (GO) nanocoating, applied without coupling agents. The coating removes fine particles from aggregate surfaces and provides waterproofing. Ceramic particles, due to higher porosity, absorb more GO than concrete particles. Experimental results show water absorption reductions of 19% (concrete) and 25% (ceramic) aggregates. GO forms a uniform layer, decreases microporosity, preserves surface roughness, and reduces particle detachment by 17% and 25% for concrete and ceramic aggregates, respectively. SEM analysis confirms a pore-filling effect consistent with waterproof behavior. These findings demonstrate the GO nanocoating effectively improves durability and performance of recycled aggregates, offering a practical route towards sustainable construction materials.

Introduction

In recent decades the demand for infrastructure has increased as a consequence of population growth, boosting the consumption of concrete and cement and the production of construction and demolition waste (CDW)1. The European Union (EU) is one of the three largest economies globally and, therefore, one of the main CDW producers2; its advanced CDW management system allows for a high recovery rate of approximately 70%3. Laws, policies, technologies, and regulations for the management of CDW in the EU are leading towards a circular and more sustainable construction sector, and thus research in this field focuses on transforming CDW into new raw materials that can be used in the manufacture of concrete. This is considered an efficient strategy, which contributes to the circular economy and avoids environmental damage4,5. In this quest for sustainable development in the construction industry, numerous studies6–14 have demonstrated the feasibility of replacing natural aggregates (NA) with recycled aggregates (RA). However, a high water-absorption capacity has also been reported in literature for recycled concrete aggregates (RCA) due to their high porosity; this characteristic hinders the approval of this material in the construction sector10,15,16.

In recent years, efforts have focused on developing techniques able to reduce RA permeability. The four main strategies to achieve this goal are:To remove the mortar adhered to the surface of the RA by applying chemical treatments (polymers or HCl) or mechanical polishing methods17–19.To improve the matrix of concrete with RA by incorporating water-reducing materials (such as plasticizers or superplasticizers)20, pozzolanic materials21,22, or nanomaterials into the mix23.To optimize the RA mixing process following a two- and three-stage mixing approach24,25.To reinforce RA by bio-deposition26–28 or carbonation treatments29–31, or by nanomaterial coatings32,33.

In recent years, nanoengineering has emerged as a powerful tool for enhancing the performance of cementitious materials, with nanomaterials such as nano-silica, nano-TiO₂, carbon nanotubes, and graphene derivatives being widely studied for their ability to refine pore structure, accelerate hydration, and improve durability34–36. At the same time, recycled aggregates (RA) have attracted increasing attention as a key component of circular economy strategies in construction. Various treatments have been proposed to mitigate their drawbacks, including mechanical, thermal, chemical, and surface-coating methods. However, the application of graphene oxide (GO) as a direct nanocoating for RA remains scarcely investigated, with only preliminary studies reported. In this context, the present research contributes to the field by demonstrating the potential of GO coating to improve the physical properties of commercial recycled aggregates of mixed and ceramic origin.

Surface nanoengineering has been studied in recent years with the aim of improving RA performance. The nanomaterials used are classified according to their morphology and shape: nanoparticles (zero dimensional; OD), nanofibers (one dimensional; 1D), and nanosheets (two dimensional; 2D)37. GO is one of the most prominent nanomaterials due to its remarkable advantages, such as a large specific surface area and a high ability to form bonds and combine with other molecules or products. This nanomaterial, available in large quantities, has been reported also as environmentally friendly38.

GO has been regarded as a nanoengineering material in the utilization of aggregate surface and silica fume to protect cementitious composites and improve their performance39–43. In addition, modifications at the concrete interface have been reported when GO-coated aggregates are used in the production of concrete, such as the formation of a more densified microstructure and a decrease in thickness at the interfacial transition zone (ITZ)43,44. These promising results have encouraged us to evaluate the effects of GO as a coating material for RA which will substitute NA in the manufacture of concrete. Our goal is to address the high permeability associated with RA.

Recent reviews have further highlighted the potential and challenges of recycled aggregate enhancement through physical and nano-scale modification strategies, including graphene-based treatments45. Other recent studies have explored the physical performance, modification mechanisms, and environmental impacts of nanomaterial-treated recycled aggregates46,47. Together with the works of Allujami et al.35 and Zhan et al.48 these studies emphasize the relevance of graphene oxide and similar nanostructures for improving recycled aggregate durability and circularity. The present research contributes to this evolving field by providing new experimental evidence on the surface nanoengineering of recycled concrete and ceramic aggregates using GO nanocoating49.

Recycled aggregates, particularly those derived from concrete and ceramic waste, typically present high porosity and water absorption, limiting their large-scale use in structural concrete. This study addresses this gap by proposing graphene oxide (GO) nanocoating to improve the surface characteristics and durability of such aggregates. It also contributes to the advancement of recycled aggregate recovery by demonstrating the viability of graphene oxide nanocoating as a sustainable, low-dose surface treatment. Unlike previous work that focused exclusively on recycled concrete aggregates or required chemical coupling agents, this work examines both recycled concrete aggregates and ceramic aggregates obtained directly from construction and demolition waste plants. The results lay the foundation for future research on mechanical and durability properties, while highlighting the potential of graphene oxide nanocoating to support circular economy goals in the construction sector.

The samples were selected mainly based on their composition because numerous studies 15,16,51–53 have reported high-water absorption capacity in RA with high presence of concrete or mortar particles and/or ceramic fraction, which limits their approval for the construction sector.

Immersion of RA in a nanomaterial suspension has been proven to cause the penetration of part of the suspension into the RA porous system, with the rest of the suspension remaining on the surface and creating a coating 49,54. A mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) test was performed on RA cement-based and ceramic particles using a MICROMERITICS porosimeter (Autopore IV 9500) which can reach a 227.5-MPa pressure, enabling the study of pore sizes up to 67 Å. The results obtained in terms of pore size distribution are discussed together with the consolidation effect of the nanoprotection treatment.

Graphene oxide (GO)

The graphene oxide (GO) used in this study was supplied by Graphenea (Gipuzkoa, Spain) as an aqueous dispersion. The product is synthesized from graphite using a modified Hummers method, according to the manufacturer’s protocol, which involves oxidation and exfoliation processes to obtain single-layer GO flakes. These flakes are finally dispersed in water, resulting in a stable aqueous suspension without additives. In this work, an aqueous GO suspension with a concentration of 0.5 mg/ml was employed. This dosage was selected based on previous studies conducted by the authors 55–57 where different types of GO (commercial and non-commercial) and a range of concentrations were evaluated to identify a suitable balance between dispersion stability, performance, and cost. From these investigations, 0.5 mg/ml was determined to be the most effective and economically viable concentration and was therefore adopted in the present study.

Before application, the dispersion was subjected to ultrasound for 5 min to ensure homogeneity and minimize agglomeration. This procedure ensures the reproducibility of the treatment and complies with the protocols described in recent literature. RA nanoprotection treatment with GO

The first step was to immerse the RA in the GO dispersion for 10 min, following previous studies in which RA were able to capture up to 70% of the water they could absorb in 24 h during the first 10 min 58,59. A 10-min immersion is therefore considered sufficient for the RA to be optimally coated by the GO dispersion.

The second step was to dry the RA in an oven at 70 ± 5 °C for 48 h to obtain dry GO-coated RA.

Testing methods

Absorption of the nanocoating

RA weight is expected to decrease following the nanoprotection process due to the cleaning action of the water present in the GO dispersion. To verify this, four samples of each type of RA were taken, and recorded their dry weight (oven-dried at 70 °C for 48 h) before and after the nanoprotection treatment. RA wet weight (after immersion) was also recorded. The weight variation would be used later to establish the amount of GO dispersion absorbed and the amount of GO particles deposited on the samples.

Water absorption

To assess the ability of the nanocoating to create a waterproof effect, water absorption tests were performed on four samples of coated RA and on four samples of uncoated RA following the pycnometer method described in the European standard EN 1097-660 with two minor modifications: the first modification is the drying temperature, set as 70 ± 5 °C as higher temperatures may eliminate the water present between the layers of some components, such as C–S–H gels61; the second modification is the drying time, which was extended to 48 h instead of 24 h because the drying temperature was lowered.

Resistance to sonication cycles

Ultrasonic treatment is a method normally used to break down or clean materials adhered to a surface62,63. The amount of material shed during this treatment can provide a direct estimate of the treatment–substrate bond strength and establish the consolidation efficiency of the surface treatment applied64. In the present work, RA with and without nanoprotection were subjected to six sonication cycles. In each cycle, the samples were immersed in deionized water and sonicated using a 45 kHz ultrasonic bath for 5 min and then dried in an oven at 70 ± 5 °C for 48 h. The amount of material released after completing the ultrasonic treatment was established by weighing the aggregates. Therefore, the sonication resistance is represented by the weight variation between the initial RA dry weight and the final dry weight after completing all sonication cycles.

The ultrasonic resistance test was limited to six cycles, in line with previous studies on the treatment of recycled aggregates, which also applied six cycles as a standard procedure. Furthermore, in our experiments no significant variation in weight was detected beyond the fourth cycle, indicating that the detachment process had stabilized. Consequently, six cycles were considered sufficient to assess the consolidating effect of the GO coating.

Characterization of GO coating by SEM analysis

To study the RA surface morphology, the microstructure and distribution of the GO coating on RACO and RACE were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and the chemical composition of the coating with energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis. SEM observations were conducted using a Hitachi S-4810 scanning electron microscope operating at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, a working distance of 10 mm, and equipped with an Oxford INCA energy-dispersive X-ray detector. Samples were oven-dried at 70 ± 5 °C for 48 h, mounted on aluminum stubs using carbon adhesive tape, and sputter-coated with a thin gold layer (≈ 10 nm) to ensure electrical conductivity before analysis.

Statistical analysis

All experimental tests were performed with four replicates per condition (coated and uncoated RA). The results are presented as mean values. To assess whether the differences observed between GO-coated and uncoated aggregates were statistically significant, a Student´s t-test (two-tailed, independent samples) was applied at a 95% confidence level (p < 0.05). In the case of comparisons involving more than two groups (e.g., RACO vs. RACE, with and without coating), a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey´s post hoc test was used to identify significant differences. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS.

The results presented in Fig. 5 show that the GO treatment does not increase the weight of the Ra samples analyzed. These results are predictable, as the water in the dispersion evaporated after the drying process, the GO particles are nanosized, and their density is only 0.26 g/cm3 65.

After the final phase, all samples show a small decrease in weight, 0.4% for RACO and 0.6% for RACE. This weight variation is due to the elimination of the fine fraction adhered to the RA as a result of the cleaning action exerted by the water in the GO dispersion66. To isolate this effect, a control test was performed out by immersing the samples in distilled water under the same experimental conditions. The results indicated a comparable loss of mass, confirming that the weight variation is due to the physical cleaning effect of the water.

Thus, indirectly, the nanoprotection treatment reduces RA water absorption, favoring their use in the production of concrete.

As can be seen in Fig. 5, following the immersion phase, all the samples show an increase in weight as a result of the GO dispersion absorption. RACO weight increased by 12.5%, while RACE weight increased by 20.2%. As expected, RACE absorbed more GO dispersion than RACO did because their porous crystalline structure and high clay content translate into a higher absorption capacity67. Water absorption

Our results (Fig. 6) indicate that GO coating reduces water permeability. The RA samples show a reduction in water absorption of 19% for RACO and of 25% for RACE. Following the nanocoating application, RACE samples showed a more pronounced waterproof effect than the RACO ones did; given that the RACE samples had absorbed more GO dispersion, it was expected that they present greater protective coating.Fig. 6RA water absorption before and after nanocoating. Furthermore, reduction in water absorption in the samples studied depends also on their composition. RACO samples contain a high percentage of NA, which are much less absorbent (1–5%) than RA (3–10%)6,10,68–71; this results in a lower reduction in water absorption for the RACO samples.

To further investigate the behavior of GO nanocoating regarding water permeability, RA porosity was analyzed, as this represents the main parameter to define water absorption capacity. An MIP analysis (Fig. 7) was performed on the ceramic particles, as these show the highest impermeability after applying nanocoating. The analysis results suggest a significant reduction in microporosity, which explains the waterproofing effect in RACE samples. A continuous well-adhered nanocoating layer is present on the surface of these RA, providing a pore-filling effect.Fig. 7Pore size distribution of ceramic particles before and after nanocoating application.

Pore size distribution of ceramic particles before and after nanocoating application.

Lu et al.49 used a silane (KH550) and ethanol coupling to ensure a higher adhesion of the GO nanocoating. Their results suggest a reduction in water absorption for the RA obtained from waste concrete. These authors observed a decrease in water absorption of up to 14%. In other studies, such as those by Nguyen et al.44 and Lu et al.43,72, GO nanocoating was also able to reduce water absorption. In both works, the authors analyzed the use of reduced GO (rGO) coating to increase the effectiveness of the treatment on fine aggregates (natural silica sand), observing a reduction in water absorption of up to 52%. All authors attribute the reduction of water absorption to the pore-filling effect of GO nanosheets.

Resistance to sonication

The RA samples present differences in resistance to ultrasonic attack depending on their composition. After sonication, samples with a higher concrete/mortar content (RACO) showed 17% less weight loss than untreated samples.

However, samples with higher ceramic content (RACE) experiment an increase in sonication resistance in both nanocoated and untreated aggregates. Similarly to the abovementioned samples, the nanocoated RACE experiment a lower weight loss (25% less) than the untreated ones. As RACE samples had absorbed more GO dispersion than the RACO ones (Fig. 5), the bond between the coating and their surface might be more consolidated, which allows for less particle detachment (Fig. 8).Fig. 8RA weight loss with and without nanocoating after sonication process. Weight variation and porosity test results suggest that ceramic particles absorb more nanocoating and, therefore, its pore-filling effect is higher in this type of particle. This consolidating effect may be a result of the interaction between the oxygenated functional groups of GO and other surface-active groups on the RA, for which the 2D assembly property of GO would be key73.

It can concluded that GO nanocoating provided RA with resistance to an ultrasonic attack since only a limited particle loss was obtained and a good union of the treatment with the surface of the RA was observed. No published results on the sonication resistance of nanocoatings on RA are available to date for us to compare them with ours. However, in previous works, such as those by Lu et al.43,72, the strength of the bond between the coating and the aggregates has been tested. The authors evaluated the bonding of the GO coating with natural fine aggregates by mixing the aggregates with deionized water and stirring vigorously for 5 min, simulating a mixing process. They then allowed the mixture to stand for 30 min, which allowed the fine particles to settle. They observed that the drainer exhibited a color similar to that exhibited by the uncoated particles. Consequently, they established a relatively good graphene-arid bond quality.

Characterization of GO coating by SEM analysis

SEM analysis facilitates the observation of the nanocoating on the surface of the aggregates. Figure 9a shows the GO coating as a uniform film along the entire aggregate surface, in agreement with the results obtained by Lu et al.49, who also studied GO as an RA nanocoating and observed the adhesion of the coating to the aggregate surface and the coating uniformly distributed.Fig. 9SEM images of GO coating on the surface of the ceramic aggregate.

SEM images of GO coating on the surface of the ceramic aggregate.

Interestingly, the rough microstructure of the aggregate can still be discerned beneath the GO coating (Fig. 9b), suggesting that the GO coating thickness is minimal. The preservation of the surface roughness of the aggregate after the application of a coating is a typical characteristic of conformal coatings. Consequently, GO coating can be defined as a conformal coating with minimal thickness that adheres to the surface of the aggregate.

This property of the GO coating is due to its composition, which is entirely made up of nanometer-thick GO nanosheets74. In addition to their thin nature, their folds and wrinkles (Fig. 10) indicate a flexibility capacity75 that affords them physical adaptability. The galleries still available between GO nanosheets may form possible water diffusion channels enabling cement hydration76.Fig. 10SEM image of GO nanosheet adapted to aggregate surface.

SEM image of GO nanosheet adapted to aggregate surface.

Figure 11 shows the partial pore-sealing effect of the nanocoating, as GO nanosheets were found on the internal surface of the pores along the entire surface of the treated aggregates, improving their impermeability. The pore-filling effect is favored by the particle size of GO nanosheets (< 10 µm), which are considered ultrafine nanoparticles capable of filling the microcracks and voids in the RA microstructure48. Considering the GO layer on the surface of the aggregate and its pore-sealing effect, a waterproof effect is therefore expected. This result is in good agreement with the decrease in water absorption values obtained for the nanocoated aggregates.Fig. 11SEM image, EDX spectra, and elemental composition of GO dispersion in nanocoated aggregates.

SEM image, EDX spectra, and elemental composition of GO dispersion in nanocoated aggregates.

The chemical composition of the nanocoating used in the present work was determined by the EDX results (Fig. 11). The main elements present on the surface of the treated samples were carbon and oxygen, note that these are also the main components of the GO dispersion.

The combined evidence from SEM/EDS and MIP analyses indicates that GO nanosheets not only form a continuous surface film but also penetrate partially into the pore network, reducing the proportion of micropores (< 100 nm). The oxygen-containing functional groups of GO likely interact with surface hydroxyls and calcium-bearing phases of the recycled aggregates, promoting hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions. This dual effect—pore refinement and interfacial bonding—explains the observed reduction in water absorption and the enhanced resistance to particle detachment under ultrasonic treatment.

Conclusions

In conclusion, GO dispersion proves to be an effective surface treatment for recycled aggregates, as it generates a uniform coating, reduces surface porosity, and results in an slight weight reduction (0.4% in concrete aggregates and 0.6% in recycled aggregates) due to the removal of fine fractions. This simple technique significantly improves the potential use of coarse recycled aggregates in cement-based materials without increasing their mass, given the nanometric size of GO particles and the evaporation of dispersion water during drying.

Treatment with GO dispersion significantly reduced water absorption – by 19% for treated recycled concrete aggregates and 25% for treated recycled ceramic aggregates – compared with untreated aggregates, confirming the limited waterproof effect of GO nanocoating on the RA. This can lead to an improvement in the workability of concrete when using these nanocoated aggregates, without the need to increase water content. At the same time, this treatment if beneficial for the permeability of the concrete and its resistance to the penetration of any type of aggressive substance that could compromise its long-term performance. The nanocoating effect was greater the higher the content of ceramic particles was in the RA samples.

Ultrasonic attack tests confirmed the consolidating effect of GO nanocoating, which was more pronounced with higher ceramic particles content. Treated concrete aggregates lost 17% fewer particles than untreated aggregates, while treated ceramic aggregates lost 25% fewer particles than untreated aggregates, indicating strong nanocoating adhesion. This effect is directly related to the greater absorption of nanocoating by ceramic particles and its deposition not only on the surface but also within the pores, which reduces the release of material after ultrasonic attack.

The results of our SEM analysis demonstrate that GO nanocoating on RA shows a peculiar appearance, characterized by flexible thin films that suitably match the surface structure of the aggregates.

From an industrial perspective, the scalability of the GO nanocoating process appears technically feasible, as the immersion-drying procedure can be integrated as an additional washing or surface-treatment step in recycling plants. Although the current cost of graphene oxide is relatively high compared with conventional additives, the very low dosages required (milligrams per milliliter) limit the overall consumption per ton of aggregates.

From a techno-economic standpoint, the estimated cost of GO nanocoating treatment corresponds to approximately 0.8–1.2 € per ton of aggregates when using a 0.5 mg/mL dispersion, based on current commercial GO prices. Although the production of GO involves oxidation and exfoliation steps with non-negligible energy demand, its low dosage substantially limits its life-cycle impact. Recent LCA studies indicate that the use of nanocarbon materials at milligram-scale concentrations contributes less than 1% to the embodied CO₂ footprint of concrete. Moreover, as the GO coating does not chemically alter the bulk aggregate, treated particles remain compatible with existing CDW recycling processes, enabling multiple reuse cycles. Nevertheless, future research should quantify the persistence and effectiveness of GO layers after successive mechanical and thermal recycling operations.

In terms of environmental impact, the GO nanocoating treatment must be considered within a broader life-cycle perspective. Although the current production of graphene oxide involves processes with a non-negligible environmental footprint, the very low dosages required in this application reduce its direct impact. Moreover, if the treatment enhances the durability of concrete by reducing permeability, it may indirectly lower the environmental costs associated with repair, maintenance, and premature replacement. At the end of service life, aggregates treated with GO remain recyclable within existing CDW management systems, as the nanomaterial does not hinder their recovery. Nevertheless, further studies are required to assess the stability of GO coatings in successive recycling cycles and to quantify the overall life-cycle benefits or burdens of large-scale implementation.

Future work will focus on extending these findings by assessing durability, long-term mechanical performance, and detailed workability behavior of concrete incorporating aggregates treated with GO dispersion. In particular, the interactions between the treated aggregates and the cementitious matrix will be investigated to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the material´s overall behavior. In addition, future studies should incorporate particle size distribution analysis before and after GO treatment to directly quantify the cleaning effect suggested by the slight weight loss observed in this work, thereby strengthening the interpretation of fines removal during the immersion step.
