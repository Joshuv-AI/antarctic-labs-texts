Analyzing ethical dimensions of mental disorders: trends and key research areas through bibliometric methods
Authors: Bo Wang, Oyyappan Duraipandi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to explore key ethical issues related to mental disorders through a bibliometric and cluster-based content analysis of existing academic literature. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the Scopus database (Elsevier) up to December 31, 2024, using ethics-and mental disorder-related keywords. The search was limited to English-language journal articles in medicine, psychology, neuroscience, and other related fields. After title and abstract screening, 1,271 articles were included (κ=0.907). Bibliometric analyses including keyword co-occurrence, citation coupling, and country/author mapping were performed using VOSviewer (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University) and Gephi (Gephi Consortium). A cluster-based content analysis was used to interpret the thematic structure of the field. The annual publication volume showed an upward fluctuating trend, with increasing scholarly attention post-1994. Coauthor networks revealed weak centralization, and the core author group remained underdeveloped. Research has been geographically concentrated in North America and Western Europe, particularly in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada. Keyword analysis identified six major thematic clusters: (1) conceptual foundations and policy frameworks in mental health ethics, (2) ethical challenges in psychiatric care, (3) research ethics, (4) patient autonomy and rights, (5) end-of-life decision-making and palliative ethics, and (6) neuroethics and emerging biomedical technologies. Recent popular topics include artificial intelligence, epistemic injustice, and medical aid for the dying. This study maps the intellectual structure and evolving focus of the ethical discourse on mental health. These findings highlight the need for ethically responsive frameworks that address patient autonomy, technological advancement, and global equity.

Introduction

The ethical considerations surrounding mental disorders represent a critical dimension of contemporary healthcare and bioethics. Mental healthcare is unique in its intersection of medical, social, and legal domains, frequently engaging in issues of autonomy, dignity, and societal obligations [1]. Unlike physical illnesses, mental disorders often affect an individual’s cognitive and emotional capacities, which can complicate informed decision-making and consent processes [2,3]. Consequently, ethical challenges in mental healthcare transcend clinical settings, thereby affecting public policy, human rights, and community integration.

A primary ethical tension in mental healthcare arises from the need to balance respect for patient autonomy with the imperative to prevent harm [4]. This is particularly evident in cases involving involuntary hospitalization, forced medication, or other coercive measures, which may mitigate risks to the individual or society but simultaneously infringe upon the individual’s autonomy and freedom. Such dilemmas necessitate the application of principles such as proportionality and the “least restrictive alternative” to ensure that interventions are ethically defensible and minimally intrusive [5].

In addition to coercion, mental healthcare raises significant questions about justice and equity. Individuals with mental disorders frequently face stigma, discrimination, and disparities in access to care, perpetuating cycles of marginalization and inequality [6]. These inequities are exacerbated by systemic biases in research, diagnosis, and treatment, which often overlook the diverse needs of populations across socioeconomic, cultural, and geographic contexts [7]. Ethical frameworks must address these disparities and advocate for policies and practices that promote inclusivity, cultural competence, and fair resource allocation.

Emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) and brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) add another layer of complexity to the ethics of mental healthcare. Although these innovations promise to revolutionize diagnosis and treatment, they also pose new ethical challenges in terms of privacy, consent, and the potential for misuse or unintended consequences. As mental healthcare increasingly incorporates such technologies, ethical discourse must evolve to address these novel concerns [8,9].

Therefore, conducting a systematic theoretical study of the ethical challenges associated with mental disorders is crucial for establishing robust moral frameworks and regulatory mechanisms. This study employed bibliometric methods to perform a quantitative analysis of the relevant literature and to identify and examine the primary ethical issues related to mental disorders. By integrating content analysis, these issues were explored in depth. This study offers theoretical and practical guidance for developing and refining ethical policies in mental healthcare.

Methods

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive search of relevant literature in the SCOPUS database (Elsevier, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) up to December 31, 2024, without limiting the start date. We searched for two categories of keywords: “ethics terms” (ethics, ethical, moral, justice, autonomy, and coercion) and “mental disorder-related search terms” (to more fully cover the relevant research; search terms included: mental disorder, mental illness, psychiatric disorder, psychological disorder, emotional disorder, behavioral disorder, neuropsychiatric disorder, dysphrenia, phrenoblabia, psychonosema, and psychogenia), which applied to the title, abstract, or keyword section of the paper. In addition, we limited the Subject area to “Medicine, Nursing, Psychology, Neuroscience, Biochemistry, Genetics and Molecular Biology, Pharmacology, Toxicology and Pharmaceutics, Health Professions.” Only articles published in English were included. This search produced 4,711 articles.

Next, we read the titles and keywords to eliminate references that were irrelevant to the topic of the study, and 2,771 articles were retained at this stage. We then conducted a two-person manual screening by reading the abstracts to ensure the validity of the included literature. For example, the content of some articles was clinical or basic research on mental disorders, and there were relevant statements such as “the research has been approved by the medical ethics committee” in the abstract, causing confusion in the search. These problems were eliminated through manual review. The two researchers examined each other’s work. The agreement (kappa value) was 0.907, which is close to 1, indicating that the two screeners had a high degree of agreement regarding the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the articles. A total of 1,271 articles were included after this screening.

This study conducted a bibliometric analysis of the literature on ethical issues related to mental disorders and cluster-based content analysis. Bibliometrics is a methodological approach for the study of scientific activity, knowledge dissemination, and academic influence through a quantitative analysis of literature and its related features. Compared to a traditional literature review, which primarily relies on qualitative synthesis and may be constrained by the reviewer’s perspective, bibliometrics provides a systematic and quantitative approach that enables the mapping of research structures, trends, and influential contributions on a larger scale. Although bibliometrics may not capture the full contextual richness of qualitative reviews, it offers strong advantages in terms of objectivity, transparency, and scalability. Scholars in many disciplines such as medicine, management, and education have applied bibliometric techniques to map the structure of knowledge in a field, thereby identifying the most important and influential research elements [10-12]. This broader applicability demonstrates that bibliometric and cluster-based content analyses are well-suited for clarifying the ethical dimensions of mental health research within a wider scientific context.

In the bibliometric analysis of this study, we used VOSviewer software (Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, Leiden, The Netherlands) for quantitative analysis, including keyword co-occurrence and citation coupling [13]. In network visualization, a network consists of nodes and edges. Nodes can represent entities, such as countries, authors, or keywords, illustrating the relationships between these entities. The distance between the nodes indicates the strength of their connection; the shorter the distance, the stronger the association [14]. In this study, we focused on networks based on total link strength (TLS). Both the link and TLS properties show the number of connections between an item and other items, and the total strength of those connections. Gephi software (Gephi Consortium, Paris, France) was used to supplement part of the data analysis and present the results more clearly and accurately. Simultaneously, to supplement the quantitative analysis, we conducted a content analysis of the X clusters found in the co-occurrence keyword analysis for a deeper exploration and comprehensive understanding of the outstanding problems in the literature metrology results. To ensure clarity and comparability, we adopted consistent parameter settings in VOSviewer. In the coauthorship and country/region analyses, only units with three or more publications were included, whereas in the keyword co-occurrence analysis, keywords that appeared at least five times were retained. A minimum cluster size of five was applied across all analyses to improve clustering clarity and avoid generating excessively small or unstable clusters. These thresholds balanced inclusivity and interpretability. Excluding rare or isolated items reduced noise while highlighting the main research themes in a way that was both representative and robust.

Results

This section begins with a bibliographic coupling of keyword co-occurrence combined with cluster-based content analysis. We then extend the coupling approach to explore influencing factors such as publishing trends, authors, and countries. Finally, we construct and interpret a conceptual research framework derived from the bibliometric and clustering analyses.

1. Keyword co-occurrence analysis and clustering-based content analysis

1) Publication trend analysis

The distribution of the number of published papers over the years is shown in Fig. 1, which directly shows the development trend of research on ethical issues in mental disorders. The earliest research on the ethics of mental disorders began in 1970. “The perils of model swapping: a view from medical suburbia” by Johnson [15], critically examines the challenges and implications of adopting and applying new theoretical or clinical models in suburban medical practices. This article explores how frequent shifts in preferred frameworks, especially in the context of psychology and psychiatry, can disrupt patient care, confuse practitioners, and potentially lead to suboptimal outcomes.

Although relevant research began sporadically in the 1970s, researchers generally did not pay attention to this field at the time. It was not until 1994 that the number of studies on the ethical issues of mental disorders reached 10 articles per year. This field slowly attracted the attention of researchers, and the number of published papers has gradually increased, showing a trend of fluctuating growth. From the perspective of academic influence, there were 19,037 citations of the 1,271 included papers on ethical issues of mental disorders, and the distribution curve of citations generally had an inverted “U” shape (2013 was the year with the highest number of citations to date [1,152]). Each paper was cited 1.50 times, and the H-index was 60. This indicates that some high-quality literature has been cited more frequently, and although the number of citations per article is low, the academic influence of the research group remains significant.

2) Author distribution characteristics

Since the study types are still increasing, as can be seen from the density visualization of coauthors (Fig. 2), which includes authors from three or more publications, the default minimum cluster size is five. A total of 125 authors were included, showing nine clusters, while 57 items were not clustered; the TLS was 325, indicating that the clustering effect of the published authors was still low. Of all the researchers, only 50 published four or more articles and only 28 published five or more articles. Laura Weiss Roberts (Stanford University, United States) published the most articles, with 11 similar articles and 207 citations. The top 10 authors published a total of 82 papers, accounting for 7.0% of the total. In addition, the visualization of the density of coauthors shows that researchers in this field are relatively scattered, and the core degree is low, indicating that the core author group has not been formed in this research field, and there is still a lot of research space.

3) Study of the distribution characteristics of countries and regions

In this part of the analysis, countries or regions with three or more publications were included, with a minimum cluster size of five by default (Fig. 3). A total of 43 countries or regions were included, forming five clusters with a TLS of 600. Content analysis identified 21 countries with more than 10 published articles and 10 countries with more than 50 published articles, including the United States (436 articles), the United Kingdom (178), Canada (113), Australia (91), Germany (89), France (73), Sweden (67), the Netherlands (60), Switzerland (54), and Norway (51). We found that most of the research literature was concentrated in a few countries in North America and Europe, among which the number of published papers in the United States alone accounted for 34.30% of the total, and the top three countries (i.e., the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada) accounted for 57.20% of the total number of published papers. This indicates that these countries have a leading academic influence on ethical research in mental disorders. Although this phenomenon is due to the popularity of English (scholars in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, and other countries have a language advantage when publishing English journal articles), it may also be related to advances in scientific health research and the ethical rule of law in European and American countries. Countries in Europe and North America, which dominate the literature, benefit from long-established traditions of bioethics legislation, robust research governance, and strong institutional infrastructure that facilitate scholarship in this area. Such conditions not only provide a favorable environment for publishing but also influence the ethical priorities emphasized in mental health research [16,17].

2. Thematic analysis of research on the ethical issues of mental disorders

1) Keyword co-occurrence network

Using VOSviewer, 3,118 keywords from 1,271 papers were extracted and keywords with a frequency of at least five times were included. To improve the clarity of the clustering and highlight the main research topics, the minimum cluster size was set to five. A total of 180 keywords were included, forming six clusters with a TLS of 3,439. This indicates that the research field has a certain breadth, covering more concepts and research directions, and there is a high co-occurrence relationship between keywords, indicating that the topics in this research field are closely related.

(1) Keyword frequency analysis

Content analysis showed that 73 keywords appeared at least 10 times, 36 keywords appeared at least 15 times, and 10 keywords appeared at least 50 times, indicating that research topics on the ethical issues of mental disorders formed the core topic community of this research. The top 10 keywords were ethics (598 times), psychiatry (154), mental health (92), informed consent (82), autonomy (74), research ethics (72), coercion (54), medical ethics (53), mental illness (53), and decision-making (50). The top 10 Betweenness Centrality keywords were ethics (3,830.2), psychiatry (1,535.44), mental health (883.5), informed consent (491.1), mental illness (481.2), decision-making (326.7), autonomy (315.3), neuroethics (288.4), schizophrenia (259.0), and research ethics (227.0). The two categories of keywords “ethics” in the research topic (including ethics, research ethics, and medical ethics) and mental disorders (including psychiatry, mental health, and mental illness) led the way; simultaneously, there was a high degree of overlap between high-frequency words and keywords with high Betweenness Centrality. On one hand, this shows that there is a high degree of thematic focus in research on the ethics of mental disorders. On the other hand, most existing research on ethical issues related to mental disorders is relatively concentrated.

(2) Average year of keyword co-occurrence

From the average year of keyword co-occurrence, the 10 most recent keywords were AI (May 2022; year of first and last occurrence 2020–2024), machine learning (2022; year of first and last occurrence 2021–2023), medical aid in dying (2022; year of first and last occurrence 2015–2024), medical assistance in dying (August 2021; year of first and last occurrence 2018–2024), equity (June 2021; year of first and last occurrence 2013–2024), pandemic (May 2021; year of first and last occurrence 2020–2023), coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) (April 2021; year of first and last occurrence 2020–2023), epistemic injustice (February 2021; year of first and last occurrence 2015–2024), serious mental illness (March 2020; year of first and last occurrence 2016–2023), and psychiatry and law (2020; year of first and last occurrence 2008–2024). It was found that some hotspot studies had obvious timeliness correlations, such as AI (May 2022), pandemic (May 2021), and COVID-19 (April 2021), which are closely related to scientific and technological development and global public health events in recent years. The research popularity of these keywords highly coincided with their technical applications and the period of the event, indicating that the research field keenly responds to external drivers. Meanwhile, other keywords such as medical aid in dying (2022), medical assistance in dying (August 2021), and epistemic injustice (February 2021) reflect the continuous attention of the ethical field to patient rights, social equity, and normative issues. These topics are closely related to the core issues of ethics, and although hotspots fluctuate, they have remained important for a long time. In addition, the emergence of serious mental illness (March 2020) and psychiatry and law (2020) reveals the deep integration of mental disorder ethics with law and clinical practice, especially the protection of the rights and interests of patients with serious mental disorders and legal norms. Multidisciplinary collaboration and in-depth discussions are required.

In summary, hot topics in the study of the ethics of mental disorders not only reflect the dynamic response to technological development and social change but also maintain the continuous exploration of traditional ethical issues. This trend indicates that future research needs to pay more attention to the ethical challenges of technology applications while finding a balance in a rapidly changing social environment and promoting the harmonious development of theory and practice.

2) Content analysis based on keyword clustering

According to the clustering results (Fig. 4) and the content analysis method, the following six core research themes exist in the research on ethical issues of mental disorders (Table 1).

(1) Conceptual basis and policy framework of mental health ethics

This cluster covers the foundational concepts and policy structures of mental health ethics, including keywords such as ethics, privacy, consent, and law. It focuses on two core issues: (1) basic principles of ethics and protection of the rights and interests of patients with mental disorders and (2) synergy between policies, regulations, and technological development.

(2) Ethical challenges in psychiatric healthcare practice

This cluster addresses ethical issues in psychiatric healthcare practices using keywords such as autonomy, intervention, and care ethics. It includes two main concerns: (1) ethical tension between patient autonomy and clinical intervention and (2) operational challenges of ethical principles in mental healthcare.

(3) Research ethics of mental disorders

This cluster revolves around ethical issues in research on mental disorders, focusing on informed consent, capacity assessment, and the protection of vulnerable populations. Its two core themes are (1) the subject’s informed consent and decision-making ability and (2) protection of research ethics for vulnerable groups.

(4) Autonomy and rights of patients with mental disorders in healthcare

This cluster emphasizes the autonomy and rights of patients with mental disorders during healthcare processes and features keywords such as autonomy, justice, and vulnerability. It centers on (1) autonomy and informed decision-making in patients with mental disorders and (2) the rights and interests of vulnerable groups among people with mental disorders.

(5) Hospice care for patients with mental disorders and medical ethical dilemmas

This cluster focuses on end-of-life care for people with mental disorders and related ethical dilemmas using keywords such as euthanasia, suicide, and end-of-life rights. It covers (1) hospice care and euthanasia for people with mental disorders and (2) the vulnerability and legal ethics of patients with mental disorders.

(6) Neuroethics and the ethics of biomedical technology

This cluster highlights the ethical issues related to neurotechnology and biomedical interventions in mental healthcare using keywords such as neuroethics, identity, and responsibility. Its core concerns are (1) emerging neurobiotechnology and the ethical challenges of identity and (2) responsibility and ethical agency in biomedical technology.

Knowledge base of ethical research on mental disorders

All 1,271 papers were subjected to citation analysis, and literature cited more than 10 times was included, with a total of 535 manuscripts (Fig. 5). Among them, only 81 articles were cited more than 50 times and only 12 articles were cited more than 100 times. To determine the knowledge base in this area of research, we analyzed the three most frequently cited papers and the three with the highest TLS.

1. Highly cited literature

Cosgrove et al. [18] investigated the financial relationships between members of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) task force, and the pharmaceutical industry. Furthermore, it explores the potential implications of these ties in the field of psychiatry, particularly concerning issues of bias, transparency, and integrity of the diagnostic criteria. The study found that 56% of the members had such ties, with particularly prevalent connections in panels addressing disorders treated primarily through medication. This raises significant ethical concerns, including the risk of biased diagnostic criteria that may favor pharmacological interventions over psychosocial or holistic approaches. Such biases can contribute to over-medicalization, reinforce stigmatization, and create barriers to equitable patient-centered care. These financial ties directly challenge fundamental principles such as beneficence, justice, and patient autonomy. Beneficence is compromised when decisions are influenced by financial interests rather than the best outcomes for patients. Justice is compromised when a diagnostic framework prioritizes medication and reflects industry influence, limiting access to diverse treatments. Moreover, the perceived erosion of objectivity in DSM-IV undermines public trust in psychiatric practices and research. Addressing these challenges requires comprehensive conflict of interest policies, greater transparency in the development of diagnostic standards, and the inclusion of diverse perspectives, particularly those of patients and independent researchers. Ethical governance in psychiatry must prioritize unbiased, equitable, and evidence-based approaches to ensure that the field maintains its integrity and continues to advance in ways that holistically serve patients and society.

Palmer et al. [19] introduce a theoretical framework to understand how a collaborative approach among patients, caregivers, and healthcare professionals can drive meaningful improvements in healthcare delivery. The model emphasizes shared decision-making, mutual respect, and integration of patient experiences into the service design and improvement process. The principles outlined in this article are particularly relevant from the perspective of mental health ethics. Coproduction and codesign are closely linked to ethical requirements for respecting patient autonomy, justice, and charity. For people with mental illnesses, who are often marginalized or have a diminished voice in healthcare decisions, a participatory approach offers pathways to empowerment and more equitable care. These approaches challenge paternalistic models and promote an ethical framework in which patients are treated as partners rather than passive care recipients. The participatory approach also addresses key issues in mental health ethics such as the stigmatization of mental illness and the historical neglect of patients’ perspectives in treatment planning. By prioritizing the lived experiences of people with mental disorders, collaboration promotes dignity, reduces perceived injustice, and aligns healthcare practices with ethical principles that respect the unique needs and identities of patients. This model calls on mental health systems to adopt participatory approaches that not only improve the quality of services but also maintain the ethical integrity of care delivery.

Hariz et al. [20] trace the largely overlooked history of deep brain stimulation (DBS) from 1947 to 1987, long before its modern resurgence in movement disorders. The article shows how pioneering neurosurgeons experimented with electrical stimulation of deep brain structures as an alternative to ablative psychosurgery, documenting both technical progress and clinical experiences. These early applications included attempts to relieve pain, psychiatric symptoms, and motor disorders, though results were inconsistent and often poorly disseminated. By reconstructing this forgotten chapter, the authors emphasize that DBS was not a sudden innovation of the 1990s, but rather the product of decades of incremental exploration and trial-and-error. This historical perspective highlights how scientific curiosity, surgical ingenuity, and clinical need interacted in shaping the foundations of modern DBS, while also illustrating why its early promise remained dormant for years until technological advances allowed safer and more reproducible outcomes.

2. High link strength literature

Bell et al. [21] noted that although DBS has shown significant clinical benefits in treating neurological disorders such as Parkinson disease, epilepsy, and obsessive-compulsive disorder, its potential impact on patient identity and personality change has aroused widespread concern in the ethics community. The article provides a detailed analysis of the emotional, behavioral, and decision-making changes that patients may experience after receiving DBS treatment, highlighting the ethical issues that such techniques may raise about “personality remodeling” or an “identity crisis.” In addition, the authors raised questions about the long-term safety and efficacy of DBS, arguing that the existing research is still insufficient to fully assess its potential risks, especially in the exploration of nontherapeutic applications in areas such as enhanced cognitive abilities or mood regulation, which may present greater ethical dilemmas. The article also discusses the complexity of informed consent, especially for patients with severe neurological or psychiatric disorders, to ensure that the risks, benefits, and possible long-term effects of treatment are fully understood. At the societal level, the authors noted that the high cost and resource allocation of DBS technology may exacerbate healthcare equity challenges, calling for attention to its accessibility among vulnerable populations. Finally, the authors proposed a dynamic ethical framework regulating the future development of DBS through interdisciplinary collaboration that integrates the forces of neuroscience, ethics, sociology, and policymaking. This framework aims to ensure the ethical acceptability of technologies for therapeutic and potential enhancement uses while promoting society’s sense of trust and responsibility for emerging neurotechnologies. Through a systematic ethical review, this study provides theoretical support for further research and applications of DBS technology and lays a foundation for the standardized and humanized development of neural regulation technology in the future.

Klein et al. [22] analyze the broad application prospects of BCIs in restoring neurological function, enhancing cognitive ability, and helping people who are disabled, and they emphasize their far-reaching significance in improving medical treatment and quality of life. However, the authors also noted that the development of BCIs involves complex ethical issues, including potential implications for personal identity and autonomy. By interacting directly with the brain, BCIs may alter behavioral choices and personality traits, triggering the ethical controversy of “technology-self integration.” In addition, this article focuses on data privacy and security, particularly how to avoid commercial exploitation or social surveillance when processing neural data. Informed consent in medical practice is also discussed, especially for patients with cognitive impairments, where it is challenging to ensure that they fully understand technological risks. The authors also focus on the equity of technology, noting that an uneven distribution of resources can exacerbate global health inequalities. In response to the enhanced use of BCIs, this paper explored the possible ethical risks to society, such as military applications, unfair competitive advantages, and social exclusion. The authors propose promoting interdisciplinary collaboration, policy development, and public engagement by establishing a dynamic ethical framework to ensure that BCIs develop ethically for the benefit of all. This manuscript provides important guidance for the sustainable and responsible development of BCI technologies.

O’Brien and Golding [23] illustrate the tension between the autonomy and welfare of patients inevitably involved in coercive measures (such as involuntary hospitalization or medications) in the care of patients with mental illness. The authors argued that, while coercive interventions may be indispensable in some circumstances, ethical principles of reducing coercive interventions should always be followed, prioritizing informed consent and voluntary participation by patients. This article further analyzed the different types of compulsory care measures, including the specific applications of law, systems, and medical practice, and indicated the negative impact they may have on patient mental health and trust relationships. The authors emphasized that coercive measures should be used as a last resort only when other noncoercive interventions have failed and there is a significant risk to the patient or society. The authors suggested that the need for mandatory care can be minimized by fostering patient engagement, providing clear communication, and creating a respectful and supportive care environment. In addition, the authors highlighted the ethical sensitivity required by professionals when implementing coercive measures, especially transparency, and the sense of responsibility when weighing patient rights against public safety. By proposing an integrated strategy of theory and practice, this study provides important ethical guidelines for clinical practice and policymaking in the field of mental health and promotes the development of care models that strike a balance between patient dignity and social responsibility.

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to identify ethical issues involved in medical care, nursing, scientific research, and other activities related to mental disorders. To this end, we conducted bibliometrics and cluster-based content analysis to identify developmental patterns of research on this topic and clarify the research basis and key areas of the ethics of mental disorders. This approach helps us understand gaps in the literature and determine future research directions based on these gaps and knowledge structures. In addition, this analysis helps researchers identify important issues related to the ethics of mental disorders in the literature. This study systematically investigated the ethical dimensions of mental disorders and identified key thematic areas within the existing body of research. The findings reveal a multifaceted discourse encompassing core issues, such as autonomy, coercion, informed consent, and stigma, alongside emerging concerns related to technological integration, global disparities in care, and resource allocation. In addition, the findings reveal multidisciplinary and multi-perspective discussions, including autonomy and compulsion in clinical and nursing care, informed consent and decision-making in mental disorder research, and ethical issues regarding the use of emerging technologies in the treatment of mental disorders, considering different perspectives based on the roles of providers, researchers, and patients. These findings set the stage for careful discussion, provide a comprehensive understanding of the ethical complexities inherent in mental healthcare, and lay the foundation for the development of strong ethical frameworks and evidence-based policies.

1. Conceptual basis and policy framework of mental health ethics

The foundational framework of mental health ethics lies in ensuring that the fundamental rights of patients are fully protected, particularly concerning core issues such as human rights, consent, and privacy [24,25]. Ethics and policies provide clear guidelines for clinical practice, emphasizing respect for patient dignity and autonomy while addressing the specific needs and vulnerabilities of individuals with mental disorders. Ethical principles aim to avoid the dilemmas posed by over-intervention and coercive treatment [26,27]. However, academic discussions on ethics and policy often confront complex value conflicts, such as the tension between autonomy and public safety, the trade-off between privacy protection and technological innovation, and the challenge of ensuring fairness and transparency in ethical decision-making during mental health crises [28,29]. As emerging technologies are applied in clinical and research settings, ethical concepts and policy frameworks must evolve accordingly [30]. Traditional ethical frameworks face new challenges and require ongoing adjustments to effectively address these ethical dilemmas.

To this end, mental health ethics must keep pace with technological advancements and refine conceptual components and policy frameworks while strengthening the ethical oversight of technology applications. This ensures that the fundamental human rights and autonomy of patients are protected, thereby minimizing potential harm. Policymakers, ethicists, clinicians, and technology developers should collaborate closely to establish interdisciplinary ethical decision-making mechanisms to address the potential dilemmas arising in the context of new technologies. Furthermore, ethical decision-making during mental health crises should prioritize transparency and fairness, ensuring that patients fully understand and participate in treatment decisions, thereby enhancing the clarity and equity of informed consent processes. These measures will help ensure that ethical standards and policies in the mental health field adapt to the evolving landscape of technology and ethics, thereby safeguarding patients’ fundamental rights.

2. Practice ethics in mental disorder healthcare between medical staff and patients

The ethical challenges of mental disorders are complex issues for healthcare professionals in clinical practice and profoundly impact the autonomy and fundamental rights of patients [31,32]. In the practical ethics of mental disorders, the “ethical challenges to psychiatric healthcare practice” and “autonomy and rights of patients with mental disorders in healthcare” represent two sides of the same coin, addressing the ethical challenges faced by healthcare providers and patients, respectively [33,34]. In psychiatric clinical care, ethical issues often involve multilayered value conflicts, such as tensions between law and morality, individual and collective interests, and rights and responsibilities. Balancing these diverse dimensions is critical for developing ethics. From the patient’s perspective, autonomy and protection of rights are central ethical concerns [35]. In treatment decision-making, particularly when a patient’s decision-making capacity is impaired, respecting the patient’s wishes while avoiding over-intervention remains an urgent ethical challenge. Throughout this process, the patient’s needs and emotions should receive full attention, and the patient’s voice should be considered an essential element in diagnosis and treatment [36]. Societal environment also plays a significant role in the experiences of patients with mental disorders. Stigma, discrimination, and social exclusion often exacerbate psychological burdens and hinder treatment outcomes [19]. Therefore, fostering a more equitable and inclusive treatment environment and avoiding bias and rejection are not only key to safeguarding the basic rights of patients but also integral to ethical practice.

To this end, first, it is essential to integrate ethical theories with practical clinical experience to develop an ethical framework that is better suited to psychiatric practice and that provides clear guidance for healthcare professionals in complex situations. Second, institutional training and education should be strengthened, along with the formulation of relevant laws and regulations to reduce ambiguities in ethical conflicts and offer robust policy support for practice. Moreover, integrating the perspectives of patients and healthcare providers can enhance the ethicality of services while focusing on patients’ subjective experiences and promoting a patient-centered mental health service model that ensures that the wishes of patients are fully respected in treatment decisions. Strengthening ethical education, refining institutional frameworks, and enhancing patient independence and dignity during treatment are crucial. Only through multidimensional ethical exploration and practical innovation can we provide fairer, more respectful, and efficient healthcare services to individuals with mental disorders, thereby ensuring autonomy, dignity, and social equality throughout the treatment process.

3. Research the ethics of mental disorders

Research on individuals with mental disorders presents unique ethical and practical challenges, primarily stemming from their vulnerability, limited decision-making capacity, and potential exploitation [37,38]. Such patients often experience cognitive, emotional, and social impairments that may hinder their ability to fully understand or assess the risks and benefits of participation, thereby affecting the validity and voluntary nature of informed consent [39,40]. Therefore, researchers must pay special attention to evaluating participants’ decision-making capacities to ensure that their participation is based on a comprehensive understanding and voluntary choices [41]. Additionally, patients with mental disorders often face societal stigma and discrimination, which diminishes their quality of life and exacerbates their vulnerability in research contexts [42,43]. Researchers should implement specific measures to guarantee that these patients are treated with equality and dignity during scientific investigations and prevent unfair treatment or discrimination.

To address these challenges, an ethical research framework must be tailored to the characteristics of the mental health field. This includes enhancing the evaluation of decision-making capacities to ensure the authenticity and validity of informed consent, establishing explicit ethical policies that protect the rights of patients with mental disorders in clinical trials, providing additional safeguards to avoid ethical oversights caused by their conditions, and strengthening ethical training for researchers to improve their sensitivity and ability to address the unique needs of these patients. By implementing these measures, the participation of individuals with mental disorders could contribute to scientific progress while ensuring that their fundamental rights and dignity are respected and safeguarded throughout the research process.

4. Hospice care for patients with mental disorders and medical ethical dilemmas

End-of-life care for patients with mental disorders and its associated medical ethical dilemmas represent a multidisciplinary intersection focusing on balancing conflicts among patient autonomy, medical ethics principles, and legal frameworks [44]. Scholars hold divergent views on the ethics debate over euthanasia and assisted suicide. Some argue that respecting a patient’s decision to die in cases of extreme suffering or incurable conditions is morally justified. Others, however, emphasize the “principle of nonmaleficence” in medical ethics, advocating for prolonging life and alleviating suffering as core responsibilities [44,45]. Striking a balance between respect for patient autonomy and adherence to medical ethics remains a central challenge in this discourse. Additionally, the vulnerability and decision-making capacity of patients with mental disorders, particularly during end-of-life decisions, raise significant ethical concerns regarding accurately assessing and ensuring the authenticity and voluntary nature of their choices [46]. The legality of euthanasia, which is permitted in only a limited number of countries, further complicates the ethical and legal discussions surrounding end-of-life care for such patients on a global scale. National and regional legal frameworks differ in their recognition of euthanasia, which is influenced by cultural, religious, ethical, and social values [47,48]. Healthcare institutions must strictly adhere to local laws to ensure that decisions involving euthanasia or assisted suicide are made within legal and ethical boundaries. In regions where euthanasia is prohibited, medical professionals should still prioritize the principles of “respect for patient autonomy” and “nonmaleficence” in providing care. The focus should be on effective pain management and emotional support to alleviate suffering while ensuring that the patient’s dignity is upheld throughout the end-of-life process [49].

Future research should focus on fostering cross-cultural and cross-legal ethical dialogue on a global scale. This effort aims to establish appropriate frameworks for formulating ethical policies on end-of-life care for patients with mental disorders, ensuring that patients across diverse legal and cultural contexts experience a dignified dying process. Furthermore, interdisciplinary collaborations that integrate medicine, psychology, ethics, and law should be actively promoted to develop ethically sound end-of-life care strategies for individuals with mental disorders. Such collaboration will ensure the maximal protection of patient dignity, rights, and well-being.

5. Neuroethics and ethics of biomedical technology

Owing to their novelty and specificity, emerging neuroscientific and biomedical technologies have introduced new ethical issues into the research and treatment of mental disorders. Based on an analysis of highly cited and highly linked literature, three out of the six most widely discussed papers focus on the application of emerging neurotechnologies [20–22]. This indicates that emerging neurotechnologies (such as DBS and brain-machine interfaces) are becoming an important research direction in the field of mental disorder ethics, and related ethical concerns are increasingly drawing the attention of the academic community. For instance, DBS alleviates disease symptoms through neural modulation, thus demonstrating significant therapeutic potential. However, it may inadvertently alter a patient’s personality, willpower, and self-perception, thus affecting their autonomy and identity, and raising serious ethical concerns [50]. Whether DBS violates the patient’s “true self” or conflicts with the ethical principle of respecting patient autonomy remains a topic of ongoing discussion in the academic field [51]. Moreover, with the rapid development of neurotechnology, the boundaries between therapeutic efficacy and ethical responsibility have become increasingly blurred. In the treatment of complex disorders such as mental illnesses, ensuring that the technology’s effectiveness does not conflict with the psychological well-being and ethical rights of the patient while preventing abuse and improper intervention has become a critical issue in neuroethics [52].

Considering the profound impact of technologies such as DBS on the psychological well-being and identity of patients, it is imperative to establish dedicated policies to protect patient rights and implement stringent ethical review mechanisms. These mechanisms should evaluate the effects of such technologies on the mental health, social identity, and autonomy of patients to ensure that the applications of these technologies remain within ethical boundaries. Moreover, a dual regulatory framework that integrates scientific and ethical oversight is essential for guaranteeing the safety and ethical compliance of neurotechnology. During treatment, thorough informed consent processes must be conducted to ensure that patients fully understand the potential risks and benefits, while respecting their autonomous decision-making. This is particularly critical in the context of DBS, where patients need to comprehend the potential effects on self-perception and behavior, and make decisions with clear and rational judgments. Future research and education should emphasize interdisciplinary collaboration and foster ethically attuned medical practitioners and researchers. This approach aims to address ethical challenges proactively while responsibly advancing neurotechnological innovations.

Conclusion

Using bibliometric and content analysis methods, this study systematically identified and analyzed the main problems and development trends in ethical research on mental disorders. The findings reveal the multidimensional nature of the ethics of mental disorders, covering core ethical issues, such as patient autonomy, informed consent, privacy protection, and decision-making ability, as well as emerging ethical challenges in the field of mental health, such as ethical dilemmas in the use of technology, euthanasia, and assisted suicide. Overall, the study of the ethics of mental disorders presents an interdisciplinary and multi-angle discussion covering the ethical responsibilities and rights of medical staff, patients, and researchers, especially ethical decision-making in clinical treatment and research practice. With the continuous development of neurotechnology in future research and practice, ethical issues related to the treatment of mental disorders will become more complex. Therefore, there is an urgent need to improve the relevant ethical frameworks and promote interdisciplinary cooperation to ensure a balance between technological innovation and ethical responsibility. Informed consent procedures and supervision mechanisms should be strengthened to protect the basic rights and interests of patients and prevent abuse of technology. The key is to maintain the autonomy and dignity of patients and ensure that ethical safeguards are advanced in tandem with technological advances.

Although this study provides important insights into the ethics of mental disorders, it has several limitations. First, as members of the research team primarily had backgrounds in health management, bioethics, and psychology, their perspectives may not have fully captured the complexity of ethical issues. Future studies will benefit from more interdisciplinary collaboration, including expertise in law and brain science, to provide a broader and more nuanced understanding. Second, the bibliometric and content analyses relied on published literature and may not have fully covered non-English or gray literature, which limits the comprehensiveness of the findings. Moreover, as the literature was concentrated in certain regions, the results may not have fully reflected the diversity of ethical perspectives worldwide, particularly in contexts with different cultural values or less-developed legal frameworks. Finally, the diversity of mental disorders and cultural backgrounds means that ethical concerns may vary considerably across settings. However, this study could not explore these differences in depth. Addressing these limitations in future research through expanded data sources, interdisciplinary integration, and a deeper exploration of cultural contexts will help strengthen the international interpretability of findings. Importantly, the thematic clusters and patterns identified in this study can serve as a foundation for guiding subsequent research agendas and informing the development of practical policies and ethical frameworks in mental healthcare, thereby situating the study within the long-term trajectory of academic and clinical advancement.
