The Obesity Paradox in Chronic Kidney Disease: Cardiorespiratory Fitness Is Associated With Mortality Independent of Body Mass Index
Authors: Swathi Balantrapu, Peter Kokkinos, Immanuel BH Samuel, Andreas Pittaras, Charles Faselis, Jonathan Myers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 37 million adults in the United States and represents a significant public health challenge with substantial morbidity and mortality burden.1 Traditional cardiovascular risk factors, including obesity, have long been considered primary targets for intervention in CKD patients. However, emerging evidence suggests that the relationship between body weight and mortality outcomes in CKD may be more complex than previously understood, with several studies reporting an “obesity paradox” in which a higher body mass index (BMI) is paradoxically associated with better survival.2

The “obesity paradox” was first identified in hemodialysis populations. Early landmark studies, including the Diaphane collaborative study by Degoulet et al3 and later confirmed by Fleischmann et al,4 reported a lower mortality risk among hemodialysis patients with higher body weight. Since these initial observations, the paradox has been documented in other chronic disease states, including heart failure,5 coronary artery disease,6 hypertension,7 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,8 and type 2 diabetes mellitus,9 as well as in general older adult populations.10

Although the mechanisms underlying the obesity paradox remain debated, proposed explanations include reverse causation, confounding by illness severity, metabolic reserve theory, and the potential role of muscle mass in higher BMI individuals. Importantly, recent work has highlighted that sources of bias inherent in observational study designs—including depletion of susceptibles, collider bias, and misalignment of exposure onset with follow-up initiation—may partially or fully account for the obesity paradox.11 Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), typically measured as peak oxygen consumption or metabolic equivalents (METs) achieved during exercise testing, has emerged as a powerful predictor of mortality outcomes across diverse populations.12, 13, 14 Previous work by Kokkinos et al15 in Veterans Affairs (VA) cohorts and others has consistently demonstrated that CRF provides prognostic information that often supersedes traditional risk factors, including BMI, in predicting cardiovascular and all-cause mortality.16,17

Previous research in the VA and other cohorts has shown that fitness levels can modify the association between obesity and mortality, with higher CRF being associated with a lower mortality risk even in the presence of elevated BMI.18,19 The biological mechanisms underlying fitness benefits include improved cardiovascular function, enhanced insulin sensitivity,20 reduced inflammation, and better endothelial function—mechanisms particularly important given the high cardiovascular disease (CVD) burden and systemic inflammation characteristic of this population.

Supporting this interpretation, a recent meta-analysis reported that higher baseline CRF in middle-aged Finnish men was associated with a significantly lower long-term risk of developing CKD.21 The current study extends work from prior veteran cohorts19 by leveraging a considerably larger CKD sample and examining whether the obesity paradox is present and whether CRF modifies the association between BMI and mortality in patients with CKD. In addition, our study addresses critical gaps in understanding by examining the association between BMI categories, age-adjusted CRF, and mortality risk in a large, well-characterized CKD cohort. We used age-stratified fitness categories following the methodology of Kokkinos et al18 and survival analysis techniques to provide clinically relevant insights into the relationships between body composition, fitness, and mortality in patients with CKD.

Methods

Ethics Approval and Informed Consent

This study was approved by the Veterans Affairs Institutional Review Board at the Washington, DC, and Palo Alto VA medical centers. The requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of deidentified data.

Study Population and Design

This retrospective cohort study analyzed data from 13,521 patients with CKD who had complete BMI, CRF, and mortality outcome data. These patients were drawn from the Exercise Testing and Health Outcomes Study, a large observational cohort within the VA health care system comprising 750,302 veterans who underwent maximal exercise tolerance testing (ETT) for clinically indicated reasons (eg, evaluation of suspected ischemia, functional capacity assessment, or preoperative evaluation) at VA medical centers across the entire United States between October 1, 1999, and September 3, 2020.22

CKD was identified based on clinical diagnosis (International Classification of Diseases codes) in the VA electronic health records. The CKD cohort was followed between 2001 and 2021, with mortality follow-up through November 2021. From the initial Exercise Testing and Health Outcomes Study cohort of 750,302 veterans, we identified those meeting CKD criteria and applied the following inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig S1).

The index date was defined as the date of each participant’s first maximal ETT. Eligible participants completed a maximal ETT using the Bruce protocol, were 30-95 years of age at the time of testing, achieved an exercise capacity of at least 2.0 METs, had a BMI of ≥18.5 kg/m2, and had a minimum follow-up period of 6 months.

Patients with BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 (underweight) were excluded because underweight status is strongly associated with frailty, malnutrition, and protein-energy wasting, which independently carry a high mortality risk and could confound the analysis of the obesity paradox.

The 6-month minimum follow-up period was employed to reduce the influence of reverse causality, in which undiagnosed terminal illness may lead to both low weight/fitness and early death; a sensitivity analysis including patients who died within 6 months yielded consistent results (Table S1).

To reduce the likelihood of including patients with undetected CVD, we excluded individuals who experienced coronary artery bypass grafting, percutaneous coronary intervention, myocardial infarction, or a new diagnosis of heart failure5 within 6 months of the index ETT. We acknowledge that this exclusion based on postbaseline events may introduce bias; accordingly, we performed a sensitivity analysis including these patients and adjusting for baseline CVD, which yielded similar results.

Variable Definitions and Measurements

All-Cause Mortality

All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause during the follow-up period. Mortality data were obtained through the VA Beneficiary Identification Records Locator Subsystem, which has been shown to be 95% complete and accurate, capturing deaths occurring both within and outside the VA health care system.22

BMI

BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2) measured at the time of the exercise test. BMI was categorized into the following 3 groups: (1) normal BMI (<25.0 kg/m2), (2) overweight (25.0-29.9 kg/m2), and (3) obese (≥30.0 kg/m2). This classification was chosen to increase statistical power while maintaining clinically meaningful distinctions. We did not specifically identify patients with amputations, whose BMI may underestimate adiposity.

CRF Assessment

CRF was assessed using standardized maximal exercise treadmill testing (Bruce protocol). Exercise capacity was expressed in METs, representing multiples of the resting metabolic rate. Following the methodology of Kokkinos et al,18 patients were categorized into age-adjusted fitness quartiles to account for the natural decline in CRF with aging. The cohort was stratified into 4 age groups (<50, 50-59, 60-69, and ≥70 years). Within each age group, patients were divided into quartiles based on peak METs achieved. The specific MET cutoffs defining each quartile within each age group are reported in Table S2. The respective quartiles from each age stratum were then combined to form the following overall CRF categories: (1) Q1 (least fit), (2) Q2 (low fit), (3) Q3 (moderate fit), and (4) Q4 (most fit). This approach minimizes age as a confounding variable, as evidenced by the balanced mean ages across fitness quartiles (Q1: 62.9 years, Q2: 65.2 years, Q3: 63.1 years, and Q4: 63.0 years).

Follow-up and Data Collection

Patients were followed from the initial exercise test, that is, the date of each participant’s first maximal ETT through November 2021. VA health care records enabled long-term tracking with minimal loss to follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for baseline characteristics, with continuous variables presented as means ± standard deviations and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Differences across groups were assessed using 1-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables, with omnibus P values reported. Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier methods to estimate survival probabilities, with log-rank tests used to compare survival distributions between groups.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for mortality risk. The following 3 sets of models were constructed: (1) unadjusted models; (2) age- and sex-adjusted models; and (3) fully adjusted models including age, sex, race, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and baseline CVD as covariates. Results from the fully adjusted models are reported as the primary analysis. We conducted the following 3 analytical approaches: (1) main effects analysis for BMI categories using normal BMI as the reference group; (2) main effects analysis for age-adjusted fitness quartiles using least fit (Q1) as the reference group; and (3) interaction analysis examining all 12 combinations of BMI categories and fitness quartiles, with normal BMI/least fit patients serving as the reference group. A formal multiplicative interaction term between BMI categories and fitness quartiles was included in the Cox model to test for effect modification. The following 2 prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) including patients who died within 6 months of the index ETT (ie, removing the 6-month minimum follow-up requirement); and (2) retaining patients who experienced postbaseline cardiovascular events (coronary artery bypass grafting, percutaneous coronary intervention, myocardial infarction, or new heart failure diagnosis within 6 months post-ETT) and adjusting for baseline CVD as an additional covariate. No covariate data were missing.

The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals, and model fit was assessed using standard diagnostic procedures. All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.11 with (Python Software Foundation) the lifelines package for survival analysis. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Study Population Characteristics

The final analytical cohort comprised 13,521 CKD patients (13,165 men, mean age 63.4 ± 8.0 years; 356 women, mean age 64.1 ± 7.9 years). The mean follow-up period was 7.4 ± 4.2 years (median 6.9 years), during which 5,144 (38.0%) deaths occurred with an average annual mortality rate of 51.3 events per 1,000 person-years. The predominantly male composition (97.4%) reflects the demographics of the VA health care system rather than the general CKD population.

Main Effects of BMI and Fitness on Mortality

Main effects analysis revealed that both a higher BMI and higher fitness levels were associated with lower all-cause mortality (Fig 1). Compared with patients with a normal BMI, overweight individuals had an 18% lower mortality risk (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.70-0.95, P = 0.007), whereas obese patients had a 29% lower mortality risk (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.61-0.82, P < 0.001).

Comparing age-adjusted CRF categories, a strong dose-response association with mortality was observed (Fig 1). Compared with the least fit patients, those with low fitness showed a nonsignificant 10% lower risk (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.73-1.10, P = 0.305), whereas moderately fit patients demonstrated a 39% lower risk (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.50-0.74, P < 0.001), and the most fit patients had a 51% lower risk (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.40-0.60, P < 0.001). The progressive reduction in mortality risk across fitness categories underscores the strong association between CRF and survival in CKD patients.

Interaction Between BMI and Fitness and Its Association With Mortality

Analysis of all 12 BMI-fitness combinations revealed that the obesity paradox was observed across all fitness levels, with normal BMI patients consistently demonstrating the highest mortality risk within each fitness category (Table 3 and Fig 2). A formal test for the multiplicative interaction between BMI categories and fitness quartiles was not statistically significant (P for interaction = 0.41), indicating that the inverse association between higher fitness and lower mortality was consistent across BMI categories. Using normal BMI/least fit patients as the reference group (55.3% mortality), all other combinations were associated with a lower mortality risk.

The combination of obese BMI and high fitness was associated with the lowest mortality risk, with a 64% lower mortality risk compared with the reference group.

Sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with the primary analysis. When patients who died within 6 months were included, and when patients with postbaseline cardiovascular events were retained with adjustment for baseline CVD, the fully adjusted HRs for all 12 BMI-fitness combinations remained similar in magnitude and direction (Table S2).

Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Fig 3A and B) visually demonstrate these relationships, showing clear separation between fitness categories within each BMI group and consistent survival advantages for higher BMI categories across all fitness levels. Discussion

Our findings suggest that an obesity paradox is observed in patients with CKD. The observation that normal BMI individuals had the highest mortality risk while obese patients had lower mortality is consistent with the obesity paradox reported in other chronic disease populations. Normal BMI patients demonstrated a mortality rate of 44.7%, which was significantly higher than overweight (36.9%) and obese (37.4%) patients.

This paradox in CKD may be explained by several mechanisms. First, CKD patients often experience protein-energy wasting and sarcopenia, conditions associated with poor outcomes.23,24 A higher BMI may serve as a buffer against these catabolic processes, providing metabolic reserves during periods of illness or stress. Second, the inflammatory milieu of CKD may be better tolerated by patients with higher fat mass, which can produce anti-inflammatory adipokines.25 Third, normal BMI CKD patients may represent a sicker phenotype, having lost weight owing to underlying disease processes, a phenomenon known as reverse causation.26 Notably, we excluded patients with BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 to limit the possibility that cachexia influenced the results.

However, it is important to acknowledge that the observed obesity paradox may be partially or entirely driven by biases inherent in observational study designs. As described by Fu et al,11 depletion of susceptible bias can occur when the start of follow-up (in our case, the date of exercise testing in a CKD population) is not aligned with the onset of the exposure (the development of obesity). Because obesity increases the risk of mortality, individuals with obesity who survive to the point of CKD diagnosis and exercise testing may have fewer other risk factors for death than individuals with a normal BMI, for whom less selection has taken place. Collider bias from conditioning on CKD status may further distort the BMI-mortality association. These methodological considerations limit the degree to which our findings can be interpreted as evidence that obesity is truly protective in CKD.

Perhaps more important than the obesity paradox itself is the association of CRF with mortality outcomes. Our data demonstrate that fitness was associated with a substantially lower mortality risk that was independent of BMI. The most fit participants exhibited a 51% lower risk of mortality compared with the least fit group, underscoring the strong inverse association between fitness and mortality in this population.27

The mean CRF in our cohort (7.7 ± 2.6 METs) was generally lower than values reported in general population cohorts, which is expected given the older age, higher comorbidity burden, and CKD status of our participants. However, these values are consistent with those reported in other CKD and heart failure cohorts, suggesting that the fitness levels observed in this study are representative of this clinical population.

Our findings are consistent with prior observations of the obesity paradox across several chronic conditions, including heart failure,5 coronary artery disease,6 hypertension,7 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,8 diabetes,9 and older adult populations.10 In patients with CKD, the paradox was first described by Degoulet et al3 in the Diaphane collaborative study of dialysis patients and later confirmed by Fleischmann et al4 in a larger hemodialysis cohort. Kalantar-Zadeh et al2 subsequently advanced this concept, reporting survival advantages of a higher BMI in dialysis patients, whereas Horwich et al28 and Curtis et al29 demonstrated similar findings in heart failure populations. Kovesdy et al30 later extended these observations to men with nondialysis CKD. Collectively, these studies established a consistent association between a higher BMI and lower mortality across the CKD spectrum and beyond, providing the foundation upon which our current findings extend.

The magnitude of the paradox observed in our study—normal BMI patients experiencing 21% higher mortality than overweight patients and 19% higher mortality than obese patients—is comparable with the effect size reported in heart failure populations by Fonarow et al.31 Importantly, unlike earlier work, our analysis incorporated detailed measures of age-adjusted fitness, demonstrating for the first time to our knowledge that fitness is strongly associated with mortality and substantially modifies the association between BMI and mortality in patients with CKD.

Our findings should be considered in the context of established clinical guidelines for CKD management. The Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative 2020 Clinical Practice Guidelines for Nutrition in CKD recommend an energy intake of 25-35 kcal/kg/d and protein restriction to 0.55-0.6 g/kg/d for metabolically stable adults with nondiabetic CKD stages 3-5 to slow disease progression.32 The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2024 guidelines recommend that CKD patients engage in at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity (eg, walking, cycling, and swimming) combined with strength training 2-3 times per week.33 Rather than challenging these established guidelines, our findings reinforce the importance of physical activity recommendations and suggest that fitness assessment should be integrated into routine CKD care alongside nutritional management.

The dose-response association we observed—with mortality risk decreasing progressively from the least fit (reference) to low fit (HR 0.90), moderately fit (HR 0.61), and most fit (HR 0.49) quartiles—suggests that even modest differences in fitness may be associated with meaningful differences in mortality risk.

Our findings suggest that risk stratification in CKD may benefit from incorporating fitness assessment alongside weight management. However, these observational data cannot determine whether interventions to improve fitness will causally reduce mortality, and randomized controlled trials are needed to address this question.

Finally, our results indicate that CRF demonstrated a stronger association with survival than BMI, and importantly, normal BMI patients with poor fitness demonstrated the highest mortality (55.3%), highlighting a vulnerable subgroup that may be overlooked by BMI-based assessments.

This study has several strengths, including a large sample size (13,521 CKD patients), comprehensive mortality follow-up through VA tracking systems (mean 7.4 years, 5,144 deaths), standardized fitness assessment using maximal exercise testing, and rigorous statistical methods including age-adjusted fitness categorization.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study population consisted predominantly of male veterans (97.4%), which limits the generalizability of these findings to women, nonveterans, and the general CKD population who do not undergo clinically indicated exercise testing. Second, we used BMI as the sole measure of adiposity, which does not distinguish between fat mass and lean mass. We did not specifically identify patients with amputations, whose BMI may underestimate adiposity. Future studies incorporating body composition measures such as dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry or bioelectrical impedance analysis may provide additional insights. Third, we did not have data on changes in fitness or BMI over time, and our study relied on single baseline measurements that cannot account for longitudinal trajectories. Fourth, residual confounding by unmeasured variables such as socioeconomic status, medication adherence, or health behaviors cannot be excluded. Fifth, although specific indications for exercise testing were not uniformly coded, the vast majority of tests in the VA system were for evaluation of suspected ischemia or functional capacity assessment; this represents a potential source of selection bias. Sixth, the observed obesity paradox may be influenced by biases inherent in observational designs, including depletion of susceptibles and collider bias, as recently described.11 Finally, the observational design precludes definitive causal inference, and randomized controlled trials of exercise interventions in CKD patients are needed to confirm whether improving fitness causally reduces mortality. In addition, CKD severity markers such as the estimated glomerular filtration rate were not available in our data set for covariate adjustment, which limited our ability to account for confounding based on CKD severity.

In conclusion, our study provides observational evidence for 2 key findings regarding mortality risk in veterans with CKD. First, an obesity paradox was observed, with higher BMI categories being associated with lower mortality across all fitness levels. Second, and more importantly, age-adjusted CRF demonstrated a strong, graded inverse association with mortality independent of BMI, with the most fit patients having a 51% lower mortality risk compared with the least fit patients. Although the observational nature of this study precludes causal conclusions, these findings support the integration of fitness assessment into CKD management and reinforce current guidelines recommending regular physical activity for CKD patients. Future randomized controlled trials are needed to determine whether interventions targeting fitness improvement can reduce mortality in CKD populations.
