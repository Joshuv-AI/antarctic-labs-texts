Can Vaccination Prevent the Development of Chagas Disease Cardiomyopathy?
Authors: Edecio Cunha-Neto, Daniela Santoro Rosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Authors

Chagas disease (CD), caused by the intracellular protozoan parasite Trypanosoma cruzi and affecting approximately 10 million patients, remains a leading cause of nonischemic cardiomyopathy across Latin America, where it is a major indication for heart transplantation.1,2 CD has become a global health concern because of migration and nonvectorial transmission routes (blood transfusion, congenital, oral, organ transplantation), with over 300,000 estimated cases in the United States and Europe.1 Acute infection is often self-limited. The powerful immune response triggered by T. cruzi, including inflammatory cytokines such as interferon-gamma and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, keeps T. cruzi in check, but fails to completely eradicate parasitism. Decades-long low-grade chronic infection leads to the development, in up to ∼30% of infected individuals, of chronic Chagas disease cardiomyopathy (CCC), an inflammatory cardiomyopathy leading to heart failure and arrhythmia.2

CCC patients display a chronic myocarditis driven by T cells recognizing T. cruzi and cross-reactive heart antigens, with abundant local production of interferon-gamma and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, but scarce heart parasitism. Inflammatory cytokine-driven cardiac fibrosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and a particularly intense energy deprivation in the heart have been proposed as the underlying mechanisms of the worse prognosis of CCC as compared with other cardiomyopathies.2,3

The only available anti–T. cruzi drugs (benznidazole, nifurtimox) were introduced in the 1960s and can reduce parasite burden, but show limited efficacy in adults with established chronic infection—the stage where most patients are diagnosed. Thus, a safe and effective vaccine that could induce sterile immunity or significantly reduce parasitism, and the ensuing inflammatory and fibrotic heart damage leading to heart failure and arrythmia, remains an urgent unmet need to prevent or limit CCC.4 However, development of vaccines effective against protozoan pathogens causing chronic infection is still a major challenge, given their complex life cycle in mammalian hosts and multiplicity of immune evasion mechanisms. In addition, CD is a highly neglected disease affecting poor people in developing countries, which makes developing a vaccine or chemotherapy for CD a low priority for the pharmaceutical industry.5

Multiple T. cruzi vaccine candidates have explored a broad antigenic repertoire—including cruzipain, trans-sialidases/Tc24 family proteins, multivalent constructs (eg, Traspain), and diverse delivery platforms (recombinant proteins, viral and bacterial vectors, DNA/RNA vaccines, live attenuated CRISPR-Cas9 strategies) and platform combinations (Bivona et al4 and Teixeira et al5). Several approaches achieved promising immunogenicity and reduced parasitism in animal models, but struggled to reliably clear tissue reservoirs or halt progressive cardiomyopathy; no vaccine candidate has yet advanced to clinical trials. Key barriers have included insufficient magnitude and durability of T-cell responses, limited antigen breadth confronting parasite genetic diversity, the reliability of animal models in mirroring CCC in humans, manufacturing/regulatory complexity, and safety concerns (particularly for replicating vectors). Moreover, only a fraction of T. cruzi vaccine studies in animal models assessed protection against development of ventricular dysfunction in the chronic phase of infection, a key outcome for any translational application.4,5

Lokugamage et al,6 in this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, describe a bicistronic nanoplasmid DNA vaccine (BCV) encoding 2 conserved antigens, TcG2 and TcG4, and an adjuvanted formulation incorporating a retinoic acid-inducible gene I agonist (referred to as BCVR) used as a prophylactic vaccine. The immunogen was designed in compliance with the regulatory guidance for plasmid DNA vaccines provided by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for human use.

To model human CD progression and assess vaccine immunogenicity and efficacy, the authors used C57BL/6 mice, in which infection with the Sylvio strain of T. cruzi produces acute parasitemia (21-40 days) and inflammatory cardiomyopathy with bona fide left ventricular dysfunction by 6 to 9 months; it must be noted that chronic parasitism in this model is more intense than in human CCC. Mice were immunized with BCV or BCVR and challenged at 21 or 100 days after the booster dose to evaluate short- and long-term efficacy, and animals were sacrificed during acute and chronic infection. Both nanoplasmid vaccines were safe and did not elicit significant local or systemic toxicity. Vaccination reduced acute and chronic heart and skeletal muscle parasite burden and attenuated cardiac inflammation. The adjuvanted BCVR vaccine provided slightly better efficacy than BCV in controlling parasites and associated inflammatory pathology.

Mechanistically, protection from acute infection was associated with differentiation of naïve splenic T cells toward a mature, functional effector (Teff) and effector/memory (Tem) CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in immunized mice. CD4+ effectors in BCVR and BCV- immunized mice displayed increased production of cytolytic molecules, whereas CD4+ memory, CD8+ effector/memory subsets exhibited minimal cytolytic/inflammatory output consistent with limiting tissue damage. By contrast, nonvaccinated/infected mice mounted a proinflammatory T-cell response. The adjuvanted BCVR formulation elicited superior anti–T. cruzi immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels before challenge than BCV, and the IgG2a/IgG1 ratio—a profile associated with protection—was higher in vaccinated groups. In addition, immunization with BCV and BCVR induced functional antibodies that inhibited parasite motility/infectivity (BCVR > BCV) that were maintained after chronic infection.

To further extend the findings, the authors evaluated the ability of the nanoplasmid vaccines to control hallmarks of CCC: tissue parasitism, inflammation and fibrosis, and left ventricular dysfunction. Vaccination markedly reduced chronic parasite burden in the heart and skeletal muscle, attenuated inflammatory infiltrate and fibrosis, and preserved left ventricular function with maximal benefits observed in BCVR group. The authors conclude that BCVR outperformed antigen-only BCV in key endpoints, with favorable tolerability.

Crucially, this study provides evidence that T. cruzi DNA immunogen is effective in inducing long-term protective immune responses that are able to control functional outcomes such as chronic tissue parasitism, fibrosis, and ventricular dysfunction. Compared with prior efforts, the BCV/BCVR platform offers an attractive balance of immunologic potency, manufacturability, and safety features that support further translational development.

The direct translatability of prophylactic anti–T. cruzi vaccine studies to human clinical trials is limited by the low incidence of the target disease and the long time required for the symptomatic forms to develop. On the other hand, assessing the effectiveness of therapeutic vaccines/immunotherapy in chronic CD patients is more feasible.5 Previous work by the same group demonstrated that immunotherapy after T. cruzi infection with a mixture of nanoplasmids encoding the same antigens (TcG2 and TcG4) provided near-complete control of tissue parasitism, inflammation, and fibrosis.7 Other studies showed that combining therapeutic vaccines with low-dose chemotherapy in early chronic disease reduces cardiac pathology and ventricular dysfunction,5,8 and multitherapeutic approaches targeting parasite persistence, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction have been proposed.9

Future directions include demonstrating efficacy across parasite genetic diversity in males as well as females, preclinical evaluation in larger animal models, robust safety in GLP studies, and mechanistic biomarker development with correlates of protection; in addition, proof of effectiveness of DNA vaccines against infectious agents in humans is still challenging.10 These steps will determine whether this approach can be advanced into human clinical trials and contribute meaningfully to the long-standing goal of an effective vaccine against Chagas disease. Although we do not have a vaccine against Chagas disease available today, the field is advancing rapidly with new approaches and technologies, with the hope that we will soon have more effective preventive or therapeutic options to combat this devastating disease, whether to prevent infection or to improve the treatment of patients already infected.
