Ouabain exerts protective effects on SH-SY5Y cells upon Zika virus infection
Authors: José Marreiro de Sales-Neto, Daniel Wilson Arruda Magalhães, Deyse Cristina Madruga Carvalho, Poliana Gomes da Silva, Lindomar José Pena, Marcelo Tigre Moura, Sandra Rodrigues-Mascarenhas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZika virus (ZIKV) remains a significant global health threat due to its association with severe neurological disorders, yet no specific treatments are available. Ouabain (OUA) has demonstrated antiviral and immunomodulatory properties, but its mechanisms against ZIKV in neural cells are not fully understood. Here, we investigated the effects of OUA on ZIKV-infected SH-SY5Y cells, a model for undifferentiated neurons, and employed in silico analyses to elucidate its protective mechanisms. OUA exhibited potent antiviral activity, reducing ZIKV titers by up to 99.9% without cytotoxicity (selectivity index > 25). Additionally, OUA decreased the ZIKV-induced production of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α. Mechanistically, OUA suppressed ZIKV-induced NF-κB phosphorylation, providing a basis for its anti-inflammatory effects. Furthermore, OUA treatment restored the phosphorylation mTOR, ERK, and p38, important key neurogenesis-related proteins compromised by ZIKV infection. Moreover, both ZIKV and OUA additively increased SRC phosphorylation, a pathway previously linked to OUA’s antiviral activity. In silico analyses predicted that OUA modulates numerous genes and proteins involved in cellular responses to viral infections. Collectively, our findings suggest a new mode of action for OUA in an undifferentiated neuronal cell model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-026-03133-1.Keywords: Arbovirus, Cardiotonic steroid, Inflammation, Neurogenesis

Introduction

Zika virus (ZIKV), a flavivirus, is associated with severe neurological conditions, including encephalitis, macular atrophy, Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), and microcephaly [1]. This neuropathogenesis arises from ZIKV’s tropism for neural cells, where it replicates, inducing apoptosis and cell-cycle arrest [2], and from deleterious host inflammatory responses [3]. With no specific treatments or vaccines available, ZIKV remains a global health threat [4].

The cardiotonic steroid ouabain (OUA) has demonstrated broad-spectrum antiviral activity against viruses like SARS-CoV-2, Ebola, and ZIKV, as well as immunomodulatory effects [5–8]. Although OUA was recently shown to mitigate ZIKV-induced neuronal impairment in a murine model of congenital Zika syndrome (CZS), its underlying cellular mechanisms are unclear [9]. Therefore, this study investigated the effects of OUA on molecular pathways related to inflammation, cell replication, and antiviral responses in a ZIKV-infected undifferentiated neuron model, using in silico approaches to elucidate its neuroprotective mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Cells and virus

SH-SY5Y cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA) and were grown in DMEM/Ham’s F-12 (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Bionutrientes, São Paulo, SP), 100 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), 1mM sodium pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The Brazilian ZIKV strain ZIKV PE243 was used throughout the study according to previous work [5].

Cell viability assay

The cytotoxicity of OUA (Sigma) was tested by MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay (Sigma). Briefly, SH-SY5Y cells were seeded in 96-well plates to reach 80–90% confluence in 24 h, then treated with increasing OUA concentrations. After 72 h, MTT (1 mg/ml) was added for 4 h. Formazan crystals were solubilized, and the optical density was measured at 540 nm.

Antiviral assay and cytokine analysis

SH-SY5Y cells were infected with ZIKV strain PE243 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. After 1 h, cells were treated with OUA at its 20% cytotoxic concentration (CC₂₀) and serial 1:2 dilutions, as previously described [5]. Culture supernatants were harvested at 72 h post-infection (hpi) for viral titration and inflammatory cytokine quantification by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Viral titration

Vero cells were cultivated in 96 well plates at the density of 1 × 104 cells/well one day prior to titration. Supernatants from antiviral assays were 10-fold serially diluted in DMEM. The diluted supernatant was then added to the cells, which were further incubated for 5 days at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cytopathic effect (CPE) was evaluated on an inverted optical microscope, and the reduction of viral titer was expressed as log10 TCID50/ml.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α levels in the culture supernatant were determined by ELISA, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Boster Biological Technology, Pleasanton, CA). Optical density was read at 450 nm.

Flow cytometry analysis

SH-SY5Y cells (5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded, infected, and treated with OUA CC₂₀ as described above. At 72 hpi, cells were harvested, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min at room temperature, and subsequently permeabilized with ice-cold 90% methanol for 30 min on ice. After permeabilization, cells were stained separately with antibodies targeting the phosphorylated proteins p-NF-κB, p-mTOR, p-ERK, p-p38, p-SRC, and p-AKT (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Data were acquired on a BD FACSCanto™ II (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA).

In silico prediction of OUA mediated effects

OUA interacting partners were predicted using STITCH selecting interactions with OUA SMILES and human (Homo sapiens) proteome [10]. Adverse effects were inferred by ADVERPred [11], and cellular viability was predicted with default parameters of CLC-Pred2.0 [12]. both using the OUA SMILES. The effects of OUA on gene expression were modeled using the DIGEP-Pred (v.2.0) using the OUA SMILES and default parameters [13], the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (https://ctdbase.org), and Connectivity Map using logFC 2.0 and Pa > 0.8 (https://clue.io.). Functional enrichment was done with GO and KEGG databases using g: Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost).

Statistical analysis

Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo® X software version 10.10.0. All data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism® software version 10.1.2 using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons. The results were considered significant when p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

In this work, we investigated OUA’s effects on cell replication, inflammation and antiviral pathways using a ZIKV-infected undifferentiated neuron model (SH-SY5Y cells) and in silico analyses to explore its neuroprotective mechanisms. First, we determined the 20% cytotoxic concentration (CC₂₀) to be 18 nM and the selectivity index (SI) to be 25.8, indicating that OUA’s anti-ZIKV activity is independent of cytotoxicity (Fig. 1A, B, F). These findings are consistent with our previous research on OUA’s activity against ZIKV in Vero cells [5] and human neural stem/progenitor cells (hNS/PCs) [9], which also demonstrated efficacy at nanomolar concentrations and a selectivity index exceeding 10.

OUA significantly reduced ZIKV titers at all tested concentrations (2.25–18 nM), with viral titer reductions up to 99.9% (Fig. 1C). At 18 nM, viral titers declined by approximately 7 log₁₀ units relative to the ZIKV group, exceeding the reduction achieved with the positive control, the thiopurine nucleoside analogue 6-methylmercaptopurine riboside (6-MMPr; 60.5 µM) [14]. Furthermore, OUA (18 nM) significantly reduced ZIKV-induced levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 by 25% and TNF-α by 50% (Fig. 1D, E). These findings corroborate our previous work demonstrating the anti-inflammatory effect of OUA in an animal model of congenital ZIKV syndrome, where OUA decreased the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β in the placenta [9]. Together, these results support a dual antiviral and immunomodulatory role for OUA during ZIKV infection.

To elucidate the cellular changes induced by OUA in ZIKV-infected SH-SY5Y cells, we assessed the phosphorylation levels of the intracellular proteins NF-κB, mTOR, ERK, p38, SRC, and AKT. During ZIKV infection, inflammatory cytokine production is driven in part by the NF-κB signaling pathway [15]. Many receptors can induce NF-κB-dependent IL-6 and TNF-α release, such as the Toll-like receptor (TLR) 3, TLR7, and TLR8 that recognizes the ZIKV RNA [15]. Then, the activated NF-κB translocate into the nucleus and modulates target gene expression at the promoter [16]. Our result demonstrated that OUA treatment reduced ZIKV-induced NF-κB phosphorylation by approximately 75% (Fig. 2A and Figure S1A, G). This result agrees with the downregulation of IL-6 and TNF-α production observed in the present work. In this way, using an in vitro neuroinflammation model, NF-κB has been shown to possess an important role in IL-6 and TNF-α induction [16]. This finding provides a possible mechanism for its anti-inflammatory effects, aligning with previous reports [9].

ZIKV also impairs neurogenesis by disrupting key pathways for cell proliferation and survival, including mTOR, ERK, and p38 [17]. Our data showed that ZIKV infection significantly reduced the phosphorylation of mTOR, ERK (by 50%), and p38 (by 66%) (Fig. 2B, C, D and Figure S1B, C, D, G). On the other hand, OUA treatment restored ERK and p38 phosphorylation to basal levels and increased mTOR phosphorylation to above baseline (Fig. 2B, C, D and Figure S1B, C, D, G). During brain development, the mTOR-mediated signaling pathway plays a central role in the maintenance of the stemness of neural stem cells and neurogenesis. The abnormalities in the mTOR signaling pathway in neural stem cells during brain development result in severe brain disorders [18]. As shown by [17], ZIKV NS4a and NS4b proteins deregulate the mTOR signaling pathway in human fetal neural stem cells to inhibit neurogenesis and induce autophagy. Besides mTOR, it has also been shown that ERK and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) regulate physiological functions such as cell survival, proliferation, and differentiation. MAPKs consist of three serine/threonine kinases in several isoforms: ERK 1–8, JNK 1–3, and p38 α/β/γ/δ. MAPK signaling cascade involves transcription factors that lead to the expression of target genes [16]. Our observations indicate that OUA treatment restored the phosphorylation of these proteins, counteracting the effect of ZIKV infection. This suggests OUA may confer neuroprotection by restoring signaling essential for cell function, particularly in undifferentiated neural cells like SH-SY5Y. This mechanism may explain the neuroprotective effect reported by Carvalho et al. [9], who demonstrated that OUA mitigated ZIKV-triggered neurogenesis impairment in hNS/PCs and prevented ZIKV-mediated reductions in fetus and head sizes.

Furthermore, OUA’s receptor, Na+/K+-ATPase, is known to trigger signaling via SRC [19]. Our data showed that ZIKV and OUA additively increased SRC phosphorylation (Fig. 2E and Figure S1E, G), supporting previous work that links OUA’s antiviral activity to this pathway [20]. Finally, AKT phosphorylation was unaffected by ZIKV infection in this model (Fig. 2F and Figure S1F, G).

Bioinformatics was used to estimate the specificity of OUA in neuronal and non-neuronal cells. In silico screening in glioblastoma cells identified ATPase genes, solute carriers, and the Src-Renin axis as interacting partners for OUA (Fig. 3A), consistent with previous reports [21, 22]. Adverse effect prediction suggested nephrotoxicity (Pa 0.263, Pi: 0.199). Further predictions on glioblastoma cells indicated heterogeneous Pa values (0.038–0.867), while most non-neuronal tumor cells displayed lower Pi (0.002–0.263) (Fig. 3B). Using CTD datasets, OUA was predicted to modulate 1,299 genes (412-up/ 887-down; Table S1), including known targets like IL6 and IL6R, and 182 proteins (92-up/90-down; Table S1). The limited target overlap between transcript and protein levels suggests complex regulation (Fig. 3C). Functional enrichment of these targets included apoptosis-related factors, transcriptional regulators, and cellular responses to viral infections (Table S3). Additional in silico gene expression profiling found fewer modulated genes in non-neuronal cell lines HL60 (n = 57), MCF7 (n = 54), and PC3 (n = 49) (Table S3). The minority of genes was shared across 2–3 cell lines, with up-regulated genes at 19.48% (15/77) and down-regulated genes at 15.51% (9/58) (Fig. 3D). Collectively, these findings suggest that OUA has context-specific effects on cell behavior, and neuronal cells may display hallmarks of OUA-mediated transcriptional changes.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate the potential role of OUA in reverting the deregulated inflammation and neurogenesis impairment caused by ZIKV (Fig. 4). These data support OUA as a promising antiviral and neuroimmunomodulatory agent, offering a potential therapeutic strategy for mitigating ZIKV-induced neuropathogenesis.
