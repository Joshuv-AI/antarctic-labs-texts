Inflammatory gut-heart interactions: Is there a link between inflammatory bowel disease and atrial fibrillation?
Authors: Dobromir Dobrev, Anke C Fender
Published: 2024

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia. Already the ancient civilizations were aware of the heart’s rhythmic movements, and related an irregular rhythm to fluids passing through a network of internal vessels via which the heart communicated with all other bodily organs [1]. Although often neglected by electrophysiologists, the systemic circulation is therefore an important conduit for pathophysiological factors that adversely affect the heart. For instance, the atrial myocardium is highly susceptible to microbial factors escaping from a leaky gut, such lipopolysacharide or trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), that are increasingly linked with AF onset and progression [2], [3].

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a disorder of the intestine that features an inappropriate shift in local and systemic immune responses away from normal mucosal repair toward sustained inflammation. This can detrimentally affect extra-intestinal tissues like the heart. The Journal of International Cardiology Heart & Vasculature has recently published two separate systematic reviews and meta-analyses on the AF risk in patients with IBD. The report by Goyal and colleagues [4] included 7 studies and identified a relative risk (RR) of 1.52 for the development of AF in patients with IBD. The other report by Ke et al. [5] considered 24 observational studies, including 6 of the 7 assessed by Goyal and colleagues [7]. Of the 24 studies, 4 were of prospective and 20 of retrospective design, assessing predominantly North American cohorts plus a handful of cohorts from Europe and Asia. Essentially the expanded study by Ke et al. verified that compared to a general population, patients with IBD were at a higher risk of developing AF (RR 1.45). Missing in these analyses is a comprehensive assessment of accompanying comorbidities such heart failure that could potentially have driven the development of AF independently of IBD. A Mendelian randomization study to identify a potential genetic causality between chronic intestinal inflammation and AF, however, reported a universal absence of any effect [6]. This suggests that the AF-promoting phenotype found in observational associative studies is likely due to the complex interplay between risk factors and accompanying diseases, rather than having a purely genetic basis.

IBD encompasses two distinct disease entities, ulcerative colititis (UC) and Crohn’s Disease (CD). Both disease phenotypes are characterized by disturbed intestinal integrity and impaired mucosal healing, but differ profoundly in terms of local and systemic molecular signatures related to immunity, inflammation and its resolution [7]. UC has been specifically associated with activation of interleukin (IL)-1β [7], a cytokine effector of the NLRP3 inflammasome system. This inflammatory signaling platform is a common mechanistic nexus in many forms of AF [8], and IL-1β is an acute direct trigger for potentially arrhythmogenic sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ release events in atrial cardiomyocytes [9]. The higher IL-1β activity in UC would conceivably be expected to more aggressively drive AF, and indeed, in the present study by Ke et al. [5], sub-analyses based on IBD type showed that the risk of developing AF was higher in patients with UC (RR 1.35) compared to those with CD (RR 1.17). By contrast, the smaller-sized study by Goyal et al. [4] had found a comparable RR of approximately 1.3 for the two IBD subtypes, likely because of a predominance of the patients with UC.

Both analyses [4], [5] incorporated the registry study by Kristensen et al. [10], the earliest of the reports on IBD and AF published 10 years ago. Importantly, the authors determined the incidence rate ratios (IRR) for AF during (i) active IBD flare-ups, (ii) persistent disease activity and (iii) remission. The risk for overall IBD-driven AF was denoted by an IRR of 1.26. This rose to 2.26 during flare-ups and to 2.06 during persistent disease activity. Presumably, the inflammatory burden was higher at these periods, whilst during remission, when inflammation subsides, the risk of AF normalized (IRR 0.97). A similar relationship was noted for the risk of stroke: IRR were 1.57, 1.71 and 1.04, respectively, for IBD flare-up, persistent disease activity and remission. IBD is per se a procoagulant state and AF is one of the major drivers of ischemic stroke risk. Thus, whether the increased stroke risk detected by Kristensen et al. [10] was due to the underlying IBD, to the accompanying higher rate of AF, or to a cumulative burden of both, requires further study and determination.

Recent systematic review and meta-analyses dealing with the potential relationship between IBD and stroke provided mixed results. In one analysis of 12 studies [11], IBD increased overall stroke risk with a RR of 1.19, but this study was biased by the lack of information on the type of stroke. When only studies that specifically differentiated between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke were analyzed, both IBD subtypes elevated the risk of ischemic stroke (RR 1.23 for UC and 1.14 for DC, respectively), but not of hemorrhagic stroke. Although the subgroup analysis considered stroke type, age, follow-up period, sex and race, the potential effects of AF-promoting comorbidities were not considered.

A pooled analysis showed that AF on a background of IBD is associated with an increase in all-cause death (RR 1.42) [12]. The authors reported that the increased mortality was not driven by higher acute rates of myocardial infarction, kidney injury or respiratory failure. Stroke or other thromboembolic events were, however, were not specifically assessed, leaving it open whether the higher mortality in this IBD cohort was due to AF per se, to its associated thromboembolic risk, or to the underlying disease medication. The management of anticoagulation for stroke prevention in AF is complicated by comorbidities and is difficult in the different clinical scenarios [13], [14], with up to half of all newly-diagnosed AF patients remaining inadequately anticoagulated [15]. In patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), for example, many do not receive guideline-recommended thromboembolic prophylaxis although the majority of RA patients with concomitant AF qualify for oral anticoagulation according to CHA2DS2-VASc scoring [16]. In the case of IBD, physicians and patients may be both hesitant to initiate anticoagulation due to concerns of an increased risk of bleeding. In such patients, the careful use of the parenteral heparinoid fondaparinux, the original factor Xa inhibitor, might be considered [17].

In conclusion, patients with IBD warrant close monitoring for AF development and for thromboembolic risk assessment once AF manifests. Recently developed AI algorithms may help to identify at-risk patients with as yet no established structural heart disease or deviation from sinus rhythm on ECG [18]. This approach could be teamed with comprehensive MRI to map and identify adverse structural and functional changes, as is increasingly implemented in patients with RA to optimize diagnosis, risk stratification and management [19]. In IBD, the decision if and how to anticoagulate, and/or whether to initiate rhythm or rate control, should be decided on an individual basis and risk profile, as early as possible after AF diagnosis [20]. Finally, manifest IBD and its subtypes could be considered in the assessment and reporting of patient clinical characteristics in studies assessing prevalent or incident AF, as is already frequently done for other chronic inflammatory diseases.
