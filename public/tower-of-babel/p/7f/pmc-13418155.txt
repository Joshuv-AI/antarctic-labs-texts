Comprehensive characterization of spinal ependymomas in NF2-Schwannomatosis
Authors: Pauline Marijon, Yu Teranishi, Franck Bielle, Marc Sanson, Matthieu Peyre, Michel Kalamarides
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpinal ependymomas (SE) account for 10% of ependymomas and are the most frequent spinal glial tumors. NF2-related Schwannomatosis (NF2-SWN) patients are predisposed to multiple SE in addition to other tumors, schwannomas and meningiomas. Surgery remains the main treatment option, but mays lead to severe morbidity. In many cases, it is the cystic rather than the solid component of the tumor that drives neurological symptoms and ultimately necessitates surgical intervention. Understanding tumor and associated cyst formation is therefore important to develop new therapeutic approaches.MethodsThis clinical and radiological study includes 33 NF2-SWN with a long-term follow-up and 11 sporadic SE patients. Tumor and cyst characteristics were assessed, and histopathological analyses performed, including multiplex immunohistochemistry and RNA sequencing.ResultsRadiological analysis revealed that peritumoral edema frequently precedes cystic formation. Histological analysis showed that macrophage infiltration, particularly by SPP1-positive macrophages, was associated with cystic growth in NF2-SWN SE. RNAseq analysis identified distinct immune microenvironments differentiating NF2-SWN from sporadic SE, including differences in VEGF-C expression. Consistent with these results, AXITINIB, a selective inhibitor of VEGFR and VEGFR3 which acts as the receptor for VEGF-C, was proposed to a NF2-SWN patient with a progressing cystic SE and resulted in clinical improvement accompanied by a reduction in the peritumoral cyst.ConclusionThese results support a role for VEGF signaling and macrophage-mediated microenvironmental changes in edema and cystic growth of NF2-SWN SE. The observed clinical response to AXITINIB in an index patient suggests that new combinations of anti-angiogenic therapies may represent a promising early targeted approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-026-02331-w.

Translational relevance

Spinal ependymomas (SE) are the most frequent intramedullary tumors in NF2-related Schwannomatosis (NF2-SWN) and a major cause of neurological disability. No effective medical therapy currently exists, and symptoms are often driven by cystic progression rather than tumor growth itself. Our comprehensive clinicopathological and molecular analysis identifies dysregulation of the VEGF-C/VEGFR3 axis and macrophage infiltration as key mechanisms associated with cystic and edematous changes in NF2-SWN SE. These findings highlight VEGFR3 inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy. AXITINIB, an oral VEGFR1–3 inhibitor, could be repurposed for NF2-SWN, offering broader and potentially more durable efficacy than bevacizumab, which targets only VEGF-A. This study provides a translational rationale for future early-phase clinical trials integrating VEGF-C/VEGFR3 expression and cystic lesion dynamics as biomarkers of response.

Importance of the study

Spinal ependymomas are the most frequent spinal tumors in patients with NF2-related Schwannomatosis and a leading cause of neurological disability. Current treatments rely almost exclusively on surgery, often triggered by cystic progression rather than solid tumor growth. By integrating long-term clinical follow-up, advanced imaging, multiplex immunofluorescence, and RNA sequencing, this study identifies macrophage infiltration and VEGF-C/VEGFR3 signaling as key mechanisms of cyst formation. The observed radiological and clinical response to axitinib provides the first translational evidence supporting pharmacological inhibition of VEGFR3 in this setting. These results offer a strong rationale for early-phase clinical trials targeting the VEGF-C/VEGFR3 pathway in NF2-SWN patients.

Introduction

Spinal ependymomas (SE) represent 10% of all central nervous system (CNS) ependymomas and are the most common glial tumors of the spinal cord [1]. However, they differ markedly from other ependymoma subtypes which have different localization and tumorigenesis associated with specific fusion genes and proteins [2]. Following the identification of ten subgroups of ependymomas based on anatomical and epigenetic methylation profiles including MYCN-amplified SE representing a distinct molecular subgroup with a unique methylation profile associated with aggressive clinical behavior, the most frequent SE subgroup generally displays a more indolent clinical course. It is characterized molecularly by a biallelic inactivation of the NF2 gene observed in over 90% of cases, regardless of whether SE occur in the context of NF2-related Schwannomatosis (NF2-SWN) or sporadically [3].

SE are rare tumors that remain poorly characterized, particularly for their natural history before treatment. Sporadic SE are typically diagnosed after the onset of neurological symptoms, often necessitating prompt surgical intervention. In contrast, systematic spinal MRI in patients with NF2-SWN enables earlier detection of SE and regular follow-up. In 75% of cases, the tumor consists of a solid and a cystic component—the cyst being either intra- or peritumoral—usually located at the tumor poles [4]. Neurological symptoms are usually caused by the growth of the cystic portion [5, 6]. Surgery is the only treatment but it can worsen patients' neurological condition, especially when they already have pre-operative neurological deficits.

To better understand the natural history of SE, we prospectively followed a cohort of NF2-SWN patients, a genetic condition predisposing individuals to multiple nervous system tumors [7], including bilateral vestibular schwannomas, meningiomas [8], and SE [9] long-term radiological and clinical follow-up that enabled us to report new features of the natural history of these tumors.

Moreover, our second aim was to characterize tumor micro-environment and to identify potential therapeutic targets, particularly to reduce cyst formation and progression [10].

Together, these approaches aimed to better understand the natural progression and investigate the pathogenesis of SE in NF2-SWN and evaluate treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Clinical and radiological analyses

Patients

NF2-SWN patients with at least one SE > 1 cm in diameter on MRI were included in the NF2-SWN group; patients operated from a sporadic SE served as controls. Cases with MYCN amplification were excluded. Enrollment was between the years 2013–2022 (NF2-SWN) and 2020–2023 (sporadic).

Clinical evaluation

Clinical data were collected, including the number and location of spinal ependymomas, date of surgery, duration of preoperative follow-up for each tumor, presence of other central nervous system tumors, and any adjuvant treatments. NF2 mutation analysis was performed in NF2-SWN patients.

Radiology

Tumor and cyst dimensions were measured on T1-gadolinium enhanced and T2 weighted MRI (sagittal/axial). Tumor-to-cyst ratios were calculated. Cystic growth was defined as either the enlargement of a pre-existing cyst (defined as any measurable increase of 2 mm in cyst size) or the formation of a new cyst.

For sporadic SE, only preoperative MRI scans were available (no serial follow-up).

Histology

All tumor slides were reviewed by an expert neuropathologist. (F. B.) to confirm the diagnosis.

Immune and tumor cell populations were analyzed using multiplex immunofluorescence: CD163 (Abcam, ab182422, 1/1000), CD8 (Dako Omnis, GA62361-2, 1/100), SPP1 (Merck, HPA027541 1/200), LYVE1 (Abcam, ab33682, 1/100), IBA1 (Tetubio, 002019-19741, 1/500). Multiplex staining was done with Opal technology (Akoya Biosciences, USA), and automated cells quantification applied after calibration on fluorescence.

Transcriptome

Bulk RNA-seq was performed on FFPE SE blocks. RNA was extracted from FFPE samples using the RNeasy FFPE Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using a 3′ RNA-seq protocol. Sequencing was performed at the iGenSeq platform (Paris Brain Institute, ICM, France) using Illumina technology, generating single-end reads of 100 bases, with an average depth of approximately 15 million reads per sample. Libraries were generated using a 3′-end counting approach suitable for degraded FFPE RNA. RNA-Seq data analyses were performed by GenoSplice technology (www.genosplice.com). Sequencing, data quality, reads repartition (e.g., for potential ribosomal contamination), and insert size estimation were performed using FastQC, Picard-Tools, Samtools and rseqc. Reads were mapped using STAR (v2.7) [11] on the hg38 Human genome assembly. Gene expression regulation study is performed as already described [12]. Briefly, for each gene present in the FASTDB v2022_1 annotation, reads aligning on constitutive regions (that are not prone to alternative splicing) were counted. Based on these read counts, normalization and differential gene expression were performed using DESeq2 (v1.42.0) [13] in R (v.4.3.2). Only genes expressed in at least one of the compared experimental conditions were further analyzed. Genes were considered as expressed if their rpkm value was greater than 96% the background rpkm value based on intergenic regions. Results were considered statistically significant for p-values ≤ 0.05 and fold-changes ≥ 1.5. Gene set variation analyses were performed on the data using GSVA (v1.50.5) in R, and clusters signatures were derived from previously published data [10].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Quantitative variables were presented as median with range, and qualitative variables as counts and percentages. Given the limited sample size and non-normal distribution of the data, non-parametric tests were used. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and comparisons between more than two groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test when appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. For RNA sequencing analyses, differential gene expression was assessed using DESeq2, with p-values adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Among the 33 NF2-SWN patients, 25 had serial MRI, while the remaining 8 had only a single MRI. No longitudinal preoperative imaging was available for the 11 sporadic SE, as these patients typically underwent only one preoperative MRI before surgery. Natural history of solid and cystic components of spinal ependymomas

The two cohorts were not significantly different in term of size and localization (Fig. 1 and Table 1). Cyst size was not significantly larger in the NF2-SWN group compared to the sporadic group (median 38.7 mm vs. 27.5 mm, respectively; p = 0.205). Cyst/tumor ratio was not statistically different between NF2-SWN group and sporadic group (p = 0.292). There was no statistically significant difference in cyst location distribution between the NF2-SWN and sporadic groups (p = 0.284) (Table 1).

Respective localization of NF2-SWN (n = 53) and sporadic (n = 11) spinal ependymomas

Cystic growth was defined as either the enlargement of a pre-existing cyst or the formation of a new one. According to this definition, 18 patients NF2-SWN exhibited cystic growth, including 13 in whom new cysts developed during follow-up and 5 who already had cysts visible on the initial MRI. In 88.9% of these cases (16 patients), cystic growth was preceded by peritumoral edema. For sporadic SE, cysts were identified in eight of these cases, with perilesional edema observed in four.

Surgery

Sixteen NF2-SWN patients underwent surgery for a total of 24 spinal ependymomas. Among these, 21 SE showed documented growth during follow-up, whereas 3 underwent immediate surgery due to symptoms of spinal cord compression at the time of NF2-SWN diagnosis. Of the 21 growing tumors, progression was cystic in 71.4% (n = 15) and solid in 28.6% (n = 6).

The median postoperative follow-up was 71 months [32–115]. Among 16 NF2-SWN patients operated on with a one-year follow-up, only one postoperative residual tumor was observed, in a bulbo-cervical SE. Clinically, 8 patients improved and 1 remained unchanged. Seven patients experienced postoperative clinical deterioration: 4 patients developed neuropathic pain, which was well controlled with pregabalin, and 3 patients had persistent motor deficits at one year; only one of them experienced tetraparesis with significant functional impairment. In contrast, all 11 patients in the sporadic group underwent upfront surgery, prompted by symptomatic presentation at diagnosis with similar post-operative results.

Characteristics of surgically treated spinal ependymomas

Investigation of spinal ependymoma micro-environment

A blinded histopathological review was conducted on a representative sampling of 10 NF2-SWN SE, 8 cystic and 2 solids, focusing on macrophage infiltration. Six among 8 cystic cases (75%) showed macrophage infiltration. Thus, multiplex immunofluorescence analyses were subsequently performed on a cohort of tumor samples, including 7 NF2-SWN and 9 sporadic cases. Immune cell profiling revealed distinct patterns between NF2-SWN and sporadic (Fig. 2A–D). CD163 and IBA1 immunolabeling identified different, non-overlapping cell populations. While IBA1+ cell percentages appeared substantially higher in sporadic tumors (66.9% in cystic and 63.1% in solid cases) than in NF2-SWN tumors (12.4% and 17.4%, respectively), this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.33). A similar trend was observed for CD163+ cells, with higher percentages in NF2-SWN tumors (41% in cystic and 72% in solid cases) compared to sporadic cases (12% and 20.8%, respectively), but without statistical significance (p = 0.33) (Fig. 2A–D). The infiltration by LYVE1+ cells was very low across all subgroups, whereas CD8+ cell infiltration was low but still higher in sporadic cases (7% in cystic and 5% in solid cases) compared to NF2-SWN cases (1% and 0.6%, respectively) but without statistical significance (p = 0.12). In contrast, SPP1+ macrophages infiltration was significantly greater in NF2-SWN cystic tumors (33.2%) compared to all other tumor types (9.3% in NF2-SWN solid, 0% in sporadic cystic, and 6.6% in sporadic solid tumors; p < 0.0001), indicating a potentially distinct immunological microenvironment in this subgroup (Fig. 2A–D). These results indicate a distinct immune profile in NF2-SWN cystic SE with a significantly higher SPP1+ macrophage infiltration compared to other subgroups, non-cystic NF2-SWN and sporadic.

Transcriptomic profiling of SE

Total tumor RNA was extracted from 24 SE (16 NF2-SWN, including 13 cystic cases, and 8 sporadic, including 5 cystic cases) and subsequently sequenced. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize global transcriptomic differences between samples. The PCA plot shows that only samples S19 and S26 differ from the rest, whereas most samples overlap substantially. This suggests that there is no marked distinction between the analyzed groups (e.g., NF2-SWN vs. sporadic, or cystic vs. solid). Hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distance and the Ward. D2 method further supported this trend, with several biologically similar samples clustering together (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Deconvolution-based analysis of bulk RNA sequencing data in NF2-SWN and sporadic spinal ependymomas

Bulk RNA-sequencing analysis did not reveal significant differences between NF2-SWN versus sporadic SE, or cystic versus non-cystic tumors globally. Therefore, we performed a deconvolution analysis using a reference signature file to investigate differences in specific tumor cell populations in greater detail [10].

A focused comparison between cystic NF2-SWN and cystic sporadic SE identified a significantly higher representation of astro-ependymal-like cells (p < 0.05) and immune-reactive cells (p < 0.05) in cystic sporadic tumors. RNA-seq analyses revealed differences in tumor microenvironment between control and NF2-SWN spinal ependymoma. A higher abundance of CX3CR1 + macrophages (p < 0.05) and CCL2 + macrophages (p < 0.05) were observed in sporadic spinal ependymoma (Fig. 3). The subgroup analysis comparing cystic sporadic and cystic NF2-SWN spinal ependymoma revealed an additional difference in the tumor microenvironment, with a predominance of ISG15 + macrophages (p < 0.01). Enrichment analysis of VEGF and HIF1A genes

GSEA revealed significant enrichment of several immune-related pathways and angiogenesis-associated processes, including the VEGF signaling pathway, in the different subgroups. These findings provided a rationale for proceeding with Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) to assess the sample-wise variation of pathway activity. GSVA results confirmed different regulation of immune-related signatures and VEGF signaling the different subgroup of tumors. The analysis of VEGF and HIF1A substrate expression revealed significant differences. Analysis of VEGF isoforms expression revealed that VEGF-C was overexpressed in cystic spinal ependymomas compared to non-cystic tumors (p < 0.01). In contrast, HIF1A expression was found to be downregulated in NF2-SWN SE relative to sporadic spinal ependymomas (p < 0.001) Fig. 3D.

Transcriptomic analyses revealed subgroup-specific patterns, with VEGF-C significantly overexpressed in cystic SE and HIF1A expression reduced in NF2-SWN compared to sporadic tumors, suggesting differential regulation of angiogenic pathways.

Transcriptomic programs associated with cystic phenotype and NF2 status

Comparative transcriptomic pathway analyses revealed distinct biological signatures according to cystic phenotype and NF2 status. Cystic SE showed relative enrichment of gene sets related to ciliated cell differentiation, ciliary organization, and epithelial polarization, suggesting a more differentiated cellular state with preservation of features reminiscent of ependymal lineage cells. In parallel, canonical angiogenesis-associated pathways were less represented in cystic tumors, whereas alternative vascular remodeling programs were observed, supporting a potential contribution of non-classical fluid homeostasis pathways to cyst formation Fig. 4A–C. By contrast, NF2-SWN SE demonstrated reduced enrichment of inflammatory and immune-related pathways compared with sporadic SE, consistent with a less inflammatory tumor microenvironment Fig. 4D–F.

AXITINIB induces peripheral related SE- oedema growth arrest in an NF2-SWN patient

Based on these preclinical studies, a 46-year-old NF2-SWN patient with uncontrolled growing and symptomatic SE, was offered a 6 months-experimental course of AXITINIB therapy. AXITINIB is an oral multi-receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) inhibitor targeting vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR), platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR), and c-KIT, currently FDA-approved for the treatment of adult patients with renal cell carcinoma. Recently, a phase II study showed vestibular schwannoma volumetric and hearing responses in a subset of NF2-SWN patients (clinical trial: NCT02129647) [14].

This patient had a diagnosis of NF2-SWN 10 years before, related to a symptomatic lower thoracic SE that was removed with some proprioceptive permanent deficit. The brain MRI showed an associated bilateral vestibular schwannoma and multiple meningeal enhancement and multiple small spinal schwannomas. He harbored a mosaic form with a stop mutation in exon 7 of the NF2 gene (4% allelic frequency in the blood test) and was the only affected member of his family.

The continuous radiological and audiologic follow-up has shown no change for the bilateral vestibular schwannoma. In contrast, a small cervical SE increased progressively with development of oedema and a secondary cyst. The patient started to present paresthesia in both hands over the last months, beginning to interfere with his daily life.

A surgical resection of the cystic SE was proposed but the patient declined it as the first-line treatment, preferring, if possible, a medical option. During a quarterly meeting of national reference centers discussing difficult cases, bevacizumab was proposed as an alternative to surgery [15]. It was at this point that AXITINIB was also considered, particularly due to the significant edema, the concomitant preclinical results and the advantage for the patient of receiving oral treatment. Given our findings of VEGF-C overexpression in cystic NF2-SWN SE, axitinib appeared to be more appropriate therapeutic option than bevacizumab, which exclusively targets VEGF-A, in contrast to AXITINIB. Informed consent was obtained from the subject.

The patient was treated for 6 months orally twice daily at 5 mg with increase to 7 mg orally twice daily and 10 mg orally twice daily after 4 and 8 weeks respectively. AXITINIB was given continuously in 28-day cycles. MRIs of the spinal cord and the brain were performed at baseline and after 12 and 24 weeks.

After 6 months of AXITINIB, the patient condition had improved with complete disappearance of paresthesia in the right hand and almost complete disappearance in the left hand. The patient was ready to continue treatment despite a skin toxicity after 5.5 months, a hand-foot syndrome (palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia) localized only in the feet, frequently observed under AXITINIB [16].

The spine MRI showed a disappearance of the oedema and slight decrease in the tumoral cyst and stability of tumor without modifications (Fig. 5). Cyst volume was measured on 3D T1-weighted MRI sequences, with a volume of 2.3 cm3 before initiation of axitinib treatment and 1.7 cm3 after six months of therapy. In contrast, the brain MRI showed stability of the vestibular schwannomas.

Discussion

Clinical and radiological insights into cyst development

This study provides new insights into SE development. Radiological analysis showed that in 90% of cases, cyst growth was preceded by perilesional edema. Perilesional edema has previously been reported to precede cyst development in CNS hemangioblastomas [17]. The development of cysts is responsible for the onset of neurological symptoms [5, 6] that often necessitate surgical intervention to achieve clinical improvement. It is therefore essential to better understand the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying cyst formation and to develop therapeutic strategies aimed at preventing their growth or even their initial development.

In our NF2-SWN cohort, 71.4% of SE underwent surgery due to cyst growth, compared to 28.6% for solid tumor progression. In the sporadic group, 4 of 11 operated patients displayed perilesional edema, suggesting that although less frequent, cystic evolution may also occur in sporadic SE. These results suggest a preferential cystic progression in NF2-SWN SE, but it's possible that’s most likely because these tumors are usually detected earlier and therefore monitored over a longer period. In contrast, sporadic SE are most often diagnosed at the onset of symptoms and immediately resected, which limits the ability to observe their natural history, particularly cyst development and edema before cyst development.

Immune microenvironment and mechanisms of cyst formation

To better understand cyst formation, we next examined the tumor immune microenvironment. A study using Single-cell RNA sequencing identified five macrophage subtypes in SE [10], including CD44+ TAMs overexpressing VEGF genes. These cells were in the tumor parenchyma rather than in the cyst wall, consistent with a localized VEGF-driven increase in vascular permeability as in hemangioblastomas [18, 19]. This mechanism likely accounts for the efficacy of bevacizumab (anti–VEGFA) in stabilizing or reversing cyst progression in SE [15].

In our cohort, histopathological and immunofluorescence analyses confirmed predominant macrophage infiltration in NF2-SWN cystic tumors, as also reported in NF2-SWN meningiomas [20]. Multiplex immunofluorescence (IBA1, CD163) showed limited co-localization, indicating that CD163+ cells represent infiltrating macrophages rather than resident microglia. Although our sample size limited statistical significance, cystic tumors showed a trend toward higher macrophage density.

In NF2-SWN cystic SE, enrichment of SPP1+ (osteopontin-expressing) macrophages was found, rarely present in other tumor subtypes. Given their roles in adhesion, migration, CD44 signaling, and VEGF modulation [21–23], these macrophages likely promote vascular and lymphatic permeability. This aligns with transcriptomic data showing selective VEGF-C upregulation in cystic SE, particularly in NF2-SWN tumors, implicating lymphangiogenesis rather than hypoxia-driven VEGF-A signaling, as seen in hemangioblastomas.

GSVA analysis revealed lower immune signature in NF2-SWN cystic SE compared to sporadic cystic SE, which showed diffuse infiltration (CX3CR1+, CCL2+, ISG15+ macrophages). Bulk transcriptomics may underestimate small but functionally relevant subsets such as SPP1+ macrophages, reconciling this apparent paradox. Collectively, these data suggest distinct mechanisms of cyst formation: VEGF-C–mediated lymphangiogenesis in NF2-SWN SE versus pro-inflammatory vascular leakage in sporadic SE.

Translational implications and therapeutic perspectives

The encouraging and consistent result observed in our index patient, together with the preclinical data, suggestes that AXITINIB can reverse the development of edema associated with SE and also induce a reduction in cyst volume. If confirmed in additional patients—ideally within a prospective clinical trial—these findings could support the early use of AXITINIB in the natural history of SE to inhibit edema formation and subsequently limit or reverse cystic expansion, which may be further reduced by Bevacizumab (anti-VEGF-A). The exact mechanism of action of AXITINIB is more complex and may be linked to changes in the microenvironment associated with the meningeal lymphatics that promote tissue clearance and immune surveillance in the central nervous system, with VEGF-C regulating meningeal lymphatic vessels development [24]. AXITINIB targets multiple VEGF receptors, including VEGFR3, the receptor for VEGF-C, whereas bevacizumab acts exclusively on VEGF-A and therefore does not inhibit VEGF-C signaling, which appears to play a role in the development and growth of NF2-SWN spinal ependymomas. In addition, other studies have demonstrated that merlin loss results in hyperactivation of VEGFR-2–mediated pathways [25]. Since AXITINIB also inhibits VEGFR-2, it could therefore target multiple dysregulated signaling cascades in NF2-SWN and may offer broader efficacy compared with bevacizumab, which specifically blocks VEGF-A.

The real challenge in the years to come for NF2-SWN patients will be to use targeted therapeutic modalities to modulate the development of the different tumor types, vestibular schwannoma and meningioma already existing [26] and for effective SE management when they appear threatening. If the response to AXITINIB treatment is confirmed, a sequential therapeutic strategy could be envisioned, whereby AXITINIB would be introduced at the onset of peritumoral edema in spinal ependymomas, with the aim of preventing subsequent cyst formation. Convenience of oral treatment must be balanced with efficacy and safety of axitinib in comparison with other therapies as bevacizumab.

Future directions should include prospective, multicenter early-phase clinical trials designed to assess efficacy and tolerability of AXITINIB in this rare population, ideally incorporating cystic volume dynamics on MRI. Combination strategies with bevacizumab or immunomodulatory agents may also be explored to enhance vascular normalization and anti-edematous effects.

Limits

The relatively small number of cases included in this study reflects the rarity of this tumor type, which inherently limits the size of available cohorts. Although NF2-SWN-associated SE are often detected earlier through systematic surveillance, surgical intervention is generally performed only when tumors become symptomatic. Consequently, the analyzed tumor samples in both NF2-SWN and sporadic groups correspond to a comparable symptomatic stage and tumor size, reducing the likelihood that differences in timing of intervention alone account for the observed immunohistochemical differences.

All samples were processed under identical experimental conditions using consistent staining protocols, and analyses were conducted in a standardized manner to ensure comparability across samples.

Conclusion

Human data presented in this study support a model in which NF2-SWN SE arise from dedifferentiated ependymal cells acquiring progenitor-like features, contributing to tumor heterogeneity. Cyst formation, a common cause for surgical intervention in NF2-SWN patients, appears to be driven by distinct macrophage subpopulations, particularly SPP1+ TAMs, promoting localized VEGF-C mediated lymphatic permeability. In contrast, sporadic tumors exhibit broader immune activation and likely undergo cystic evolution via inflammatory vascular leakage. These findings highlight the importance of early detection of perilesional edema in NF2-SWN SE and suggest a potential therapeutic window for anti-VEGF agents prior to cystic expansion. Notably, one NF2-SWN patient treated with axitinib demonstrated a marked radiological response with regression of peri-cystic edema and clinical symptoms.
