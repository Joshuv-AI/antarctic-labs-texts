Associations of chronic pain and genetic risks with incident atrial fibrillation: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Zexin Lou, Yu Wang, Mengtong Sun, Guoxian Li, Jianing LI, Yujie Shi, Ziqing Sun, Boyan Liu, Hanqing Zhao, Ze Ma, Qiang Han, Qida He, Shigang Qiao, Yueping Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent knowledge on whether chronic pain contributes to atrial fibrillation (AF) risk, especially under varying genetic susceptibilities, is limited. This study aimed to explore the association between chronic pain and AF risk and assess the combined impact of chronic pain and genetic susceptibility to AF, as defined by polygenic risk scores (PRS), in the UK Biobank cohort.MethodsWe analyzed data from 290,345 UK Biobank participants who were initially AF-free. Based on pain duration and location, participants were categorized into four groups: no pain, short-term pain, chronic localized pain, and chronic widespread pain (CWP). Genetic susceptibility to AF was quantified using PRS, categorizing individuals into low, intermediate, and high genetic risk groups. Cox proportional hazard regression analyses were conducted to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for assessing the risk of AF about chronic pain and genetic susceptibility.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 14.2 years, 19,998 participants (6.9%) developed AF. All pain categories were associated with a statistically significant increased risk of AF compared with the no pain category. Both chronic pain and higher PRS were independently associated with incident AF. Joint analyses revealed that, in the high genetic risk group, individuals with chronic localized pain and CWP had HRs of 3.52 (95% CI: 3.25–3.81) and 3.32 (95% CI: 2.71–4.08), respectively, compared to the low genetic risk and no pain group.ConclusionsChronic pain and PRS were independently associated with an increased risk of AF, and individuals with combined exposures had the highest risk.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05994-4.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF), recognized as the most prevalent cardiac arrhythmia impacting over 33 million individuals globally [1], is a supraventricular tachyarrhythmia characterized by rapid and disorganized atrial electrical activity, which leads to ineffective atrial contraction and irregular ventricular rhythm. Despite advances in catheter ablation and surgical techniques such as the Cox-Maze IV procedure [2, 3], and emerging biomarkers like exosomes [4], the pathogenesis of AF remains incompletely understood. This condition is linked to a notable rise in both morbidity and mortality rates [5]. Due to the global aging trend, the prevalence of AF is predicted to increase further, thereby amplifying the related socioeconomic burden [6]. Research has indicated that inflammation, genetic factors, and dietary habits are risk factors contributing to AF [7]. However, these factors do not fully explain the pathogenesis of AF. Given its increasing prevalence, further exploration of the risk factors for AF is necessary.

Chronic pain, defined as pain that persists or recurs for more than three months according to the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) [8], ranks among the most widespread conditions worldwide. Contemporary research is increasingly regarding chronic pain not merely as a symptom but as a disease [8, 9]. It imposes a significant personal and economic burden, affecting more than 30% of people worldwide [10]. Previous studies have established a link between chronic pain and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [11]. A Mendelian randomization study demonstrated that experiencing chronic pain in multiple sites elevates the risk of developing coronary artery disease [12]. Another study indicated that self-reported pain was associated with a previous diagnosis of coronary heart disease [13]. Research has also demonstrated a link between chronic pain and variability in heart rate [14–21]. Given the interaction between chronic pain and inflammation [22], which can contribute to the development of AF, it is plausible to consider a connection between chronic pain and AF. To our knowledge, no prospective cohort study has investigated the association between chronic pain and the risk of developing AF.

Genetic risk factors are regarded as significant contributors to the development of AF [23]. Over the past few decades, over 160 genes have been linked to the occurrence of AF [24]. Syed S Mahmood et al. confirmed through the Framingham Heart Study that genetic factors are an important contributor to AF [25]. The study by Jason D Roberts et al. showed that current research has made great strides in unravelling the complex genetic basis of AF, and that polygenic risk score (PRS) targeting AF has become increasingly valued because of its large effect size and relevance to a wide range of populations [26]. PRS has proven effective in quantifying the cumulative impact of multiple risk-associated genetic variants. However, it is still unclear whether polygenic risk for AF and chronic pain jointly affect AF.

Based on the above findings, we hypothesize that chronic pain is an independent risk factor for the development of AF, and that an individual’s genetic susceptibility to AF significantly modifies this association. In addition, there may be an interaction between genes and chronic pain. The primary aims of this study were to examine the association between chronic pain and the risk of AF, as well as to investigate the potential combined influence of chronic pain and genetic risk on AF using data from the UK Biobank.

Methods

Study population

In this study, we incorporated participants from the UK Biobank, a substantial prospective cohort study. The methodology of the UK Biobank is detailed in the literature [27]. This extensive cohort study gathers a diverse array of psychosocial, sociodemographic, physical, and genetic data through questionnaires, interviews, regular assessment center visits, and medical record linkages. A summary of the cohort’s baseline characteristics is available on their website (www.ukbiobank.ac.uk). The analyses were performed using Application Number 68,136.

A flowchart of the participant screening process is shown in Fig. 1. We excluded individuals with missing self-reported pain information at baseline (n = 8,784) and participants with AF before baseline (n = 8,190). Participants who did not provide complete covariate data (n = 195,173) were excluded as well. 290,345 participants were studied regarding chronic pain and the risk of developing AF. Finally, participants with missing values for the AF PRS (Data-Field 26212, n = 2,701) were excluded from genetic analyses.

Assessment of chronic pain

In this study, pain levels were categorized based on the classification originally developed by Macfarlane et al. in the UK Biobank [28]. The baseline questionnaire of the UK Biobank survey asked participants, “In the last month, have you experienced any of the following that interfered with your usual activities?” Participants were provided with options for pain in seven different body parts (head, face, neck, shoulder, back, abdomen/stomach, hip, and knee), pain all over the body, none of the above, and a preference for not answering. Participants selected one option from these choices. Those acknowledging a painful condition were further asked if the pain had persisted for three months or longer. Participants who preferred not to respond were classified as having missing data. Based on this information, we classified participants into four groups: no pain (no pain in the past month), short-term pain (pain in the past month that has not lasted three months or longer), chronic localized pain (pain in certain areas for more than three months), and chronic widespread pain (CWP) (pain all over the body more than three months) [11].

Assessment of atrial fibrillation

The outcome was defined as the first occurrence of AF and was identified using the ‘first occurrence of health outcomes defined by a three-character International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision code’ (I48). The diagnosis of AF was obtained through links to death registers, primary care records, hospital admission records, and self-reports. Participants were considered AF-free at baseline if they had no record of AF in the “first occurrence” field with a date before the baseline assessment visit. All remaining participants without prior AF and with complete covariate data were included as the reference group.

Polygenic risk score

Detailed information on UK Biobank genotyping has been reported previously [29]. In brief, participants underwent genotyping using the custom UK Biobank Axiom array and subsequent imputation based on the UK10K and 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 panels. The AF PRS used in this study is the pre-computed Standard PRS (Data-Field 26212) from the UK Biobank PRS Release. As detailed in the systematic evaluation by Thompson et al. (2024) [30], the PRS was derived using a Bayesian approach, with LDpred2 as the primary method and an ensemble of models to optimize predictive performance. The LD reference was based on European ancestry panels from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3. The PRS includes genome-wide variants passing standard quality control (minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.01, imputation INFO score > 0.8). The reported area under the ROC curve (AUC) for the AF PRS in the UK Biobank testing subgroup is approximately 0.60–0.65 [30]. For analysis, the PRS was categorized into three genetic risk groups: low risk (lowest quintile, 20% of the sample), intermediate risk (second to fourth quintiles, 60% of the sample), and high risk (highest quintile, 20% of the sample). This 1-3-1 classification ensures balanced sample sizes in the extreme risk groups while using the large intermediate group as the reference.

Covariates

Age, sex, ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index (TDI), smoking status, metabolic equivalent (MET), body mass index (BMI), C-reactive protein (CRP), education level, alcohol consumption, creatinine, cholesterol (CHOL), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), hypertension, heart failure, valvular heart disease, sleep apnea disorders, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, rheumatoid arthritis, spine arthritis, osteomyelitis, and myositis were included as potential confounders.

Data on demographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, TDI, educational level, and BMI), lifestyle (MET, alcohol consumption, and smoking status) were collected using a touchscreen questionnaire, physical measures, and verbal interviews during recruitment. Participants’ height and weight were measured at the initial visit, and BMI was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m2). MET scores were estimated from the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [31]. Smoking status was categorized as ‘never smoked, ever smoked, and currently smoked’ based on questionnaire responses. We calculated the average daily alcohol intake (in grams) for each participant and included it as a covariate in the regression model. Blood biochemistry information (CHOL, HbA1c, CRP, creatinine, and HDL-C levels) was obtained from blood assays. The presence of heart failure, valvular heart disease, sleep apnea disorders, hypertension, as well as inflammatory diseases, was determined based on hospitalization information and baseline questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

We summarized baseline characteristics using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and means with standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables, stratified based on pain status.

The follow-up period was defined as the duration from the initial assessment center visit to the earliest occurrence of the following events: first AF incidence, death, loss to follow-up, or the censorship date (January 1, 2023). Multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression models were employed to evaluate the relationships between chronic pain, PRS, and their combination with AF incidence. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. Participants were classified into four exposure groups: no pain (reference), short-term pain, chronic localized pain, and CWP.

Three models were applied: unadjusted (Model a); adjusted for age at baseline, sex, and ethnicity (Model b); and fully adjusted additionally including education level, TDI, smoking status, CHOL, HDL-C, HbA1c, hypertension, CRP, MET, heart failure, valvular heart disease, alcohol consumption, creatinine, sleep apnea disorders, and inflammatory diseases (Model c). We conducted stratified analyses by sex (male, female), age (< 57 years, ≥ 57 years), ethnicity (white, others), education level (college or university degree, others), BMI (< 30 kg/m², ≥ 30 kg/m²), smoking status (non-smokers, ever smokers, current smokers), MET (low, moderate, high), CHOL (< 5.18 mmol/L, ≥ 5.18 mmol/L), HbA1c (< 39 mmol/mol, ≥ 39 mmol/mol) and CRP (≤ 3 mg/L, > 3 mg/L)based on relevant literature [11, 32–34]. For the primary confirmatory analysis (chronic pain categories → incident AF), we applied Bonferroni correction for the three comparisons (short-term pain, chronic localized pain, CWP vs. no pain), with a corrected significance threshold of α = 0.05/3 = 0.0167.

We conducted a joint analysis of chronic pain and PRS of AF to assess the combined effect of genetic susceptibility and chronic pain on AF. Subgroup analyses by PRS were performed to assess potential modification of the chronic pain-AF relationship by genetic susceptibility.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to confirm robustness. First, participants with AF occurring within the initial two years were excluded. Second, individuals with more than four pain sites in the chronic localized pain group were excluded. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed by reclassifying PRS scores into a triple classification for genetic hereditary risk. Finally, to address the potential bias introduced by excluding patients with incomplete data on the covariates, we performed Multiple Imputations on the covariates.

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4, and significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-sided).

Results

Participant characteristics

Out of the 290,345 participants, 19,998 (6.9%) developed AF over a median follow-up period of 14.2 years (interquartile range, 13.4–15.0 years). Among them, 119,609 reported no pain, 48,539 reported short-term pain, 118,881 reported chronic localized pain, and 3,316 reported CWP. The baseline characteristics of participants grouped by chronic pain are detailed in Table 1. The average age of participants across different pain categories ranged from 55 to 57 years, and the mean BMI varied from 26 to 30 kg/m2. Those with short-term pain tended to be male, with slightly higher BMI, and lower MET scores compared to those without pain. Participants with chronic localized pain were more likely to be female, smokers, have a slightly higher BMI, and lower MET scores compared to the no-pain group. Participants with CWP were more likely to be female, smokers, have higher BMI, lower MET scores, and higher CRP compared to the no-pain group. The baseline characteristics of participants categorized by AF incidence are presented in Supplemental Table S1. Participants with AF, in contrast to those without, tended to be female, older, smokers, less educated, had higher CRP, and had higher rates of hypertension.

Associations between chronic pain and atrial fibrillation

Table 2 provides a detailed account of the link between chronic pain and AF. In the fully adjusted model, the HRs were 1.05 (95% CI: 1.00–1.09), 1.07 (95% CI: 1.04–1.10), and 1.17 (95% CI: 1.05–1.30) for the short-term pain cohort, chronic localized pain cohort, and CWP cohort, respectively, compared to the no pain group. After applying Bonferroni correction for the three primary pain group comparisons (α = = 0.05/3 = 0.0167), chronic localized pain (P < 0.0001) and CWP (P = 0.0045) remained statistically significant, whereas short-term pain (P = 0.0406) did not survive correction (Table 3).

The joint effect of genetic risk and chronic pain on atrial fibrillation

Participants with an intermediate or high PRS exhibited an elevated risk of AF compared with those with a low PRS (Supplemental Table S2). In the fully adjusted model, the relationship between chronic pain and AF was stratified by low, intermediate, and high PRS scores, as shown in Fig. 2. The HRs for AF among participants with CWP were 1.39 (95% CI: 1.03–1.87), 1.16 (95% CI: 1.01–1.34), and 1.07 (95% CI: 0.88–1.30) for those with low, intermediate, and high genetic risk for AF, respectively. The main-effects model showed independent associations of chronic pain and genetic risk with incident AF. Joint analysis revealed that individuals with both high genetic risk and chronic pain had the highest risk of AF, indicating a combined effect of the two exposures without confirmed statistical interaction (Fig. 3). In participants with high genetic risk and CWP, the HR of AF was 3.32 (2.71–4.08) compared to those with low genetic risk and no pain.

Sensitivity analysis

Excluding participants who developed AF within the initial two years of follow-up did not produce significant alterations in the findings (Supplemental Table S3), similar to those of the sensitivity analyses performed on the number of pain sites (Supplemental Table S4). This indicates that even having a few chronic pain sites can elevate the risk of developing AF. Finally, our sensitivity analyses for genetic risk yielded results consistent with previous analyses (Supplemental Tables S5-S7). To address the potential bias introduced by excluding patients with incomplete data on the covariates, we performed Multiple Imputations on the covariates, and the results have not changed significantly (Table 3).

Discussion

Our prospective cohort study involving 290,345 adults demonstrated that chronic localized pain and CWP were associated with an increased risk of developing AF, irrespective of the AF PRS category. In the fully adjusted model, the HR for AF was 7% higher in patients with chronic localized pain and 17% higher in those with CWP than in those without pain. In addition, the HR for AF in those with the highest genetic risk and CWP was 3.32 times higher than that in those with the lowest genetic risk and no pain. These findings deepen our understanding of AF’s association with chronic pain and provide new insights into the interaction between chronic pain and genetic susceptibility to AF. In our study, the absolute risk increase for CWP was 1.19% over 10 years, corresponding to a number needed to harm of 84. The population attributable fraction of chronic localized pain (2.78%) was comparable to that of cigarette smoking and diabetes, highlighting the potential public health relevance of chronic pain as a risk factor for AF.

Research on the connection between chronic pain and AF is sparse. A recent UK Biobank‑based study by Qin et al. also reported associations between chronic localized pain and CWP with incident AF, with HRs of 1.09 (95% CI: 1.05–1.14) and 1.33 (95% CI: 1.15–1.55), respectively, consistent with our findings [35]. However, our study extends this evidence by demonstrating that the association between chronic pain and AF is independent of genetic risk, and that high genetic risk and chronic pain have a joint effect on AF development, although a formal test for multiplicative interaction reached nominal significance (P = 0.001), the additive interaction was not significant (RERI 95% CI included 0), suggesting that the findings reflect independent contributions and a joint effect rather than a confirmed biological interaction. To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate AF PRS into the analysis of chronic pain and AF, providing a novel precision medicine perspective. In addition, numerous longitudinal studies have noted a link between chronic pain and various CVDs, suggesting that chronic pain may be a risk factor for CVD [11, 36–40]. One prospective cohort study found that individuals with fibromyalgia are at an increased independent risk of developing coronary heart disease [40]. Since AF is highly associated with CVD and can result in several CVDs, including stroke, it is plausible to hypothesize a potential link between chronic pain and AF. Our findings suggest that chronic pain may be a new independent risk factor for AF, which provides new ideas for early prevention and intervention of AF. For example, clinicians should pay more attention to the cardiac health status of patients with chronic pain, especially screening and monitoring for AF. Cryoablation has also been shown to improve quality of life and reduce adverse events as first-line AF therapy [41]. In addition, our findings illustrate the potential joint effect of chronic pain and genetic susceptibility on AF risk, suggesting a possible joint role of pain and genetic factors in influencing cardiovascular health. This underscores the complexity of the pathogenesis of AF and may lead to a more personalized approach to risk assessment and management, especially in those patients at higher genetic risk for AF.

Although the stratified analysis (Fig. 2) showed a numerically higher HR for CWP in the low PRS group than in the high PRS group, the formal additive interaction test was not significant (relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) − 0.23, 95% CI included 0) [42, 43]. The apparent attenuation in the high-risk group may be explained by baseline risk saturation (i.e., the already high background AF risk in the high PRS group limits additional relative gain from pain), residual confounding, or limited statistical power [44]. Thus, the results are best interpreted as independent associations of chronic pain and genetic risk with AF, with a joint effect when both exposures are present, rather than a confirmed statistical interaction. Biologically, genetic risk for AF primarily affects ion channel and structural remodeling pathways [45]; when genetic load is high, additional environmental triggers such as chronic pain may contribute only a smaller proportional effect. Thus, while multiplicative interaction is present, there is no evidence of additive interaction, and the pattern does not imply that pain is protective in high-risk individuals.

Numerous mechanisms could elucidate the connection between chronic pain and AF. Initially, chronic pain might be associated with inflammation. The physiological pathways’ alterations that lead to nociceptive sensitization or abnormal pain responses define the pathophysiology of chronic pain [22]. Nociceptive sensitization can occur at both peripheral and central levels. The pathophysiological mechanisms of peripheral sensitization can play a role in diseases characterized by inflammation [46], which is considered one of the main factors in the development of AF. The mediator of the inflammatory response can alter atrial electrophysiology and the structural matrix of the atria, thereby increasing susceptibility to AF [47]. Overall, there are complex interactions between chronic pain and the neuroimmune system. It has been noted that in patients with chronic pelvic pain, higher levels of inflammatory factors were expressed by peripheral blood mononuclear cells isolated after Lipopolysaccharide stimulation in vitro in patients with pain compared with healthy controls [48]. These findings suggest that individuals with chronic pain may have an initiated immune system that plays a role in the development of their disease over time through, among other things, activation of the inflammatory response [49]. This mechanism could explain the interaction between inflammation and chronic pain, whereby chronic pain induces more inflammation, which in turn exacerbates the pain effect and thus promotes disease progression. Furthermore, oxidative stress has been explored as a potential underlying mechanism for AF [50]. Glutathione (GSH) is an important intracellular antioxidant. GSH redox potential (EhGSH) is used to assess the intracellular redox status, and an elevated index represents an enhanced intracellular oxidative status, i.e., an elevated level of oxidative stress. Research by Tahhan et al. indicated that a 10% rise in EhGSH levels correlated with a 40% heightened risk of incident AF [51]. Considering the intricate interplay between pain and redox, oxidative stress may also be a plausible mechanism by which chronic pain contributes to the development of AF. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that exosomes and their microRNA cargos also contribute to AF pathogenesis, potentially serving as diagnostic biomarkers [4, 52]. Previous studies have focused on traditional risk factors for AF (age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, etc.) [53–57]. Prior to our study, few prospective studies had examined the association between chronic pain and AF, and long-term follow-up data from large populations remained limited. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically study and explore the independent association between chronic pain (including localized chronic pain and widespread chronic pain) and the risk of AF through a large-sample cohort analysis. Our study suggests that individuals with chronic pain may benefit from closer monitoring of cardiovascular risk factors, though further research is needed to determine whether this translates to a reduced risk of AF. Future studies should explore the mechanisms underlying the association between chronic pain and AF, including the role of common risk factors and potential confounders.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, as an observational study, it does not allow us to establish a causal link between chronic pain, AF, and coronary heart disease. Secondly, the baseline assessment of the UK Biobank did not include pain intensity, and reliance on self-reported pain may have introduced reporting bias [58]. Thirdly, since AF can often present without symptoms, patients with asymptomatic AF or those who did not seek medical attention might have been overlooked [59]. Fourthly, the AF PRS used in this study was derived from European-based Genome-wide association study (GWAS). In our cohort, approximately 90% of participants were of European ancestry (defined by UK Biobank Data-Field 22006). Although the UK Biobank PRS Release has shown that the AF PRS remains statistically significant across multiple ancestry groups, we acknowledge that European-derived PRSs generally have reduced predictive accuracy in non-European populations. Given that non-European participants comprised only about 10% of our cohort, a well-powered stratified analysis by ancestry was not feasible. Future studies with more diverse samples are needed to validate our findings across different ancestral backgrounds. Fifthly, we used a single ICD-10 code (I48) to define AF, rather than requiring two or more codes separated by time. This approach may miss some asymptomatic or mild cases, leading to non-differential misclassification of the outcome. According to classical epidemiological theory, such misclassification would bias hazard ratios toward the null, potentially underestimating the true associations. Therefore, although our effect estimates may be conservative, the reported positive associations between chronic pain and AF are unlikely to be explained by this limitation. Sixthly, the UK Biobank pain definition may underestimate CWP due to single-site selection. Any non-differential misclassification of the exposure would bias hazard ratios toward the null (conservative bias), so the reported positive associations are unlikely to be explained by this limitation. Lastly, although we made rigorous adjustments for a range of significant confounders, there remains the possibility of biases from unknown and unmeasured confounding factors. Despite these limitations, this study boasts several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first population-based prospective cohort study to thoroughly examine the association between chronic pain and the risk of developing AF, while considering genetic risk. The findings indicate that chronic pain and genetic factors jointly influence the development of AF, particularly in individuals at high genetic risk. This underscores the importance of addressing chronic pain in people with a high genetic predisposition to AF. Future studies integrating advanced statistical genetics methods such as multi-trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG), pleiotropic analysis under composite null hypothesis (PLACO), and bivariate LD score regression are warranted to further elucidate the shared genetic architecture and potential causal pathways between chronic pain and AF.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that chronic pain and genetic susceptibility (as measured by PRS) were independently associated with an increased risk of AF. In addition, individuals with combined exposures had the highest risk, indicating a joint effect of chronic pain and genetic factors on the development of AF.
