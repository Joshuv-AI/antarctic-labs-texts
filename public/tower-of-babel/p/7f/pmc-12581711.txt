Energy restriction optimizes energy partitioning and remodels intestinal energy metabolism in layer pullets
Authors: Qiuyu Jiang, Kaichen Zheng, Bingjian Huang, Jianjun Wang, Zhibin Ban, Bingkun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the effects of different energy-restricted program on energy metabolism in layer pullets. A total of 168 birds were randomly divided into 3 groups: regular energy (RE), energy level restriction (ER, 0.55 MJ/kg lower than RE) and feed restriction (FR, about 88 % of ad libitum intake), with 7 replicates per group and 8 birds per replicate. Indirect calorimetry and targeted metabolomics were employed to assess energy partitioning and intestinal metabolic profiles in pullets across different groups. Results showed that feed restriction significantly decreased feed conversion ratio (FCR), apparent metabolizable energy (AME) intake, retained energy and retained energy as fat compared to RE group (P < 0.05). Moreover, feed restriction up-regulated ileal gene expression of glucose transporter 2 (GLUT2), phosphofructokinase (PFK) and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase β1 subunit (ATP1B1) in pullets (P < 0.05). Compared to RE group, energy level restriction improved retained energy as protein in pullets (P < 0.05). Additionally, pullets in ER group exhibited significantly (P < 0.05) increased adenosine monophosphate (AMP)/adenosine triphosphate (ATP) ratio and reduced adenylate energy charge (AEC) content. In terms of metabolic profiling, energy restriction down-regulated 7 metabolites in ileum, including glycolytic intermediates, such as phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), fructose-1,6-biophosphate (F-1,6-BP) and 2-phosphoglycerate (2-PGA), while feed restriction decreased two metabolites and improved flavin-mononucleotide content in ileal mucosa. Hence, both feed and energy level restriction optimized energy deposition without compromising intestinal morphology and growth performance. Introduction

Energy homeostasis is essential for animal welfare, and adequate energy intake can promote productive efficiency (Renema and Robinson, 2004). Under normal energy intake, approximately 85 % of energy is allocated to fat deposition in poultry, while 15 % supports protein deposition. Moderate energy restriction shifts this balance toward protein deposition, whereas severe restriction mobilizes body fat for protein synthesis (Boekholt et al., 1994). Therefore, adequate energy intake plays essential role in regulating body composition. There are literatures highlight the impacts of energy restriction on physiology and growth performance of poultry. For instance, 85.97 % energy restriction in layer pullets increased egg weight and total egg production during laying phase (Lu et al., 2021). A 5 % reduction in dietary energy level enhanced feed efficiency without compromising growth performance (Ceylan et al., 2023). Moreover, energy restriction could modulate gut microbiota (Li et al., 2024), phosphorus requirements (Gardiner, 1971) and influence ovarian metabolism (Yin et al., 2024) of poultry. Collectively, adequate energy restriction may optimize body energy composition and productive performance in poultry, thereby enhancing economic returns.

Ad libitum feeding may lead to excessive feed intake and rapid weight gain in poultry, and its associated metabolic alterations were reported widely. Ad libitum feeding may disrupt systemic metabolism, such as enhancing circulating ceramide, promoting lipid accumulation, and triggering inflammatory responses, thereby negatively influencing poultry performance (Pan et al., 2012; Xie et al., 2012, 2014). In contrast to ad libitum feeding, feed restriction altered chemical contents in serum, such as glucose, total cholesterol and volatile fatty acids in broilers (Zhan et al., 2007). Accordingly, feed restriction induced daily rhythm variations in body temperature, heart rate and activity levels of broiler breeders by activating the secretion of growth hormone and corticosterone in plasma (De Jong et al., 2002). Furthermore, feed restriction enhanced mobilization of fatty acids and redirected energy substrates toward muscle tissue, triggering compensatory growth (Hornick et al., 2000; Zhan et al., 2007). These endocrine and behavior changes suggest that feed restriction remodels energy metabolism and modulates basal metabolic rate in poultry.

The intestine, as a high-metabolic rate organ essential for nutrient absorption and epithelial turnover, requires substantial energy provision (Fan et al., 1997; Yang et al., 2016). This makes intestinal mucosal homeostasis sensitive to fluctuations in energy supply and availability. Energy restriction reduced energy expenditure of liver and gastrointestinal tract, resulting in reductions of whole-body energy consumption (Ramsey et al., 2000). Moreover, energy restriction significantly modulated intestinal physiology in broilers by enhancing digestible enzyme activity, nutrient transporter expression and enterocyte proliferation rates (Palo et al., 1995a, 1995b; Yamauchi et al., 1996; Gilbert et al., 2008). Energy restriction triggered intestinal adaptation to conserve energy through increasing gastrointestinal mass and reducing metabolic rate (Zubair and Leeson, 1994; Ramsey et al., 2000). At cellular level, both Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity and mitochondrial proton leak were decreased under energy restriction (Ramsey et al., 2000). Moreover, dietary low energy enhanced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, lowered adenosine monophosphate (AMP)/ATP ratio and suppressed AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylation, thereby reshaping duodenal mucosal energy metabolism (Miao et al., 2017). Currently, the energy metabolic profiling in intestine of pullets under energy restriction remains unclear and requires comprehensive investigation.

Although effects of energy level restriction and feed restriction have been extensively studied, comparative data on different restriction programs remain limited. In order to optimize energy utilization in layer pullets, this study investigated the effects of feed restriction and energy level restriction on growth performance, energy partitioning and intestinal energy metabolic profiling of layer pullets during the rearing phase.

Materials and methods

Diets and dietary treatment

As shown in Table 1, the corn-soybean based regular diet was formulated with an apparent metabolizable energy (AME) level of 11.68 MJ/kg, consistent with the recommendations in the “Jing Tint 6 Laying Hens Management Standards” (2022). The low-energy diet provided 11.13 MJ/kg of AME, 0.55 MJ/kg lower than the regular diet, while maintaining equivalent protein, minerals and essential amino acids concentrations. The low energy level was determined with reference to previous studies (Ceylan et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2025a). Wheat bran (Techlex company, Hebei, China) was incorporated into the low-energy diet to reduce dietary energy density. For the feed-restricted pullets, weekly feed intake was predicted based on actual body weight (BW), average daily gain (ADG), and the energy requirement equation established by our laboratory (unpublished data): AME intake = 478.19 × BW0.75+14.93 × ADG. Based on calculations, daily feed intake was restricted at 52.1, 54.4, 57.0 and 57.9 g/bird for 11, 12, 13 and 14 wk, respectively. The average feed intake in feed restriction group amounted to approximately 88 % of ad libitum intake.

Animal management and respiratory calorimetry trial

Animal experiment were conducted at the Jilin Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Gongzhuling, Jilin, China). A total of 168 “Jing Tint 6″ layer pullets at 10 wk of age with average body weight of 764.79 g were randomly divided into 3 groups: regular energy (RE), energy level restriction (ER) and feed restriction (FR). Each group contained 7 replicates with 8 pullets per replicate, housed 2 birds per cage. From 10 to 14 wk of age, pullets were managed according to the “Jing Tint 6 Laying Hens Management Standards” (2022), with temperature maintained at 24∼26 °C and an 8L: 16D lighting schedule (light intensity: 5∼10 lux). Individual BW of each bird was recorded and average daily feed intake (ADFI) was calculated. The feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated by dividing feed intake by body weight gain. Flock uniformity was determined as the percentage of birds falling within ± 10 % of average BW. At 14 wk of age, 2 birds with average BW were selected from each of the 7 replicates and transferred to respiratory chambers.

The 7-d calorimetry trial consisted 1 d for environmental adaptation, 4 d for respiratory calorimetry and 2 d for fasting calorimetry. Throughout the 4 d calorimetry period, daily feed intake was recorded, excreta were collected each morning, and BW was measured at the beginning and end of the trial. The BW0.75 was calculated based on the average of start and end BW during the calorimetry. Following calorimetry, one bird per replicate was selected and slaughtered for sample collection.

Chemical composition analysis and calculation

All nutrient contents of diet and excreta were analyzed based on Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC) methods (2006). In detail, nitrogen content was determined by the Kjeldahl method (KT200; FOSS, Hillerød, Denmark), and crude protein was calculated as nitrogen × 6.25 (method 984.13). Crude fat content was quantified by Soxhlet extraction with petroleum ether as the solvent. Calcium concentration was analyzed by dry ashing and titration with 0.03 mol/L KMnO4, while total phosphorus was determined colorimetrically using a molybdovanadate reagent (method 965.17). Gross energy (GE) content was measured using an adiabatic bomb calorimeter (IKA-C3000, Staufen, Germany).

AME was calculated as the difference between GE intake and GE excretion, divided by feed intake. The nitrogen corrected apparent metabolizable energy (AMEn) was calculated as AME adjusted for nitrogen deposition (34.41 kJ/g).

Indirect calorimetry

The open-circuit respiration chambers were previously described by Jiang et al. (2025a). Briefly, the chambers were equipped with air-conditioned systems and maintained at 22∼26 °C under an automated 8L:16D lighting program. The chambers were equipped with zirconium oxide sensor (Model 65-4-20; Advanced Micro Instruments Inc., USA) and non-dispersive infrared sensor (AGM 10; Sensors Europe GmbH, Germany) for detection of O2 and CO₂ concentration, respectively. The real-time O2 and CO2 data were applied to calculate heat production (HP) and fasting heat production (FHP) as illustrated by Brouwer (1965).

The heat increment (HI) was obtained by subtracting FHP from HP. Additionally, respiratory quotient (RQ) was defined as CO2 output divided by O2 intake.

Moreover, net energy (NE) was calculated as AME minus HI. AME intake was the difference between GE intake and GE excretion, expressed as kJ/kg BW0.75 basis to assess actual energy needs. Retained energy (RE) was determined as AME intake minus HP. Retained energy as protein was computed by multiplying nitrogen deposition by 6.25 (protein conversion factor) and then by 23.86 kJ/g, where 23.86 was the energy content of protein. Moreover, retained energy as fat was the difference between retained energy and retained energy as protein.

Sample collection and intestinal morphology analysis

The jejunum (from the duodenal terminus to Meckel's diverticulum) and ileum (from Meckel's diverticulum to the cecal junction) were gently isolated post-euthanasia. Approximately 1 cm jejunal and ileal middle sections were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. An additional 2 cm segment from jejunal and ileal mid-sections was divided into three pieces and rinsed with physiological saline, while the remaining ileal tissue was used for mucosal collection. Mucosal layers were gently scrapped off with sterile microscope slides. These samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (real-time qPCR) analysis and targeted metabolomics analysis.

The jejunal and ileal tissue sections were examined under a light microscope (TE 2000; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Villus height and crypt depth were measured using Image-pro plus software (Version 6.0; Media Cybernetics, USA) with at least eight replicates per section. The villus height to crypt depth ratio (VCR) was then calculated.

RNA extraction and qPCR analysis

Approximately 1 g of frozen tissue was homogenized in 1 mL Trizol reagent (Genstar, Beijing, China) for RNA extraction. RNA concentration and purity were determined at 260/280/230 nm wavelengths using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 1,000 ng total RNA with a reverse transcription kit (Genstar, Beijing, China). For qPCR amplification with two-step protocol: Initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s (denaturation) and 60°C for 30 s (extension). Target gene primers are listed in Table 2. Gene expression was normalized to β-actin and calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Metabolomic analysis

Targeted energy metabolomics analysis of ileal mucosa was conducted using ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). Briefly, 50 mg ileal mucosa was homogenized in 100 μL ddH₂O and mixed with 500 μL ice-cold methanol/ddH₂O (7:3, v/v) by vortexing for 3 min. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, approximately 300 μL supernatant was collected and incubated at −20°C for 30 min, followed by another centrifugation before UPLC-MS/MS analysis. The chromatographic system (Waters, Milford, USA) used a 2.1 × 100 mm ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (1.7 μm; Waters) while maintained at 45°C, with mobile phases consisting of (A) H₂O containing 10 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 0.3 % ammonia hydroxide, and (B) acetonitrile/H₂O (9:1, v/v). Mass spectrometry was performed using a QTRAP® 6500+ system (SCIEX, Framingham, USA) with electrospray ionization temperature at 450°C, curtain gas at 35 psi, ion spray voltage at +5,500 V (positive) and −4,500 V (negative). Statistical analysis

The data of the current study was analyzed by using SPSS software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA) and exhibited as the form of mean and pooled standard error of the mean (SEM). Data were analyzed using a general linear model, where “dietary energy treatment” was considered as a fixed factor. Meanwhile, differences among 3 treatments were evaluated using Duncan’s multiple comparisons when significant interaction was observed. The differential metabolites were screened by variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and fold change (FC) > 1.5 or < 0.67. The principal component analysis (PCA) was performed through R software (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Furthermore, the pearson correlation heatmap was generated using the Metware Cloud platform provided by Metware Metabolism Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China).

Results

Growth performance

Results showed that neither energy level restriction nor feed restriction significantly affected (P > 0.05) BW of layer pullets at 14 wk of age (Table 3). Feed restriction significantly decreased ADFI by 12.13 % during 10 to 14 wk of age (P < 0.001) and reduced FCR compared to RE group (P < 0.05). However, the flock uniformity of pullets at 14 wk of age was not significantly influenced by dietary restriction (P > 0.05).

Gene expression involved in energy metabolism

Ileal gene expression of AMP-activated protein kinase α1 (AMPKα1) showed a trend toward an increase (P = 0.086) in the FR and ER groups compared to the RE group (Table 8). Feed restriction up-regulated the gene expression of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase β1 subunit (ATP1B1) and phosphofructokinase (PFK), a key glycolytic enzyme. Energy level restriction increased ileal gene expression of phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK), another critical enzyme involved in glycolysis.

Discussion

Both energy level and feed restriction decreased the FCR to different extent in layer pullets, without negative effects on body weight. Conversely, evidence suggested that both energy level and feed restriction compromised BW of broilers (Lunedo et al., 2019). This study indicates that ad libitum-fed pullets maintained a BW within standardized ranges, averaging 1,088 g. In contrast, other research has reported that broiler breeder pullets fed ad libitum became overweight, and feed restriction successfully controlled BW (Carneiro et al., 2019). These conflicting effects on BW of poultry may be attributed to the variations in breed, growth phase and restriction intensity. Notably, pullets in the ER group had lower energy intake than the RE group despite exhibiting higher feed intake, a result consistent with Hill and Dansky (1954). A flock with high uniformity will reach peak egg production earlier and achieve a higher peak production level compared to a flock with low uniformity. In the current study, energy restriction not only maintained flock uniformity but numerically improved it from 91.07 % to 98.21 %. These results probably demonstrate that energy level restriction promotes more equitable feed distribution and allows for a uniform flock, aligning with previous findings in the literature (De Beer and Coon, 2007).

Ad libitum-fed pullets consumed energy beyond their growth and maintenance needs, leading to greater energy deposited as fat compared to feed-restricted birds. The proportion of energy intake retained as fat (29.93 %) in RE group was higher than previously reported ratio of 25.4 % (Carre and Juin, 2015). During sexual maturation, appropriate body fat content is crucial for the onset of laying, but excessive body fat may delay the age at first egg and impair laying performance (Milisits et al., 2015). Feed restriction reduced AME intake and retained energy in pullets, with a greater proportion of energy allocated to protein deposition. As previously illustrated, feed restriction enhanced energy utilization efficiency in laying hens (Donaldson and Millar, 1962). However, feed restriction did not alter FHP, an indicator of maintenance energy requirements in poultry nutrition. Differently, feed restriction decreased the energy requirement for maintenance in broiler breeders, but the body composition was not influenced (Teofilo et al., 2022). Energy level restriction significantly increased the retained energy as protein, thus enhancing the energy utilization efficiency of pullets. As energy intake increased, the proportion of body protein content decreased, while fat content rose in broiler breeders (Attia et al., 1995). Partly consistent with the current study, dietary low energy significantly altered body composition and decreased fasting heat production of growing chickens, though the latter was not statistically significant in females (Mellen et al., 1954). One potential mechanism for the body composition changes could be that higher energy level enhanced lipid metabolism and promoted lipid accumulation (Wang et al., 2014). In contrast, energy restriction up-regulated genes involved in fat oxidation and prioritized energy allocation toward protein deposition (Lunedo et al., 2019). The unchanged FHP under dietary restriction suggested that energy was primarily allocated to satisfy the maintenance needs, with the rest to deposition purpose. Moreover, individual variation among animals and instrumental accuracy of calorimetry could affect the FHP values.

It is often assumed that animal’s metabolic rate could be estimate by measuring the whole-organism oxygen consumption rate. However, oxygen consumption alone is insufficient to indicate energy metabolism in many situations. The inherent variability in the amount of ATP and energy substrates generated in the tissue should be further investigated. Under dietary energy restriction, high metabolic rate organs such as the liver and gastrointestinal tract were preferentially affected (Dumas et al., 2004). Interestingly, feed restriction did not alter intestinal morphology but enhanced gene expression of GLUT2 in ileal mucosa, thereby promoting glucose transporter to meet energy demand in ileum. Although not directly related, feed restriction improved the transportation of glucose, fructose and proline in intestine of mice (Ferraris et al., 2001). Glucose serves as the principle energy source for intestinal epithelium (He et al., 2022). This study suggested that feed restriction improved the gene expression of PFK to accelerate the conversion of fructose-6-phosphate to F-1,6-BP, thereby enhancing glycolysis flux and compensating for reduced nutrient intake. Moreover, the up-regulated ATP1B1 gene expression in ileum probably suggested the functional change of Na+/K+-ATPase in adaptation to feed restriction (Park et al., 1998). Similarly, feed restriction enhanced activity of enzymes involved in glycolysis and TCA cycle in liver (Zhan et al., 2007). However, other study illustrated that feed restriction decreased ATP production and energy metabolism in mitochondria (Dumas et al., 2004). In the current study, feed restriction triggered a metabolic adaptation in the ileum, enhancing glucose transport capacity and modulating glycolytic enzyme expression. This adaptation effectively maintained adenine nucleotides levels and intestinal energy homeostasis in ileal mucosa.

Although low energy diet did not significantly influence contents of single adenine nucleotides (ADP, AMP and ATP), it increased the AMP/ATP ratio and decreased AEC content in ileal mucosa. These changes indicate an adaptive regulation of intestinal energy homeostasis. Generally, the AEC serves as a reliable indicator of intestinal energy status, as it accounts for the relative proportions of adenine nucleotides. In accordance with the results, low energy diets decreased energy status in ileum, while high starch: fat ratio restored the abundance of adenine nucleotides (Jiang et al., 2025a). Contrary to these results, energy restriction could stimulate ATP generation in intestinal tissues (Miao et al., 2017). The differences in poultry breed, physiological status and target organ may explain the variation in energy status. In the present study, energy restriction did not significantly alter ileal genes related to AMPK pathway, likely due to the minimal reduction in intestinal energy status that insufficient to trigger AMPK activation.

Metabolomics analysis further demonstrated that energy restriction reshaped intestinal energy metabolism. Energy restriction remodeled metabolites involved in glycolysis, TCA cycle, and nucleotide and amino acid metabolism. For instance, energy restriction decreased metabolites involved in glycolysis, such as PEP, F-1,6-BP, 2-PGA and 3-PGA, thereby decreasing ATP production. Similarly, the protein involved in glycolysis and energy metabolism in intestine was down-regulated following energy restriction (Genton et al., 2015). Specifically, under energy restriction, the gene expression of PGK related to glycolysis was up-regulated to enhance 3-PGA levels and promote ATP production as a compensatory mechanism. The 3-phenyllactate, a recognized inflammatory marker, was significantly down-regulated in the ileal mucosa of energy-restricted birds, suggesting the alteration of local inflammatory status (Vakhitov et al., 2016). However, the absence of results on inflammatory biomarkers prevents further mechanistic investigation. It is noteworthy that F-1,6-BP in ileum, a key glycolytic intermediate, was consistently down-regulated in both energy-restricted and feed-restricted groups. Similarly, though not directly correlated, energy restriction decreased hepatic fructose-1-phosphate level but elevated F-1,6-BP level in mice (Hagopian et al., 2005). Hence, energy restriction modulated energy metabolism by altering metabolites and enzymes involved in glycolysis and ATP production.

To illustrate the relationship between whole body energy partitioning and intestinal energy metabolism, we performed Pearson correlation analysis and visualized the results in a heatmap. The positive relationship between AMPK gene expression and retained energy as protein implicates the AMPK pathway as an energy-sensing mechanism that mediates the regulation of protein synthesis (Saha et al., 2010). The negative correlation between glycolytic enzyme expression and retained energy as fat suggests that during energy insufficiency, glycolytic enzymes are activated to meet energy demands while energy retained as fat was decreased. Interestingly, retained energy as protein was negatively correlated with metabolites involved in glycolysis, such as PEP, 3-PGA, 2-PGA and 3-phenyllactate. This could be speculated that increase in glycolytic flux and intermediate metabolites links to the inhibition of the highly energy-consuming process of protein synthesis, resulting in a decline in energy deposited as protein. HP and FHP were positively correlated with PEP content in ileal mucosa, a finding that is consistent with results reported by Jiang et al. (2025b). Moreover, HI was observed to be positively correlated with d-glucose and gluconate contents in ileal mucosa. It is plausible that elevated gut nutrient levels (high glucose) during nutrient ingestion may produce more HI. However, the lack of supporting literature prevents further interpretation of the relationship between energy partitioning and intestinal energy metabolism, which warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

This study recommends feed restriction at approximately 88 % of ad libitum intake for pullets aged 10∼14 wk, as it improved FCR and optimized energy deposition without compromising body weight. Energy restriction enhanced the efficiency of energy utilization by preferentially partitioning energy toward protein deposition over fat storage. At the molecular level, feed restriction up-regulated ileal GLUT2 and PFK expression, reflecting an adaptive response to the limited nutrient availability. Moreover, energy restriction modulated intestinal energy metabolism by decreasing glycolytic flux and elevating the AMP/ATP ratio, without negative effects on intestinal morphology. This study provides a theoretical foundation for optimizing energy utilization and energy metabolism in pullets.
