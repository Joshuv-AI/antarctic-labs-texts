The value of second-generation gene sequencing in lung cancer immunotherapy with concurrent infections
Authors: Yaqin Zhang, Qiangqiang Zhang, Lu Wang, Yan Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo collect clinical baseline data of patients with lung cancer and infection, explore the association between clinical characteristics and infection characteristics between the immunotherapy group and the non-immunotherapy group. At the same time, analyze the distribution of pathogens in patients, compare whether there are differences in pathogen distribution between the two groups, and thus explore the diagnostic value of metagenomic next-generation sequencing for patients with lung cancer immunotherapy and infection.MethodsA total of 107 patients with lung cancer and infection treated at Jingzhou First People’s Hospital from December 2022 to July 2025 were included in the study. They were divided into an immunotherapy group (40 cases) and a non-immunotherapy group (67 cases) based on whether they received immunotherapy. All patients underwent electronic bronchoscopy and mNGS testing. The pathogen detection results and various clinical baseline information of the enrolled patients were collected to explore the association between infection characteristics and clinical characteristics in the two groups and to compare whether there were differences in pathogen distribution between the two groups.ResultsIn terms of clinical manifestations, the proportion of patients with fever was higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group. The length of hospital stay and hospitalization costs were higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). In terms of laboratory indicators, patients in the immunotherapy group had higher levels of D-dimer and inflammatory markers (CRP, PCT, IL-6) than those in the non-immunotherapy group, while albumin and hemoglobin levels were lower, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). In the immunotherapy group, 11 cases of pure bacterial infection, 0 cases of pure fungal infection, 2 cases of pure viral infection, and 25 cases of mixed infection were detected. In the non-immunotherapy group, there were 5 cases of pure bacterial infection, 6 cases of pure fungal infection, 1 case of pure viral infection, and 51 cases of mixed infection. We found that the detection rate of pure bacterial infection was higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group (X² = 7.907, P = 0.005 < 0.05), with the tuberculosis bacillus infection rate significantly higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group (X² = 5.738, P = 0.017 < 0.05). Among mixed infections, the detection rate of fungal mixed infections was higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group (X² = 7.474, P = 0.006 < 0.05). with the immunotherapy group showing significantly higher infection rates for Pneumocystis jirovecii (X² = 4.292, P = 0.038 < 0.05) and Aspergillus terreus (X² = 4.485, P = 0.034 < 0.05) compared to the non-immunotherapy group, with statistically significant differences. The detection rate of mixed bacterial infections in the non-immunotherapy group was higher than that in the immunotherapy group (X² = 8.568, P = 0.003 < 0.05). The detection rates of single bacterial and viral infections, as well as other mixed infections, in the immunotherapy group were not significantly different from those in the non-immunotherapy group (P > 0.05).ConclusionThe incidence of bacterial infections and mixed fungal infections increased in lung cancer patients after immunotherapy. Among them, the detection rates of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Pneumocystis jirovecii, and Aspergillus fumigatus were significantly higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group. The detection rate of bacterial mixed infections was higher in the non-immunotherapy group than in the immunotherapy group. In the immunotherapy group, bacterial infections were primarily caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Streptococcus pneumoniae, fungal infections were primarily caused by Aspergillus fumigatus and Pneumocystis jirovecii, and viral infections were primarily caused by Epstein-Barr virus (EBV). In the non-immunotherapy group, bacterial infections were primarily caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Haemophilus influenzae, fungal infections were primarily caused by Aspergillus fumigatus, Pneumocystis jirovecii, and Aspergillus flavus, and viral infections were primarily caused by EB virus and influenza A virus H1N1. Additionally, mNGS demonstrated good applicability in the population undergoing immunotherapy for lung cancer and had a significant impact on treatment outcomes.

Lung cancer is one of the common malignant tumors, and its incidence and mortality rates rank among the top malignant tumors worldwide [1]. According to the China Cancer Center, the number of new lung cancer cases in 2015 was 787,000, and the number of deaths was 630,000, and according to the JMIR Health and Detection data, it is predicted that China will attract a large number of new lung cancer patients in 2035 [2, 3]. Currently, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy are the main treatment options for lung cancer patients. In recent years, the emergence of immunotherapy has significantly prolonged the survival and improved the prognosis of patients.Clinical studies such as KEYNOTE-189 and IMpower-150 have found that the objective remission rate of patients treated with immunotherapy in combination with other medications is significantly higher than that of the chemotherapy group alone (P < 0.01) [4, 5]. However, at the same time, immunotherapy can lead to over-activation of the immune system, triggering the occurrence of immune-related adverse events, especially serious infections [6]. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) is a novel test with the advantages of high throughput, short time-consumption, and unbiased identification of common as well as rare pathogenic microorganisms, which can provide reference for early and precise anti-infective treatment, improve the prognosis of patients, and effectively shorten the number of hospital days and hospitalization costs [7]. This study aims to collect clinical baseline data from patients with lung cancer and infection, explore the association between clinical characteristics and infection characteristics in the immunotherapy group and the non-immunotherapy group, and analyze whether there are differences in the microorganisms causing infection in lung cancer patients after immunotherapy.

Data and methods

General

From December 2022 to July 2025, Jingzhou First People’s Hospital treated 107 patients with lung cancer complicated by infection. Based on whether they received immunotherapy, the patients were divided into an immunotherapy group (40 cases) and a non-immunotherapy group (67 cases). In the immunotherapy group, there were 31 males and 9 females, aged 38–77 years; in the non-immunotherapy group, there were 42 males and 25 females, aged 43–82 years. Inclusion criteria: (a) Age ≥ 18 years old; (b) Clear pathological diagnosis of lung cancer by biopsy pathology (fiberoptic bronchoscopy, percutaneous lung puncture or tumor resection surgery, etc.) before treatment; (c) All patients were required to meet the diagnostic criteria of community-acquired pneumonia/hospital-acquired pneumonia in China [8]; (d) All patients and/or authorizers had signed the relevant informed consent forms, including the bronchoscopy consent form; (e) All patients in each group have completed the relevant pathogenicity testing and mNGS delivery as required; (f) Pathogens have not been clarified by traditional laboratory testing methods; (g) Empirical anti-infective treatment for ≥ 3 days (including the time of anti-infective treatment has been in an outside hospital) without remission; (h) Patients’ clinical medical records, tests and other data are complete. Exclusion criteria: (a) Pregnant women or age < 18 years old; (b) Radiation pneumonia, immunopneumonia, etc. have been diagnosed before the use of immunotherapy or combined with serious lung infections; (c) Patients who did not sign the relevant informed consent and did not improve the relevant pathogenicity testing; (d) Those who have incomplete clinical medical records, tests and other data. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Jingzhou City.

Method

Observation indicators

Basic information, basic diseases, clinical symptoms, blood routine, blood biochemistry, thrombosis index, procalcitonin, C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), sputum culture, mNGS, G test ((1,3)-β-Dlucan test), and GM test (Galactomannan test), etc., were collected from the included cases. Sputum culture, mNGS, G test ((1)-β-D-Glucan test), and GM test (Galactomannan test) were performed.

mNGS detection method and sputum culture

The enrolled patients were operated in strict accordance with the standards of electronic bronchoscopy and alveolar lavage, and the alveolar lavage specimens of the patients were collected and immediately sent to the same third-party testing organization for genome sequencing and biological information analysis, and at the same time, sent to the laboratory of our hospital for microbial culture, G test and GM test. Sputum smear and sputum culture were retained according to the traditional sputum culture method, and the experimental results were observed and recorded.

mNGS detection

Collect 0.5 ml of alveolar lavage specimen from patients, extract DNA, construct DNA library, and then sequencing. Finally, low quality and short reads were removed, and the human host sequences were identified and excluded from the human reference genome by comparison with the microbial large database for data analysis.

Determination of pathogen detection results

mNGS positive determination criteria [9]: analyze the quality of the report, determine the credibility of the results and grade them, according to the clinical characteristics and laboratory examination results, combined with the microbial species detected by mNGS, the relative abundance of the number level of the specific sequence to determine whether it is a pathogenic bacterium, a colonizing bacterium or a background bacterium. If a single specimen cannot be determined, a comprehensive determination can be made by combining several different types of specimens.

Currently, there is a lack of unified interpretation standards in clinical practice. This study summarizes the reference standards [10] as follows:①Bacteria: mNGS unique sequences exceed 50, and the detected pathogens are consistent with culture results. If only mNGS detects pathogens, they should be considered potential pathogens. If mNGS does not detect obvious pathogenic bacteria but only detects some common oral colonizing bacteria, the patient’s overall condition should be considered for judgment.②Viruses, fungi, and atypical pathogens: Exclude the possibility of colonization or contamination; mNGS sequence count for a single species ≥ 50.③Mycobacteria: mNGS requires only one sequence; when compared to the species or genus level, it is considered an infectious pathogen.④Parasites: Detection of any pathogen at the species or genus level by mNGS indicates an infectious pathogen.⑤Background or suspected microorganisms: This study considers uncertain clinical microbiological diagnoses to be negative.

Statistics

SPSS 25.0 software was used for statistical analysis, and the measurement information was expressed as mean (quartiles) and compared using the rank sum test. Count data were expressed as the number of cases (constitutive ratio) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability method. p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant difference.

Results

Basic clinical data

This study included a total of 107 patients, with 40 in the immunotherapy group and 67 in the non-immunotherapy group. The average length of hospital stay in the immunotherapy group was 23.20 days, and the average hospital cost was 21,772.36, both of which were higher than those in the non-immunotherapy group. Nine patients in the immunotherapy group had their immunotherapy delayed due to severe infection. Most patients could adjust their antimicrobial therapy based on mNGS results, with 33 cases (82.5%) in the immunotherapy group and 51 cases (76.1%) in the non-immunotherapy group. Six patients in the immunotherapy group died during hospitalization, with a slightly higher mortality rate than the non-immunotherapy group (see Table 1 for details).

Comparison of basic data between two groups of patients

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, lung cancer patients who underwent enhanced electronic bronchoscopy and mNGS testing were divided into an immunotherapy group and a non-immunotherapy group. The immunotherapy group received immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs): 18 patients received sintilimab, 13 patients received tislelizumab, 1 patient received atezolizumab, 1 patient received cadunilumab, sintilimab (1 case), toripalimab (1 case), and pembrolizumab (5 cases). The immunotherapy group comprised 40 patients, including 31 males and 9 females, with an average age of 63.25 years; the non-immunotherapy group comprised 67 patients, including 42 males and 25 females, with an average age of 65.51 years. The mean age in the non-immunotherapy group was higher than that in the immunotherapy group, but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.130). Additionally, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of gender, underlying conditions (hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, COPD, bronchiectasis, old tuberculosis, and pulmonary embolism), or treatment methods, indicating that the baseline data of the two groups were comparable. The immunotherapy group had significantly higher hospital stay duration, hospitalization costs, albumin, hemoglobin, D-dimer, CRP, PCT, and IL-6 levels than the non-immunotherapy group (P < 0.05). There were no statistically significant differences between the immunotherapy group and the non-immunotherapy group in terms of white blood cell count, lymphocyte percentage, absolute lymphocyte count, G test, and GM test. In terms of clinical manifestations, we observed that the proportion of patients with fever was higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group, and the difference was statistically significant.There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of clinical symptoms such as coughing, sputum production, dyspnea, hemoptysis, and chest pain. There were also no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of the positive rate of pathogen detection in mNGS and sputum culture (see Table 4 for details).

mNGS result evaluation

Distribution of pathogens detected by mNGS

A total of 107 qualified specimens were submitted for testing in this study, of which 101 were positive. Among them, the immunotherapy group detected a total of 22 pathogens and 79 strains through mNGS, with bacteria accounting for 41.8% (33/79), fungi accounting for 40.5% (32/79), and viruses accounting for 17.7% (14/79). The top five pathogens were Pneumocystis jirovecii (14 strains), Aspergillus fumigatus (9 strains), Epstein-Barr virus (8 strains), Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (8 strains), and Streptococcus pneumoniae (6 strains). In the non-immunotherapy group, mNGS detected a total of 45 pathogens, 167 strains, with bacteria accounting for 52.1% (87/167), fungi accounting for 27.50% (46/167), and viruses accounting for 20.4% (34/167). The top five pathogens were Pseudomonas aeruginosa (17 cases), Aspergillus fumigatus (15 cases), Haemophilus influenzae (13 strains), Streptococcus pneumoniae (10 cases), and Pneumocystis jirovecii (10 cases) (see Figs. 1 and 2, and 3 for details).

Characteristics of pathogens co infected in two groups of patients detected by mNGS

Among 107 patients with lung cancer and concurrent infections, we compared the infection characteristics between the immunotherapy group (n = 40) and the non-immunotherapy group (n = 67). We found that the detection rate of pure bacterial infections was significantly higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group (X²=7.907, P = 0.005 < 0.05). The detection rate of pure fungal infections in the non-immunotherapy group was higher than that in the immunotherapy group, showing a trend toward statistical significance (P = 0.051). There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in the detection rates of pure viral infections and mixed infections (see Table 5 for details).

Discussions

Lung cancer is one of the malignant tumors with high morbidity and mortality worldwide, which seriously endangers human life and health. According to statistics, the number of new lung cancer cases is as high as 1.8 million and the number of deaths reaches about 1.6 million every year in the world [11]. It has been found that the traditional chemotherapeutic regimen, although able to reduce tumor size and relieve symptoms in a short time, is prone to drug resistance [12]. The emergence of immunotherapy has brought new hope to patients with advanced lung cancer, as Paul Ehrlich proposed the doctrine of “immunosurveillance”in 1909, which opened a new chapter of immunotherapy, and the first immunotherapeutic drug, Ipilimumab, was approved in the United States in 2011 for the treatment of malignant tumors [13]. Currently, the immunosuppressants that have been approved for the treatment of lung cancer in China include sindilizumab, tirilizumab, pembrolizumab, and karelizumab [14]. The principle of immunotherapy is that immunosuppressants specifically interact with Programmed Cell Death Protein 1 (PD-1), Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1), and Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-Associated Antigen-4 (Cytotoxic T-). Lymphocyte-Associated Protein 4 (CTLA-4) binding, destroying the immune tolerance of tumor cells and restarting the immune system to kill tumor cells. However, at the same time, immunosuppressants can also non-selectively disrupt the negative regulation in immune regulation, and the immune system is over-activated, releasing immune cells and immune factors, thus destroying alveolar epithelial cells and leading to an increased incidence of infections in patients with lung cancer [15]. mNGS is a new type of sequencing technology with a wide and unbiased coverage, and its sensitivity and specificity are superior to that of traditional means of pathogen detection [16]. It has been reported that mNGS has less impact on the prophylactic antibiotic use population, can more accurately detect pathogens in mixed and rare infections, and its results may accelerate clinical decision-making in immunocompromised populations [17].

A retrospective cohort study was designed to assess the incidence of serious infections in lung cancer patients after treatment with ICIs and to further analyze the independent risk factors associated with them. The study found that 191 (26.86%) of the 711 lung cancer patients ultimately enrolled developed serious infections during the follow-up period. The main site of infection was the lungs (75.61%), and the aetiologic analysis showed that bacterial infections were predominant (82.05%) predominantly, followed by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (6.47%), viral (4.98%) and fungal (3.48%) infections. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, systemic glucocorticoid use, low lymphocyte counts and low CD4/CD8 ratio were identified as significant independent risk factors [18]. Therefore, focused monitoring of these high-risk patients and timely prophylactic treatment should be performed in clinical management to reduce the incidence of infection and improve prognosis.

Different immune checkpoint inhibitors develop slightly different infectious events.Weber et al. [19] found that patients using CTLA-4 inhibitors (e.g., ibritumomab) were at increased risk for early bacterial and fungal infections, such as pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and candidiasis. There is also a corresponding increase in the incidence of opportunistic infections (e.g., Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia, cryptococcosis), but they are relatively rare. The main mechanism is the overactivation of T cells by CTLA-4 inhibitors, which enhances pathogen clearance in the early stages but causes regulatory T cell depletion and disruption of immune tolerance with prolonged use. Therefore, the anti-tumor effect needs to be weighed against the risk of infection when treating with high-dose CTLA-4 inhibitors in the clinic. Salerno et al. [20] investigated the association between PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and viral reactivation, and found that when the PD-1 pathway was inhibited, the over-activation of latent viruses (e.g., hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus, etc.)-specific T cells led to tissue damage, while Ineffective control of viral replication and a significant increase in the reactivation rate of hepatitis B virus after long-term use of PD-1 inhibitors. Therefore, it is recommended to routinely screen for hepatitis B virus/hepatitis C virus and consider prophylactic antiviral therapy before using PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Secondly the use of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors may increase the risk of respiratory infections (e.g., the risk of COVID-19 severe infections). Del Castillo et al. [21] retrospectively examined the risk of infections in the combination of CTLA-4 and PD-1 inhibitors, and found that the prevalence of infections in the combination group (35%) was significantly higher than that of the monotherapy group (15%), with a predominantly Significantly higher risk of multiple infections, including bacterial, viral, and fungal infections (e.g., invasive aspergillosis), mainly due to the synergistic effect of the combination therapy leading to widespread activation of the immune system and the development of cytokine-release syndromes, which can further inhibit myelopoiesis or lead to immune depletion. Therefore, patients on combination therapy need to be closely monitored for signs of developing infections, especially fungal and drug-resistant organisms, in the clinical setting.

A total of 107 lung cancer patients who met the criteria were included in this study, 40 in the immunotherapy group and 67 in the non-immunotherapy group. The number of hospital days and hospitalization costs were higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group. Nine (22.5%) patients in the immunotherapy group delayed immunotherapy due to severe infections, and six (15.0%) patients died during hospitalization, compared with seven (10.4%) in the non-immunotherapy group, and the mortality rate of patients in the immunotherapy group was slightly higher than that in the non-immunotherapy group. We also compared the antibiotic use of patients in the two groups, and found that the top three antibiotic types used in both groups were third-generation cephalosporins, β-lactams, and quinolones, with a higher proportion of carbapenem antibiotic use in the immunotherapy group (12.7%) than in the non-immunotherapy group (9.4%). This indicates that patients with lung cancer undergoing immunotherapy combined with lung infections have rapid disease progression, high disability and mortality rates, high chances of developing severe infections such as respiratory failure and severe pneumonia, and some patients even die, causing serious economic burdens to the patients’ families [22].

In the comparison of clinical baseline data, we found that the differences between the two groups were not statistically significant in terms of potential confounders such as gender, age, underlying disease, and treatment modality (e.g., chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeting), which suggests that the degree of matching of the baseline data between the two groups was controlled for and is comparable. In this study, we also found that by comparing the number of days of hospitalization and hospitalization costs between the two groups, we found that the number of days of hospitalization and hospitalization costs were significantly higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group. Secondly, we found that the inflammatory indicators (CRP, PCT, IL-6, etc.) were higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group, and the difference was statistically significant. Most of the patients could adjust the anti-infection treatment regimen according to the results of mNGS, including 33 cases (82.5%) in the immunotherapy and 51 cases (76.1%) in the non-immunotherapy group. And we compared the detection return time between mNGS and conventional detection methods, and found that the detection time of mNGS was 2–3 days, which was significantly shorter than that of conventional detection, and the difference was statistically significant. Therefore, in clinical practice, after immunotherapy for lung cancer, we should monitor the relevant inflammatory indexes of patients at an early stage and apply mNGS and other detection methods to effectively identify complex pathogens and rationally use antibiotics, improve prognosis, shorten treatment time and cost, and accelerate patients’ recovery.

Bacteria and fungi were most commonly detected in patients in the immunotherapy group in this study, with commonly detected pathogens in bacteria including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Klebsiella pneumoniae, and commonly detected pathogens in fungi including Aspergillus fumigatus, Pneumocystis japonicus, and Aspergillus flavus. We also found that the rate of simple mixed infections of multiple bacteria was significantly higher in the non-immunotherapy group than in the immunotherapy group (X2 = 8.568, p = 0.003 < 0.05). The detection rate of simple bacterial infections was significantly higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group (X2 = 7.907, p = 0.005 < 0.05), of which the detection rate of Mycobacterium tuberculosis in the immunotherapy group was significantly higher than that in the non-immunotherapy group (X2 = 5.738, p = 0.017 < 0.05).Fan et al. [23] summarized the case studies related to the last few years, in which 8 cases were treated with nabulizumab and 6 cases with parvulizumab. All patients were not screened for Latent Tuberculosis Infection (LTBI) prior to immunotherapy, and it was not clear whether the TB infection was primary or secondary. Tuberculosis was diagnosed during the course of immunotherapy and regular anti-tuberculosis treatment was administered. 14 patients were suggested to have reactivation of tuberculosis as a well-defined adverse effect [24]. It has also been found that treatment of the tumor itself (e.g., chemotherapy, etc.) may also be a source of immunosuppression, thereby increasing the risk of TB. Among solid tumors, lung cancer has the highest incidence of TB reactivation [25, 26], which is consistent with the results of this study. The specific mechanism leading to TB reactivation in patients is still unclear and requires further study. However, it has been found that in PD-1-deficient mice and PD-1-blocked populations, increased interferon production by CD4 + T cells after infection with Mycobacterium tuberculosis promotes bacterial replication and tissue destruction [27]. Therefore, the application of ICIs during immunotherapy can complicate the infection, and the possibility of tuberculosis should be prioritized when new lesions appear in clinical workups, especially in patients with lung cancer, lymphoma, or a history of exposure to tuberculosis, and timely LTBI screening or mNGS testing should be necessary, which is essential for the rapid diagnosis of tuberculosis and adjustment of the treatment regimen.

Lung fungal infection is one of the common complications in lung cancer patients, which not only affects the life and health of the patients, but also is able to hinder the therapeutic process, thus affecting the therapeutic effect [28]. A study included 167 patients with non-small cell lung cancer who were concurrently treated with nabulizumab immunotherapy, and found that 33 (19.2%) patients developed infections, including 25 bacterial infections, 2 fungal infections, and 6 viral infections [29]. Another report found a case of acute exacerbation of pre-existing chronic progressive pulmonary aspergillosis after receiving 20 courses of nabulizumab [30]. In this study, we found that the rate of simple mixed infections of multiple fungi was significantly higher in lung cancer patients treated with ICIs (X2 = 7.474, p = 0.006 < 0.05), in which the rates of Pneumocystis jiroveci (X2 = 4.292, p = 0.038 < 0.05) and Aspergillus tularensis (X2 = 4.485, p = 0.034 < 0.05) infection after treatment with ICIs were found to be were significantly elevated. ICIs treatment has been reported to induce acute exacerbation of chronic invasive pulmonary aspergillosis as well as fungal sinusitis [31]. ICIs treatment leads to abnormalities in the immune status of the organism, which induces an over-immune response to the colonizing bacteria and promotes the growth of the fungi, which is consistent with the present study. In clinical practice, new fungal infections during immunotherapy are sometimes more difficult to distinguish from tumor progression. Therefore, clinicians should monitor early during immunotherapy and need to be alert to the emergence of fungal infections when patients develop infections and use antifungal drugs rationally. Some studies have reported an increased incidence of viral reactivation after patients underwent PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, but this result was not observed in the present study, probably because of the small sample size, which needs to be further investigated by subsequent expansion of the sample size.

Conclusion

In this study, we found that the incidence of mixed bacterial and fungal infections increased after ICIs in lung cancer patients, with bacterial infections dominated by Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Klebsiella pneumoniae, fungal infections dominated by Aspergillus fumigatus, Pneumocystis japonicus and Aspergillus flavus, and viral infections dominated by EBV. The detection rate of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Pneumocystis japonicum and Aspergillus terreus infections was significantly higher in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group. Secondly, mNGS showed good applicability in the population of lung cancer treated with ICIs and had a greater impact on the anti-infection treatment program. Therefore, clinicians should pay attention to identify the type of infection in the process of diagnosis and treatment, and timely apply mNGS and other detection means to adjust the anti-infection treatment program to improve the prognosis and reduce the economic burden of patients. However, this study also has certain limitations, first of all, the sample size is small, resulting in a certain risk of bias in some of the data, and there is a certain degree of randomness in the results. Therefore, some uncertain findings need to be handled with caution and further validation is needed by expanding the sample size or conducting a multicenter prospective study. Secondly, mNGS also has problems such as the adulteration of background bacteria, low detection rate of intracellular bacteria and thick-walled microorganisms. Multiple bacterial, fungal and viral information can be detected in the same specimen during the testing process, therefore, after obtaining the sequencing results, clinicians should firstly judge according to the sequencing value, and at the same time, combine with the patient’s medical history, symptoms, laboratory tests, etc. to distinguish the pathogenic microorganisms whether they are pathogenic, colonized or contaminated.
