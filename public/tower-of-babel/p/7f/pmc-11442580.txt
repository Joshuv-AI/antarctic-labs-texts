Convolutional neural network based detection of early stage Parkinson’s disease using the six minute walk test
Authors: Hyejin Choi, Changhong Youm, Hwayoung Park, Bohyun Kim, Juseon Hwang, Sang-Myung Cheon, Sungtae Shin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The heterogeneity of Parkinson’s disease (PD) presents considerable challenges for accurate diagnosis, particularly during early-stage disease, when the symptoms may be extremely subtle. This study aimed to assess the accuracy of a convolutional neural network (CNN) technique based on the 6-min walk test (6MWT) measured using wearable sensors to distinguish patients with early-stage PD (n = 78) from healthy controls (n = 50). The participants wore six sensors, and performed the 6MWT. The time-series data were converted into new images. The results revealed that the gyroscopic vertical component of the lumbar spine displayed the highest classification accuracy of 83.5%, followed by those of the thoracic spine (83.1%) and right thigh (79.5%) segment. These findings suggest that the 6MWT and CNN models may facilitate earlier diagnosis and monitoring of PD symptoms, enabling clinicians to provide timely treatment during the critical transition from normal to pathologic gait patterns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-72648-w.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic, degenerative neurological disorder that affects movements1. The disease is characterized by symptoms such as difficulty walking, speaking, or completing other simple tasks, as well as a combination of motor and non-motor symptoms that can confer functional disability, loss of independence, and decreased quality of life2,3. The heterogeneity of PD presents considerable challenges for accurate diagnosis, especially in early-stage PD that may include extremely subtle symptoms4. The clinical severity of PD can be categorized into five stages known as the Hoehn and Yahr stages (H&Y stages 1–5)5. In stages 1 and 2, which include patients in the early stage of this study, individuals may experience unilateral symptoms such as symmetrical movement reduction of one limb, asymmetrical hand movements, and shuffling when walking6. However, identifying PD during the early stages is challenging, because aging individuals, who experience conditions such as joint osteoarthritis or sarcopenia, may also demonstrate a progressive decline in walking speed, termed senile gait7.

The H&Y Scale, which is a Movement Disorders Society (MDS)-sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), MDS-UPDRS Part III, PD phenotype assessment, and discrete variable-based analysis of physical and fitness factors, as well as postural and gait characteristics, are used to assess the severity of motor symptoms in PD8,9. However, these assessments are subjectively scored through clinical observation by neurospecialists10 which has led to the need for research on objective, accurate body-part-specific motor-symptom assessment and symptom impact-based classification of disease severity11. Specifically, there are limitations in identifying the early motor symptoms of PD, and determining the body segments that are most appropriate to detect early signals.

Gait in PD serves as a potential status and trait marker, because gait impairments often appear during early-stage PD, precede overt motor signs, and progress more rapidly than other motor characteristics of PD2,12. To objectively quantify individual gait characteristics, tools such as three-dimensional (3D) motion capture, force plates, instrumented walkways, treadmills, and electromyography13 are essential to accurately characterize PD gait disorders. However, the application of these tools is primarily limited to research environments due to their cost and the need for specialized expertise14. Wearable sensors may be a useful, inexpensive alternative for monitoring gait impairment in a controlled daily living environment15–17.

The 6-min walk test (6MWT) is a widely applied submaximal exercise assessment that is routinely employed to evaluate an individual’s aerobic capacity and physical mobility18,19. The 6MWT was initially developed for use in patients with chronic respiratory or cardiovascular conditions, but is currently used to assess diverse populations, including older adults, individuals who have experienced a stroke, those with lower extremity amputations, and persons with PD20–23. The 6MWT is a simple (requires minimal setup and space), cost-effective, safe, functional test that is easily reproducible24. Despite the limitation that the traditional 6MWT only measures the distance walked in 6 min, the distance walked exhibits a moderate correlation with more complex aerobic capacity tests, such as maximum oxygen uptake (VO2 max), and can thereby minimize the burden on patients and clinicians25,26. Additionally, compared to other gait tests, the well-defined settings and longer duration of the 6MWT make it favorable for obtaining useful information from both turning and straight gaits, which potentially increases the efficiency of the test27. Therefore, the 6MWT conducted using wearable sensors appears to be a fast, easy, clinically applicable, comprehensive method that provides objective details of gait quality28,29.

Recent trends involving the use of artificial intelligence-based machine learning and deep learning models to classify and predict diseases30 have advanced research to resolve the problem of motor-symptom severity classification with the comprehensive gait characteristics of individuals with PD. For example, a machine learning algorithm has been used to distinguish three types of neurodegenerative diseases, namely PD, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis31, and for diagnosing and assessing PD severity, according to the H&Y scale32. Borzì et al. reported the use of neural network-based freezing of gait (FOG) detection from the 6MWT in PD using waist sensor-derived acceleration data33. Juneau and Daines et al. used a random forest model to classify the fall risk through accelerometer- and gyroscope-derived data that were recorded using a smartphone placed over the posterior pelvis during the 6MWT in patients with lower limb amputations22,34. However, these studies have limitations such as small sample size; low accuracy due to the analysis of movement data of specific body segments, based on a single wearable sensor without the objective, specific identification of body segments according to disease severity; and the use of unproductive, inefficient, and less sensitive tools16.

Comparative analysis of passive signal classification and gait parameter extraction from wearable sensors for the detection and prediction of gait-disorder signals in PD through gait assessment has limited utility in the identification of subtle motor symptomatic disorders in early- to mid-stage PD15. To overcome these limitations, there has been increased interest in deep learning technologies, which are automated approaches for objective diagnosis, quantitative assessment, and accurate predictive disease progression modelling35–39. EI Maachi et al. and Yang et al. proposed an intelligent gait-based PD-detection system that used a convolutional neural network (CNN)35,36. In a wearable sensor-based study, Nguyen et al. classified abnormal gait in musculoskeletal disorders through a CNN-derived sensor-based spectrogram approach40. Previous research has employed a rapid instrumented functional test using inertial sensors to classify PD grades using deep learning models that combine the raw signals from the sensors, with CNN one-dimensional (1D) and long short-term memory (LSTM) and CNN two-dimensional (2D) layers for the images generated from the signals38,39. For objective gait-disorder signal detection, we believe that accuracy could be improved by using time-series (TS) gait patterns and classifying them into CNN architecture-converted visual images37. However, studies specifically related to CNN-based 6MWT identification to categorize early-stage PD are limited.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify segments and components that exhibit optimal accuracy for classifying individuals with early-stage PD and healthy controls using a CNN algorithm for sensor-based 6MWT data. The research hypothesis is as follows: First, CNN algorithms can improve the classification of early-stage PD and healthy controls, wherein TS signals of accelerometer and gyroscope-based gait patterns obtained using six segmentally mounted wearable sensors are converted into images to determine the classifiability of the two groups. Second, based on the acceleration and gyroscope data obtained from the wearable sensors, the characteristics of early-stage PD that are prominent in specific components of the six segments can be identified.

Methods

Participants

A total of 79 individuals with PD (early PDs) and 50 age-matched healthy controls (controls) participated in the study and were included in the final analysis. The study process and participant details are depicted in a flowchart (Fig. 1). A neurospecialist diagnosed patients with idiopathic PD according to the United Kingdom’s Parkinson’s Disease Society Brain Bank41 criteria. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (a) age 40–85 years, (b) ability to walk and move independently without assistive devices, with a modified H&Y stage of 1–25,42, (c) a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ≥ 2443, and (d) a stable response to anti-Parkinson’s medications. One participant who dropped out during the 6MWT was excluded from the analysis. Individuals with a history of cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, vestibular, or other neurological diseases, who required mobility-assistive devices, and with pharmacotherapy-refractory dyskinesia were excluded. The control group comprised individuals with no medical history of orthopedic surgery or cognitive impairment and gait disturbance within the previous 6 months preceding the study. Experimental procedures

Participants completed two sessions. In the initial session, participants completed the informed consent form and were assessed using the MMSE, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)44, UPDRS, and modified H&Y scale. Physical characteristics such as sex, age, height, body mass, and body mass index were recorded (Table 1). In the second session, before performing the main task, wearable sensors were attached to the left and right upper arms (5 cm above the lateral humeral epicondyle), thighs (10 cm above the lateral femoral epicondyle), thoracic spine (T10), and lumbar spine (center of the left and right posterior superior iliac spines) region using a stretchable belt (Fig. 2a). The participants then performed the 6MWT as the main task, walking as fast as possible, without running, in the preferred direction for 6 min. The 20-m course was marked with cones placed at each end (Fig. 2b). The raters followed the participants to ensure their safety, offer encouragement, and inform them of the remaining time. All experiments were performed in the “On” medication state approximately 2 h after taking the medication.

Data acquisition

Data were collected using an Xsens DOT Wearable Sensor platform (Movella Technologies, Enschede, the Netherlands) with a package size of 36.30 × 30.35 × 10.80 mm and a weight of 11.2 g. Signal-based TS gait characteristics were extracted from each wearable sensor (accelerometer and gyroscope) in each axis (x-axis: vertical, y-axis: mediolateral, and z-axis: anteroposterior; Fig. 2a). We collected data on 3D-acceleration (m/s2; up to ± 16 g) and 3D angular velocity (°/s; up to ± 2000°/s) via the accelerometer and gyroscope, respectively, in the wearable sensor. By default, the local earth-fixed reference coordinate system (L) used is defined as a right-handed Cartesian coordinate system (East-North-Up [ENU]) with X positive to the East (E), Y positive to the North (N), and Z positive when pointing up (U) and is the standard reference in inertial navigation for aviation and geodetic applications. With the default ENU (L) coordinate system, the X-sens yaw output is defined as the angle between East (X) and the horizontal projection of the sensor x-axis, positive about the local vertical axis (Z) following the righthand rule. The orientation calculated by X-sens DOT is the orientation of the sensor coordinate system (S) with regard to L. The orientation output is in the ENU frame: accordingly, the roll, pitch, and yaw will equal 0 degrees when the x-, y-, and z-axes of the sensor are aligned with the east, north, and upward directions, respectively. Therefore, X, Y, and Z are positive when pointing to the local magnetic east, the local magnetic north, and upward, respectively. The data output rate was set at 60 Hz, and 3D data were collected using an iPad (iOS 15.6.1; Apple, Cupertino, CA) with the MovellaDOT app using Bluetooth 5.0 communication. MATLAB R2023a (MathWorks, Natick, MA) was used to analyze the data.

Data pre-processing

Before conducting data pre-processing, we visually inspected the graphs of the raw data from each of the six sensors for the entire 6-min duration. Next, we performed the following data pre-processing steps. First, we divided the 6 min (360 s) of TS data from each participant’s six sensors into 15-s segments, representing one phase of the 24 data phases, including straight and turning gait. Second, we normalized the data to eliminate the effects of differences in the participants’ heights and sensor-placement variations following reattachment. The normalization procedure involved centering the sensor data within each trial to achieve a median of 0 while retaining the original scale of the sensor data. The median value of the data was adjusted to align with the 0-reference point. This was done by calculating the median value of the data for each axis and then subtracting this median value from all data points on the respective axis. By centering the data around 0, we ensured that any systematic offsets due to participant height differences or sensor placement variations were minimized. The data normalization aimed to adjust the central tendency (median) without affecting the spread (variance) of the data, thereby maintaining the integrity of the sensor measurements. The normalization process was performed separately for each axis. This means that the median of each axis was calculated individually and used to center the data for that specific axis. This approach addresses any directional biases that may exist in the data due to sensor orientation or participant movement patterns. Last, we set the length of the sample to 896 to create a single image. The length of the 896 samples is proportional to the pixel size. This specific length was selected based on the requirements of our deep learning model, which uses images as input data. Each input image has a pixel size of 224 pixels. This pixel size is a standard size that is typically used as the input size for CNN models, and it provides sufficient information and the appropriate resolution for the network to learn efficiently. To align with the model’s requirements and ensure consistent input dimensions, we multiplied the pixel size of the input images (224 pixels) by an even number factor (4) for the sample in each data phase for all participants to obtain a sample length of 224 pixels × 4 = 896 (approximately 9 s). The factor of 4 was selected to achieve an appropriate sample length that balances the trade-off between capturing sufficient data for analysis and maintaining computational efficiency. For the samples in each data phase, we used the time image of the signal for all axes. This process constitutes the standardized approach to effectively construct a CNN model and train it with an optimized resolution image45. For trials lasting less than 8.96 s, we applied zero padding.

The resulting values of the three axes, as well as the acceleration and gyroscope data for each of the x-, y-, and z-axes, were used to analyze the TS data and their components were calculated with resultant acceleration and gyroscope data according to the following formula:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Resultant\:data=sqrt(x^2+y^2+z^2)$$\end{document}

Data pre-processing was performed using MATLAB.

Data generation

For training and testing CNN models using the accumulated imaging dataset of all phases, the dataset was randomly divided, per subject, into the training, validation, and test sets in a ratio of 50%, 20%, and 30%, respectively. The dataset initially consisted of 1776 samples for early PDs and 1056 samples for controls across six sensors for each segment (left arm, right arm, left thigh, right thigh, thoracic spine, and lumbar spine). Therefore, the unbalanced dataset was randomly oversampled using the imbalanced-learn (version 0.10.1) Python package to perform an unbiased analysis, and data balancing was performed by generating new samples in the subset of samples belonging to the minority class to reach a balancing ratio46. For each body segment, a sample count of 1776 was generated for both early PDs and controls. Therefore, 3552 samples were generated for each body segment in the entire dataset.

Training models and classification

The deep learning classification analysis based on the CNN models and TS imaging methods was performed as follows. For the variance analysis, fivefold cross-validation was employed. The batch size used in this experiment was 128; Adam optimizers were used, and the learning rate of 1e-05 was used for the optimizer. After 150 epochs, the proposed model reaches saturation, and training is halted with the help of early stopping. To mitigate the risk of overfitting, only the best model was saved. This meant that, during the training phase, if the validation accuracy of the epoch exceeded the highest accuracy achieved thus far, then the model was saved.

TS imaging methods

The perspective of the data was expanded from 1D TS to 2D images to analyze the temporal aspect of the gait data processed using TS imaging methods, specifically recurrence (Rec) plot47. This approach provides two distinct advantages. First, it augments the dimensionality of the feature space, thereby enabling a more comprehensive examination of the gait data. Second, it leverages established CNN-based deep learning architectures for classification. The acceleration and gyroscope data of 24 phases (6 min divided into 15-s intervals) were used to analyze the TS data. After converting TS data from each phase to a matrix form, the converted data were generated in a new imaging, as described further in the subsections on the TS imaging methods.

CNNs

To classify early PDs and controls using the processed TS images, three CNN architectures were employed in the analysis: Residual Neural Network (ResNet)48, Dense Convolutional Network (DenseNet)49, and SqueezeNet50. In this study, the input image size for the CNN models was configured as 224 × 224 pixels (Fig. 3). We employed the ResNet model with 18 layers. We utilized the DenseNet-121 model consisting of four dense blocks with 6, 12, 24, and 16 channels, respectively. SqueezeNet v1.0 was also utilized. These CNN architectures were selected for classification tasks based on the processed TS images.

ResNet

ResNet is a widely recognized convolution-based deep neural network that classifies 2D images. It introduces the concept of a residual block, which addresses the challenge of training deeper neural networks by creating a shortcut connection between the input and output layers. This shortcut connection helps prevent issues such as vanishing or exploding gradient problems during training, thereby facilitating the training of deep neural networks. In ResNet, this skip connection is implemented by summing the output, represented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H(\:\bullet\:\:)$$\end{document}, with the input, denoted as x:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H\left(x\right)=F\left(x\right)+x$$\end{document}

where F represents an activation function with weight parameter w, and bias parameter b, similar to the output of a standard neural network. Adding x in this equation effectively performs identity mapping, transferring the input directly to the output. Achieving this identity mapping within the residual block, especially when dealing with cases where the dimensions of the input and output layers differ, requires special consideration in two distinct cases: (1) when the dimensions of input and output layers are equivalent and (2) when they differ. In the former case, element-wise summation suffices for identity mapping. In the latter case, Tank et al. proposed two solutions: zero padding and the utilization of 1 × 1 convolutional layers48. Zero padding aligns the dimensions by adding zero-valued elements to the input tensor. Conversely, 1 × 1 convolutional layers enable dimension projection, ensuring compatibility between input and output dimensions. These techniques, along with the concept of residual blocks, contribute to the effectiveness of ResNet in training deep neural networks for image-classification tasks.

DenseNet

DenseNet, which was introduced as an enhancement over ResNet49, was devised to improve performance whilst utilizing fewer network parameters. Its primary innovation revolves around dense connectivity, where all preceding feature maps are utilized for identity mapping. The authors’ core motivation stemmed from concerns that the summation operation employed in residual blocks might hinder the efficient flow of information through the network. To address this limitation, they introduced dense connectivity, concatenating all preceding feature maps for identity mapping. Besides dense connectivity, the authors introduced two operations to reduce the number of feature maps: bottleneck layers and compression. Bottleneck layers consist of BN-ReLU-1 × 1 convolution, followed by BN-ReLU-3 × 3 convolution. The inclusion of a 1 × 1 convolution in the bottleneck layer aims to reduce the parameter count of the input-feature maps. Compression is employed to reduce feature map dimensions using a compression factor θ within a transition layer, which acts as a down-sampling layer. The transition layer comprises a 1 × 1 convolutional layer followed by a 2 × 2 average pooling layer, positioned between the dense blocks.

DenseNet effectively mitigates the vanishing gradient problems by utilizing connectivity, which ensures that information from earlier layers is preserved and reused throughout the network. This advantageous feature enables DenseNet to surpass the performance of ResNet whilst maintaining more efficient parameter utilization, resulting in a reduction of computational resource requirements.

SqueezeNet

SqueezeNet aims to create a compact neural network with a reduced number of parameters whilst maintaining high performance, which makes it well-suited for resource-constrained computational environments50. The authors noted that AlexNet had a parameter count of 240 MB, whereas SqueezeNet achieved a remarkable reduction to merely 4.8 MB. The authors introduced three key strategies to achieve this substantial reduction in model size. First, instead of using 3 × 3 convolutions, SqueezeNet relies on 1 × 1 convolutions in certain layers. This change significantly reduces the number of parameters in the network whilst preserving its capacity to learn important features. Second, another strategy involves reducing the number of input channels. The model’s overall parameter count is effectively reduced by decreasing the number of input channels. Third, later in the network architecture, SqueezeNet adopts a down-sampling approach that is strategically placed to ensure that the network generates large activation maps. This design optimizes the model’s performance whilst maintain a low parameter count.

Central to the architecture of SqueezeNet is the fire module, which consists of a squeeze layer (comprising only 1 × 1 convolutions) followed by an expand layer (comprising a combination of 1 × 1 and 3 × 3 convolutions). Notably, the authors designed the squeeze layer to have fewer hyperparameters than the total number in the expanding layer, which aligns with the abovementioned second strategy.

Evaluation index

The performance of the two-group classification model was assessed based on its accuracy, which signifies the ratio of the correctly predicted number of samples to the total number of samples. Specifically, accuracy is a practical evaluation metric for classification problems, which are well-balanced and not skewed, or feature no class imbalance.

To evaluate the performance of classifiers on datasets, a confusion matrix for the binary classification problem under analysis is utilized. For the confusion matrix, four measures, namely “true positive” (TP), “true negative” (TN), “false positive” (FP), and “false negative” (FN), have been reported. TP is an outcome wherein the model correctly predicts the positive class, which are the real early PDs labeled data in the test dataset and are classified as early PDs by training. TN is an outcome wherein the model correctly predicts the negative class, which are the real control-labeled data in the test dataset and are classified as controls through training. FP is an outcome wherein the model incorrectly predicts the positive class, which are the real early PD-labeled data in the test dataset and are classified as controls via training. FN is an outcome wherein the model incorrectly predicts the negative class, which are the real control-labeled data in the test dataset and are classified as early PDs through training.

Precision refers to the proportion of correct identifications, while recall measures the number of positive cases correctly predicted by the classifier out of all the positive cases in the data. This metric is also referred to as the sensitivity. The F1-Score combines precision and recall and is a comprehensive measure that is generally described as the harmonic mean of the two constituent metrics. In essence, the harmonic mean is an alternative approach for calculating the average of values, which is a more suitable method for calculating the average of ratios, such as precision and recall, than the traditional arithmetic mean51,52.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used for testing multivariate normality. Fisher’s exact test, the Mann–Whitney U test (for non-normal data), or the independent t-test (for normal data) was conducted to assess the differences in physical and clinical characteristics between early PDs and controls. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), MATLAB, and Python (Python 3.10, Python Software Foundation). Statistical significance was set at 0.05.

Discussion

The primary finding of the present study is that, in the CNN-algorithm and sensor-based 6MWT-derived classification of early PDs and healthy controls, the gyroscopic vertical component of the lumbar spine (Gyr_X) achieved the highest classification accuracy (83.5%), followed by the gyroscopic vertical component of the thoracic spine (Gyr_X; 83.1%), the acceleration mediolateral component of the right thigh (Acc_Y; 79.5%), the acceleration resultant component of the left thigh (Acc_XYZ; 78.4%), the gyroscopic anteroposterior component of the left arm (Gyr_Z; 76.6%), and the acceleration anteroposterior component of the right arm (Acc_Z; 70.4%).

Recent studies have applied the 6MWT method as a promising tool in gait analysis by utilizing wearable-sensor technology and have explored its potential applications for examining the observability and classifiability of continuous and segment-specific gait patterns over time in diverse populations, including individuals with lung transplants53, multiple sclerosis29,54, lower-limb amputations22,34, and PD33,55 (Table 2). Despite variations in disease groups, the 6MWT shows promise in detecting a decline in motor function and facilitating the management of prolonged gait. This suggests exciting possibilities for further studies to determine the accuracy of the 6MWT in early identification and determination of PD severity29,33. These studies are crucial to advance our understanding and application of the 6MWT.

The research conducted by Borzì et al. using waist-wearable sensors and multi-head CNN techniques for the detection and prediction of FOG during a 6MWT in PD achieved a specificity of 100% in healthy older adult participants33. Bailo et al. extracted gait domain-related metrics, such as rhythm and pace, regularity, intensity, dynamic instability, and asymmetry from lower truncal accelerations and angular velocities, during the 6MWT in participants with mild PD, and reported significant differences compared with healthy controls55. Pedrero-Sánchez et al. applied CNN- and LSTM-based techniques to identify H&Y stages 1–3 in individuals with PD using a single inertial sensor attached to the back of the waist, and achieved a mean accuracy of 99.64% using a two-minute functional assessment38. In another study, they reported accuracy rates of 55–88% across two parametric classifiers for healthy controls and patients with Alzheimer’s and PD on a simple functional mobility test using a smartphone based on CNN technology39.

However, none of these studies focused on classifying early-stage PD using CNN algorithms and components of sensor-based triaxial accelerations and gyroscopic data obtained from the 6MWT. The novelty of our study lies in the use of wearable sensors and CNN algorithms to classify early-stage PD using the 6MWT, which may have significant implications in clinical and daily-life applications. We fitted wearable sensors to each of the six segments during the 6MWT and used a CNN algorithm to determine the classification possibilities of the two groups.

Axial rigidity, increased postural tone, en bloc turning, FOG, and loss of intersegmental flexibility may be evident during gait in PD, particularly in walking and turning gait56–59. Specifically, individuals with early-stage PD experience a reduced range of motion at the hip joint60, asymmetric tremors, decreased coordination6, and reduced postural stability control61 during gait, which worsen with progression to moderate PD62. However, in early-stage PD, as the symptoms are not well defined and can easily be attributed to other conditions, further investigation is required60,61.

Cai et al. suggested that asymmetry and axial features of the trunk and lumbar spine contribute to the early PD classification model for distinguishing healthy controls from individuals with H&Y Stage 1 limitations63. During walking, individuals with early-stage PD can exhibit significant asymmetry because of unilateral parkinsonism-related symptoms, with consequent axial impairments64. In this study, early-stage PD was classified through six body segments at H&Y stages 1 and 2, with similar classification accuracy for the affected and unaffected limb segments, achieving the highest accuracy in the lumbar spine and truncal segments, which is consistent with the results from previous studies. Nonetheless, it is difficult to generalize our results because of the limited evaluability of a single waist sensor29,33. Therefore, asymmetric and unilateral measurements, including those of axial features, should be used whenever possible to detect early-stage PD in clinical and community settings.

Despite asymmetrical basal ganglia degeneration in early-stage PD that may lead to unilateral symptoms, measurement of gait variation between the left and right sides may not be reliable for quantifying motor asymmetry in patients61. Furthermore, as the side on which symptoms will occur is unclear, a comparative analysis of bilateral-limb sensor data is necessary6. Buckley et al. proposed that upper-body acceleration may serve as a biomarker for gait impairment in early-stage PD65. When combined with spatiotemporal information, variables of upper body acceleration provide a more accurate description of the PD gait66 and could be associated with a reduced ability to regulate repetitive steps and strides during gait or control the rhythmic displacements of the upper body during walking65.

Our study revealed that specific components of the limb segments, such as the mediolateral component for the right thigh position, exhibited high classification accuracy. Research on symmetry in the walking domain indicated that measuring gait symmetry in the mediolateral and vertical directions was significantly associated with walking endurance (6MWT), whereas no relationship was identified for balance scales, suggesting that gait symmetry may be primarily associated with the energy efficiency of locomotion, rather than balance55.

Similarly, our study demonstrated that the gyroscopic vertical (Gyr_X) component achieved the highest discrimination accuracy for the lumbar spine (83.5%), followed by the thoracic spine (83.1%). In particular, increased rigidity and signs of akinesia and/or bradykinesia were associated with smaller truncal sway, mainly in the anteroposterior and vertical directions67. With regard to gait and balance, lower truncal acceleration amplitude was associated with lower walking endurance and poorer dynamic balance; therefore, reduced truncal acceleration may result from diminished upper body movements during walking to compensate for lower-limb impairment and maintain balance55.

Our research identified the prominent characteristics of early-stage PD and classification accuracy in individual axial components, specifically in each of the six segments. We determined that considering the three-axis factors together may help identify and pre-screen for gait characteristics that indicate early-stage PD in the 6MWT. Although we do not intend to substitute clinical assessment, this approach could lead to more accurate classification and patient monitoring related to functional capability, and represents a first step towards the development of automated, continuous, and non-invasive functional monitoring in clinical settings38.

Nevertheless, there are some limitations to this study. We conducted our study in the “medication ON state.” Considering both ON and OFF medication states may provide a better understanding of the characteristics of early-stage PD. However, a larger dataset is required to validate the effectiveness of the model. Future studies should consider the following points to improve the accuracy of our model. By using specific intervals (e.g., 15 s), we can compare and analyze the accuracy over time, such as for 2–3 min, and a measurement time of 6MWT can be suggested to account for measurement efficiency53,68. Our study involved a CNN analysis of the entire 6 min, but without distinguishing between straight and turning gaits in the 6MWT, and merely identified schematic frequency patterns for straight and turning gaits. Therefore, in the future, it is necessary to extract data specific to turning and straight gait sections and compare the classification performance using a combination of deep learning techniques, not only CNNs but also various machine learning techniques38,69. Additionally, further analysis of specific biomarkers, such as amplitude and magnitude based on wearable sensors, may provide more information than the identification of the best-performing body segments63.

To further analyze detailed gait characteristics, we identified frequency patterns for the last phase of the 6MWT, including straight and turning gaits, for individuals with early PD and healthy controls (Fig. 6). We identified the highest accuracy in the Gyr_X component, particularly in the lumbar and thoracic spine, where characteristics such as the differences in frequency patterns on the graph during turning and straightforward walking between early PDs and controls (Fig. 6) on the gyroscope data of the x-axis may have influenced the image generation, and affected the accuracy. In future research, it is necessary to perform non-linear analysis to extract relevant features that can identify straight and turning gait patterns of the 6MWT in the domains of bilateral coordination, asymmetry, truncal symptom severity, and variability, and then analyze the correlations between the features. Additionally, it is important to use machine learning techniques to analyze specific biomarkers associated with early-stage PD70. Finally, we only considered patients with early H&Y-stage PD; however, patients with severe symptoms may differ from those analyzed herein. Future studies should include more subjects with intermediate (i.e., H&Y stage 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, and 3) to explore the capability of the model to classify all the early-to-moderate stages of the disease.

Conclusion

Our study confirms the feasibility of classifying two groups by converting 6MWT TS data into images, using CNN algorithms, and identifying which segments and components exhibit optimal accuracy. We propose that 2D imaging for analyzing 1D TS data of indefinite length may provide an opportunity to screen the gait patterns of patients with early-stage PD for primary classification. Furthermore, the classification models were based on information from sensors that can be easily attached to the six segmental locations of the participants in the 6MWT. The use of simple wearable sensors and the brief examination time make it feasible for use in clinical settings. Overall, this work has the potential to improve the accuracy of motor-symptom-severity assessment and classification, and also contribute to the development of more efficient, cost-effective, and reliable experimental and analytical procedures, facilitate early disease detection in clinical settings, and provide objective indicators for the assessment of disease severity2.
