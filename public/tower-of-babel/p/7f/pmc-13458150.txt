Targeting the Ip6k2–Runx2 axis disrupts osteoblast–osteoclast coupling to treat osteoporosis
Authors: Guixing Ma, Yong Chen, Siyuan Cheng, Fen Wang, Yingying Han, Litong Chen, Zhen Ding, Yiran Li, Huiling Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Excessive osteoclast activation disrupts bone homeostasis, contributing to osteoporosis (OP). While Runx2 is known to regulate osteoblast differentiation, its role in osteoclasts is less clear. Using conditional knockout models, we demonstrate that Runx2 directly drives osteoclast differentiation by regulating Ctsk transcription. However, its dual function in both osteoblasts and osteoclasts limits therapeutic potential. We identify Ip6k2 as a Runx2-interacting protein that enhances Runx2-dependent Ctsk transcription specifically in osteoclasts. Genetic deletion of Ip6k2 phenocopies Runx2 loss in osteoclasts, suppressing osteoclastogenesis and increasing bone mass without affecting osteoblast function. Notably, dual deletion of Runx2 and Ip6k2 yields no additive effects, indicating a shared regulatory pathway. Importantly, both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of Ip6k2 protect against estrogen deficiency- and age-induced bone loss. Collectively, our findings position Ip6k2 as a promising osteoclast-specific target and highlight that disruption of the Ip6k2-Runx2 complex may offer an effective strategy for OP treatment.

Introduction

Bone homeostasis is maintained by a tightly regulated balance between osteoblast-mediated bone formation and osteoclast-mediated bone resorption1–3. Disruption of this balance results in metabolic bone disorders, most notably osteoporosis (OP), a prototypical age-related skeletal disorder, which is characterized by decreased bone mass, compromised skeletal integrity, and an elevated risk of fractures4,5.

Emerging evidence has demonstrated that osteoblastogenesis and osteoclastogenesis are tightly coupled processes that are essential for maintaining bone homeostasis6–11. This coupling is first reflected in the spatial association between osteogenic lineage cells and osteoclasts. Emerging evidence suggests that osteoclasts initially carry out bone resorption and subsequently activate osteogenic activity in a sequential manner12. Furthermore, studies in mice have shown that bone surface cells adjacent to osteoclasts are the predominant source of receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (Rankl), which plays a crucial role in regulating osteoclast activity13. Besides, this coupling is partially mediated by Rankl-Rank reverse signaling. A recent study showed that vesicles released by mature osteoclasts carry Rank, which binds to membrane-bound Rankl on osteoblasts and promotes bone formation by activating Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) through the induction of reverse Rankl signaling. Disruption of this reverse signaling impairs the coupling between bone resorption and formation, resulting in a marked reduction in bone mass in mice7. The coupling between osteoblastogenesis and osteoclastogenesis is also reflected in the convergence of key signaling pathways traditionally associated with either cell lineage. Canonical signaling molecules previously believed to be specific to one cell type have now been recognized to function in both. For instance, nuclear factor of activated T cells cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1), classically defined as a master regulator of osteoclast differentiation, has been shown to play essential roles in osteoblast function as well14–16. Similarly, ATF4, originally identified as a crucial transcription factor for osteoblast activity, has also been implicated in osteoclastogenesis17–20. These findings highlight the intricate molecular crosstalk and shared regulatory mechanisms that govern osteoblast and osteoclast differentiation, emphasizing the importance of coordinated signaling in maintaining skeletal integrity. However, this overlap also presents a major challenge: the dual role of many key regulators in both lineages complicates the development of targeted therapeutics. Specifically, modulating these pathways may inadvertently affect both bone formation and resorption16, making the development of safe and selective drugs for OP both difficult and complex.

Runx2 is a pivotal member of the Runx family and is widely recognized as a master regulator of osteoblast differentiation. Studies have shown that global deletion of Runx2 (Runx2−/−) in mice results in perinatal lethality due to the complete absence of bone formation, which is attributed to a failure in osteoblast differentiation21,22. Additionally, conditional deletion of Runx2 in MSCs or osteoblasts leads to similar defective ossification, reinforcing its central function in osteogenesis23–30. Interestingly, osteoclast differentiation is completely absent in the Runx2−/− mice21, and point mutations in the human Runx2 gene have been associated with impaired osteoclastogenesis31. However, the cell-intrinsic role of Runx2 in osteoclastogenesis remains poorly studied. This knowledge gap may be due to the indispensable role of Runx2 in the coupling between osteblastogenesis and osteoclastogenesis. In osteoblasts, Runx2 regulates the expression of multiple factors critical for both bone formation and resorption, including Sp7 (Osterix), Osteocalcin (Ocn), Sclerostin (Sost), Rankl, and Osteoprotegerin (OPG)-a decoy receptor that antagonizes RANKL signaling21–24,26,28,29,32–37.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the role of Runx2 in osteoclast differentiation and function. We hypothesized that Runx2 directly regulates osteoclastogenesis and osteoclast activity, thereby contributing to bone homeostasis. To test this hypothesis, we employed osteoclast-specific Runx2 knockout mice, in vitro osteoclast differentiation assays, and mouse models of osteoporosis induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and ovariectomy (OVX). This integrated approach was designed to define the contribution of Runx2 to osteoclast biology and its potential role in coordinating osteoclast–osteoblast interactions.

Results

Runx2 is highly expressed in osteoclast precursors and is essential for osteoclast differentiation

Osteoclasts originate from the monocyte-macrophage lineage and differentiate in response to macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (Rankl)4,38–40. Activation of Rankl-Rank signaling axis initiates a cascade of intracellular events that culminate in the induction of NFATc1, the master transcription factor essential for driving osteoclastogenesis14,41. This signaling cascade also leads to the upregulation of cathepsin K (CTSK), a lysosomal cysteine protease predominantly expressed in osteoclasts, which plays a critical role in bone resorption by degrading type I collagen and other bone matrix proteins42. Enhanced osteoclastogenesis contributes to the development of OP in conditions such as postmenopausal estrogen deficiency.

To explore the intrinsic role of Runx2 in osteoclastogenesis, we first analyzed its expression profile across tissues and cell types using publicly available bulk and single-cell transcriptomic datasets43. In human tissues, the highest levels of Runx2 expression were observed in hematopoietic stem cells and their immune cell derivatives-including monocytes, which are osteoclast precursors. Interestingly, Runx2 expression in bone tissues enriched for osteoblast lineage cells was significantly lower, although Runx2 is a well-known master transcription factor governing osteoblast differentiation. For example, in fetal calvaria and rib tissue, Runx2 expression level was less than half, or even one-third, of that observed in hematopoietic lineages, including monocytes (Fig. 1a). Single cell transcriptomic data from human samples further supported these findings, showing that Runx2 expression in fibroblasts or mesenchymal cells was approximately half that in monocytes and dendritic cells (Fig. 1b). A similar trend was observed in mouse single-cell datasets44, where Runx2 expression was most enriched in marrow-resident macrophages (Fig. 1c). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) and Western blot (WB) analyses revealed that both mRNA and protein levels of Runx2, along with key osteoclast differentiation markers, including NFATc1, Ctsk, etc., were significantly upregulated during recombinant human-RANKL (rhRANKL)-induced osteoclastogenesis (Fig. 1d–f). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining also demonstrated the dramatic upregulation of Runx2 by rhRANKL treatment (Fig. S1a, b). Notably, treatment with CADD52245, a selective Runx2 inhibitor, markedly suppressed osteoclast differentiation, as evidenced by reduced expression of NFATc1 and Ctsk at both transcript and protein levels (Fig. 1g–i), suggesting a functional requirement for Runx2 in this process. To further define the cell-intrinsic role of Runx2 in osteoclast differentiation, we generated a mouse model with Runx2 conditional knockout in osteoclast precursors (Lyz2-Cre; Runx2fl/fl mice, hereafter referred to as Runx2LKO) by crossing Lyz2-Cre transgenic mice with Runx2fl/fl mice (Fig. 1j). Cre-negative Runx2fl/fl littermates were used as controls (Ctrl). Body weight did not differ significantly between Runx2LKO mice and control mice in either males or females (Fig. 1k). Micro-computed tomography (μCT) analyses of the distal femur and lumbar vertebrae (L4) demonstrated a significant increase in bone mass in both male and female Runx2LKO mice compared to controls (Figs. 1l–r and S1c–f). Three-point bending tests showed that Runx2 deletion significantly enhances the mechanical properties of long bones (Fig. S1g, h).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that Runx2 is highly expressed in osteoclast lineage cells and plays a critical intrinsic role in osteoclastogenesis. Loss of Runx2 impairs osteoclast differentiation and leads to a systemic increase in bone mass.

Runx2 directly regulates Ctsk transcription in osteoclasts

To study how Runx2 modulates osteoclast differentiation, we performed a series of in vivo and in vitro analyses. Consistent with RT-qPCR and WB results, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining of tibial sections demonstrated significantly impaired osteoclast differentiation in Runx2LKO mice compared to controls (Fig. 2a, b). ELISA assay showed a marked decrease in serum collagen type I cross-linked C-telopeptide 1 (CTX-1) levels, a key indicator of osteoclast-mediated bone resorption46 (Fig. 2c). In vitro osteoclastogenesis assays further demonstrated that primary bone marrow monocytes (BMMs) isolated from Runx2LKO mice exhibited significantly impaired differentiation into osteoclasts (Fig. 2d–f). Similarly, shRNA-mediated Runx2 knockdown in wild-type BMMs led to a substantial downregulation of osteoclastogenic markers, including NFATc1 and Ctsk5,14,16,42, at both mRNA and protein levels, as evidenced by RT-qPCR and WB analyses (Fig. 2g–i). Notably, Runx2 knockdown did not affect early osteoclast differentiation, proliferation, or apoptosis, as evidenced by unchanged mRNA expression of genes associated with early differentiation (Csfir), cell proliferation (Ki67, Pcna), and cell apoptosis (Bax). To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which Runx2 regulates osteoclast differentiation, we performed a cross-analysis of osteoclast-related gene sets from the Harmonizome and Gene Ontology (GO) databases with Runx2 target genes from the GTRD database. Ctsk emerged as the only gene shared across all datasets (Fig. 2j). Consistent with this, our previous results demonstrated that Runx2 knockdown or inhibition markedly reduced both Ctsk mRNA and protein expression (Figs. 1g–i and 2g–i). Given that Runx2 is a transcription factor, we hypothesized that Ctsk is a direct transcriptional target of Runx2. Promoter analysis revealed two tandem Runx2-binding motifs located within ~1 kb upstream of the Ctsk transcription start site in humans, chimpanzees, rats, and mice (Fig. 2k), both containing conserved ACCG/AC and TGTGG core sequences (Fig. 2l, m). To validate the functional relevance of these motifs, we generated dual-luciferase reporter constructs harboring the human Ctsk promoter with either wild-type or mutated Runx2-binding sites (Fig. 2n). Runx2 overexpression significantly increased, while knockdown reduced, luciferase activity. Notably, mutations in the conserved binding motifs abolished Runx2 responsiveness (Fig. 2o, p), confirming that Runx2 directly activates Ctsk transcription via these conserved promoter elements.

Given the critical role of osteoclasts in inflammatory responses46, we evaluated whether deletion of Runx2 in osteoclast precursors affects inflammation-induced bone mass loss. To this end, we employed an LPS-induced acute inflammation mouse model46,47. μCT analysis revealed a significant decrease in bone mass in control mice following LPS administration, whereas Runx2LKO mice exhibited a markedly attenuated bone loss (Fig. 2q–t). In line with these findings, TRAP staining of tibial sections and ELISA-based measurement of serum CTX-1 levels showed a marked increase in osteoclast activity in LPS-treated control mice, an effect that was absent in Runx2LKO mice (Fig. 2u–w).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Runx2 promotes osteoclast differentiation and activity by directly regulating Ctsk transcription, and that its deletion in osteoclast precursors efficiently protects against inflammation-induced bone mass loss.

Runx2 coordinates osteoblast–osteoclast coupling by concurrently regulating their differentiation

To further investigate the role of Runx2 in mature osteoclasts, we generated mice with Runx2 deletion in mature osteoclasts using CTSK-Cre (CTSK-Cre; Runx2fl/fl mice, hereafter referred to as Runx2CKO) (Fig. 3a). Cre-negative Runx2fl/fl littermates served as controls (Ctrl). At 3 months of age, Runx2CKO mice exhibited significantly reduced body weight compared to controls (Fig. 3b, c). μCT analysis revealed a marked increase in trabecular bone mass and a dramatic decrease in cortical bone thickness in Runx2CKO mice (Fig. 3d–f, Fig. S2), which was corroborated by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 3g). Importantly, analysis of weight-matched mice indicated that cortical bone thinning in Runx2CKO mice was not closely associated with reduced body weight (Fig. S2d, e). Given that CTSK is expressed in both mature osteoclasts and periosteal stem cells (PSCs)48–50, and Runx2CKO mice exhibited a pronounced decrease in cortical bone thickness (Fig. 3d–f and S2), we next examined whether Runx2 deletion affects PSC differentiation and function. Calcein double labeling revealed a significant reduction in cortical bone formation rate in Runx2CKO mice, with no comparable change in trabecular bone formation (Fig. 3k–n). IHC staining for Ocn and Col1a1, markers of mature osteoblasts, and serum levels of procollagen type 1 amino-terminal propeptide (P1NP) further confirmed impaired cortical osteoblast activity (Fig. 3o, p). To assess osteogenic potential ex vivo, PSCs were isolated from control and Runx2CKO mice. Colony-forming unit fibroblast (CFU-F), colony-forming unit-osteoblast (CFU-OB), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining assays revealed a significant reduction in osteoblast differentiation capacity upon Runx2 deletion (Fig. 3r–v).

Taken together, these findings highlight the critical intrinsic role of Runx2 in osteoclast lineage cells. Combined with its established function as a master transcription factor for osteoblast differentiation, our data suggest that Runx2 orchestrates osteoblast–osteoclast coupling by concurrently regulating the differentiation of both cell types.

Ip6k2 is associated with Runx2 in regulating osteoclastogenesis

Given that Runx2 regulates both osteoblast and osteoclast activity, its dual role poses a challenge for therapeutic targeting in OP. We therefore hypothesized that selectively inhibiting the pro-osteoclastogenic function of Runx2, while preserving its osteogenic activity, could decouple bone resorption from formation, offering a therapeutic strategy for OP. To explore this possibility, we performed transcriptomic profiling of primary BMMs following Runx2 knockdown. Gene enrichment analysis revealed that Runx2 deficiency altered extracellular matrix-related pathways, which are closely linked to osteoclast function (Fig. 4a). Cross-referencing differentially expressed genes with drug-target data from the ChEMBL database51 identified seven candidate genes, among which Ip6k2 was significantly upregulated in response to Runx2 knockdown (Fig. 4b, c). IF staining, WB, and RT-qPCR analyses confirmed that Runx2 knockdown led to a marked increase in Ip6k2 expression and a concomitant decrease in Ctsk expression at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4d–h). Notably, like Runx2, Ip6k2 expression was also induced by RANKL stimulation (Fig. 4i, j). Moreover, both of Runx2 and Ip6k2 were found to be highly expressed in hematopoietic cells of the bone marrow (Fig. S3). Knockdown of Ip6k2 significantly reduced protein levels of both Runx2 and Ctsk, and dramatically downregulated Ctsk mRNA expression, without affecting Runx2 mRNA levels (Fig. 4k–m). Notably, similar to Runx2, Ip6k2 is highly expressed in hematopoietic and macrophage lineages, which give rise to osteoclast precursors (Fig. S3a, b). To assess the role of Ip6k2 in osteoclastogenesis, we generated conditional knockout mice lacking Ip6k2 in osteoclast precursors (Lyz2-Cre; Ip6k2fl/fl mice, hereafter referred to as Ip6k2LKO) (Fig. 4n). Ip6k2 modulation of osteoclastogenesis is mediated by Runx2

We next studied the regulatory relationship between Ip6k2 and Runx2 in osteoclasts. Knockdown of Ip6k2 significantly suppressed Ctsk expression, while ectopic expression of Runx2 fully rescued the Ip6k2 deficiency-induced reduction in Ctsk levels (Fig. 5a, b). In contrast, while Runx2 knockdown markedly downregulated Ctsk expression, overexpression of Ip6k2 failed to restore Ctsk levels (Fig. 5c, d). These results suggest that Ip6k2 functions upstream of Runx2 to promote osteoclastogenesis. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that Ip6k2 knockdown did not alter Runx2 mRNA levels, whereas Ctsk mRNA expression was drastically reduced (Fig. 4k–m), suggesting that Ip6k2 regulates Runx2 post-transcriptionally, potentially by modulating its activity or protein stability. IF staining showed clear co-localization of Ip6k2 and Runx2 (Fig. 5e), and co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays confirmed a physical interaction between the two proteins (Fig. 5f, g). Structural modeling using AlphaFold predicted that Ip6k2 may interact with Runx2 to enhance its DNA-binding capacity, possibly through cooperation with transcriptional cofactors such as Fli1 or Erg52. To validate these findings in vivo, we generated a conditional double-knockout mouse model lacking both Ip6k2 and Runx2 in osteoclast precursors (Lyz2-Cre; Runx2fl/fl; Ip6k2fl/fl mice, hereafter referred to as Runx2/Ip6k2LKO). μCT analysis revealed that deletion of either Runx2 or Ip6k2 alone, or in combination, resulted in a comparable increase in bone mass, with no additive effect observed in the double-knockout mice (Fig. 5l–o). These data further support the conclusion that Ip6k2 and Runx2 function within the same linear signaling pathway. TRAP staining of tibial sections (Fig. 5p, q) and serum CTX-1 levels (Fig. 5r) consistently demonstrated comparably impaired osteoclast activity in all knockout groups.

Targeting Ip6k2 disrupts osteoblast–osteoclast coupling to increase bone mass in mice

To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of Ip6k2 as a target for OP, we employed an ovariectomy (OVX)-induced OP mouse model to mimic estrogen deficiency–associated bone mass loss. μCT analysis showed that Ip6k2 deletion in osteoclast precursors significantly protected against OVX-induced bone mass loss (Fig. S4a–d). Consistently, TRAP staining and serum CTX-1 levels revealed that osteoclast activity was not further enhanced in Ip6k2LKO mice following OVX (Fig. S4e–g).

To assess the role of Ip6k2 in mature osteoclasts, we generated mice with conditional deletion of Ip6k2 using CTSK-Cre (CTSK-Cre; Ip6k2fl/fl mice, hereafter referred to as Ip6k2CKO) (Fig. S5a). Ip6k2CKO mice displayed normal body weight (Fig. S3b) but exhibited a marked increase in trabecular bone mass (Fig. S5c–f) and a significant reduction in osteoclast activity compared to controls, both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. S5g–l). Unlike Runx2-deficient mice, cortical bone thickness in Ip6k2-deficient mice remained unchanged (Fig. S5f). Calcein double labeling confirmed that the cortical bone formation rate was unaffected (Fig. S5m–p). Furthermore, IHC staining for Ocn and Col1a1 (Fig. S5q, r), along with serum P1NP levels (Fig. S5s), showed no alterations in the number or activity of mature osteoblasts. CFU-F and CFU-OB assays, along with ALP staining for osteogenesis and Oil red O staining for adipogenesis using primary BMSCs, further demonstrated that Ip6k2 knockdown did not impair osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs (Fig. S5t–z). These findings suggest that Ip6k2-mediated modulation of Runx2 activity is specific to the osteoclast lineage.

Together, these results demonstrate that targeting Ip6k2 enhances bone mass accrual primarily by suppressing osteoclast activity via the Runx2/Ctsk signaling axis, without compromising osteoblast function. Thus, Ip6k2 represents a promising therapeutic target to circumvent the limitations of directly targeting Runx2 in bone anabolic treatments.

Pharmacological inhibition of Ip6k2 is a promising strategy for OP treatment

Given that Ip6k2 is a druggable target, we next evaluated the bone-protective effects of UNC7467 (UNC), a Ip6k2 inhibitor53. IHC staining and RT-qPCR analyses confirmed that Ip6k2 and Runx2 were more highly expressed in osteoclast lineage cells compared to osteoblast lineage cells, both in human and mice, with Ip6k2 showing particularly prominent expression (Figs. 6a–c and S6a–c). WB analysis demonstrated that UNC7467 treatment led to a dose-dependent decrease in Ip6k2, Runx2, NFATc1, and Ctsk protein levels (Fig. 6d, e). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that UNC7467 did not affect Runx2 mRNA expression but significantly reduced Ctsk mRNA levels (Fig. 6f). The results further demonstrated that UNC-mediated inhibition of IP6K2 significantly suppresses osteoclast differentiation in human bone marrow mononuclear cells (hBMMs) without affecting osteogenic differentiation in human bone marrow stromal cells (hBMSCs) (Fig. 6g–i). This decoupling strategy highlights the potential for targeted therapies that selectively inhibit osteoclastogenesis while preserving osteoblast differentiation. To assess the therapeutic potential of Ip6k2 inhibition, we examined the effects of UNC7467 in an aging-associated OP model. 18-month-old wild-type C57BL/6 mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of UNC7467 for 6 weeks (Fig. 6j). No significant differences in body weight were observed between UNC7467 and control groups (Fig. 6k). UNC treatment significantly inhibited the expression of Ip6k2 and Runx2 in osteoclasts, along with reduced CTSK levels (Fig. S6c–f). Given the established role of IP6K2 in renal phosphate homeostasis, we examined whether UNC treatment affects renal function in mice54. No apparent renal toxicity was observed at this dose, as evidenced by normal histology and unaltered serum urea nitrogen, creatinine, and phosphate levels and Ip7 level (Fig. S6g–k). μCT analysis showed that UNC7467 treatment significantly increased trabecular bone mass by approximately 50% (Fig. 6l–n). TRAP staining and CTX-1 serum levels indicated a marked reduction in osteoclast activity following UNC7467 administration (Fig. 6o–q). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that Runx2 orchestrates bone remodeling by simultaneously promoting osteoblast and osteoclast differentiation, thereby maintaining bone formation-resorption coupling. Discussion

Our study provides compelling evidence that both Runx2 and Ip6k2 play critical intrinsic roles in promoting osteoclast differentiation and identifies the Ip6k2-Runx2-Ctsk signaling axis as a promising target for OP treatment. While Runx2 is a well-established transcription factor essential for osteoblastogenesis, our findings demonstrate its previously underappreciated involvement in osteoclastogenesis. This perspective sheds light on the mechanism underlying impaired osteoclastogenesis in Runx2-deficient mice21, thereby expanding our understanding of Runx2’s multifaceted role in bone biology and homeostasis.

Mechanistically, we show that Runx2 directly regulates Ctsk, a hallmark gene for osteoclasts whose deletion leads to impaired osteoclast function42. Both in vitro and in vivo analyses confirmed that Runx2 binds to the Ctsk gene promoter, and mutation of the Runx2-binding site abrogates Ctsk expression. These findings not only explain the elevated bone mass in Runx2Lyz2 mice but may also clarify why Ctsk expression is observed in PSCs, despite its initial identification as a marker of mature osteoclasts49. Later research has shown that Ctsk is also expressed in PSCs, making it a specific marker for cortical bone48. Our CTSK-Cre-driven Runx2 knockout mouse model supports this by showing severe impairments in osteoclast function and a concurrent decrease in cortical bone thickness.

Our results suggest that Runx2 orchestrates the coupling of osteoblast and osteoclast differentiation, contributing to coordinated bone remodeling. This dual regulatory capacity, shared with other transcription factors such as ATF4 and NFATc114–20, points to a conserved mechanism for maintaining bone homeostasis. Evolutionarily, such a mechanism may serve as a safeguard to prevent catastrophic skeletal defects. However, this same coupling imposes significant limitations on targeting these factors therapeutically in OP, as inhibiting their activity affects both bone resorption and formation. Consequently, targeted therapies directed at these factors seem to have limited clinical value16. To circumvent this, we identified Ip6k2 as a Runx2-interacting protein that enhances its transcriptional activity specifically in osteoclasts. Interestingly, Ip6k2’s effects on osteoclastogenesis are entirely Runx2-dependent. Ip6k2 emerges as a viable, targetable factor53,55–58. Our findings show that Ip6k2 expression is markedly enriched in osteoclast lineage cells relative to osteoblast lineage cells and that its inhibition spares osteoblast differentiation. This selective action offers a compelling strategy to bypass Runx2’s dual role-suppressing its function in osteoclasts while preserving its anabolic role in osteoblasts-thus enabling targeted bone mass enhancement.

Interestingly, we observed that Runx2 knockdown induces a compensatory upregulation of Ip6k2 expression; however, this increase fails to rescue the impaired osteoclast differentiation because Ip6k2-mediated regulation of osteoclastogenesis is Runx2-dependent as demonstrated in this study. This finding suggests that Runx2 may directly or indirectly regulate Ip6k2 expression. Further investigation of the regulatory relationship may provide insights into osteoclast biology and inform the development of therapeutic strategies targeting bone resorption.

In addition, using transgenic mouse models, we found that deletion of either Ip6k2 or Runx2 in osteoclast precursors leads to reduced bone resorption and increased bone mass. Notably, these effects exhibited a significant sex difference-although both sexes showed similar trends, male mice displayed a markedly stronger response in both genetic models. This observation indicates that the Ip6k2-Runx2 signaling axis exerts sex-dependent effects on bone homeostasis and suggests a potential role for sex hormones in modulating this pathway. Elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms will be an important direction for future studies.

Additionally, our study supports the therapeutic relevance of targeting Ctsk, a validated anti-resorptive strategy. While previous attempts using Ctsk-neutralizing antibodies like odanacatib were halted due to off-target effects9, our data suggest that targeting Ip6k2 suppresses Ctsk expression in a cell-type–specific manner via Runx2, which may minimize adverse effects. Compared to traditional anti-resorptive therapies, the CTSK inhibitor odanacatib was shown to suppress bone resorption without reducing osteoclast numbers. However, its clinical development was halted due to an increased incidence of off-target cerebrovascular events in treated patients9. In contrast, our study demonstrates that targeting Ip6k2 can inhibit bone resorption without compromising osteoblast activity. Notably, Ip6k2 exerts its effect by suppressing Ctsk expression, aligning with previous findings. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of the Ip6k2-Runx2-Ctsk signaling axis as a strategy for osteoporosis treatment.

Among the three Ip6k isoforms, Ip6k2 is the most evolutionarily distinct56,59. Although partial compensatory effects by Ip6k1 and Ip6k3 cannot be ruled out, our in vivo data clearly demonstrate that Ip6k2 deficiency alone is sufficient to enhance bone mass, validating its central role in osteoclast regulation. Current inhibitors generally target all Ip6k isoforms, but the development of Ip6k2-specific inhibitors could offer more selective and safer therapeutic options60. Given Ip6k2’s additional critical roles in tumorigenesis and metastasis56,61–63, its inhibition may also yield benefits beyond bone diseases.

Indeed, pharmacological inhibition of Ip6k2 using UNC746753 significantly improved bone mass in aged mice without impairing osteoblast function. These findings challenge the traditional notion that certain targets are “undruggable,” suggesting that deeper mechanistic insights can uncover therapeutic strategies. Basic research, therefore, remains a cornerstone for clinical drug development.

In vivo experiments using Ip6k2 inhibitors, including UNC and other pharmacological agents, did not reveal significant systemic toxicity or apparent off-target effects in our model. However, the broader consequences of long-term or chronic Ip6k2 inhibition, particularly in non-osteoclast tissues, remain to be determined. Further studies assessing the long-term safety profile of Ip6k2 inhibition will be essential for the development of Ip6k2-targeted therapies, with careful evaluation of potential adverse effects to ensure both efficacy and safety.

In conclusion, our study identifies Runx2 and Ip6k2 as critical regulators of osteoclast differentiation. While Runx2’s dual role poses a therapeutic challenge, targeting the Ip6k2-Runx2 complex enables selective suppression of osteoclast activity without affecting osteoblast function. This uncoupling of bone resorption from bone formation via the Ip6k2-Runx2-Ctsk axis represents an osteoblast-sparing therapeutic approach for OP.

Methods

Animal study

Genetically engineered Runx2fl/fl and Ip6k2fl/fl mice were obtained from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. (Shanghai, China). To generate conditional knockout models, transgenic mice expressing Cre recombinase under the control of the Lyz2 or Ctsk promoters (Lyz2-Cre and Ctsk-Cre, respectively) were crossed with Runx2fl/fl and/or Ip6k2fl/fl mice. The resulting genotypes included Lyz2Cre; Runx2fl/fl (hereafter referred to as Runx2LKO), CtskCre; Runx2fl/fl (Runx2CKO), Lyz2Cre; Ip6k2fl/fl (Ip6k2LKO), CtskCre; Ip6k2fl/fl (Ip6k2CKO), and Lyz2Cre; Ip6k2fl/fl; Runx2fl/fl (Runx2/Ip6k2LKO). Littermates lacking Cre expression were used as control (Ctrl) mice. All strains were maintained on a C57BL/6 genetic background.

To pharmacologically inhibit Ip6k2 activity, 18-month-old mice were administered UNC746753 (5 mg/kg body weight) via daily intraperitoneal injection for 6 weeks. Age-matched control mice received vehicle (DMSO) injections. Mice were housed in groups of five per cage with ad libitum access to food and water under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 20-24 °C; standard 12-h light/dark cycle).

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical guidelines. Protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the Southern University of Science and Technology (SUSTech-JY202509040).

Isolation and culture of mouse BMMs and BMSCs

Bone marrow cells were isolated from the femurs and tibiae of 3-month-old male or female mice under sterile conditions. After removal of surrounding muscle tissue and bone ends, bone marrow was flushed with α-MEM (Gibco) containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). The cell suspension was passed through a 70-μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL recombinant murine M-CSF. Cells were plated in dishes and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ for 3 days. Non-adherent cells were harvested as BMMs, and adherent cells were maintained as BMSCs for downstream assays.

Cell culture and transfection

Primary bone marrow monocytes (BMMs) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

For gene silencing experiments, cells were transfected with small interfering RNA (siRNAs) targeting Runx2 or Ip6k2 using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, #13778100), following the manufacturer’s protocol. A non-targeting siRNA was used as a negative control (siNC). Sequences and other details of the siRNAs are listed in Table S1.

For gene overexpression studies, cells were transfected with plasmids encoding Runx2 or Ip6k2 using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, #L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Micro-computerized tomography (μCT) analysis

Fixed femurs were analyzed using micro-computed tomography (μCT) with the SkyScan 1172 system (Bruker MicroCT, Kontich, Belgium), following established protocols64. Scans were performed at 60 kV and 100 μA, with an exposure time of 926 ms and an isotropic voxel resolution of 10 μm. All scanning and data analysis procedures adhered to the guidelines and standardized nomenclature recommended by the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research (ASBMR).

For trabecular bone analysis, a region of interest (ROI) measuring 1.5 mm in length was selected, starting 0.5 mm proximal to the distal femoral growth plate. For cortical bone assessment, a 1.0 mm ROI was defined at the mid-diaphysis of the femur. Quantitative parameters, including bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), Trabecular Separation (Tb.Sp), Trabecular Number (Tb.N), Cortical Porosity (Cort.Po), and cortical thickness (Cort.Th.), were calculated using CTAn software (Bruker MicroCT).

Histological evaluation

Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C overnight, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at a thickness of 5 μm. The sections were then deparaffinized, rehydrated, and processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, immunohistochemistry staining (IHC), and immunofluorescence (IF) staining following standard protocols.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and qPCR analysis

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed following established protocols65. Briefly, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min, and the reaction was quenched by the addition of glycine to a final concentration of 125 mM for 5 min. Cells were washed twice with cold PBS, collected, and lysed in SDS lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Chromatin was sheared by sonication to an average fragment size of 200-500 bp, which was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. After centrifugation, the supernatant containing soluble chromatin was diluted tenfold in ChIP dilution buffer. An aliquot (1-5%) was saved as input DNA. Equal amounts of chromatin were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibody or normal IgG control with rotation. Immune complexes were captured by incubation with protein A/G magnetic beads for 2 h at 4 °C. Beads were sequentially washed with low-salt wash buffer, high-salt wash buffer, LiCl wash buffer, and TE buffer to reduce nonspecific binding. Chromatin was eluted from the beads, and cross-links were reversed by incubation at 65 °C overnight. Samples were treated with RNase A and proteinase K, and DNA was purified using phenol-chloroform extraction. Purified DNA was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) using SYBR Green chemistry. Enrichment was calculated as a percentage of the input. Primer sequences used for ChIP-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) analyses

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and further purified with a total RNA isolation kit (TransGen, ER601-01). RNA concentration and purity were assessed by measuring the A260/A280 ratio using a NanoDrop 3000 spectrophotometer. For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using a reverse transcription kit (Beyotime, D7190) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Beyotime, D7260) on a real-time PCR system. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔCt method. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech, and their sequences are listed in Table S2.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Protein extraction and Western blot analysis were performed as previously described66. Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Sigma, R0278), and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Cwbio, CW0014) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of total protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to internal loading controls. A complete list of primary and secondary antibodies used is provided in Table S3.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Tissues were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval using a citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 58 °C for 16 h. For IHC staining, sections were incubated with primary antibodies or control IgG, followed by detection using the EnVision+ System-HRP (DAB) kit (Dako North America Inc., K401111-2), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Whole-slide images were acquired using a Leica Aperio VERSA scanner.

For IF staining, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Confocal images were obtained using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope. Staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ software, with values normalized to the control group.

TRAP staining for osteoclast

Tibial sections were fixed in 4% PFA, decalcified, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. Sections were then deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining using a commercially available kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions and previously described protocols67. Briefly, tissue sections were incubated in TRAP staining solution at 37 °C for 30 min. After rinsing, TRAP-positive multinucleated osteoclasts were visualized as red-stained cells under a light microscope. Quantification was performed by calculating the ratio of TRAP-positive area within predefined regions of interest (ROIs) using ImageJ software. For osteoclast analysis, the region of interest (ROI) was specifically defined within the secondary spongiosa.

Isolation and culture of human BMMs and BMSCs

Human bone marrow aspirates were obtained with informed consent under institutional ethical approval (Table S4). Protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the Drum Tower Hospital Affiliated (Nanjing, China) (202119203).

Mononuclear cells (MNCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation (Ficoll-Paque PLUS, 1.077 g/mL), washed twice with PBS, and counted. For BMSC culture, MNCs were seeded at 1-2 × 10⁵ cells/cm² in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin on flasks. Non-adherent cells were removed after 48-72 h, and adherent BMSCs were expanded, with medium changes every 3-4 days. Cells were passaged at 80-90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA.

For BMM differentiation, MNCs were plated at 1-2 × 10⁶ cells/mL on plates in α-MEM containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin for 2 days. Non-adherent cells seeded to a new plate contain α-MEM containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 25-50 ng/mL recombinant human M-CSF. Medium was refreshed every 3 days.

In vitro osteoclast differentiation assay

Primary BMMs were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured overnight in proliferation medium to allow for cell adherence. Osteoclast differentiation was induced by culturing the cells in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), and 20 ng/mL receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL). The culture medium was replaced every 2-3 days, and the differentiation was continued for 7-10 days. At the endpoint, cells were fixed and stained for TRAP to assess osteoclast formation and multinucleated cell development.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

HEK293 cells were co-transfected with a firefly luciferase reporter plasmid containing either the wild-type (WT) or mutant (Mut) Ctsk -1K promoter, along with a Renilla luciferase plasmid as an internal control. 48 h post transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (GenePharma), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity to account for transfection efficiency.

Isolation and detection of 5-IP7

The purification, separation, and visualization of cellular 5-diphosphoinositol pentakisphosphate (5-IP7) were performed as previously described68. Briefly, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed with perchloric acid to extract inositol phosphates. Phosphate-rich metabolites were then enriched using titanium dioxide (TiO₂) beads. The enriched samples were separated by 35.5% native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), and phosphate-containing metabolites, including IP6 and 5-IP7, were visualized using toluidine blue staining.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for P1NP and CTX-1

Mouse serum samples were collected, and the concentrations of procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (P1NP) and C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX-1) were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions and previously established protocols69.

Calcein double labeling and dynamic bone histomorphometry

Dynamic bone formation was assessed by calcein double labeling as previously described. Briefly, animals were intraperitoneally injected with calcein (10 mg/kg body weight) at 10 days before sacrifice, followed by calcein at 3 days before sacrifice. Fluorochromes were dissolved in sterile saline and filtered prior to use. At the experimental endpoint, animals were euthanized, and femurs or tibiae were harvested, fixed in 70% ethanol, dehydrated, and embedded in methyl methacrylate. Undecalcified sections (5–10 μm thick) were prepared using a microtome and mounted without staining. Images were acquired under identical exposure conditions. Mineral apposition rate (MAR), mineralizing surface (MS/BS), and bone formation rate (BFR/BS) were quantified using standard histomorphometric software according to established guidelines. The regions of interest (ROI) included the endosteal surface of cortical bone and the trabecular bone.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays

Co-IP assays were conducted as previously described70. Briefly, cells were lysed in immunoprecipitation (IP) lysis buffer (pH 7.4) containing 25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, and 5% glycerol (Thermo Fisher), supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were incubated on ice for 10 min and then centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibodies, followed by incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads for 1 h at room temperature. Immune complexes were captured using a DynaMag™-2 magnet (Thermo Fisher) and washed three times with cold IP lysis buffer. Bound proteins were eluted by resuspending the beads in 60 μL of 1× SDS loading buffer and heating at 95 °C for 5 min. Eluted samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed by Western blotting.

Structure predictions

The structural predictions of the Ip6k2-Runx2-Erg or Ip6k2-Runx2-Fli1 complexes were performed using AlphaFold3. The resulting protein complex models were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL (Schrödinger, LLC) and UCSF Chimera.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups. IF, IHC, and histological assessments were performed and evaluated in a double-blinded manner. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (version 9.5.0). We assumed that all samples were normally distributed. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, while multiple group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc tests. When analyzing two variables, we used two-way ANOVA to assess both main effects and their interactions. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (see Source Data file).
