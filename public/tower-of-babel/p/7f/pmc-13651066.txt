Deep progressive learning reconstruction for fast and low-dose whole-body PET scans in an integrated PET/MR system
Authors: Jie Ding, Yihong Yang, Zhe Wang, Haiyan Wang, Na Qi, Zirong Zhou, Xing Chen, Zhiwen You, Chen Xi, Zheng Qian, Lingzhi Hu, Jianmin Yuan, Jun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPET/MR combines molecular and functional imaging but faces challenges such as prolonged scans, noise from reduced tracer activity, and suboptimal reconstruction methods (e.g., OSEM). Deep learning techniques, such as deep progressive learning (DPL), show promise in enhancing low-tracer activity PET/CT imaging but remain understudied for PET/MR. As MR acquisition continues to accelerate through technological advances, adaptive PET reconstruction methods will become increasingly important for maintaining overall throughput in integrated PET/MR protocols. This study evaluates DPL on a uPMR790 PET/MR system to determine its potential for reducing injected activity or scan durations in whole-body [18F]FDG PET/MR while preserving image quality.MethodsIn our study, we included 115 patients for whole-body PET/MR examinations, of whom 100 received a full injected activity of [18F]FDG, and 15 received injected activity with half the activity. The list mode PET data from the patients were reorganized to reconstruct PET raw data for each bed position for 420, 210, 140, and 105 s, simulating 1/n (n = 1, 2, 3, 4) of the acquisition time. PET reconstructions were performed using two different methods: ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) and DPL [divided into three levels (1, 2, 3)]. In terms of subjective assessment, we conducted a five-point Likert scale visual analysis. For quantitative assessment, we measured the standardized uptake value (SUV) and assessed image quality using the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), contrast, contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), coefficient of variation (COV), and target-to-background ratio (TBR).ResultsAccording to subjective visual assessment methods, DPL scores were significantly higher than OSEM, with DPL1 achieving the highest scores. The advantage of DPL1 became more pronounced with shorter reconstruction times. In both the full injected activity and half injected activity groups, at one-quarter of the reconstruction time, the average Likert visual scores for OSEM and DPL1 were 2.36 vs. 4.01 in the full injected activity group and 1.23 vs. 3.21 in the half injected activity group, respectively (P < 0.01). Compared to the OSEM group, the DPL1 group exhibited significantly improved image quality with markedly reduced noise; the SNR, CNR, contrast, COV, and TBR values were all significantly higher than those of OSEM. These gains were still observable in obese patients within the DPL1 group. There were no significant differences in the lesion SUVmax and SUVmean measured between the OSEM and DPL1 groups (all P > 0.05); however, lesion volumes measured from DPL1 were smaller than those in the OSEM group.ConclusionsDPL reconstruction suggests that it may be feasible to reduce the administered activity of [18F]FDG to half or decrease the scanning time to one-quarter in whole-body PET/MR imaging, while maintaining image quality. This approach is also advantageous for obese patients. However, further clinical task-based validation is needed before routine implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02568-w.Keywords: PET/MR, Deep progressive learning, Low dose, Image quality

Background

Positron emission tomography (PET) is a non-invasive molecular imaging technique extensively utilized in oncology, neurology, and cardiology [1, 3]. Instruments combining PET with magnetic resonance (MR) imaging have been assembled for human use, potentially offering superior diagnostic performance in specific clinical and research applications compared to PET/computed tomography (CT) [4]. The primary advantages of PET/MR over PET/CT include enhanced soft tissue contrast resolution, the ability for multiplanar image acquisition, and functional MR imaging capabilities. However, PET/MR is characterized by longer scan durations and lower patient throughput compared to PET/CT, with additional complexities in extracting PET data attenuation correction information and image reconstruction.

High-quality imaging and precise quantification are paramount for the clinical application of PET/MR. Recent technological advancements in scanner hardware and image reconstruction algorithms have significantly enhanced PET image quality [5]. These improvements facilitate the reduction of radioactive tracer dosage and scan duration, thereby increasing patient throughput, reducing scan costs, and minimizing radiation exposure. However, lowering radioactive tracer dosage and scan time impairs the detection of PET annihilation events, leading to reduced diagnostic image quality, increased data noise, and potential artifacts in reconstructed images [6].

Currently, the ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm stands as the predominant method for PET reconstruction [7]. A critical parameter in the OSEM algorithm is the number of iterations. However, a significant drawback of this algorithm is the rapid escalation of image noise with an increase in iteration numbers in the case of a high number of iterations. As a countermeasure, images are often subjected to post-smoothing using additional filters. While a variety of post-processing techniques, including Gaussian filtering, Metz filtering, wavelet transformations [8], non-local means [9, 10], and block-matching [11] and 3D filtering [12], have been employed to reduce noise in PET images, their effectiveness is limited in terms of balancing noise reduction and maintaining lesion detectability. Simple post-processing operations such as denoising are insufficient to restore the reduced image quality to the standard full-count PET imaging. Consequently, the use of sophisticated deep learning techniques is increasingly becoming prevalent in enhancing PET image reconstruction.

Over recent years, deep learning methods have achieved significant success in image recognition, segmentation, registration, super-resolution, and image denoising, demonstrating their potential in converting low-dose to full injected activity images in various research settings [13–16]. However, as a data-driven approach, deep learning not only requires substantial data but also is time-consuming. Furthermore, direct learning poses challenges when there is a significant disparity between the input and output images.

To address this, recent advancements have led researchers to develop a progressive learning strategy. This approach involves a deep progressive learning (DPL) reconstruction method that integrates two pre-trained neural network models into an iterative process [17, 19, 23]. The network design in DPL incorporates a feedback structure, which significantly reduces the number of parameters required. The training data for this method were derived from the uEXPLORER PET scanner [17]. Studies have shown that this technique can reduce noise and enhance contrast in low-dose [18F]FDG PET images [18–20]. However, most current research on deep learning-based reconstruction methods has been focused on PET/CT scanners, with scant reports on PET/MR reconstruction methods. There is limited reporting on the reconstruction methods of PET in whole-body PET/MR imaging. The prolonged duration of clinical PET/MR scans is currently limited primarily by the extended acquisition time required for multi-sequence MR imaging, rather than by PET acquisition (which in modern scanners can be completed in 5–10 min for a whole-body study). Nevertheless, as accelerated MR acquisition techniques (e.g., compressed sensing, parallel imaging, deep learning–based reconstruction) continue to mature, the relative contribution of PET to total scan time will increase, making PET acceleration increasingly relevant in future integrated protocols. In the present study, DPL validation on PET/MR serves as a prospective investigation to establish feasibility and characterize performance ahead of this anticipated shift. Consequently, we employed the uPMR790 PET/MR machine (United Imaging, China) to validate the DPL reconstruction method using both simulated and real low-dose data, comparing it against the OSEM method. The ultimate goal is to determine whether the DPL reconstruction method may help reduce the tracer injection dosage or shorten scan time in whole-body [18F]FDG PET/MR examinations.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study enrolled 115 consecutive patients with known or suspected malignant tumors (female/male: 56/59; age range: 25–94 years), who underwent clinical [18F]FDG PET/MR examinations at Shanghai East Hospital from August 3 to December 15, 2023. The detailed distribution of primary tumor types for the enrolled patients is summarized in Supplemental Table 1. The imaging was performed using the uPMR790 PET/MR system from United Imaging Healthcare Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China. Their demographic and clinical data are detailed in Table 1. All patients were required to fast for at least six hours and had their blood glucose levels measured before the injection of [18F]FDG to ensure levels were ≤ 10 mmol/L. Among these, 100 patients were administered a body weight-based full injected activity of [18F]FDG, while the remaining 15 patients were administered half the dose. The two cohorts serve complementary purposes: the simulated cohort (n = 100) allows for a controlled evaluation of DPL across multiple dose-equivalent levels using the same patient data, thereby eliminating inter-patient variability; conversely, the real half injected activity cohort (n = 15) serves to validate whether the simulated results translate accurately to actual low-injection-activity conditions, where biodistribution and counting statistics may differ. The study obtained approval from the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai East Hospital (IRB protocol 2020085). The necessity for written informed consent was waived for the simulated group, while patients in the real group were required to sign informed consent forms. PET/MR acquisition

Following [18F]FDG injection, patients rested for 60 min prior to image acquisition. All patients in the study underwent comprehensive whole-body PET/MRI scans…All patients in the study underwent comprehensive whole-body PET/MRI scans, encompassing five-bed positions from the head to the thighs. Due to the extended acquisition time required for abdominal and pelvic MR imaging, to maintain consistency and facilitate subsequent reconstruction, each bed position was scanned for seven minutes, incorporating respiratory gating. The total duration of a single scan session approached 40 min. PET attenuation correction was based on MRI sequences.

PET reconstruction

All PET data were collected and stored in list mode format, allowing for flexible reconstruction using various methods. The acquired data were reconstructed using both OSEM and DPL algorithms. The OSEM algorithm utilized three iterations and 20 subsets, incorporating time-of-flight (TOF) technology to improve temporal resolution. Images were reconstructed with a matrix size of 192 × 192, a slice thickness of 2.8 mm, and a field of view of 600 × 600 mm. Standard corrections for scatter, randoms, dead-time, attenuation, and normalization were incorporated in the reconstruction process.

The DPL algorithm employs two networks: a convolutional neural network for denoising (CNN-DE) capable of removing noise from input images, and an enhancement network (CNN-EH) that maps low-convergence images to high-convergence ones. Both CNN-DE and CNN-EH are trained based on the designed feedback network (FB-Net). The result of the third branch is considered to be the output of the network, which is a denoised/enhanced intermediate PET image for the next iteration. Details regarding the algorithm design, network training, and testing can be found in previously published literature [17]. The DPL algorithm uses the same field of view, matrix size, and slice thickness as the OSEM.

It is important to note that DPL is a scanner-integrated iterative reconstruction-level method, not a post-processing filter. It embeds the pre-trained neural networks directly inside the expectation-maximization (EM) iteration loop. The numbered DPL strength levels (DPL1, DPL2, DPL3) represent discrete, user-selectable parameter settings that modulate the magnitude of the neural network’s intervention. Higher numerical designations correspond to progressively stronger regularization by the deep learning model. In this study, DPL1 prioritizes the preservation of original count statistics and fine structural detail, while DPL2 and DPL3 apply more aggressive denoising. The list mode PET data of patients were rearranged to reconstruct PET raw data for each bed position for 420, 210, 140, and 105 s, simulating 1/n (n = 1, 2, 3, 4) of the acquisition time and corresponding reduced injection activity. For each patient, 16 series of images were generated and compared. These include OSEM_full, OSEM_1/2, OSEM_1/3, OSEM_1/4, DPL(1,2,3)_full, DPL(1,2,3)_1/2, DPL(1,2,3)_1/3, and DPL(1,2,3)_1/4. The same method of data reconstruction was applied to patients in the real group receiving half injected activity injections.

Network training and testing

One hundred patient data were collected for network training and testing. The injected dose of [18F]FDG was 3.7 ~ 4.4 MBq/kg, while the acquisition time was fixed at 900 s for whole-body PET imaging with uEXPLORER. Benefits from its ultra-high sensitivity, uEXPLORER has demonstrated its ability to provide clinically acceptable images for a scan as short as 60 s and good images for a scan of more than 180 s. The minimum and maximum values for the cyclical learning rate were 1e-5 and 1e-4, respectively. The process of DPL network training and testing was showed in Fig. 1. The details of network design and algorithm implementation could be found in our previous work 17.

Image analysis

This study conducted image quality assessments on an advanced workstation (uWS-MI, United Imaging Healthcare). Qualitative analysis of PET image quality was performed by four nuclear medicine physicians, including two senior (ZH and WHY,>10 years of experience) and two mid-level physicians (DJ and QN, 5–8 years of experience). The evaluation of quantitative image quality was conducted by another experienced nuclear medicine physician (YYH).

Subjective assessment

The reading order of each PET image series was randomly assigned by an independent operator. Additionally, the readers were blinded to the patients’ clinical histories and the reconstruction algorithms used. Image quality was independently evaluated from two aspects: lesion contrast and overall image noise, using a 5-point Likert scale (1, poor; 2, subpar; 3, moderate; 4, good; and 5, excellent). A Likert score of ≥ 3 was defined as clinically acceptable. The scoring criteria were as follows: 5 points: Excellent image quality with minimal noise and superior display of anatomical details; 4 points: Good image quality with low noise, clear visualization of anatomical structures, with all structures distinctly discernible without impacting the observation and diagnosis of lesions; 3 points: Moderate image quality with medium noise, average display of anatomical structures, minor impact on lesion observation but sufficient for clinical diagnosis; 2 points: Subpar image quality with significant noise, incomplete visualization of anatomical structures, poor display of anatomical details, affecting the observation of lesions; 1 point: Poor image quality with severe noise, incapable of providing useful information, unsuitable for diagnostic purposes. The scoring data were collected and compiled by dedicated data processing personnel, followed by statistical analysis.

Quantitative assessment

On the PET/MR fusion images, the LIFEx software (version 7.1.0) was utilized for the semi-automatic delineation of the volume of interest (VOI) of the lesions. Our methodology incorporated a 40% threshold segmentation, combined with manual adjustments, to accurately measure the three-dimensional volume of these lesions. Additionally, for each patient, a specific VOI, measuring 30 ± 3 mm in diameter, was manually outlined at a consistent position and slice in a normal and homogenous area of the right liver lobe. This VOI was then saved as a DICOM RT structure, which was subsequently uploaded into the same LIFEx software. This procedure enabled the extraction of critical parameters such as maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), SUVmean, metabolic tumor volume (MTV), total lesion glycolysis (TLG), standard deviation (SD) of liver, lesion size and number.

For target selection, all identifiable lesions with an SUVmax > 2.5 were evaluated. For patients with ≥ 5 lesions, up to 10 representative lesions were assessed. SNR was calculated using the normal liver tissue as the reference background. CNR was calculated for each targeted lesion; for patients with multiple lesions, the average CNR was recorded. These measurements were pivotal in calculating image quality metrics such as the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), Contrast, contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), coefficient of variation (COV), and target-to-background ratio (TBR). The respective formulae for these calculations are as follows:

SNR = SUVmean_lesion / SD_liver;

Contrast = (SUVmean_lesion – SUVmean_liver) / (SUVmean_lesion + SUVmean_liver).

CNR = (SUVmean_lesion – SUVmean_liver) / SD_liver;

COV= (σ/µ) ×100%; (σ is the standard deviation of pixel intensities within the image region, µ is the mean of the pixel intensities within that region.)

TBR = SUVmax_lesion / SUVmean_liver.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were represented as mean ± standard deviation and range. The Fisher’s exact test was employed to investigate the distribution of sex across the two cohorts. The independent t-test was utilized to assess other demographic parameters of the enrolled patients in both groups. Subsequently, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to compare the subjective scores of image quality. The normality of all parameters in the semi-quantitative analysis was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Inter-rater reliability was evaluated using the Cohen’s weighted kappa (linear) coefficient. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was then used to compare scores of qualitative image quality. Statistical significance was considered at a p-value of less than 0.05. All statistical tests were performed using SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and PRISM.

Discussion

Applying a DPL algorithm originally trained on the uEXPLORER PET/CT scanner to an integrated PET/MR system presents unique challenges and novel clinical opportunities. First, cross-platform generalization is not guaranteed due to the inherent domain shift between PET/CT and PET/MR, including fundamentally different attenuation correction methodologies (MR-based vs. CT-based) [21], detector physics, and noise profiles. This study represents the first systematic validation of such cross-platform transfer. Second, unlike the sequential acquisition in PET/CT, PET/MR acquires PET and MR data simultaneously during each bed position, which reduces the risk of inter-scan misregistration due to physiological motion between separate acquisitions. However, it should be noted that the 7-minute-per-bed acquisition in the present protocol still provides opportunity for intra-scan patient movement. Shortening PET acquisition time through DPL may help mitigate motion artifacts, complementing the intrinsic co-registration advantage of simultaneous PET/MR. Third, validating DPL on PET/MR has potential value for multiparametric studies. Improved PET image quality directly benefits quantitative PET metrics (e.g., SUV) that are co-analyzed with MR-derived parameters (e.g., ADC from diffusion-weighted imaging, perfusion parameters from DCE-MRI). More accurate PET quantification enables more reliable spatial correlation between functional PET signals and MR biomarkers, which is critical for applications such as brain PET/MR where subtle metabolic changes must be mapped onto high-resolution structural and functional MR data. Additionally, novel PET tracers with relatively low specific uptake (e.g., tau or amyloid tracers) demand a higher image quality floor, which DPL may help meet without increasing administered activity or scan time.

It should be acknowledged that the OSEM–DPL comparison was performed under fixed, clinically standard iteration settings (OSEM: 3 iterations, 20 subsets; DPL: three predefined strength levels). Because OSEM and DPL follow fundamentally different reconstruction frameworks—OSEM being a purely statistical iterative method and DPL incorporating learned priors within the EM loop—a direct “iteration-to-iteration” convergence comparison is not methodologically meaningful. However, the three DPL levels (DPL1, DPL2, DPL3) provide a proxy for varying degrees of deep learning–based regularization, with DPL1 representing the least aggressive intervention and DPL3 the most aggressive. The finding that DPL1 consistently outperforms DPL2 and DPL3 across all metrics suggests that the observed improvements reflect more than simply stronger regularization; rather, the network appears to reach an effective operating point at DPL1 beyond which additional intervention degrades image quality. Nonetheless, the extent to which the OSEM–DPL differences are attributable to differing effective noise–resolution trade-offs versus intrinsic advantages of learned reconstruction remains incompletely characterized, and future work incorporating OSEM at multiple iteration–subset combinations would help disentangle these effects.

Image denoising based on deep learning outperforms traditional algorithms, yet there is still potential for further enhancement. For example, while CNN has ameliorated image noise, they have also reduced the detection rate of small lesions [22]. Our novel DPL method employs a two-step learning approach [17], initially using a CNN-DE followed by an EM iteration for significant noise reduction while preserving small lesion detectability. Despite potential contrast loss in small lesions from CNN-DE’s output, it serves as a solid foundation for the second EM iteration, where CNN-EH enhances and restores lesion contrast, complementing the initial EM results. Our research findings suggest that among the three levels of DPL differentiated by iteration counts, DPL1 exhibits the best image quality, indicating that a higher number of iterations does not always equate to better outcomes. This phenomenon could be attributed to several factors: overfitting, where the model learns noise instead of useful patterns from the data, resulting in poor generalization capabilities; early convergence, where the model reaches an optimal solution after fewer iterations, and further iterations merely contribute to noise fitting; and the inherent characteristics of the dataset, where certain complexities or features may be best captured with fewer iterations. Moreover, the optimal performance of DPL depends not only on the number of iterations but also on the correct combination of parameters and optimization strategies, highlighting the importance of balancing underfitting and overfitting to achieve high-quality imaging results.

The preference for DPL1 in this study, while perhaps at odds with some clinical practices that favor more aggressive denoising levels (DPL2 or DPL3) for perceived sharpness, is supported by comprehensive quantitative evidence: DPL1 achieved significantly higher SNR (adjusted P = 0.018 vs. DPL2; adjusted P = 0.009 vs. DPL3), CNR (adjusted P = 0.024 vs. DPL2; adjusted P = 0.012 vs. DPL3), and Contrast (adjusted P = 0.031 vs. DPL2; adjusted P = 0.016 vs. DPL3) in our dataset. DPL1 also preserved SUVmax and SUVmean values most faithfully relative to OSEM, making it the optimal choice for applications prioritizing quantitative accuracy over maximum visual sharpness. It should be noted, however, that the optimal DPL level may be task- and reader-dependent. In clinical workflows emphasizing rapid visual screening, DPL2 or DPL3 might subjectively appear sharper, and the choice among DPL levels should ultimately be guided by the specific clinical question and the radiologist’s preference.

In our clinical evaluation, the DPL method consistently demonstrated superior noise reduction and lesion contrast enhancement capabilities compared to the conventional OSEM method, both from subjective and semi-quantitative perspectives. On one hand, DPL maintained its efficacy in significantly shortened acquisition times, reconstructing diagnostically reliable images even when the scanning duration was reduced to a quarter of the original time. This was achieved without compromising the quantitative SUV values. All lesions remained clinically identifiable on DPL1-reconstructed images across all acquisition times. Furthermore, the shorter the acquisition time, the more pronounced the advantages of DPL over OSEM became, highlighting DPL’s promise in low-dose injection studies. Interestingly, some half injected activity DPL images exhibited numerically higher measured contrast than full injected activity images. This observation can be attributed to a combination of inter-patient biological variability and the noise floor effect at low counts. In very noisy images, the measured background SUV is artificially elevated, which reduces calculated contrast. When DPL successfully suppresses background noise, the calculated contrast naturally increases. However, subjective assessments revealed that despite higher mathematical contrast, lesions in half injected activity images were not always depicted as completely or accurately as in full injected activity references. This underscores that quantitative metrics (e.g., SNR, CNR) should not be interpreted in isolation, and qualitative visual assessment remains essential for evaluating true diagnostic adequacy.

On the other hand, our research also demonstrated the encouraging performance of the DPL method when the patient’s injected dose was reduced to half the standard dose. Additionally, it is well-known that the quality of PET images obtained from the [18F]FDG dosage protocol based on linear body weight deteriorates as patient weight increases. However, our study indicates that reducing the administered activity of [18F]FDG by 50% for overweight and obese patients does not affect image quality when compared to the standard [18F]FDG administration. This finding could contribute to reducing the total administered activity for overweight patients.

Furthermore, our results showed that the DPL algorithm can offer enhanced performance for patients with higher BMI [20]. It is well-known that PET image quality, using a linear weight-based [18F]FDG dosing scheme, deteriorates as the patient’s weight increases. However, our study indicates that, compared to standard [18F]FDG administration, the DPL reconstruction approach suggests that acquisition time for obese patients may potentially be reduced to one-fourth while maintaining clinically acceptable image quality. This could help to reduce the total amount of activity for overweight or obese patients and decrease the risk of radiation exposure for operators.

Our study indicates that, compared to OSEM reconstruction, the significant reduction in focal volume in PET/MR images reconstructed with DPL mirrors findings from previous research [19]. This discovery suggests that DPL may offer several advantages, including potentially improved spatial resolution for clearer delineation of lesions, enhanced detection capabilities for smaller tumors, more accurate quantification of tracer uptake, reduced partial volume effects, and better differentiation between types of tissue. These benefits contribute to enhanced diagnostic accuracy, more informed treatment planning, and more effective monitoring of treatment responses, potentially leading to improved patient outcomes. However, there are also potential downsides, such as the requirement for substantial training data in DPL reconstruction. If the dataset is not sufficiently large or diverse, the model’s generalizability may be limited, leading to suboptimal performance in practical applications.

Regarding clinical implementation, a critical trade-off exists between reducing injected activity and shortening acquisition time to maximize patient comfort. For pediatric patients, who are highly sensitive to radiation, minimizing the injected dose is the absolute priority. Conversely, for adult patients who experience discomfort during prolonged immobilization (e.g., obese patients or those with severe pain), shortening the acquisition time may yield more immediate clinical benefits by reducing motion artifacts.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center investigation, which may introduce selection bias; multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to confirm the generalizability of these findings. Second, only patients undergoing[18F]FDG PET/MR for oncologic indications were included; extending validation to other tracers (e.g., [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE, [18F]PSMA) would broaden the applicability of DPL. Third, the comparison between OSEM and DPL was conducted under fixed, clinically standard reconstruction settings (OSEM: 3 iterations, 20 subsets; DPL: three predefined strength levels). Because algorithms based on different iterative paradigms may exhibit markedly different convergence dynamics, evaluating each method at a single clinically selected reconstruction setting provides only a single operating point; therefore, this comparison does not fully characterize the relative behaviour of the two reconstruction approaches across different convergence states. The observed differences may partially reflect the fact that DPL was evaluated near its optimal operating point, whereas the reference OSEM reconstruction may correspond to a different, potentially suboptimal, convergence state. Future studies incorporating OSEM at multiple iteration levels would help disentangle these effects. Fourth, lesion segmentation was initialized with a fixed 40% threshold (combined with manual adjustments applied uniformly across methods). Although manual correction mitigates some dependency, the initial threshold boundary is inherently reconstruction-dependent because DPL alters image contrast and lesion boundary sharpness; therefore, the volumetric comparisons (MTV, TLG) should be interpreted with caution and are reported primarily to illustrate relative partial volume trends. Beyond these methodological considerations, DPL itself faces practical limitations, including its reliance on pre-trained network weights derived from a specific scanner (uEXPLORER PET/CT) and the need for sufficiently large and diverse training datasets. Large-scale multicenter trials with task-based endpoints (e.g., diagnostic accuracy, lesion detection rate, Deauville scoring) are needed before DPL can be recommended for routine clinical use.

Conclusion

In summary, our study suggests that the DPL method, a whole-body PET-guided reconstruction approach, may be utilized to reduce noise and enhance contrast in low-dose [18F]FDG PET images. By applying DPL, it suggests the feasibility of reducing the administered activity for whole-body PET/MR imaging to half or decrease the scanning time to one-quarter. However, the present study primarily demonstrates improvements in image quality metrics and subjective visual assessment. Formal task-based evaluations—including lesion detection sensitivity analysis, diagnostic accuracy against a reference standard, and diagnostic non-inferiority assessment—have not been conducted. Such studies are warranted before DPL reconstruction can be recommended for routine clinical protocols involving reduced injected activity or shortened acquisition times.
