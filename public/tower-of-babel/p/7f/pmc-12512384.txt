The relationship between heart rate variability and affective disorders: associations with symptomatic improvement and therapeutic alliance
Authors: Alexandra F. Gonçalves, Eugénia Ribeiro, Adriana Sampaio, Natividade S. Couto-Pereira, Pedro Moreira, Joana F. Coutinho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart rate variability (HRV) is a cardiac measure that reflects individuals’ ability to adjust their physiological activation at a given time and has been associated with several psychological conditions, including affective disorders like depression and anxiety disorders. The objective of this study was to gain insight into the relationship between HRV and depression and anxiety disorders, and to explore how this association may be reflected in the establishment of the therapeutic alliance (TA) with patients diagnosed with these disorders. We hypothesize that lower levels of symptomatology will be associated with higher HRV, and that the decrease in psychological symptomatology over time will be associated with changes in HRV. Also, drawing on Polyvagal Theory, we hypothesize that higher HRV will be related to stronger TA ratings.MethodsThe study included 42 participants − 21 individuals with depression or anxiety and 21 healthy controls. HRV was measured during a baseline task and throughout all the psychotherapy sessions. The Outcome Questionnaire and the Working Alliance Inventory were administered at the beginning and at the end of each session respectively for process and outcome and therapeutic alliance assessment. Comparisons of means and mixed generalized linear models were used to test our hypothesis.ResultsWe found that HRV differed between groups at baseline, and we also found evidence for an increase in HRV over the course of therapy. However, the hypothesis that an increase in HRV throughout the therapy would be accompanied by an increase in the TA was not corroborated.ConclusionsOur findings support the role of the HRV as an important psychophysiological measure for differentiating emotional disorders from healthy subjects, as well as for tracking changes in clinical symptoms over time.

Background

The World Health Organization [1] recognizes mental health as a fundamental aspect of an individual’s health, well-being, and society. According to the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), the two most common mental disorders are depression and anxiety, with a global prevalence of 4.3% and 4.7%, respectively [2]. Both affective disorders significantly affect the quality of life and occupational functioning, and their impact extends beyond mental health to include other adverse health outcomes [3, 4]. For instance, depression has been identified as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease, even in physically healthy individuals [5]. Similarly, anxiety disorders are associated with the onset and progression of cardiac disease, and in many instances have been linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including mortality [3].

A physiological marker of both mental well-being and cardiovascular health is indexed by heart rate variability (HRV) [6–8]. HRV can be defined as the fluctuation of heart rate over time, usually measured by electrocardiogram (ECG [6–8]. Indeed, the oscillations of a healthy heart are complex and constantly changing, and these changes in the time intervals between consecutive heartbeats is what allows the cardiovascular system to adjust to physical and psychological challenges.

Together with Heart Rate, HRV is one of the most common autonomic measures used in the literature. The autonomic nervous system (ANS) exerts control over the heart modelling cardiac activity to adapt to environmental challenges., playing a crucial role in regulating HRV. HRV reflects the balance between the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the ANS and it is used to assess autonomic function in various physiological and pathological conditions. More specifically, the sympathetic system generally decreases HRV, while the parasympathetic system increases it, reflecting a healthy autonomic balance (Silva et al., 2021; Gregoire et al., 2023; Sztajzel. 2004; Taralov et al., 2016).

Psychiatric disorders, such as anxiety and depression are associated with autonomic dysfunction, characterized by reduced HRV and increased heart rate, indicating altered sympathetic and parasympathetic activity (Agorastos et al., 2023).

One central theory to the understanding of HRV in the context of mental health is the Polyvagal Theory [9], which links autonomic nervous system functioning—especially through the vagus nerve—to social behaviour and emotional regulation. The vagus nerve is a long cranial nerve, which carries parasympathetic input to the body, forming a two-way pathway between the brain and the heart, with connections to many other organs throughout the body like the gut for example (Tindle & Tadi, 2022). According to Polyvagal theory the myelinated vagus provides the physiological foundation facilitating social engagement and compassion (Porges, 2007b), facilitating calm states and conditions of safety and trust (Porges, 2022).

According to this theory, when individuals perceive their environment as safe, a physiological state of increased vagal influence predominates, indicated through higher HRV. This elevated HRV supports spontaneous social engagement behaviours, reflecting a physiological state that leads to adaptive social interaction and emotional regulation [9]. The relationship between HRV and social engagement behaviours has been empirically demonstrated in both healthy and clinical populations (Wang et al., 2023). For example, Mauersberger and colleagues (2022) demonstrated that individuals with higher HRV reported more positive and fewer negative emotions and perceived fewer negative emotions in their interaction partners. This association has been observed in clinical samples. For instance, Maiß et al. (2021) found that HRV predicted approach behaviour for angry faces in a sample of borderline patients (Maiß et al., 2021). Kim and colleagues (2025) found that higher social avoidance distress and panic disorder were associated with reduced HRV, mainly in the time domain. Recently, Cheah et al. (2025) conducted a study comparing individuals with social anxiety disorder (SAD) to those without social anxiety. Their findings indicated that the SAD group tended to activate their emotion regulation strategies more proactively in anticipation of social-evaluative situations. In contrast, the non-SAD group engaged in emotion regulation primarily during the actual stressor.

HRV can be measured with frequency-based or time-domain measures. From the several indices of HRV available in the literature, the high-frequency HRV (HF-HRV), and the Root Mean Square of Successive Differences HRV (RMSSD-HRV) have been associated with cardiac vagal tone by several authors (Berntson et al., 1997; Laborde et al., 2017; Shaffer et al., 2014). Both the HF-HRV and the RMSSD are vagally (or parasympathetically ) mediated. These measures are often used to assess one’s ability to adaptively respond to the environment and have been linked to mental health [10, 11]. The RMSSD is calculated from the time domain and is especially sensitive to beat-to-beat changes in heart rate, whereas the HF power is derived from the frequency domain, representing HRV components within the respiratory frequency range (typically 0.15–0.4 Hz), capturing the respiratory sinus arrhythmia tied to breathing cycles. Both time-domain metrics like RMSSD which measure how much HRV was observed during monitoring periods that may range from ~ 2 min to 24 h, and frequency-domain metrics that calculate the absolute or relative amount of signal energy within specific component bands, have been used to assess clinical and non-clinical populations as well as their progress in pretreatment/posttreatment, within-session, and across-session [12].

Reduced HRV has been proposed as a transdiagnostic marker of psychopathology, reflecting impaired autoregulation and cognitive control [13]. HRV correlates with many indices of mental health, including anxiety and negative emotion (Chalmers et al., 2014).

Meta-analyses have consistently demonstrated that individuals with depression or anxiety display significantly lower HF-HRV compared to healthy controls [14–16]. Of note, this reduction in HRV is thought to reflect a diminished ability to regulate emotional and physiological responses to stress [17].

Research has demonstrated that depression is associated with both lower HRV and blunted HRV reactivity in response to stressful episodes [18]. Similarly, in anxiety disorders, decreased HRV has been proposed to reflect a failure to inhibit cognitive, affective, behavioral, and physiological responses [14]. A study by Blanck et al. [11] showed that higher baseline HRV at the onset of therapy is correlated with greater symptom improvement. In contrast, lower HRV is often associated with heightened anxiety symptoms [19].

A specific context where these social, emotional, and physiological regulatory skills are recruited is within interpersonal settings such as psychotherapy. In particular, the quality of the collaborative and affective relationship between client and therapist [20], i.e., therapeutic alliance (TA), is pivotal in establishing a safe interpersonal environment that supports the development of emotional regulation, empathy, and stress reduction, which, in turn, may influence HRV [11]. Indeed, the efficacy of this negotiating process is mutually beneficial to both patient and therapist, facilitating the formation of a trusting relationship and enabling engagement in interpersonal and social processes, including affiliative and aligned interactions [21]. Studies have demonstrated that increased vagal influence (i.e., higher HRV), is accompanied by the client-therapist dyad engaging in socially contingent behaviors (e.g., 11). Evidence suggests that more prosocial behaviors are likely to strengthen TA [22], and as such, higher in-session HRV may be linked to a better quality of the TA [11].

In summary, reduced HRV has been consistently associated with affective disorders such as anxiety and depression and is closely linked to inhibition of social behavior [23, 24], and cognitive inflexibility [25]. Moreover, previous studies suggest that increased HRV may be associated with improved psychological symptoms [26, 27], particularly in therapeutic settings. However, there is limited research investigating HRV in affective disorders, namely in real-time psychotherapeutic processes. Thus, to the best of our knowledge, there are no studies analysing the relationship between affective disorders and HRV where HRV measures have been collected in the psychotherapeutic context throughout all sessions using a cognitive-behavioural therapeutic (CBT) approach.

The objective of this study is to analyse the relationship between HRV and psychological symptoms, while examining how these variables might influence TA, proposing the following hypothesis: H1) Individuals with affective disorders will exhibit lower HRV compared to a nonclinical control group at baseline condition; H2) a decrease in psychological symptomatology will be associated with increase in HRV; H3) an increase in HRV will be associated with an increase in the quality of TA throughout the therapeutic process.

Methods

Participants

The sample comprised 41 subjects: 21 patients and 21 healthy individuals (nonclinical control group), selected from the dataset of the ColPsi research project (CA_CIPsi-072012). The inclusion criteria for the participants were: (a) age over 18 years; (b) having a diagnosis of depression or anxiety disorder. The exclusion criteria were: (a) comorbidity with other Axis I disorders, (b) substance abuse; and (c) suicidal ideation or severe mental disorder. Of the 21 patients, 14 (66.7%) were females, and 7 (33.3%) were males, with ages ranging from 18 to 52 years (M = 31.52, SD = 11.21). Additionally, 15 (71.4%) had a diagnosis of MDD, and 6 (28.6%) were diagnosed with SAD. Among the 21 individuals in the nonclinical control group, 14 (66.7%) were females, and 7 (33.3%) were males, with ages ranging from 18 to 51 years (M = 29.76, SD = 10.22).

The therapists who participated in the study included 5 women and 1 man. In terms of training, they had on average 10.3 years of clinical experience (SD = 6.25), all with cognitive behaviour training (CBT). From the 21 therapeutic dyads, 10 were same sex dyads, and 11 were other sex dyads.

Measures

Cardiac activity

The electrocardiogram (ECG) was recorded using the BioNomadix (BIOPAC System, Santa Barbara, CA, USA). Physiological recordings were performed continuously at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. Two modules RSPEC-R of the Biopac BioNomadix wireless device (Biopac Systems; Santa Barbara, CA) were connected to the same MP150-Biopac data acquisition system to record the cardiac activity. The receiver devices were connected to a host computer running AcqKnowledge software (version 4.4), while the transmitter devices were connected to the electrodes placed into the participants. This wireless system enables the acquisition and subsequent analysis of physiological data via the AcqKnowledge software. The raw Electrocardiogram (ECG) was used to measure the participants’ HR (measured in beats per minute or bpm) using an adjusted 3-electrode Lead-II configuration. The nonpolarizable disposable Ag–AgCl electrodes connected to the transmitter devices, with a circular contact area of 1 cm diameter (EL-503, Biopac Systems), were placed on the participants’ right and left mid-clavicle, and a third one on the top of the left shoulder bone.

From the several metrics that can be derived from the HRV, we chose the HF-HRV as a frequency-based measure and the RMSSD as a time domain measure. The RMSS is a reliable index of the vagal tone, which was described as having important functional roles for emotion regulation (e.g., Appelhans & Luecken, 2006; Shaffer et al., 2014). This choice was also due to the fact that compared to other time-domain measures that can be extracted from the ECG, like the SDNN (standard deviation of all R-R intervals), the RMSSD is less affected by respiratory influences (Laborde, 2017). The HF-HRV is a frequency component that oscillates in the 0.15–0.4 Hz range, corresponding to respiratory-linked variations in heart rate (respiratory sinus arrhythmia). It is thus a robust measure of the reduced parasympathetic tone that often characterizes emotional disorders. In comparison with other frequency measures such as the Low-Frequency HRV (LF-HRV) that reflects a mix of sympathetic and parasympathetic influences, the HF-HRV is more specifically linked to vagal tone.

Clinical assessment

The diagnosis of patients who participated in the study was conducted using the Structured Clinical Interview (SCID), which is based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th edition (DSM-IV) criteria (SCID-I) [28]. Additionally, SCID was employed to identify participants from the nonclinical control group who did not meet clinical criteria for a diagnosis.

Therapeutic alliance

The Working Alliance Inventory – Short Revised [29] was employed to evaluate the quality of the therapeutic alliance. The WAI-SR comprises 12 items, which are rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“Rarely”) to 5 (“Always”). In this sample, the questionnaire demonstrated adequate internal reliability (α = 0.93) for the complete questionnaire, as well as for each of the factors (0.72 ≤ α ≤ 0.91).

Symptomatic change

Given that the clinical group exhibited two emotional disorders, a transdiagnostic measure was employed for the assessment of psychological distress. The Outcome Questionnaire 45.2 [30] was employed as the outcome measure, on session 1 and 16. The 45-item self-report instrument was employed to evaluate the symptomatic change across psychotherapeutic treatment. This instrument measures clinical progress across three different domains of experience, namely psychological suffering (e.g., “I get tired quickly”, “I don’t feel interested in things”, “I blame myself for things”, “I feel irritated”), interpersonal functioning (e.g., “I get al.ong well with others”, “I am unhappy in my marriage or significant relationship”, “I am worried about family problems”, “I have an unsatisfactory sex life”) and social roles (e.g., “I feel stressed at work/school”, “I find my work/school satisfactory”, “I work/study too much” ). In this sample, the OQ-45.2 exhibited a good internal consistency (α = 0.92) for the complete questionnaire, as well as for each subscale (0.69 ≤ α ≤ 0.91). To determine whether the change was clinically significant, we used the cut-off point estimated in accordance with the formula proposed by Jacobson and Truax [31], resulting in a value of 62 for the total scale. A score below the cut-off point indicates that the client’s functioning is more similar to that of the non-clinical population than the clinical population at that time. The cases were classified as either successful or unsuccessful based on the comparison of the total score of the initial and final sessions in each case. This comparison was conducted using the questionnaire established cut-off value (62 points) and the index of significant clinical change (15 points) as a reference point. Moreover, the OQ-10.2, a short version of the OQ-45.2, was used on sessions 2 to 15.

Procedures

The data used in this study are part of a larger research project focused on therapeutic collaboration and its physiological correlates (BIAL foundation - Grant number: 178/12) which was approved by the Ethics Committee from the Research Centre in Psychology of the University of Minho (CA_CIPsi-072012). (November 16, 2012). Moreover, all methods were carried out following guidelines for research in psychology, enshrined in the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association (WMA) (2008).

The participants in the clinical group were patients from the Service of Psychology of University of Minho. An evaluation of the inclusion and exclusion criteria to assess if participants were eligible for the study was performed at session (session 0). In this session the goals and procedures of the study were explained. The patients that accepted to participate in this study signed a consent form, where they were informed about their requirements and their rights, including the right to continue the therapy process even if they quit the study. The sessions were offered free of charge. For the composition of the non-clinical control group participants with similar characteristics to the clinical group that have never had been diagnosed with any type of mental disorder were selected and paired by gender and age.

The therapist and the client were both asked to refrain from caffeine, intense physical exercise and nicotine for at least 4 h before each session. Before each psychotherapeutic session, both patient and therapist were prepared for the collection of physiological data. The data was recorded in real-time on a computer in a room adjacent to the consultation room, where research assistants monitored the recording process during the session.

To establish the baseline values of physiological activity participants went through a minimally demanding baseline task (i.e., description of neutral images that were presented on a computer) that lasted for approximately 10 min. This was followed by the completion of the symptom’s questionnaire (i.e., OQ-45 in the first session and OQ-10 in the following sessions). Subsequently, the therapeutic session began, lasting on average approximately 50 to 60 min. After the session, the patient completed the WAI. All the data was appropriately anonymized to ensure patient’s confidentiality. The treatment plan included 16 sessions according to the Cognitive Behavior Therapy protocol; however, treatment ended earlier whenever the therapist and patient felt that the treatment goals had already been achieved.

The nonclinical group performed the acquisition of physiological data for the baseline task 10-minute period.

Considering the therapy process, specifically the therapeutic alliance management and promotion, therapists were encouraged to implement their interventions based on collaborative empiricism, which is a central aspect in CBT (e.g., Kazantizis, 2017), framing their work on clients’ experience and immediate needs, and negotiating with them the agreement regarding specific session goals and tasks. This collaborative agreement as a key alliance dimension was also discussed on a weekly basis within the supervision with a senior therapist.

Data analysis

Preprocessing of cardiac data

Prior to analysis, all physiological recordings were pre-processed to minimise the impact of artefacts. The ECG recordings were subjected to pre-processing using an infinite impulse response filter, resulting in the definition of a low-frequency cutoff of 1 Hz and a high-frequency cutoff of 35 Hz.

The raw ECG data was filtered using the recommended standard filter settings for the acquisition device: an IIR high-pass filter of 1 Hz, and an IIR low-pass filter set at 35 Hz. For the data reduction, the recordings were examined visually for ectopic beats and remaining artifacts. Then, ECG signals were replayed to verify and validate each R-wave visually. In a few cases, when the automated QRS detector misplaced some R-spikes, they were manually corrected. Finally, the HR was computed from the raw ECG using AcqKnowledge. For the calculation of the HRV that measures the changes in the time intervals between consecutive heartbeats (IBIs), we used a time-domain measure – the Root mean square of the successive differences (RMSSD) - using a script from MatLab.

Subsequently, all the data were processed in the JupyterLab [32]. To this end, a code was devised to rectify the artefacts, and a data sheet was then generated in Microsoft Office Excel for each patient. Subsequently, a new database was created comprising the extracted cardiac activity records for each patient. The database comprised a separate record for each of the 21 clinical cases, along with data from the nonclinical control group.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using the R 4.2.2 software [33]. Two distinct statistical models were employed for the analyses: a non-parametric distribution comparison test (R package exactRankTests), and a generalised linear mixed-effects model (glmer: R package lme4). The assumptions of normality, homoscedasticity and linearity were not met; therefore, a non-parametric test was employed for the comparison of distributions and a Gamma distribution with logarithmic link was employed for the glmer model.

Regarding the first hypothesis, a Mann-Whitney test was conducted to determine if individuals with MDD or SAD would exhibit lower HRV values compared to a nonclinical control group at baseline condition.

To test the second hypothesis, a glmer was conducted to assess if a decrease in psychological symptomatology would be associated with increase in HRV in MDD and SAD patients (measured with the OQ-10.2). Consequently, three models were constructed, including Subject as the random factor. The first model (null model) comprised solely the dependent variable (HF-HRV or RMSSD-HRV). The second model included the dependent variable (HF-HRV or RMSSD-HRV) and a predictor variable ‘Session’ (i.e., all the sessions, for a longitudinal analysis). The final model was a conditional model, which included the dependent variable (HF-HRV or RMSSD-HRV), and two predictor variables (‘Session’ and ‘OQ-10.2’), as well as their interaction (Session x OQ-10.2).

A further glmer was conducted to understand if an increase in HRV would be associated with an increase in the quality of TA throughout the therapeutic process in accordance with the third and final hypothesis. In this instance, the analyses were conducted using the total WAI-SR score. Again, subject was included as the random factor. The null model was then constructed with the dependent variable (WAI-SR), while the second model was composed of the dependent variable (WAI-SR) and a predictor variable (‘Session’). The conditional model was constructed with the dependent variable (WAI-SR) and two predictor variables (session and HF-HRV or RMSSD-HRV), as well as their interaction session x HF-HRV. Only successful cases were analysed for hypothesis 2 and 3, consisting of 154 sessions total.

For all glmer models, R package performance was employed for visual inspection of assumptions and model fit. Exponentiated coefficients for second and conditioned final models are reported. The continuous independent variables (HF-HRV, RMSSD-HRV and OQ-10) were scaled to allow model convergence.

Results

The results section is organized according to the main hypothesis.

H1. HRV Group comparison at baseline.

To ascertain whether there were notable discrepancies between the nonclinical control group and the clinical group regarding HF-HRV and RMSSD-HRV values in the initial session, a Mann-Whitney test was conducted. The results of this test indicated that there was a statistically significant difference between the two groups for the RMSSD-HRV variable (W = 129, p = 0.02), with the clinical group exhibiting lower RMSSD-HRV values (Mdn = 28.37, IQR = [19.70, 55.61]) when compared with the nonclinical control group (Mdn = 51.45, IQR = [36.05, 65.43] ). However, this difference was not significant for HF-HRV values (W = 174, p = 0.25), even though the clinical group demonstrated lower values of HF-HRV (Mdn = 0.004, IQR = [0.001630, 0.009440] ) than the nonclinical control group (Mdn = 0.006, IQR = [0.003719, 0.009549] ).

H2. Prediction of HRV by Symptomatology.

To examine the associations between symptomatology and in-session HRV, we set up models of increasing complexity (see Table 1). For the HF-HRV value the baseline model (model 0) only had the dependent variable (HF-HRV; b = -4.91, p < 0.001). In a second model (model 1) we added the variable ‘Session’, however, ‘Session’ was not significant (b = 1.05, p = 0.17) and the inclusion of this new variable did not improve the model fit (χ2 = 1.39, p = 0.24). When adding the interaction term between OQ-10 and session (session × OQ-10) to the final model (model 2), neither the session variable (b = 1.02, p = 0.53) nor the OQ-10 variable alone (b = 1.46, p = 0.10) were significant. However, there was a significant interaction effect (b = 0.94, p < 0.05) suggesting a dynamic relationship between HRV and symptomatology across the course of therapy. The addition of this variable did not improve model fit (χ2 = 5.10, p = 0.08), however, the examination of the Fig. 1, reveals that individuals exhibiting reduced symptomatology during therapy exhibited a heightened HRV value in comparison to the initial HRV value at the start of therapy.

For the third hypothesis, we examined the relationship between in-session HRV and WAI scores, by setting up models of increasing complexity (see Table 1). The baseline model (model 0) only included the dependent variable, WAI scores (b = 3.80, p < 0.001). In a second model (model 1), we added the session that was found to be significant predictor of alliance (b = 1.02, p < 0.001), with a significant improvement in model fit (χ2 = 61.66, p < 0.001), indicating an increase in WAI scores that throughout therapy. In the final model (model 2), we tested whether HF-HRV was related to WAI scores, but neither the interaction term nor the main effect of HF-HRV was significant, and the model fit did not improve (χ2 = 2.56, p = 0.28). We additionally tested if RMSSD was related to WAI scores. The only difference from this analysis to the one for the HF-HRV is in the last model (i.e., model 2), in which an interaction between ‘Session’ and the RMSSD-HRV was tested. In this model there were no associations between the interaction and the WAI scores or the main term and the WAI scores. Additionally, the model fit for this model did not improve (χ2 = 0.98, p = 0.61).

Discussion

HRV has been proposed as a physiological measure that reflects individual differences in the regulation of emotional responses, particularly in the context of social processes [10]. Indeed, HRV decreases with stress or potential threat [34] and increases with individual’s attempts to regulate emotions and engage socially [9, 35].

However, empirical research on the HRV in the context of psychotherapy sessions remains limited, which motivated the current study. Specifically in this work we aimed to examine the influence of anxious and depressive symptomatology on HRV as well as the impact of HRV on the development of the TA throughout the psychotherapeutic process. Additionally, we also aimed to investigate whether there were differences in HRV in individuals diagnosed with affective disorders in comparison to healthy individuals.

Our first hypothesis proposed that individuals with affective disorders would demonstrate reduced HRV values when compared to the control group. In line with our hypothesis, individuals with MDD and SAD exhibited significantly lower RMSSD-HRV when compared to the nonclinical control group. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies [11, 19] and underscore the importance of HRV in evaluating the body’s capacity to regulate emotions and control autonomic activation [10].

Depression can disrupt the central autonomic network, resulting in a reduction in vagal nerve control over the heart and in the disinhibition of the sympathetic system, which is responsible for increasing heart activity [17]. Consequently, there is a diminished capacity to flexibly respond to environmental stimuli and to regulate emotions [17]. Similarly, anxiety disorders are characterized by hypervigilance to threatening stimuli [36] resulting in heightened levels of stress hormones, such as corticotropin-releasing factor and cortisol, which in turn can lead to the chronic withdrawal of parasympathetic activity (i.e., low vagal tone) [37]. Furthermore, anxious individuals may also demonstrate impaired cardiac autonomic regulation in response to non-threatening stimuli [38]. Therefore, behavioural rigidity observed in anxiety disorders has been linked to a reduction in HRV [39]. Our results go in this direction providing additional empirical support for the role of the HRV as a measure of the capacity of the cardiovascular system to adjust to physical and psychological challenges.

In psychotherapy, particularly within a CBT approach, the use of techniques that facilitate cognitive restructuring, thereby reducing cognitive rigidity, is a common practice. Additionally, techniques that enhance emotional regulation in response to daily circumstances are also employed. Therefore, our second hypothesis proposed that a reduction in psychological symptoms would be associated with an increase in HRV among individuals with affective disorders. Our results confirmed this hypothesis, as HF-HRV increased over the course of therapy. Several researchers have proposed that CBT, which seeks to modify maladaptive cognitions and behaviours through a range of behavioural mechanisms, may prove beneficial in reducing cardiac risk biomarkers, including HRV. These behavioural mechanisms target negative mood, as well as stress perception and reactivity, which can interact with autonomic dysregulation [40–45]. While further research is required to fully understand this association, the results of our study point to an increase in HRV throughout CBT psychotherapy sessions, suggesting that HRV may constitute a candidate biomarker to assess psychotherapeutic outcome.

It is important to note that the statistically significant difference between the clinical and non-clinical groups at baseline was found for the RMSSD-HRV, but this difference was not significant for HF-HRV values even though the clinical group demonstrated lower values of HF-HRV than the nonclinical control group. On the other hand, the increase in HRV over the course of therapy was only demonstrated for the HF-HRV. Time-domain metrics such as the RMSSD quantify the amount of HRV observed during a given period, whereas frequency-domain values calculate the absolute or relative amount of signal energy within component bands [12]. Our results seem to suggest that RMSSD can help initially distinguish clinical groups, while HF power may serve as a dynamic indicator for tracking symptom progression and treatment efficacy in the clinical population.

Contrary to our third hypothesis, there was no significant association between in-session HRV and improvements in TA. According to the Polyvagal Theory [9] HRV is linked with affiliation and social behaviour, so it would be expected to be associated with TA [11, 46]. However, in this study, while TA improved throughout therapy, HRV values did not show corresponding changes. This suggests that other factors—either physiological or non-physiological—may contribute to the development of TA throughout sessions. Another explanation for the null findings regarding H3 may be related with the level of analysis of the TA that was adopted, in that, a global measure of the alliance captured at a session level may not have been able to capture more transient dynamics of alliance negotiation. For example, the analysis of the patterns of alliance formation, including moments of tension or collaboration breaks between the therapist and the patient as well as the adequate resolution of these events could have better captured the processes of interpersonal emotional regulation occurring within the dyad. Thus, the use of a microscopic analysis of the TA instead of a global measure of the TA could be better able to address the complex relationship between the HRV and the alliance formation. Moreover, in this work we focused on the patient’s HRV, his/her clinical improvement and his/her perception of the alliance. Future studies should more carefully look at the therapist factors. For example, therapists’ HRV was not evaluated in this study, but future work should also explore if therapists with higher HRV have increased prosocial behaviours and are better able to regulate their own and their patient’s emotions. Therefore, it will be important in the future to also measure the therapist’s HRV, as well as the synchronization of the HRV between the therapist and the patient. The work by Tschacher and Meier [47] found evidence for the existence of physiological synchrony in heart rate, and heart rate variability in psychotherapy sessions, which was positively associated with alliance ratings, suggesting sympathetic and parasympathetic coupling between the therapist and the patient and its link with ratings of the therapy process.

Furthermore, we acknowledged that the uneven distribution of patients across diagnoses, may constitute a limitation of this study emphasizing the need for a cautious interpretation of the results. Our sample was composed by 41 subjects (21 healthy subjects vs. 21 patients) which may also be considered a limitation. However, we must note that for the test of the association between decrease in symptoms and increase in HRV (H1) and increase of the quality of the alliance (H2) across treatment, we did a longitudinal analysis in which a total of 336 sessions were included (21 clinical cases* 16 sessions). Moreover, the models’ estimates, error measures and fit indices as reported in Table 1 are adequate to support our conclusions.

Finally, we must mention the possible role of medication use in cardiac measures. Contrarily to lab-based or RCT studies in which the effect of medication is explicitly controlled, in naturalistic studies in psychotherapy, participants’ use of medication is difficult to control due to ethical and clinical reasons, nevertheless we cannot exclude its possible influence on HRV.

Conclusion

This study contributed to the understanding of the relationship between affective disorders and HRV, as well as the ways in which these association manifest in the context of therapeutic intervention and how therapists can more effectively respond to these changes.

Our findings provided valuable insights into HRV as a relevant biomarker in clinical and healthy populations. The RMSSD was shown to discriminate between healthy individuals and those with affective disorders suggesting that parasympathetic nervous system function differed between the two groups. Healthy individuals likely show higher baseline RMSSD values, indicating greater autonomic flexibility and resilience, while clinical patients may have reduced RMSSD, signalling reduced parasympathetic activity or increased autonomic dysregulation. On the other hand, the HF power’s relationship with symptom outcomes over time suggests it may be a predictive marker for clinical progress or treatment response. A higher or increasing HF power in clinical patients might indicate symptom improvement and recovery, as it implies enhanced parasympathetic regulation. Conversely, if HF power remains low or decreases, it could correlate with worsening symptoms or less favourable outcomes, indicating ongoing autonomic imbalance.

Together, these results highlight that RMSSD can help initially distinguish clinical groups, while HF power may serve as a dynamic indicator for tracking symptom progression and treatment efficacy in the clinical population. This dual use of HRV metrics can provide insights into both diagnosis and prognosis.

Overall, our results point to an association between HRV and emotional distress, contributing to the growing body of evidence linking HRV to MDD and SAD. This finding is particularly significant for gaining a comprehensive understanding of these mental disorders. Furthermore, it offers valuable insights that can guide treatment strategies, as an increase in HRV may have potentially positive implications for therapeutic outcomes, and psychotherapy in general. For this reason, it should be regarded as a promising variable for future psychophysiological research.
