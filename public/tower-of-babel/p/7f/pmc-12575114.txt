Endovascular Thrombectomy for Large Vessel Occlusion in a Patient on Venoarterial Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation: A Case Report
Authors: Yuki EBISUDANI, Masafumi HIRAMATSU, Keiichiro IWASAKI, Kenji SUGIU, Jun HARUMA, Ryu KIMURA, Masato KAWAKAMI, Yuta SOTOME, Takahiro NISHIHARA, Shinsuke YUASA, Shota TANAKA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation is utilized in the treatment of severe acute cardiac failure and respiratory failure. While it provides the advantage of oxygenating blood through extracorporeal circulation, it also carries risks of intracranial ischemic and hemorrhagic complications due to the continuous presence of artificial materials within the body. We encountered a case in which venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was initiated for fulminant myocarditis, and the patient subsequently developed a large vessel occlusion. The diagnosis was confirmed using perfusion computed tomography. A visible thrombus was observed on the arterial cannula of the extracorporeal membrane oxygenation circuit, and the large vessel occlusion was determined to have been caused by thromboembolism. An immediate extracorporeal membrane oxygenation circuit exchange was performed, followed by endovascular thrombectomy. The patient experienced no perioperative complications and achieved a favorable neurological outcome. Endovascular thrombectomy in extracorporeal membrane oxygenation patients requires careful perioperative management and should be promptly performed in eligible cases of thromboembolic events. Furthermore, because patients on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation are often sedated and under intensive systemic management, regular neurological assessments and intracranial monitoring are essential for the early detection of intracranial pathologies.

Introduction

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is employed in the treatment of severe acute cardiac failure and respiratory failure. While it provides the benefit of blood oxygenation through extracorporeal circulation, the continuous presence of artificial materials within the body necessitates anticoagulant use, which affects coagulation function and increases the risk of intracranial ischemic and hemorrhagic complications. Cerebral ischemic complications occur in 1 to 8% of ECMO cases, with associated mortality rates reported at 44%-76%.1)

ECMO is broadly categorized into venovenous (VV)-ECMO, which withdraws and returns blood to the venous system, and venoarterial (VA)-ECMO, which withdraws blood from the venous system and returns it to the arterial system. VA-ECMO is further classified based on the arterial return site: peripheral ECMO, where blood is returned via the femoral artery, and central ECMO, where the return occurs to the ascending aorta or brachiocephalic artery via open chest surgery. Blood is returned to the body through an ECMO circuit, and the arterial return location plays a crucial role in the management of thromboembolism.

We encountered a case of central VA-ECMO in which venous drainage was achieved via the right atrium (RA), and arterial return was directed to the ascending aorta. The patient subsequently developed a large vessel occlusion (LVO), which was successfully treated with endovascular thrombectomy (EVT), resulting in a favorable neurological outcome. Cases of EVT during ECMO therapy are still rare. Here, we report the specific thrombectomy method used and key precautions for treatment.

Case Report

A 35-year-old woman presented with dyspnea 2 days after experiencing symptoms of an upper respiratory infection and was diagnosed with fulminant myocarditis. Peripheral VA-ECMO was initiated; however, lower limb ischemia developed on the arterial side, necessitating conversion to central VA-ECMO with venous drainage via the RA and arterial return to the ascending aorta.

During ECMO management, continuous heparin infusion was administered to maintain an activated clotting time (ACT) of 180-200 secs and an activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) of 50-60 secs to prevent thrombotic complications. The course of cardiac failure treatment was favorable, but on day 30 of central ECMO support, the patient developed left hemiparesis when the assist flow was reduced during ECMO weaning evaluation. A stroke team was immediately convened. Intra-aortic balloon pumping was not in use at the time.

Neurological deficits included left-sided hemiparesis and facial paralysis, with a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of 10. Non-contrast head computed tomography (CT) revealed an Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score of 9, and perfusion CT showed right middle cerebral artery (MCA) occlusion with an ischemic core of 2.4 mL and a penumbra of 59 mL, leading to the decision to perform EVT (Fig. 1). At the time of hemiparesis onset, thrombi were visually observed on both the arterial and venous cannulas of the ECMO circuit, raising suspicion of embolism originating from the circuit. Circuit exchange was performed promptly before EVT (Fig. 2A).

EVT procedure

EVT was performed under local anesthesia while maintaining central ECMO support. Bilateral femoral arteries were not palpable due to continuous flow control by VA-ECMO and prior peripheral ECMO, which had caused skin contracture over the femoral arteries. Therefore, a right femoral artery approach was utilized under ultrasound guidance. Supplemental File 1 illustrates the equipment layout in the interventional radiology room. The EVT video is available as Supplemental File 2.

A Radifocus 4 Fr, 10 cm introducer (Terumo Co., Tokyo, Japan) was inserted, and an ASAHI FUBUKI 6 Fr, 90 cm straight guiding sheath (ASAHI Intecc, Aichi, Japan) was advanced to the right common carotid artery. Common carotid artery angiography confirmed occlusion near the right internal carotid artery (ICA)-MCA bifurcation, distal to the posterior communicating artery, although the exact level was unclear due to poor imaging caused by patient movement.

A Headway21 plus 156 cm microcatheter (Terumo Co., Tokyo, Japan) was navigated into the M2 segment with an ASAHI CHIKAI black 0.014” 200 cm wire (ASAHI Intecc, Aichi, Japan). Microcatheter angiography confirmed that the distal end of the thrombus was located at M1-distal (Fig. 3A), where a Solitaire X 4 × 20 mm stent retriever (Covidien, MA, USA) was deployed. An EMBOVAC aspiration catheter 0.071” 132 cm (Johnson & Johnson Medical Ltd, Wokingham, UK) was advanced into a wedged position in the proximal M1 segment (Fig. 3B). The Solitaire was retrieved first, although no thrombus was captured. Upon careful withdrawal of the EMBOVAC, a large, continuous thrombus with a firm texture, measuring approximately 12 cm, was retrieved (Fig. 2B).

Post-procedural ICA angiography demonstrated modified Thrombolysis In Cerebral Infarction (mTICI) grade 3 reperfusion (Fig. 3C and D). The onset-to-puncture (O2P) time was 203 mins, and the puncture-to-recanalization time was 17 mins. Although the procedure was performed under continuous flow, there was no noticeable difference in operability or visibility compared to procedures performed under native cardiac pulsation.

The puncture site was then exchanged for a Radifocus introducer 7 Fr, 10 cm (Terumo Co., Tokyo, Japan), and surgical hemostasis via the cutdown method was performed by the ECMO team the following day. Histopathological analysis of the retrieved thrombus was not conducted in this case.

Postoperative course

Immediately after EVT, the patient's left hemiparesis improved, achieving a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of 0. Anticoagulation targets for APTT and ACT remained unchanged, and no recurrence of cerebral infarction was observed. Cardiac function showed signs of recovery, allowing ECMO support to be discontinued 14 days after EVT. Following ECMO removal, heparin was discontinued, and the patient remained free of recurrent cerebral infarction.

The patient's overall clinical course was favorable, and on day 86, she was transferred to a rehabilitation facility with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 0. At the most recent outpatient follow-up, 18 months post-discharge, she maintained an mRS score of 0.

Discussion

We successfully performed EVT for LVO in a patient supported by central VA-ECMO, resulting in a favorable neurological outcome. Cerebral ischemia is a rare but serious complication of ECMO cases, often associated with high mortality.1) ECMO-related cerebral ischemic complications can be broadly classified into hypoxic factors and embolic factors, both linked to a prothrombotic state. Risk factors for ischemic complications vary widely among reports and include VA-ECMO,2) central VA-ECMO,3) high platelet count (>350 × 109/L),3) severe pre-ECMO acidosis,4) elevated lactic acid levels (>10 mmol/L),5) and higher PO2 levels (+10 mmHg) at 24 hrs.6) In the present case, the use of VA-ECMO and central ECMO represented significant risk factors for ischemic complications.

Monitoring of ECMO patients

ECMO is often administered with intubation, sedation, and muscle relaxants, making neurological assessment and the detection of cerebral circulation changes difficult. Given the high incidence of ischemic complications in VA-ECMO, non-invasive intracranial monitoring may be beneficial. Available modalities include transcranial Doppler,7) near-infrared spectroscopy for measuring regional oxygen saturation,8,9) and electroencephalography.10)

Each monitoring method serves as a supplementary tool. While these methods provide some efficacy in evaluating intracranial blood flow in the anterior circulation, their ability to detect posterior circulation ischemia early is likely limited, highlighting the need for further investigation. Fortunately, in our case, the patient was in the end stage of ECMO management and fully conscious, allowing for the early diagnosis of LVO.

Diagnosis of LVO during ECMO therapy

Confirming LVO in ECMO patients is challenging due to limited magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) accessibility caused by device-related mobility restrictions. Typically, non-contrast CT and perfusion CT are used for diagnosis. However, when evaluating LVO with perfusion CT, 2 important considerations should be noted. First, the insertion site of the ECMO outflow cannula. In cases where the outflow cannula is inserted into the axillary artery, the unmixed ECMO outflow (lacking contrast media) and the native cardiac output (containing contrast media) can create a “mixing zone” near the brachiocephalic artery. This phenomenon has been reported to mimic right-sided LVO on perfusion CT, warranting careful interpretation.11) In such cases, digital subtraction angiography can be useful for identifying the occlusive site. In our case, the outflow cannula was placed in the ascending aorta, so the perfusion CT findings were not affected by this issue.

Second, the reliability of perfusion CT under continuous flow has not been sufficiently validated. As perfusion CT assumes contrast agent bolus transit under native cardiac pulsation, its accuracy may be compromised in continuous flow conditions. Thus, treatment decisions should not be based solely on absolute core and penumbra volumes obtained from perfusion imaging. Clinical factors―including time of onset, symptoms, and early ischemic changes on non-contrast CT―should be carefully integrated when determining EVT eligibility.

Notes of LVO during ECMO therapy

In non-ECMO patients with LVO, the administration of recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) prior to thrombectomy is often considered. However, ECMO patients frequently present with contraindications such as recent surgical interventions or an APTT >40 secs, limiting the use of rt-PA in these cases.

Determining the access route is crucial when performing thrombectomy in ECMO patients. In cases of central VA-ECMO, access can generally be obtained via the femoral or brachial artery. However, if the outflow cannula is placed in the axillary artery, femoral access is the only viable option. For peripheral VA-ECMO, access can be achieved through the brachial artery or the femoral artery on the side opposite to the outflow cannula insertion. When an intra-aortic balloon pump is also in place, the balloon resides in the descending aorta, and brachial access becomes the only option. In any case, close discussions with the ECMO team are necessary to determine the optimal access route. In our case, access was obtained via the right femoral artery to the right ICA. Although the patient had experienced right lower limb ischemia during peripheral ECMO treatment, other access options, such as the right brachial artery, were considered. However, due to the continuous flow, arterial pulsation could not be palpated, and because of the patient's obesity, femoral artery puncture was ultimately performed under ultrasound guidance.

In ECMO patients receiving heparin, reliable hemostasis is critical. In this case, hemostasis was achieved using a cutdown method the day after EVT. This technique allows direct visualization of the vessel, enabling reliable hemostasis. However, it carries risks such as blood loss, postoperative pain, and surgical site infection. Recently, the safety of percutaneous closure techniques using the Perclose device (Abbott Vascular, CA, USA) has been reported in ECMO patients involving large-bore catheters.12) Although unsuitable for calcified vessels, it is considered beneficial for achieving hemostasis at puncture sites following thrombectomy, similar to its use for ECMO cannula removal.

However, none of the reports mentioned whether the ECMO circuit was exchanged before or after EVT. To date, no consensus exists regarding the optimal timing of circuit exchange. In our case, the thrombus was suspected to have originated from the ECMO circuit. Therefore, we replaced the ECMO circuit before EVT, resulting in an O2P time of 203 mins. Since this case involved an in-hospital onset, the O2P time was considered equivalent to the door-to-puncture (D2P) time. Fortunately, the patient experienced no perioperative complications, neurological deficits, or recurrence, achieving a favorable outcome. This case highlights the importance of early diagnosis and treatment of LVO, active cooperation with the ECMO team, and comprehensive supportive care.

Anticoagulant therapy during ECMO treatment

The fundamental principle of thrombosis prevention during ECMO is the proper control of heparin infusion, maintaining target values of APTT or ACT. However, even with adherence to these targets, thrombosis within the ECMO circuit is reported to occur in 81 to 90% of cases,18,19) indicating that thrombus formation during ECMO use is almost inevitable. From the perspective of preventing embolic complications, the ideal approach would be to replace the circuit if thrombosis is observed. However, disadvantages such as blood loss from the circuit and the associated medical costs must also be considered.

The appropriate timing for circuit exchange after the onset of LVO should be carefully considered on a case-by-case basis, weighing the risks and benefits mentioned above. However, the procedure for ECMO circuit exchange takes approximately 30 mins, including preparation time. If LVO originating from the circuit is strongly suspected, it is advisable to replace the ECMO circuit before EVT, as long as sufficient manpower and time are available from the onset of stroke to the procedure.

Thrombus components attached to the ECMO oxygenator have been reported to include fibrin, platelets, von Willebrand factor, white blood cells, and, occasionally, pump axle thrombus fragments.18) However, there are no reports on thrombus components in LVO cases with ECMO treatment. In contrast, an analysis of LVO thrombi in patients with a left ventricular assist device (LVAD) found no significant difference in thrombus volume compared to non-LVAD patients, although a trend toward larger thrombi was observed. Additionally, thrombi in LVAD-assisted patients contained lower red blood cell content and higher levels of platelets and fibrin components.20) These findings may help explain the unusually long thrombus observed in our case. EVT should be performed with consideration for the potential presence of large thrombi. Given the limitations of MRI-based thrombus evaluation during ECMO, pathological analysis of retrieved thrombi is recommended.

Conclusions

In patients undergoing VA-ECMO, limitations such as the inability to perform MRI, inapplicability of rt-PA, and difficulty with puncture access pose significant challenges. However, except in cases where the arterial cannula is inserted into the axillary artery, contrast-enhanced CT or perfusion CT is useful for determining the suitability for thrombectomy. When thrombectomy is considered appropriate, EVT should be promptly performed.

During ECMO, long-term sedation and systemic management are typically required, making it crucial to conduct regular neurological assessments to ensure early diagnosis of LVO and implement appropriate treatment.
