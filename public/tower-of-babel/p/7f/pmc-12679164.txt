The impact of dynamic kidney function prior to using sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors in type 2 diabetes patients with low‐risk renal disease progression
Authors: Yen‐Chen Chiu, Wanchana Singhan, Surarong Chinwong, Dujrudee Chinwong, Shang‐Jyh Hwang, Yaw‐Bin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionFew studies have assessed whether dynamic kidney function prior to sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) initiation influenced subsequent renal outcomes. Thus, the study aimed to investigate whether combining estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) slope and urinary albumin/creatinine ratio (UACR) change prior to using SGLT2i contributes to subsequent kidney outcomes.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized data from the Kaohsiung Medical University Hospital Research Database (KMUHRD) in Taiwan. We identified 975 SGLT2i new users with type 2 diabetes from 2016 to 2020, who had an eGFR >60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and UACR <30 mg/g 1 year prior to using SGLT2i. Patients were categorized into four groups based on an eGFR decline rate of 2.5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year and a UACR increase of 30%. The primary study outcomes included a >30% eGFR decline with a drop in eGFR categories and a >43% UACR increase with progression in UACR categories.ResultsAfter SGLT2i treatment, compared with the non‐progressive renal function group, the glomerular injury group significantly lowered the risk of eGFR decline (adjusted hazard ratio [95% CI] 0.384 [0.199, 0.740]) and UACR progression (adjusted hazard ratio [95% CI] 0.514 [0.313, 0.846]). In addition, for those with a major eGFR decline before starting SGLT2i, significant improvements in the eGFR slope (P < 0.05) were observed after the treatment, irrespective of UACR increase.ConclusionsEarly initiation of SGLT2i among type 2 diabetes patients with low‐risk renal disease progression and glomerular injury renal status may prevent them from progressing into chronic kidney disease.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with type 2 diabetes have higher chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression, posing a significant global healthcare concern 1 . The progression of kidney disease in diabetes is usually asymptomatic until the onset of kidney failure 2 . Recently, combining both the rate of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline and albuminuria (proteinuria) changes have been regarded as surrogate endpoints for kidney disease in clinical trials 2 . In the multicenter retrospective study, an eGFR decline of >2.5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year and a >30% increase in UACR were used to categorize patients among type 2 diabetes patients because these metrics established a strong predictor of increased risk of end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD). Additionally, this association was stronger in patients with normal kidney function (eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2) than in those with kidney dysfunction. However, the study did not account for pharmaceutical interventions 3 .

Sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) could promptly target hyperfiltration, maintain the eGFR slope, reduce the risk of proteinuria progression, and exhibit anti‐inflammatory properties 4 . In addition, clinical studies have recently demonstrated that SGLT2i could effectively reduce worsening kidney function among patients with type 2 diabetes and variable baseline renal function 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 . Nevertheless, few studies have tried to assess whether the association between combined change in eGFR slope and UACR prior to using SGLT2i contributes to different kidney outcomes after SGLT2i therapy among type 2 diabetes patients. Since the early initiation of preventive strategies is essential for preventing kidney deterioration in type 2 diabetes 11 , the aim of this study was to examine the impact of dynamic kidney function 1 year prior to using SGLT2i on subsequent kidney outcomes in type 2 diabetes patients with low‐risk renal disease progression.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data sources

All patient information in this retrospective cohort study was collected from Kaohsiung Medical University Hospital Research Database (KMUHRD) in Taiwan. The variables collected for the analysis included patients' demographic characteristics, diagnostic codes, prescription details, clinical visit dates, laboratory data, and date of death. Diagnoses were identified using ICD‐9‐CM or ICD‐10‐CM codes, and medications were verified using ATC codes (Tables S1 and S2). Approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Kaohsiung Medical University Hospital (approval number: KMUHIRB‐E(I)‐20230172) before conducting this study. Moreover, KMUHRD offered anonymity through pseudonymization of identification numbers to protect individual privacy.

Study population

The index date was regarded as the initial prescription date of SGLT2i. Since the first supply of SGLT2i was purchased by KMUH beginning in 2016, all of the patients taking SGLT2i were considered as new users. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (1) Patients aged ≥20 years, diagnosed with type 2 diabetes with at least one inpatient diagnosis or two outpatient diagnoses from 2016 to 2020; (2) patients with complete information regarding gender, birthday, comorbidities, and comedication within 1 year before the index date; (3) patients treated with empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, or canagliflozin, irrespective of dose, brand, or formulation (single or compound) during the enrollment period; (4) patients with at least two eGFR and UACR measurements within 1 year before the index date and at least one eGFR and UACR measurement within 1 year after the index date.

To confirm the specific study population, patients were withdrawn if they met any of the exclusion criteria such as: (1) Cumulative days of prescription of SGLT2i for <28 days; (2) a history of ESKD, acute kidney disease, dialysis, or kidney transplant within 1 year before the index date; (3) with eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and/or UACR >30 mg/g 1 year prior to using SGLT2i; (4) two pre‐index eGFR measurements were less than 90 days.

Study design

Because early identification of patients who may benefit most from SGLT2i is critical to prevent kidney deterioration in type 2 diabetes, this study focused on patients with low‐risk renal disease function progression (eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and UACR ≤30 mg/g). To capture the recent kidney trajectory, changes in eGFR slope and UACR percentage change 1 year prior to using SGLT2i were evaluated. Patients were categorized based on dynamic changes in renal function to investigate subsequent renal outcomes after using SGLT2i.

The linear mixed method was utilized to calculate the subject‐specific eGFR slope for each participant based on eGFR measurements 1 year prior to the index date. The eGFR slopes were then classified into >2.5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year decline (major eGFR decline) and ≤2.5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year decline (minor eGFR decline) 3 , 12 . Meanwhile, the percentage change in UACR was calculated based on two UACR measurements recorded at baseline and 1 year prior. UACR increase were then classified into >30% increase (significant UACR increase) and ≤30% increase (non‐significant UACR increase) 3 , 13 , 14 , 15 . To categorize the dynamic changes in kidney function 1 year before using SGLT2i, eGFR slope decline and UACR percentage change were grouped into four categories, following a similar classification in the previous study 16 : Non‐progressive group with eGFR slope decline ≤2.5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year and UACR increase ≤30%; Hyperfiltration group with eGFR slope decline ≤2.5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year and UACR increase >30%; Glomerular collapse group with eGFR slope decline >2.5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year and UACR increase ≤30%; Glomerular injury group with eGFR slope decline >2.5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year and UACR increase >30%.

Outcome measurements

A >30% decline in eGFR was considered a reasonably likely kidney surrogate endpoint in many trials 2 . A >43% increase in albuminuria over 2 years was associated with the increased risk of ESKD 17 . Considering the relatively higher baseline eGFR and relatively shorter observation period in this study, the primary study outcomes included a >30% eGFR decline with a drop in eGFR categories and a >43% UACR increase with progression in UACR categories. Patients would be followed up until 2022.12.31 or the occurrence of the interested outcome. Individuals would be excluded when the following occurs: (1) interested outcomes taking place within 180 days after the index date; (2) death; (3) discontinued SGLT2i records over 90 days. Furthermore, we would examine changes in eGFR slopes before and after SGLT2i treatment.

Data management and statistical analyses

Based on combined changes in UACR and eGFR slope, continuous and categorical variables among the four groups were analyzed by using one‐way ANOVA (or Kruskal–Wallis test) and Pearson chi‐squared tests (or Fisher's exact test); multivariable Cox proportional regression was used to analyze the association between changes in UACR and eGFR and the kidney outcomes. In this analysis, we defined the non‐progressive group as the reference group and adjusted baseline covariates that were unbalanced at baseline, including sex, age, A1c, UACR, eGFR, insulin, heparin, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitor (DPP‐4i), angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (ACEI/ARB), and fibrate. The interval between the first recorded outpatient visit and SGLT2i initiation was used as a surrogate for diabetes duration and included as a covariate in the multivariable model to account for disease chronicity. To conduct a self‐comparison of the eGFR slope before and after the administration of SGLT2i based on the four groups, we employed a paired t‐test (or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test). To identify the differences in the eGFR slope change before and after using SGLT2i among the four groups, anova and Tukey's post hoc analysis were employed. All statistical analyses were two‐tailed, with significance at a P‐value <0.05 and conducted by SAS 9.4.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

During the period from 2016 to 2020, 2,157 type 2 diabetes patients who were treated with SGLT2i and met the criteria of eGFR and UACR measurements before and after the index date were included in the study. After that, those patients who met exclusion criteria were phased out. Finally, our cohort was comprised of 975 (45.2%) type 2 diabetes adults with low‐risk renal disease progression, among whom 44.0% received dapagliflozin, 52.8% empagliflozin, and 3.2% canagliflozin. They were categorized into four groups based on the combined changes in UACR and eGFR slope before using SGLT2i. The non‐progressive group had the highest proportion of patients (n = 371, 38.05%), while the glomerular injury group had the fewest (n = 114, 11.69%) (Figure 1). The mean age of the study cohort was 61.29 (SD 10.74) years and 57.54% were men. The baseline mean eGFR was 90.53 mL/min/1.73 m2 (SD 15.96), median UACR was 8.23 mg/g (IQR 4.50–17.63), and mean A1c was 60.79 mmol/mol (SD 12.69). At the baseline, the glomerular injury and glomerular collapse groups were likely to be older males with lower levels of eGFR. The hyperfiltration and glomerular injury groups were likely to have higher levels of UACR and A1c. Regarding comedications, the glomerular injury group was likely to be users of insulin and heparin; the glomerular collapse group was likely to be users of DPP‐4i, ACRI/ARB, and fibrates (Table 1).

Association between eGFR and UACR increase and kidney outcomes

Regarding the outcome of >30% eGFR decline with drop in eGFR categories, the mean follow‐up was 3.69 ± 1.88 years. Compared with the non‐progressive group, the hyperfiltration group and the glomerular collapse group showed no significant difference in the risk (adjusted HR = 1.072, 95% CI: 0.712–1.616, P = 0.7390; adjusted HR = 0.744, 95% CI: 0.443–1.250, P = 0.2645, respectively). Conversely, the glomerular injury group significantly lowered the risk (adjusted HR = 0.384, 95% CI: 0.199–0.740, P = 0.0043) (Table 2). On the other hand, with respect to the outcome of >43% UACR increase with progress in UACR categories, the mean follow‐up was 3.44 ± 1.86 years. Compared with the non‐progressive group, the hyperfiltration group and the glomerular injury group significantly reduced the risk (adjusted HR = 0.560, 95% CI: 0.397–0.792, P = 0.0010; adjusted HR = 0.514, 95% CI: 0.313–0.846, P = 0.0089, respectively). The glomerular collapse group showed no significant difference in the risk (adjusted HR = 0.969, 95% CI: 0.665–1.412, P = 0.8711) (Table 3).

eGFR slope change after SGLT2i treatment

Within the first year after using SGLT2i, eGFR slopes significantly declined in both the hyperfiltration and the non‐progressive group, compared with baseline 1 year (pre and post difference: −0.8 [SE 0.10], P < 0.05; pre and post difference: −0.84 [SE 0.11], P < 0.05, respectively). Conversely, eGFR slopes significantly improved in the glomerular injury and the glomerular collapse group, compared with baseline 1 year (pre and post difference: 1.38 [SE 0.21], P < 0.05; pre and post difference: 1.22 [SE 0.13], P < 0.05, respectively). Within the second year after using SGLT2i, eGFR slopes significantly improved across four groups, compared with baseline 1 year (pre and post difference in the hyperfiltration group: 0.05 [SE 0.16], P < 0.05; pre and post difference in the non‐progressive group: 0.02 [SE 0.14], P < 0.05; pre and post difference in the glomerular injury group: 3.54 [SE 0.30], P < 0.05; pre and post difference in the glomerular collapse group: 3.56 [SE 0.25], P < 0.05). The results of self‐comparison in an eGFR slope are shown in Table 4. Additionally, the improvement in an eGFR slope before and within the first and the second year after using SGLT2i was significantly greater in the glomerular injury and the glomerular collapse group, compared with the hyperfiltration and the non‐progressive group (Table S3).

DISCUSSION

In this single‐center, retrospective cohort study of 975 adults with type 2 diabetes who had an eGFR >60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and UACR <30 mg/g 1 year prior to using SGLT2i, we found that those with glomerular injury renal status before the treatment may be less likely to progress to CKD. In terms of the renal‐related clinical outcomes, the glomerular injury group significantly decelerated >30% eGFR decline with a drop in eGFR categories after the therapy. Regardless of eGFR slope decline level, both the hyperfiltration group and the glomerular injury group significantly reduced >43% UACR progression with worsening of the categories after the therapy. Although we could not observe the long‐term clinical outcomes due to the short follow‐up period, CREDENCE post hoc analysis has demonstrated that compared with an acute increase in eGFR, an initial eGFR decline >30% was associated with an increased risk of kidney‐related and overall adverse events 13 . In Taiwan, Chan et al. 17 reported that compared with individuals with no eGFR decline, those with an initial eGFR decline >30% had a higher cumulative risk of new‐onset atrial fibrillation, major adverse cardiovascular events, and composite renal outcomes. As for change in albuminuria, one individual meta‐analysis found a 43% progress in UACR over 2 years was associated with cardiovascular mortality (adjusted HR: 1.14, 95% CI 1.06–1.22) and all‐cause mortality (adjusted HR: 1.07, 95% CI 1.05–1.09). Besides, a 43% progress in UACR over 2 years was also associated with ESKD, even in UACR level of <30 mg/g (adjusted HR: 1.22, 95% CI 1.17–1.28) 13 . These studies revealed that early protection of eGFR decline or UACR progression may lower the risk of adverse clinical outcomes in the future.

In addition, our study showed that regardless of the UACR change level, patients with a major eGFR slope decline (eGFR decline >2.5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year) before starting SGLT2i treatment demonstrated greater improvement in their eGFR slope. Similar to previous studies, Hirai et al. observed that among 85 type 2 diabetes patients with eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2/year, those who experienced a major eGFR decline rate (eGFR decline >3 mL/min/1.73 m2/year) before initiating SGLT2i therapy exhibited a notably slowed annual eGFR decline after SGLT2i treatment 18 . In addition, Sada et al. 19 demonstrated that among 165 Type 2 diabetes patients with eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2, Pearson's correlation analysis showed the eGFR slope in the 2 years before SGLT2i treatment was inversely correlated with the (Δ eGFR slope) after the therapy. Miyaoka et al. 20 noted that among 128 CKD patients, logistic analysis showed that the rapid decline of eGFR before SGLT2i treatment was a significant variable for the improvement in eGFR slopes. However, these three studies did not take the combined change in eGFR slope and albuminuria into consideration.

Although the prior study has demonstrated that combined changes in eGFR and UACR are strongly associated with the risk of ESKD, that study did not account for the effects of SGLT2i treatment 3 . Our study investigated whether dynamic changes in eGFR slope and UACR during 1 year prior to SGLT2i initiation can identify patients who may derive the greatest renal benefit from SGLT2i therapy. To our knowledge, such pre‐treatment dynamic evaluation has not been extensively explored in a real‐world cohort with low‐risk renal disease progression.

There were several strengths to our cohort study. First, since the KMUHRD can offer laboratory data, the current study could utilize a combination of change in UACR and eGFR before SGLT2i treatment to investigate trajectories of parameters after the therapy. Second, the albuminuria was measured by UACR test, which was recommended for kidney disease screening annually by guidelines 21 , 22 . Third, eGFR was calculated by CKD‐EPI equation, which was more accurate than the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation (MDRD) formula, particularly for eGFR values greater than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 23 . Last but not least, the eGFR slope was calculated by a linear mixed model which provided a more comprehensive analysis 24 . Nevertheless, some limitations existed in our retrospective cohort study that needed to be addressed. First, this was a retrospective single‐center study; naturally, the sample size might be a cause for concern. Second, some laboratory data were based on single measurements—6 patients (0.6%) had only one post‐treatment eGFR value, and 22 patients (2.3%) had only one post‐treatment UACR value. Though these values may be affected by regression to the mean, the potential impact on overall findings is likely minimal due to the small proportion of such cases. Third, the duration of diabetes could not be directly determined due to limitations of the database. As a surrogate, the interval between the first recorded outpatient visit and the initiation of SGLT2i was used and included in the multivariable model to account for disease chronicity. Lastly, since SGLT2i was first introduced in 2016, the follow‐up period was too short to observe long‐term outcomes. Despite some unavoidable limitations, the findings from this study could inform clinical hypotheses for future research and assessment.

In conclusion, we found that for those with major eGFR slope decline before starting SGLT2i, significant improvements in the eGFR slope were observed after the treatment, irrespective of UACR increase. Of note, as there is still no therapeutic drug that can restore renal function once it has declined, early SGLT2i treatment may help prevent progression to CKD among type 2 diabetes patients with initially low‐risk renal disease progression who exhibit a major eGFR decline and a significant increase in UACR.
