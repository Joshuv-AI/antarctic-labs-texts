The Texas Abortion Ban and Maternal Mental Health
Authors: Mir M. Ali, Alyssa L. Burnett, Jonathan H. Cantor, Annie Song, Fang Zhang, Hao Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study examines the association of the implementation of Texas Senate Bill 8, which banned abortions after embryonic cardiac activity, with maternal mental health outcomes.

Introduction

Mental health disorders affect approximately 1 in 5 US women during the perinatal period, making it one of the leading causes of maternal mortality.1,2,3 Most often these conditions remain untreated due to lack of adequate access to mental health care and inequities in the mental health care delivery system.4 Changes in maternal health care access are often associated with changes in maternal mental health outcomes. In June 2022, the US Supreme Court decision on Dobbs vs Jackson Women’s Health Organization abolished the federal right to abortion, which has been associated with possible reductions in access to maternal care,5,6 and in turn maternal mental health care.7,8

Multiple mechanisms may explain the association of abortion bans with maternal mental health. The presence of abortion bans may create an overarching sense of insecurity and a loss of personal autonomy.9 For many, the lack of autonomy may contribute to increased anxiety and distress, even for individuals who are not currently pregnant or seeking an abortion.10 Further, restrictions on future reproductive choices may also be associated with increased anticipatory stress and anxiety, particularly among individuals who wish to retain control over family planning.10 Restrictive abortion laws, such as mandatory waiting periods, parental consent laws, and travel requirements, create substantial logistical and financial barriers to maternal care.11 These system-level changes may be associated with access to preventive, reproductive, and mental health care for all mothers, potentially contributing to increased stress and other mental health problems.12 While some of these mechanisms are more directly relevant to individuals who are pregnant or considering pregnancy, taken together these broader structural and psychosocial pathways may affect the mental health of all reproductive-aged mothers, including those with older children.

Research into the effects of abortion restrictions on maternal mental health is limited,13 despite a growing interest in this topic following the Dobbs decision.14 Literature in this area has focused mostly on overall population-level mental health rather than examining maternal mental health specifically.7,15,16,17 In addition, previous research on abortion restrictions has examined different outcomes, such as infant mortality, pregnancy-related mortality, and female mental health, rather than maternal mental health.14,16,18 We aim to address these gaps in the literature by examining the association of Texas Senate Bill 8 (SB8) with maternal mental health. SB8 was enacted in September 2021,19 and at that time was the nation’s most restrictive abortion law, banning abortions after embryonic cardiac activity (as early as 5-6 weeks’ gestation) and offering no exemptions for congenital anomalies.14 Similar to previous research,16 we compare mental health outcomes among reproductive-age mothers in Texas with the mental health outcomes of reproductive-age mothers in states that did not restrict abortion in order to estimate the association of abortion bans with maternal mental health. We extend on the existing literature by focusing on reproductive-age mothers. Studying this population is critical because their mental health is closely associated with the well-being and developmental outcomes of their children and the stability of the entire family unit.20,21 Specifically, in this study we use a difference-in-differences (DiD) research design with recent national survey data to test the hypothesis that reproductive age mothers in Texas reported worse mental health status after SB8 compared with reproductive age mothers in states without an abortion ban.

Methods

Study Sample

This cross-sectional study used data from the National Survey of Children’s Health (NSCH) from 2016 to 2023. The NSCH is an annual, cross-sectional survey that collects parent-reported data on parents’ and children’s physical and mental health, child access to and quality of health care, and child and family sociodemographic characteristics on children ages 0 to 17 years. The NSCH has been used to study maternal mental and physical health.3 Our study sample included reproductive-age mothers (18-49 years). All female parents were included regardless of biological or adoptive status.3 In households with 2 female parents, both were included. Parents with missing values for the outcome or covariates were not included. The study was determined exempt from review and the requirement of informed consent by the Harvard Pilgrim Health Care Institute institutional review board in accordance with the Common Rule because the NCHS was deidentified, public-use data. Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Outcome Measure

Female parent respondents were asked, “If this child’s mother is a primary caregiver and lives in the household, in general, what is the status of mother’s mental and emotional health?” Our primary outcome was whether the mother was reported to have fair or poor mental and emotional health status.

Covariates

Child-Level Characteristics

Self-reported by the parent, child race and ethnicity were categorized as non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, and non-Hispanic other (including multiracial and any race or ethnicity not otherwise specified) based on categorical levels of the child race variable in the publicly available data. Race and ethnicity were included to examine differential policy impacts and to help isolate the policy effect from preexisting group-level differences. Parent-reported child insurance type was defined as private (including employer and Marketplace coverage), public (including Medicaid and Children’s Health Insurance Program), other (including Tricare, Indian Health Services, and other types of insurance), and uninsured. We also included an indicator for whether the child had special health care needs.

Mother-Level and Household-Level Characteristics

Mother-level variables included mother age group (18-29, 30-39, and 40-49 years), education level (high school graduate or less and greater than high school), whether the mother was born in the US, mother marital status (married; not married, living with spouse or partner; never married; divorced; separated; or widowed), and whether the mother was in a same-sex relationship. Household-level variables included household language (English, Spanish, and other), household structure (single parent, 1 child; single parent, 2 children; single parent, ≥3 children; 2 parents, 1 child; 2 parents, 2 children; and 2 parents, ≥3 children), income as percentage of the federal poverty level (<100%, 100%-199%, 200%-399%, and ≥400%), and census region (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West).

Statistical Analysis

We used a DiD design to estimate the potential association of Texas SB8 with the mental and emotional health of reproductive-age mothers. Mothers residing in Texas were considered the treatment group, and mothers who resided in any non–abortion ban state (Alaska, Arizona, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Hawaii, Illinois, Kansas, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Dakota, Ohio, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, and Wyoming) were considered the control group.14 We considered 2016 to 2020 as the prepolicy period and 2021 to 2023 as the postpolicy period.

We first calculated unweighted and weighted descriptive characteristics of the study sample overall and by treatment and control group. We conducted both unadjusted and adjusted event study analyses to confirm the parallel trends assumption held between Texas residents and residents in control states (eTable 1, eFigure 1, and eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). All analyses were conducted using the Stata svy command to account for the complex survey design of the NSCH and weighting structure. Finally, we estimated unadjusted and adjusted DiD models (logistic regression) to assess the change in the likelihood of mothers’ fair or poor mental and emotional health outcomes following the abortion ban in Texas compared with mothers in control states. Margins were reported using the Stata margins command for interpretable DiD estimates. Because income as a percentage of the federal poverty level is sparsely populated, the NSCH provides 6 multiply imputed poverty variables and recommends using multiple imputation procedures in the analyses.22 Stata could not report margins in multiple imputation settings and therefore we did not include income level as a covariate in our adjusted models.

After estimating the models on the entire sample, we stratified the analysis by mother’s age, mother’s education, and child’s insurance (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). We performed 4 sensitivity analyses of the likelihood of (1) fair or poor maternal mental health using linear regression (eTable 3 in Supplement 1), (2) fair or poor maternal mental health using 2021 as a washout year (eTable 4 in Supplement 1), (3) fair or poor maternal physical health (eTable 5 in Supplement 1), and (4) father’s fair or poor mental health to assess the specificity of the observed associations (eTable 6 in Supplement 1).

Statistical significance was set at a 2-sided P < .05. All analyses were completed using Stata version 19 (StataCorp) from April to October 2025.

Results

Table 1 presents demographic characteristics of our study sample after stratification by the control group in non–abortion ban states (152 573 individuals; 47.1% [95% CI, 46.4%-47.7%] aged 30-39 years) and treatment group in Texas (4323 individuals; 47.2% [95% CI, 45.1%-49.4%] aged 30-39 years). Children’s racial and ethnic makeup varied by location. In nonban states, the largest group was non-Hispanic White at 50.8% (95% CI, 50.2%-51.4%), followed by Hispanic at 27.0% (95% CI, 26.4%-27.7%), non-Hispanic other race or ethnicity at 13.6% (95% CI, 13.2%-13.9%), and non-Hispanic Black at 8.6% (95% CI, 8.2%-8.9%). Among children in Texas, the largest group was Hispanic at 50.3% (95% CI, 48.1-52.4%), followed by Non-Hispanic White at 32.4% (95% CI, 30.6%-34.3%), non-Hispanic Black at 9.7% (95% CI, 8.4%-11.1%), and non-Hispanic other race or ethnicity at 7.6% (95% CI, 6.8%-8.5%). The prevalence of children with special health care needs was similar between the groups: 17.8% (95% CI, 17.4%-18.2%) in nonban states and 15.8% (95% CI, 15.3%-18.3%) in Texas. Private insurance was the dominant type in both, covering 63.0% (95% CI, 62.4%-63.6%) in nonban states and 54.0% (95% CI, 51.8%-56.2%) in Texas. Notably, the rate of uninsured children and children covered under other insurance in Texas was more than double compared with nonban states, at 11.9% (95% CI, 10.4%-13.7%) vs 4.3% (95% CI, 4.0%-4.5%), respectively. For mother age, the majority in both groups fell into the 30- to 39-year age group (47.1% [95% CI, 46.4%-47.7%] in nonban states and 47.2% [95% CI, 45.1%-49.4%] in Texas). A majority of mothers in both the nonban states and Texas were in the greater than high school group (75.7% [95% CI, 75.1%-74.4%] in nonban states and 67.2% [95% CI, 64.8%-69.4%] in Texas). Similarly, a majority of mothers in both groups were born in the US (73.8% [95% CI, 73.1%-77.4%] in nonban states and 68.5% [95% CI, 66.3%-70.6%] in Texas) and spoke English as the primary language in the household (84.0% [95% CI, 83.5%-84.6%] in nonban states and 77.1% [95% CI, 74.9%-79.1%] in Texas).

The DiD analysis results are reported in Table 2 and show a statistically significant increase in the proportion of mothers reporting fair or poor mental health in Texas after the abortion ban, relative to mothers in states without a ban. Specifically, the proportion of Texas mothers reporting fair or poor mental health rose from 4.6% (3.2-6.0) in the preban period to 9.3% (7.3%-11.2%) in the postban period. In contrast, mothers in control states experienced a more modest increase from 5.4% (5.0%-5.8%) to 7.5% (7.1%-7.9%). Our adjusted DiD model, which accounted for the complex survey design of the NSCH and weights and controlled for household covariates, estimated an increase of 2.5 percentage points (95% CI, <0.0-5.0 percentage points) in the likelihood of fair or poor mental health among Texas mothers relative to the control group.

The sensitivity analysis using linear regression and the Stata margin command found a slight difference in the magnitude of the association compared with our main results, but the overall conclusion remained the same (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). Our sensitivity analysis of the association of the Texas abortion ban with mothers reporting fair or poor physical health compared with nonban states did not show any statistically significant association (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). The association of an abortion ban with the likelihood of fathers’ reporting fair or poor mental health was also not statistically significant (eTable 6 in the Supplement 1).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study found that mothers in Texas experienced a significant decline in mental health following the SB8 abortion ban compared with mothers in comparison states without an abortion ban. We found the estimated association to be more pronounced among mothers who were covered by public health insurance.

The results of this analysis are consistent with previous literature, which documents significant adverse outcomes associated with abortion restrictions and/or ban on maternal health,15,16 but contribute to the field by providing evidence of a decline in mental health of mothers regardless of whether they were seeking abortion or not. This increase in fair or poor mental health suggests that Texas’s unique legal environment—which includes civil enforcement and no exemptions for congenital anomalies—may have broader implications for health care access more generally. The shortage of medical residents and the exodus of clinicians from the state suggests that the observed decline in mental health may be a negative externality of a strained maternal health infrastructure that affects all mothers, including those past their early reproductive years.5,6

This finding could be associated with several factors that are related to broader negative consequences of an abortion ban for the health care system. For example, compared with states where abortion is accessible, states with restrictive policies may have fewer maternal clinicians,23 more maternity care deserts,23,24 and higher rates of care delays,25 which are associated with worse mental health outcomes for mothers. In addition, the literature has found that in states with abortion bans, there has been a documented exodus of clinicians and a decrease in the number of medical residents choosing to train there.26,27,28 This shortage of skilled health care professionals can lead to a decline in the overall quality of maternal and reproductive health care,29 thus potentially having an adverse impact on maternal mental health.

Our findings also suggest that the mental health implications of SB8 extend beyond individuals who are pregnant or actively seeking abortion care. While mechanisms such as delayed or foregone pregnancy-related care are likely most salient for mothers of younger children or those considering additional births, broader system-level and psychosocial mechanisms may affect all reproductive-age mothers. These include reduced availability of maternal and reproductive health clinicians, increased care fragmentation, and heightened stress associated with constrained reproductive autonomy.10,11,12 Such mechanisms may plausibly relate to mothers of older children through reduced access to preventive care, increased caregiving strain in a constrained health system, and broader stressors linked to policy environments.23,25

The findings of our analysis have several implications for the post-Dobbs health policy landscape. A decline in maternal mental health observed alongside abortion bans may restrict the ability to parent in a safe and healthy environment, thus having a population health influence that goes beyond the direct ramifications of an abortion ban. The findings also highlight the importance of expanding mental health services, especially for mothers, and integrating mental health screenings and support into routine prenatal and postpartum care. We further note that Texas represents a particularly important policy context. SB8 was among the earliest and most restrictive abortion laws, with a unique enforcement mechanism and limited exemptions. As such, Texas may serve as an early indicator of how similar policies might be associated with maternal mental health and health systems functioning in other states.

Limitations

Despite using a large national dataset and a DiD research design, the study has several limitations. First, our data are self-reported, which introduces the potential for social desirability bias and recall bias, and thus may result in an underestimation or overestimation of the parent’s mental health status. In addition, the public discourse surrounding SB8 may have increased emotional salience, leading to a heightened awareness (and thus increased reporting) of distress. Because these measures are subjective rather than clinical diagnoses, our results may reflect changes in perceived psychological well-being rather than clinical pathology. Furthermore, the survey relied on parent 1 as the respondent for parent 2 and children in the household. While 69% of our sample was considered parent 1, the proxy-reported measures for parent 2 may also have resulted in underestimation or overestimation of the primary outcome. Second, the DiD approach relies on key causal assumptions, including parallel trends between treatment and control groups, absence of differential concurrent shocks, and stable composition over time. Although our event study analyses support the parallel trends assumption, violations of these assumptions due to unobserved time-varying confounders cannot be ruled out. Third, our data are cross-sectional, which limits our ability to make causal inferences. Fourth, we are unable to directly observe pregnancy status, recency of childbirth, or fertility intentions, which limited our ability to distinguish mechanisms that may differentially affect mothers of younger vs older children. Fifth, SB8 was passed during the COVID-19 pandemic and effects of the COVID-19 pandemic alongside newly legal abortion restrictions may be compounded. Sixth, our study period ended in 2023, 2 years after the SB8 implementation, and our results indicated its short-term impact. Future studies need to assess the long-term impacts.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study of reproductive-age mothers between 2017 and 2023, we found that residence in a state with an abortion ban was associated with worse mental health outcomes compared with residence in states without such a ban. This finding is particularly relevant after the Supreme Court Dobbs decision and suggests that a negative externality of abortion bans might be adverse maternal mental health outcomes.21,24
