Structure–activity relationship studies of febuxostat derivatives identify potent anti-lung cancer agents that induce DNA damage and autophagy
Authors: Xixi Hou, Xuanwei Zhang, Jingjing Guo, Yajie Guo, Jianxue Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A series of febuxostat–1,2,3-triazole derivatives were designed and synthesized to investigate their structure–activity relationships (SARs) and anticancer mechanisms. Their cytotoxic activities were evaluated against human lung cancer cell lines (A549, PC-9, and H460). Several compounds exhibited notable antiproliferative activity against PC-9 and H460 cells while showing low toxicity toward normal L02 hepatocytes. Among them, compound 14h displayed the most potent activity, with IC₅₀ values of 13.87 ± 0.25 µM (A549), 3.81 ± 0.50 µM (PC-9), and 10.01 ± 0.03 µM (H460). Further biological studies revealed that 14h significantly inhibited tumor cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner and primarily induced apoptosis in A549 and H460 cells, accompanied by the activation of autophagy. In addition, 14h markedly triggered DNA damage, as evidenced by increased DNA strand breaks and elevated γH2AX expression. RT-PCR and Western blot analyses further demonstrated that 14h regulated the expression of key genes and proteins associated with oxidative stress, DNA damage response, apoptosis, and autophagy, including p21, Bcl-2, caspase-6, Keap1, H2AX, ATG7, γH2AX, LC3, caspase-9, and PARP. Molecular docking analysis suggested that 14h could effectively bind to the active site of PARP through hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions with key residues, potentially contributing to DNA damage accumulation. Collectively, these findings indicate that compound 14h exerts potent anticancer activity through DNA damage–mediated activation of apoptotic and autophagic pathways, highlighting febuxostat–triazole hybrids as promising candidates for lung cancer therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-60785-3.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, posing a serious threat to human health with persistently high incidence and death rates1–3. The discovery and development of novel anticancer agents with high efficacy and low toxicity have therefore become a long-standing focus in biomedical research4. Although clinically used anticancer drugs can suppress tumor growth to some extent5,6, their therapeutic outcomes are often limited by drawbacks such as single-target specificity, severe toxicity to normal tissues, and the rapid emergence of drug resistance7,8. Consequently, identifying new anticancer compounds with multiple mechanisms of action and high selectivity toward tumor cells is of great importance for improving therapeutic efficacy and patient prognosis.

During cancer initiation and progression, biological processes such as aberrant DNA damage repair9, dysregulated apoptosis10, and imbalanced autophagic activity11,12 play crucial roles. When DNA damage surpasses the cellular repair capacity, it can trigger apoptotic cascades and lead to cell death13. Meanwhile, autophagy acts as a double-edged sword: it can either protect cells by removing damaged organelles or promote cell death under sustained stress conditions14. The intricate crosstalk between DNA damage, apoptosis, and autophagy offers multiple molecular targets for the development of next-generation anticancer agents.

Among various heterocyclic scaffolds, the 1,2,3-triazole ring has attracted considerable attention in medicinal chemistry owing to its unique physicochemical properties, metabolic stability, and broad biological activity spectrum15,16. As a bioisostere for amides, esters, and carboxylic acids17, it can coordinate with metal ions and inhibit metalloprotein enzymes, thereby affecting cancer cell proliferation and survival18–25. Moreover, the rigid, planar structure of 1,2,3-triazole facilitates DNA intercalation, enhancing its potential to induce DNA damage and disrupt genomic integrity. These characteristics make the triazole moiety an ideal pharmacophore for designing multi-target anticancer agents: Recent studies have demonstrated the diverse anticancer potential of 1,2,3-triazole-based hybrids. For instance, Zhao et al. incorporated a 1,2,3-triazole ring into the 25-OCH₃-PPD (AD-1) scaffold via click chemistry and obtained a series of compounds capable of significantly inducing DNA damage and apoptosis in cancer cells. These derivatives exhibited potent inhibitory activity against A549, MCF-7, and HeLa cells, and γ-H2AX immunofluorescence confirmed the induction of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) and cell-cycle arrest. Mechanistic studies revealed that compound 1 enhanced DNA damage through ROS generation and modulated the PI3K/Akt/Bcl-2 signaling pathway, downregulating PI3K, Bcl-2, and Akt while upregulating Bax and cytochrome c, leading to potent inhibition of A549 cells (IC₅₀ = 2.84 µM)26. Similarly, Nukala et al. reported a series of chloroquinoline-linked fused 1,2,3-triazole derivatives that exhibited strong cytotoxicity against multiple human cancer cell lines, including MCF-7, HeLa, A549, and PC-3, with compound 2 showing activity comparable to the classical topoisomerase II inhibitor etoposide. Given that etoposide stabilizes the DNA–enzyme cleavage complex, resulting in lethal DSBs, compound 2 likely exerts its anticancer effects through DNA damage and topoisomerase II inhibition. Molecular docking confirmed its strong binding affinity toward topoisomerase II, validating its potential as a Topo II inhibitor. Moreover, pharmacokinetic predictions using SWISS/ADME and pkCSM indicated that compound 2 meets Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge drug-likeness criteria, supporting its further development27. El Hamaky et al. designed and synthesized a novel series of quinazoline–1,2,3-triazole hybrids with dual or multiple targeting capabilities, simultaneously inhibiting EGFR, VEGFR-2, and Topo II. These compounds demonstrated potent antiproliferative effects against HeLa, HepG2, MCF-7, and HCT-116 cells, with excellent selectivity over normal WI-38 cells. Among them, compound 3 displayed remarkable multi-target potency, showing strong inhibition of EGFR (IC₅₀ = 0.103 µM), VEGFR-2 (IC₅₀ = 0.069 µM), and Topo II (IC₅₀ = 19.74 µM)—1.7 times more potent than etoposide (IC₅₀ = 34.19 µM). Mechanistic investigations indicated that compound 3 caused G2/M cell-cycle arrest and markedly increased apoptosis (44.08%), consistent with a Topo II–mediated DNA damage mechanism28. In another study, Boda et al. efficiently synthesized a new class of ethyl 2,7-dimethyl-4-oxo-3-[(1-phenyl-1 H-1,2,3-triazol-4-yl)methyl]−4,5-dihydro-3 H-pyrano[2,3-d]pyrimidine-6-carboxylates, which exhibited potent cytotoxicity across several cancer cell lines, including A549, HepG2, MCF-7, and SKOV3. Notably, compound 4 showed selective inhibition of A549 lung cancer cells with an IC₅₀ of 0.69 µM. Molecular docking suggested that these compounds effectively inhibit human topoisomerase II, implicating a DNA damage–related mechanism. Spectroscopic and viscosity analyses revealed that compound 4 binds CT-DNA primarily through groove binding, while DNA cleavage experiments confirmed its ability to directly induce DNA strand breaks in the absence of external reagents29. Collectively, these studies highlight the potential of 1,2,3-triazole-based hybrids as promising scaffolds for designing multi-target anticancer agents that act through DNA damage, apoptosis, and autophagy pathways. Building on these findings, we designed and synthesized a new series of febuxostat-1,2,3-triazole derivatives, aiming to establish their structure–activity relationships and elucidate their anti-lung cancer mechanisms involving DNA damage and autophagy regulation (Fig. 1).

The concept of structural hybridization integrates two or more pharmacophores with complementary biological activities into a single molecular framework through chemical coupling or covalent linkage. This strategy enables the creation of novel hybrid molecules that combine the beneficial features of their parent structures, potentially enhancing therapeutic potency, improving bioavailability, reducing toxicity, and achieving better clinical efficacy. Febuxostat (compound 5, Fig. 2), a selective xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) inhibitor, effectively suppresses uric acid production and exhibits additional pharmacological activities, including the reduction of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby delaying hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression and prolonging survival. Recent studies have shown that substitution of the thiazole ring in febuxostat with a 1,2,3-triazole moiety (compound 6, Fig. 2) significantly enhances XOR inhibitory activity. Notably, both the thiazole scaffold itself and the 1,2,3-triazole motif possess reported anticancer properties30–32. Considering febuxostat’s favorable hepatic accumulation and emerging antitumor potential, we introduced an alkyne terminus via amide condensation and subsequently performed a click reaction with azide derivatives to construct 1,2,3-triazole-containing hybrids. Based on this strategy, we employed a molecular hybridization approach to design and synthesize a series of febuxostat derivatives featuring a thiazole ring, an amide linkage, and a 1,2,3-triazole moiety33–40. These compounds were designed to exploit the 1,2,3-triazole scaffold’s ability to induce DNA damage and disrupt redox homeostasis in tumor cells, thereby exerting potential anticancer effects.Based on this rationale, a series of febuxostat-1,2,3-triazole derivatives were synthesized and evaluated for their cytotoxicity against multiple human cancer cell lines, including A549, PC-9, and H460 (non-small cell lung cancer), as well as normal L02 cells. Through cell viability and molecular mechanism assays, this study elucidated the structure–activity relationship and anticancer mechanisms of these hybrids, providing a theoretical and experimental foundation for the development of novel anti-lung cancer lead compounds.

Chemistry

In this synthetic route, ethyl 2-(4-hydroxyphenyl)−4-methylthiazole-5-carboxylate (7) was used as the starting material and reacted with polyphosphoric acid and hexamethylenetetramine to afford ethyl 2-(3-formyl-4-hydroxyphenyl)−4-methylthiazole-5-carboxylate (8). Subsequently, a substitution reaction with bromoisobutane yielded ethyl 2-(3-formyl-4-isobutoxyphenyl)−4-methylthiazole-5-carboxylate (9). Reaction of compound 9 with hydroxylamine hydrochloride produced ethyl 2-(3-cyano-4-isobutoxyphenyl)−4-methylthiazole-5-carboxylate (10), which was then hydrolyzed to give febuxostat (5)41. Next, febuxostat was coupled with 3-ethynylbenzoic acid or 4-ethynylbenzoic acid in the presence of HATU and DIPEA to generate the terminal alkyne intermediates 11 and 13, respectively. Compounds 11 and 13 subsequently underwent copper-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) reactions with various substituted azides to furnish 26 novel target derivatives (12a–12m and 14a–14m), as illustrated in Fig. 3. The structures of all target compounds were confirmed by ¹H and ¹³C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (Scheme 1).

Results and discussion

Cell viability of novel synthetic compounds

To systematically evaluate the cytotoxic potential of the newly synthesized compounds, an extended panel of human cancer cell lines was selected for in vitro testing, including A549 (non-small cell lung cancer, NSCLC), PC-9 (EGFR-mutant NSCLC), H460 (NSCLC), and H1299 (NSCLC). Additionally, the human normal liver cell line L02 was included to assess the compounds’ selectivity toward cancer cells. All cell lines were treated with each compound at a concentration of 20 µM for 72 h, and cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay.

The results indicated that most of the tested compounds exhibited limited inhibitory effects against these cancer cell lines (Table 1). Notably, only compound 14h demonstrated appreciable cytotoxicity across all four tumor cell lines. At 20 µM, the relative cell viabilities were 42.71 ± 0.49% for A549, 43.14 ± 0.17% for PC-9, 17.54 ± 0.26% for H460, and 71.74 ± 3.39% for H1299. Importantly, minimal toxicity was observed in normal L02 cells, with relative viability remaining above 70%, indicating favorable cancer cell selectivity.

Based on these findings, we further determined the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of compound 14h in A549, PC-9, and H460 cells to quantify its inhibitory potency. Compound 14h exhibited the most potent cytotoxicity among all tested compounds, with IC₅₀ values of 13.87 ± 0.25 µM, 3.81 ± 0.50 µM, and 10.01 ± 0.03 µM against A549, PC-9, and H460 cells, respectively. Its activity was comparable to that of 5-FU (IC₅₀ = 17.26 ± 1.82 µM, 9.04 ± 0.71 µM, and 22.79 ± 3.09 µM, respectively). Therefore, compound 14h was selected as the lead compound for subsequent mechanistic studies.

Live and dead cell staining

To visually confirm the cytotoxic effects of the lead compound 14h, Live/Dead cell staining was performed using Calcein-AM (a fluorescent probe for live cells) and propidium iodide (PI, a fluorescent probe for dead cells). The tested cell lines (A549, PC-9, H460) were treated with 14h at different concentrations (10µM and 20µM or 5µM and 10µM) for 24 h, followed by staining and imaging with a confocal laser scanning microscope (Fig. 3). Representative fluorescent images showed that, compared to the untreated control group (where only green fluorescence from live cells was dominant), the number of dead cells (labeled by red fluorescence) in the compound-treated groups increased significantly in a dose-dependent manner especially in PC-9 cell line. These results directly corroborated the cytotoxicity of 14h observed in the CCK-8 assay, further confirming their ability to induce cancer cell death.

Analysis of apoptosis assay

To investigate whether the cytotoxicity of 14h was mediated through the induction of apoptosis, flow cytometry analysis was performed using the Annexin-V-FITC/PI staining. The key cancer cell lines (A549, PC-9, H460) were treated with 14h at different concentrations (10µM and 20µM or 5µM and 10µM) for 72 h. The flow cytometry results revealed distinct cell line-specific apoptotic responses to the compounds (Fig. 4). Specifically, 14h exerted a more prominent apoptotic effect on A549 and H460 cells, elevating their apoptotic rates to 5.80% and 28.43% respectively (vs. 3.19% and 15.81% in control groups). Notably, PC-9 cells treated with 14h showed no significant changes in apoptotic rate compared to the control group. These findings suggest that 14h may exert its cytotoxic effects through different mechanisms across cell lines, with apoptosis playing a critical role in certain cell types.

Autophagy staining assessment

Given the potential crosstalk between autophagy and cancer cell survival/death, the ability of 14h to induce autophagy was evaluated using MDC staining, a specific probe for autophagic vesicles. All tested cell lines (A549, PC-9, H460) were treated with different concentrations of 14h, followed by MDC staining and fluorescence microscopy imaging (Fig. 5). Quantitative analysis of MDC-positive puncta (representing autophagosomes) showed that 14h induced autophagy in tested cell lines, with the number of autophagosomes increasing in a dose-dependent manner. These results indicate that autophagy induction is a common cellular response to 14h across different cancer cell lines, potentially contributing to their cytotoxic effects.

DNA damage staining detection

To explore whether DNA damage contributes to the cytotoxicity of 14h, a DNA damage-specific staining kit was used to visualize DNA damage foci in tested cell lines. A549, PC-9, and H460 cells were treated with 14h at different concentrations, followed by staining and imaging with a confocal microscope (Fig. 6). Results revealed that 14h induced significant DNA damage in tested cell lines, as evidenced by a marked increase in green fluorescent foci within the cell nuclei compared to the untreated control group. These findings suggested that DNA damage may be a core mechanism underlying the antiproliferative activity of the compounds.

RT-PCR analysis

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the biological effects of 14h, RT-PCR analysis was performed to quantify the expression of key genes involved in DNA damage response, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and autophagy. A549, PC-9 and H460 cells were treated with 14h at different concentrations (10µM and 20µM or 5µM and 10µM) for 72 h, and gene expression levels were normalized to the internal reference gene GAPDH. Results showed that 14h differentially modulated the expression of these functional genes in a cell line-specific manner (Fig. 7). Specifically, 14h treatment of H460 cells led to a significant upregulation of multiple genes: p21(cell cycle arrest), Bcl-2 (anti-apoptotic), caspase-6 (anti-apoptotic), Keap1 (oxidative stress regulator), and ATG7 (autophagy-related). These changes in gene expression align with the observed induction of apoptosis, autophagy, and oxidative stress responses, providing molecular evidence for the multifaceted mechanisms of these novel compounds.

Protein expression analysis

To validate the transcriptional changes observed in the RT-PCR analysis, western blot analysis was performed to detect the protein expression levels of key molecules involved in DNA damage, apoptosis, and autophagy. Cells were treated with 14h at different doses for 72 h, and protein expression levels were normalized to the internal reference β-actin. Results confirmed the RT-PCR findings and further revealed post-translational changes in functional proteins (Fig. 8). Specifically, 14h, a more pronounced effect on apoptotic and DNA damage-related proteins was observed: it significantly increased the expression of caspase-9 (a key initiator caspase in mitochondrial apoptosis), γH2AX, and PARP (a downstream effector of apoptosis) in A549, PC-9, and H460 cells. These results collectively confirm that 14h exert their biological effects by modulating the expression of critical proteins involved in DNA damage, apoptosis, and autophagy, providing a comprehensive molecular basis of their cytotoxic activity.

Molecular docking analysis and study of the PARP pathway reveals potential inhibition of the compound 14h to PARP protein

To further elucidate the potential mechanism by which compound 14h induces DNA damage, molecular docking analysis was conducted to explore its binding interaction with the PARP protein. The 2D and 3D docking results are presented in Fig. 9A and B, respectively. As shown, the 1,2,3-triazole ring of compound 14h acts as a hydrogen bond acceptor, forming a crucial hydrogen bond interaction with Arg878. Additionally, the thiazole ring of 14h establishes a pronounced π-π stacking interaction with Tyr907, while the phenyl ring attached to the amide group engages in another π-π interaction with His862. These key interactions are consistent with previously reported binding patterns of potent PARP inhibitors, further supporting that compound 14h may possess significant biological activity through PARP1 inhibition and contribute to the accumulation of DNA damage in cancer cells.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated the cytotoxic potential and underlying mechanisms of a series of newly synthesized febuxostat-1,2,3-triazole derivatives, with a particular focus on the lead compound 14h. Among the tested compounds, 14h exhibited the most potent antiproliferative activity across multiple human lung cancer cell lines, including A549, PC-9, H460, and H1299, while displaying minimal cytotoxicity in normal L02 liver cells. At a concentration of 20 µM, 14h reduced the relative cell viability to 42.71 ± 0.49% (A549), 43.14 ± 0.17% (PC-9), 17.54 ± 0.26% (H460), and 71.74 ± 3.39% (H1299), indicating both selective anticancer activity and variable sensitivity across cell lines. Based on these findings, IC₅₀ values were further determined for A549, PC-9, and H460 cells, revealing 13.87 ± 0.25 µM, 3.81 ± 0.50 µM, and 10.01 ± 0.03 µM, respectively, confirming 14h as the lead compound for mechanistic investigations.

Live/Dead cell staining provided visual confirmation of the cytotoxic effects of 14h, showing a dose-dependent increase in dead cells (PI-positive) particularly in PC-9 cells, corroborating the results of the CCK-8 assay. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that apoptosis contributed significantly to 14h-induced cell death in A549 and H460 cells, whereas PC-9 cells displayed limited apoptotic response. These results suggest that 14h may exert its cytotoxic effects via cell line-dependent mechanisms, with apoptosis being predominant in certain contexts.

Given the well-established interplay between autophagy and cancer cell survival, MDC staining was performed to assess autophagic responses. Quantitative analysis showed that 14h induced autophagy in all tested cell lines in a dose-dependent manner, indicating that autophagy may act as a common cellular response to 14h, potentially contributing to its cytotoxicity. Complementarily, DNA damage staining revealed marked increases in nuclear foci in all treated cells, suggesting that induction of DNA damage is a core mechanism underlying 14h-mediated growth inhibition.

At the molecular level, RT-PCR and western blot analyses provided further mechanistic insight. 14h modulated the expression of key genes and proteins involved in DNA damage response, apoptosis, autophagy, and oxidative stress. Notably, H460 cells treated with 14h showed upregulation of p21 (cell cycle arrest), Bcl-2 (anti-apoptotic), caspase-6, Keap1 (oxidative stress regulator), and ATG7 (autophagy-related), while western blot confirmed increased levels of γH2AX, caspase-9, and PARP in multiple cell lines. These findings collectively indicate that 14h exerts multifaceted anticancer effects by simultaneously inducing DNA damage, promoting apoptosis, and modulating autophagy.

The observed cell line-specific differences in response to 14h may be attributed to intrinsic variations in apoptotic sensitivity, DNA repair capacity, and autophagic regulation among different NSCLC cell lines. Moreover, the incorporation of the 1,2,3-triazole scaffold into the febuxostat framework likely enhances DNA intercalation, ROS generation, and metal coordination, collectively contributing to its potent antiproliferative effects. These results highlight the structure-activity relationship of this series, supporting the potential of febuxostat-1,2,3-triazole hybrids as selective lead compounds for further anticancer drug development.

In summary, compound 14h demonstrates promising anticancer activity against NSCLC, with selective cytotoxicity, DNA damage induction, apoptosis promotion, and autophagy modulation as key mechanistic features. These findings provide a solid experimental basis for further optimization and preclinical evaluation of febuxostat-based 1,2,3-triazole derivatives as novel therapeutic candidates for lung cancer treatment.

Conclusion

In this study, a series of novel febuxostat-1,2,3-triazole derivatives were designed and synthesized to explore their anticancer potential against non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells. Among them, compound 14h exhibited the most potent and selective cytotoxicity across multiple cancer cell lines (A549, PC-9, H460 and H1299), while showing minimal toxicity to normal liver cells (L02). Mechanistic investigations revealed that 14h exerts its anticancer effects through a multifaceted mode of action, including induction of DNA damage, promotion of apoptosis, and modulation of autophagy, with cell line-specific variations in response. Furthermore, molecular docking analysis demonstrated that compound 14h could effectively interact with the PARP active site, forming a key hydrogen bond between its triazole ring and Arg878, along with two prominent π–π stacking interactions involving the thiazole ring and Tyr907, and the amide-linked phenyl ring and His862. These binding interactions are consistent with reported PARP inhibitor motifs, suggesting that 14h may enhance DNA damage accumulation by inhibiting PARP activity.Collectively, the transcriptional, translational, and molecular docking results provide a comprehensive mechanistic basis for the cytotoxic activity of 14h. These findings highlight the potential of febuxostat-1,2,3-triazole hybrids as promising lead compounds for NSCLC therapy and lay a solid foundation for further optimization, mechanistic exploration, and preclinical development of novel anticancer agents.

General synthetic procedure for compound 5

In a reaction flask, 300 g of ethanol was added and stirring was started. Ethyl 2-(3-cyano-4-isobutoxyphenyl)−4-methylthiazole-5-carboxylate (10) (30 g) was added and the mixture was heated to 55 °C. Then, 25 g of 20% sodium hydroxide solution was added dropwise while maintaining the temperature. After addition, the reaction was stirred for 1 h. When the content of ethyl 2-(3-cyano-4-isobutoxyphenyl)−4-methylthiazole-5-carboxylate was less than 0.1%, the reaction was complete. The reaction mixture was cooled below 20 °C, and 2 mol/L hydrochloric acid was slowly added dropwise to adjust the pH to 2–3, keeping the temperature below 30 °C. A large amount of milky-white solid precipitated during acidification. The suspension was stirred at 10–20 °C for 1 h to complete crystallization, then centrifuged. The solid was washed once by soaking with drinking water (2 volumes), rinsed once with water, and finally washed with 90% ethanol (0.5 volume) until the filtrate reached near-neutral pH. The product was dried in a blast oven at 60–70 °C for more than 6 h to obtain febuxostat (5) as a solid.

General synthetic procedure for compounds 12a-12m and 14a-14m33

Febuxostat (5, 0.01 mol), 3-ethynylbenzoic acid (0.011 mol), HATU (0.015 mol), DIPEA (0.03 mol), and 200 mL DMF were added to a 500 mL reaction flask at room temperature and stirred under nitrogen for 24 h, monitored by TLC. After the reaction was complete, DMF was removed by vacuum evaporation. The reaction mixture was extracted with dichloromethane (150 mL × 3), and the combined organic extracts were washed with saturated sodium chloride (150 mL × 2). The resulting brownish-yellow viscous liquid was concentrated under vacuum. Methanol was added dropwise under ultrasonic agitation, causing the solid to precipitate. After standing, the solid was filtered, dried, and compound 11 was obtained.

In a reaction flask, compound 11 (1 mmol), substituted azide (1.1 mmol), tert-butanol (50 mL), water (50 mL), tetrahydrofuran (50 mL), anhydrous copper sulfate (0.2 mmol), and sodium ascorbate (0.4 mmol) were added sequentially. The mixture was stirred and refluxed for 6 h, monitored by TLC. After the reaction, the mixture was extracted with dichloromethane (30 mL × 3), and the organic solution was washed with saturated sodium chloride (30 mL × 2). The combined organic layer was further washed with brine (30 mL × 2), dried over sodium sulfate, and concentrated under vacuum to yield the crude product. The desired compound was obtained by recrystallization from ethyl acetate and was pure enough for further characterization and anti-tumor studies33.

Biological evaluation

Cell culture

The A549, H1299, PC-9, and H460 cell lines were purchased from the ATCC. Cells were retrieved from liquid nitrogen cryopreservation, carefully resuscitated, and subsequently cultured under standard conditions according to ATCC guidelines. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) or RPMI-1640 medium, each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, qualified grade) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with an atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

CCK-8 assay

Cells were passaged when they reached 80–90% confluence and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2,000–4,000 cells per 100 µL of complete medium. After overnight incubation, 20 µM of each test compound was added. Each experimental group was set up with three biological replicates. Following 72 h of incubation, the culture medium was removed, and 100 µL of fresh medium containing 10% Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was added to each well. After 1 h of incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance value at a wavelength of 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and the cell viability was calculated based on the absorbance data.

IC₅₀ determination

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2,000–4,000 cells per 100 µL of complete medium. After attachment, cells were treated with test compounds at different concentrations (0, 10, 20, 40, 80 µM), with three replicates set for each concentration. After 72 h of incubation, the cell viability was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay as described above. The inhibition rate was calculated using the formula: Inhibition (%) = 100% – Viability (%), where viability (%) represents the relative viability of compound-treated cells compared to untreated control cells. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values were calculated using GraphPad Prism software.

Live/dead cell staining

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at the density of 2,000–4,000 cells per 100 µL. After 24 h of treatment with test compounds at different concentrations, the culture medium was removed, and 100 µL of Live/Dead staining solution (prepared by mixing 1 mL of Solution A and 1 µL of Solution B according to the kit instructions, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was added to each well. The plates were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, fluorescent images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Green fluorescence (excitation wavelength: 488 nm) was used to identify live cells, while red fluorescence (excitation wavelength: 568 nm) indicated dead cells.

Apoptosis assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 3 × 10⁵ cells per well and treated with various concentrations of test compounds for 72 h. After the treatment period, both floating and adherent cells were collected. The collected cells were washed with PBS, and then resuspended in 200 µL of Annexin V-FITC binding buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). After 20 min of incubation at 4 °C in the dark, 10 µL of propidium iodide (PI) staining solution was added, followed by another 5 min of incubation. Samples were analyzed by flow cytometry within 1 h, and data were processed and analyzed using FlowJo software to determine the apoptotic rate of cells.

Autophagy staining

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with test compounds for 24 h. After the treatment, monodansylcadaverine (MDC) (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was diluted at a ratio of 1:1000 in assay buffer and added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Cells were washed three times with assay buffer to remove unbound MDC. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope with an ultraviolet excitation wavelength of 330–360 nm and an emission wavelength of 510–540 nm. The intensity of MDC fluorescence was used to evaluate the level of autophagy in cells.

DNA damage staining

Cells were treated with test compound at different concentrations for 24 h. After the treatment, the extent of DNA damage was assessed using a DNA damage staining kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Fluorescent images were acquired using a confocal microscope. Green fluorescence (excitation wavelength: 488 nm) indicated the presence of DNA damage, while blue fluorescence (excitation wavelength: 360 nm) corresponded to cell nuclei, allowing for the localization of DNA damage within cells.

RT-PCR

Cells were seeded into 12-well plates at 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and treated with test compounds for 72 h. Total RNA was extracted from the treated cells using a total RNA extraction kit (Takara, China) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Gene expression levels were detected by RT-PCR and the relative expression levels of genes were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCT) method. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene to normalize the expression levels of target genes.

Western blot

Cells were seeded into 12-well plates at 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and treated with test compounds for 72 h. After treatment, total protein was extracted from the cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer. Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The primary antibodies used were: caspase-3, caspase-9, γH2AX, PARP, cyclin D, cyclin E, LC3, and β-actin (all from CST). The intensity of the protein bands was quantified using Bio-Rad software, with β-actin used as the internal reference to normalize the expression levels of target proteins.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Experimental graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism software. For statistical comparisons between two groups, unpaired t-tests were used; for comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by a post-hoc test was applied. A probability value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.
