Malaria misdiagnosis among febrile patients in Senegal: implications for the underestimation of Plasmodium species diversity and malaria burden
Authors: Babacar Souleymane Sambe, Aissatou Diagne, Arona Sabène Diatta, Serigne Ousmane Mbacké Diaw, Ibrahima Sarr, Hélène Ataume Mawounge Diatta, Rokhaya Sané, Patindé Yann Bianca Guigma, Bruno Senghor, Babacar Diouf, Papa Mbacke Sembene, Ines Vigan-Womas, Makhtar Niang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a leading cause of febrile illness in Senegal, disproportionately affecting young populations. Test-and-treat remains a key strategy for malaria control but relies on accurate diagnosis and effective treatment. This study evaluated and compared the diagnostic accuracy of malaria among febrile patients in three regions in Senegal with high incidence of malaria.MethodsData from 948 febrile patients were included to compare the diagnosis of malaria between standard of care malaria diagnostics (RDT and Microscopy) and references quantitative and qualitative PCR methods, which discriminates the four major human malaria species.ResultsThe analysis showed that PCR detected malaria infection in 75.29% (716/948) of patients, of which 70.25% involved non-falciparum species, including P. ovale, P. vivax, and P. malariae. Microscopy and RDT missed 31.5% and 18.92% of PCR-positive infections, respectively. In addition, half of mono-infections with non-falciparum species were misdiagnosed as P. falciparum by both microscopy and RDT. Furthermore, microscopy failed to detect a high proportion of P. falciparum infections confirmed by PCR, including those with higher parasitemia than the microscopy theoretical limit of detection.ConclusionsThe study highlights notable limitations and concerns for malaria diagnostic in Senegal, especially for microscopy-detectable P. falciparum parasitemia and non-falciparum infections. As malaria epidemiology trends shift toward mixed-species infections, the solely reliance on microscopy and RDT for the diagnosis of malaria may be critical since missed infections can lead to untreated cases and sustained transmission and impede elimination efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05845-9.Keywords: Malaria, Diagnosis, Non-falciparum, Molecular surveillance, Senegal

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Malaria Report 2024 [1], an estimated 263 million malaria cases occurred globally in 2023, reflecting an incidence rate of 60.4 cases per 1,000 population at risk. The African Region bore a disproportionate burden, accounting for 94% of global cases and 95% of malaria-related deaths worldwide. Critically, children under five represented 76% of all malaria deaths in this region [1]. In Senegal, malaria remains endemic, with the highest incidence rates recorded in the south and south-eastern regions [2]. Despite a low population density (1,822,372 inhabitants, representing only 1–2% of Senegal’s total population) and low geographical occupation (11% of the national territory) [3]. The administrative regions of Tambacounda, Kédougou, and Kolda account for 81% of confirmed malaria cases, nationwide [2]. The burden among vulnerable groups is even heavier, with nearly 90% of cases occurring in children under five and pregnant women [2].

The National Malaria Control Program (NMCP) routine surveillance of malaria uses the standard of care malaria Rapid Diagnostic Test (RDT) and microscopy methods for the diagnosis of malaria and identifies P. falciparum as the major cause of malaria cases. However, non-falciparum species (P. ovale, P. vivax, and P. malariae) infections are recurrently reported, albeit at low levels [4–10]. Often neglected by the routine surveillance system and national policies, non-falciparum species contribute to severe malaria, including anaemia and renal complications, among others [11]. For instance, P. vivax and P. ovale can persist as hypnozoites, leading to relapses and febrile illnesses in the absence of new infectious mosquito bites, while P. malariae is associated with chronic infections [12]. The rise of non-falciparum species and the co-circulation of vector-borne diseases with similar/overlapping clinical symptoms increase the risk of misdiagnosis of non-P. falciparum species due to their lower parasiteamia, delay treatment and threaten malaria elimination efforts [13, 14].

Microscopy and RDT, though cost-effective and widely used, exhibit some shortcomings in terms of sensitivity and specificity. Both methods poorly detect non-falciparum species and low-level parasitemia, and their performance further declines as malaria transmission intensity decreases during the elimination phase of the disease [15]. Microscopy is highly operator-dependent: variations in blood smear preparation, staining quality, and microscopist expertise directly affect the accuracy of the diagnosis. Most malaria RDTs detect P. falciparum by targeting the histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2) antigen, but their accuracy is threatened by the persistence of the HRP2 antigen in the peripheral bloodstream for weeks following parasite clearance, along with the increasing reports of parasites with deleted hrp2/3 genes. already documented in several endemic countries across Africa, Asia, and the Middle East.

These diagnostic limitations systematically underestimate non-P. falciparum (non-falciparum) malaria cases, distorting surveillance data and undermining treatment accuracy. For instance, standard RDTs fail to detect co-infections or minor Plasmodium species, allowing reservoirs of P. ovale and P. vivax hypnozoites (which cause relapses) and chronic P. malariae infections to persist undetected [15–17]. Consequently, misdiagnosis triggers cascading public health failures: unnecessary use of antimalarials, delayed and/or inadequate management of co-occurring febrile illnesses (e.g., arboviruses, typhoid), and silent transmission chains that jeopardize elimination efforts [1]. This study assessed the misdiagnosis of malaria in febrile patients living in a high-transmission zones in Senegal.

Materials and methods

Study sites

The regions of Kedougou, Kolda, and Tambacounda are epidemiologically critical for malaria control in Senegal. Together, they encompass 37% of the country’s landmass but account for only 11.3% of its population [3]. Despite this modest demographic share, they bear a disproportionate burden of malaria, contributing approximately 80% of nationally confirmed clinical cases [2]. This high incidence is exacerbated by their strategic location along international borders with Mali, Guinea, and The Gambia. Frequent cross-border population movements in these areas create a persistent risk of Plasmodium parasite importation, complicating efforts to interrupt local transmission cycles.

Study design and sample collection

This study focused on febrile patients who visited healthcare facilities during the malaria transmission season in Kedougou (2020 and 2022), Kolda (2020), and Tambacounda (2021). Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 6 months, (2) axillary temperature > 37.5 °C, and (3) presence of one or more malaria-related symptoms (e.g., headache, nausea, dizziness, chills, or fatigue). Participants or their guardians provided consent or assent before inclusion. A total of 948 venous blood samples collected on EDTA tubes were obtained from patients included as part of a malaria molecular surveillance [18] and a PATH-Institut Pasteur malaria evaluation RDT [19] studies in south and south-eastern Senegal. Both study protocols were reviewed and approved by the “Comité National d’Ethique pour la Recherche en Santé” (references 00000134/MSAS/CNERS/Sec and 00000185/MSAS/CNERS/SP], Senegal. Demographic data (age, gender) and diagnostic results performed by clinicians (microscopy and RDTs) were recorded in a dedicated registry for further evaluation.

Malaria diagnosis

Microscopy and RDT diagnosis

In this study, microscopy and RDTs were solely conducted by laboratory technicians at the healthcare facilities. The Senegalese NMCP conducts regular quality control of malaria microscopic diagnosis through on-site supervision carried out by WHO-approved experts, complemented by refresher training. This quality assurance system allows the most qualified practitioners to attain WHO accreditation in malaria microscopy [2].

The involved staff strictly follow NMCP protocols, including the preparation of malaria microscopic slides (thin smear and thick film), and performing and interpreting RDTs. In line with this operational framework, our study employed: (i) standard Giemsa-stained microscopy, and (ii) SD Bioline™ Malaria Ag Pf (Abbott) RDT kits, following NMCP-approved procedures for routine diagnosis of malaria in Senegal [2].

Molecular detection and characterization of Plasmodium species

Commercial QIamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used to extract genomic DNA (gDNA) from ~ 200 µL aliquots of frozen blood pellets that were initially brought to room temperature.

The presence of Plasmodium parasites was first determined using a genus-specific real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting the Plasmodium cytochrome b gene, following the protocol described by Niang et al. [4–6]. DNA samples that tested positive by qPCR were then subjected to a species-specific qualitative nested PCR with primers targeting the Plasmodium spp. 18S small sub-unit ribosomal RNA (18S ssrRNA) gene as described previously by Snounou & Singh [20]. Nested PCR results were scored as a categorical variable (presence vs. absence of amplification). Genomic DNA from four human-infecting Plasmodium species (P. falciparum, P. malariae, P. ovale and P. vivax) was used as positive controls in all amplifications, while sterile water and DNA from uninfected blood served as negative controls to ensure the absence of contamination.

Quantification of Plasmodium falciparum parasitemia

A laboratory-adapted P. falciparum 3D7 strain culture was used to generate the standard curves to quantify P. falciparum parasitemia. A serial dilution series (D0 to D10) was prepared from 3D7-infected red blood cells. D0 represented the highest parasitemia (approximately 117,000 parasites/μL), while D10 corresponded the lowest concentration (~ 0.04 parasites/μL). Ten-fold dilutions were performed from D0 to D5, followed by two-fold dilutions from D6 to D10. Genomic DNA extracted from 200 µL of each dilution point was used to construct the standard curve, which had a coefficient of determination (R2) of 99%. This curve enables the estimation of parasite density in clinical samples based on qPCR Ct values.

Molecular detection and quantification of P. falciparum DNA were performed by qPCR targeting the cytochrome b gene as previously described by Canier et al. [21]. This protocol also allowed the establishment of standard curves for parasite quantification.

Data analysis

The structure of the study population was analysed according to gender and age of patients, and their distribution according to three arbitrarily defined age groups: < 5 years, 5–15 years, and > 15 years. The frequency of the different Plasmodium species was determined by calculating the proportions of each species and each type of infection (single or mixed). Statistical differences in P. falciparum parasitaemia between groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. For the current study, the detection limit of microscopy and RDTs was considered to range between 50 and 500 parasites/μL, as described by various authors [13, 17, 22, 23] and their sensitivity and specificity were calculated using real-time PCR as the reference method. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1 [24].

Results

Study population

Febrile patients included in this study were in the majority relatively young, with a mean age of 18.54 [0.5—99] years and a median age between 10 and 18 years with minor variations across study sites and periods (Tables 1 and 2). Male and female patients were evenly distributed across the regions, the unique exception being in Kolda (2020) where males predominated over females (60.2% vs 39.7%). Febrile participants were of all age groups and older patients (> 15 years) were more represented than younger ones (< 15 years) in Kedougou in 2020 (54.55% vs 45.45%) and 2021 (56.91% vs 43.09%).

Diagnostic tests sensitivity

Figure 1A shows that out of the 946 collected samples, 44.27% tested positive by microscopy, while positivity rates were 56.99% and 75.29% by RDT and qPCR, respectively. These correspond to additional 121 RDT-positive samples as compared to microscopy, 174 and 295 PCR-positive samples as compared to RDT and microscopy, respectively (Fig. 1A).

Positivity and concordance of malaria diagnosis tests. A Positivity (number of samples and percentage) by malaria diagnostic method; B Venn diagram showing the concordances between the malaria diagnostic methods

The three tests exhibited a concordance rate of 67.82% (Fig. 1B). The discordant results included samples positive by either qPCR (18.51%) or RDT (0.21%) alone, and samples with inter-method discrepancies between qPCR and RDT (12.83%), RDT and microscopy (0.32%), and qPCR and microscopy (0.32%).

Diagnostic tests specificity

All microscopy-positive samples were reported as singly P. falciparum-infected by technicians at the healthcare facilities, but molecular method detected four Plasmodium species (Fig. 2A), predominantly P. falciparum in 69.56% of samples, while P. ovale, P. vivax and P. malariae were respectively found in 51.59%, 3.7% and 2.22% of the tested samples. Mono and mixed Plasmodium species infections were retrieved in 36.17% and 63.83% of positive samples, respectively (Fig. 2B). Among the mono-infected samples, P. falciparum (29.75%) constituted the majority (Fig. 2B), while P. ovale, P. vivax, and P. malariae mono-infected infections were retrieved respectively in 5.17%, 0.84% and 0. 42% of samples (Supplemental Table 1). Similarly, the majority of mixed infections (62.57%) included P. falciparum, but mixed infections with non-falciparum species only were found in 7.68% of mixed-infected samples and their detailed proportions are presented in Supplemental Table/Figure/Material 1.

Plasmodium species composition among tested samples. A Prevalence of malaria parasite species; B Infection diversity

Among the qPCR-positive samples, 41.24% were negative by microscopy of which, 17.18% were unique P. falciparum, 19.69% were concurrent P. falciparum-non-falciparum species, and 4.75% were exclusively non-falciparum species (Fig. 3A). On the other hand, among the 25% qPCR-positive and RDT-negative samples (Fig. 3B), unique P. falciparum accounts for 10.2% against 11.03% of mixed P. falciparum/non-falciparum parasites, and 3.77% exclusive non-falciparum species. The concurrent 58.76% qPCR/microscopy-positive samples (Fig. 3A) and 75% qPCR/RDT-positive ones (Fig. 3B) comprised a significant proportion of mixed P. falciparum/non-falciparum and unique non-falciparum infections that were missed by both microscopy and RDT.

Plasmodium falciparum parasite density

The qPCR P. falciparum-positive samples had median parasitemia levels within or above a defined detection threshold of 50–500 parasites/µL (Fig. 4). The majority (~ 75%) of samples that were positive by both microscopy and RDT tests had parasite densities exceeding the threshold (Fig. 4). In contrast, 50–60% of microscopy and RDT false-positive results (qPCR positive and microscopy or RDT negative samples) corresponded to parasite densities at or below this range (Fig. 4). Surprisingly, a substantial proportion of samples with P. falciparum parasite densities well above 500 parasites/µL were not detected by RDT or microscopy. Finally, for the three microscopy-positive/RDT-negative samples, parasitemia was quantified in only one at a density below 50 parasites/µL.

Discussion

Senegal experiences seasonal malaria transmission although few regions, notably those situated in the South-East are characterized by more intense transmission, influenced by geography, human and environmental factors, and rainfall variability. The study population, characterised by a young age range (median 10–18 years) and a balanced gender ratio (~ 1), reflects Senegal’s demographic profile as reported by the National Agency for Statistics and Demography [3]. The younger age of our study population mirrors the country’s overall population structure, thus making the findings within this demographic population of relevance with regards to a broader epidemiological context in Senegal where malaria remains a leading cause of febrile illness among young people. Moreover, the balanced gender ratio minimises gender-related biases, supporting the broad applicability of our conclusions to public health strategies in Senegal.

The study identified a high proportion (75.29%) of patients positive for malaria parasites by qPCR among the 946 febrile patients analyzed in this study, including 70.25% of non-falciparum species. Although the study focused on malaria-related febrile illnesses, non-malarial causes of fever due to bacterial, viral, or other parasitic infections were not systematically investigated at inclusion. In these regions, malaria remains the leading cause of febrile illness and malaria testing (RDT and/or microscopy) is routinely performed in all febrile patients. Nevertheless, fever may also be caused by other pathogens, including arboviral or bacterial diseases, with malaria occurring as a concomitant or incidental infection, particularly in high-transmission settings [25].

The high rates of non-falciparum highlight critical limitations of the conventional microscopy and RDT diagnostics for the surveillance and control of malaria. Strikingly, microscopy missed 31.5% of qPCR-positive cases, while RDTs missed 18.92%. Notably, almost half of non-falciparum mono-infections or co-infections without P. falciparum were misdiagnosed as P. falciparum by both microscopy and RDT. The frequent failure of microscopy to detect low-density parasitemia (50–500 parasites/μL), combined with its reliance on technician expertise [13, 17], likely explains many of these discrepancies. Similarly, HRP2-based RDTs, which are optimized for P. falciparum, systematically fail to detect non-falciparum species [11, 14], thus contributing to the underestimation of the burden of P. ovale, P. vivax, and P. malariae, which typically circulate at low-level parasitemia despite being implicated in severe malaria cases in Senegal [10]. Although HRP2 gene deletions have not yet been reported in Senegal, there is a need for proactive monitoring to prevent false-negative RDT results, as observed in other regions [12, 17, 23].

The persistence of non-falciparum species, despite Senegal’s focus on P. falciparum, demands improved training on the microscopic diagnosis of non-falciparum parasites and the urgent integration of molecular tools into surveillance programs. While microscopy and RDT remain pragmatic in resource-limited settings, their insensitivity to co-infections and non-falciparum species compromises diagnosis accuracy and treatment efficacy. For instance, misdiagnosed P. vivax or P. ovale infections may lead to relapses in the context of no systematic administration of primaquine [15]. Emerging technologies, such as AI-enhanced microscopy, could improve species differentiation [26], but molecular methods (e.g., qPCR) remain the reference assay for surveillance in elimination settings.

While microscopy and RDTs detected 421 and 542 positive cases, respectively, qPCR identified 176 additional infections missed by both. Median parasitemia of these missed samples exceeded the theoretical detection threshold of microscopy, including the extended range of 50–500 parasites/μL defined within the current study. This suggests that the poor diagnostic accuracy may be due to operational challenges such as poor staining quality of microscopic slides or technician workload rather than intrinsic test limitations. This is further supported by the fact that two RDT positive samples tested negative for microscopy and qPCR, revealing either slide’s quality or reading issue and possible DNA degradation during qPCR. The observed discordance between qPCR and conventional methods is consistent with previous studies highlighting the labor-intensive nature of microscopy and its variability under field conditions [16, 27]. For example, Diallo et al. [27] reported that Senegalese microscopists often misidentified non-falciparum species due to limited training. In addition, the reported persistence of HRP2 antigens after parasite clearance may account for false positives [13], while environmental factors like heat and humidity during storage may impair test performance [28]. The latter is further supported by the fact that RDT performances were much improved on samples tested in health facilities in Kolda and Tambacounda regions, which are modern hospital-based laboratories as compared to rural health posts in Kedougou, where RDT stability is challenged by storage and usage conditions in humid and uncontrolled environments.

In high-transmission settings, clinicians often face a significant influx of patients during the peack malaria transmission season, leading to a rushed reading of microscopic slides and missed infections [14]. This situation is further compounded by the NMCP’s historical emphasis on P. falciparum-centered training [27]. Our findings support the need for continued training of technicians on malaria diagnosis and speciation, the development and validation of standardized protocols, and regular proficiency assessments, efforts already underway through WHO's External Competency Assessment on Malaria Microscopy (ECAMM), as implemented by Senegal’s NMCP [27]. We emphasize the need for continuous training of laboratory technicians in non-falciparum species detection by microscopy, but also for P. falciparum daily diagnosis given the observed low specificity of microscopy for the diagnosis of malaria, even caused by P. falciparum.

As Senegal’s ambitions to eliminate malaria by 2030 [2], a paradigm shift for a better detection of non-falciparum malaria parasites is pivotal. This is of relevance in the high transmission regions where the NMCP struggles to curve down malaria due to P. falciparum. Both microscopy and RDTs remain vital for frontline testing and prompt care of malaria, but their limitations in detecting non-falciparum species and low-density infections undermine control and elimination efforts. Improved diagnostics such as novel ultra-sensitive RDTs including Pan-Plasmodium-specific lactate parasite dehydrogenase (pan-pLDH) or aldolase, AI-enhanced microscopy, or field-based PCR assays are critical to sustain surveillance efforts, particularly in regions such as Kolda, Kedougou and, Tambacounda, where non-falciparum circulation is confirmed [4–6, 8–10].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that in high malaria transmission regions in Senegal, the conventional diagnostic methods significantly missed the primary P. falciparum parasite and underestimate non-falciparum infections and co-infections. This diagnostic gap compromises both individual case management and broader malaria strategies for the control and elimination of the disease. The use of qPCR has revealed a hidden diversity of parasites and underscores the need for improved and easy-to-use diagnostics. Accordingly, a holistic approach that combines improved diagnostics, genomic surveillance, and targeted interventions is essential to ensure sustainable malaria control in sub-Saharan Africa.
