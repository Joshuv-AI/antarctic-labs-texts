Changes in Medication Use After Medicare Part D Annual Out-of-Pocket Spending Caps
Authors: Christopher L. Cai, Kelly E. Anderson, Aaron S. Kesselheim, Benjamin N. Rome
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study reports on changes in prescription drug use after the Inflation Reduction Act implemented out-of-pocket spending caps.

Introduction

Medicare Part D provides prescription medication coverage for more than 57 million US individuals, including most adults older than 65 years.1 Since its enactment in 2006, Part D has required substantial cost-sharing by patients, including a deductible, 25% cost-sharing up to a catastrophic level of spending ($7400 in 2023), and unlimited 5% cost-sharing after this threshold.2 As the cost of prescription medications increased over the last 2 decades, this approach became increasingly untenable, with patient costs exceeding $10 000 for some expensive medications, such as oral chemotherapy medications and biologics.3,4 Higher cost-sharing is associated with medication nonadherence and worse clinical outcomes, including higher mortality.5,6,7 In a 2022 survey, 1 in 5 adults older than 65 years reported cost-related medication nonadherence.8

To help Medicare patients, US Congress implemented several changes to the Part D program in the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) of 2022.9 Starting in January 2024, the IRA eliminated cost-sharing after patients reached the catastrophic spending limit, effectively capping patients’ annual out-of-pocket costs to approximately $3300.10 In January 2025, the annual out-of-pocket spending cap was lowered to $2000. By one estimate, the $2000 spending cap was expected to lower costs for approximately one-fifth of Medicare beneficiaries.11 The IRA also includes several other changes to Part D, including shifting more financial liability from the government to private Part D plan sponsors for patients with high-cost medications, changing how the maximum out-of-pocket cost threshold was calculated, and expanding which patients were eligible for low-income subsidies; these subsidies substantially lower out-of-pocket costs for those who qualify.4

These changes in the Part D program—particularly the annual out-of-pocket spending cap—could improve medication access and affordability for the millions of US residents with Medicare, particularly for high-cost medications previously associated with out-of-pocket costs of thousands of dollars. The goal of this study was to measure changes in medication use by Medicare-insured patients after the implementation of the annual out-of-pocket spending caps compared with changes in use of the same medications by commercially insured patients.

Methods

Data Sources and Medication Cohort

In this cohort study, we identified medications covered by Medicare Part D using the public Medicare Part D spending dashboard data from 2023, which was the year prior to the out-of-pocket spending caps and the most recent data available (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1).12 We excluded vaccines and insulins, 2 classes of medications subject to separate out-of-pocket limits for Medicare beneficiaries during this time.13,14 We also excluded medications with no use in the reference quarter (2023 quarter [Q] 4), the most recent quarter prior to the start of the IRA Part D reforms (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Medications were defined by active ingredient and brand vs generic status since brand name medications typically cost more than generic medications (eMethods in Supplement 1). The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for observational research. This project was exempt from review and informed patient consent because it was deemed not to represent human participants research by the Massachusetts General Brigham Institutional Review Board.

To measure changes in medication use over time, we linked this cohort at the medication level to Symphony Health Metys, an all-payer retail pharmacy database that estimates nationally representative counts of prescriptions based on a large sample of retail, mail-order, and long-term care pharmacies (eMethods in Supplement 1). Generic and brand name formulations were treated as separate entities during this linkage given that they typically have different prices. We benchmarked Metys against the Medicare Part D dashboard to measure what percentage of prescriptions Metys captured in its dataset (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

We used these data to measure the estimated number of filled prescriptions reimbursed by Medicare or commercial insurers from the first quarter of 2022 through the fourth quarter of 2025, the most recent full quarter of data available at the time of analysis. We excluded a small number of medications that could not be linked to data from Symphony Health Metys (unlinked medications represented 0.4% of 2023 gross Medicare Part D spending).

To account for changes in the size of the Medicare Part D and commercially insured populations over time, we used quarterly national Medicare Part D and commercial insurance enrollment estimates from public Medicare Part D enrollee files and the US Bureau of Labor Statistics Current Population Survey (eMethods in Supplement 1). Medicare enrollment did not change substantially from pre-IRA historical trends over the study period.

Exposure and Covariates

The primary exposure was enactment of annual out-of-pocket spending caps in Medicare in January 2024. We chose 2024 as the start of our intervention period because this was the first year the IRA implemented a spending cap in Medicare Part D (eTable 2 in Supplement 1); although the spending cap was higher in 2024 (approximately $3300) than in 2025 ($2000), we presumed that the impact of adding a cap may be larger than the impact caused by lowering the cap in 2025. Additionally, low-income subsidy expansions under the IRA took effect in January 2024.

We hypothesized that this policy would disproportionately affect access to the most expensive medications, because patients using these medications would reach the spending cap early in the year and have no cost-sharing for remaining prescription fills. We thus stratified medications based on monthly cost into 4 categories: low (<$100), medium ($100-$829), high ($830-$6999), and very high (≥$7000). Monthly costs were determined based on mean annual spending per Medicare beneficiary using the 2023 Part D dashboard data (most recent year available) and reflect gross spending prior to any confidential rebates. The $830 threshold was chosen because medications above this threshold can be designated in specialty tiers subject to higher cost-sharing requirements by Part D plans that might be more sensitive to out-of-pocket costs.15 The $7000 threshold was selected because medications above this cost would likely lead patients to reach the $2000 out-of-pocket cap after filling a single month’s supply.15 In sensitivity analyses, we further stratified high-cost medications at $830 to $2999, $3000 to $4999, $5000 to $6999, and $7000 or more.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was the quarterly number of prescriptions per capita for each medication, calculated by dividing the number of filled prescriptions in Medicare or commercial insurance by the number of Medicare or commercial enrollees in the calendar year.

Statistical Analysis

We used a difference-in-differences analysis to compare changes in the use of medications in Medicare before and after the Medicare annual out-of-pocket caps were implemented in January 2024 compared with changes in the use of the same medications in the commercially insured population. Data were analyzed at the medication-quarter insurance-type level with longitudinal panel estimates at the medication-insurance–type level. We clustered errors at the medication-insurance–type level to account for repeated measures over time. We used ordinary least-squares models to estimate the quarterly number of prescriptions per capita, and the outcome was log-transformed so that model results could be interpreted as relative changes over time. We calculated the overall average treatment effect comparing quarters before (2022-2023) and after (2024-2025) the policy change. We also calculated quarter-specific effects using an event study framework to measure changes over time.16 Models included fixed effects for each medication (to account for time-invariant differences between drugs and to focus the analysis on within-drug changes in use) and calendar quarter (to account for within-year seasonal variation). To better reflect national trends, the primary results were weighted based on the number of Medicare prescriptions for each drug in the baseline study quarter (fourth quarter of 2023). This was done so that common medications contributed more to results than rarely prescribed medications. Unweighted results were also reported.

We evaluated whether models satisfied the parallel-trends assumption using models of the untransformed and log-transformed outcomes using an interaction term between quarter and treatment group (Medicare vs commercial) in the 8 preintervention quarters. Separate models were created for medications in each cost group.

To ensure that differences between Medicare and commercial plans were related to the implementation of the Medicare Part D out-of-pocket spending caps implemented in January 2024, we performed a falsification test in which we treated the first quarter of 2023 as the exposure. To illustrate how changes in medication use varied across therapeutic categories, we performed a subgroup analysis by therapeutic category among the high- and very-high-cost categories; these groups were combined to adequately power the subgroup analyses (eMethods in Supplement 1).

Analyses were considered significant based on 2-sided tests with statistical significance defined as P < .05. Analyses were performed in Stata version 18.1 (StataCorp).

Results

The analysis included 3053 medications, covering 92.9% of gross Medicare Part D spending in 2023. Overall, 1534 (50.2%) medications were low cost, 882 (28.9%) were medium cost, 455 (14.9%) were high cost, and 182 (6.0%) were very high cost. The median monthly costs were $15 for low-cost (IQR, $7-$39), $253 for medium-cost (IQR, $159-$395), $2392 for high-cost (IQR, $1358-$4616), and $11 196 for very-high-cost (IQR, $8609-$18 091) medications. The most common therapeutic category for low-cost medications was gastroenterological (286 of 1534 [18.6%]), while the most common category for very-high-cost medications was oncology (65 of 182 [35.7%]) (Table 1). In analyses benchmarking our dataset, Symphony Health Metys, to the Medicare Part D dashboard, the percentage of prescriptions captured by Metys decreased over time (eg, from 92% to 88.2% for low-cost medications), which could bias our results toward the null (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). The most prescribed medications for Medicare beneficiaries by cost category in the fourth quarter of 2023 were atorvastatin for low cost (15 061 045 prescriptions), apixaban for medium cost (4 865 374 prescriptions), bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir for high cost (136 822 prescriptions), and nintedanib for very high cost (117 979 prescriptions) (Table 2).

In the primary (pre-IRA vs post-IRA) difference-in-differences analyses, there was no significant change in Medicare vs commercial use of low-cost medications (difference-in-differences estimate, 3.9%; 95% CI, −1.2% to 9.4%; P = .14), medium-cost medications (20.4%; 95% CI, −7.4% to 56.4%; P = .17), or high-cost medications (14.1%; 95% CI, −1.3% to 32.0%; P = .08). There was a 22.7% (95% CI, 6.0% to 42.0%; P = .01) increase in the use of very-high-cost medications after the spending cap in Medicare compared with commercial insurance.

In event study analyses, differences between the Medicare and commercial groups increased over time (Figure 2). By the fourth quarter of 2025, mean changes in medication use were 4.1% higher (95% CI, −3.4% to 12.3%; P = .29) for low-cost medications, 20.7% higher (95% CI, −10.7% to 63.0%; P = .22) for medium-cost medications, 23.1% higher (95% CI, 2.7% to 47.4%; P = .02) for high-cost medications, and 35.9% higher (95% CI, −4.1% to 92.3%; P = .08) for very-high-cost medications. Confidence intervals were wide due to heterogeneous trends among individual medications.

Using alternate cost thresholds, medication use in Medicare vs commercial insurance increased by 8.9% (95% CI, −9.0% to 30.3%; P = .35) for medications costing $830 to $2999 monthly, 10.3% (95% CI, −9.6% to 34.5%; P = .33) for medications costing $3000 to $4999, and 54.1% (95% CI, −13.8% to 175.3%; P = .14) for medications costing $5000 to $6999 (eFigure 4, eTable 5 in Supplement 1).

In unweighted models, results were more homogeneous across medication pricing levels. In 2 period difference-in-differences estimates the use of low-cost medications in Medicare increased by 9.2% (95% CI, 6.6% to 11.8%; P < .001), medium-cost medications by 11.8% (95% CI, 8.2% to 15.5%; P < .001), high-cost medications by 7.9% (95% CI, 3.5% to 12.4%; P < .001), and very-high-cost medications by 13.6% (95% CI, 6.3% to 21.5%; P < .001) (eFigure 5, eTable 6 in Supplement 1). In sensitivity analyses excluding 2025, the use of very-high-cost medications increased by 13.0% (95% CI, 0.3% to 27.4%; P = .045); other groups did not change significantly (eTable 7 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this cohort study using difference-in-differences analysis, the use of medications in Medicare increased relative to commercial insurance after annual out-of-pocket costs were enacted for Medicare Part D in 2024. The differences increased over time after the policy took effect, and the differences were generally larger among higher-cost medications. For example, the use of very-high-cost medications increased by 35.9% by the last quarter of 2025 compared with use of the same medications by commercially insured individuals. These findings suggest that the Part D annual out-of-pocket spending caps enacted under the IRA in 2024 were associated with substantially increased patient access to high-cost medications, with major implications for clinical treatment decisions and government health care spending.

Our results provide important additional evidence of the salutary impact of out-of-pocket spending caps on patients’ access to medications.11,17,18 Most prior studies have focused on caps for specific medications or classes of medications, such as insulin and vaccines, finding smaller effects on use.13,14 State-level caps on out-of-pocket costs for insulin19,20 and specialty medications21 were not associated with increased use, perhaps because many of the commercially insured patients affected by these caps already had lower out-of-pocket costs. By contrast, the Part D annual spending caps in the IRA applied to all medications and represented a large reduction in cost for many patients who previously could pay $10 000 or more for some expensive medications. As a result, the magnitude of increased use was higher than seen in most prior studies. Our results are generally aligned with a prior study that found a substantial increase in the use of oral oncology medications in Medicare after the Part D spending caps in 2024; our study included a much broader range of drugs and more time after the policy took effect.22 Another study23 found that patient-reported cost-related medication nonadherence decreased among those with Medicare in 2024 compared with those with commercial insurance, which aligns with the increases in use observed in the current study.

These results suggest that the caps may have achieved the intended policy goal of improving access and adherence to medications. For example, before the cap, many patients abandoned new prescriptions for many high-cost categories of medications,4 and such rates of abandonment have decreased after the caps. Although some of the higher use after the spending caps is likely related to patients accessing needed medications, there could also be some substitution of lower-cost medications with higher-cost alternatives, now that patients may pay similar out-of-pocket costs and thus have limited incentives to select the lower-cost options. Similarly, the caps might incentivize some shifting from injection and infusion medications reimbursed by Medicare Part B to retail medications reimbursed under Medicare Part D. For example, patients with cancer may pay less out of pocket for oral cancer treatments covered under Part D rather than infused medications covered under Medicare Part B, which continues to have unconstrained 20% cost-sharing.

Whether the changes we observed will translate to better patient outcomes is likely to be disease-specific, because increased medication use could reflect both high-value and low-value care. Further study of whether the increasing use of high-cost medications by Medicare-insured patients translates to improved clinical outcomes for Medicare Part D–insured patients will be needed.

In the short term, the increased use of high-cost medications will raise the government’s spending on the Medicare Part D program.15 The Congressional Budget Office estimated that the Medicare Part D redesign would increase Medicare spending by $60 billion from 2022 to 2031.24 However, spending increases exceeded expectations from 2024 to 2025; the substantial growth in the use of high-cost medications observed in our study may partially explain this rapid increase in spending.25 Much of this added cost will be borne by taxpayers who fund Medicare; some of the cost may be passed on to Medicare beneficiaries in the form of higher premiums, although premium increases have been restricted by the IRA and premium stabilization demonstrations through 2026 that have allocated billions of additional federal dollars to the program to prevent premium increases. But if these programs are not continued and if premiums do eventually increase, this could result in some Medicare-insured patients leaving the voluntary Part D program altogether. The higher costs might also lead to changes in how private plan sponsors offer coverage; in 2024-2025, plans became less generous in subsidizing out-of-pocket costs (before patients reached the annual cap) and some plans left the Part D marketplace altogether.15,26

Under the IRA, the additional costs of funding a more generous Part D benefit were expected to be offset by lower costs from other policies, such as Medicare price negotiation, which was expected to save $100 billion from 2022 to 2031.27,28 However, in 2025, US Congress eliminated an estimated $9 billion of these savings by excluding many rare disease medications from qualifying for negotiation.29,30 Additionally, the IRA introduced a new Medicare Discount Program that requires manufacturers to reimburse Medicare for 20% of medication costs after patients reach the annual out-of-pocket spending cap. For very-high-cost medications, this will translate into discounts of approximately 20% that will offset higher spending related to their increased use.31

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. We used aggregated medication-level data, so we were unable to make direct inferences about patient-level adherence or clinical outcomes and could not account for different effects among patients taking multiple medications. Furthermore, small-volume, high-cost prescriptions may contribute to large CIs. We were unable to exclude Medicare-insured patients unaffected by the caps, including those with low-income subsidies, which meant that the results may reflect an underestimate of the increase in use among Medicare-insured patients without these subsidies. There was substantial variation in the trends in use of individual medications; thus, we may not have been adequately powered to detect differences between Medicare and commercial insurance in all price groups or violations of the parallel-trends assumption.

We estimated medication costs using 2023 Medicare spending data, but prices can change over time, which could lead to some misclassification of medications in the cost groups. Our measure of monthly cost was imprecise because it was calculated based on annual Medicare spending per beneficiary, which could lead to misclassification for drugs typically not used for a full year. Similarly, our per-capita estimates for Medicare enrollees may be imprecise as not all Medicare beneficiaries are continuously enrolled during the calendar year; this could bias results toward the null. Our dataset may undercount Medicare prescriptions, biasing our results to the null. We measured changes in use based on the quarterly prescriptions filled, which could change if patients fill a different amount of the medication, such as switching from 30-day to 90-day supplies.32 However, because we compared usage patterns for the same medications in Medicare and commercial insurance, this would only affect the results if trends differed between these insurance types. We used commercial insurance as a comparison for Medicare, and there may be different trends in the use of medications in these 2 different populations, including if medications are used for different indications; however, the results met parallel-trends assumptions, suggesting that these differences did not bias the results. We could not disentangle the effects of the out-of-pocket caps from other policies implemented by the IRA, including expanded eligibility for low-income subsidies in 2024 and introduction of a program in 2025 that allowed patients to pay out-of-pocket costs across the calendar year. However, these programs affected a very small number of beneficiaries compared with the annual out-of-pocket caps.

Our results only reflect trends in the first 2 years after the Medicare spending caps; although we found that the use of medications increased over time, these trends may change in subsequent quarters and it will be important to update these analyses as newer data become available. Use of medications in Medicare in subsequent years may be affected by other policies, including lower prices for some medications negotiated by Medicare that began to take effect in 2026.33

Conclusions

In this study, the implementation of annual spending caps in Medicare Part D under the IRA was associated with increased use of prescription medications, particularly for very-high-cost medications. These results suggest that the policy may have substantially improved patients’ access to high-cost medications, and the magnitude of these changes has important implications for future policies to address Medicare Part D spending by patients and the government.
