Delayed herniation of cauda equina root through occult dural tear following unilateral biportal endoscopic decompression: illustrative case
Authors: Keitaro Matsukawa, Kanehiro Fujiyoshi, Yoshiomi Kobayashi, Takahiro Kitagawa, Yoshiyuki Yato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Incidental dural tears are a known complication of spinal surgery, with a reported incidence ranging from 2.0% to 20.0% in lumbar procedures depending on the procedure and patient factors.1,2 Typically, these are identified intraoperatively by visualization of arachnoid or nerve roots and clear CSF leakage. However, small or occult tears can remain undetected, posing risks for serious complications,3 such as cauda equina herniation.4,5

Unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE) has emerged as a minimally invasive technique for treating various spinal pathologies. This approach offers excellent visualization and reduced tissue disruption; however, it requires continuous saline irrigation to maintain a clear endoscopic field. The fluid environment can theoretically hinder the detection of subtle dural breaches. Irrigation may also tamponade or dilute small amounts of CSF, preventing the surgeon from noticing a dural tear if one occurs.6–10 To our knowledge, cauda equina or nerve root herniation through an occult dural tear in the UBE procedure has not been widely documented.11 We present a rare case of this complication without visible CSF leakage during surgery. The case underscores the importance of vigilance and specific intraoperative strategies to avoid undetectable dural injuries in endoscopic spine procedures.

Illustrative Case

A 60-year-old man underwent UBE decompression at L3–4 and L4–5 for lumbar canal stenosis (Fig. 1). The procedure was uneventful, with no evidence of dural injury and rapid symptom resolution. However, 3 weeks postoperatively, acute severe radicular pain developed in the left lower extremity during straining with defecation. The pain was aggravated by standing and relieved in the supine position. Neurological re-examination showed no muscle weakness, but the straight leg raising test was positive at 30° on the left side. MRI confirmed adequate decompression but showed mild enlargement of the left-sided disc herniation at L4–5 (Fig. 2). Left L5 radiculopathy secondary to disc herniation was suspected. A second UBE discectomy was performed. While symptoms improved slightly, they subsequently worsened and became severe and persistent despite conservative management. Repeat MRI demonstrated sufficient neural decompression without any sign of CSF leakage. Subtle abnormalities included asymmetry of the dural canal, with an indistinct delineation of the dural contour and cauda equina on the left side at L3–4 (Fig. 3). Importantly, there was no visible hematoma or compressive disc fragment to explain the deficit. Based on these findings, cauda equina herniation through an occult dural tear was suspected.

Preoperative sagittal (A) and axial (B and C) MR images showing lumbar canal stenosis at L3–4 and stenosis with central disc herniation at L4–5 (C).

Postoperative axial MR images after UBE laminectomy, showing adequate decompression at L3–4 (A) and mild enlargement of the left-sided disc herniation at L4–5 (B).

MR images obtained after the second UBE discectomy demonstrating sufficient neural decompression without evidence of CSF leakage at both L3–4 (A) and L4–5 (B). The left dural contour appears partially blurred, and the cauda equina is asymmetrically delineated on the left side at L3–4.

Exploratory surgery via a midline approach was performed. Following additional laminectomy, herniated cauda equina encased in intact arachnoid membrane was found extruding through a 5-mm dural tear, without visible CSF leakage. An additional 3-mm dural incision facilitated tension-free reduction, and the defect was repaired with sutures and reinforced with a polyglycolic acid patch and fibrin glue (Fig. 4). Postoperatively, the patient experienced rapid pain relief and full recovery without neurological deficits.

Intraoperative findings during the third exploratory surgery. The herniated cauda equina, encased in intact arachnoid membrane, is visible without CSF leakage (A). After an additional 3-mm dural incision (B), the herniated cauda equina was reduced back into the intradural space (C), and the dural defect was closed with sutures (D).

Discussion

Observations

We describe an unusual complication of endoscopic spine surgery: herniation of cauda equina nerve roots through an unrecognized dural tear. Neuroradiological diagnosis is often challenging, because symptoms such as intense radicular pain can mimic disc herniation or epidural hematoma. Most reported cases involved inadequately repaired or unrecognized intraoperative tears, typically with CSF leakage.4,5,7,10–12 In contrast, cases without clear radiological findings of CSF leakage, as in this case, are exceedingly rare in the literature. Therefore, the intense radicular symptoms served as the primary clinical clue raising suspicion of cauda equina herniation, and a definitive diagnosis was achieved only through surgical exploration.

This case highlights two important aspects. First, the dural tear remained entirely occult during the perioperative periods. Postoperative CT imaging confirmed that the bone edge at the decompression site was smooth, and no prominent bony spicule was observed adjacent to the site of the dural defect, making this mechanism unlikely. We hypothesize the following mechanism underlying the clinical course: 1) an unrecognized intraoperative microtear with intact arachnoid membrane; and 2) repeated postoperative straining or coughing that elevated intrathecal pressure and gradually enlarged the defect, allowing neural herniation. Second, technical characteristics of UBE can predispose to unrecognized dural injuries. The one-handed technique limits tissue retraction and may increase the risk of minor injury, especially when dissecting fibrous bands adjacent to the dura. Moreover, intraoperative bleeding or floating debris may obscure the endoscopic view, while continuous irrigation may compress the dura, mask subtle defects, or tamponade minor leaks.6–11

Our experience suggests several important precautions for surgeons performing UBE or similar endoscopic spine procedures. Gentle and meticulous dissection is paramount when working adjacent to the dura mater to minimize the risk of creating dural tears. Using fine instruments and avoiding aggressive maneuvers near the thecal sac can help prevent incidental durotomies. Equally important is a thorough inspection for dural integrity at the end of the decompression. We recommend temporarily halting irrigation and performing a Valsalva maneuver (or otherwise increasing intrathecal pressure) to provoke any hidden CSF leakage.13,14 In an open surgery setting, this step is a common practice when a dural tear is suspected or after a dural repair, to confirm a watertight closure. Incorporating it into endoscopic cases, despite the presence of continuous fluid, could aid in identifying occult dural defects that would otherwise be masked by irrigation.

Lessons

Cauda equina herniation through an occult intraoperative dural tear is a rare but serious complication of spinal surgery. This case is, to our knowledge, one of the first reported in the setting of UBE and is distinguished by the absence of visible CSF leakage during the initial procedure. This case emphasizes the necessity of considering dural pathology in patients with persistent postoperative radicular pain, even in the absence of intraoperative findings or conclusive imaging. Although such complications may arise following both conventional and endoscopic procedures, spine surgeons should be aware that UBE’s technical environment carries the potential risk of undetected dural injuries. Early suspicion and timely surgical exploration are essential to prevent irreversible neurological sequelae and ensure favorable outcomes.
