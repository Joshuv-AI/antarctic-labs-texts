A closed-loop cell therapy engineered to autonomously secrete activin A inhibitor protects from fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva
Authors: Pratik Koirala, Ziyu Chen, Mengfan Wu, David Maridas, Ashley Siegel, Chang Liu, Samerender Hanumantharao, Yuji Mishina, Vicki Rosen, Shailesh Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEngineered cell therapies present an opportunity for endogenous, site-specific production of therapeutic agents. Fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP) is a morbid condition in which patients develop extensive heterotopic bony lesions in response to aberrant sensitivity to Activin A, through a mutation in the type I bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) receptor ACVR1 (ACVR1 R206H). We designed a transposon plasmid containing the transgene encoding ActR2A-Fc, with expression controlled by the BMP-responsive element (BRE), which is pathologically activated upon exposure to Activin A in FOP cells. FOP-derived marrow cells modified with this plasmid exhibited closed-loop functionality, with increased ActR2A-Fc expression upon exposure to Activin A and reduced expression upon its withdrawal. Bone marrow transplantation of labeled engineered FOP marrow cells into FOP mice resulted in the trafficking of engineered cells to sites at risk of FOP and a reduction of heterotopic bony lesions. These data provide proof-of-concept and a blueprint for marrow-derived cell therapies across the disease spectrum.

Introduction

Drug delivery systems that enable local, spatially restricted delivery of therapeutic agents to the affected disease site offer the possibility of administering larger doses than would be tolerated systemically. Cell therapies offer an opportunity for spatiotemporally restricted drug delivery by taking advantage of three unique characteristics. First, cell therapies synthesize the pharmacologic agent, thereby obviating exogenous drug delivery. Second, the cells utilized are able to traffic to the involved sites of tissue injury through the local presence of recruitment cytokines such as stromal-derived factor 1 (SDF1)1,2; as a result, their function can be spatially restricted. Finally, cell therapies are activated only when engaged with the offending disease process. This feature can enable cell therapies to activate in a pseudotemporal fashion,3 in which time is defined by the presence of the disease.

Chimeric antigen receptor T cell therapy (CAR T cell therapy) presents one of the most well-recognized strategies for spatiotemporally restricted treatment.4 Outside of treatment for malignancy, cell therapy adoption specifically for drug delivery has been unrealized. There are myriad conditions, many of them driven by local inflammation, which would benefit from cell therapies able to traffic to sites of tissue injury, activate locally, and secrete a recombinant therapy. Here we report on the development of a cell therapy which is suited to treat fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP),5 a rare condition in which patients develop painful heterotopic bony lesions at sites of tissue injury. Patients with FOP harbor a germ-line mutation in the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) receptor ACVR1; this mutation (ACVR1 R206H) renders fibro/adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) pathologically susceptible to osteogenic differentiation6,7 upon exposure to Activin A8, a member of the TGFβ superfamily. Activin A is a major component of the inflammatory response. The concentration of circulating activin A has been shown to increase during inflammation.8

Recent therapeutic strategies for FOP have demonstrated notable progress, though the two leading approaches. Treatment with an anti-Activin A antibody, garetosmab, has shown high efficacy and favorable tolerability in clinical trials, leading to strong patient adherence in late-stage studies.9 In contrast, the approved oral therapy palovarotene (Sohonos),10 which acts on downstream signaling, has seen limited clinical adoption due to concerns over its modest efficacy and significant side effect profile. This highlights the ongoing need for FOP therapies that combine efficacy with long-term safety and patient tolerability.

Using transposon technology,11 we have engineered a closed-loop cell therapy that is indefinitely capable of secreting an Activin A inhibitor upon exposure to Activin A, using an Activin A-inducible promoter. This cell therapy, designed to be an autologous marrow replacement, traffics to sites of inflammation and expresses the Activin A inhibitor, ActR2A-Fc, using the BMP responsive element (BRE) promoter,12 which is uniquely activated by Activin A in cells harboring the causative ACVR1 R206H mutation. Despite high levels of marrow reconstitution with our cell therapy, we show the absence of systemically detectable drug levels. Marrow cells are recruited to sites of injury, and there is a near-complete absence of heterotopic bone in FOP mice that receive the engineered cell therapy. While systemic drug delivery has demonstrated therapeutic promise for FOP, the requirement for chronic administration raises potential concerns for off-target effects and long-term toxicity. Our findings suggest that spatiotemporal drug delivery via genetically modified cell therapies provides a compelling alternative strategy. This approach, which restricts therapeutic protein expression to sites of injury and avoids systemic detection, may offer an improved safety profile for managing localized disease flares.

Results

Design of closed-loop plasmid targeting activin A

We designed a transgenic plasmid that would activate gene expression in cells upon the activation of BMP signaling using the BMP-BRE promoter. The plasmid includes 2 BRE repeats. To specifically target Activin A as the pathologic ligand responsible for BMP signaling in FOP cells carrying the ACVR1 R206H mutation, our plasmid included the transgene encoding the Activin-binding receptor, ActR2A-Fc (Figure 1A). This protein consists of the signal peptide and functional domain of the human ActR2A protein from Met1-Pro135, followed by the IEGRMD sequence and Fc tag from human IgG1. The closed-loop regulatory system for ActR2A-Fc expression responds to fluctuating Activin A levels. During inflammation, elevated Activin A concentration activates the BMP-BRE promoter, driving the expression of ActR2A-Fc. The secreted ActR2A-Fc sequesters extracellular Activin A, lowering its bioavailability and attenuating further ActR2A-Fc expression, thus creating a feedback loop. Transfected bone marrow cells expressing ActR2A-Fc in response to Activin A serve as a therapeutic intervention for FOP caused by the ACVR1 R206H mutation. By trapping Activin A, the ActR2A-Fc produced by these therapeutic cells inhibits downstream Smad1/5 pathway activation, effectively suppressing pathological osteogenesis and reducing heterotopic bone formation (Figure 1B).Figure 1Closed-loop strategy targeting Activin A(A) Schematic representation of the BRE-ActR2A-Fc construct.(B) Proposed model for transfected bone marrow cells-based therapy in fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP) mice.

Next, we sought to validate the closed-loop functionality of our plasmid. To do this, we directed our efforts to FOP mouse bone marrow cells as these were the desired target cells for our plasmid in vivo. We designed an in vitro experiment to simulate the exposure, withdrawal, and re-exposure to the Activin A ligand (Figure 2F). We found that upon exposure to Activin A, transfected bone marrow cells exhibited the desired upregulation of ActR2A-Fc expression. This was attenuated upon withdrawal of Activin A, but then re-activated upon re-exposure to Activin A (Figure 2G). The reactivation was not as robust as the first activation; however, the second activation was after 3 days in the media, compared to 7 days for the first activation, which might have led to lower expression. The expression of ActR2A-Fc in FOP bone marrow-derived cells was Activin A dose-dependent. No activity was seen in the absence of Activin A. The expression significantly increased with 50 ng/mL Activin A (Figure 2H).

Validation of bioactive ACVR2A-Fc production

Upon the validation of our genetic construct, we verified the bioactivity of the secreted ActR2A-Fc recombinant peptide using bone marrow cells from the FOP mice. Bone marrow cells from FOP mice were divided into two groups: transfected and untransfected. Both groups were treated with Activin A, and conditioned media were collected after 7 days (Figure 3A schematic). We observed an increase in osteogenic gene expression, including Runx2, Bglap, Sox9, and Col10a1, when FOP mouse-derived bone marrow cells were treated with Activin A and grown in the presence of conditioned media from untransfected bone marrow cells (Figures 3B–3E). In contrast, osteogenic gene expression was notably reduced when FOP mouse-derived bone marrow cells treated with Activin A were grown in the presence of conditioned media from transfected bone marrow cells (Figures 3B–3E). To test whether ActR2A-Fc may reduce the effect of other ligands known to bind ActR2A, we performed experiments with BMP9, a ligand that binds ActR2A. A similar trend was seen when WT bone marrow cells were treated with BMP9 and grown in the presence of ActR2A-Fc containing conditioned media (Figures 3F–3I). Transplantation of transfected bone marrow into FOP mice

Next, we proceeded with in vivo validation of our construct’s therapeutic efficacy. We followed a one-step marrow transfection procedure; marrow was isolated from tamoxifen-induced mT/mG FOP mice and transfected using electroporation (200 V/5 msec/1 pulse) (Figure 4A). We verified that immediately after and 1 week after electroporation, the ratio of CD45+ (hematopoietic) and PDGFRα+ (mesenchymal cell) populations remained similar in the untransfected and transfected groups (Figure 4B). Following electroporation, FOP marrow cells were immediately injected via retro-orbital injection into syngeneic, same-sex, tamoxifen-induced, busulfan-treated FOP recipient mice to simulate autologous transplantation; control experiments were ones in which FOP marrow cells were electroporated without plasmid delivery (Figure 4A, schematic). After three weeks, marrow reconstitution rates were 92.47% and 95.57% for the control and experimental arm, with an SD of 3.04% and 1.64%, respectively (Figure 4D) as calculated using the illustrated gating strategy. Following 3 weeks of reconstitution, mice received intra-muscular cardiotoxin injection (3 μg × 4 times) into both hindlimbs to stimulate heterotopic bone formation. A second cohort of mice was euthanized 4 weeks after cardiotoxin injection, followed by serum ELISA for ActR2A-Fc, showing no difference in the systemic detection of Fc levels between mice that had received transfected or untransfected FOP bone marrow (Figure 4E, threshold of detection 0.01 ng/mL). Hindlimbs were examined via X-ray (Figure 4F) and microCT (Figure 4G) 4 weeks after local cardiotoxin injury to induce heterotopic bone formation. We found robust heterotopic bone formation in 8 of 10 hindlimbs in FOP mice, which received control bone marrow, while we found no bone formation in 11 of 12 hindlimbs with experimental bone marrow (Figure 4H). To demonstrate that solely correcting the defect in marrow cells would not be sufficient to prevent FOP lesions, we transplanted wild-type bone marrow into the same-strain FOP recipient mice; mice made FOP bone lesions identifiable on X-ray (Figure 4J). This finding suggests that the therapeutic efficacy observed with our engineered cells is not entirely due to an autocrine inhibition of osteogenic differentiation but includes a paracrine effect of tissue-resident cells. This is consistent with previous findings showing that bone marrow transplantation alone is not sufficient to cure FOP, as FAPs are responsible for providing cells for HO.7

Next, we sought to verify trafficking of the transplanted marrow to the site of injury, and to verify that the presence of ACVR2A-Fc remained undetectable systemically. Following bone marrow transplantation with GFP+ transfected or untransfected FOP marrow, FOP mice received intramuscular cardiotoxin injection and were euthanized after 1 week; histologic evaluation of hindlimbs demonstrated the presence of GFP+ cells, verifying trafficking to the site. However, we noted that GFP+ cells were not present in areas of the hindlimb distant from cardiotoxin injury where there were no inflammatory infiltrates (Figure 5A) 27.2% of the total cells at the injury site were positive for GFP fluorescence. These cells exhibited immunostaining for PDGFRα but not for CD45, indicative of mesenchymal cell fate (Figure 5B). The majority of trafficked bone marrow cells also exhibited immunostaining for an HSC panel containing CD11b, TER119, B220, Ly-6G, and CD3 Ab (Figure 5C). Tissues from mice treated with transfected and untransfected bone marrow cells were stained for human Fc. Fc immunostain was observed in the muscle tissue at the site of CTX injection but not inside the bone marrow compartment, indicating that the secretion of ActR2A-Fc is spatially restricted. Mice that received an untransfected bone marrow transplant did not display any signs of Fc stain (Figure 5D). After 5 days, average marrow reconstitution rates were 83.97% and 82.4% for the control and experimental arm, with an SD of 6.38% and 4.41%, respectively. (Figures 5E and 5F) The serum of these mice was collected, and an ELISA was performed to measure the concentration of serum ActR2A-Fc. We saw no significant difference in the concentration of serum ActR2A-Fc in mice with transfected and untransfected bone marrow transplant (Figure 5G). Long-term efficacy of engineered bone marrow cells

Next, we sought to assess the efficacy of transplanted engineered bone marrow after 8 weeks of CTX injury. Mice were transplanted with engineered bone marrow, and the right hindlimb was injected with CTX. Mice were euthanized after 8 weeks, and the hindlimbs were scanned using X-rays. Mice that received an untransfected bone marrow transplant displayed identifiable FOP bone lesions on X-rays (Figure 6A). We noted that the GFP+ transplanted cells were present in the muscle even after 8 weeks of cardiotoxin injection. Most of the GFP+ cells exhibited CD45 staining, indicative of hematopoietic cell fate (Figure 6B). Tissue from the site of CTX injection was stained for Fc on the right hindlimb of the mice. Fc immunostain was observed in the right hindlimb at the site of CTX, whereas the Fc stain in the uninjured left hindlimb was minimal, suggesting the local nature of Fc production (Figure 6C). H&E staining of the CTX injection site showed ectopic bone formation in FOP mice with an untransfected bone marrow transplant. Transfected mice, however, showed intact muscle fibers with no signs of ectopic bone. Discussion

Here we report on a proof-of-principle closed-loop, autologous cell therapy which is capable of trafficking to sites of injury and activating locally to express an inhibitor of a pathologic ligand. Within the context of FOP, our cell therapy offers a new avenue for drug delivery and has the potential to improve site-specific tissue targeting and toxicity profile of the therapeutic agent. Importantly, this technique is amenable only to agents that can be produced by cells in a controlled fashion, such as recombinant peptides. However, our lab has initiated work on the cell therapy-based secretion of exosome-packaged therapeutic oligonucleotides as well.13 In the future, we envision strategies for targeted delivery of chemical agents, which may be synthesized within cells, such as steroid hormones or chemotherapeutic agents.

Our work complements other recent advances in cell-based delivery of therapeutic protein against FOP. Prior studies have demonstrated the efficacy of systemic, AAV-mediated delivery to achieve constitutive expression of an ActR2B-Fc in FOP models.14 Similarly, another approach has utilized engineered mesenchymal cells modified to continuously secrete ActR2B-Fc along with rapamycin, an inhibitor of HO in FOP.15 While these strategies are promising, they rely on the continuous and systemic production of a biologic agent. Our closed-loop approach is predicated on the ability to detect the presence of a ligand associated with the disease and produce the therapeutic protein in response locally and without a detectable systemic presence. We surmise that by being responsive to local levels of the offending ligand, the cell therapy is finely tuned to the microenvironment, preventing large amounts of gene expression which would result in systemic “spillage.” Indeed, we did not identify systemically elevated levels of ActR2A-Fc, and we did not identify any obvious systemic effects associated with intravenous infusion of the drug, such as hemorrhage or telangiectasias. However, further studies are required to rigorously determine whether these may develop over longer periods of time. We are encouraged by the absence of systemically quantifiable ActR2A-Fc levels, however.

The therapeutic system we have developed is distinct in that it is designed as a closed-loop circuit. In our model, the expression of ActR2A-Fc is not constitutive; rather, it is induced specifically by the local, pathogenic BMP signaling that characterizes an inflammatory flare leading to HO. Previously, BRE was shown to be uniquely activated by Activin A in cells harboring the ActR1 R206H mutation, but not in wild-type cells.12 Our validating experiments for the BRE promoter selection indicated that upon a decrease in Activin A levels, there is a concomitant decrease in expressivity. This lends itself to closed-loop expression, which is important given the genomic permanence of the transposon. As our results demonstrate, the engineered marrow cells traffic to the site of injury, ensuring that the therapeutic is produced precisely where and when it is needed. This spatiotemporal control is a key differentiator from constitutive delivery systems and may offer a significant advantage by minimizing systemic exposure, thereby potentially enhancing the long-term safety profile of the treatment.

Our selection of ActR2A-Fc as the therapeutic peptide of interest is based on our knowledge of its Activin A binding capacity. Other candidates, including ActR2B-Fc, may be considered in the future as well. While structurally different from these peptides, anti-Activin A antibodies (e.g., Garetosmab) may be considered in the future. In this way, our cell therapy may behave similarly to a pre-programmed B-cell therapy that is capable of residing in the marrow. In this manuscript, we did not compare ActR2A-Fc with Garetosmab with respect to efficacy or adverse effects; we believe that peptide selection is important, but in this proof-of-concept study, it is secondary to the development and deployment of a guiding therapeutic framework.

Our cell therapy also provides a practical benefit over exogenous peptide delivery and oral therapies such as orally administered ALK2 kinase inhibitors.16 By enabling the endogenous production of the recombinant peptide, unique peptide manufacturing, storage/shipping, and injection requirements are obviated. Instead of ex vivo bioreactor-based manufacturing with complex purification requirements, the burden of manufacturing is instead directed toward standard DNA synthesis. Systemic infusion of the agent is no longer required due to the ability of these cells to traffic to the desired site. Technical constraints for local injection are also bypassed as cells directly migrate to the affected site, and we can take advantage of the power of a cell therapy system capable of detecting and responding to the earliest indicators of disease, even before typical signs/symptoms are observable by the patient or provider. Autologous BMT is a significant undertaking. Ultimately, the viability of a BMT-based approach for FOP will depend on a clear demonstration of profound, lifelong efficacy and a safety profile that outweighs both the acute risks of the transplant procedure and the convenience of chronically administered alternatives.

Limitations of the study

Our study has some important limitations that may guide future improvements in the technology. First, we have shown undetectable differences in ActR2A-Fc serum levels in control and experimental mice at delayed timepoints after injury and shorter time points after injury. The latter suggests that even early after injury, the systemic levels of ActR2A-Fc are low. This may be due to the retention of the secreted therapy within the affected tissues, thereby bypassing systemic exposure, which is necessitated by other forms of drug delivery. However, long-term effects of our system require further evaluation, especially when considering the potential effects on the bone marrow itself or accumulation within off-target tissues over a longer duration.

In addition, our reported approach uses the BRE promoter to drive drug production. However, BRE is activated by exposure to BMP ligands, and therefore, there is a risk for the cell therapy to be activated by normal BMP signaling. The contribution of Activin A versus BMP ligand signaling to BRE activation in the setting of the ACVR1 R206H mutation is unclear and requires further investigation. However, the requirement for inflammation limits the off-target effects of this therapeutic approach; additionally, while ActR2A-Fc may bind ligands other than Activin A (Activin B, GDF8, GDF11), it is not expected to substantially impair BMP signaling.

The translational potential for this closed-loop cell therapy approach requires further study. Given the growth in use of CAR T cell therapy, marrow-based approaches are likely to gather more attention. The inclusion of an autonomous system for expression control would make permanent genomic integration more palatable; however, it also requires that the autonomous control remain intact and high-fidelity. This requires long-term studies to verify that expression does not proceed unchecked after longer durations of marrow residence.

With these limitations in mind, our strategy offers numerous opportunities for iterative improvements and modifications. For FOP, one may consider modifying promoter selection from BRE to another promoter that may have improved expressivity or specificity for the site of interest. Importantly, the selection of a promoter is a driver for not only the specificity but also the intensity of drug expression. This presents an immense opportunity to leverage the trove of transcriptional data that exists for myriad diseases to then identify optimal disease-specific promoters. The nature of disease initiation may be redefined to the activation of a specific promoter, which is sufficient to indicate the appropriate temporal window during which to treat the patient. One may also alter the desired product from ActR2A-Fc to another peptide of interest, opening the door to myriad different peptide therapeutics being considered without the costly infrastructure required for peptide synthesis and purification. Most importantly, this strategy is amenable to use across different diseases for which systemic and long-term drug delivery are not acceptable due to adverse effects. As we continue to develop this technology, we will address these permutations and examine them for therapeutic viability using in vivo models.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

The experiments and handling of mice were performed in accordance with the protocol approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Brigham and Women’s Hospital (BWH, Protocol number 2020N000036).

ACVR1[R206H]fl/wt mice12 were mated with Ubc.CreERT2;Rosa26mTmG mice17 to generate.

Ubc.CreERT2;Rosa26mTmG;ACVR1[R206H]fl/wt and Ubc.CreERT2; ACVR1[R206H]fl/wt mice.

Ubc.CreERT2;Rosa26mTmG;ACVR1[R206H]fl/wt mice severed as donors for bone marrow transplant whereas Ubc.CreERT2; ACVR1[R206H]fl/wt mice were recipients. These were maintained in heterozygosity on a C57BL/6 background. Both male and female mice were used between 6 and 12 weeks of age. All mice were age and sex matched between groups.

Cell lines

HEK293 cells were obtained from ATCC and were not authenticated by the authors.

Method details

Tamoxifen treatment

Tamoxifen was intraperitoneally injected into UBC-Cre-ERT2;ACVR1[R206H] mice at the age of 6 weeks twice at a interval of 3 days. Tamoxifen (T0200, Chemdex) was dissolved in corn oil (SC-214761, Chemcruz) at a concentration of 20mg/ml, 100 μl was injected.

Activin A reporter vector construction

The BMP Recognition Element (BRE) was used upstream of the minimal CMV promoter and ligated with the ActR2A-Fc encoding gene. The product was inserted into a PiggyBac18 vector containing Blasticidin-resistance gene for selection (VectorBuilder). E. coli DH5α cells carrying the plasmid was cultured in LB medium containing 100μg/ml ampicillin, and the plasmid was isolated using ZymoPURE II plasmid maxiprep kit (D4202, Zymo Research).

Cell culture

HEK293 cells were cultured in DMEM (10-013-CV, Corning) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15070-063, Gibco). Bone marrow cells were isolated from mice. Specifically, tamoxifen treated donor FOP mice were euthanized using cervical dislocation and both hindlimbs were obtained with femur, tibia, and fibula intact. Muscle and tissue residues around the femur were removed, and the proximal end of the femurs were cut using sharp scissors. A 10 cc syringe with a 21-gague needle was filled with ice-cold PBS and inserted into the distal end of the femur. PBS was injected into the femur to flush the bone marrow out from the proximal end onto a 70 μm nylon cell strainer. The cells were collected in a 50 ml Falcon tube and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 minutes at 4 °C. The cells were resuspended in ice-cold PBS and electroporated immediately. After electroporation, cells were cultured in a growth medium containing DMEM, 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Transfection

HEK293 cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher). Transfections were performed in 6-well plates (1x106 cells/well). For each well, 4 μg vector was mixed with 1 μg pBASE plasmid carrying PiggyBac transposase. The transfection mix was prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol and added into the cell culture media. Cells were washed with fresh culture media after 48 hours and 100ng/ml Activin A (592004, Biolegend) was added to half of the wells. Cells were harvested after 1 week and the ability to produce ActR2A-Fc mRNA and protein was examined by RT-PCR and ELISA.

Electroporation

Bone marrow cells were counted using a hemacytometer. Appropriate dilution was performed in PBS to achieve a concentration of 1x104 cells/μL. 500 μL cell suspension was added to a 2 cm gap electroporation cuvette along with 15 μg BRE-ActR2A-Fc vector and 5 μg pBASE transposase plasmid and chilled on ice for 15 minutes. A single 5 ms pulse of 200 volts was used to electroporate (GenePulser Xcell, BioRad) the cells and the cuvette was placed on ice for another 15 minutes. 1.5 ml DMEM/FBS/Pen/Strep was added to the cells and plated in a 6-well plate. Cells were washed with fresh culture media after 48 hours and 100 ng/ml Activin A was added to half of the wells. Cells were harvested after 1 week and the ability to produce ActR2A-Fc mRNA and protein was examined by RT-PCR and ELISA.

RT-qPCR

Total mRNA was isolated from the transfected cells using Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kits (Zymo Research). Reverse transcription was performed using qMax cDNA Synthesis Kit (Accuris) with 100ng of starting RNA. Real-Time PCR was performed using the following primers for ActR2A-Fc, Runx2, Bglap2, Sox9, Col10A1, and Actin respectively. The PCR condition was: 95 °C for 10 minutes; 40 cycles 95°C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 minute, 72 °C for 30 s; 72 °C for 10 min The PCR was performed using the machine QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR Systemand analyzed using QuantStudio™ Real-Time PCR Software. Β-Actin was used as the house keeping gene and gene expression was measured using the ΔΔCt method.

ELISA

Blood was collected from mice using cardiac puncture, followed by incubation for 30 minutes at room temperature. The clot was removed by centrifuging the tube at 1000g for 10 minutes and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was stored at -80oC until further use. Human Fc ELISA kit (EK000095-HUFC-2, Syd labs) was used to measure Fc concentration in the serum. Similarly, culture media was collected after 7 days of cell culture. Human Fc ELISA kit was used to measure Fc concentration in the culture medium.

In vivo mouse model of FOP

The model was initiated by replacing wild-type ACVR1 sequence with its R206H counterpart into the sense strand. This was achieved by treating the UBC-Cre-ERT2;FOP mice with tamoxifen (100mg/kg, intraperitoneally) in corn oil for 4 days, which activated CreERT2. The same injection also replaced tdTomato-encoding exon with EGFP-encoding exon into the sense strand in UBC-Cre-ERT2;FOP;mTmG mice. To ablate bone marrow cells from the recipient mice, 25mg/ml stock of busulfan in DMSO was diluted in 0.2% saline and injected into the mice (20mg/kg, intraperitoneally) for 4 days. For bone marrow transplant, donor bone marrow cells were isolated from tamoxifen-treated UBC-Cre-ERT2;FOP;mTmG mice. Bone marrow was flushed with PBS, filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, and electroporated with or without the BRE-ActR2A-Fc vector. A total of 1 × 106 cells were then injected retro-orbitally into recipient UBC-Cre-ERT2;FOP mice. Mice were anesthetized using isofluorane before the injection. Three weeks after the bone marrow transplant, cardiotoxin (0.1 mg/ml in PBS) was injected intramuscularly into the hindlimb of the mice to induce muscle injury and promote heterotopic ossification.

Micro-CT and anatomical measurements

Left and right hind limbs were collected using high-resolution Micro-Computed Tomography (μCT40, SCANCO Medical AG, Brüttisellen, Switzerland). Scan parameters were 30 μm3 isotropic voxel size, 55 kVp peak X-ray tube intensity, 145 μA X-ray tube current, and 300 ms integration time. Resultant DICOM images were exported for measurement of heterotopic bone volume.

Flow cytometry

Muscle and tissue residue around the femur were removed, and the proximal end of the femurs was cut using sharp scissors. A 10 cc syringe with a 21-gauge needle was filled with ice-cold PBS and inserted into the distal end of the femur. PBS was injected into the femur to flush the bone marrow out from the proximal end onto a 70 μm nylon cell strainer. The cells were collected in a 50 ml Falcon tube and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 minutes at 4°C. The cells were then resuspended in ice-cold PBS and counted using a hemacytometer. Appropriate dilution was performed to achieve a concentration of 1x104 cells/μL. The cells were resuspended in blocking buffer (5% goat serum) for 1 hour. The cells were washed twice in PBS and the pellet was suspended in APC-PDGFRa (135908, Biolegend) or APC-CD45 (103112, Biolegend) for 1 hour. The cells were washed in PBS to remove the unconjugated antibodies and analyzed using Beckmac Coulter with appropriate filters. The data was analyzed using FlowJo. Live single cells were gated based on FSC/SSC followed by FSC-A/FSC-H for singlet gating. Singlets were gated with marker-specific gates.

Immunofluorescence staining

Tissue was fixed in paraformaldehyde for 48 hours and washed with PBS. The fixed tissue was frozen in OCT using liquid nitrogen and stored in -80oC before cutting. The blocks were cut using a cryostat with the tissue thickness of 10μm and mounted on a positively charged slides. Tissue sections were blocked with 10% goat serum, 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 0.3% Triton (MilliporeSigma) in PBS for 1 hour and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. The sections were probed with antibodies for APC-PDGFRα (1:100). The HSC panel included CD11b, TER119, B220, CD3, Ly-6G (101224, 116232, 103227, 100214, 127612, Biolegend) conjugated with pacific blue. Sections were washed for 3 x 5 min in PBS. ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (P36971, Invitrogen) was used to stain the nuclei and mount the samples. Fluorescent images were taken by Olympus FluoView confocal microscope at original magnification, 20×. Quantification of immunofluorescence staining was performed by using ImageJ software.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± S.D. Statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Comparisons between two groups were performed by using 2-tailed Student’s t-tests. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed by using a one-way ANOVA with a Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. Significant difference was established when P values was smaller than 0.05.
