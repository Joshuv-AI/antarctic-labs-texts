A decline in renal function is associated with a reduction in myocardial mechano-energetic efficiency in individuals at high cardiometabolic risk
Authors: Chiara M A Cefalo, Teresa Vanessa Fiorentino, Mariangela Rubino, Velia Cassano, Gaia Chiara Mannino, Alessia Riccio, Elena Succurro, Maria Perticone, Angela Sciacqua, Francesco Andreozzi, Giorgio Sesti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure and chronic kidney disease are closely interrelated conditions with rising global prevalence and incidence. Depressed myocardial mechano-energetic efficiency (MEE), reflecting the left ventricle’s capacity to convert chemical energy from oxidative metabolism into mechanical work, is recognized as an early marker of systolic dysfunction. This cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the relationship between estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and myocardial MEE in individuals at high cardiometabolic risk.MethodsMyocardial MEE per gram of left ventricular mass (MEEi) (mL/sec*g−1) was assessed via echocardiography in 3,572 adults participating in the CATAnzaro MEtabolic RIsk factors study. Participants were stratified into four categories based on eGFR (G1-G4), in accordance with the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes classification.ResultsIndividuals with mildly to severely reduced eGFR (G2, G3 and G4 categories) exhibited a significant reduction in myocardial MEEi compared to the G1 group. In multivariate linear regression analyses, declining eGFR remained significantly associated with reduced MEEi, even after adjusting for a broad range of cardiometabolic confounders, including age, sex, BMI, lipid profile, glucose tolerance status, hsCRP, HOMA-IR index, and the use of glucose-lowering, antihypertensive, and lipid-lowering medications.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that a consistent reduction in renal function across eGFR categories is independently associated with reduced myocardial MEEi in individuals with high cardiometabolic risk suggesting that even a mild decline in nearly normal renal function may contribute to altered myocardial energetics.Keywords: Myocardial energetics, Heart failure, Cardiometabolic risk factors, Chronic kidney disease

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are two interrelated clinical conditions whose prevalence and incidence are increasing worldwide, thus representing a significant challenge for public healthcare systems [1]. Data from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study has shown that during a mean follow-up of 13.2 years, the incidence of HF in patients with CKD defined based on persistently reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 was three-fold higher compared to the reference group with eGFR > 90 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (18 versus 6 per 1000 person-years) [2]. Furthermore, a decrease in eGFR is associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and hospitalization in patients with HF with preserved (HFpEF) or reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) [3, 4]. A large meta-analysis, including 25 prospective studies, revealed that approximately 55% of patients with either HFrEF or HFpEF had CKD, with a stepwise increase in the risk of all-cause mortality as CKD severity worsened, particularly among patients with HFrEF [5]. The bidirectional relationship between cardiac and renal dysfunction is driven not only by shared predisposing risk factors such as hypertension, impaired glucose metabolism, and dyslipidemia but also by a complex pathophysiological network originating mainly from expanded dysfunctional visceral fat and involving hemodynamic, neurohormonal, and inflammatory pathways that influence both organs.

Depressed myocardial mechano-energetic efficiency (MEE), a measure of the left ventricle's (LV) ability to transform the chemical energy produced by oxidative metabolism into mechanical work [6], is an early defect in left ventricular systolic dysfunction, which predicts incident HF not related to acute myocardial infarction [7]. In extensive epidemiological studies, myocardial MEE can be estimated using a non-invasive, ultrasound-derived method as the ratio of external work performed by the left ventricle during a single contraction (stroke work, SW), determined by multiplying systolic blood pressure by stroke volume and the myocardial oxygen consumption (MVO2) required to perform that work, estimated by the product of systolic blood pressure and heart rate (the so-called pressure-rate product) [8–10].

Recent evidence has highlighted the role of MEE as a potential marker of subclinical cardiac dysfunction in a variety of clinical conditions. In obesity, primary aldosteronism, metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes, reduced MEE has been associated with impaired myocardial energy utilization and early alterations in left ventricular function, even in the absence of overt heart disease [11–21]. Similarly, in various forms of cardiomyopathy, particularly hypertrophic and dilated types, MEEi reflects the energetic burden imposed by structural remodeling and altered loading conditions [21, 22]. These findings suggest that MEEi may serve not only as a functional parameter but also as a pathophysiological link among diverse conditions characterized by metabolic and myocardial stress.

Although impaired myocardial MEE has been associated with several cardiometabolic conditions affecting both heart failure (HF) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), it remains unclear whether impaired kidney function directly contributes to the impairment of myocardial MEE.

In light of the growing recognition of the bidirectional relationship between cardiac and renal dysfunction, we aimed to determine the relationship between eGFR and myocardial MEE in a group of adult individuals participating in the CATAnzaro MEtabolic RIsk factors (CATAMERI) study [18, 19].

Materials and methods

Study subjects

The CATAMERI study is an observational study that enrolled White adult individuals with one or more cardiometabolic risk factors, including dysglycemia, hypertension, dyslipidemia, or overweight/obesity, as previously described in detail [20]. Participants were consecutively enrolled from the referral hospital at the University "Magna Graecia" of Catanzaro, which serves a catchment area representative of the regional at-risk population, including both urban and rural referrals. Exclusion criteria included a history of malignant, autoimmune, or infectious diseases; end-stage renal disease; advanced chronic liver disease; chronic respiratory diseases; cardiovascular disease or valvopathies; gastrointestinal diseases associated with bleeding or malabsorption; chronic pancreatitis; history of drug abuse; self-reported alcohol consumption > 20 g/day in women and > 30 g/day in men; positivity for antibodies to hepatitis C virus or hepatitis B surface antigen; and use of drugs known to affect glucose metabolism, including corticosteroids and estroprogestins.

All participants attending the clinical center between 2006 and 2012 were visited in the morning after an overnight fast. Information on medical history, smoking status, drug use, and alcohol consumption was collected. During the visit, anthropometric measurements, including body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and clinic blood pressure, were recorded, and a venous blood sample was drawn for biochemical analyses. For participants without a known history of type 2 diabetes, a second visit was scheduled within the following four days, during which they underwent a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Plasma glucose levels were measured at baseline, as well as at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after ingestion. According to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria [23], participants were classified as having normal glucose tolerance (NGT) when fasting plasma glucose was < 100 mg/dL (5.5 mmol/L) and 2-h post-load glucose was < 140 mg/dL (7.77 mmol/L); prediabetes when fasting plasma glucose was 100–125 mg/dL (5.5–6.9 mmol/L), or 2-h post-load glucose was 140–199 mg/dL; and type 2 diabetes when fasting plasma glucose was ≥ 126 mg/dL (> 7 mmol/L), 2-h post-load glucose was ≥ 200 mg/dL (> 11.1 mmol/L), or if participants were being treated with antidiabetic drugs.

Participants were classified according to eGFR into four categories: G1 normal or high if eGFR was > 90 [mL/min/1.73 m2], G2 mildly decreased if eGFR was 60–89 [mL/min/1.73 m2], G3 moderately to severely decreased if eGFR was 30–59 [mL/min/1.73 m2], and G4 severely decreased if eGFR was 15–29 [mL/min/1.73 m2] [24]. No subjects were classified as G5 kidney failure with eGFR < 15 [mL/min/1.73 m2] as per protocol of exclusion criteria recruitment.

Written informed consent was obtained from each participant using the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. The study protocol was approved by the Hospital Ethics Review Board (Comitato Etico Azienda Ospedaliera “Mater Domini”) on October 12, 2012, with approval number 2012.63.

Echocardiographic assessments

All echocardiographic examinations were performed by a single trained operator, who was blinded to the clinical and laboratory data of the participants, using a VIVID-7 Pro ultrasound system (GE Technologies, Milwaukee, WI) equipped with a 2.5-MHz annular phased array transducer [14–20]. Two-dimensional imaging was used to record the tracings, and M-mode measurements were taken at or near the tip of the mitral valve. MEE was calculated as the ratio between stroke work (SW), which was determined using the equation: systolic blood pressure (SBP) (mmHg) × stroke volume (SV) (ml), and myocardial oxygen consumption (MVO₂), estimated using the equation: SBP (mmHg) × heart rate (HR) (bpm), known as the double product [7–10]. Due to the strong relationship between MEE and left ventricular mass, MEE was normalized to left ventricular mass to yield an energy expenditure index per myocardial mass unit (MEEi, expressed in mL/s/g) [7, 8].

Laboratory determinations

Plasma glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, and HDL cholesterol concentrations were measured using enzymatic methods (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Serum creatinine concentrations were assayed by an automated technique based on a creatinine Jaffé compensated method for serum (Roche Diagnostics), implemented in an auto-analyzer. Plasma insulin levels were measured using a chemiluminescence-based assay (Immulite, Siemens, Italy), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) levels were determined using an automated instrument (CardioPhase® hsCRP, Milan, Italy).

Calculations

The homeostasis model assessment index of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as fasting insulin (μU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/22.5 [25].

The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation was used to measure eGFR as follows: 141 × min(Scr/k, 1)α × max(Scr/k, 1) -1.209 × 0.993Age × 1.018 [if female] × 1.159 [if black], where Scr is serum creatinine, k is 0.7 for females and 0.9 for males, α is—0.329 for females and—0.411 for males, min indicates the minimum of Scr/k or 1, and max indicates the maximum of Scr/k or 1 [24].

Statistical analyses

Variables with skewed distributions, including triglycerides, hs-CRP, fasting insulin, and the HOMA-IR index, were log-transformed using the natural logarithm for statistical analyses. Continuous variables are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test. Differences in anthropometric and metabolic characteristics among eGFR categories were assessed using a general linear model adjusted for age and sex, with post hoc pairwise comparisons conducted via Fisher's least significant difference method. A multivariate linear regression analysis was then performed to assess the independent contribution of eGFR to MEEi, excluding variables that are part of the MEEi calculation (e.g., systolic blood pressure) to avoid collinearity issues. A variance inflation factor (VIF) value < 2 was considered acceptable to prevent multicollinearity among variables. For all tests, a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 27 for Windows; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

The mean age of the participants was 53 ± 14 years, with women comprising 50.7% of the total study population.

As detailed in Table 1, a comparison of the anthropometric and metabolic characteristics of study participants, stratified according to eGFR categories, revealed that individuals with decreased eGFR (G2, G3 and G4 categories) were older than those with normal eGFR (G1 category) and exhibited differences in sex distribution. After adjusting for age and sex, individuals in the G2, G3 and G4 categories had higher levels of triglycerides, glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR, and hsCRP, along with lower HDL cholesterol concentrations compared to those in the G1 category. A higher prevalence of type 2 diabetes was observed, along with increased use of glucose-lowering medications in the G2, G3 and G4 categories relative to the G1 category. Additionally, compared to the G1 category, ACE inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, and lipid-lowering medications were more prevalent in the G2, G3, and G4 categories, whereas beta-blockers were more prevalent in the G3 and G4 categories. Systolic blood pressure was higher in individuals in the G2 and G3 categories than in the G1 category, while diastolic blood pressure showed the opposite pattern.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we observed that individuals with mildly to severely reduced eGFR (G2, G3 and G4 categories) exhibited a significant reduction in myocardial MEEi. Notably, myocardial MEEi was significantly reduced even in individuals within the G2 category, compared to those with normal renal function (G1). This finding suggests that early, mild declines in eGFR may already be linked to impaired myocardial efficiency, supporting the notion of a progressive and continuous interplay between renal dysfunction and subclinical cardiac impairment. These results underscore the importance of early detection and monitoring of both renal and cardiac function, even in individuals without overt disease. Moreover, in multivariate linear regression analyses, declining eGFR remained significantly associated with reduced myocardial MEEi, even after adjusting for a broad range of cardiometabolic confounders, including age, sex, BMI, lipid profile, glucose tolerance status, hs-CRP, HOMA-IR, and the use of glucose-lowering, antihypertensive, and lipid-lowering medications.

Several pathophysiological mechanisms may account for the observed association between declining renal function and impaired myocardial MEEi. Previous studies have demonstrated that impaired kidney function is linked to endothelial dysfunction [26] and elevated levels of asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA), an endogenous inhibitor of nitric oxide synthase [27]. Both endothelial dysfunction and increased ADMA concentrations have been implicated in reduced myocardial MEEi [28, 29]. This suggests that impaired endothelium-dependent vasodilation may represent a shared pathophysiological pathway contributing to renal and myocardial dysfunction.

Insulin resistance may also play a mediating role in this relationship. It has been associated with both prevalent and incident CKD [30, 31] and reduced myocardial MEEi [13, 16]. Consistent with this, we observed that individuals in the G2, G3 and G4 eGFR categories had higher HOMA-IR and fasting insulin levels, paralleling a progressive decline in myocardial MEEi. Insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia may affect renal function through mechanisms including glomerular hyperfiltration, hypertrophy, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) activation, sodium retention, fibrosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction—fostering a vicious cycle of renal deterioration [32].

Additionally, our findings indicate a role for low-grade inflammation, as evidenced by elevated hsCRP levels in participants with decreased eGFR. Chronic inflammation is a well-established contributor to CKD and myocardial MEEi [17, 30], initiating a cascade of deleterious processes involving endothelial dysfunction, insulin resistance, metabolic dysregulation, fibrosis, oxidative stress, and damage to podocytes and cardiomyocytes. In this context, visceral obesity may represent an additional and clinically relevant factor linking reduced renal function to impaired myocardial MEEi. Visceral adipose tissue, which was found to be elevated in our study population, as indicated by higher waist circumference values, is a major source of proinflammatory adipokines that contribute to a chronic low-grade inflammatory state. These adipokines are known to promote endothelial dysfunction and insulin resistance, both of which, as previously mentioned, are established contributors to the pathogenesis of chronic kidney disease and impaired myocardial efficiency [33]. Although our study supports these associations, further studies are needed to elucidate the causal mechanisms involved.

The present study has several strengths deserving consideration. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the relationship between the declining renal function across the eGFR categories and decreased myocardial MEEi in a large, well-characterized population at high cardiometabolic risk. These findings carry significant clinical implications, as reduced myocardial MEEi reflects an early impairment in left ventricular systolic function and has been identified as a predictor of future HF [7]. Notably, myocardial MEEi can be noninvasively assessed via echocardiography, providing a practical tool for cardiovascular risk stratification even in individuals with mildly reduced renal function.

It was conducted in a relatively large group of adults attending a public hospital in Italy, where clinical evaluations were performed as part of routine care. The universal, tax-funded nature of the Italian healthcare system helps minimize selection bias, which is more common in studies conducted in settings with user-funded healthcare systems. Other strengths include the inclusion of both sexes, centralized and standardized biochemical analyses using fresh samples, and echocardiographic assessments performed by a single, blinded examiner—ensuring consistency and minimizing measurement variability.

However, some limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes inference of causality between reduced eGFR and myocardial MEEi. Longitudinal studies are necessary to confirm or refute these findings. Additionally, metabolic variables—including fasting and post-load glucose during OGTT—were measured only once, which may have introduced some misclassification due to intra-individual variability. This study is observational and not a randomized controlled trial, leaving the possibility of residual confounding. Moreover, although sex was included as a covariate in our multivariable models, we could not assess potential sex-specific differences in the relationship between eGFR and MEEi. Further studies with sex-stratified analyses are needed to clarify whether this association varies meaningfully between men and women. Finally, the study population consisted exclusively of White individuals with one or more cardiometabolic risk factors attending a tertiary care hospital, which may limit the generalizability of our results to other ethnic groups or the general population.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that a consistent reduction in renal function across eGFR categories is independently associated with reduced MEEi in individuals at high cardiometabolic risk. These data suggest that even a mild decline in nearly normal renal function may contribute to altered myocardial energetics, potentially explaining the increased risk of heart failure observed in patients with declining renal function [1–5]. Reduced myocardial MEEi may serve as an early marker of cardiac dysfunction in patients with CKD even in the absence of overt heart failure. This highlights the potential role of MEEi in risk stratification and underscores the need for future interventional studies aimed at improving myocardial energetic efficiency and renal function to prevent progression to clinical heart failure in this population.
