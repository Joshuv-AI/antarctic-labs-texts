Effects of maternal singing or voice on language and social-emotional development of preterm infants: protocol for a systematic review
Authors: Christine Halajian, Gladys Honein-Abouhaidar, Ola El Zein, Saadieh Masri, Lama Charafeddine
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPreterm birth remains a major risk factor for language delay, which may impact learning throughout life. Systematic reviews have shown conflicting effects of maternal voice exposure on premature infants’ physiological parameters, oral feeding and preterm cognitive and neurobehavioural development. Although simple measures to promote language development include exposure to music and maternal voice, the beneficial effects of maternal singing and voice on language and social-emotional development of preterm infants have not been systematically reviewed to confirm these findings. The overall aim of this systematic review is to assess the effects of maternal singing or voice, live or recorded, on the language and social-emotional development of preterm infants compared with standard of care. We will also determine the specific effects of maternal singing or voice on preterm infants’ physiological and behavioural parameters and on maternal stress and mother-infant bonding.Methods and analysisA search strategy will be developed to identify randomised controlled trials published in any language from inception till July 2025 on Medline OVID, EMBASE and CINAHL. We will also search the reference lists of relevant studies and reviews. Two reviewers will independently extract data on study design, baseline characteristics, details of the interventions employed and primary and secondary outcomes using a pre-piloted standardised data extraction form. The risk of bias and quality of evidence assessment will be done independently and in duplicate using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool and Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation approach, respectively. Random-effects models will be performed in case meta-analysis is possible, otherwise a qualitative summary will be provided. We will use RevMan 5.1 for data synthesis. We will adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.Ethics and disseminationSince this is a systematic review or previously published studies, it is exempt from IRB approval and was approved by the school’s Graduate Studies Committee (GSC) studies. The results of this systematic review will be disseminated at relevant conferences and submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal.PROSPERO registrationCRD42024613086.

Introduction

Rationale

Prematurity remains a significant public health problem globally as rates continue to increase, with most preterm births occurring in developing countries.1 With advancing technology, more preterm infants are surviving the neonatal period, and compared with term infants, children born preterm are at higher risk of neurodevelopmental delay, have behavioural problems, impaired learning and language development, and they have a three-to-fourfold increased risk of mental health disorders.24

Language disorders in premature infants are related to multiple factors other than the degree of prematurity, and these include neonatal morbidities and their severity, gender, language environment in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) and at home, the level of maternal education, the social and environmental status of the family, and access to early intervention.5 Another contributing factor to the poor language outcome, in addition to the noxious sensory stimuli in the NICU is the loss of exposure to maternal voice after preterm birth.68 These factors are important to address because language impairment has long-lasting effects on school performance, social relationships9 and later achievements in life.1012

Interventions to improve language outcomes in preterm children13 14 have shown that exposure to parental talk in the NICU was a stronger predictor of infant vocalisations and conversational turns than language from other adults. Aside from exposure to maternal voice and parental talk, there is evidence that music has short-term benefits on physiological parameters of preterm infants and that it affects brain structure and connectivity; however, long-term cognitive and behavioural outcomes remain unexplored15 16 Additionally, maternal singing during kangaroo care has been shown to improve infants’ physiological stability and reduce maternal anxiety.17 Recent studies reported improved language development of term infants after musical exposure at home including parental singing161820 and parent-infant singing interaction.21 22 Recent systematic reviews have shown conflicting effects of maternal voice exposure on premature infants’ physiological parameters (heart rate, respiratory rate and oxygen saturation), oral feeding and cognitive and neurobehavioural development.2325 Aside from the small sample sizes in the studies included, the inclusion criteria and methods of exposure were inconsistent, leading to different conclusions.

After an extensive search, we did not find a systematic review nor a protocol for systematic review addressing the question of maternal singing and voice effect on premature infants’ language development. Systematic reviews examining the effects of maternal voice exposure on preterm infants suggest that this intervention is beneficial as it showed that maternal voice (live or recorded) was associated with improved physiological and behavioural stability (fewer cardiorespiratory events and strengthened autonomic regulation), and more consistent benefits were seen for feeding behaviours and neurobehavioural outcomes.23 25 26 The systematic review by Provenzi et al23 included cognitive and neurobehavioural development outcomes. Though language outcomes were not a primary focus, the review noted positive signals for neurodevelopmental benefits, which may be related. Another more recent systematic review by Pino et al27 examined the impact of musical stimulation on the fetus and preterm newborns, showing that early music intervention in the NICU appears to have long-lasting influences on emotion regulation and neurodevelopment among preterm newborns. However, the studies reviewed had many limitations precluding a high level of evidence. Overall, while most studies showed short-term benefits, evidence for long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes remains limited and inconsistent.

Maternal singing and voice are low-cost interventions that could potentially have a great impact on long-term language and social-emotional development of preterm children. The reason for conducting this systematic review is to evaluate the existing studies related to the topic thoroughly and in an unbiased method to generate evidence-based data for clinical practice, policy and future research recommendations. This systematic review may yield results that would inform policies for practice change in terms of care of preterm infants in the NICU.

Objectives

This systematic review aims at assessing the effects of maternal singing and voice on the language and social-emotional development of preterm infants (born <37 weeks of gestation) compared with standard of care. We will also determine specific effects of live versus recorded maternal singing and voice on language and social-emotional developmental outcomes and physiological and behavioural parameters of preterm infants, in addition to the effects of this activity on maternal stress and mother-infant bonding.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

Study design

We will include randomised control trials including children born preterm (gestational age <37 weeks) and conducted in the setting of neonatal intensive care units (NICU) with parallel, cluster or factorial design in addition to the first phase of cross-over trials to avoid contamination from carry-over effect. We will include studies concerning human subjects published or accepted for publication from inception till July 2025 with no language limitation.

We will exclude observational and cohort studies as well as studies not meeting inclusion criteria; if the subjects were the same in several studies, only the study with the largest sample size will be included. Abstracts and conference proceedings will also be excluded.

This study is a systematic review which does not involve direct intervention with human participants. Thus, ethical approval is not required, and informed consent is not applicable.

Population/Participants

We will include preterm infants, singleton or multiple twins, born at less than 370/7 weeks of gestation and hospitalised in the NICU. We will include mothers who were involved in talking or singing (live or recorded) in any language, during the NICU stay.

Patient and public involvement

This systematic review involving patients or the general public in the planning, execution, reporting or distribution was neither appropriate nor feasible. This paper is a systematic review studying randomised clinical trials targeting effects of maternal singing or voice on preterm infants, which does not include direct inclusion of participants and manipulation of interventions.

Intervention

We define maternal singing or voice as singing or humming or talking in any language for at least 10 min per day two times per week, starting at any time after birth and before discharge home. The intervention can be delivered live or recorded. It can be delivered in combination with other interventions like Kangaroo mother care (KMC) or skin-to-skin, provided this is also the case for the control group.

Comparator

Preterm infants receiving standard care in the NICU.

Primary outcomes

Language development at 5–6 years as measured by the standardised assessment tools (such as Bayley Scale for infant development (BSID) or Communicative Development Inventory (CDI) or Test of Early Language Development (TELD-3) or any other relevant tools).

Information sources

Literature search strategies will be developed using medical subject headings (MeSH) and text words related to language development and maternal voice. We intend to use the following electronic databases for literature search: Medline OVID, EMBASE and CINAHL from inception and onwards. We will supplement the electronic database search by searching trial protocols on ClinicalTrials.gov for ongoing or recently completed trials and PROSPERO for ongoing or recently completed systematic reviews. The search will be limited to human subjects. We will also scan the reference lists of included studies or relevant identified reviews for additional relevant cited and citing articles. Finally, we will search the authors’ personal files to capture all relevant materials.

Search strategy

Only quantitative randomised or quasi-randomised studies will be sought. The specific search strategy will be created by the principal investigators and peer reviewed by an information specialist and the director of evidence synthesis who is an expert in systematic review searching. The search strategy in all databases will be developed with input from the project team, then peer reviewed by an independent expert in systematic reviews and not associated with the project, using the PRESS standard. A draft of the Medline OVID, CINAHL and EMBASE search strategies is included in online supplemental appendices 1–3 respectively. After the MEDLINE strategy is finalised, it will be adapted to the syntax and subject headings of the other databases. PROSPERO will also be searched for ongoing or recently completed systematic reviews. Additionally, we will scan the reference lists of included studies or relevant identified reviews for additional relevant cited and citing articles. The search will not include the grey literature and it will be updated toward the end of the review, after being validated to ensure the retrieval of a high proportion of eligible studies.

For the search, we will use medical subject headings (MeSH) terms and text words related to the following concepts: premature birth, mother, mother-infant relationship and singing.

Study records

Data management

Literature search results will be stored in the Covidence software. First, we will search for and remove duplicate titles, then we will develop “title and abstract screening sheet” based on inclusion and exclusion criteria set by the team as per the PICO. We will follow the screening questions by using a word file screening guide. We will then perform a calibration exercise to pilot, refine and standardise the reviewers’ title and abstract screening process using the screening sheet before completing the formal screening using the Covidence software. We will train all members of the review team on the review process and the software use prior to starting the review. In case of any discrepancy, we will resort to a third independent reviewer who is known to be a content expert.

Selection process

Two independent reviewers will screen the titles and abstracts obtained by the search against the inclusion and exclusion criteria (online supplemental appendix 4). The review team will retrieve full texts for all titles meeting the inclusion criteria or potentially eligible for review. The review team will then screen the full text reports and decide whether these meet the inclusion criteria (online supplemental appendix 5). A thorough review of the included studies will be conducted to ensure similar care was received by both groups (intervention and control) with the exception of the intervention itself (maternal singing or voice). To maintain consistency in inclusion criteria, studies with unclear reporting will be excluded from data extraction. In case of unresolved questions regarding eligibility, the team will seek additional information from a third reviewer or a content expert or the study authors. Any disagreement will be resolved through discussion among the team members or a third reviewer/content expert. At the end of the full text screening, the team will report the total number of included and excluded studies, as well as the reasons for exclusion. The degree of agreement between the reviewers will be assessed using Kappa. The selection process will be summarised in a PRISMA flow diagram as shown in online supplemental appendix 6. Neither of the review authors will be blind to the journal titles or to the study authors or institutions.

Data collection process

Data will be extracted in duplicate by two reviewers who will extract data independently from each eligible study using previously piloted and standardised extraction forms. Data abstraction will be done on an Excel sheet using a data abstraction template. Prior to starting the full review, calibration exercises will be conducted to ensure consistency across reviewers. Data to be abstracted will include demographic information, methodology, intervention details (live vs recorded singing or voice, duration, frequency etc.) and all reported outcomes. Reviewers will resolve disagreements by discussion and by having one of two arbitrators and content experts adjudicate unresolved disagreements. Uncertainties will be resolved by contacting the study authors whenever possible. In case of any missing information concerning the outcome of interest, we will contact the authors by email with two reminders (separated by 2 weeks each). We will ask authors to either send us the data of interest, if available, or to fill a table containing the outcomes of interest for them to fill out and send back.

Data items

The intervention of interest is maternal singing or voice (live or recorded). We will extract the following data from each study: author, year of publication, country, study design, sample size, clinical trial registration number, intervention method (type of maternal singing or voice live or recorded, the duration and the frequency of the intervention) and the procedure for the control group. We will also extract patient characteristics (gestational age, birth weight, gender, neonatal complications (Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS), Broncho-Pulmonary Dysplasia (BPD), Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA), Intra-Ventricular Haemorrhage (IVH), Necrotising Enterocolitis (NEC), Retinopathy Of Prematurity (ROP)), mean behavioural scores, time to full oral feeding, average weight gain per week, length of stay, language development score, social-emotional scores, maternal stress index and maternal bonding scores. We will also extract the duration of follow-up, type and source of financial support and publication status. For cross-over trials, we will extract data from the first period only because of possible carry-over effects. Whenever possible, we will use results from an intention to treat analysis. If effect sizes cannot be calculated, we will contact the authors for additional data.

Outcomes and prioritisation

Main primary outcomes:We will prioritise the language development of preterm children at 5–6 years as measured by standardised assessment tools (such as but not limited to BSID or CDI) and the social-emotional behaviour at 2–3 years as assessed using standardised tools such as ASQ-SE. The rationale behind the above is the following: Between the ages of 2 and 3, screening for social-emotional behaviour is crucial due to their significance in children’s social adaptation and emotional regulation. This age group is ideal for early identification of problems, as it is marked by the emergence of social adaptability, peer interaction and environmental adaptation, which are essential for emotional development.28 Numerous difficulties might appear during this developmental period, making screening crucial for capturing behavioural and emotional concerns.29 On the other hand, screening for language development between ages 5 and 6 years is important for academic success, as it coincides with the beginning of formal education. Children between ages 5 and 6 often demonstrate important language skills, such as comprehending complicated instructions and building longer, more complex sentences.30 Hence, early screening helps identify children at risk of language delays, allowing for prompt treatments.

Risk of bias in individual studies

To assess the studies’ risk of bias, we will use the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool for randomised trials (the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, chapter 8). This tool allows the classification of studies as having low, high or unclear risk of bias. The Cochrane tool addresses the random sequence generation (Selection bias), allocation concealment (selection bias), blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias), blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias), incomplete outcome data (attrition bias), selective reporting (reporting bias) as well as other sources of bias related to conflict of interest, for example. For each study, the above information will be abstracted, and study procedures will be described including verbatim quotes. We will perform calibration exercises for risk of bias assessment between reviewers as the risk of bias will be assessed by the two reviewers independently. In case of insufficient reported details in a study, the risk of bias will be judged as ‘unclear’ and the original study investigators will be contacted for more information. In case of disagreement, consensus will be sought after discussions or after consulting a third reviewer content expert for arbitration.

We will use the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (RoB 2) to record potential bias within and across studies (the template for completion is attached in online supplemental appendix 7. We will consider each item in the risk of bias assessment independently without an attempt to collate and assign an overall score.

We will use the RoB 2 to evaluate the overall quality and reliability of the evidence. In cases where implementation fidelity is mentioned, we will highlight it and discuss this information accordingly in the analysis.

Data synthesis

Study data, whether reported as continuous (eg, CDI scores) or categorical (eg, ASQ pass, retest or refer), will be quantitatively synthesised in case the studies have some degree of homogeneity in terms of study design and comparators. In this case, we will conduct meta-analyses using a random-effects model. A meta-analysis will be conducted when at least two studies report the outcome of interest.

Data analysis: If data are appropriate for quantitative synthesis, we will measure the intervention effect by determining the risk ratio (RR) with 95% CI for dichotomous (categorical) outcomes data and mean differences (with 95% CI) or standardised mean differences (95% CI) if different measurement scales are used for continuous outcomes. If most studies report RRs and we have control event rates, we will convert ORs to RRs. If the majority of studies report ORs, we will convert RRs to ORs. In all cases, we will report in the methods how conversions were done and justify the choice. We will describe separately skewed data and non-quantitative data.

We will be recording the scores reported from the different tools used across studies without prioritisation. Results from studies reporting on the same tool will be pooled for the meta-analysis. For studies reporting on different tools, we will record whether or not neurodevelopmental delay is present, and actual numerical scores will not be combined due to measurement scale differences.

Unit of analysis: We will assess all included trials to determine the unit of randomisation (individual or cluster) and whether this unit of randomisation is consistent with the unit of analysis. Studies with non-standard design, like cluster randomised trials, cross-over trials and studies with multiple treatment groups, will be addressed accordingly. For example, we will compute an interclass correlation co-efficient for cluster randomised trials to account for clustering according to the methods described in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. Due to the potential of carry-over effect, we will only use data from the first phase of cross-over trials, guided by the Cochrane Heart Group. When a study has more than two treatment groups, the additional treatment arms will be presented unless they are not relevant.

Missing data: When there is missing data, we will attempt to contact the original study authors to obtain the relevant missing data. If this cannot be achieved, we will use an imputation method as per Cochrane Handbook. Additionally, we will do sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of including trials that do not report an intention to treat analysis or have high rates of participant attrition, or have substantial missing data.

Heterogeneity: We will assess the clinical heterogeneity by examining the subjects’ factors among the different trials (eg, gestational age) and the trials’ characteristics (randomisation concealment, blinding of outcome assessment, losses to follow-up, treatment type and co-interventions). We will test the statistical heterogeneity using the χ2 test (significance level: 0.1) and I2 statistic (0% to 40%: might not be important; 30% to 60%: may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50% to 90%: may represent substantial heterogeneity; 75% to 100%: considerable heterogeneity). If heterogeneity tests are not significant, we will use the Mantel–Haenszel method for the fixed effect model. If we find high levels of heterogeneity among the trials (I2 ≥50%), then we will investigate the source of heterogeneity by performing subgroup analysis.

Data synthesis: We will use the statistical software RevMan 5.1 to combine and calculate each outcome, according to the statistical guidelines referenced in the current version of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. If statistical heterogeneity is observed (I2≥50% or p<0.1), the random effects model will be chosen. If heterogeneity is substantial, we will do a narrative, qualitative summary and will not perform a meta-analysis.

Subgroup analysis: We will perform subgroup analyses to explore possible sources of heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis will be based on subject characteristics (gestational age, sex), types of intervention (live vs recorded), duration and/or frequency of the intervention.

Sensitivity analysis: We will perform sensitivity analysis to explore the robustness of the overall analysis and assess the effect of missing data or risk of bias. We will examine the quality of study components, including full-text publications versus abstracts, preliminary results versus mature results and published versus unpublished data. We will do the analysis omitting studies with missing data, high attrition rates, those studies that do not report intention to treat or those that are judged to be at high risk of bias.

We will summarise and explain the characteristics and findings of included studies by reporting a systematic narrative synthesis of the information presented in the text and tables. The narrative synthesis will explore the relationship and findings both within and between the included studies, in line with the guidance from the Centre for Reviews and Dissemination.

Meta-bias(es)

We will assess whether there is reporting bias by reviewing the study protocol in case it was published before the start of the study. To look for each study protocol, we will screen PROSPERO and the Clinical Trial Register at the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform of the WHO. We will construct a funnel plot for asymmetry if more than 10 studies were identified to look for outcome reporting bias. We will also compare the fixed effect estimate against the random effects model and assess whether having small sample studies introduces bias. When there is small sample bias, it is more beneficial to use the random effects estimate of the intervention rather than the fixed effect estimate.

Confidence in cumulative evidence

We will assess the certainty of evidence for all the outcomes of interest using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology, which includes the five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. We will evaluate the quality across all the risk of bias domains. We will consider other domains as deemed appropriate. The certainty will be judged as high when there is considerable confidence in the effect estimate and further research is very unlikely to change the effect estimate; moderate when further research is likely to impact the confidence and likely to change it; low when further research is very likely to impact the confidence and likely to change the effect estimate or very low when any estimate of the effect is very uncertain.

Discussion

The importance of maternal singing or voice starts from week 30 of gestation, where repeated maternal sounds can improve the anatomical development of the primary auditory cortex of the preterm infant during the first month after birth.31 Infant-directed speech is defined as repetitive, simplified speech patterns, amplified vowel sounds, linguistic cues and emotional expression.31 These help newborns divide speech into comprehensible chunks, improve intelligibility and strengthen emotional connections between the infant and caregiver.31 In addition, maternal singing improves infant oxygenation, heart rate, weight gains and sucking reflexes, and shortens hospitalisation duration. Although there is a lack of evidence about the long-term effects of maternal singing and voice, it has been implied that early maternal singing may improve the early communication skills in infants, which would eventually prevent speech and language delays.31 On the maternal level, maternal singing increases oxytocin and decreases anxiety and improves the relation between mother and infant, which improves the infant’s emotional regulation.31 This low-cost intervention may be of particular benefit in low-resource settings as it may prove to contribute to improved social and emotional health of preterm infants.

Conducting this systematic review may provide a higher level of evidence on the effects of maternal singing or voice on the later language and social-emotional development of preterm infants, which may potentially have implications for the clinical practice and future research. From the clinical practice perspective, this review may provide further evidence for advising on the implementation of maternal singing or talking to preterm infants during their NICU stay. This low-cost and attainable practice aligns with family-centred care approach that is highly advocated in the NICU. This review may also provide stronger evidence for the impact of maternal singing or talking on maternal mental health outcomes. The results of this review may shed light on further unanswered research questions such as cultural considerations or contextual implications in practice. Finally, findings from this review may prove to be useful for policy development aiming at changing the practice in the NICU to encourage and support maternal involvement in singing or talking to their infants to promote their development. This aligns with the concept of family-centred and individualised care approach that can be applied at relatively low cost in all NICUs and most importantly in low resource settings.
