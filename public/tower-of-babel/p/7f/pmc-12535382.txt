A Water Soluble and Solid‐State Molecular Solar Thermal (MOST) System as Renewable Solar Fuel
Authors: Sara Hernáez‐Troya, Nil Sanosa, Alberto Giménez‐Gómez, Víctor Pozo‐Gavara, Diego Sampedro, Ignacio Funes‐Ardoiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolar energy storage is key to overcome the intermittent character of sunlight. We present a sustainable solution based on norbornadiene–quadricyclane pairs for molecular solar thermal (MOST) energy storage working in highly concentrated neutral water solutions and solid state. Photochemical preparation of high‐energy, metastable isomers in previously unattainable 1.64 M solutions was achieved. The best compound can be stored for 1.9 years and is stable in solid state, allowing for the preparation of up to 3 M water solutions. Catalyzed back‐conversion ensures efficient heat release and starting material recovery with remarkable fatigue resistance in water solution and solid state, exhibiting macroscopic heat release in both states, providing a ΔT of 39.6 K and fully recyclable use. These results highlight a promising avenue for the practical application of MOST technology.Keywords: Catalysis, Heat release, MOST, Solar energy, Solar fuel

Introduction

Energy demand has continually grown alongside human progress, and access to energy has now become a major necessity in modern societies.[ 1 ] The traditional reliance on fossil fuels is shifting toward greater engagement with renewable energies due to the former's contribution to climate change and their limited availability.[ 2 ] Among the energy sources alternative to fossil fuels (oil, coal, natural gas), solar energy exploitation is gaining momentum due to its wide availability and abundance (Figure 1a). In addition to the transition to sustainable energy sources, energy storage has also arisen as a significant challenge. In recent years, various energy technologies like artificial photosynthesis[ 3 ] and photovoltaics[ 4 ] have emerged as promising avenues to reduce dependence on fossil fuels. Complementarily, different technologies as the molecular solar thermal (MOST) energy storage systems based on organic photoswitches have been considered promising alternatives. MOST technology relies on a reversible chemical transformation upon light absorption that converts a ground‐state parent compound (blue dot, Figure 1a) into a metastable photoisomer (green dot, Figure 1a).[ 5 ] This photoreaction implies the storage of part of the light energy as chemical energy within chemical bonds. The back conversion of the metastable isomer to the parent molecule allows for the release of the stored energy as heat, which can be used further, along with the recovery of the starting material. The cyclic nature of the process ensures the formal conversion of light energy into heat mediated by chemical species that can be stored and transported, while no waste product is produced. This simple operation mode is limited by the complex design of the MOST materials required for practical uses.[ 6 ]

Over the last few decades, many different compounds have been proposed as potential candidates for MOST applications:[ 7 ] fulvalene diruthenium derivatives,[ 8 , 9 ] azocompounds,[ 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ] hydrazones,[ 16 ] dihydroazulenes,[ 17 , 18 ] and styrylpyryliums[ 19 ] have been studied in this context, but the most successful system to date is the norbornadiene (NBD) / quadricyclane (QC) pair.[ 20 ] For any MOST compound, including NBD derivatives, a long list of requirements must be optimized to maximize heat release, including:[ 21 ] the solar absorption spectrum match of the parent isomer, no spectral overlap between the two isomers, high photoisomerization quantum yields, high energy density, adequate half‐life time of the photoisomer, efficient back‐conversion, and fatigue resistance. Extensive research in the field has enabled the design of efficient MOST systems at the lab scale, in which most of these properties have been achieved (Figure 1b) independently.[ 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ] However, clear limitations persist, making the upscaling of MOST systems and the real‐world application of this technology still unfeasible.

First, most of the compounds designed to meet the required properties are soluble in organic solvents, typically toluene (Figure 1b). This allows for standard synthetic routes, small‐scale heat release optimization, and ease of operation in lab environments. Unfortunately, it also requires very specific conditions (high vacuum, isolated chambers) to detect macroscopic heat release at small scales.[ 22 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] Also, these solvents pose serious concerns regarding safety, toxicity, and sustainability that would hinder any practical use. Additionally, engineering issues stemming from the compatibility of materials used for the device with organic solvents would complicate and increase the cost of the prototype. Thus, shifting from toluene to greener, nontoxic, and nonflammable solvents is essential for end‐user applications. Our research group recently reported a set of NBDs soluble in polar solvents as the starting point toward designing more efficient molecules in a sustainable scenario.[ 30 ] Castro et. al also reported a slightly soluble in basic water medium NBD‐QC pair.[ 31 ] However, this compound did not release macroscopic heat due to the extremely low energy density of the MOST fluid. This is strongly linked to the second critical limitation of this technology.

NBDs have been designed to maximize the energy difference between isomers, but even in the best scenario, poor solubility results in low energy density on the MOST fluid (a solution of NBD in the selected solvent). Efforts have been made to minimize this problem by using neat MOST compounds,[ 32 ] but it typically leads to poor photochemical conversions due to the inner filter effect, which again results in low energy density of the charged MOST fluid. The combination of these two limitations makes the design of NBDs with increased solubility in neutral water an extremely valuable objective on the path to a paradigm shift in MOST technology.

In a complementary approach, solid‐state applications have also been considered to store energy and release heat. This would bypass the toxicity of solvents and solubility issues but, in turn, would complicate photochemical activation significantly (Figure 1b). For instance, Chakraborty et al. have recently reported[ 26 ] an anthracene‐based system capable of solid‐state self‐activation of energy release, mimicking the performance of fossil fuels. Besides, NBD thin films have shown potential in practical uses.[ 33 ] This illustrates a complementary approach to using MOST compounds in solution phase.

Considering that the water solubility of any potential MOST compound must be significantly increased for practical applications, we aimed to design and prepare a new set of NBD derivatives capable of effectively operating in highly concentrated neutral water solutions and in solid state. Herein, we report a pioneering family of water‐soluble NBD derivatives, demonstrating the performance of MOST technology and paving the way for upcoming technological applications.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of New MOST Compounds

We based the design of the new NBDs on the choice of a suitable core to assemble different water‐soluble amines. Following our previous results on amide‐substituted NBDs,[ 30 ] we first synthesized NBD1a using the same synthetic route that we explored previously. Next, we incorporated a highly water‐soluble amine using a standard amidation reaction, resulting in the formation of NBD2 with a 64% yield (Figure 2a). While NBD2 showed outstanding solubility in water (1.29 ± 0.02 M at 25 °C, see section 4, Figure S32), NBD and QC displayed overlapping bands in the UVC range (λ max(NBD2 ) = 244 nm, λ max(QC2 ) = 212 nm). This has a direct impact on the photoisomerization quantum yield (ɸ = 4%). Even so, irradiation of NBD2 fully converts it to QC2 using a 14 X 8 W lamp photoreactor (λ max = 303 nm) in 25 min at 4.07·10−3 M concentration solution in water. Similarly to other symmetric NBDs previously reported in the group,[ 30 ] the thermal back reaction is extremely slow, and QC2 does not turn into NBD2 after reflux in water for 48 h (see, Figure S61), which is in good agreement with the calculated thermal free energy barrier (ΔG ‡‐NBD2 = 39.1 kcal mol−1), at the ωB97xD/6–311++(3d,2p) level of theory. This high stability is crucial to allow on‐demand heat release. In this case, extremely high thermal stability allows for the long half‐life time (an estimated value of ∼88 million years was obtained from the computed energy barrier). In contrast, energy release should be as fast as possible when required, by using an external stimulus. Thus, we explored the back‐conversion reaction using commercial supported gold nanoparticles on carbon support (Au/EC300J), which provided excellent results in our preliminary study, as well as in recently reported Libuda's work.[ 30 , 35 , 34 ] Unfortunately, QC 2 takes 50 hours to full back‐convert to NBD2 in the presence of the heterogenous gold catalyst (kAuEC300J‐NBD2 = 2.05·10−5) at 10% weight using a 1 mg mL−1 concentration of NBD (4.07·10−3 M NBD2 , 10% (w/w) of catalyst, see Figure S49). This reaction rate is considerably slower than reported back‐reaction rates in toluene (k ref = 1.01·10−3).[ 36 ]

Given these preliminary results, we hypothesized that an asymmetric NBD core should be used to improve both photochemical isomerization and catalyzed back‐conversion reactivity. This strategy, based on an increased conjugation, might affect the photophysical properties by red shifting the absorption while keeping the activity as MOST material. Focused on this purpose, we conducted three additional transformations using highly polar amines 1, 2, and 3 starting from an unsymmetrical NBD core, labeled as NBD1c , which is synthesized from the corresponding ester derivative (Figure S4), bearing a phenyl and an acid group as substituents. As expected, the solubilities of NBD3 and NBD4 are lower than NBD2 due to the fewer hydrogen bond acceptor and donor groups (NBD4 0.108 ± 0.002 M at 25 °C, NBD3 0.072 ± 0.008 M at 25 °C). However, NBD5 showed a water solubility of 1.64 ± 0.01 M at 25 °C, which is rapidly increased with temperature and enables MOST reactivity at a unique record value concentration (∼3 M at 80 °C, see below). In addition, absorption of NBD5 is red shifted, compared to NBD3 and NBD4 (λ max(NBD5 ) = 286 nm), (λ max(QC5 ) = 230 nm), resulting in no overlapping bands between QC and NBD.

Finally, back‐conversion of NBD3‐5 was explored under Au/EC300J gold catalyst. 35 Interestingly, the complete transformation of QC3‐5 into NBD3‐5 takes place in less than 1 hour under the same conditions described above, which is 50X faster than NBD2 and implies an adequate timeframe for macroscopic heat release demonstration. Isomerization quantum yields (ɸ iso) at 313 nm for NBD3‐5 , and at 240 nm for NBD2 were determined by potassium ferrioxalate actinometry. While the ɸ iso values are similar for the asymmetric structure (NBD3 = 30%, NBD4 = 20%, and NBD5 = 20%), the ɸ iso for the NBD2 structure is markedly lower at just 4% (see, Figures S39–S42). We hypothesized that the asymmetric structure provides a larger dipole moment in the excited state potential energy surface that lowers the reaction barrier in the excited state minimize other nonreactive deactivation channels, such as fluorescence.

Due to the excellent properties of NBD5 as MOST system, we scaled up the synthesis to perform macroscopic heat release experiments (Figure 3a). Gratifyingly, NBD5 could be crystallized as a white solid in multigram quantities (Figure 2a), and after isomerization in solution, QC5 was also purified as a white and bench‐stable solid, enabling, for the first time, a solid‐state application of NBD as a MOST system (Figure 2a).

NBD5 Full Characterization as MOST Compound

Due to the excellent properties shown, we focused on a more detailed study of NBD5 . The reaction enthalpy (ΔH°storage) of QC5 back conversion was obtained using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, Figure 3b). The measured value of 16.6 kcal mol−1, which is centred at 126.1 °C, is slightly lower than other NBD/QC pairs, which typically range around 20 kcal mol−1. This is due to the overlap of the energy release with the melting enthalpy at 98.1 °C. The relatively high temperature of reversion indicated a substantially long half‐time of QC5 . Thermogravimetric analysis showed a small weight loss around 100 °C, assigned to crystallization water loss, and a large stability up to 230 °C (see, Figure S86). So, we measured the thermal back conversion of QC5 to NBD5 at different temperatures (50, 70, 80, and 90 °C) and the room temperature half‐life time of QC5 was extrapolated to an estimated value of 1.9 years. This excellent result is adequate for daily, seasonal, and long‐term MOST applications. Given its stability and the high half‐life time, we proceeded with an accelerated cyclability test to assess the performance of the NBD5 /QC5 couple over multiple MOST cycles. Using ethylene glycol as the solvent (b.p. = 197.3 °C), the system underwent 20 complete cycles of photoisomerization and thermal back conversion at 100 °C. Unlike other green solvents or water, ethylene glycol allows heating up to 100 °C without significant evaporation, thereby reducing thermal back‐conversion times to 30 min per cycle. Similar back‐conversion has been observed in water at longer times (Figures S61–S63), but it is not practical for cyclability studies in reasonable times. Remarkably, degradation was minimal after 20 cycles −18% (see, Figure S48), underscoring the long‐term cyclability and fatigue resistance of the material.

Despite the limited absorption in the visible region at the low concentration used in the UV–vis, NBD5 can be easily photoisomerized using standard batch conditions under air at very high concentrations (0.15 to 1.00 M) using a solar simulator (SF‐300‐B, 300 W xenon lamp), due to the increased absorption of highly concentrated solutions (see Figures 3c and S82–S85). This underscores the promising properties for solar energy exploitation of this compound (see Figure 3). This high efficiency contrasts with redshifted nonpolar NBDs, which usually require a more complex setup, including flow photoisomerization or much lower concentrations to prevent polymerization.[ 36 ] The addition of a catalyst to the QC5 solution takes no more than 75 min to revert to NBD5 under the same conditions. In order to improve back‐conversion rates, we moved from heterogeneous supported gold nanoparticles to commercially available gold nanoparticles (Au‐NP, 5 nm, OD1 in citrate buffer) dispersed in water. After reaction optimization (see, Figure S55), the fastest back‐conversion experiment was achieved using a 1.48·10−3 M QC5 solution, adding 1000 µL of Au‐NP at 50 °C (effective QC5 concentration of 1.12·10−3 M). Under these conditions, NBD5 was fully recovered in less than 150 s. With these conditions in hand, we next scaled up the experiment, aiming for macroscopic heat release.

Macroscopic Heat Release in Solution and Solid state

Next, we explored the macroscopic heat release in solid and water solution experiments (Figure 4). In both cases, a real‐time infrared (IR) camera was used to measure temperature differences between QC [solid/solution] and NBD [solid/solution] and record the variation in the maximum temperature differences between QC5 and NBD5 . This procedure allowed to differentiate the heat generation of the QC sampled compared to the standard heating of the NBD sample.

We used the following procedure for measuring the heat release of 1, 2, and 3 M water solutions. First, 200 µL of aqueous Au‐NP solution was placed into each of the two cuvettes at the same time, which were positioned approximately 10 cm apart. Once both Au‐NP solutions had been heated (60 s to reach bath temperature), the specific amount of NBD5 and QC5 was added to each cuvette that is 160 mg to achieve a 2 M solution and 240 mg for the 3 M solution. The evolution of temperature of each sample, as well as the temperature difference between both samples, was monitored using the real‐time IR camera (Figure 4a). Different samples were measured using three different setups (see, Figures S71 and S81), where the oil bath was heated at 80 °C in a heating plate (setup 1 and setup 2) and in a water tank with an accurate stirring heater (setup 3). Setup 1 allowed a rapid heating of the samples and a quicker back‐conversion reaction, providing high ΔT values due to the small heat dissipation, while setup 2 provided a more stable bath temperature and a constant heat release over time, at the expense of smaller ΔT due to the efficient heat transfer to the oil bath. At 2 M concentration, setup 1 reached a ΔT max of 7.5 K. The heat release extended over time for more than 30 min. Using setup 2, the same reaction conditions yielded a constant ΔT max between 0.5 and 1.0 K for more than 35 min. The progress of the reaction in both setups was monitored by UV–vis. However, the intense heating pulses of the heating plate did not allow for a stable thermal bath, and the reaction temperature was not constant enough. Despite this effect was minimized by measuring the temperature difference between the QC sample and a reference NBD sample (with similar thermal properties), we finally moved to a water tank heater that provided a more stable thermal control (setup 3, Figure 4a). Interestingly, the stability of the thermal bath allows to perform the back‐conversion reaction with low catalyst:NBD ratio at 2 M and high catalyst:NBD ratio at 1 M, using the same amount of NBD (160 mg) and 200 µL or 400 µL, respectively. When high concentration of NBD and low concentration of catalyst is used, the heat release takes longer and is very stable although the ΔT is considerably lower (∼0.5–0.7 K). However, adding double volume amount of catalyst for same amount of NBD (x2 catalyst/NBD ratio), we observed a rapid heat release with maximum ΔT of 4.3 K and a short back‐conversion time (less than 310 s).

All the macroscopic heat release experiments were measured using standard conditions, under air and with no special isolation or vacuum systems. This represents a notable advance, from a practical point of view, when compared to previously reported approaches, as the design and building of the final device would be simpler and cheaper.[ 20 , 21 , 22 ] As a note, the higher heat capacity of water limits the temperature difference measured respect to other organic solvent, although this effect is mitigated due to the large NBD:water ration in high concentrated solutions (see section 12 in the Supporting Information for further details).

Finally, based on the interesting solid‐state properties of NBD5 and inspired by the seminal work by Prof. Han,[ 26 ] we explored the potential of QC5 as solid‐state fuel. To verify this hypothesis, we performed solvent‐free thermal back‐conversion experiments on compact solid pellets of QC5 (see Supporting Information for sample preparation and experimental setup, Figure S70). We performed these experiments at elevated temperatures with the aim of increasing the back‐conversion rate and measuring macroscopic heat release. QC5 and NBD5 pellets were placed on an aluminum plate and heated by a heating bath at 170 °C (Figure 4b, 1). After 14 seconds (Figure 4b), the heating curve of both samples showed remarkable differences (ΔT = 12.4 K). Then, a sharp decrease in ΔT is observed until t = 20 s (Figure 4b, ΔT plot). We attribute this heating transfer decrease to the melting process of QC5 sample (m.p = 110–111 °C), which is reached before. Thus, during this interval, two thermal processes coexist in the QC5 pellet: the back conversion to NBD (exothermic) and the melting process (endothermic). Once the solid is melted, the abrupt increase in ΔT (t = 30 s, Figure 4b, 3, ΔT = 39.6 K) for the QC5 pellet is exclusively attributed to the exothermic process of the [2 + 2] cycloreversion of QC. At the end of the experiment, ΔT gradually decreases until t = 60 s, where ΔT = 6.5 K, suggesting that the thermal recovery of NBD is accomplished. 1H‐NMR analysis demonstrated that the full conversion of QC5 to NBD5 is reached after 90 s (see, Figure S65‐A).

Motivated by the clean recovery of the initial NBD5 , we then explored the potential recyclability of the solar fuel, which would be unique for MOST systems. To do so, an initial sample of a pellet of NBD5 was photoisomerized to QC5 . Then, the resulting sample was compressed as a pellet and back converted to NBD5 . The process was repeated three times, exhibiting a minimum recyclability value of 86% (see, Figure S65‐B). This value is reduced mainly due to the manual recovery of the solid sample, which required scratching of the solid sample, dissolution, photoisomerization, solvent evaporation, and pellet preparation. However, this demonstrates the potential of this molecule as a MOST system in a renewable solid‐state solar fuel.

Conclusion

In summary, a new family of NBD derivatives with excellent solubility in water was synthesized. The four compounds are capable of storing solar energy in chemical bonds effectively and releasing their energy using gold nanoparticles as efficient catalysts. The most efficient compound showed an unprecedented 1.64 M water solubility, large stability for both NBD and QC isomers, and excellent photoreactivity under solar simulator conditions at very high concentrations. Finally, this compound was synthesized on a multigram scale, allowing macroscopic heat release in both water solution (7.5 K) and solid state (39.6 K). Its excellent recyclability turns this compound into the first effective renewable solar fuel in solid state. These outstanding MOST properties will open an excellent opportunity to transition this technology toward real applications.
