Effect of Endovascular Treatment in HERMES Patients With Isolated Occlusion of the Intracranial Internal Carotid Artery
Authors: Daniel P.O. Kaiser, Thanh N. Nguyen, Bruce C.V. Campbell, Jeffrey L. Saver, Diederik W.J. Dippel, Andrew M. Demchuk, Charles B.L.M. Majoie, Scott B. Brown, Peter J. Mitchell, Johannes C. Gerber, Serge Bracard, Francis Guillemin, Tudor G. Jovin, Keith W. Muir, Philip White, Daniela Schoene, Michael D. Hill, Mayank Goyal, Volker Puetz, the HERMES Collaborators
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal management of patients with symptomatic isolated internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusion is unknown. We aimed to assess whether endovascular treatment (EVT) compared with standard medical care was associated with improved functional outcomes in patients with acute symptomatic isolated intracranial ICA occlusion without involvement of the middle or anterior cerebral artery, that is, ICA‐I occlusion. Additionally, we aimed to compare ICA‐I with ICA‐L/T occlusion, which involves themiddle and anterior cerebral artery, respectively.MethodsWe analyzed data from the highly effective reperfusion evaluated in multiple endovascular stroke trials (HERMES) collaboration, which performed an individual patient data meta‐analysis of 7 randomized controlled trials conducted between 2010 and 2017 assessing the benefit of EVT compared to medical management in patients with anterior circulation large vessel occlusion. We assessed the association between EVT and 90‐day good functional outcome (modified Rankin scale scores 0–2), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores at 24 hours, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage rates and mortality in patients with ICA‐I and ICA‐L/T occlusion.ResultsWe included 442 patients with intracranial ICA occlusion, of whom 38 (8.6%) had ICA‐I occlusion. In the ICA‐I group, the median age [interquartile range] was 69.5 [61.7–79.5] years, 42.1% were male, and median baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale was 17 [15–20]. Compared with standard medical care alone, EVT resulted in higher good outcome rates in patients with ICA‐I (42.9% versus 25%; P=0.296) and ICA‐L/T occlusion (32.5% versus 14.4%; P<0.001), and significant improvement in National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores at 24 hours. Mortality and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage rates were similar between the treatment groups for both occlusion types.ConclusionsA minority of patients with intracranial carotid occlusion presented with ICA‐I occlusion in the HERMES population. EVT in patients with ICA‐I occlusion and moderate‐to‐severe deficit was safe and tended to be similarly effective as compared to patients with ICA‐L/T occlusion.

Endovascular treatment (EVT) is an effective and safe treatment for patients with acute ischemic stroke due to anterior circulation large vessel occlusion (acLVO) including the intracranial internal carotid artery (ICA). 1 , 2 However, the term ICA occlusion covers a broad spectrum of occlusion scenarios.

Isolated intracranial ICA occlusion, that is, ICA‐I occlusion, does not involve the middle and anterior cerebral arteries. Therefore, antegrade collateral filling of the middle cerebral artery via the circle of Willis (ie, the anterior communicating artery) may compensate for anterior circulation perfusion deficits caused by ICA‐I occlusion. In contrast, intracranial ICA occlusions involving the middle and anterior cerebral arteries, referred to as ICA‐L and ICA‐T occlusions, are characterized by more severe perfusion deficits in the middle cerebral artery due to the absence of antegrade filling (Figure 1). Thus, these etiologies are pathophysiologically distinct and a differentiated assessment of the benefit of EVT is warranted. Studies comparing EVT versus medical care alone in patients with this infrequent occlusion type are scarce or imbalanced. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6

We aimed to assess whether the treatment effect of EVT on improved functional outcome in patients with ICA occlusions from the highly effective reperfusion evaluated in multiple endovascular stroke trials (HERMES) collaboration is maintained in patients with ICA‐I occlusion. Moreover, we sought to compare clinical outcomes of patients according to the ICA occlusion type (ie, ICA‐I versus ICA‐L/T occlusion).

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Patient Sample and Study Design

The HERMES collaboration pooled individual data of 1764 patients from 7 randomized controlled trials (MR CLEAN [Endovascular Treatment for Acute Ischemic Stroke in the Netherlands], 7 ESCAPE [Endovascular Treatment for Small Core and Proximal Occlusion Ischemic Stroke], 8 EXTEND‐IA [Extending the Time for Thrombolysis in Emergency Neurological Deficits‐Intra‐Arterial], 9 SWIFT PRIME [Solitaire FR as Primary Treatment for Acute Ischemic Stroke], 10 REVASCAT [Endovascular Revascularization With Solitaire Device Versus Best Medical Therapy in Anterior Circulation Stroke Within 8 Hours], 11 THRACE [Trial and Cost‐Effectiveness Evaluation of Intra‐Arterial Thrombectomy in Acute Ischemic Stroke] , 12 and PISTE [Pragmatic Ischemic Thrombectomy Evaluation] 13 ) and established EVT as a safe and effective treatment for patients with acute ischemic stroke due to acLVO. 1 , 2 Detailed methodology, inclusion criteria of the individual trials, and the HERMES meta‐analysis are published elsewhere. In brief, all patients had proximal acLVO other than THRACE that included 2 patients with basilar artery occlusion. Patients were randomized to an EVT or control (CTL) group receiving standard medical care alone including administration of intravenous alteplase if indicated. In SWIFT‐PRIME and THRACE, concurrent extracranial carotid occlusion was an exclusion criterion whereas such patients could be enrolled in other studies analyzed in HERMES.

All of these trials required baseline vessel imaging to identify patients with anterior circulation ischemic stroke and used predominantly stent retrievers or second‐generation neurothrombectomy devices in the EVT groups. As part of the HERMES collaboration, imaging data sets were read by independent HERMES core laboratories including baseline CT angiography, MRA, and follow‐up CT or MRI. Readers were blinded to all clinical information, except the side of stroke. 2

For the present study, we performed a post hoc analysis of all patients with intracranial ICA occlusion in the HERMES population. The ICA occlusion type was characterized on the baseline CT angiography or MRI angiography. An ICA‐I occlusion was classified if the occlusion did not involve any part of the middle cerebral artery or anterior cerebral artery, as compared with ICA‐L occlusion (additional involvement of the middle cerebral artery) or ICA‐T occlusion (additional involvement of the middle and anterior cerebral artery). We did not obtain data on contralateral ICA stenosis or occlusion. The study complied with The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 14 All participants provided written informed consent according to each trial protocol, and the local ethics board approved each study.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome for this analysis was the 90‐day modified Rankin scale score, with good functional outcome defined as a modified Rankin scale score of 0–2. Secondary outcomes were the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at 24 hours and the final infarct size as assessed with the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) at 24 hours. Safety outcomes were mortality at 90 days and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage at 24 hours using the individual symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage definitions of the trials.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented using median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and frequency tables for discrete variables. Comparisons between groups (eg, ICA‐I versus ICA‐L/T cohorts or EVT versus medical management) were performed using t‐tests or Wilcoxon's rank‐sum test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for binomial variables.

Due to small sample size in the ICA‐I cohort, covariate adjustments were not employed for analysis of outcome data. Odds ratios (ORs) were derived from logistic regression modeling and are presented with 95% CIs; modified Rankin scores are illustrated using horizontal stacked bar graphs where relevant.

All P values are 2‐sided, with values less than 0.05 regarded as statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient Population

Among 1764 patients from the HERMES collaboration, 442 patients presented with an intracranial ICA occlusion, of whom 39 (8.8%) had ICA‐L occlusion, 365 (82.6%) had ICA‐T occlusion, and 38 (8.6%) had ICA‐I occlusion. In patients with intracranial ICA occlusion, 41.4% had concomitant extracranial ICA occlusion or stenosis (>70%) defined by the baseline imaging.

Compared with patients with ICA‐L/T occlusion, patients with ICA‐I occlusion were older (69.5 [IQR 61.7–79.5] versus 66.1 [IQR 55.0–76.0]; P=0.049) and had higher median [IQR] ASPECTS at baseline (9 [6–10] versus 7 [6–8]; P=0.006). The median baseline NIHSS was similar between the ICA‐I and ICA‐L/T groups (17 [15–20] versus 18 [15‐22]; P=0.06). Patients with ICA‐I occlusion more often received MRI as baseline imaging and, as predicted by the occlusion type definition, had higher clot burden scores indicating less intracranial thrombus burden (Table S1). The treatment characteristics including intravenous thrombolysis, type of anesthesia, recanalization, and reperfusion status did not differ between the ICA‐I and ICA‐L/T groups (Table S2).

Relationship Between Treatment and Outcome According to Occlusion Type

Among patients with ICA‐I occlusion, 14 patients were in the EVT group and 24 patients in the CTL group. Patients in the EVT group were less likely to have hypertension (23.1% versus 70.8%; P=0.008) (Table 1). In patients with ICA‐L/T occlusion, there were no differences in baseline characteristics between the EVT (n=201) and CTL group (n=203) (Table 1).

Outcomes of Patients With ICA‐I Versus ICA‐L/T Occlusion

In total, 31.6% of patients with ICA‐I occlusion achieved 90‐day favorable functional outcome (modified Rankin scale 0–2) compared with 23.4% of patients with ICA‐L/T occlusion (P=0.43). Moreover, patients with ICA‐I occlusion had higher median ASPECTS scores at 24 hours compared with patients with ICA‐L/T occlusion (5.0 [4.0–7.0] versus 3.0 [1.0–6.0]; P=0.001). We observed no differences in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage rates (2.7% versus 3%; P=0.423) and mortality (23.7% versus 21.1%; P=0.709) between patients according to ICA occlusion types (Table S3).

Predictors of Good Outcome

In univariate analysis, lower age (OR, 0.983 [95% CI 0.968–0.999]; P=0.048) was associated with good functional outcome in patients with ICA‐I occlusion. In patients with ICA‐L/T occlusion, lower age, lower systolic blood pressure, lower baseline NIHSS score, left‐sided occlusion, and higher grade of collaterals were associated with good functional outcome (Table S4, Figure S1).

Discussion

Our analysis of data from the HERMES collaboration suggests that EVT is a safe and effective treatment option for patients with acute ischemic stroke with moderate‐to‐severe deficits due to an intracranial isolated ICA‐I occlusion, that is, ICA‐occlusion without involvement of the middle or anterior cerebral artery. However, while we observed a significant improvement in the NIHSS score at 24 hours post‐treatment, the precision of the estimated 17.9% increase in good outcomes at 90 days with EVT is low (not statistically significant), likely due to the small number of patients with ICA‐I occlusion in the HERMES population. This assumption is supported by the results of the larger ICA‐L/T group, where EVT demonstrated a statistically significant estimated 18.1% increase in good outcome at 90 days. Moreover, there was a similar effect of EVT in patients with ICA‐I and ICA‐L/T occlusions, with most of the baseline characteristics of both groups, including the baseline NIHSS score, being comparable. Interestingly, the baseline ASPECTS, indicating early ischemic changes, was higher in the ICA‐I group. This finding could be partially explained by compensated anterior circulation perfusion deficits via the circle of Willis and the patent middle cerebral artery. However, it is important to note that the rate of baseline MRI was higher in the ICA‐I group, which may have also impacted the visual ASPECTS scoring.

The rate of patients with ICA‐I occlusion was 2.2% in the overall HERMES population, due to the small number of patients with this occlusion type included in the randomized trials. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 The actual rate of patients with isolated cervical or intracranial carotid occlusion in real‐world settings is estimated at 2.5% to5%. 15 , 16 The proportion of patients with isolated intracranial ICA‐I occlusion is unclear, in part, due to the difficult differentiation of patients with cervical compared with isolated intracranial ICA‐I occlusion on acute CT angiography. 17 Recent registry studies have reported widely varying ICA‐I occlusion rates of 1% to7.8% among acLVO patients who received EVT. 18 , 19 However, these numbers may underestimate the actual rate of patients with acute ICA‐I occlusion as these patients may present with minor symptoms due to preserved collateral flow, or as EVT is less frequently considered due to a patent middle cerebral artery and assumed periprocedural distal embolization risk. In fact, intravenous thrombolysis has recently been shown to be associated with a 33% risk of early neurological deterioration, mostly due to early artery‐to‐artery embolism in patients with isolated cervical or intracranial ICA occlusion presenting with minor symptoms. 20 While this study was not designed to analyze risk factors for early neurological deterioration, it is worth noting that intravenous thrombolysis was not found to be among the predictors of functional outcome at 90 days.

Studies comparing EVT versus medical care alone in patients with this infrequent occlusion type are scarce or imbalanced. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Of note, patients enrolled in this HERMES analysis with ICA‐I occlusion presented with high NIHSS indicative of moderate‐to‐severe deficits. Supporting our findings, a recent multicenter study using propensity score matching indicated that EVT resulted in higher favorable outcome rate of 56% of patients compared with 36% of patients who were treated with best medical care alone (propensity score matching: OR, 1.89; 95% CI 0.84–4.24; P=0.12).21 The median NIHSS in patients treated with EVT was 13. However, this study was also limited by a small sample size (n=45 patients in each group after propensity score matching).

While the number of patients with isolated intracranial ICA‐I occlusion was low in the HERMES population, our findings support the use of EVT in patients presenting with moderate‐to‐severe deficit as it was safe, was associated with early neurological improvement at 24 hours which is a marker for the efficacy of acute stroke therapies, 22 and was associated with a numerically higher good outcome rate (42.9% versus 25%), albeit a difference not reaching statistically significance. Our findings add to previous work from the HERMES collaboration that showed benefit of EVT across a broad range of intracranial acLVO and clinical subgroups. 1 , 2 Future studies or registries should enroll and analyze a larger number of patients with ICA‐I occlusion to provide a more robust assessment of the safety and efficacy of EVT in this patient population.

Limitations

Limitations of our study include the small sample size, likely reflecting the low prevalence of patients with isolated intracranial ICA‐I occlusion compared with other ICA occlusion types. Due to the small sample size, P values have limited meaning and we were unable to adjust for potentially confounding factors. The study lacks information regarding an underlying intracranial ICA pathology and specific types of treatment used, including angioplasty and stenting among different ICA occlusion types. Moreover, information on additional standards of care during the acute phase was lacking; however, it was based on national guidelines and standard procedures in highly experienced stroke centers participating in the HERMES collaboration. As patients enrolled with ICA‐I occlusion in the HERMES collaboration presented with moderate‐to‐severe deficit, we do not know the safety or efficacy profile of EVT in ICA‐I occlusion patients who present with mild deficit. Our findings may therefore not be generalizable to patients with ICA‐I occlusion and mild deficit. The strength of our study was the analysis of pooled individual patient data from randomized controlled trials assessing the efficacy of EVT. We therefore are confident that, despite the aforementioned limitations, the results of this study are of value for the often‐complex treatment decision‐making processes in patients suffering from isolated intracranial ICA occlusion.

Conclusion

In this analysis of patients from the HERMES population with isolated intracranial ICA‐I occlusion and moderate‐to‐severe deficit, EVT was safe and beneficial although the benefit on functional outcome did not reach statistical significance likely due to the sample size. Future studies and registries should enroll a larger number of patients with ICA‐I occlusion to provide a more robust assessment of the safety and efficacy of EVT.
