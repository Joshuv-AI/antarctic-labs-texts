Efficacy and Thrombotic Risk of Thrombopoietin Receptor Agonists for Immune Thrombocytopenia Secondary to Systemic Lupus and Antiphospholipid Syndrome: French Experience With 80 Patients
Authors: Cindy Marques, Guillaume Moulis, Mickaël Roussotte, Bernard Bonnotte, Jean‐François Viallard, Julie Graveleau, Hervé Lobbes, Nathalie Costedoat‐Chalumeau, Alban Deroux, Delphine Gobert, Antoine Dossier, Laurent Gilardin, Olivier Lambotte, Mylène Dufrenoy, Matthieu Mahévas, Marc Michel, Pascal Sève, Bertrand Godeau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOnly few data are available regarding the efficacy and safety of thrombopoietin receptor agonists (TPO‐RAs) for treating systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and/or antiphospholipid syndrome (APS)‐associated immune thrombocytopenia (ITP). We retrospectively assessed the efficacy and safety of TPO‐RAs in 80 adults with ITP in three subgroups: (1) 37 patients with definite or incomplete SLE and no antiphospholipid antibodies (APAs), (2) 27 patients with definite or incomplete SLE associated with APAs and no history of arterial or venous thrombotic events (TEs) and (3) 16 patients with APS. In total, 39 (48.8%) patients received eltrombopag, 14 (17.5%) romiplostim and 27 (33.8%) both TPO‐RAs sequentially. The overall response of ITP was 78.8%. In total, 17 (21.3%) patients had 21 TEs (7 venous and 14 arterials, including 3 catastrophic APS); 3 were fatal. The rate of TEs was 8.1% in the SLE or lupus without APAs group, 22.2% in the SLE or lupus with APAs group and 50% in the APS group. In 12 patients, TPO‐RAs were continued after TE onset, combined with an anticoagulant (n = 12) and/or an antiplatelet agent (n = 3). Only one TE relapse occurred in the nine patients with TPO‐RAs maintained and combined with an anticoagulant after TE. TPO‐RAs may be effective safe in ITP associated with SLE and no APAs. Their use should be carefully considered in the presence of thrombotic risk factors and/or APA positivity and should be avoided in patients with definite APS. When a TE occurs, risk of relapse is low with maintenance of the TPO‐RA combined with anticoagulation.Keywords: antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, immune thrombocytopenia, thrombopoietin

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease associated with frequent hematological manifestations; immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is found in 7% to 30% of cases [1]. SLE‐associated ITP is defined as SLE with platelet count < 100 × 109/L without any other obvious cause [2]. ITP in this setting is usually mild to moderate and does not require any specific treatment. SLE can be associated with the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (APAs) in one third of cases and with definite secondary antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) in 10%–30% of cases [3]. Thrombocytopenia is also a common manifestation of APS and has been recently included as a diagnostic criterion [4].

The pathophysiological mechanisms of SLE‐ and APS‐associated ITP are similar to those for primary ITP, and a number of patients also have ITP with antinuclear antibodies (ANAs), anti‐double stranded DNA (anti‐dsDNA) antibodies, anti‐Sm antibodies and/or APA, without fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for SLE or APS [4, 5, 6].

Hence, the therapeutic sequences for SLE‐ and APS‐associated ITP are usually extrapolated from those of primary ITP, including corticosteroids (CSs) with or without intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) as first‐line treatment, often combined with hydroxychloroquine, the cornerstone of SLE treatment [5, 7]. Second‐line therapies include rituximab and immunosuppressive agents, but splenectomy should be avoided [5].

Thrombopoietin receptor agonists (TPO‐RAs) have emerged over the last decade as a major second‐line therapeutic option for ITP, with efficacy in more than 70% of patients [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. However, to date, data are few and are conflicting on the efficacy and safety of TPO‐RAs in SLE‐ and/or APS‐associated ITP, especially on the risk of venous and arterial thrombotic events (TEs). In a previous retrospective study, we reported a 90% efficacy rate of TPO‐RAs as second‐line therapy in 18 patients with SLE‐associated ITP but also a significant rate of 28% serious adverse effects, including four TEs, mostly in SLE patients with secondary APS [17]. These findings agree with data from other series [15] but contradict those from recent reports, including a French series reporting only one TE among 15 patients with SLE‐associated ITP treated with TPO‐RAs [18].

In the present study, we assessed the safety and efficacy of TPO‐RAs in a larger retrospective cohort of ITP patients, with a longer follow‐up. Patients had (1) ITP associated with definite or incomplete SLE and no APAs; (2) ITP associated with definite or incomplete SLE and positivity for at least one APA, with no history of arterial or venous TEs; or (3) ITP associated with a definite primary or secondary APS. Incomplete SLE was defined by the presence of lupus antibodies (i.e., ANA titers ≥ 1/160 or anti‐dsDNA or anti‐Sm antibodies) without clinical SLE manifestations and/or organ involvement. We evaluated the rate and risk factors of TE. In the case of TEs, we analyzed the management of thrombosis and the benefit/risk ratio of stopping TPO‐RAs or continuing them in combination with antiplatelet agents and/or anticoagulants.

Methods

Patient Selection

This study was a multicenter retrospective cohort involving centers in France affiliated with the French national reference center network for adult ITP. Patients were selected from the prospective CARMEN‐France registry (Cytopénies Auto‐immunes Registre Midi‐Pyrénées) [19]. We called for participation from all teams within the CARMEN network. Only seven teams involved in the CARMEN‐France registry that agreed to provide an exhaustive list of all patients meeting the inclusion criteria participated in the study, in order to avoid any selection bias, particularly the inclusion of patients with severe adverse events that may have sparked the clinician's memory. Older cases, observed in the centers not involved in the CARMEN‐France registry but participating in the referral center network, including previous reports, were also included [18]. The study involved adults receiving TPO‐RAs from 2012 to 2024. Data were collected by the same investigator (CM) who used a standardized form and were reviewed by the senior author (B.G.) if needed to ensure appropriate classification of ITP.

Study Population

Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, ITP defined as platelet count < 100 × 109/L according to the international consensus [2] without any other obvious cause, and one of the following criteria at the time of ITP diagnosis and before TPO‐RA treatment:

ITP associated with definite SLE or lupus antibodies and no APAs. SLE was defined according to the 2019 ACR/EULAR or 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics criteria [20, 21]. Lupus antibodies included ANAs detected on immunofluorescence assay of Hep2 cells at ≥ 1/160 titers alone or anti‐dsDNA or anti‐Sm antibodies. This group was called the “SLE or lupus antibodies without APAs group.”

ITP associated with definite SLE or lupus antibodies and no APAs. SLE was defined according to the 2019 ACR/EULAR or 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics criteria [20, 21]. Lupus antibodies included ANAs detected on immunofluorescence assay of Hep2 cells at ≥ 1/160 titers alone or anti‐dsDNA or anti‐Sm antibodies. This group was called the “SLE or lupus antibodies without APAs group.”

Presence of a marker of APS (i.e., anticardiolipin, anti‐beta‐2‐glycoprotein 1, and/or lupus anticoagulant) measured before the introduction of a TPO‐RA, without definite APS associated with definite SLE or presence of lupus antibodies without definite SLE. This group was called the “SLE or lupus antibodies with APAs group.” The APA diagnostic tests are those routinely used in the clinical practice of the prescribing physician and are based on the various assays available in France.

Presence of a marker of APS (i.e., anticardiolipin, anti‐beta‐2‐glycoprotein 1, and/or lupus anticoagulant) measured before the introduction of a TPO‐RA, without definite APS associated with definite SLE or presence of lupus antibodies without definite SLE. This group was called the “SLE or lupus antibodies with APAs group.” The APA diagnostic tests are those routinely used in the clinical practice of the prescribing physician and are based on the various assays available in France.

ITP associated with definite primary or secondary (associated with SLE) APS according to Sidney or 2023 ACR/EULAR criteria [4, 22]. This group was called the “APS group.”

ITP associated with definite primary or secondary (associated with SLE) APS according to Sidney or 2023 ACR/EULAR criteria [4, 22]. This group was called the “APS group.”

We did not exclude patients with neutropenia and/or autoimmune hemolytic anemia associated with ITP.

Data Collection and Criteria for Treatment Response

Demographic data, medical history, and thrombotic risk factors collected at the time of first TPO‐RA exposure included tobacco use, obesity, diabetes, history of cancer, exposure to combined estrogen‐oral contraceptives, and family history of TE. We also gathered information on prior arterial, venous, and/or obstetric TEs before the initiation of TPO‐RAs, as well as their management. History of severe bleeding was collected using the French bleeding score censored for age [23]. History of TE was identified from primary, related, and associated hospital discharge reports. Criteria for venous TE were a history of proven thrombosis by objective criteria (i.e., ultrasonography for deep vein thrombosis, multidetector computed tomography [CT] or ventilation–perfusion scanning for pulmonary embolism, CT or MRI for splanchnic vein thrombosis). Arterial TEs, such as cerebral infarction and acute myocardial infarction, were confirmed by CT, MRI, or electrocardiography findings. Catastrophic APS (CAPS) was diagnosed as a condition characterized by multiple vascular occlusive events (defined as at least three distinct events), typically affecting small vessels over a short period (≤ 7 days), possibly confirmed by histopathology if available, and the persistence of APAs over 12 weeks.

For treatment response of ITP to TPO‐RAs, we used the standardized international criteria as follows: complete response (CR): platelet count ≥ 100 × 109/L and absence of bleeding; response (R): platelet count ≥ 30 × 109/L and at least a two‐fold increase of the baseline count and absence of bleeding; and non‐response (NR): no platelet count increase ≥ 30 × 109/L with at least a doubling of the baseline count or the need for rescue therapy (IVIgs and/or CSs) [2].

Statistical Analysis

The follow‐up started at first TPO‐RA administration and was censored at death or the end of follow‐up. Some patients had several exposures to TPO‐RAs, including a switch from one TPO‐RA to another and sequential exposures to the same TPO‐RA separated by exposure to other treatments. In that case, we provide the cumulative median exposure to TPO‐RAs. We describe the proportion of patients who achieved R and CR to TPO‐RAs as well as the proportion who experienced one or several TEs during follow‐up overall and by the three subgroups. For all descriptive analyses, categorical variables are described with numbers (percentage) and continuous variables with means (SD) or median (interquartile range or range) according to their distribution.

Ethics

The CARMEN‐France registry (NCT02877706) received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Toulouse University Hospital (no. 27‐0512) and from the French Advisory Committee on Information Processing in Health Research (CCTIRS, no. 12.067), as well as the French National Commission on Informatics and Liberty (CNIL, DE‐2012‐438). The present study has been recorded at Toulouse University Hospital, the sponsor of the registry (https://www.chu‐toulouse.fr/projets‐de‐recherche‐carmen).

The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Characteristics of ITP Patients

We included 80 ITP patients (59 females, 73.8%). Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. In total, 37 (46.3%) were included in the “SLE or lupus antibodies without APAs group”: 29 had definite SLE and 8 had only lupus antibodies but did not fulfill the diagnostic criteria for SLE. Also, 27 (33.8%) patients were included in the “SLE or lupus antibodies with APAs group”: 12 had definite SLE and 15 had only lupus antibodies but did not fulfill the diagnostic criteria for SLE. Finally, 16 (20%) patients were included in the “APS group.” For 12 patients, APS was primary and associated with SLE in 4. All APS patients had a history of venous (n = 12) and/or arterial (n = 7) TE. A total of 28 (35%) patients had a history of TE before treatment with TPO‐RAs, with venous TE in 19 (23.8%) and arterial TE in 10 (12.5%); 41 (63.1%) patients had at least one risk factor of venous and/or arterial thrombosis (Table 1). The presence of thrombotic risk factors was higher in the “APS group” than the “SLE or lupus antibodies without APAs group” and “SLE or lupus antibodies with APAs group” (76% vs. 66.7% and 52%, respectively) because of a higher prevalence of diabetes in the former group (30.8% vs. 11.1% and 12%, respectively).

The median age at ITP diagnosis was 48 years, higher in the “APS group” than the other two groups (64 vs. 43 and 49 years, respectively) (Table 1). The median French bleeding score censored for age at ITP diagnosis was 6 (0–24), with a history of severe bleeding requiring admission to hospital and ITP rescue treatment in 23 (35.4%) patients. Fifteen (18.8%) patients had Evans syndrome defined by the association of ITP with autoimmune neutropenia in six and warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia in nine.

ITP Treatments Before TPO‐RA Initiation

All but one patient received CSs and 60 (75%) received IVIg as first‐line ITP treatment. Beyond these treatments, patients received a median of 1 (0–6) treatment line before TPO‐RAs, including rituximab (n = 37, 46.3%), azathioprine (n = 13, 16.3%), dapsone (n = 13, 16.3%), splenectomy (n = 12, 15%), mycophenolate mofetil (n = 8, 10%), vinca alkaloids (n = 6, 7.5%), methotrexate (n = 5, 6.3%), ciclosporin (n = 4, 5%), cyclophosphamide (n = 4, 5%), and belimumab (n = 1, 1.3%).

Outcomes

Efficacy of TPO‐RAs

A total of 39 patients (48.8%) received eltrombopag, 14 (17.5%) romiplostim, and 27 (33.8%) both TPO‐RAs sequentially (Table 2). The median time from TPO‐RA initiation to last follow‐up was 1315 days (28–5576) for the whole cohort and longer in the “SLE or lupus antibodies without APAs group” (n = 1358 days) and “SLE or lupus antibodies with APAs group” (n = 1349 days) than the “APS group” (n = 774 days). In all, 49 (61.3%) patients achieved CR, 14 (17.5%) achieved R, and 17 (21.3%) did not show response after the first TPO‐RA administration. After a TPO‐RA switch, 16 (59.3%) patients achieved CR, 2 (7.4%) achieved R, and 9 (33.3%) did not show response.

Incidence of TEs During TPO‐RAs Exposure

We followed 63 (78.8%) patients without any TEs for a median TPO‐RA exposure of 1259 days (112–1286). Twenty‐one TEs occurred in 17 (21.3%) patients after a median TPO‐RA exposure of 108 days (7–4380) (Table 3). The median exposure was 122 days (18–1095) for eltrombopag (n = 9) and 94 days (87–4380) for romiplostim (n = 12). Among these 17 patients, 14 presented only one TE, 2 patients presented 2 TEs, and 1 patient presented 3 TEs during TPO‐RA exposure. Two patients were receiving antiplatelet treatment, five were on anticoagulant treatment (low molecular weight heparin, n = 1; vitamin K antagonist, n = 1; and direct oral anticoagulant, n = 2), and 10 were not receiving any treatment at first TE onset.

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective study conducted within the French adult ITP network, we report the main characteristics, management, and outcomes of 80 patients with ITP secondary to SLE and/or APS or associated with strong markers of autoimmunity with no overt SLE. This cohort represents a substantial fraction of adults with ITP in clinical practice. TPO‐RAs were efficacious in up to 75% of patients without a durable response after CSs, IVIg, rituximab, and/or standard immunosuppressant treatments. The response rates were slightly higher with romiplostim than eltrombopag, but the number of patients was not large enough to draw any conclusions. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the efficacy of TPO‐RAs in primary ITP and ITP secondary to other autoimmune diseases [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. The major finding from our study is the high rate of TEs, including 18 life‐threatening events in 17 patients, leading to death in three patients.

The incidence of TE in primary ITP treated with TPO‐RA is well known, and a comprehensive review of the literature by Rodeghiero et al. [24] reported an incidence of 4% to 6% in patients receiving eltrombopag or romiplostim. Only limited data are available for secondary ITP treated with TPO‐RA. With regard to ITP secondary to autoimmune diseases other than SLE and APS, such as mixed connective tissue disease, systemic scleroderma, or primary Sjögren's syndrome, only isolated cases have been published, probably because of the rarity of cases, and assessing the risk of TE associated with the use of TPO‐RA in this context is impossible. For ITP associated with common variable immunodeficiency, our group reported 10.7% TE (one myocardial infarction and two venous thromboses) in 28 patients receiving TPO‐RA [25]. A lower incidence of TE has been reported in patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia and ITP treated with eltrombopag, with only one TE in 18 and 24 patients, respectively [26, 27]. However, comparing these data with ours is difficult because of their retrospective nature, the small number of patients included, and the sometimes limited follow‐up. In our study, only three patients (8.1%) in the “SLE or lupus antibodies without APAs group” experienced TEs, despite a high rate of thrombotic risk factors (66.7%), a previous history of thrombosis for 8 (21.6%), and a long follow‐up of 44 months. This percentage is similar to the incidence of TE reported in primary ITP or connective tissue disease‐related thrombocytopenia treated with TPO‐RAs [28]. Also, in two patients, TPO‐RAs were maintained for several months after a TE without recurrence, but patients received anticoagulation for only 6 months. These data are reassuring regarding the safety of TPO‐RA use in ITP‐associated SLE in the absence of APAs.

In contrast, TEs occurred in almost one quarter of the “SLE or lupus antibodies with APAs group” and half of the “APS group,” including five with no history of thrombosis. Notably, all but two patients who experienced a TE had at least one APA, and 12 of the 17 patients had a lupus anticoagulant. Ten patients (58.9%) had a history of TE before TPO‐RA treatment, a major risk factor for TE recurrence for patients receiving TPO‐RAs [29]. TEs were not preceded by thrombocytosis except in two patients, so the TEs induced by TPO‐RAs were not related to thrombocytosis in most cases. Clinicians must remain vigilant and not be reassured about the risk of TEs even in the absence of marked thrombocytosis. Also, the median age of patients with TEs was 66 years, and most had one or several risk factors for TEs. We identified an increased risk of arterial thrombosis in patients with ITP‐APS and an increased risk of venous thrombosis in patients with ITP‐SLE. However, given the small sample size, these results should be interpreted with caution.

TEs were severe, including cerebral venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and three cases of CAPS. As reported in the literature, episodes of CAPS mainly occur a few weeks after the introduction of TPO‐RAs in patients with pre‐existing APS, which strongly suggests a non‐fortuitous association. These three new cases, together with those already reported, strongly argue against the use of TPO‐RAs in patients with APS‐associated ITP [17, 30, 31, 32].

Notably, one quarter of the patients who experienced a first TE while on TPO‐RAs eventually had a second or even a third TE if the treatment with TPO‐RAs was maintained, and the TE was fatal in one patient. Two of the three TE relapses occurred in patients in whom TPO‐RAs were associated with only aspirin. In contrast, only one TE relapse occurred in the eight patients with APAs in whom TPO‐RAs were maintained in association with an anticoagulant after a first TE. TPO‐RAs cannot always be stopped when the patient has multirefractory ITP and a history of serious bleeding, so the decision to stop TPO‐RAs must be based on a careful and individualized evaluation of the bleeding and thrombotic risk. Our results suggest that maintenance of TPO‐RA combined with an anticoagulant may be an option. In addition, physicians preferred to continue treatment with TPO‐RAs after a TE while proposing splenectomy, although all but one patient did not undergo splenectomy. Splenectomy is associated with risk of thrombosis, which probably explains why clinicians prefer to use TPO‐RAs [33, 34]. This situation has led experts to favor TPO‐RAs rather than splenectomy for thrombocytopenia in lupus in updated SLE treatment guidelines, even though one study found no increased risk of thrombosis in patients with APS and splenectomy [5, 35]. The development of new therapeutic approaches such as fostamatinib or Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitors, with no increased risk of thrombotic events reported to date, could be an interesting approach in this setting [36, 37].

The retrospective nature of our study and the small number of patients included in each group are the main limitations of our study. These limitations did not allow for a multivariate analysis to measure the respective weight of APA positivity, thrombotic history, age, and thrombosis risk factors to predict the risk of TE occurrence with TPO‐R. However, severe ITP associated with lupus or APS is rare, as evidenced by the lack of large series reported in the literature. Also, a prospective controlled study comparing TPO‐RAs with other therapeutic strategies, such as fostamatinib, for example, will likely never be performed. Pooled data from large international ITP registries could be helpful to confirm our findings. The prolonged follow‐up of our study, the study of the exposure to anticoagulants in managing thrombosis, and thrombosis co‐factors had never been taken into account in the few previous studies published on this topic and are a strong point of our study. The retrospective nature of this study carries an inherent risk of potential selection bias. Given that our work focuses on TEs, clinicians may have been more likely to report cases involving thrombosis. To mitigate this risk, we adopted a stringent method by explicitly requesting that all participating teams provide comprehensive data on all patients meeting the inclusion criteria. All seven CARMEN teams submitted exhaustive datasets for their eligible patients, and we deliberately excluded isolated cases of thrombosis when complete patient data from a center could not be provided.

In conclusion, our findings provide some reassuring data for the use of TPO‐RAs in SLE‐associated ITP or ITP patients with lupus‐related biology but no APAs, with a favorable benefit/risk ratio. However, this ratio should be carefully weighed in the presence of thrombotic risk factors and APA positivity, and the negative risk of TPO‐RAs use is high in patients with definite APS. When a TE occurs during TPO‐RA treatment, TPO‐RA continuation combined with anticoagulation therapy seems safe, but the risk of TE relapse persists over time, and alternative treatments for ITP may be considered.
