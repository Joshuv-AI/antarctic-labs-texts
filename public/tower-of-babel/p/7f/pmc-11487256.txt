Acute exposure to LPS induces cardiac dysfunction via the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome
Authors: Tshiamo T Maluleke, Ashmeetha Manilall, Nandi Shezi, Sooraj Baijnath, Aletta M.E. Millen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Systemic inflammation contributes to left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, however the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in LV dysfunction in acute inflammatory conditions is unclear. This study investigated the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in acute (24 h) cardiac structural and functional changes in vivo and in vitro in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammation. LPS-treated Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats showed increased LPS metabolite abundance in their LVs as measured by atmospheric pressure matrix-assisted laser desorption ionisation (AP-MALDI) mass spectrometry imaging (MSI). Echocardiography and histology showed that in LPS-exposed rats, LV internal diameter was decreased, with evidence of macrophage infiltration and oedema. However, there were no changes in LV wall thickness or collagen volume. Additionally, LPS-exposed rats exhibited impaired LV relaxation, potentially contributing to decreased stroke volume. While global systolic function was preserved, LPS exposure in SD rats resulted in impaired myocardial deformation assessed by speckle-tracking echocardiography. Exposure to LPS resulted in upregulation of the expression of components of the NLRP3 inflammasome in rodents. In vitro LPS exposure resulted in increased gene expression of NLRP3 and downstream cytokines IL-1β and IL-18, antioxidant SOD2, and elevated markers of pyroptosis (GSDMD) which were inhibited by treatment with a NLRP3 antagonist. However, LPS-induced increases in the gene expression of apoptosic markers (BAX/Bcl2) were not impacted by NLRP3 antagonism. These findings suggest that inflammation induced adverse cardiac structural and functional changes is, at least in part, mediated by the NLRP3 inflammasome in acute, high-grade inflammatory states. Introduction

Inflammation is an important underlying mechanism in the pathophysiology of several disorders, including cardiovascular diseases1. Inflammation in the human body can manifest acutely or chronically. Acute inflammation is typically a rapid and short-lived response to harmful stimuli such as infections or tissue injuries, whereas chronic inflammation is a prolonged and dysregulated immune response that can persist for extended periods. It is well accepted that chronic systemic inflammation contributes to cardiac remodelling and dysfunction2. These inflammation-induced cardiac changes comprise multiple complex molecular and cellular interactions that precipitate adverse cardiac remodelling and functional impairment3. Indeed, patients with diseases characterised by chronic systemic inflammation have a higher risk for adverse cardiac remodelling and left ventricular (LV) dysfunction4.

Despite the well-known risk of chronic inflammation in cardiac dysfunction, when the immune response is dysregulated in acute high-grade inflammatory states, it may contribute to cardiovascular damage5. Indeed, in patients with acute infections, substantial evidence shows the development of myocardial injury and LV dysfunction6,7, which is likely mediated by pro-inflammatory cytokines5,8,9. Investigations into the mechanisms whereby inflammation contributes to cardiac dysfunction have primarily concentrated on individual cytokines. However, the complex aetiology of cardiac disorders suggests a more coordinated response of a host of pleiotropic cytokines modulating myocardial function. Therefore, a more nuanced understanding of these complex cytokine interactions is needed to improve our insight of acute inflammation in cardiac pathophysiology.

Evidence has suggested the involvement of multiprotein complexes known as inflammasomes in coordinating the cellular inflammatory reaction10, and are central to the innate immune response11. Of particular interest in cardiac dysfunction is the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome12. The NLRP3 inflammasome comprises a sensor molecule (NLRP3), which is responsible for detecting various pathogenic and danger signals, an adaptor protein (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain, ASC) which maintains signalling of the NLRP3 inflammasome and an effector protein (Caspase-1, Cas1) which is responsible for the maturation and release of potent pro-inflammatory cytokines including interleukin (IL)-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18 11,13. The NLRP3 inflammasome principally functions by detecting and reacting to danger signals, including pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), indicative of infection, cellular stress, or tissue damage11. The NLRP3 inflammasome has been implicated in the pathogenesis of several chronic diseases, such as multiple sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, metabolic disorders, and atherosclerosis10. However, the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in acute cardiac dysfunction precipitated by high-grade inflammation remains inadequately understood. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in LPS-induced acute inflammation in vivo and in vitro and its role in acute cardiac dysfunction.

Results

Validation of LPS-induced inflammation in the in vivo model

Following LPS administration, animals exhibited pronounced sickness behaviour, as evidenced by elevated sickness behaviour scores compared to controls (Median (IQR): control: 1.00 (1–1), LPS: 16 (16–17), P = 0.002). The LPS rats showed increased serum IL-1β and IL-6 concentrations compared to control rats (P = 0.02, P = 0.03, respectively). There were no differences in body mass (control: 571.40 ± 36.17 g, LPS: 561 ± 38.89 g, P = 0.63), systolic (control: 120 ± 1 mmHg, LPS: 112 ± 2 mmHg, P = 0.84) or diastolic blood pressure (control: 79 ± 2 mmHg, LPS: 79 ± 2 mmHg, P = 0.87 between the groups (Supplementary Table 1).

LPS induces cardiac structural and functional changes in vivo

Geometric and echocardiographic changes

Table 1 shows the LV geometry, systolic and diastolic function measures in control and LPS rats. Heart mass indexed to body mass in the LPS group was significantly higher compared to the control group (P = 0.002). The internal diameter of the LV chamber during both diastole (P = 0.04) and systole (P = 0.04) were significantly smaller in LPS rats compared to controls. The relative wall thickness was increased in the LPS rats compared to controls (P = 0.04).

These geometric changes were confirmed by a decreased stroke volume (control: 0.77 ± 0.14 ml, P = 0.05) and impaired longitudinal strain in LPS-treated rats compared to controls (control: -16.26 ± 0.89 %, LPS: -10.56 ± 3.20 %, P = 0.002; Table 1). However, there were no differences in other measures of systolic function, including ejection fraction, endocardial and midwall fractional shortening and (all P > 0.05). Measures of LV diastolic function, including mitral inflow velocity (E) (P = 0.001), lateral mitral annular velocity (lateral e’) (P = 0.002), and e’/a’ ratio (P = 0.02) were significantly decreased in LPS-treated rats compared to controls. There were no differences in the E/e’ ratio between the groups (P > 0.05).

LPS-induces NLRP3 inflammasome upregulation in vitro and in vivo

NLRP3 mRNA expression was higher in LV tissues in LPS-treated rats versus controls (control: 1.05 ± 0.37, LPS: 1.67 ± 0.39, P = 0.03, Fig. 4A). There were no differences in mRNA expression of ASC between the groups (control: 0.87 ± 0.33, LPS: 1.24 ± 0.75, P = 0.25, Fig. 4B). Although there was a difference in Cas1 (Fig. 4C) between the control and LPS rat groups, it did not reach statistical significance (control: 1.08 ± 0.44, LPS: 1.65 ± 0.47, P = 0.06, Fig. 4C). In AC16 cells, the mRNA expression of NLRP3 (control: 1.02 ± 0.23, LPS: 1.82 ± 0.61 P = 0.01; Fig. 4D) and Cas1 (control: 1.02 ± 0.21, LPS: 1.79 ± 0.23, P < 0.001; Fig. 4F), were significantly increased in LPS-treated cells compared to controls. There were no differences in the mRNA expression of ASC (control: 1.01 ± 0.14, LPS: 0.90 ± 0.16, P = 0.25; Fig. 4E) between the control and LPS-treated cells. These results suggest that the NLRP3 inflammasome is activated in response to LPS administration.

This NLRP3 inflammasome activation resulted in increased mRNA expression of downstream cytokines in both the animal and cell models. In the rodent model, IL-1β (control: 1.06 ± 0.11, LPS: 10.83 ± 2.81; P = 0.01; Fig. 5A) and IL-18 (control: 0.72 ± 0.09, LPS: 2.06 ± 2.31, P = 0.01; Fig. 5B) mRNA expressions were significantly increased in LV tissues in the LPS group compared to controls. Similarly, in LPS-treated cells, IL-1β (control: 1.03 ± 0.28, LPS: 2.16 ± 0.68, P = 0.004; Fig. 5D), and IL-18 (control: 1.02 ± 0.21, LPS: 2.60 ± 0.31, P < 0.0001; Fig. 5E) mRNA expressions were increased relative to control cells. Exposure to LPS resulted in an upregulation of SOD2 mRNA expression in AC16 cells (control: 1.01 ± 0.14, LPS: 3.04 ± 0.85, P = 0.0002; Fig. 5F) and tended towards significance in the rodent model (control: 1.01 ± 0.10, LPS: 1.19 ± 0.23, P = 0.13; Fig. 5C).

LPS-induced upregulation of gene expression markers of cell death in vitro and in vivo

LPS exposure resulted in a significant increase in GSDMD mRNA expression, a gene coding for a marker of pyroptosis, in LPS-treated rodents compared to controls (control: 1.10 ± 0.61, LPS: 1.63 ± 0.72, P = 0.03, Fig. 6A). Although GSDMD mRNA expression was increased in AC16 cells exposed to LPS compared to control, it did not reach statistical significance (control: 1.00 ± 0.1, LPS: 1.16 ± 0.29, P = 0.24; Fig. 6C). LPS exposure resulted in an increased BAX/Bcl-2 ratio in both the rodent model (control: 1.34 ± 1.23, LPS: 2.90 ± 0.98, P = 0.04, Fig. 6B) and AC16 cells (control: 1.01 ± 0.06, LPS: 2.89 ± 0.98, P = 0.04, Fig. 6D), suggesting LPS-induced pro-apoptotic state in both the in vitro and in vivo models.

In vitro antagonism of NLRP3 inflammasome reduces markers of inflammation and pyroptosis but not apoptosis

When co-treating AC16 cells with LPS and Dimethyl Fumarate (DMF), a known antagonist of the NLRP3 inflammasome, the mRNA expression of NLRP3 (LPS: 8.89 ± 5.54, LPS + DMF: 1.76 ± 0.52, P = 0.03, Fig. 7A), Cas1 (LPS: 2.03 ± 4.00, LPS + DMF: 0.18 ± 0.09, P < 0.001, Fig. 7B), IL-1β (LPS: 10.71 ± 0.29, LPS + DMF: 0.79 ± 0.50, P = 0.002, Fig. 7C), IL-18 (LPS: 2.53 ± 0.62, LPS + DMF: 0.62 ± 0.14, P = 0.008, Fig. 7D), SOD2 (LPS: 4.17 ± 1.45, LPS + DMF: 1.09 ± 0.10, P = 0.005, Fig. 7E) and GSDMD (LPS: LPS: 1.7 ± 1.54, LPS + DMF: 0.49 ± 0.27, P < 0.001, Fig. 7F) were significantly decreased compared to LPS-treated cells and no different to controls (all p > 0.05). Compared to controls, LPS and DMF co-treatment also resulted in a lower expression of Cas1 (control: 1.00 ± 0.06, P = 0.0003, Fig. 7B) and GSDMD (control: 1.03 ± 0.25, P = 0.02, Fig. 7F) mRNA expression. However, co-treatment with LPS and DMF did not prevent the LPS-induced increase in BAX/Bcl-2 (LPS: 1.70 ± 0.06, LPS + DMF: 0.49 ± 0.27, P = 0.85, Fig. 7G).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that LPS induced a state of high-grade systemic inflammation in rats. The presence of LPS-associated metabolites in cardiac tissue were confirmed using MSI, which were accompanied by macrophage infiltration. We showed significant adverse changes in cardiac geometry and impairments in LV systolic and diastolic function. LPS exposure resulted in an increased mRNA expression of components of the NLRP3 inflammasome, accompanied by increased downstream expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 both in vivo and in vitro. These changes were concomitant with increased SOD2 mRNA expression, an antioxidant marker and an increase in GSDMD, a marker of pyroptosis. Antagonism of the NLRP3 inflammasome with DMF co-treated with LPS prevented NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis but not apoptosis. These results suggest that the downstream cardiac and molecular changes are a consequence of LPS-induced inflammation. Interestingly, these cardiac functional changes were not explained by fibrotic remodelling but are more likely attributed to LV oedema. Furthermore, the LPS-induced activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, at least in part, mediate LV dysfunction in acute, high-grade systemic inflammation.

The administration of LPS induced a state of systemic inflammation as evidenced by increased circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines concentrations of IL-6 and IL-1β. It is well known that LPS administration induces a state of systemic inflammation15. However, for the first time using AP-MALDI-MSI, we showed the presence of LPS-associated metabolites in LV tissue. This demonstrated that systemic LPS administration results in tissue localisation of LPS. The MS analysis of LPS is an analytical challenge based on the complexity and diversity of the molecule. Therefore, it is difficult to measure the molecule in its native form. However, a previous study has characterised the liver metabolites of LPS-treated animals14. Using AP-MALDI-MSI, we were able to successfully image these LPS-associated metabolites, hexadecenoic acid and hexacosanediote, in LV tissue. Moreover, we also showed increased mRNA expression of proinflammatory cytokines (IL1β and IL18) in LV tissues, which were accompanied by adverse cardiac geometry and impaired LV function. This suggests that the presence of LPS-associated metabolites initiate the subsequent inflammation-induced changes in cardiac structure and function.

Indeed, we showed adverse structural and functional changes in the LV of LPS-treated rats. We demonstrated increased heart weight indexed to body weight and relative wall thickness, suggesting a concentric remodelling pattern. However, this increased relative wall thickness was driven by a decrease in LV end-diastolic diameter and not by an increase in the septal or posterior wall thickness in diastole. In contrast, other studies using similar systemic inflammatory models have shown eccentric LV remodelling, characterised by thinning of the LV wall and dilation of the LV chamber16,17. It has been suggested that this eccentric pattern of remodelling is driven by cardiomyocyte lengthening and apoptosis18,19. In the present study, we performed histology to better understand the mechanism of the concentric remodelling pattern. We showed no changes in collagen volume in LPS-treated rats. Similarly, other studies using LPS rodent models with different histological techniques reported no changes in markers of fibrotic remodelling. The aforementioned studies showed disruption of myocardial fibres and immune cell infiltration20–22. Indeed, in the present study we also showed significantly greater macrophage infiltration and the presence of oedema in LPS rats compared to controls. Concomitant with the presence of LPS-associated metabolites in LV tissue, our findings indicate a localised tissue immune response in acute, high-grade inflammation. In high-grade inflammatory conditions such as sepsis, the infiltration of immune cells in tissues increases myocardial microvascular permeability, leading to fluid leakage into the interstitial space, resulting in local oedema23. Indeed, oedema has been previously reported in similar models of LPS-induced inflammation22,24. Taken together, the geometric changes observed in the present study are likely a result of fluid shifts in the heart due to immune infiltration and subsequent LV oedema rather than fibrotic remodelling, which was coupled with the presence of LPS-associated metabolites in LV tissue as shown by AP-MALDI MSI.

The stiffening of the LV due to oedema can impair the relaxation and filling capacity of the LV during diastole. In the present study oedema appears to have compromised the efficacy of LV relaxation as indexed by several measures of LV diastolic function, including mitral inflow velocity E, lateral e’, a measure of impaired LV relaxation, and decreased e’/a’ ratio, a marker of LV stiffness. Our findings corroborate previous LPS rodent studies where impaired LV diastolic function has been consistently reported17,24. Furthermore, we showed a reduced stroke volume, whilst measures of contractility and pump function, including EF and FS, were preserved. These results further confirm the impaired filling capacity of the LV. Indeed, previous studies have also shown decreases in stroke volume with acute, high-grade inflammation17,25,26. In contrast, others have shown impaired pump function and contractility in similar models16,17,27. However, the impairements in systolic function in these aforementioned studies were largely driven by eccentric remodelling16,17. Hence, in the present study, the preserved systolic function may be a result of the preserved wall structure. Nevertheless, we have shown subclinical impairment in LV systolic function, as indexed by longitudinal strain. LV strain measures the deformation and change in shape of the LV as it contracts to eject blood during systole28. Similar to our findings, LPS injections in rabbits demonstrated impairments in global longitudinal strain, even in cases where global measures of systolic function, such as EF, were preserved29. Taken together, these results show that acute, high-grade inflammation contributes to the development of cardiac remodelling and LV dysfunction. This requires a coordinated immune response. It is well known that in chronic inflammation-induced cardiac dysfunction, the NLRP3 inflammasome is implicated.

In healthy cardiomyocytes, the expression of components of the NLRP3 inflammasome are insufficient for the oligomerisation of the NLRP3 complex30. However, this changes in the presence of PAMP signals, such as LPS. When LPS binds to the Toll-like Receptor 4 (TLR4) on the cell surface, via NFκB signalling, it triggers the transcription of NLRP3 complex components, consisting of NLRP3, ASC and the inactive pro-CAS1 31. Once critical expression of these NLRP3 components is reached, a secondary activation signal is usually required to promote the complex formation. Several of these activation signals have been proposed, including ATP, K+ efflux, mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress32,33. Despite this, several previous in vivo34,35 and in vitro32,36 studies using LPS alone has been shown to sufficiently trigger the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome without the need for a secondary activation signal. Similarly, in the present study, in response to LPS administration, we demonstrated increased NLRP3 mRNA expression in rat LV tissue and NLRP3 and Cas1 mRNA expression in AC16 cardiomyocytes. Furthermore, consistent with mitochondrial dysfunction in the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, we showed an increased expression of SOD2, an essential cytoprotective enzyme that neutralises superoxide radicals37. We demonstrated that LPS induced high-grade inflammation, which is a known trigger for producing ROS in the heart, which contributes to oxidative stress2. These results are similar to previous reports of elevated levels of oxidative stress markers, including SOD, nitric oxide, and inducible nitric oxide synthase21. SOD2 is a marker of mitochondrial health since previous studies have reported that elevated levels of SOD2 are associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and increased ROS38. ROS production can directly activate the NLRP3 inflammasome by promoting the inflammasome complex assembly39, which promotes the oligomerisation and triggers the autocatalytic cleavage of pro-Cas1 into its active form, Cas1, thereby completing the inflammasome activation10.

When the NLRP3 inflammasome has assembled and activated, the activated Cas1 acts as the functional component of the NLRP3 inflammasome and cleaves immature pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18, thereby converting them to their active forms, IL-1β and IL-1810. In the present study, we show increased mRNA expression of the genes coding for the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18, indicating oligomerisation and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Previous studies have also demonstrated that LPS increased the gene and protein expression of IL-1β and IL-18 in vivo17,27,40 and invitro41,42. Similarly, increased NLRP3-induced upregulation of IL-1β and IL-18 have been reported in other high-grade inflammatory animal models26,43. The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome has several cellular consequences. The most well-accepted cellular consequence of NLRP3 inflammasome activation is pyroptosis, an inflammasome-dependent lytic cell death distinct from apoptosis31. Our data suggests that NLRP3 activation induced pyroptosis in vitro, as evidenced by increased mRNA expression of GSDMD. Gasdermin-D (GSDMD), an important mediator of pyroptosis, is activated by Cas-1 downstream of the NLRP3 inflammasome33,44,45. GSDMD oligomerises and binds to the plasma membrane upon activation, forming pores that facilitate cytosolic swelling and eventual rupture of the cell, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines into the extracellular space33. Similarly, previous studies have shown the upregulation of pyroptosis markers in high-grade inflammatory models46 and rodent cardiomyocytes40. The pyroptosis-mediated release of cellular contents, including pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18, aligns with the presence of oedema observed in the LPS-treated animals in the present study. The pyroptosis-mediated release of cardiac cellular content may have caused a fluid shift in LV tissues, thereby contributing to the development of oedema and impaired LV relaxation during diastole. Indeed, pyroptosis-mediated fluid shifts have been implicated in the development of oedema in the brain47. Moreover, increased IL-1β and IL-18 expression have been identified as contributors to impaired LV relaxation25,48. Therefore, pyroptosis-induced release of IL-1β and IL-18 may have contributed to impaired LV relaxation in the LPS groups in the present study. In addition to pyroptosis, our results showed that LPS exposure in rats and AC16 cells increased BAX/Bcl-2 ratio, indicating a pro-apoptotic state. An increased BAX/Bcl-2 ratio is a critical indicator of the balance between the pro-apoptotic (BAX) and anti-apoptotic (Bcl2) signals within a cell. BAX mediates apoptosis by translocating to the mitochondria, causing cytochrome c release and subsequent caspase cascade activation, ultimately culminating in apoptotic cell death and by stimulating the release of oxidised mitochondrial DNA and potassium efflux49,50. Bcl-2 inhibits the apoptotic pathway by binding to BAX and preventing its activation51. Increases in the BAX/Bcl-2 ratio indicates a cell’s susceptibility to apoptosis, and when the ratio is greater than 1, the pro-apoptotic state is apparent52.

We have demonstrated dysregulation of several molecular markers associated with acute high-grade inflammation and cell damage. To elucidate the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in the changes of these markers, we treated AC16 cardiomyocytes with DMF. DMF has been shown to antagonise activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome via Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)53, a transcription factor that modulates the expression of antioxidant and cytoprotective genes by binding to the Antioxidant Response Element (ARE) in the promoter sequences of these genes54,55. These genes transcribe proteins which play a critical role in scavenging ROS and reducing ROS-induced NLRP3 transcription56. In this regard, our findings demonstrate the activation of these antioxidant pathways by DMF, as shown by the decreased mRNA expression of SOD2 in the presence of LPS-induced inflammation. Our findings indicate that DMF prevents ROS-mediated activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by activation of the Nrf2 pathway. Indeed, we showed that DMF attenuated the gene expression of both NLRP3 and Cas1 in AC16 cardiomyocytes. Furthermore, inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome by DMF prevented the upregulation of IL-1β, IL-18 and GSDMD mRNA expression, and hence pyroptosis.

Interestingly, antagonism of the NLRP3 inflammasome did not prevent the LPS-induced increase in BAX/Bcl-2 ratio. These results suggest that NLRP3 inflammasome activation does not mediate apoptosis in acute, high-grade inflammatory conditions16,27. Several pathways, independent of the NLRP3 inflammasome, has been shown to regulate apoptosis. In this regard, the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis, where BAX and Bcl-2 exert their effects, can be triggered by several stimuli, including cellular stress, radiation and DNA damage57. Indeed in LPS models, DNA damage, as evidenced by elevated P53 expression, has been shown to activate the intrinsic pathway, which leads to apoptosis-mediated cardiac dysfunction50,58. Taken together, our results from in vitro antagonism of the NLRP3 inflammasome suggest that while the inflammasome mediates pyroptosis, apoptosis occur independently.

In the present study, the contribution of the NLRP3 inflammasome activation to LPS-induced cardiac dysfunction may have been strengthened by including protein expressions. However, gene expression analysis provides important insights into the transcriptional changes at a cellular level. These changes were validated in both in vitro and in vivo models. In addition, previous studies have a strong correlation between mRNA expression and protein expression of the NLRP3 inflammasome components25,27,40. To better confirm the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome, future studies may consider the measurement of potassium efflux and Cas1 activity. Although NLRP3 inflammasome antagonism has provided insight into the role of the inflammasome in the in vitro model, the inclusion of a DMF treatment group in the in vivo study may have further strengthened our findings and should be considered for future studies. Furthermore, while DMF is not a direct inhibitor of the NLRP3 inflammasome, several previous studies have shown, similar to our results, its efficacy in modulating the expression of the NLRP3 inflammasome and its downstream markers of inflammation and pyroptosis. Future studies could explore the molecular consequences of a more selective inhibitor of the NLRP3 inflammasome. The high mortality of the LPS-treated rats highlights the destructive effects of LPS on cardiac function, which may have impacted the statistical power of this study.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that acute systemic inflammation induced by LPS resulted in significant adverse effects in cardiac geometry and impairments in LV systolic and diastolic function. These changes were accompanied by the presence of LPS-associated metabolites and tissue macrophage infiltration in the LV tissues of LPS-treated rats. We further showed that LPS exposure resulted in the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome components, increased inflammatory cytokines and downstream markers of pyroptosis, which were prevented by antagonism with DMF. Lastly, we showed that cellular damage via apoptosis and pyroptosis are differentially regulated in acute high-grade inflammation. Future studies should determine the individual contributions of these pathways to cardiac dysfunction in acute inflammation.

Methods

Study Design

In vivo model of acute high-grade inflammation

Animal experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of the University of the Witwatersrand (AREC clearance certificate number: 2021/04/06 C) and in compliance with the South African National Legislation for animal husbandary, welfare, and experimentation using laboratory animals. All in vivo experiments are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Sixteen, three-month-old male Sprague Dawley rats were housed individually in cages in temperature-controlled rooms at the Wits Research Animal Facility (WRAF). Standard rat chow and drinking water were available ad libitium. The rats were habituated to handling conditions and measurements for two weeks. The rats were randomly assigned to either an LPS-injected group (LPS, n = 10) or a saline-injected group (control, n = 6). Rats in the LPS group were injected intraperitoneally with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (10mg.kg− 1), as previously reported22. The control group received intraperitoneal saline (0.1 ml). After the LPS injection, animals were monitored for sickness behaviour. Four animals in the LPS-treated group died after the injection, prior to termination. Body weight and blood pressure were measured as previously reported59. Rats were terminated after 24 h by cardiac puncture; a blood sample was obtained, and the heart was removed and weighed. The free wall of the right ventricle was trimmed from the septum, and the LV was weighed. The LVs were further dissected into three portions, one of which was stored in RNAlater®™ at -80 °C for further analyses. The other portions were stored in formalin for histological analysis frozen and stored at -80 °C for further analysis. Blood samples were centrifuged and stored at -80 °C.

In vitro model of acute high-grade inflammation

In vitro experiments were carried out using commercially available immortalised human adult ventricular cardiomyocyte AC16 cells (Catalogue number 9Q019D; Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). The AC16 cells are derived from the fusion of primary ventricular cells and SV-40-transformed fibroblasts60. AC16 cells were maintained under physiological conditions (37 °C with 5% CO2) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) + Ham’s F-12 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were grown to 80% confluence in 24-well plates (WUXI NEST Biotechnology, China) and then switched to serum-free DMEM + Ham’s F-12 for 24 h before treatments. To induce inflammation, AC16 cells were incubated with 10 µg/ml lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), a component of the cell membrane from Escherichia Coli (LPS group) as previously reported42 (LPS group) or with serum-free media (control group) for 24 h.

To investigate whether LPS-induced changes on the NLRP3 inflammasome can be inhibited, we antagonised the inflammasome using a known NLRP3 inhibitor, Dimethyl Fumarate (DMF)61. AC16 cells were co-treated and incubated with LPS (10 µg/ml) and DMF (10 µg/ml) (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for 24 h.

Assessment of LV structure and function using echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed using a non-invasive, high-resolution (12Mhz) ultrasound probe coupled to an echocardiogram (Affiniti 70 ultrasound systems, Phillips N.V. Koninklijke, Amsterdam, Netherlands) under inhalation anaesthesia (1% isoflurane). Cardiac dimensions, LV systolic and diastolic function and Speckle tracking echocardiography (STE) were measured as previously reported59. Briefly, two-dimensional, directed M-mode echocardiography was used to measure LV dimensions, including the internal diameters at the end of systole (LVESD) and diastole (LVEDD), septal wall thickness at the end of diastole (SEPED), and posterior wall thickness at the end of diastole (PWED)62 and were used to calculate the relative wall thickness (RWT), stroke volume, LV ejection fraction, and LV endocardial and midwall fractional shortening62. LV diastolic function was assessed using pulsed Doppler and tissue Doppler imaging in the apical four-chamber view. Mitral inflow velocity was measured in early (E) and late (A) diastole and expressed as E/A as an index of LV relaxation. Lateral mitral annular tissue lengthening was measured during the early (e’) and late (a’) diastolic period. The ratio of early diastolic mitral inflow velocity to mitral annular tissue lengthening (E/e’) was calculated as an index of filling pressure, and e’/a’ was calculated as an index of LV stiffness.

For STE, B-mode Doppler videos of the heart were captured in the apical four-chamber view to determine the LV longitudinal myocardial deformation (strain and strain rate). B-mode videos were analysed according to convention63. The endocardial and epicardial borders were traced automatically by the vendor’s software. Borders were adjusted manually as necessary to ensure adequate tracking of the endocardial and epicardial borders. The global strain and strain rate were calculated by averaging the six anatomical segments over three consecutive cardiac cycles.

All echocardiographic images and videos were analysed using the TOMTEC IMAGE-COM software (TOMTEC Imaging Systems GmbH, Germany).

Circulating pro-inflammatory markers

IL-1β and IL-6 concentrations in serum were quantified using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Elabscience Biotechnology Co. Ltd, Wuhan, China. The lower detection limit for both IL-1β and IL-6 was 12.5pg/ml. The inter-assay and intra-assay coefficients of variant was below 15% for the IL-1β kit and below 10% for the IL-6 kit.

Determination of tissue distribution of LPS using atmospheric pressure matrix-assisted laser desorption ionisation mass spectrometry imaging (AP-MALDI-MSI)

AP-MALDI-MSI allows for the label-free identification of various bioactive molecules and their spatial distribution in tissues64. In this study, we used AP-MALDI-MSI to determine the spatial distribution of LPS-specific metabolites in LV tissue sections. The heart tissues were sectioned using a Leica CM1860 cryostat microtome (Leica Biosystems, Germany). Fresh-frozen 12 μm sections were prepared, thawed and mounted onto indium-titanium oxide (ITO) glass slides (TechnoStar, Mumbai, India).

Prior to matrix application, the slides were dried in a vacuum desiccator for 5 min. The slides were uniformly coated with DHB matrix (50 mg/ml) in a 50% acetonitrile (1% TFA) solution using a SunCollect MALDI sprayer (SunChrom Scientific Devices, Friedrichsdorf, GmbH). The matrix was applied over the tissue surfacein 3 passes of the matrix solution with the following conditions: flow rate of 100 µl/min; line spacing, 2 mm; z-distance, 25 mm.

Mass spectrometric images of the tissues were obtained using an atmospheric pressure matrix-assisted laser desorption ionisation (AP-MALDI) (MassTech, US) coupled to a Thermo Exactive orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, US). The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ion mode; scan range, 150–200 m/z, mass resolution of 140 000, scan rate of 0.7 scan/sec. The AP-MALDI source was operated using Target Next software (MassTech, USA) to define regions of interest (ROIs) for imaging experiments. All experiments were run at a spatial resolution of 20 μm. LPS-specific metabolites were visualised, and relative intensities were extracted using Mozaic software (SpectroSwiss, Switzerland). In addition, MSI data was converted into .imzml file formats and uploaded to Metaspace.eu for metabolite annotations. The default METASPACE m/z tolerance of ± 3 ppm was used.

Gene expression of molecular markers

mRNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Cultured cells were collected and lysed following treatment incubations. LV tissue samples were homogenised by sonication. Total RNA was extracted using a NucleoSpin® RNA mini kit for RNA purification (Marchery Nagel, Düren, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript IV VILO reverse-transcription cDNA synthesis mastermix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, USA).

Relative gene expression with RT-PCR

Relative gene expression RT-PCR was performed using a StepOne Plus thermocycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, USA) with cDNA used as templates for gene expression analysis of pre-designed TaqMan™ probes for NOD-like receptor (NLR) family genes NLRP3, ASC and Caspase 1 (components of the NLRP3 inflammasome complex), IL-6, IL-1β and IL-18 genes (markers of inflammation), GSDMD (a marker of NLRP3 mediated pyroptosis), SOD2 gene (an anti-oxidant marker), and BAX and Bcl-2 (pro- and anti-apoptotic markers respectively). Changes in mRNA expression levels of the genes were quantified relative to the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAPDH) in the AC16 cells and hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyl transferase (HPRT1) in the animal LV tissues as previously optimised in our lab65. The relative mRNA levels were determined by the 2−∆∆CT method66.

The TaqMan™ assay ID’s of genes used for relative gene expression analysis in rat LV tissue: HPRT1: Rn01527838_g1; NLRP3: Rn04244620_m1; ASC: Rn00597229_g1; Cas1: Rn00562724_m1; IL-1β: Rn00580432_m1; IL-18: Rn01422083_m1; SOD2: Rn00690588_g1; BAX: Rn01480161_g1; Bcl-2: Rn99999125_m1; and GSDMD: Rn06429108_s1.

The TaqMan™ assay ID’s of genes used for relative gene expression analysis in AC16 cells: GAPDH: Hs02786624_g1; NLRP3: Hs00918082_m1; ASC: Hs01547324_g1; Cas1: Hs00354836_m1; IL-1β: Hs01555410_m1; IL-18: Hs01038788_m1; SOD2: Hs00167309_m1; BAX: Hs00180269_m1; Bcl-2: Hs04986394_s1 and GSDMD: Hs00986739_g1.

Assessment of collagen content with histology

Formalin-stored LV tissues were fixed in 10% buffered formalin and routinely processed for paraffin embedding. Tissues were deparaffinised, rehydrated, and cut into 4 μm thick sections. The cut sections were stained with a 0.1% Sirius Red solution dissolved in an aqueous saturated picric acid for 60 min at room temperature. After washing in two changes of acidified water, the slides were dehydrated in 100% ethanol, cleared with xylene and mounted with DPX mounting medium. The tissue sections were analysed using a Zeiss Axiocam ERc5s (Zeiss, Peabody, MA, USA). The tissue sections were viewed under bright-field light and a 10x objective lens (x100 magnification). ImageJ software67 was used to calculate the collage area fraction as a percentage for each tissue section by determining the area of collagen present relative to the total tissue area.

Assessment of immune cell infiltration and cell structure with histology

Due to the presence of LPS in the LV tissue, we performed histological analysis of rat LV to characterise, and provide evidence of the local cardiac inflammatory response. Fresh frozen LV tissues were sectioned to a thickness of 12 μm using a Leica 1900 UV cryomicrotome (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were thaw-mounted onto indium-titanium oxide (ITO) glass slides (TechnoStar, Mumbai, India) and dried in a vacuum desiccator for 5 min prior to staining. The sections were stained with Meyer’s Hematoxylin for one minute and then washed with tap water. The sections were counterstained with Eosin for 45 s before being immersed in 95% ethanol, followed by 100% ethanol. Sections were fixed in 100% xylene and then cover-slipped with DPX mounting media. Slides were allowed to dry for 24 h prior to microscopic analysis using a DVM6 Digital Microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) using the 12.55 mm objective. Total macrophage counts within the cardiac tissues of three control rats and three LPS-treated rats were measured using the Leica LAS X Life Sciences Microscope Software (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) by an independent observer blinded to the experimental design.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., USA). Data are represented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), as appropriate. Differences between the LPS and control groups were determined using an unpaired T-test or a Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the distribution of the data. Results were considered statistically significant if P ≤ 0.05. Graphs depicting data were generated using GraphPad Prism (ver 10.2.2).
