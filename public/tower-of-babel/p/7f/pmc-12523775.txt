Characterization of adeno-associated viral transduction of retinal ganglion cells in adult and old mice
Authors: Delaney CM Henderson, Balwantray C Chauhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors are being used increasingly in developing treatments for age-related ocular diseases, including those impacting retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), the output neurons of the eye. However, the effects of persistent transgene expression on RGC structure and function have not been thoroughly investigated. In this study, viral transduction was studied in parallel cohorts of adult (3–6 month-old) and old (18–24 month-old) mice and the persistence of viral expression characterized in aging mice. Age-related differences in viral transduction were investigated using in vivo imaging and immunohistochemical analyses. Effects of viral transduction on RGC function were examined using calcium imaging in intact-isolated retinal preparations. We demonstrate that AAV expression persists over 18 months following a single viral injection. Decreases in RGC density observed in AAV-injected aged mice were not significantly different from age-matched controls, suggesting age-related RGC loss unrelated to viral injection. Moreover, mice aged to 18 months post-AAV injection and old mice had similar loss of RGC function when compared to adult mice, despite differences in transduction durations. Our study found no deleterious effects of AAV transduction and highlights the potential of AAV-based methods for long-term RGC labeling and therapeutic strategies.Keywords: adeno-associated virus, viral transduction, retinal ganglion cell, age, in vivo imaging, mouse, calcium imaging

Introduction

Gene therapy using adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors is being used increasingly in the treatment of various eye diseases.1,2,3,4,5 AAVs are small, non-enveloped, non-pathogenic viruses that have demonstrated successful transduction in the retina.6,7,8 AAV serotypes have been thoroughly characterized, with AAV serotype 2 (AAV2) being the most routinely used to deliver transgenes to cells within the inner retina, specifically retinal ganglion cells (RGCs). In addition to therapeutics, AAV vectors have been used to transduce genes and proteins in RGCs for investigating cell structure and function. Many investigators have used AAV delivery, via intravitreal injection, to transduce both structural (e.g., green fluorescent protein; GFP)9,10,11,12 and functional markers (e.g., GCaMP)10,13,14,15,16,17,18,19,20,21 to RGCs to perform longitudinal in vivo imaging.

GCaMP is a family of genetically encoded calcium (Ca2+ indicators consisting of a GFP, the M13 fragment of myosin light-chain kinase, and calmodulin. When Ca2+ is present, it causes a conformational change in the calmodulin and M13 components resulting in increased fluorescence.22 GCaMP fluorescence in response to both chemical and optical stimulation has been used in situ13,14,15 and in vivo17,18,19,20,21 to evaluate the structural and functional viability of RGCs. GCaMP variants are being continually developed to improve kinetic properties of Ca2+ binding. For example, GCaMP3, utilized in the Thy1-GCaMP3 transgenic mouse,23 has lower sensitivity and slower kinetics compared to GCaMP6s.24,25 The ability to label RGCs using exogenous functional fluorescent markers such as GCaMP and monitor changes in cell function with normal physiological aging or following experimental injury is critical for understanding pathologic changes at the cellular level.

Further investigation into AAV biology has determined that viral capsid proteins, choice of viral promotor, and method of administration are highly important in achieving efficient transduction in retinal cells.26 To date, the ubiquitous promotor, cytomegalovirus/enhancer chicken β-actin (CAG), has been used routinely for labeling inner retinal cells, and in translational strategies for clinical therapeutics because of high levels of transgene expression.12,27,28 Ubiquitous promoters are also typically smaller in size, making them preferable due to the small packaging capacity of AAV vectors.7,29 Overall, successful gene therapy strategies would require that the viral vector yields long-lasting transgene expression in the specific target cells within the retina and be safe for use in both adult and old individuals. Although age is a risk factor for many ocular diseases, experimental gene therapy research is typically performed in juvenile mice. To our knowledge, effects of persistent transgene expression on RGC structure and function have not been thoroughly investigated, and a deeper understanding of both the persistence of viral expression and viral transduction in the aging retina are critical.

In the present study, we characterized the AAV2-CAG-GCaMP6s viral vector in adult (3 months of age) and old (18 months of age) mice using longitudinal in vivo retinal imaging, intact retina functional Ca2+ imaging, and immunohistochemistry (IHC). We explored transduction rates longitudinally in adult and old mice following intravitreal injection of the viral vector, and the persistence of viral expression in the aging retina. Additionally, we characterized age-related differences in the hit rate and specificity of labeling in adult and old mice. It is expected the results of this study will provide foundational knowledge on AAV gene therapy strategies in experimental models of inner retinal disease and determine if there are deleterious effects of long-term AAV transduction.

Results

In vivo fluorescence and OCT imaging

Fluorescent GCaMP6s-positive cells were observed with in vivo imaging in all experimental groups 1-week post-injection. In adult mice, the density of these labeled cells increased from 150 (57) cells/mm2 at 1 week to 384 (43) cells/mm2 at the final 8-week post-injection time point (data reported as mean [standard deviation]). There was a significant progressive increase in labeled cells over the initial 5 weeks (p < 0.05) which stabilized thereafter (Figure 1). In old mice, the density increased from 177 (26) cells/mm2 at 1 week to 405 (43) cells/mm2 at 8 weeks post-injection, with significant increases over the first 4 weeks (Figure 1). There was no significant difference in density among adult and old mice 8 weeks post-injection (p = 0.34). Aged mice showed similar trends to adult mice over the first 8 weeks (Figure 1), however, in subsequent 6-monthly imaging sessions, there was a gradual decrease of approximately 10% in the last imaging session at 18 months post-injection (p < 0.01; 8 weeks compared to 18 months). Additionally at 18 months post-injection, aged mice had a significantly lower labeled cell density (348 [54] cells/mm2) compared to old mice (405 [43] cells/mm2, p = 0.04).

Outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness and total retinal thickness (TRT) were measured and compared between adult and old mice over the post-injection time course (Figures 2C and 2D). There were significant differences found in ONL (61.5 [2.3] μm in adult mice, vs. 52.2 [1.4] μm in old mice; p < 0.05), and TRT (229.5 [9.4] μm in adult mice, vs. 219.6 [4.3] μm in old mice; p < 0.05) baseline measurements between adult and old mice. Following injection, there were no significant differences in ONL or TRT between baseline and the 8-week post-injection time point in either adult mice (ONL: p = 0.32; TRT: p = 0.73) or old mice (ONL: p = 0.12; TRT: p = 0.78). In old mice, there was a transient increase in TRT at 3-, and 4-weeks post-AAV injection which was similar to the changes observed in GCC thickness at these time points (Figures 2B–2D). This phenomenon was not observed in ONL thickness following AAV-injection.

Calcium imaging

In adult mice, 50 μM kainic acid (KA) generated robust and consistent Ca2+ transients over four KA treatments (average ΔF/F0 = 1.51 [0.32]; Figure 3A). In old and aged mice, the response to KA was less robust (ΔF/F0 = 1.10 [0.30], and 1.06 [0.33], respectively; Figure 3B), however, they were consistent across the four KA treatments (p = 0.72 and p = 0.67 in old and aged mice, respectively). There was no significant difference in responses between old and aged mice (p = 0.93; Figure 3B).

Immunohistochemical analyses

Retinas were stained for RNA binding protein with multiple splicing (RBPMS) to quantify RBPMS-positive RGCs. Old and aged mice had comparable RBPMS-positive cell densities (p = 0.98; Figures 4A and 4B, however, RBPMS-positive cell densities were significantly lower in old and aged mice compared to adult mice (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively; Figures 4A and 4B). Compared to adult mice, there were no significant differences in choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)-positive cell densities in old and aged mice (p = 0.65 and p = 0.66, respectively), nor in GFP-positive cell densities (p = 0.25 and p = 0.37 in old and aged mice, respectively; Figures 4A and 4B). In adult mice, there were no differences in RBPMS-positive cell densities in virus-injected eyes, contralateral eyes, and eyes of naive mice (p = 0.66; Figure 4C) showing that virus injection did not result in loss of RGCs.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that AAV vectors administered by intravitreal injection can be used to successfully transduce inner retinal cells with similar efficacy in adult (3–6 months of age) and old (18–24 months of age) mice. The viral labeling was sufficiently strong in both adult and old mice to be visualized with longitudinal in vivo fluorescence imaging. We determined that AAV expression persists in retinal cells for more than 18 months following a single intravitreal injection of the viral vector. Moreover, we demonstrated that age-related decreases in RGC density and function were not exacerbated by long-term AAV transduction. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that introducing fluorophores into RGCs for long-term structural and functional assessment of RGCs via AAV transduction is a viable and useful experimental approach.

We found a progressive increase in the density of GCaMP-labeled cells in both adult and old mice following injection, stabilizing after 5 weeks in adult mice and 4 weeks in old mice. The average in vivo cell density 8 weeks following injection was similar in both groups. In aged mice, results showed a similar trend in the average in vivo cell density when compared to adult mice over the initial 8 weeks post-injection, however, it gradually decreased by approximately 10% over 18 months. Previous research in AAV transgene expression in mice has shown persistence beyond 100 days in inner retinal cells,11 and 6–12 months in muscle.30,31 Evidence from human clinical trials suggests that AAV gene therapy can be maintained upwards of 4 years following administration.1,32 However, there is also evidence of gradual loss of AAV transgene expression,33 which must be explored further as AAV gene therapies continue to be used in long-term treatment of various ocular conditions.

We found that GCC thickness measured with OCT remained relatively stable in the short term in both adult and old mice following AAV injection. There was a transient increase in both GCC thickness and TRT in old mice at 3 and 4 weeks following injection which could be attributed to an inflammatory response. Changes in ONL thickness were not observed. Additionally, in most mice, vitreal haze in combination with retinal thickening was visible with OCT 1–2 weeks following injection. When present, vitreal haze cleared and GCC thickness and TRT returned to baseline levels in all mice when analyzed over 8 weeks following injection. Our findings agree with previous studies which have demonstrated elevated levels of ocular inflammation by 10–12 days following injection, which subsides by approximately 4 weeks in adult mice, and is exacerbated with age.34,35 In mice, sex differences in inflammatory responses across different age groups following AAV injection in the retina have been shown,35 in addition to sexual dimorphism in AAV tropism in multiple AAV serotypes.36 In our study, we elected to age female mice to minimize attrition due to aggression among fellow mice in the same cage, a phenomenon commonly observed in male mice. This risk, in addition to the relatively high attrition rate in aging studies dictated our choice of using female mice, however, we acknowledge the need for further research investigating sex differences in long-term AAV2 transduction in the retina.

Over the long term, we observed a gradual decline in GCC thickness over 18 months in aged mice, in agreement with previous studies reporting age-related thinning in both inner and total retinal thickness measurements.37,38,39,40 There are also reports of age-related retinal thickening, potentially due to retinal remodeling in inner retinal layers as an adaptation to preserve inner retinal cell function.41 We found no significant difference between the GCC thickness of aged mice and non-injected old mice at 18 months, indicating that the observed thinning could be attributed to natural aging processes rather than a consequence of viral transduction.

Functional experiments revealed robust Ca2+ transients in response to KA treatment across all age groups. Adult mice exhibited the most pronounced functional responses, while old and aged mice showed consistent but decreased responses when compared to adult mice. The transient responses in old and aged mice did not differ from each other, indicating two important findings. First, age-related decreases in function can be determined using AAV2-CAG-GCaMP6s irrespective of the timing of intravitreal injection. Second, using this methodology, long-term transduction does not appear to cause deleterious effects on function. Age-related changes in retinal function in rodents have been observed in both the outer and inner retina by evaluation with electroretinography (ERG).37,42,43,44,45 In the inner retina specifically, ERG studies have found age-related decreases in negative scotopic threshold response (nSTR) and positive scotopic threshold response (pSTR) amplitudes,41,46 however, these age-related changes may be strain dependent.41 To our knowledge, comparison of function using chemical stimulation following short-term transduction in old mice and long-term transduction in aging mice has not been performed to date. Our future work seeks to build upon these findings and perform in vivo optical stimulation to study longitudinal changes in function.

Old and aged mice had decreased RBPMS-positive cell density when compared to adult mice, indicative of age-related RGC loss. Compared to adult mice, old and aged mice had a 22% and 23% reduction in RGC density, respectively. Figures on the magnitude of RGC loss with age in rodents is controversial with some reports suggesting practically no loss47 while other reporting as 50% loss by 18 months of age.48 We did not find significant decreases in ChAT-positive cell densities in old or aged mice when compared to adult mice, potentially suggesting a differential vulnerability of amacrine cells in the aging murine retina. Samuel and colleagues explored this by analyzing age-related alterations in dendritic structure in RGCs and amacrine cells. They found significant changes in dendritic arbor size in multiple RGC subtypes, but not in starburst and AII amacrine cells,47 further emphasizing the potential for differential age-related changes based on cell type.

We expected that the viral hit rate in old mice would have been less compared to adult mice because of reduced viral transduction efficacy in the aging retina. Although we found an age-related decrease in RBPMS-positive cells in old mice compared to adult mice, the in vivo cell density was not-significantly different in these two groups of mice following 8 weeks of transduction. Moreover, the GCaMP-positive cell density in old mice was not significantly different than in adult mice when quantified with IHC. It is important to note that similar to our findings, there have been reports showing no significant differences in GFP-positive cell densities quantified in adult and old mice.49 Polinski and colleagues found no differences between adult and old mice with quantification of GFP-immunoreactive cell counts, however, they did find significantly lower GFP protein levels in old rats compared to adult rats using Western blot.49 Cell counts provide a binary measure of expression, and utilizing more sensitive protein quantification methods is important for quantifying these age-related changes in viral expression. It is possible age-related changes in GCaMP protein levels across groups could have been observed in the present study using western blot analysis. Future work is needed to determine a time course of AAV expression following long-term AAV administration to better understand how expression patterns may change with time and age.

Finally, our colocalization data showed high specificity of AAV2-CAG-GCaMP6s to RBPMS-positive cells across all age groups. In adult, old and aged mice aged, we found a proportion of cells that were RBPMS-negative/ChAT-negative which could represent either RBPMS-negative RGCs or displaced amacrine cells. Since RBPMS has been shown to be highly specific to RGCs,50,51 and that approximately 60% of neurons within the GCL in mice are displaced amacrine cells,52,53,54,55 it is probable that some of the RBPMS-negative/ChAT-negative cells observed are other classes of displaced amacrine cells, namely glutaminergic or glycinergic amacrine cells. Comparison of the proportion of RBPMS-negative/ChAT-negative cells labeled by the ubiquitous promoter CAG, used in this study, with other viral promoters with increased specificity to RGCs, could be beneficial when developing targeted therapeutic strategies for RGCs.

In conclusion, our study highlights the potential of AAV-based methods for long-term RGC labeling and potentially therapeutic strategies in both adult and old mice. We demonstrated age-related changes in RGC density (by in vivo fluorescence imaging, OCT, and IHC) and function (by intact retina Ca2+ imaging) are not exacerbated by AAV transduction with intravitreal injection. Understanding age-related differences in transduction efficacy is crucial for successful translation to humans as AAV-based gene therapy continues to be used in the management and treatment of age-related optic neuropathies.

Materials and methods

Animals

Ethics approval was obtained from the University Committee on Laboratory Animals at Dalhousie University and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Canadian Council of Animal Care (CCAC). Female wildtype C57BL/6 mice (JAX Mice Stock Number: 000664, Charles River Laboratories, Saint-Constant, QC, Canada), were used in this study. They were divided into 3 groups: (1) adult (injected with AAV2-CAG-GCaMP6s at 3 months of age), (2) old (injected at 18 months of age), and (3) aged mice that were injected at 3 months of age and followed to 18 months post-injection. All mice were housed in an environment with a 12-h light-dark cycle and had access to food and water ad libitum. Anesthesia for all procedures was performed with 2% isoflurane (Baxter Corporation, Mississauga, ON, Canada) and 1% O2 administered at a flow rate of 1.5 L/min through a nose cone.

Intravitreal injection

Mice were anesthetized and the left eye was dilated with topical 1% tropicamide (Alcon, Mississauga, ON, Canada) and 2.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride (Alcon). A 30-gauge needle was used to create a puncture in the sclera, 0.5 mm posterior to the limbus. The tip of a 33-gauge needle (Hamilton Company, Reno, NV, USA) was guided through the puncture site and 1.5 μL of the AAV2.CAG.GCaMP6s.WPRE.SV40 viral vector (8.38 × 1012 GC/mL titer; Penn Vector Core Lab, University of Pennsylvania, PA, USA) was slowly injected into the vitreous body. Following the injection, topical antimicrobial eye drops were applied and the animals recovered overnight on a heating pad.

In vivo imaging

Mice were anesthetized and the left eye was topically dilated as described previously. Ophthalmic liquid gel (Tear-Gel, Novartis Pharmaceuticals Canada, Inc, Mississauga, ON, Canada) was applied to the cornea. A polymethyl methacrylate contact lens (Cantor and Nissel Limited, Brackley, UK) was placed on the cornea to maintain corneal hydration and enhance image quality. Imaging was performed using a confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscope (CSLO) and spectral domain optical coherence tomography (OCT) device modified for use in mice (Spectralis Multiline, Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany).56

Three types of images were obtained: (1) CSLO infrared (820 nm) images to visualize the optic nerve head (ONH), retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and retinal vasculature; (2) CSLO fluorescence images with 488 nm excitation and emission bandpass filter of 500–550 nm,56 to monitor the number of GCaMP expressing cells and (3) OCT images to monitor peripapillary inner retinal thickness. CSLO images were obtained at the same transverse image frame with a 30-degree field of view, centered on the ONH. OCT comprised of a 12° circular B-scan centered on the ONH. All three types of images were averaged 100 times to achieve a high signal-to-noise ratio. Real-time eye tracking software was used to minimize motion artifacts.56

We segmented and quantified ganglion cell complex thickness (GCC; encompassing the RNFL, ganglion cell and inner plexiform layers), outer nuclear layer thickness (ONL; encompassing the outer nuclear layer between the OPL/ONL interface, and the external limiting membrane), and total retinal thickness (TRT; encompassing all retinal layers from the inner limiting membrane to the retinal pigment epithelium). Follow-up CSLO and OCT images were obtained with image registered acquisition and eye-tracking software to ensure that subsequent images to baseline were acquired in the same locations so an accurate serial analysis could be performed.

Baseline CSLO and OCT imaging were performed pre-injection. After injection, follow-up imaging was conducted weekly for 8 weeks to monitor transduction rate and GCC thickness. For aged mice, imaging was repeated at 6, 12, and 18 months.

Calcium imaging

We estimated functional capacity of GCaMP labeled retinal cells with Ca2+ imaging of intact retinal preparations. Following the final in vivo imaging session for adult, old, and aged mice, mice were sacrificed with an overdose of sodium pentobarbital by intraperitoneal injection. Retinas were carefully isolated, hemisected, and mounted on filter paper before being submerged in oxygenated Hanks Balanced Salts Solution (HBSS; 100% O2, 1 mM HEPES; Praxair, Dartmouth, NS) at a pH of 7.4. The retinas were placed in a microscope mounted superfusion chamber and perfused with HBSS at a rate of 2 mL/min for the duration of the Ca2+ imaging experiments.

A 40X water immersion objective (0.80W numerical aperture, Achroplan; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Oberkochen, Germany), connected to a charge-coupled device camera (Sensicam PCO, Kelheim, Germany), was used for imaging. GCaMP6s fluorescence was elicited with 488 nm excitation and collected at 516 nm emission. The microscope was focused on the GCL and experiments began with five minutes of baseline imaging at one frame per 20 s. Thereafter, 15 s treatments of 50 μM kainic acid (KA; a glutamate receptor agonist), dissolved in oxygenated HBSS were applied, followed by 15 min of washout with oxygenated HBSS solution. Each retina was treated four times with KA. Images were acquired at one frame per five seconds during KA treatments, and one frame per 20 s during washout periods. All experiments were recorded using Axon Imaging Workbench 4 software (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Immunohistochemistry and epifluorescence microscopy

Retinas were dissected and fixed at room temperature in 4% paraformaldehyde for one hour. Retinas were then washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and blocked in 10% normal donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc., West Grove, PA, USA) in PBS with 0.3% Triton X-100 overnight at 4°C. Retinas incubated in primary antibodies against RNA binding protein with multiple splicing (RBPMS; 1:1000 guinea pig anti-RBPMS, phosphosolutions #1832, Aurora, Colorado, USA) and choline acetyltransferase (ChAT; 1:100 goat anti- ChAT, PhosphoSolutions #315, Aurora, CO, USA) for five days at 4°C to label RGCs and cholinergic amacrine cells, respectively. Retinas were washed with PBS and incubated in Cy3 (1:1000 Cy3 conjugated donkey anti-guinea pig, Jackson Immuno Research Laboratories Inc., West Grove, PA, USA), Alexa Fluor 633 (1:1000 Alexa Fluor 633 conjugated donkey anti-goat, Invitrogen #A21082), and Alexa Fluor 488 (1:400; Alexa Fluor 488 conjugated rabbit anti GFP, Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA) overnight. Retinas were washed with PBS, mounted on microscope slides with Vectashield anti-fade fluorescent mounting medium (Vector Labs, Burlingame, CA).

Tiled images of retinal whole-mounts were obtained using a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) and a 20x Plan-Apochromat objective (0.8 numerical aperture; Carl Zeiss). Using ZEN Software (Carl Zeiss), exposure times were set for each of the three channels and fluorescence images of cells expressing RBPMS, ChAT, and GFP (GCaMP) in the ganglion cell layer (GCL) were acquired.

These images were used to quantify the density of RBPMS-positive cells, hit rate of viral labeling (defined as the percentage of RBPMS-positive cells that were also GCaMP6s-positive) and the specificity (defined as the percentage of GCaMP6s-positive cells that were RBPMS-positive).

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism (version 9.5.0 for Macintosh, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) and statistical significance was assumed when p < 0.05. All data were reported as mean (standard deviation; SD), and t-tests and ANOVAs were performed for inter-group comparisons.

In vivo fluorescence images acquired with CSLO were imported into ImageJ.57 Using the cell counting tool, all fluorescently labeled cells within the field of view were manually counted for each time point and expressed as cell density (cells/mm2).

For Ca2+ imaging analysis, GCaMP6s-postive cells within the GCL were traced as individual regions of interest. Approximately 50–75 cells were selected and analyzed for the duration of the experiment. Data for each cell were reported as changes in arbitrary fluorescence units over time. Baseline fluorescence for each cell was measured as an average of the first 15 frames of the recorded experiment. Maximum fluorescence was determined for each cell for each of the KA treatments and averaged. KA-induced Ca2+ transient amplitudes were defined as the peak fluorescence change over the baseline fluorescence (ΔF/F0).

For IHC, analysis was performed using Zen2Lite software (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Oberkochen, Germany). From the tiled images of the retinal wholemounts, 318 μm × 318 μm regions of interest were selected from the central, mid-peripheral and peripheral retina, at distances of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mm from the ONH, respectively. For each region, the number of cells labeled with RBPMS, ChAT, and GFP (GCaMP) were quantified and reported as cell densities. Additionally, the density of GFP-positive cells that colocalized with RBPMS-positive cells and ChAT-positive cells was also quantified.
