Neural Architecture of Familiar Person Perception: Contributions of Unimodal and Multimodal Brain Regions
Authors: Tianxing Zheng, Stefania Mattioni, Olivier Collignon, Xiaoqing Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFaces and voices provide essential information for recognizing familiar people. The brain regions involved in processing person familiarity through these modalities have typically been studied independently, leaving unclear whether and where the auditory and visual networks overlap. In this study, we developed a novel frequency‐tagging‐based fMRI paradigm to examine the neural basis of familiarity processing from a multimodal perspective. Our findings indicate that the perception of person familiarity through faces and voices predominantly activates sensory‐specific regions—the posterior superior temporal cortex (pSTC) for faces and the anterior superior temporal cortex (aSTC) for voices. Discrete regions in the temporal (posterior superior temporal sulcus, pSTS) and frontal (inferior frontal cortex, IFC) areas showed overlapping multisensory activity. pSTS sits as a spatial boundary between unimodal regions, whereas IFC shows connectivity‐driven convergence without boundary constraints. These findings indicate shared neural processes for familiarity processing from faces and voices. All together, these results highlight the presence of distributed unisensory and multisensory networks engaging in recognizing familiar people.

Introduction

Faces and voices convey overlapping information about a person's age, sex, identity, emotional state, and other characteristics. One of the first perceptual cues that emerge when encountering someone we know is the sense of familiarity. Previous research has shown that the process of recognizing familiarity through faces and voices occurs within a fraction of a second (Besson et al. 2017; Bruce and Young 2012; Schweinberger et al. 2014). For faces, participants can match familiar ones more quickly and accurately across changes in viewpoint compared with unfamiliar ones (Caharel et al. 2009, 2011). Familiar faces can even be identified as early as 380 ms after presentation—approximately 80 ms faster than unfamiliar faces (Ramon et al. 2011). This behavioral advantage is also reflected in shorter saccadic reaction times, which vary with task difficulty and identity presentation probability (Ramon et al. 2018, 2019). Although voice identification occurs more slowly than face recognition, electrophysiological evidence indicates that familiarity effects on voice processing remain rapid, starting around 200 ms after voice onset (Beauchemin et al. 2006; Joassin et al. 2011). Whether familiarity from faces and voices relies on overlapping neural mechanisms remains, however, poorly investigated.

The seminal work by Bruce and Young (1986) on the cognitive model of face recognition has inspired subsequent studies aimed at identifying the brain regions involved in the cognitive processes underlying face recognition. By synthesizing evidence from neuroimaging, lesion, and neurodevelopmental studies, researchers have identified a network of brain areas implicated in face perception (Duchaine and Yovel 2015; Haxby et al. 1999; Kanwisher et al. 1997). This network includes highly specialized regions, such as the fusiform face area (FFA) and occipital face area (OFA), which are thought to constitute the core face recognition system, as well as a broader network—the extended system—which encompasses areas like the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, and superior temporal sulcus (STS). These extended regions integrate information related to emotions, social cues, and other aspects of face processing, such as identity, gaze direction, and emotional expressions. Within this face network, regions such as the fusiform face area (FFA), posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), anterior temporal lobe face area (aTL‐FA), and frontal face area are implicated in the processing of face familiarity (Elfgren et al. 2006; Gobbini and Haxby 2007; Ishai et al. 2002; Nikel et al. 2022; Rapcsak 2019).

Research on voice processing has delineated cognitive processes that parallel those involved in face processing, while also identifying brain regions associated with these processes in the auditory modality, such as the temporal voice areas (TVAs, Belin et al. 2000, 2002). More specifically, the anterior superior temporal sulcus (aSTS), middle STS (mSTS), and inferior frontal cortex (IFC) are considered to be involved in the recognition of familiar voices (Aglieri et al. 2021; Belin and Zatorre 2003; Kriegstein and Giraud 2004; von Kriegstein et al. 2005, 2003; Latinus et al. 2011, 2013). These findings are effectively summarized by the “auditory face” model (Belin et al. 2004; Yovel and Belin 2013), which suggests that the system responsible for voice processing mirrors the face processing system functionally, with distinct neural networks independent of those engaged in visual face processing. The visual and auditory person recognition systems are thought to converge at higher levels of cognition, such as the semantic or episodic memory levels (e.g., memory of the person's identity, Campanella and Belin 2007).

Recent neuroimaging evidence, however, revealed that the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS) responds to and integrates both faces and voice stimuli (Anzellotti and Caramazza 2017; Davies‐Thompson et al. 2019; Tsantani et al. 2019; Watson et al. 2014). Watson et al. (2014) investigated modality‐specific processing across various regions of the superior temporal sulcus (STS) using both unimodal and audiovisual stimuli. Their findings indicated that the bilateral pSTS and inferior frontal cortex (IFC) may be considered heteromodal regions, as they responded to audiovisual integration stimuli but not to unimodal stimuli. Additionally, pSTS has been suggested to encode individual identity across modalities (Anzellotti and Caramazza 2017; Tsantani et al. 2019). The pSTS is also thought to integrate visual information about faces with higher‐order conceptual information regarding other individuals (Deen et al. 2015, 2020). These neuroimaging findings suggest that, while face and voice processing occur in parallel via distinct modality‐specific neural pathways, cross‐modal interactions may also occur within certain multisensory regions, though the precise loci and mechanisms of which remain to be further investigated.

In this study, we aim to investigate the brain regions that respond to person familiarity through both faces and voices, prior to the semantic or episodic levels. Critically, to capture the rapid processing of familiarity, we employed a unified paradigm—a frequency‐tagging‐based fMRI approach (also known as fast periodic stimulation fMRI, FPS‐fMRI)—which presents images and voices at a high rate (up to six images per second or four sound clips per second). This approach measures neural responses in the frequency domain by presenting stimuli at a fixed periodic rate and directly quantifies difference responses at specific frequencies (Barbero et al. 2021; Gao et al. 2018). It eliminates reliance on assumptions of pure insertion/cognitive subtraction (i.e., assuming that adding a cognitive process does not interact with existing processes) (Friston et al. 1996) and assumptions about the shape of the hemodynamic response (Boynton et al. 1996; Buxton et al. 2004), thereby enabling a direct and unbiased comparison of familiarity responses across modalities. Additionally, we incorporated two levels of comparison: a category level (faces vs. objects and vocal sounds vs. non‐vocal sounds) and a familiarity (subordinary) level (familiar vs. unfamiliar faces and familiar vs. unfamiliar voices). This dual‐level comparison allows for a comprehensive examination of modality‐dependent and multimodal brain responses in face and voice processing (Figure 1).

Materials and Methods

Participants

Twenty‐one adults (11 females; mean age = 23.8 ± 2.6 years, range = 18–30 years) from the Zhejiang University community participated in the current study. All of them had normal or corrected‐to‐normal vision and normal hearing. All but one participant was right‐handed (Oldfield 1971). None of the participants reported any history of psychiatric or neurological disorders or current use of any psychoactive medications. All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the Center for Psychological Sciences, Zhejiang University Research Ethics Board (Certificate #: 2019‐018) and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. We obtained informed written consent from all the participants prior to the experiment. Each participant received monetary compensation for their time participating in the current study.

Stimuli

Non‐Face Objects

The non‐face images consist of 200 photographs of 50 sub‐categories including scenes, objects, vehicles, plants, animals, and so forth. Each photograph contains only one object, which varies naturally in size, color, lighting, and background. The same set of non‐face images was used in previous studies (Gao et al. 2018, 2022; Rossion 2014). All the non‐face object images are resized to 256 by 256 pixels.

Familiar and Unfamiliar Faces

Before the experiment, each participant sent us 14 photographs of their friends or relatives with whom they are highly familiar in their daily lives (2 individuals, 7 photographs for each individual). An experimenter screened the photographs with the following criteria: only one face in the picture; good image resolution for seeing the face clearly; no heavy makeup; not wearing sun glasses; accessories (e.g., scarf) not covering face area. Such criteria ensured that the face in the picture is clearly visible and can be easily recognized by the person providing the picture. At the same time, we excluded pictures bearing non‐facial cues that may contribute to familiarity (e.g., a unique hat may be a cue to a specific identity). Participants were asked to replace the photographs until all 14 photographs each person provided met the criteria. The experimenter then cropped the photographs centering on the faces to the same size and were prescreened to be highly visually homogeneous, as identity‐related effects are more subtle than category‐level contrasts. This cropping manipulation also removed most of the background scenes of the photographs to minimize potential familiarity cues and reduce low‐level visual noise that could confound identity‐specific familiarity processing. After cropping, the photographs were no longer identical to their original form. Therefore, the face images were visually new to the participants during the experiment. While these photographs serve as stimuli of personally familiar faces, they also serve as stimuli of unfamiliar faces for the other participants. Importantly, since all the faces are from the same image pool, there is no statistical difference between the familiar faces and the unfamiliar faces on any low‐level image characteristics (such as luminance, contrast, and color). In total, we have collected 294 face images. All face images are resized to 256 by 256 pixels.

Vocal Sounds and Non‐Vocal Sounds

The vocal sounds and non‐vocal sounds stimuli were the same as those used in a previous study (see Barbero et al. 2021 for a detailed description of the stimuli). There were 55 vocal sounds including speech and non‐speech vocalizations from speakers of different sex and age with various emotional states. There were 137 non‐vocal sounds including environmental sounds, musical instrument sounds, and sounds produced by objects. Each of the vocal sounds and the non‐vocal sounds was 250 ms long.

Familiar and Unfamiliar Voices

Prior to the experiment, we recorded 30 clips of three‐word phrases from two familiar individuals of each participant. The two familiar individuals were the same for both familiar faces and voices, ensuring a consistent level of person familiarity across modalities. Our pilot experiment revealed that participants could not reliably achieve a high accuracy in judging familiar voices within a time window (250 or 500 ms) shorter than 800 ms. Therefore, each audio clip was set to a duration of 800 ms. These stimuli were recorded in a quiet room using Adobe Audition (2023) in mono with a sampling rate of 44,100 Hz and a bit depth of 16. The sound level of all stimuli was unified to 75 dB. As with the face stimuli, the voice stimuli serve as familiar voices for the current participant, while they serve as unfamiliar voices for other participants. For each clip of recording, we calculated three acoustic parameters: Fundamental Frequency (F0), Formant Dispersion (FD), and Harmonics‐to‐Noise Ratio (HNR), using Praat (Latinus et al. 2013). These parameters were used to calculate Euclidean distances between different individual voices. Based on the Euclidean distances, for each participant, we selected 75 clips of recordings of unfamiliar voices from five strangers that are on average the most different from the voices of the two familiar individuals of the participant. This method maximizes the contrast between familiar and unfamiliar voice stimuli in each participant's auditory materials. By enhancing this distinction, participants could more easily recognize familiar voices during passive listening, which likely facilitates neural activation associated with familiarity perception. Since the familiar voice clips and unfamiliar voice clips are from the same pool, there is no statistical difference between the familiar voice clips and unfamiliar voice clips on any low‐level acoustic features.

Procedures

Verification Task

Before the fMRI experiment, we ran a verification task to gauge how well the participants could recognize the voices of familiar people among strangers' voices. Each participant made known/stranger judgments to clips of voice recordings as described in the stimuli section. The clips selected for each participant were the same as those used in the subsequent fMRI experiment of familiar‐voice condition. Each clip was presented separately for 800 ms after a 300 ms‐long cue tone. The participants were given sufficient time to make their judgment. Before the task, they completed 7 practice trials, with feedback provided. Then, they were tested with 105 trials (30 familiar and 75 unfamiliar). The stimuli were presented in a pseudo‐randomized order. The verification task lasted for about 8 min. Participants were instructed to respond by pressing a key: J (right index finger) for familiar, and K (right middle finger) for unfamiliar. Accuracy of the verification task averaged 92.88% (ranged from 82.85% to 100%) with SD of ±5.07%, indicating that participants can recognize the familiar voices well.

fMRI Experiment

The fMRI experiment consists of four conditions identified by modality (visual or auditory) and by level of comparison (category level or familiarity level) (Figure 2). In the visual modality, at the category level, we measured neural responses selective to faces in contrast to non‐face objects (Condition 1). At the familiarity level, we measured neural responses selective to familiar faces in contrast to unfamiliar faces (Condition 2). In the auditory modality, at the category level, we measured neural responses selective to vocal sounds in contrast to non‐vocal sounds (Condition 3). At the familiarity level, we measured neural responses selective to familiar voices in contrast to unfamiliar voices (Condition 4).

Visual Modality

Category Level: Face‐Selective Brain Responses (Condition 1)

We used a frequency‐tagging based fMRI paradigm to identify face‐selective brain responses. In each run, the visual presentation started with images from non‐face categories (the base images) presented in serial at a fixed rate of 6 Hz (i.e., 6 images/s). Every 8 s, a “burst” of 7 faces (the target images) was presented in the same stimulus train, alternating with the images from the non‐target categories. The alternating presentation of faces and objects stimuli was designed to promote the contrast between categories and minimize the neural adaptation. Effectively, the 7 face images covered 2.167 s with 6 non‐face images in between (Figure 2A). The burst period created a direct contrast between faces and non‐face images. Since the burst happened at a fixed frequency (every 8 s, i.e., 1/8 = 0.125 Hz), the differential neural response related to the contrast between faces and non‐face images can be measured with Fourier analysis as in previous studies using frequency tagging (Gao et al. 2018). All images were contrast modulated by a sinusoidal function to provide a smooth transition between successive images. Each functional run had a length of 360 s and consisted of a 4 s fade‐in period, in which the maximum image contrast gradually rose from 0 to 1, followed by a 352 s visual stimulation, and a 4 s fade‐out period, in which the maximum image contrast gradually dropped from 1 to 0. The “bursts” containing target images appeared 44 times (cycles) in each run.

Since faces were presented less frequently (7 images in every 8 s) than the non‐face images (41 images in every 8 s), we matched the frequency of each image in a 360 s run by using proportionally more non‐face images than the face images. Specifically, we used 28 face images, while we used 164 object images as the contrasting category to match the 7/41 ratio. Therefore, each image would appear the same number of times in each run.

Familiarity Level: Familiar‐Face‐Selective Brain Responses (Condition 2)

In Condition 2, we used the same visual stimulation paradigm as in Condition 1 except for the following differences. Here, the base images are the unfamiliar faces, while the target images are the familiar faces. The familiar faces are tagged with 1/8 Hz. Since familiar faces appear less frequently than unfamiliar faces (7/41 ratio), we used 14 familiar faces (7 pictures from each of the 2 familiar individuals) and 84 unfamiliar faces (7 pictures from each of 12 unfamiliar individuals) to achieve a similar number of presentations for each picture. Other settings are identical to Condition 1.

Auditory Modality

Category Level: Vocal‐Selective Brain Responses (Condition 3)

In Condition 3, we adapted the visual stimulation paradigm to auditory stimulation (Figure 2B). We used the non‐vocal sounds as the base category which were played at a frequency of 4 Hz (250 ms for each sound clip). Every 8 s, a “burst” of 6 vocal sounds was played continuously, covering 1.5 s. So, as in the visual stimulation, the target category (vocal sounds) here is also tagged with a frequency of 1/8 Hz. Unlike the alternating presentation of target and non‐target stimuli in the visual modality, target voices were presented continuously in the auditory modality, due to the temporal nature of voice, which needs time to unfold. Adopting the same alternation rate as in the visual condition would not allow sufficient time for complete auditory stimulus presentation in 8 s per cycle. As a result, the vocal sounds and non‐vocal sounds were played with a ratio of 6/26. To match this ratio, we used 30 vocal sounds and 130 non‐vocal sounds. As with the visual stimulation, each run of the auditory had 44 cycles, adding to a total of 352 s of stimulation time.

Familiarity Level: Familiar‐Voice‐Selective Brain Responses (Condition 4)

In Condition 4, the base stimuli were the unfamiliar voices. They were played at a frequency of 1.25 Hz (every 800 ms). Every 8 s, a burst of three clips of familiar voices played continuously, covering 2.4 s. So, familiar voices are also tagged with a frequency of 1/8 Hz. Since familiar voices and unfamiliar voices were presented with a ratio of 3/7, in each stimulation run, we used 30 clips of familiar voices (15 clips from each of 2 familiar individuals) and 75 clips of unfamiliar voices (15 clips from each of 5 unfamiliar individuals) so that each clip of voice was played for a similar number of times. As in other conditions, each run had 44 cycles, adding to a total of 352 s of stimulation time.

Behavioral Tasks in the fMRI

During the fMRI sessions, the participants were instructed to pay attention to the visual or the auditory stimuli, but were not required to make any response to the contents of the visual or the auditory stimuli. To ensure that the participants' attention was directed to the stimuli, we introduced a secondary task that is orthogonal to the stimulus presentation. In the visual sessions, participants pressed a button to detect the color change of a central fixation cross overlapped on the stimulation images. During each run, the cross turns its color from black to white for 200 ms. The interval between two color changes was random, with a minimal interval of 2 s. There were 70 color changes in each run. In the auditory sessions, participants pressed a button to detect a clip of white noise, which had the same length as the stimuli (250 ms in Condition 3 and 800 ms in Condition 4). The interval between two clips of white noise was randomized with a minimal interval of 2 s. In each run, there were 40 clips of white noise.

Design

Each condition was repeated three times, resulting in a total of 12 experimental runs in the fMRI experiment. The order of the runs was pseudorandomized to ensure that no two successive runs were the same condition. Each run lasted approximately 6 min to finish, with participants resting briefly between the runs. In total, the fMRI session lasted for around 1.5 h.

fMRI Image Acquisition

The data were collected on a 3 T Siemens Magnetom Prisma system (Siemens, Eerlangen, Germany) with a 20‐channel head coil. The MP‐RAGE sequence was used for T1‐weighted structural image acquisition (TR 2300 ms, TE 2.32 ms, TI 900 ms, Voxel size 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, FoV 240 × 240 mm2). Multi‐echo and multi‐gradient plane echo (EPI) sequence is used for functional image data acquisition (TR 1000 ms, TE 15 ms, 37.72 ms, 60.42 ms, FoV 240 × 240 mm2, Voxel size 3 × 3 × 3 mm3, 44 slices, GRAPPA with an acceleration factor of 4).

fMRI Analysis

Preprocessing

The MP2RAGE sequence simultaneously acquires the T1w and PDw image volumes. These two image volumes were used to generate a uniform T1w image volume which was used in surface reconstruction in FreeSurfer.

The functional runs were preprocessed using AFNI libraries (https://afni.nimh.nih.gov/). The first four volumes of each functional run were discarded to allow for T1 equilibration effects. The functional runs of each echo were despiked (3dDespike) and slice‐time corrected (3dTshift). The first echo images (TE = 15 ms) of each time point were used to compute 6 rigid motion parameters for motion correction (3dvolreg) by aligning all the first echo images to the first frame of the first functional run. Then T2* map was estimated from the first functional run in the fit of the equation assuming mono‐exponential signal decay: (1) STEn=S0×e−TET2*, where S0 is the initial signal, n is the number of TE, and STEn is the signal at TEn. The functional to anatomy coregistration parameters were estimated by registering the T2* map to the uniform skull‐stripped T1w image. Motion correction and anatomical coregistration were applied in a single step separately for each echo of the functional runs. The multi‐echo time series were then combined using a T2*‐weighted scheme. Independent component analysis (ICA) was subsequently performed on the combined data after spatial smoothing with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel to improve component estimation stability. Components were classified as BOLD‐ or non‐BOLD‐like based on their echo‐time dependence. Non‐BOLD components were regressed out from the non‐smoothed combined dataset, yielding the final denoised time series used for all subsequent analyses.

Magnitude and Phase of Neural Response

We firstly removed linear trends from the preprocessed time series data of each voxel and converted the time series to percentage of BOLD signal change. We then performed fast Fourier transform (FFT) to obtain the amplitude spectrum in the frequency domain. Then, we calculated SNR of the neural activity at the target frequency for each voxel independently using a z‐score transformation: (2) SNR=As−μN/σN, where As is the amplitude of the target stimulation frequency (0.125 Hz), μN is the mean of 40 neighboring frequency bins (20 on each side, with a bin width of 0.0025 Hz) and σN is the standard deviation of the amplitude of the 40 neighboring frequency bins.

Phase information obtained from the FFT analysis was used to define the direction of neural responses in the functional runs. We firstly plotted the histogram of phase values of all the voxels with a SNR above 3 and then we used the phase value of the histogram bin that has the largest number as the center phase (φ) and defined the voxels as activation with their phase values within the window of (φ ± π/2) and as deactivation with their phase values outside of this window.

Combining Data From All Three Runs For Each Condition

In the functional runs, the BOLD responses to the target stimulations in a given population of neurons should have the same phase, while any physical noise component (e.g., pulse, respiration) with the same frequency could have different phases across runs. Therefore, we averaged the time series across three runs of same condition to increase the signal‐to‐noise ratio, similar to the use of this approach in electrophysiology (Regan 1989). Then we calculated condition‐averaged SNR map for each condition in the same way as with individual runs using averaged time series.

Regions of Interest

Individual variability in brain function has been widely observed in previously fMRI studies (Frost and Goebel 2012; Zilles and Amunts 2013). Face perception studies have also shown a considerable amount of variability across individuals (Gao et al. 2022; Zhen et al. 2015). Therefore, it is of great importance to analyze the category‐selective responses at an individual level. In the present study, we defined regions of interest (ROIs) for each participant using an automatic parcellation scheme implemented in FreeSurfer, which provides reliable individual‐level cortical segmentation (Desikan et al. 2006; Fischl et al. 2004; Reuter et al. 2012). The cortical surface was parcellated into anatomically defined sulci and gyri based on the Destrieux atlas, and ROIs were selected based on previous research identifying face‐ and voice‐selective cortical regions (for faces: Haxby et al. 2000; Gobbini and Haxby 2007; Duchaine and Yovel 2015; Kanwisher et al. 1997; Elfgren et al. 2006; Ishai et al. 2002; Deen et al. 2015; for voices: Belin et al. 2000; Pernet et al. 2015). This anatomical grounded approach enabled consistent ROI identification across participants and allowed for group‐level comparisons of both the number and magnitude of activated voxels within each region. Specifically, in the face‐selective condition, the ROIs include LO, fusiform gyrus, STS, and IFC. In the familiar face‐selective condition, the ROIs include STS, IFC, and precuneus. In the voice‐selective condition, the ROIs include STG, MTG, and IFC. In the familiar voice‐selective condition, the ROIs include STG and IFC. Figure 3 shows the ROIs on an inflated cortical surface.

Anatomically defined regions of interest (ROIs). We selected ROIs based on the results from the group analysis. The ROIs were automatically parcellated by Freesurfer based on reconstructed cortical surface from high‐resolution anatomic scans.

Some of the ROIs we selected are composed of several regions from Destrieux Altas in Freesurfer. We combined the anterior occipital sulcus (S_occipital_anterior), inferior occipital gyrus and sulcus (G_and_S_occipital_inferior), middle occipital gyrus (G_occipital_middle), and middle occipital sulcus (S_occipital_middle_and_Lunatus) to form a lateral occipital cortex (LO). We selected the lateral aspect of the superior temporal gyrus (G_temp_sup‐Lateral), anterior transverse superior temporal gyrus (G_temp_sup‐G_T_transv), planum polare of the superior temporal gyrus (G_temp_sup‐plan_polar), and planum tempolare of the superior temporal gyrus (G_temp_sup‐plan_tempo) parts as STG. We also combined inferior frontal sulcus (IFS) and inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) (including opercular part of the inferior frontal gyrus (G_front_inf‐Opercular), orbital part of the inferior frontal gyrus (G_front_inf‐Orbital), and triangular part of the inferior frontal gyrus (G_front_inf‐Triangul)) as inferior frontal cortex (IFC).

To investigate the hemispheres lateralization in different conditions, for each participant, we compared the volume of activated voxels and peak z‐score within defined ROIs in two hemispheres. We also compared these two measures at the group level.

We additionally defined functional ROIs (fROIs) using category‐level contrasts (faces > objects for the visual modality; vocal > nonvocal sounds for the auditory modality). Thresholded activation maps from these contrasts within STC and IFC were binarized to create individual fROIs. These masks were then applied to the thresholded familiarity‐level contrasts to quantify the number and strength of activated voxels within each participant's category‐selective regions. This procedure allowed us to directly compare familiarity‐related responses between anatomical ROIs (aROIs) and category‐selective fROIs, thereby assessing whether the familiarity effects were confined to category‐selective cortex or extended more broadly.

Overlap Analysis

We conducted an overlap analysis within the visual modality (between Condition 1 and Condition 2), the auditory modality (between Condition 3 and Condition 4), and across modalities (between Condition 1 and Condition 3, as well as between Condition 2 and Condition 4) at both group and individual levels. For visualization, we plotted overlap images of group analyses using FreeSurfer.

Whole‐Brain/Regional Clusters Analysis

Clusters of voxels were identified that were connected to each other by at least a corner, with a minimal volume of 81 mm3 (3 voxels at the size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm3 as in previous studies (Gao et al. 2018, 2022) using AFNI). At the group level, we localized the peak MNI coordinates of the clusters, as described in the Group Analysis section above. At the individual level, we plotted the activation clusters from both modalities to identify modality‐specific and multi‐modal brain responses.

Results

aROIs and fROIs

Across participants, the number of activated voxels within fROIs was significantly smaller than within aROIs (Figure S1). The proportion of activated voxels in fROIs relative to aROIs was also low (Table S1), indicating that the familiarity‐related responses extended beyond the core category‐selective regions. Consistently, mean SNR z‐scores within fROIs were lower than those within aROIs in IFC (Figure S1).

To further evaluate whether the aROIs captured the majority of the task‐related activation, we examined whole‐brain activation maps (z > 3.09, p < 0.001) for each participant and for the group average. For each map, we quantified the number of suprathreshold voxels falling within the aROIs and calculated the proportion of these voxels relative to the total number of activated voxels across the whole brain (Table S2). Across participants, the aROIs encompassed a large proportion of whole‐brain activation, indicating that the anatomical ROI definition effectively captured the core task‐related responses.

Visual Modality

Face‐Selective Areas

We identified brain regions selectively activated for faces in the group analysis. We observed stronger activation for faces compared to non‐face objects in the bilateral lateral occipital cortex (LO) and fusiform gyrus at the category level. In the fusiform gyrus, activation was limited to the lateral si of the middle fusiform fissure, extending to the temporal pole in the right hemisphere. We also noticed activation in the bilateral posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), extending to the temporal parietal junction (Figure 4A and Table S3). We then quantified the volumes of activation voxels and peak z‐scores within regions of interest in the face‐selective condition at the group level. Familiar Face Selective Areas

At the group level, the bilateral pSTS and IFC were more active to familiar faces compared to strangers' faces, with a stronger effect in the right hemisphere (Figure 4B, Tables S3 and S4). Overlap Within Visual Modality

When comparing the category level and familiarity level within the visual modality, we found that the LO and fusiform gyrus are primarily associated with face categorization, while the pSTC, IFC, and precuneus were involved in familiar face perception (Figure 4A,B). We also found overlapping brain areas involved in both levels of processing. The bilateral pSTC responded to both faces and familiar faces. The right IFC and right precentral sulcus also showed overlapping activation to a less extent (Figures 4C and 10B).

Discussion

We investigated the neural mechanisms underlying person familiarity perception across both visual and auditory modalities using a novel and highly sensitive frequency‐tagging paradigm tailored to fMRI recordings. Our approach investigated both face/voice categorization and familiarity discrimination, allowing us to disambiguate the respective networks of these two processes across modalities. Our findings indicate that: (1) Person familiarity perception predominantly engages modality‐specific brain regions, with the posterior superior temporal sulcus/gyri (pSTS/G) involved in processing familiar faces and the anterior superior temporal sulcus/gyri (aSTS/G) engaged in processing familiar voices. (2) A proportion (around 10%) of the familiarity‐selective volumes in each modality, located in the bilateral posterior superior temporal cortex (pSTC) and right inferior frontal cortex (IFC), exhibited overlapping responses to both visual and auditory familiarity.

Modality‐Specific Networks for Familiar Faces and Voices

The frequency‐tagging fMRI paradigm has been employed in studies of categorical face‐selective brain responses, demonstrating a high signal‐to‐noise ratio and high reliability (Gao et al. 2018, 2019). In the current study, we extended this paradigm within the visual domain to localize familiarity‐selective brain regions. We were able to reliably identify familiarity‐selective responses in the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), precuneus, and inferior frontal cortex (IFC). These findings are consistent with previous literature (Bernstein and Yovel 2015; Gobbini and Haxby 2007; Ubaldi and Fairhall 2021). For the first time in the auditory domain, we adapted the frequency‐tagging fMRI paradigm to identify voice‐selective and familiar voice‐selective brain regions. Consistent with previous studies, we reliably localized voice‐selective areas in the middle temporal gyrus (MTG) and superior temporal sulcus (STS) (Belin et al. 2000, 2002). Additionally, we identified familiarity‐selective areas in the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS) and inferior frontal cortex (IFC), consistent with prior work (Bethmann et al. 2012; Kriegstein and Giraud 2004; Maguinness et al. 2018; Stevenage 2018). We did not include an audiovisual condition presenting faces and voices simultaneously here. Instead, we intentionally focused on unimodal (visual and auditory) presentations to avoid potential visual‐dominance effects (Colavita 1974; Sinnett et al. 2007) and to reduce complexity of the paradigm (e.g., the need to consider congruent vs. incongruent face–voice pairings; Davies‐Thompson et al. 2019). This allowed us to maintain a design that directly aligns with our primary research objective.

The use of a unified fMRI paradigm—employing the same target frequency across all conditions and eliminating the need to assume hemodynamic response functions tailored to specific stimulus types—enabled a relatively fair comparison across sensory modalities and processing levels (i.e., category level vs. familiarity level). Because auditory information requires more time to unfold, identical timing parameters could not be used across modalities. As a result, fewer auditory stimuli were presented within the same time window compared to visual stimuli. Although the presentation rate and number of individual stimuli differed between modalities, we kept the total presentation duration (i.e., the number of cycles) identical across all conditions. This choice was guided by our supplementary analysis showing that activation strength was primarily determined by the number of cycles rather than the sheer number of stimulus presentation (Figure S3). The equal appearance frequency of the target stimuli across modalities, ensuring that in all conditions, the target of interest was tagged with the same frequency (0.125 Hz) in all four conditions in the current study. Importantly, no significant responses were observed at the harmonic frequencies (e.g., 0.25 Hz), even in the most strongly activated condition (face versus object) (Figure S4), indicating that the observed effects were specific to the fundamental stimulation frequency. Additionally, the rapid presentation of stimuli in this paradigm (Rossion 2014) was designed to reduce—though not entirely eliminate—the involvement of higher‐order cognitive processes, such as explicit memory retrieval (Xu et al. 2017). Although familiar faces and voices were likely consciously recognized, the fast periodic stimulation primarily targets automatic aspects of familiarity processing rather than deliberate memory recall.

Two Types of Multimodal Regions

Beyond these robust unimodal effects at both levels, we also identified two loci of overlap between face‐ and voice‐driven brain activity.

Multimodal Region Type 1: pSTC as a Spatial Boundary Zone

One was located in the posterior superior temporal cortex (pSTC), which was found at both category and familiarity levels. At the category level (faces/voices vs. objects), the overlap was located at the boundary between the voice‐selective and face‐selective areas within the bilateral posterior superior temporal cortex (pSTC), posterior to the voice‐selective area and anterior to the face‐selective areas in this region (Figure 8A). Voxels that responded to both face and voice stimuli constituted 3.3% of all voice‐selective voxels and 7.3% of all face‐selective voxels. Although familiarity‐selective responses were weaker compared to category‐selective responses (Figure 8B), they also overlapped at the boundary between voice‐familiarity responses (anterior) and face‐familiarity responses (posterior) in pSTC. Voxels responsive to both face familiarity and voice familiarity comprised 3.2% of all voice‐familiarity–selective voxels and 2.4% of all face‐familiarity–selective voxels.

Previous human studies have reported that pSTC respond to—or even integrates—phonological (Audenhaege et al. 2025; Karthik et al. 2024; Zhu and Beauchamp 2017), emotional (Barbero et al. 2025; Davies‐Thompson et al. 2019; Kreifelts et al. 2009; Peelen et al. 2010), and identity‐related (Tsantani et al. 2019) information conveyed through both faces and voices. These findings support the notion that the pSTC serves as a multisensory hub for integrating facial and vocal cues.

Our findings extend this body of literature by suggesting an integrative role of the pSTC in familiarity perception. Specifically, we propose that person‐related perceptual and conceptual information may be encoded in a shared format within this region. This interpretation is supported by our observation that multimodal voxels in the pSTC—sensitive to both faces and voices—are preferentially located at the boundary between auditory‐ and visual‐dominant cortical areas. Such spatial organization may enable a discrete population of neurons (comprising less than 10% of the modality‐specific neurons) to receive convergent input from both sensory modalities, analogous to how residents of a border city may be fluent in the languages of both neighboring countries. This proposal aligns with prior evidence from neuromodulation and electrophysiological studies. For example, TMS studies have shown that temporary disruption of the STS impairs the integration of incongruent auditory and visual speech cues, providing causal evidence for its role in multisensory speech processing (Beauchamp et al. 2010). Complementary findings from intracranial EEG (iEEG) studies further support this: in humans, neurons within the auditory cortex (STS and pSTG) have been shown to respond selectively to visual speech cues, such as lipreading and mouth movements (Karthik et al. 2024; Metzger et al. 2020). Similarly, cross‐modal responses have been observed in non‐human primates, where neurons in STS face patches are modulated by auditory input, indicating audiovisual integration at the single‐neuron level (Khandhadia et al. 2021).

Taken together, while these findings underscore the multisensory nature of the pSTC, it remains unclear whether this property arises from its unique spatial positioning or reflects intrinsic multimodal characteristics that coexist alongside modality‐specific processing. Further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Multimodal Region Type 2: IFC as a Connectivity‐Driven Hub

Another region showing overlapping responses to faces and voices was identified in the inferior frontal cortex (IFC), which was only seen at the familiarity level. However, the IFC exhibited a distinct response pattern compared to the pSTC. First, overlapping activation in the IFC was observed only at the familiarity level, not at the category level. Second, unlike the pSTC, the IFC did not show a specific spatial organization of overlapping areas in relation to modality‐specific activations. This raises an intriguing question: how does the IFC contribute to multisensory familiarity processing in the absence of a clear sensory‐boundary organization, as seen in the pSTC? Insights from non‐human primate studies offer potential explanations. Single‐neuron recordings have identified multisensory neurons within the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC) that respond to audiovisual communication signals (Sugihara et al. 2006) and encode identity‐related information at the population level (Sharma et al. 2024), suggesting that this region can function as a multimodal convergence zone. Anatomically, this integrative function may be supported by robust projections from the anterior temporal lobe, STG and the dorsal bank of the STS to the lateral prefrontal cortex (Romanski et al. 1999). These cascading multimodal inputs from temporal association cortices may enable projection neurons to carry integrated signals into the VLPFC (Romanski 2007, 2012). Moreover, functional specialization within the VLPFC further supports its integrative capacity. Subregions within the VLPFC have shown selective responses to visual objects feature (e.g., color, type, or shape) (Wilson et al. 1993) as well as to nonspatial auditory information (Romanski 2007), suggesting that multisensory integration may occur within overlapping zones between visual‐ and auditory‐responsive regions (Romanski 2012). Thus, although the human IFC is not positioned at the boundary of sensory cortices like the pSTC, its extensive anatomical and functional connectivity with multimodal temporal regions may underlie its capacity to support familiarity perception across modalities.

Given that the IFC is also implicated in top‐down control (Giesbrecht et al. 2003), memory processes (Greenberg et al. 2005) and imagery (Zvyagintsev et al. 2013), we considered whether the IFC activation observed here might reflect imagery or other associative processes. Although hearing a familiar voice could theoretically evoke the speaker's face, auditory‐induced visual imagery typically elicits co‐activation in IFG, early visual cortex, and the fusiform gyrus (Daselaar et al. 2010; De Volder et al. 2001). In our auditory condition, however, we did not detect significant activation in the visual areas. Thus, the present findings support the view that IFC activity primarily reflects familiarity‐related multimodal processing rather than imagery‐driven top‐down effects.

Quantitative Comparison of Unimodal and Multimodal Networks

We used a Venn diagram to visualize the relationship between unimodal and multimodal responses across the four experimental conditions. Given that perceptual differences between categories are generally larger than those within a category (e.g., between faces and objects vs. among faces) (Kriegeskorte et al. 2008), it is expected that the category‐level conditions elicited more activated voxels than the familiarity‐level conditions, in both unimodal and multimodal analysis (Figure 10A,D). Notably, compared with previous studies using familiar celebrity faces and voices that primarily identified potential multimodal regions in the pSTS (Tsantani et al. 2019), our paradigm demonstrated greater sensitivity, revealing an additional multimodal familiarity‐related regions in the IFC (Figure 10D). This may reflect the ability of our paradigm to detect more subtle aspects of familiarity representation. We also examined the overlap between multimodal regions across the category and familiarity levels and found only a minimal number of shared voxels (Figure 10E). Together with the fROI‐based results (Table S1), this may suggest that the core multimodal regions supporting familiarity processing are not fully subsumed by those engaged in category‐level processing. Overall, both the spatial distribution and quantitative patterns of activation reflect the strengths of our experimental design and provide new insights into how the human brain integrates across modalities to process familiarity information.

Revising Models of Person Identity Processing

A previous theoretical model by Campanella and Belin (2007) proposed that visual and auditory identity processing occurs in parallel, with information from both modalities converging at the Person Identity Node (PIN) (Bruce and Young 1986; Ellis et al. 1997). Building on this framework, our findings motivate a revised model. In this revised model, visual and auditory processing primarily occurs within modality‐specific brain regions, while cross‐modal interactions emerge at two key multimodal hubs: the pSTC, which supports both category‐ and familiarity‐level analysis, and the IFC, which is specifically involved in familiarity level integration. Modality‐specific regions account for over 90% of the brain volume implicated in familiarity processing, whereas the multimodal regions‐ such as the pSTC and IFC‐ contribute less than 10% (Figure 10). Given prior evidence that both the pSTC and IFC exhibit multisensory properties (Awwad Shiekh Hasan et al. 2016; Tamber‐Rosenau et al. 2013) and are anatomically interconnected, we propose that these regions may form part of a hierarchical system for familiarity processing, with each contributing at distinct levels of abstraction. However, future research—such as single‐neuron or population‐level recordings—is required to clarify their specific roles and interactions within this proposed framework.

Although the current frequency‐tagging paradigm was designed to minimize mnemonic influences on familiarity perception by employing rapid stimulus presentation, the involvement of memory‐related processes cannot be entirely excluded. Activation was observed in brain regions previously associated with memory functions—such as the ventral precuneus gyrus and subparietal sulcus (Figure 4B) for familiar faces, and the posterior midcingulate cortex (Figure 6B) for familiar voices (Lee et al. 2013; Petit et al. 1998; Silson et al. 2019; Visconti di Visconti Oleggio Castello et al. 2017; Woolnough et al. 2020). Notably, our current findings showed that these regions were engaged in a modality‐specific manner, suggesting that visual and auditory systems may retain distinct processing pathways even at the level of memory‐based familiarity representations.

Conclusion

By examining familiarity processing, our study offers new insights into the neural mechanisms underlying person perception through faces and voices. The findings indicate that while familiarity is primarily processed in a modality‐specific manner within the visual and auditory systems, cross‐modal interactions also occur—potentially at different stages within a hierarchical system. Although our results provide novel evidence regarding the spatial organization of unimodal and multimodal regions involved in familiarity processing, further research is needed to clarify the specific roles of the pSTC and IFC in coding person familiarity, as well as their respective contributions to perceptual and cognitive processing. Moreover, future research could further investigate the typical development of these regions, as well as their potential reorganization in populations with atypical sensory experiences, such as individuals with visual or auditory deprivation (Mattioni et al. 2020).
