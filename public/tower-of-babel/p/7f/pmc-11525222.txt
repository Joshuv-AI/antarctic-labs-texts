Protocol for protein modification using oxalyl thioester-mediated chemoselective ligation
Authors: Francesco Terzani, Chen Wang, Simindokht Rostami, Rémi Desmet, Benoît Snella, Magalie Sénéchal, Birgit Wiltschi, Jérôme Vicogne, Oleg Melnyk, Vangelis Agouridas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe development of fast ligation chemistries for the site-specific modification of proteins has become a major focus in chemical biology. We describe steps for preparing an oxalyl thioester precursor in the form of an N-oxalyl perhydro-1,2,5-dithiazepine handle, i.e., the oxoSEA group, and incorporating it into a peptide modifier using solid phase peptide synthesis. We then detail procedures for its application for the modification of an N-terminal Cys-containing B1 domain of the streptococcal G protein using the native chemical ligation.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Snella et al.1

Before you begin

The modification of proteins through site-specific conjugation involves attaching custom-designed modifiers to precise locations on a protein of interest in order to modulate its biophysical properties, binding affinity or cellular functions.2,3 The key to achieving this precise control lies in the development of fast and chemoselective ligation chemistries. The native chemical ligation (NCL4,5) reaction, a popular synthetic method for chemoselective peptide bond formation between a thioester and a cysteinyl (Cys) peptide, has a strong asset for this purpose, in that proteins featuring an N-terminal Cys residue are easily accessible through classical recombinant techniques. Unfortunately, this reaction suffers limitations when applied to the modification of proteins at low concentrations because of the modest reactivity of conventional thioesters that makes the chemistry poorly efficient in the low micromolar range. In search for a simple solution to this problem, we recently introduced an approach based on oxalyl thioester precursors relying on the N-oxalyl perhydro-1,2,5-dithiazepine scaffold1,6 whose reactivity is reminiscent of the redox-controlled bis(2-sulfanylethyl)amide-based N,S-acyl transfer systems7,8,9 (Figure 1). From a mechanistic perspective, a peptide equipped with a latent oxalyl thioester handle is activated upon exposure to a reductant. Reduction of the disulfide bond in the perhydro-1,2,5-dithiazepine cycle (Figure 1A in green) enables an intramolecular rearrangement to occur and results in the formation of a highly activated transient oxalyl thioester. The latter chemoselectively reacts with β-aminothiol motifs such as N-terminal cysteine residues present in target proteins. Owing to the outstanding reactivity of oxalyl thioesters, this approach allows protein conjugation to be performed at fast rates (∼30 M–1 s–1) and at reactant concentrations in the high nanomolar range. In addition, the reactivity of the oxoSEA group being redox-controlled, ligation and protein modification can be triggered on demand upon exposure to thiol or phosphine reductants such as dithiothreitol (DTT) or tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP).Figure 1The chemistry of oxalyl thioester precursors(A) General principle of the oxoSEA-mediated protein ligation.(B) oxoSEA mediated modification of the K1 domain of HGF/SF equipped with an N-terminal cysteine residue.(C) oxoSEA-mediated modification of Lys48 oxoSEA-functionalized ubiquitin.

In previous works, we showed that the oxoSEA group can be conveniently placed on the side chain of a lysine residue and incorporated within peptide modifiers accessed through conventional solid phase approaches (Figure 1B).1 This strategy enabled the successful and highly efficient conjugation of the 10 kDa K1 domain of the hepatocyte growth factor (HGF-SF) with a V5 peptidic tag. Note that the two reactive functionalities are interchangeable, allowing for flexible design and broader applicability. For example, we also demonstrated that the oxoSEA group could be introduced within a protein partner by producing an oxoSEA-modified ubiquitin at Lys48 residue (Figure 1C).10

In the approach disclosed in this Protocol article, we describe a straightforward and high-yielding four step procedure for preparing a simplified oxalyl thioester precursor handle. This oxoSEA handle can be readily coupled at the N-terminus of peptide segments following peptide assembly and further serve as a latent thioester to modify N-terminal Cys-containing proteins. This last property is specifically illustrated by the preparation of an oxoSEA-modified V5 peptide and its conjugation to the B1 domain of the streptococcal G protein capped by a Cys residue at its N-terminus.

A streamlined method for preparing multigram quantities of 2-(1,2,5-dithiazepan-5-yl)-2-oxoacetic acid, i.e., oxoSEA handle 1, from commercial reagents has been optimized (Scheme 1). This method minimizes the number of purification steps required to isolate the target product. The yield of each step is reported as an average value with standard error based on the yields obtained for 3 to 5 replicates (95% confidence limit). oxoSEA-mediated ligation of V5 peptide 6 with Cys-GSGS-GB1 protein

To showcase the power of the oxoSEA method, we chose reaction conditions with only 100 μM of the Cys-GSGS-GB1 substrate and 120 μM of the V5 modifier. Interestingly, oxoSEA-mediated ligation already achieves satisfying rates in the common millimolar concentration range. This reactivity positions oxalyl thioester precursors competitively with classical aryl thioesters for NCL reactions (∼4 M−1 s−1).18,19 However, a surprising trend emerges when reactant concentrations, i.e., oxoSEA precursor and Cys peptide, are reduced to the micromolar range. The reaction rate can reach up to 30 M−1 s−1 from 100 μM reactant concentration and below. Therefore, such dilution conditions become particularly interesting, as they reveal a distinct advantage of our approach compared to classical NCL.

In addition, the original article by Snella et al. put forward the utilization of a nitrogen-regulated glovebox, which is not a common equipment in most chemistry and biology laboratories. Here, we present a more accessible alternative using a glovebag. Expected outcomes

Application of this procedure is expected to yield oxoSEA handle 1. Step-by-step analytical data are provided throughout the protocol. Additionally, processed NMR spectra are available in the supplementary material file for further comparison. The handle demonstrates versatility and can be attached not only at the N-terminus of a peptide segment as shown here but also to any aliphatic amine under similar reaction conditions.

The procedure provided herein also details how to perform an oxoSEA-mediated ligation reaction. Thanks to its chemoselectivity and efficiency, this process is suitable for preparative-scale modifications of N-terminal cysteine-containing proteins as well as in situ derivatization within complex mixtures as demonstrated by Snella et al.1 It can be of course applied to any type of substrate harboring a β-aminothiol motif, with the constraint that redox activation of the oxoSEA motif requires excess TCEP and that such conditions can lead to the unfolding of disulfide bond-stabilized proteins.

Limitations

Decreasing the concentration of reducing agents to a few equivalents (low millimolar range) might be imposed by constraints such as sample sensitivity or analytical limitations. While the reaction is robust under excess reducing agents and can proceed smoothly even without special precautions (in air), its sensitivity to oxygen becomes apparent when reducing agent concentrations are lowered. In such a case, conversion rates can significantly decrease, and rigorous inert conditions are crucial to maintain reaction efficiency.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Triphenylmethyl derivatives are notoriously known to sequester solvent molecules. During the synthesis of intermediate 3, 1H NMR analysis may evidence product contamination by substantial amounts of solvents (DMF and EtOAc). This can impair crystallization after evaporation.

Potential solution

If 1H NMR shows high contamination by DMF, dissolve the crude compound in EtOAc (∼10 mL per gram of intermediate 3). Wash the organic layer 6 to 8 times with 5% w/v aqueous solution of KH2PO4. Dry over MgSO4, filter and concentrate under reduced pressure. If a solid is not obtained, redissolve the residue in dichloromethane and evaporate to dryness. Repeat the last step until a yellowish powder is isolated (3 times might be necessary).

Problem 2

During the synthesis of intermediate 5, massive trityl byproducts precipitation can compromise efficient separation by silica gel column chromatography.

Potential solution

The crude mixture after extraction should be evaporated to a residual volume of ∼10–15 mL. Evaporating the organic layer to dryness is not recommended as trityl byproducts formed during the reaction will massively precipitate and severely complicate column loading. Addition of 10% (v/v) of DCM in the eluent is also important to prevent precipitation of trityl derivatives during column chromatography purification. For larger scale reactions (exceeding 30 mmol), consider increasing the DCM fraction in the eluent to 15% (v/v).

Problem 3

TLC analysis indicates complete conversion, yet the final product yield is low. This discrepancy suggests potential product loss during the final extraction of oxoSEA handle 1.

Potential solution

oxoSEA handle 1 can be discarded in the final aqueous layer after extraction. Ensure the pH of this layer is below 2. If necessary, adjust the pH with 4 M HCl instead of 1 M HCl for improved acidification and reduced aqueous layer volume.

Problem 4

The conversion of the oxoSEA-mediated ligation does not exceed 70%–80% at 100 μM and above reactant concentration.

Potential solution

The observed issue likely stems from a combination of factors. Here are two key areas to investigate.•Stoichiometric imbalance: Insufficient starting quantities of a reagent can lead to its premature depletion, thus limiting conversion. Kinetic monitoring by HPLC, UV determination of peptide concentration20 can reveal such depletion. In such a case, consider adjusting the stoichiometry.•Inert atmosphere negligence: Failure to maintain an inert atmosphere during the reaction can lead to reactants oxidation. Notably, the activated acyclic oxoSEA moiety can revert back to its cyclic and inert form through the formation of an intramolecular disulfide bond (Figure 1). Also, the side chain thiol of Cys residues can be inactivated through the formation of disulfides with MPAA or other Cys residues. These side reactions effectively kill the process and form unwanted products/adducts detectable by HPLC analysis.
