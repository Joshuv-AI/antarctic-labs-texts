Comprehensive evaluation of sacchachitosan-based transdermal films for wound care: Physical, chemical, and biological parameters
Authors: Sneha Paul, C. Sheela Sasikumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the present investigation, the development and comparative assessment of sacchachitosan-based transdermal films loaded with biosynthesized silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) for wound-healing applications were reported. Sacchachitosan, a fungal chitosan obtained from Ganoderma lucidum, was mixed with gelatin and glycerol to improve film flexibility, bio-adhesiveness, and moisture retention. Silver nanoparticles were added to the transdermal film as an antimicrobial agent and a cross-linking agent. Transdermal films were prepared by solvent casting with different concentrations of AgNPs, and characterised by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), High-Resolution Transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES). The optimized formulation showed tensile load values of 0.94 N (maximum), length up to 83.4 mm, with significantly increased mechanical flexibility. Controlled drug release was maintained for 24 h (R2 = 0.9471) and provided effective skin permeation kinetics (R2 = 0.9852). Antimicrobial studies showed inhibition zones of up to 22 mm for E. coli and Proteus, and about ≈20 mm for A. niger and C. albicans respectively, while degradation ranged from 52.3% to 81.9% under physiological conditions. Bioadhesive strength reached 79 g in higher concentration of SNPs formulations. The results of cytocompatibility demonstrated that >70% cell viability in optimal films (SF1-SF4), while excessive AgNPs concentration (SF5-SF7) decreased viability to below 30%, suggesting dose-dependent cytotoxicity. Hemolysis remained within acceptable limits in lower–concentration formulations. Mushroom produced sacchachitosan was found to exhibit better hydrophilicity (contact angle 40°) than traditional chitosan, higher antimicrobial activity and superior film stability. These findings highlight sacchachitosan-based nanocomposite films as promising sustainable biomaterials for advanced wound management, while emphasizing the need for nanoparticle optimization to balance antimicrobial efficacy and biocompatibility.

Introduction

Transdermal drug delivery systems (TDDS) have evolved beyond systemic drug administration to become multifunctional platforms for localized wound management [1]. Recent advances in biomaterial engineering [2] emphasize the development of bioactive, mechanically adaptive, and infection-resistant wound dressings capable of sustaining therapeutic release while maintaining an optimal healing microenvironment. Compared to traditional oral administration, TDDS provides notable benefits, such as bypassing hepatic first-pass metabolism, minimizing gastrointestinal side effects, enhancing patient adherence, and ensuring more consistent plasma drug levels without pronounced peaks and troughs [3,4].

Chronic wounds represent a rapidly growing healthcare burden, with global advanced wound care markets projected to exceed USD 30–40 billion by 2030 due to rising incidences of diabetes, aging populations, and surgical complications [5]. This increasing demand has intensified interest in cost-effective, sustainable, and high-performance wound dressings. As the skin serves as the primary protective barrier of the body, regulating moisture balance and preventing microbial invasion. Disruption of this barrier due to burns, trauma, or diabetic ulcers significantly impairs tissue regeneration [6] and increases susceptibility to infection. Although conventional wound dressings such as gauze, hydrocolloids, and synthetic polymer films provide basic protection, they frequently lack intrinsic antimicrobial activity, exhibit insufficient mechanical adaptability to dynamic skin movement, or fail to maintain balanced moisture and oxygen permeability [7,8]. Furthermore, many commercially available dressings rely on petroleum-based polymers or require chemical antimicrobial coatings, raising concerns regarding sustainability and long-term biocompatibility. These limitations highlight the need for biodegradable, bioactive, and mechanically robust alternatives [9,10].

Natural biomass-derived polymers have attracted increasing attention as sustainable wound-dressing materials. Cellulose- and starch-based hydrogels have demonstrated tunable swelling behavior and biodegradability; however, they often require extensive chemical modification to achieve antimicrobial functionality and may suffer from poor mechanical durability under exudative conditions [11]. In contrast, chitosan-based materials possess inherent antimicrobial and hemostatic properties due to their cationic amino groups, which enable electrostatic interactions with negatively charged microbial membranes [12]. Recent studies have demonstrated that chitosan nanocomposites enhance wound contraction, collagen deposition, and epithelialization. Nevertheless, most research has focused on crustacean-derived chitosan, which involves acid-intensive extraction processes due to the presence of many mineral deposits and may present allergenic concerns [13].

Mushroom-derived chitosan analogues, such as sacchachitosan obtained from Ganoderma lucidum, represent a promising yet underexplored alternative. However, crustacean-derived chitosan, which requires extensive acid-mediated demineralization due to high calcium carbonate content, whereas sacchachitosan contains significantly lower inorganic mineral fractions [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Consequently, extraction processes are comparatively milder and potentially more environmentally sustainable. Thus reducing the allergenic contamination. Additionally, sacchachitosan exhibits distinct crystalline patterns and potentially enhanced hydrogen-bonding interactions, which may contribute to improved film integrity, flexibility, and bioadhesive properties [18]. Despite these potential advantages, systematic studies evaluating sacchachitosan-based transdermal films compared with conventional chitosan systems remain limited [19].

Incorporation of bioactive nanoparticles is an effective modification strategy to enhance wound dressing functionality. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were synthesized by the biological method using G. lucidum and have demonstrated broad-spectrum antimicrobial efficacy through membrane disruption and reactive oxygen species-mediated bacterial inactivation. Green synthesis using medicinal mushroom extracts not only reduces the use of toxic reagents but also introduces bioactive phytochemicals that may synergistically enhance therapeutic performance [20,21]. However, increasing silver nanoparticle concentration may induce oxidative stress and cytotoxicity, emphasizing the importance of optimizing nanoparticle loading to balance antimicrobial efficiency with cellular compatibility.

Gelatin, a collagen-derived biopolymer, mimics extracellular matrix components and enhances cell adhesion, elasticity, and moisture retention. When combined with chitosan derivatives, gelatin forms polyelectrolyte complexes that improve mechanical stability and controlled drug release behaviour [22]. The integration of sacchachitosan, gelatin, and biosynthesized AgNPs may therefore produce a multifunctional composite film capable of providing structural support, infection control, and sustained therapeutic delivery.

Given the increasing prevalence of chronic wounds and the demand for sustainable biomedical materials, fungal-derived chitosan systems represent a promising alternative to conventional sources. In this study, sacchachitosan–gelatin nanocomposite transdermal films incorporated with green-synthesized silver nanoparticles were fabricated and systematically evaluated. Physicochemical properties, mechanical performance, antimicrobial efficacy, degradation behaviour, and cytocompatibility were analysed across varying nanoparticle concentrations to identify an optimized balance between antimicrobial activity and biocompatibility. This work provides experimental insight into the potential of mushroom-derived sacchachitosan as a sustainable component for multifunctional wound dressing systems.

Biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles

Preparation of G. lucidum extract

G. lucidum mushroom was collected, washed, and dried till it was hard. The mushroom was shredded and mixed with double-distilled water at a ratio of 20 g to 100 mL. The obtained solution was boiled for 2 min, filtered, and stored at 4 °C until further use [23].

Preparation of the silver nanoparticles

A total of 35 mg of silver nitrate was added to 250 mL of water, and the mixture was stirred until uniform. To obtain silver colloids, 10 mL of the mushroom extract and 90 mL of the silver nitrate solution were mixed and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The presence of silver nanoparticles was confirmed by the appearance of a light yellow to dark brown colour upon complete reduction. The solution's pH was adjusted to around 7.5. The sample was analysed by UV-visible spectroscopy to detect silver nanoparticles [24,25]. The confirmation of silver nanoparticles was done by using various analytical techniques such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM), energy dispersive X-ray analysis (EDAX), Field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD).

Isolation, extraction, and purification of sacchachitosan

The mushrooms were initially rinsed thoroughly with sterile tap water followed by distilled water to remove surface contaminants, then chopped into small pieces and dried in a hot-air oven at 40–50 °C for 24 h. The dried samples were pulverized using a mechanical grinder to obtain a fine mesh-like powder. Approximately 100 g of this powder was subjected to deproteinization by treating it with 1 N sodium hydroxide at 100 °C for 5 h to remove protein content. The residue was filtered and bleached using 35% hydrogen peroxide across various pH conditions for 24 h, followed by repeated washing with deionized water until a neutral pH was achieved [19,20]. The resulting sacchachitin was sieved and oven-dried at 45 °C. For deacetylation, the dried sacchachitin was treated with 50% sodium hydroxide at 110 °C for 5 h, followed by another round of bleaching using 35% hydrogen peroxide at 90 °C for 2–3 h. After filtration and washing with deionized water, a pale yellow fibrous crude sacchachitosan was obtained. This crude product was purified by dissolving it in 2% lactic acid and filtering to remove insoluble residues. The filtrate was then precipitated using 1 N sodium hydroxide under continuous stirring, and the precipitate was washed with distilled water to neutral pH [26]. Finally, the purified sacchachitosan was freeze-dried into powder form for further application and characterization is performed by FTIR (Fourier transmission infrared) and X-ray diffraction (XRD).

Characterization and evaluation of SF transdermal film

The SF transdermal film was initially stabilized by immersing it in a 4% glutaraldehyde solution for 2 h, followed by a series of washes to eliminate any excess reagent, and then the films were air-dried. Surface morphology was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM), while silver content in the film was quantified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) and functional groups presence analysed by FTIR.

Physiochemical properties of SF transdermal film

Thickness and weight

The thickness of the SF films was measured at multiple locations using a digital vernier screw gauge (Ultra Science Aids, Bangalore, India), and the average value was calculated to ensure uniformity [28].

Water solubility

To evaluate solubility, small sections of each film were immersed in 1 mL of distilled water and incubated for 24 h. Observations were recorded hourly to assess dissolution behavior [28].

Water absorptivity and humidity response

Six film samples (1 cm each) were initially weighed and soaked in distilled water for one week. Three were exposed to ambient humidity, and the remaining three were subjected to saturated humidity conditions at room temperature for two days [28]. Moisture uptake was quantified using the formula:% Moisture Absorption = ((Final Weight - Initial Weight) / Initial Weight) × 100

Temperature and pH initial weight

The thermal resilience of the films was tested by immersing them in a hot water bath across a temperature range of 10–100 °C. pH tolerance was also assessed to determine the structural integrity of the films under varying conditions [28].

Tensile strength

Mechanical properties such as tensile strength and elongation at break were determined using an Instron universal testing machine (Model 5565, Instron Engineering Corporation, USA). Rectangular strips (2.54–15.00 cm) were cut using a precision cutter (Model LB.02/A, Metrotec, Spain). Tests were conducted at a crosshead speed of 50 mm/min, and multiple specimens from each film type were tested to generate stress-strain curves [28].

Folding endurance

Each film was repeatedly folded at the same location up to 250 times or until visible cracking occurred. This helped evaluate the film's flexibility and endurance [28].

In vitro drug release profile

The drug release behavior was assessed using a modified in vitro diffusion method to quantify the drug concentration released through the film over time [[29], [30], [31]].

In vitro skin permeability assay

Chicken skin was used as a model for permeability studies. Fat and hair were removed by soaking the skin in 0.32 M ammonia solution for 1 h. The cleaned skin was mounted on a 1000 mL glass beaker filled with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the transdermal film was placed over the skin. At 4-h intervals, 1 mL samples were withdrawn for UV-Vis spectrophotometric analysis at 376 nm to determine drug diffusion across the skin barrier.

In vitro drug release from polymer matrix

Drug release from the biopolymer matrix was further investigated using a dialysis bag technique. The liquid formulation was introduced into a sealed dialysis bag, one end of which was attached to a magnetic stirrer for continuous motion. Samples (1 mL) were collected every 4 h and analysed at 376 nm using UV-Vis spectrophotometry.

Oxygen permeability

Six SF films were randomly selected and placed over the mouth of flasks containing 200 mL of water. A tightly sealed flask served as the negative control (blocking oxygen), and an open flask served as the positive control (allowing oxygen entry). After 24 h in ambient conditions, the dissolved oxygen content in each flask was measured using Winkler's titration method [32,33].

Contact angle measurement

The wettability of the transdermal films was assessed by determining the static contact angle of water droplets on their surface. This was carried out manually using a contact angle meter. Film samples were cut into 2 × 2 cm squares, and the angles formed by the water droplets were measured and recorded to evaluate the hydrophilic or hydrophobic nature of the films [32,33].

Transparency assessment

The transparency of the transdermal films was evaluated by placing each film over a sheet of printed text to visually assess the clarity and light transmission through the material.

Wet integrity

Film stability in aqueous conditions was visually assessed by immersing the films in 60 mL of distilled water in a 100 mL beaker. The beaker was gently swirled for 1 min without forming a vortex, and the film's structural integrity was evaluated by direct observation [34].

Bio-adhesive strength assessment

The in vitro evaluation of the films' bio-adhesive strength was carried out using a simple pulley-based setup. Defatted and dehaired chicken skin was selected as a model for the moist environment of a fresh wound. The freshly excised skin was thoroughly cleaned with acetone and 2% ammonia solution. A saline-moistened transdermal film (CF or SF) was gently placed on the prepared chicken skin. The adhesive force was assessed by gradually adding water to a polypropylene sheet attached to the pulley system until the film detached from the skin. The weight of the water required to cause detachment was recorded as a measure of the film's bio-adhesiveness [35,36].

In vitro degradation assay

The degradation behavior of the transdermal films (CF and SF series) was evaluated in vitro using 40 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) supplemented with 1.6 μg/mL human serum. From each formulation batch, six patches were prepared by cutting the films into 1 cm × 1 cm sections. These samples were then immersed in the buffer-serum solution and incubated in a shaking water bath at 37 ± 0.5 °C for 2 h. After incubation, the films were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water and dried in an oven at 80 ± 0.5 °C. The extent of degradation was assessed by calculating the percentage of remaining weight using the formula:Remaining weight (%) = (Wd / Wo) × 100Where: Wo is the initial weight of the film, Wd is the final dry weight after degradation.

Biological properties of SF transdermal film

Hemocompatibility evaluation

Thrombus formation test

Transdermal patches measuring 2 × 2 cm were cut from each batch (total of six). These patches were soaked in normal saline for 24 h at 37 °C to allow swelling. Following this, 0.5 mL of ACD (acid-citrate-dextrose) blood and 0.03% calcium chloride (4 M) were added to induce thrombus formation. To terminate the reaction, distilled water was added. After 10 min, the samples were fixed using 36% formaldehyde, rinsed with distilled water, air-dried, and then weighed to assess the mass of the thrombus formed. The same procedure was performed on a glass surface to serve as a control, and the thrombus weight was recorded for comparison [34].

Hemolysis assay

Six film samples (2 × 2 cm) were immersed in 10 mL of saline and incubated at 37 ± 2 °C for 30 min. Then, 0.2 mL of ACD blood was added to each test tube and incubated for 60 min. For the positive control, 0.2 mL of ACD blood was mixed with 0.5 mL of 0.01 N hydrochloric acid; for the negative control, 0.2 mL of ACD blood was combined with 10 mL of saline and similarly incubated. After centrifugation, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 545 nm [34]. The percentage of hemolysis was calculated using the following formula:Hemolysis (%) =(Absorbance of test sample – Absorbance of negative control) / (Absorbance of positive control – Absorbance of negative control) × 100

Antibacterial activity

Microbial penetration test

To assess the microbial barrier property of the films, each transdermal patch was placed as a cap over the opening of a vial containing 5 mL of nutrient broth. For comparison, the positive control vials were left uncapped, while the negative control vials were covered with sterile cotton plugs. All samples were kept undisturbed for 7 days, and turbidity in the broth was used as an indicator of microbial contamination [34].

Well diffusion assays

The antibacterial efficacy of the samples was tested against human pathogenic bacterial strains. Nutrient agar medium was prepared and poured into sterile Petri dishes. Once solidified, 1 mL of bacterial culture was evenly spread over the agar surface using sterile cotton swabs. For the well diffusion assay, six wells (approximately 10 mm in diameter) were aseptically created using a cork borer, and each well was filled with varying concentrations of the SF transdermal film extract. All plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, after which the zones of inhibition were measured to evaluate antibacterial activity [27].

Cell line maintenance

The Vero cell line was sourced from the King's Institute, Chennai, and the 3T3-L1 fibroblast cell line was procured from the National Center for Cell Sciences, Pune. Both cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 μg/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin and then placed in a CO2 incubator.

MTT assay

The MTT assay was utilized to evaluate the cytotoxicity of the SF transdermal films. This colorimetric test is based on the conversion of MTT dye into an insoluble formazan product by mitochondrial dehydrogenase enzymes in metabolically active cells. Cells were seeded in 96-well flat-bottom plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL and treated with varying concentrations of the SF transdermal film. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Post incubation, 10 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well and incubated again for 4 h. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved using 100 μL of a dissolving solution from the assay kit. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm using an ELISA plate reader. All experiments were performed in triplicate. The IC50 value, representing the concentration of the transdermal film that caused 50% cell viability reduction, was then calculated [37,38].

Preparation of DCF-DA dye

A solution of 5 mmol/L DCF-DA was prepared by dissolving it in 5 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), followed by mixing with 0.1 mmol/L Evans blue dye. The dye mixture was stored at −20 °C in the dark to maintain stability.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) detection assay

DCF-DA, a widely used fluorescent probe for detecting intracellular ROS, was employed for this assay. Once inside the cell, DCF-DA is converted to fluorescent DCF upon oxidation. Fibroblast cells at 60%–70% confluence were treated with various concentrations of SF transdermal films. Control and negative control wells (treated with H2O2) were also included. After a 6-h incubation in a CO2 incubator, 2 mL of DCF-DA dye was added to each well and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. The wells were rinsed with PBS, air-dried, and examined under a fluorescence microscope using a blue filter (485–590 nm wavelength range) [39].

Statistical analysis

All data related to the physicochemical characterization and application of transdermal films were expressed as mean ± SEM (n = 6). Statistical comparisons were made using Coefficient of Variation, correlation and regression via Ms-Excel, GraphPad Prism version 6.00 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA).

Results

Synthesis and characterization of silver nanoparticles

The biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) was achieved using the aqueous extract of Ganoderma lucidum, as evidenced by a visible color transition from pale yellow to dark brown, characteristic of surface plasmon resonance (SPR). Extraction and validation of sacchachitosan

Sacchachitosan was extracted from Ganoderma lucidum through sequential deproteinization, bleaching, deacetylation, and purification steps. The yield of sacchachitin obtained was 41% relative to the dried mushroom biomass, and the final sacchachitosan product was recovered as a fine powder suitable for film casting. The extracted sacchachitosan exhibited a moisture content of 5.6%, ash value of 5%, loss on drying of 1.75%, and a pH of 6.7. Solubility analysis demonstrated that sacchachitosan was insoluble in water, ethanol, and alkaline solutions, while showing high solubility (90–95%) in lactic acid, confirming its cationic nature and effective deacetylation. These values fall within the reported range for fungal-derived chitosans and indicate satisfactory purification with minimal inorganic residues. The degree of deacetylation (DDA) was estimated using the FTIR absorbance ratio method and was calculated to be 63.48%, indicating substantial conversion of acetyl groups to free amino groups during alkaline treatment. FTIR spectra (Fig. 2A) showed characteristic absorption bands at 3475.3 cm−1 (O–H/N–H stretching), 1652.8 cm−1 (amide I, CO stretching), 1439.1 cm−1 (C–N stretching), and strong bands between 1220 and 1020 cm−1 corresponding to free amino groups (–NH2) at the C2 position of glucosamine, a defining structural feature of chitosan. These peaks confirm successful deacetylation and preservation of the polysaccharide backbone. XRD analysis (Fig. 2B) revealed broad diffraction peaks at 2θ = 11°, 19.9°, and 30.8°. The peak at 19.9° corresponds to semi-crystalline chitosan, while the additional peak at 30.8° has been reported in certain fungal-derived chitosans, indicating structural variation compared with crustacean-derived chitosan. The calculated crystallinity index was 90.76%, reflecting a highly ordered semi-crystalline structure following deacetylation. The FTIR spectrum of the SF transdermal film (Fig. 4B) displayed characteristic absorption bands corresponding to the constituent components. A broad band centered at 3448.2 cm−1 was attributed to overlapping O–H and N–H stretching vibrations. Physiochemical properties of SF transdermal film

The average weight of SF transdermal films was measured across six zones per formulation to evaluate uniformity in drug and polymer distribution. As shown in Fig. 5A, mean weights ranged from 10.75 mg (SF7) to 24.57 mg (SF5). Among all formulations, SF5 exhibited the highest average weight, whereas SF7 had the lowest. The standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV%) were calculated to assess distribution uniformity. Formulations SF7 (CV = 9.01%) and SF5 (CV = 10.75%) showed excellent and very good uniformity, respectively, indicating consistent polymer and drug dispersion. SF3 and SF4 also demonstrated acceptable uniformity, with CVs below 15%. In contrast, SF1 and SF2 presented significantly higher variability (CVs = 30.69% and 25.38%, respectively), suggesting uneven distribution across the film surface. Such variability can impact dosage consistency and drug release kinetics. SF6 showed moderate variability (CV = 19.87%). These results emphasize the importance of optimizing the film casting and drying process to achieve consistent film thickness and weight, which are critical for uniform therapeutic efficacy.Fig. 5Physio-chemical properties of transdermal films. Discussion

The present study demonstrates the successful integration of sacchachitosan, gelatin, and biosynthesized silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) into a transdermal film matrix with favorable physicochemical, mechanical, and biological properties, underscoring their potential as advanced wound dressing materials.

Synthesis and characterization of silver nanoparticles

The biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles using Ganoderma lucidum extract was confirmed by a distinct surface plasmon resonance peak at 420 nm, consistent with spherical AgNP formation in biologically mediated systems [40,41]. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed a polydisperse suspension with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.707 and a Z-average diameter of 60.2 nm, indicating a heterogeneous nanoparticle population with nanoscale predominance and a minor fraction of aggregates. The slightly larger size observed in DLS compared to electron microscopy can be attributed to measurement of the hydrodynamic diameter, which includes the solvation layer and potential particle clustering in suspension [42]. The size distribution was primarily dominated by particles in the 20–30 nm range, which are widely reported to exhibit enhanced antimicrobial activity due to increased surface-to-volume ratio and improved interaction with microbial membranes. The presence of a small aggregate fraction is typical in green synthesis systems, where natural biomolecules simultaneously function as reducing and stabilizing agents.

FTIR spectra showed characteristic bands at 3414 cm−1 (O–H stretching) and 1635 cm−1 (amide I, CO stretching), indicating the participation of fungal polysaccharides and proteins in nanoparticle reduction and stabilization [43]. The crystalline metallic nature of the nanoparticles was confirmed by XRD peaks at 2θ values of 38.1°, 44.3°, 64.5°, and 77.4°, corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of face-centered cubic silver (JCPDS 04-0783). TEM images further validated nanoscale dimensions (10–50 nm) and spherical morphology. The predominance of nanoscale crystalline particles suggests compatibility for uniform dispersion within the sacchachitosan–gelatin film matrix.

Extraction and characterization of sacchachitosan

Sacchachitosan extracted from Ganoderma lucidum exhibited physicochemical characteristics consistent with fungal-derived chitosan analogues. The extraction yield of sacchachitin (41%) and recovery of sacchachitosan as a fine film-forming powder demonstrate efficient biomass utilization and suitability for polymer processing. The material showed high solubility in lactic acid (90–95%) and insolubility in water and ethanol, confirming its cationic nature and successful deacetylation.

The degree of deacetylation (DDA), determined using the FTIR absorbance ratio method, was 63.48%, indicating substantial conversion of acetyl groups to free amino functionalities. In comparison, crustacean-derived chitosan isolated from prawn shell waste has been reported to exhibit DDA values of up to 90.47% [29]. While higher DDA is often associated with increased charge density and solubility, excessively deacetylated chitosan may exhibit reduced chain flexibility due to intensified intermolecular hydrogen bonding. The moderate DDA observed in sacchachitosan may therefore provide a balanced acetylation profile, potentially contributing to improved film elasticity and controlled swelling behavior by modulating polymer chain interactions. These structural distinctions indicate that sacchachitosan is not merely an alternative source of chitosan but a structurally modified biopolymer with distinct crystallographic behavior.

FTIR spectra revealed characteristic absorption bands at 3475.3 cm−1 (O–H/N–H stretching), 1652.8 cm−1 (amide I), and 1439.1 cm−1 (C–N stretching), with prominent signals in the 1220–1020 cm−1 region corresponding to free amino groups at the C2 position of glucosamine. These spectral features confirm the preservation of the polysaccharide backbone following alkaline deacetylation and support the polymer's structural integrity [44].

XRD analysis showed broad diffraction peaks at 2θ = 11° and 19.9°, characteristic of semi-crystalline chitosan. Notably, an additional diffraction peak at 30.8° was observed, which has been reported in certain fungal-derived chitosans but is not typically present in prawn-shell chitosan [45]. The calculated crystallinity index of 90.76% indicates a highly ordered semi-crystalline network despite the moderate DDA. This structural organisation may contribute to enhanced mechanical integrity, as reflected by the maximum load of 0.94 N and improved bioadhesive strength (up to 79 g) observed in optimized SF formulations.

Beyond structural differences, fungal sacchachitosan offers practical and translational advantages. Unlike crustacean chitosan, which requires extensive acid-mediated demineralization to remove calcium carbonate, fungal biomass contains significantly lower inorganic mineral content. This reduces chemical consumption during extraction, potentially lowering environmental impact and production costs. Moreover, fungal-derived polymers eliminate concerns about shellfish-derived allergenic contaminants, thereby enhancing their suitability for biomedical and wound care applications [46].

Collectively, the moderate DDA (63.48%), high crystallinity (90.76%), distinctive diffraction profile (30.8°), and strong acid solubility (90–95%) demonstrate that sacchachitosan is structurally differentiated fungal-derived chitosan variant with suitable properties for the fabrication of mechanically stable and biologically compatible transdermal films [47].

Physicochemical parameters

The progressive modulation of physicochemical properties across SF1–SF7 highlights the synergistic interaction between sacchachitosan, gelatin, glycerol, and AgNPs. FTIR spectra confirmed hydrogen bonding (3448 cm−1) and amide I band overlap (∼1638 cm−1), indicating formation of a polyelectrolyte complex between protonated amine groups of sacchachitosan and carboxyl groups of gelatin [48]. Similar electrostatic reinforcement mechanisms have been reported in chitosan–gelatin composite systems, where interpolymer complexation enhances structural cohesion and mechanical performance [49]. Unlike conventional crustacean chitosan systems, the fungal sacchachitosan matrix demonstrated high crystallinity (90.76%) despite a moderate DDA (63.48%), suggesting a structurally ordered yet flexible polymer network. This balance may explain the enhanced elasticity observed in advanced formulations.

Film weight ranged from 10.75 mg (SF7) to 24.57 mg (SF5), with optimized formulations (SF5 and SF7) showing acceptable uniformity (CV < 11%). Thickness values ranged from 0.356 mm (SF3) to 0.66 mm (SF4), with SF7 exhibiting the lowest variability (CV = 4.1%). Uniform thickness is critical in transdermal systems, as variability directly affects drug diffusion path length and release kinetics [50,51]. The improved uniformity observed in later formulations suggests better polymer dispersion and matrix stabilization with increasing AgNP incorporation.

A concentration-dependent increase in solubility was observed, rising from 13.43% (SF2) to 69.0% (SF7). This enhancement can be attributed to increased exposure of hydrophilic functional groups and improved polymer chain mobility in the presence of glycerol and dispersed nanoparticles. Similar swelling enhancement has been reported in nanoparticle-reinforced chitosan films, where nanoparticles disrupt polymer chain packing and increase water accessibility [52,53]. However, while solubility increased, humidity retention decreased from 73.21% (SF1) to 26.72% (SF7), indicating that higher AgNP concentrations may promote tighter network formation and reduced free water retention. This inverse relationship between swelling and water retention has been described in crosslinked hydrogel systems, where increased crosslink density reduces free volume [54]. Water absorption ranged from 32% to 52%, with SF4 exhibiting optimal uptake (∼52%), suggesting balanced swelling capacity without excessive matrix loosening. Controlled swelling is advantageous in wound dressings, as it supports exudate absorption while maintaining structural stability. Therefore, SF4 appears to provide a favorable compromise between hydration capacity and matrix stability.

Thermal analysis revealed progressive enhancement in structural resilience. Early formulations (SF1–SF3) began dispersing at 60 °C, whereas SF7 remained stable up to 80–90 °C. Similarly, all films maintained integrity within physiological pH (6–8), confirming suitability for dermal application. The enhanced stability of SF6 and SF7 is likely attributable to increased interpolymer interactions and nanoparticle-mediated physical crosslinking. Nanoparticle-mediated stabilization has been widely reported in nanocomposite films, where nanoparticles act as physical crosslinking points and restrict polymer mobility [55].

All films remained structurally stable over a physiological pH range (6–8), confirming their suitability for dermal application. Loss of integrity at extreme pH likely results from protonation/deprotonation of sacchachitosan amine groups and gelatin carboxyl groups, consistent with previously reported chitosan hydrogel behaviour [56].

Mechanical analysis revealed a near doubling of load-bearing capacity, from 0.42 N (SF1) to 0.94 N (SF6). Young's modulus increased to 0.0175 MPa in SF6, indicating enhanced stiffness. Folding endurance improved from 115 folds (SF1) to 310 folds (SF7), demonstrating improved resilience.

Nanoparticles are known to function as nano-fillers that enhance stress transfer across polymer matrices by creating additional interfacial bonding sites [57]. The increased tensile strength and elongation at break (83.4 mm in SF7) suggest that AgNPs not only reinforce the matrix but also maintain elasticity, likely due to combined hydrogen bonding and nanoparticle–polymer coordination interactions [58].

Contact angle decreased from 54.2° (SF1) to 40° (SF6), indicating increased hydrophilicity. Enhanced wettability improves interfacial adhesion with moist wound surfaces, supporting prolonged dermal retention. Correspondingly, bioadhesive strength increased from 28.1 g (SF1) to 79.0 g (SF7).

Hydrophilic polymer matrices enhance adhesion through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interaction with tissue proteins, consistent with mucoadhesive chitosan systems reported by Agarwal & Mishra [59].

Oxygen permeability ranged from 10.24 mg to 14.23 mg, with SF4 approaching the positive control (16.6 mg). Adequate oxygen transmission is essential for aerobic metabolism and collagen synthesis during wound repair [60]. Excessively dense films may impede oxygen diffusion, whereas highly porous systems may compromise barrier function. The balanced permeability observed in SF4 suggests an optimized microstructure.

Transparency across all formulations further enhances clinical utility by enabling visual monitoring without film removal, reducing mechanical disturbance of healing tissue.

Degradation studies indicated that higher AgNP content reduced film breakdown, extending film stability under physiological conditions. This is advantageous for sustained drug delivery, aligning with observations [61] that stable matrices allow prolonged release kinetics. SF4 exhibited the most balanced profile; sufficient degradation coupled with effective drug release, validated by both the dialysis and skin permeation assays, which showed steady, controlled release behavior.

Biological parameters

Hemocompatibility is a critical requirement for wound-contact materials, particularly in acute or bleeding wounds. The thrombus formation assay demonstrated a concentration-dependent increase in clot weight, rising from 0.025 g (SF1) to 0.071 g (SF7), compared to 0.09 g in the positive control. Early formulations (SF1–SF3) exhibited significantly lower clot formation, suggesting minimal interference with coagulation pathways. This behavior may be attributed to the relatively lower nanoparticle density and smoother polymer surface, which reduce platelet adhesion and activation [62].

Similarly, hemolysis increased progressively from 21% (SF1) to 77% (SF7). Although ASTM F756-00 standards classify materials with <5% hemolysis as non-hemolytic and 5–25% as slightly hemolytic, the elevated values observed in higher formulations indicate that excessive nanoparticle concentration may compromise erythrocyte membrane integrity. Silver nanoparticles are known to interact with phospholipid bilayers, inducing membrane permeability changes and hemoglobin leakage in a dose-dependent manner [63]. The observed increase in hemolysis in SF6 and SF7 is consistent with reports that nanoparticle aggregation or high surface charge density enhances red blood cell membrane disruption. Therefore, while AgNP incorporation enhances antimicrobial efficacy, its concentration must be carefully controlled to maintain blood compatibility.

The antimicrobial analysis revealed a clear dose-dependent enhancement in activity across the SF formulations. Notably, SF6 and SF7 exhibited inhibition zones up to 22 mm against E. coli and Proteus sp., and greater than 22 mm against Aspergillus niger, surpassing the activity observed for silver nanoparticles alone (10–12 mm). This enhanced antimicrobial efficacy likely arises from synergistic interactions among the film components. Sacchachitosan contributes through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged microbial membranes, leading to cell wall disruption, while AgNPs induce membrane destabilization, protein denaturation, and DNA damage. Gelatin, although not inherently antimicrobial, supports a moist wound environment that facilitates sustained nanoparticle contact with microbial cells. Previous studies on chitosan–silver nanocomposites have reported inhibition zones in the range of 12–18 mm [64,65], suggesting that the sacchachitosan–gelatin matrix in the present system enhances nanoparticle dispersion and microbial interface interaction. Importantly, all SF films demonstrated complete microbial barrier function, with no turbidity observed over a 7-day incubation period, confirming that the dense polymer network effectively prevents pathogen penetration while simultaneously providing active antimicrobial action. This combined protective and therapeutic functionality is consistent with recent developments in biomass-derived advanced wound dressings [66]. Importantly, the films also demonstrated complete microbial barrier function (no turbidity over 7 days), confirming that the dense polymer network effectively prevents pathogen penetration while providing active antimicrobial action. This dual protective and therapeutic role aligns with recent advances in biomass-derived wound dressings.

The MTT assay demonstrated a clear dose-dependent response. Formulations SF1–SF4 maintained 70–80% cell viability, indicating acceptable cytocompatibility. In contrast, SF6 and SF7 reduced viability below 30%, suggesting significant cytotoxicity at higher nanoparticle concentrations.

These findings are consistent with literature demonstrating that low concentrations of AgNPs promote antimicrobial efficacy while maintaining fibroblast viability, whereas higher doses induce mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis [52,67]. Silver nanoparticles are known to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential, and activate apoptotic pathways in a concentration-dependent manner. For wound healing applications, maintaining fibroblast viability is essential for collagen deposition and tissue remodelling [68]. Therefore, formulations exhibiting >70% viability (SF1–SF4) are more suitable for clinical translation.

The DCF-DA assay further supported these findings. Minimal fluorescence in SF1–SF4 indicates low ROS generation, comparable to untreated controls. However, SF5–SF7 showed significantly elevated green fluorescence, confirming increased intracellular ROS levels. AgNP-induced oxidative stress is widely documented and involves the generation of superoxide radicals, hydroxyl radicals, and hydrogen peroxide through redox cycling mechanisms [69,70]. Excessive ROS disrupts cellular redox homeostasis, leading to lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, and apoptosis.

The correlation between increased ROS and reduced MTT viability confirms that cytotoxicity in higher formulations is likely mediated through oxidative stress pathways. While ROS generation contributes to antimicrobial efficacy, excessive oxidative stress may impair wound healing by damaging host cells.

Collectively, the biological assays highlight a clear formulation-dependent trade-off between antimicrobial efficacy and biocompatibility. Early formulations (SF1–SF3) exhibited moderate antimicrobial activity but demonstrated comparatively better hemocompatibility and higher cytocompatibility. In contrast, advanced formulations (SF6–SF7) showed very strong antimicrobial performance and enhanced mechanical strength; however, these benefits were accompanied by significantly elevated hemolysis levels (68–77%) and markedly reduced fibroblast viability (<30%), indicating potential safety concerns at higher nanoparticle concentrations.

Among all formulations, SF4 emerged as the most balanced system. It demonstrated strong antimicrobial activity with inhibition zones ranging from 14 to 18 mm, while maintaining acceptable hemocompatibility and preserving approximately 70% fibroblast viability. Furthermore, ROS generation in SF4 remained controlled compared to higher-concentration formulations, suggesting reduced oxidative stress-mediated cytotoxicity.

These findings emphasize the importance of optimizing nanoparticle concentration to achieve an effective antimicrobial response without compromising cellular compatibility, particularly for wound healing applications where both infection control and tissue regeneration are critical.

Despite the promising physicochemical and biological performance demonstrated in vitro, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, scalability of sacchachitosan extraction from fungal biomass requires further optimization to ensure batch-to-batch consistency and industrial feasibility. Second, long-term stability of silver nanoparticle dispersion within the polymer matrix, including potential aggregation, silver ion release kinetics, and shelf-life under varied environmental conditions, was not evaluated in this study. Finally, although in vitro hemocompatibility and cytotoxicity assessments provide preliminary safety insights, comprehensive in vivo toxicological evaluation and regulatory compliance studies will be essential before clinical translation, particularly given the dose-dependent effects observed at higher nanoparticle concentrations.

Conclusion

Sacchachitosan isolated from Ganoderma lucidum demonstrated structural suitability for biomedical application, with a degree of deacetylation of 63.48% and a crystallinity index of 90.76%. Sacchachitosan–gelatin transdermal films incorporated with biosynthesized silver nanoparticles exhibited concentration-dependent improvements in mechanical strength (maximum load up to 0.94 N; folding endurance up to 310 cycles), sustained drug release over 24 h (R2 = 0.9471 dialysis; R2 = 0.9852 skin permeation), and enhanced antimicrobial activity (inhibition zones up to 22 mm against Gram-negative bacteria and >22 mm against fungal strains). However, increased nanoparticle loading was associated with elevated hemolysis (up to 77%) and reduced fibroblast viability (<30%), indicating a balance between antimicrobial potency and cytocompatibility. Formulations SF3–SF4 provided a more favorable overall profile, maintaining >70% cell viability with effective antimicrobial performance. These findings support fungal-derived sacchachitosan as a promising alternative to conventional chitosan for transdermal wound applications. However, the present study is limited to physicochemical and in vitro biological evaluation. In vitro wound scratch assays and in vivo wound healing studies have been conducted as part of a continuation study and will be reported separately to further validate translational potential.
