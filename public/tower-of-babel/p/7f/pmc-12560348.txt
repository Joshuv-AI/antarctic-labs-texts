Evaluation of bone regenerative potential of sticky bone combined with DPBM and APRF in a rabbit tibial model: an in vivo study
Authors: Yizhuo Shu, Liwei Wu, Wanqian Zheng, Saijie Qi, Yeting Yang, Yiyang Chen, Hongyu Zhu, Toru Ogawa, Chuan Lv, James K H Tsoi, Sheng Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone regeneration often relies on biomaterials to promote osteogenesis and modulate inflammation. Growth-factor–rich matrices and xenogeneic bone scaffolds have been reported to influence bone formation, vascularisation, and immune responses in vivo. This study therefore compared four biomaterials for their bone-regenerative potential in a rabbit non-critical tibial defect model: (1)Deproteinized porcine bone mineral (DPBM) (2)APRF (Advanced Platelet-Rich Fibrin) (3)Sticky Bone (STB, formed by combining DPBM and APRF) (4)Bovine collagen scaffolds (CS).MethodsThirty 15-week-old male New Zealand White rabbits were assigned to five groups: empty control (EC), DPBM, APRF, STB, and CS. Bilateral tibial defects (2.2 mm in diameter, 6 mm in depth) were filled with the respective materials and covered with porcine collagen membranes. Assessments at 2 and 6 weeks included micro-CT, histology, and immunohistochemistry (Runx2, ALP, CD31, TNF-α) to evaluate bone formation, vascularization, and inflammation.ResultsSTB exhibited consistently greater new bone fill and a stronger trabecular framework, alongside elevated Runx2 and ALP levels, indicating enhanced osteogenic capacity. Concurrently, higher CD31 expression and lower TNF-α suggested improved vascularization and attenuated inflammation. By contrast, collagen alone showed limited osteoinduction, whereas APRF alone provided mild early bone promotion. Taken together, these findings highlight STB’s enhanced bone-regenerative capacity. However, no statistically significant differences in new bone formation were observed between STB and DPBM at all time points. Notably, at the 2-week mark, STB demonstrated a more pronounced anti-inflammatory effect than DPBM, reflected by significantly lower TNF-α levels.ConclusionsSTB demonstrated a pronounced capability for promoting bone formation and reducing inflammation, suggesting its potential as an effective biomaterial for clinical bone regeneration. Nonetheless, differences between STB and DPBM were not statistically significant at all time points, warranting further investigation into their comparative efficacy.Keywords: Bone graft materials, Socket preservation, Signaling pathways, Regeneration, Osteogenesis, Sticky bone, Deproteinized porcine bone mineral, Platelet-Rich fibrin

Background

Since 1913, the use of various biomaterials in bone regeneration procedures has become a crucial approach for repairing bone defects at different anatomical sites [1]. Socket preservation involves the immediate placement of graft materials into extraction sites to minimize alveolar ridge resorption and restore lost soft and hard tissue [2]. Commonly used materials include autografts, allografts, xenografts, and synthetic substitutes. Autologous bone is considered the “gold standard” due to its excellent immune compatibility [3], strong osteoconductive properties, and ability to preserve the native tissue structure [4, 5]. However, its requirement for additional surgical sites, extended operative time, higher risk of secondary infection, and increased cost limit its widespread clinical application. As a result, allografts derived from bovine or porcine sources have emerged as an alternative to autologous bone [6]. These materials offer significant osteoconductive potential and have become an important choice for bone defect repair [7, 8]. Nevertheless, allografts still face challenges such as insufficient vascularization, lack of early osteoinductive capacity, and potential inflammatory rejection during graft integration [9].

In addition to these traditional bone repair materials, collagen fibers, owing to their excellent processability, biocompatibility, and biodegradability, are key components of the repair process in animal tissues and are commonly used as scaffolds for bone formation or as vehicles for novel drug delivery systems [10]. Osteoblasts initially secrete procollagen into the extracellular environment [11–13], where it is cleaved, self-assembled, and arranged into a mature collagen type I network, embedding osteoblasts that subsequently become osteocytes [14]. These osteocytes secrete osteopontin (OPN), osteocalcin (OCN), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), thus facilitating the deposition of densely mineralized bone matrix [15]. However, collagen fiber sponges alone lack the capacity to induce bone formation and thus exhibit only osteoconductive, rather than osteoinductive, properties [16].

To promote angiogenesis and tissue healing, platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) has been employed in alveolar ridge preservation, maxillary sinus augmentation, and dental bone reconstruction since 2000. This autologous biomaterial—comprising a dense fibrin network, platelets, and leukocytes—can release high levels of bioactive factors [17], reduce postoperative pain, promote early immunomodulation by leukocytes, and increase the number of pre-osteoblasts [18, 19]. Nevertheless, its role in hard tissue regeneration remains inconclusive. In the aesthetic zone, for instance, the use of PRF alone is considered insufficient to prevent postoperative alveolar ridge resorption [20], while other studies on third molar extractions have demonstrated significantly accelerated soft and hard tissue remodeling and effective pain relief [21]. Consequently, a PRF derivative known as “sticky bone” (STB)—formed by cross-linking PRF with bone graft substitutes—has been proposed. This material is hypothesized to provide enhanced bone regeneration and ridge stability by acting as both a growth factor reservoir and a scaffold guiding new bone formation [22].

Accordingly, the aim of this vivo study was to evaluate newly formed bone in rabbit tibiae with non-critical-sized defects using four different socket preservation biomaterials—sticky bone, Purgo bone grafts, APRF and bovine type I collagen scaffolds —from histological and imaging perspectives.

Materials and methods

Animal model and study design

Thirty male New Zealand White rabbits, aged 15 weeks and weighing 2.5 ± 0.5 kg, were used for this study conducted at the Animal Research Center of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University. Approval was granted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University Laboratory Animal Research Center (IACUC-20230814-08). As the animals were supplied by the institutional animal center rather than privately owned, additional informed consent from individual owners was not required.

Throughout the experiment, all rabbits were individually housed under standardized conditions at the university’s animal research facility. They were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 6 per group):

STB: Defects filled with sticky bone, created by combining APRF with DPBM;

STB: Defects filled with sticky bone, created by combining APRF with DPBM;

DPBM: Defects filled with deproteinized porcine bone matrix;

DPBM: Defects filled with deproteinized porcine bone matrix;

APRF group: Defects filled with autologous platelet-rich fibrin membranes;

APRF group: Defects filled with autologous platelet-rich fibrin membranes;

CS: Defects filled with a collagen sponge;

CS: Defects filled with a collagen sponge;

Empty control (EC): Full-thickness tibial defects left unfilled.

Empty control (EC): Full-thickness tibial defects left unfilled.

All defects were covered with a porcine pericardium collagen membrane (Bio-Gide®) to stabilize the grafts. Group assignments were disclosed only to a designated recorder, who relayed the procedural details to the surgeon during operations, ensuring blinded conditions.

Surgical procedures

All procedures were performed under the supervision of a veterinarian by an experienced implant surgeon at the university’s animal research facility. General anesthesia was maintained with 2–3% isoflurane inhalation; local anesthesia was induced using 2% lidocaine with epinephrine at a 1:80,000 concentration. Bilateral tibiae were shaved and disinfected, and their medial surfaces were accessed through careful retraction of skin, muscle, and periosteum. Circular bone defects (2.2 mm in diameter, ~ 6 mm deep) were prepared using sequential dental drills (1.0 mm, 1.8 mm, and 2.2 mm) at 1500 rpm under continuous saline irrigation.

Preparation of APRF and sticky bone

For the APRF group, two 10 mL tubes of whole blood were drawn from the auricular vein and collected in glass vacuum tubes without anticoagulant. The blood was centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 14 min, followed by a 5-minute clotting period. The layers formed included platelet-poor plasma, APRF (rich in growth factors and fibrin), and a red blood cell layer. The APRF layer was gently separated and compressed into a membrane using a specialized metal press.

For the STB group, an additional two tubes of blood were collected in glass tubes with a roughened interior surface and similarly centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 14 min. The supernatant containing S-PRF was combined with 0.3 g of DPBM, along with exudate from the APRF membrane preparation. This mixture was then wrapped in the APRF membrane, forming a malleable, platelet- and leukocyte-rich “sticky bone” capable of adapting to diverse defect geometries.

Implant volume standardisation

To minimize volumetric bias, all biomaterials were pre-shaped ex vivo in a custom cylindrical mould before implantation. The mould (internal diameter = 2.2 mm; height = 6 mm; calculated volume ≈ 22.8 mm³) was designed in SolidWorks 2022, fabricated from medical-grade polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) with a Form 3B + stereolithography printer (Formlabs, USA), and sterilized in the hospital’s central sterile-supply department by low-temperature ethylene-oxide gas (45 °C; 3 h exposure, 12 h aeration).

DPBM granules and APRF gel were gently packed into the sterile mould with a micro-spatula and levelled flush with the rim. Sticky bone (STB) was allowed to polymerize for 3 min and then pressed into the mould to assume the same geometry. Cylindrical plugs of collagen sponge (CS) were produced with a single-use 2.2-mm biopsy punch. All pre-shaped cylinders were transferred en bloc into the freshly drilled tibial defects.

This standardization guaranteed that each defect received a biomaterial volume equal to its theoretical capacity, thus eliminating volumetric bias across groups.

Graft placement and postoperative care

After graft placement according to the designated group, hemostasis was achieved, and tissues were sutured layer by layer. Rabbits were returned to their cages upon recovery from anesthesia. To reduce infection risk, penicillin (80,000 units) was administered intramuscularly immediately postoperatively, and again at 24 h and 48 h. Animals were sacrificed at 2 and 6 weeks post-surgery. Bilateral tibiae were harvested, with surrounding soft tissues carefully removed. In total, 12 bone samples from each group at each time point were collected and fixed in 20% formalin with calcium chloride. All procedures were conducted following approved ethical guidelines for animal studies.

Microtomographic analysis

Before the implantation of the biomaterials, the STB material and DPBM particles embedded in unclotted blood.

were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to examine their microstructures. The samples were sputter-coated with gold-palladium to enhance conductivity and imaged at 2 kV using a HITACHI SU8010 scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan). This allowed for a detailed observation of the material structure prior to their use in the bone defect model.

After several weeks of bone healing and regeneration, specimens were fixed in 20% formalin containing calcium.

chloride for 72 h and then scanned using a Skyscan1176 micro-CT system (Bruker microCT, Kontich, Belgium) at 85 kV and 292 µA. High-resolution, isotropic datasets (voxel size = 8.73 μm; 2672 × 4000 px) were obtained, providing at least 10 voxels across the mean trabecular thickness of rabbit tibia (60–150 μm). The region of interest (ROI) was defined as a 2.2 mm-diameter, 2 mm-height cylindrical volume centered on the defect. Segmentation was performed in CTAn v1.17.9.0 using a dual-threshold approach. Voxels between 88 and 128 Gy values (GV) were classified as newly formed bone. Voxels > 128 GV, corresponding to highly attenuating xenograft particles, were excluded from quantitative analysis; in the EC, CS and APRF groups such voxels were negligible, so the same 88–128 GV interval was applied uniformly across all groups. Speckles < 50 voxels were removed prior to morphometry. Key parameters including bone volume to tissue volume (BV/TV), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), structure model index (SMI) were evaluated. Three-dimensional reconstructions were generated with CTVolx (v3.0, Sky-Scan), with the graft material shown in pink and the newly formed bone in light gray for clear visualization.

Histological staining

Collected tibial samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for one week and then rinsed in running water for 24 h. They were subsequently dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (80–100%) and cleared in xylene for four days. Samples were embedded in a resin mixture comprising methyl methacrylate (MMA), 15% dibutyl phthalate (DBP), and 1% dicyclohexyl phthalate (DCP). Coronal sections, ~ 200 μm thickness, were mounted on acrylic resin slides. Using a Leica SP1600 grinding and polishing system, sections were thinned to a final thickness of 50 μm until scratch-free under optical microscopy (Nikon i50). Mc-Neal’s staining was applied to polished sections.

Twelve randomly selected sections from each group were evaluated by a pathologist under both 10× and 50× optical magnifications. At 10× magnification, the distribution and morphological features of newly formed bone, graft particles, and connective tissue fibers were assessed. Subsequently, under a double-blind protocol, an independent pathologist, who had no involvement in group assignment, selected one central field (ROI) per section at 50× magnification. The images were analyzed using ImageJ v1.53q (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to compute the vital bone% (%), defined as the ratio of the positively stained new bone area relative to the total ROI. Notably, in the STB and DPBM groups, to exclude graft interference, this percentage was calculated by dividing the newly formed bone area by the total ROI area, excluding the area occupied by graft particles. In contrast, the other groups were assessed by calculating the newly formed bone area relative to the entire ROI. All histomorphometric and statistical analyses were subsequently conducted by a trained professional who remained blinded to the evaluation groups, ensuring complete masking.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was performed to localize angiogenic protein CD31, osteogenic markers Runx2 and ALP, and the inflammatory mediator TNF-α in the tissues surrounding the tibial defects. Sections were immersed in preheated citrate buffer (0.01 M, pH 6.0; Solarbio, Beijing, China), boiled for 20 min, and cooled to room temperature. Primary antibodies were applied at 4 °C overnight: CD31 (ARG52748; Arigo, Hsinchu, Taiwan), Runx2 (SD208-0; HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), ALP (SA40-00; HuaBio), and TNF-α (PS00-40; HuaBio). On the following day, sections were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min with corresponding secondary antibodies (ZGB-Bio, PV-9000) and visualized with DAB substrate (ZLI-9018, ZGB-Bio, Beijing), yielding a brownish precipitate at sites of immunopositivity.

In this study, Runx2 (SD208-0), ALP (SA40-00), and TNF-α (PS00-40) antibodies are all rabbit monoclonal antibodies, specifically recombinant antibodies. We selected recombinant monoclonal antibodies for these markers to enhance antigen-binding specificity, reduce lot-to-lot variability, and minimize background staining in quantitative analyses, particularly for nuclear and cytoplasmic markers. On the other hand, the CD31 antibody (ARG52748) is a rabbit polyclonal antibody. CD31 is a heavily glycosylated endothelial membrane protein with multiple extracellular epitopes, making it suitable for detection with polyclonal antibodies. The polyclonal ARG52748 antibody, validated for paraffin IHC, offers broader epitope coverage and greater sensitivity for detecting microvessels in formalin-fixed tissues.

For the quantification of Runx2, ALP, and TNF-α expression, color deconvolution (H-DAB) was performed in ImageJ v1.53q to isolate the DAB channel. A single global threshold, derived from isotype controls, was applied uniformly to all images. The percentage of positively stained area was calculated as follows: Positive Area% = (DAB-positive pixels/Total pixels) × 100%. For CD31, microvessel density was assessed by counting DAB-positive endothelial profiles (≤ 50 μm, thin-walled) within one randomly selected field at 100× magnification per section.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, USA). Normality and homogeneity of variances were verified with the Shapiro–Wilk and Brown–Forsythe tests (α = 0.05). When both assumptions were met, inter-group differences were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test (adjusted p values reported). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SEM characterization

SEM images revealed significant differences in the bonding patterns of collagen fibers and bone graft particles between the STB material and the DPBM particles embedded in a blood clot (Fig. 1a-d).At low magnification (×1500), the surface of DPBM bone blocks displayed a thin, discontinuous collagen layer, partially detached from the rough surface of the graft. In contrast, the STB material exhibited a dense, reticular arrangement of collagen fibers interwoven into the porous structure of the graft particles. At higher magnification (×3500), the DPBM surface showed loosely arranged collagen fibrils with a higher proportion of erythrocytes. In contrast, the STB samples demonstrated a tightly packed fibrin network, with porcine bone granules firmly bonded by fibrin, and platelets were observed interspersed among the granules, making direct contact with the biomaterial. The fibrin layer in DPBM was thin and discontinuous, partially enveloping the graft particles, resulting in comparatively poor integration.

Histological analysis

By the 2-week postoperative time point, substantial differences in new bone formation were observed among the groups (Fig. 3c). In the histological sections from each group, newly formed woven bone, stained magenta, was observed extending into the center of the defect or interwoven among the graft particles. Bone graft particles appeared as light-red, block-like structures. Meanwhile, the remaining areas contained scattered vacuolar and reticular collagen fibers, which appeared relatively sparse. In the EC group, a thin layer of newly formed bone was noted surrounding the original tibial defect. The CS group showed sparse, striated new bone in the defect center. In the DPBM group, abundant new bone had formed around the grafted particles, particularly near the marrow cavity, whereas distal bone formation was less apparent. The APRF group exhibited notable new bone formation at both proximal and distal edges of the defect, though only a thin strip of new bone was observed centrally. In contrast, the STB group maintained the integrity of the graft particles and displayed abundant new bone infiltration around the periphery and within internal pores, especially near the bone marrow cavity.

By 6 weeks, further differences in bone healing emerged (Fig. 3d). The EC group showed relatively satisfactory bone healing and tissue integration. The CS group exhibited limited new bone and noticeable residual voids in the tissue. Although grafted particles remained visible in the DPBM group, the surrounding bone tissue was largely consolidated. Compared with CS, the APRF group showed more evenly distributed new bone and vascular channels.

Quantitative ImageJ-based analysis (at 100× magnification) measured the percentage of defect area filled by new bone (new bone fill rate). At 2 weeks, the mean new bone fill rates in the STB, DPBM, APRF, CS, and EC groups were 66.12 ± 6.32%, 60.36 ± 6.39%, 60.26 ± 3.59%, 51.02 ± 6.76%, and 52.77 ± 11.38%, respectively. Statistical analysis showed that STB had a significantly higher fill rate than CS (P < 0.0001) and EC (P < 0.001). Both DPBM and APRF significantly outperformed CS (P < 0.0015) and EC (P < 0.05). Additionally, EC had a higher fill rate than CS (P < 0.05). At 6 weeks, the new bone fill rates in STB, DPBM, APRF, CS, and EC were 88.01 ± 4.91%, 84.98 ± 6.72%, 79.06 ± 6.71%, 70.07 ± 5.67%, and 73.18 ± 4.92%, respectively. STB remained significantly higher than APRF (P < 0.001), CS (P < 0.001), and EC (P < 0.001). DPBM also showed superior outcomes to APRF (P < 0.05), CS (P < 0.001), and EC (P < 0.001). Meanwhile, APRF exceeded CS (P < 0.001) and EC (P < 0.05).

Discussion

With the aging population increasing worldwide, the demand for effective bone defect reconstruction continues to rise. Numerous studies have evaluated the osteoconductive and osteoinductive potential of dental bone regeneration materials. In this preclinical study, we compared four types of bone regeneration materials—STB, DPBM, APRF, and a bovine type I collagen scaffold (CS)—using a rabbit tibial model for non-critical-sized bone defects. We assessed the bone healing process via histology, microCT imaging, and immunohistochemistry, and performed SEM to differentiate between sticky bone and DPBM particles embedded in unprocessed blood clots. These findings provide valuable insights for clinical material selection and offer a better understanding of the underlying mechanisms of bone regeneration.

By mixing APRF membranes and DPBM particles, we created sticky bone (STB). SEM images revealed a multifiber protein network that trapped leukocytes, erythrocytes, and platelets while cross-linking bone particles. This was consistent with previous findings [12, 13]. Scarano et al. found that mixing bovine bone graft with autologous platelet concentrates significantly enhanced compressive strength (by 175%) compared to bovine bone mixed with whole blood, as well as increased the elastic modulus by 117.2% [12, 14]. The improved mechanical stability likely supports space maintenance within bone defects and promotes the slow release of cytokines and growth factors, further stimulating bone healing [16].

Histological analysis using Mc-Neal’s staining demonstrated that at both 2 and 6 weeks the STB and DPBM groups exhibited more rapid early-stage bone formation than the negative controls (CS and EC). At the 2-week time point, however, no statistically significant difference was detected between the STB group and either positive control (DPBM or APRF). By 6 weeks, the STB group continued to outperform the negative controls-presumably because a pure type I collagen sponge possesses limited osteoinductive capacity [17]-yet the quantity of newly formed bone remained statistically indistinguishable from that observed in the DPBM and APRF groups.

Micro-CT findings supported the histological trends: while STB and DPBM demonstrated similar bone-regenerative capacity at 2 weeks, both continued to promote trabecular maturation at 6 weeks, as indicated by SMI, Tb.N, and Tb.Th data. By contrast, APRF alone appeared to resorb within 7–28 days, limiting its early osteogenic potential [18, 19]. This finding suggests that xenografts such as DPBM or DBBM may exhibit inherent osteoinductive properties, given that small amounts of proteins and cytokines remain within the bone matrix, potentially serving as a biological scaffold for initiating the healing process [20].

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the STB group exhibited significantly higher key osteogenic markers, such as Runx2 and ALP. Furthermore, the STB group showed an increased density of CD31-positive microvessels (angiogenesis marker) relative to the other groups. At the same time, the STB group exhibited lower TNF-α expression, indicating an attenuated inflammatory response. Taken together, these findings suggest that STB simultaneously enhances osteogenesis and angiogenesis while mitigating the inflammatory response during bone healing [21].The enhanced bone regeneration observed in the STB group may be related to the modulation of osteogenic signalling pathways—specifically, activation of the Smad1/5/8 cascade—accompanied by inhibition of NF-κB signalling. Factors released from STB (e.g., TGF-β, BMPs) can activate Smad1/5/8 signaling, leading to upregulation of osteogenic genes such as Runx2 and ALP and thereby driving osteoblast differentiation [22–24]. In addition, by lowering TNF-α levels, STB may attenuate NF-κB activity, which would lessen its inhibitory effect on osteogenesis (excessive TNF-α/NF-κB activity is known to suppress BMP-induced Runx2 expression). However, these mechanisms still require further experimental confirmation, particularly regarding the precise activation of the BMP-Smad1/5/8 or TGF-β-Smad2/3 axes [25]. This osteoimmunomodulatory effect of STB highlights how coupling bone formation with immune regulation can synergistically enhance regeneration.

In summary, this study provides histological, radiographic, and molecular evidence that sticky bone enhances osteogenesis and angiogenesis and mitigates local inflammation. These findings offer new insights into TGF-β/BMP and NF-κB interplay and highlight the importance of multidimensional evaluation for bone substitutes. Future studies involving larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods, along with direct measurements of key signaling molecules (e.g., SMAD1/5/8, p65), are expected to provide deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms of sticky bone-mediated bone repair and further support its clinical application in orthopedics and implant dentistry.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that Sticky Bone (STB), a combination of Deproteinized Porcine Bone Mineral (DPBM) and Advanced Platelet-Rich Fibrin (APRF), exhibits superior bone regenerative capabilities compared to DPBM, APRF, and Bovine Collagen Scaffolds (CS) alone. Histological, micro-CT, and immunohistochemical analyses revealed that STB significantly enhances new bone formation, promotes trabecular bone maturation, and facilitates angiogenesis, particularly at the 2-week postoperative time point. Immunohistochemical results further highlight that STB upregulates key osteogenic markers such as Runx2 and ALP while reducing the expression of the inflammatory mediator TNF-α, indicating its dual role in promoting osteogenesis and modulating inflammation.

While both STB and DPBM showed comparable bone regeneration at 6 weeks, STB demonstrated a more pronounced anti-inflammatory effect, which could contribute to an improved healing environment. The absence of statistically significant differences between STB and DPBM in terms of new bone formation at later stages suggests that both materials share similar long-term regenerative potential. However, the additional benefits of STB, such as enhanced angiogenesis and immune modulation, position it as a promising biomaterial for clinical bone regeneration, particularly in applications such as ridge preservation and bone defect repair.

In conclusion, the findings from this study provide strong evidence supporting the clinical application of STB in bone regeneration procedures. Future studies with larger sample sizes, extended follow-up, and direct molecular measurements of key signaling pathways (e.g., SMAD1/5/8, p65) will be necessary to further explore the molecular mechanisms of STB-mediated bone repair and validate its efficacy in complex bone regeneration contexts.
