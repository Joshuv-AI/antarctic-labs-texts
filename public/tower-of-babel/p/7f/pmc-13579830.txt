Background

Cancer is the second leading cause of mortality worldwide. According to a recent report by the World Health Organization (WHO), the global incidence of cancer is expected to rise from 19.2 million cases in 2020 to 29.4 million cases by 2040. Cancer comprises a heterogeneous group of diseases characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of somatic cell clones influenced by multiple factors, often with metastatic potential. Hallmarks of cancer include genomic instability, loss of sensitivity to growth inhibitors, resistance to cell death mechanisms, induction of angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, evasion of apoptosis, and immune system evasion [1–6].

Among various cancer types, Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most prevalent malignancy globally and in Iran and was the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths in 2020 (GLOBOCAN 2020). The development of CRC is primarily driven by genetic mutations that transform normal epithelial cells into adenocarcinomas and carcinomas. Disease stage at diagnosis significantly influences prognosis, with five-year survival rates of 90.3%, 70.4%, and 12.5% for localized, regional, and distant-stage tumors, respectively. Therefore, early detection through screening can substantially improve survival rates and reduce mortality [7]. One of the early events in colorectal tumorigenesis is the disruption of apoptosis in colonic epithelial crypt cells, which leads to uncontrolled proliferation. Apoptosis resistance, a hallmark of cancer, is regulated by proteins involved in the intrinsic and extrinsic mitochondrial pathways. Genetic mutations accumulate over time in colonic epithelial cells, leading to the formation of colorectal adenomas and adenocarcinomas, which may eventually invade other tissues and metastasize [8–13].

The risk factors for CRC include age, diet, lifestyle, genetic predisposition, and environmental factors. Current treatment strategies, such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and antibody-based therapies, are often associated with significant limitations, including lack of specificity, high toxicity, rapid development of multidrug resistance (MDR), and numerous side effects (e.g., weight loss, severe nausea and vomiting, cardiotoxicity, congestive heart failure, and immunosuppression). Furthermore, the inefficacy of these interventions in eradicating metastatic lesions, poor penetration of chemotherapeutic agents, overall reduced patient survival, and post-treatment complications, particularly in elderly patients, underscore the need for novel therapeutic approaches. Therefore, there is an urgent demand for the development of more effective and less toxic anticancer agents for CRC treatment [14–16].

Mitochondria have emerged as a promising therapeutic target in cancer research. These organelles play a crucial role in cellular energy metabolism and cell cycle regulation, both of which are essential for tumor progression. Recent studies have shown that cancer cell survival and proliferation are highly dependent on mitochondrial respiration. Mitochondrial damage induces irreversible apoptosis, making these organelles key targets for anticancer therapies. Mitochondria regulate not only calcium homeostasis and redox signaling but also apoptotic pathways [17–19]. Studies have revealed that proteasome inhibitor-resistant multiple myeloma cell lines adapt their metabolic activity by increasing the expression of proteins involved in electron transport and cellular respiration, thereby maintaining ATP levels. Furthermore, cancer recurrence after prolonged treatment is often associated with the persistence of cancer stem cells (CSCs), which rely heavily on mitochondrial function. CSCs have demonstrated resistance to chemotherapy, proteasome inhibitors, and monoclonal antibodies, highlighting the need for novel strategies that target mitochondrial bioenergetics. Disrupting mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation could suppress ATP production and oxygen consumption in cancer cells, enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Mitochondrial dysfunction can trigger programmed cell death through the release of caspase-activating proteins such as cytochrome c, mitochondrial depolarization, disruption of the electron transport chain, and alterations in energy metabolism, all of which contribute to apoptosis via the Bcl-2 family protein interactions [20–23].

The significance of nanotechnology in healthcare is undeniable. Despite challenges in designing and developing nanoparticles for therapeutic applications, rapid advancements in nanomedicine have demonstrated their potential in both diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Metallic nanoparticles are increasingly recognized as promising anticancer agents due to their cost-effectiveness and high therapeutic efficacy [24]. Among these, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) have garnered substantial attention due to their unique physicochemical properties. Toxicity remains a major concern in the clinical application of nanoparticles; however, AuNPs have the advantage of a well-documented history of safe use in humans. Studies have shown that AuNPs do not inhibit the proliferation of normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), confirming their biocompatibility at therapeutic concentrations. Despite recent advances, the biological interactions of AuNPs remain incompletely understood [25–28].

Emerging evidence suggests that metal nanoparticles can selectively target cancer cells. Several research groups have demonstrated the potential of AuNPs in various malignancies, and nanotechnology has enabled their application in drug delivery, diagnostics, and cancer therapy. The unique physical, chemical, and biological properties of AuNPs make them highly attractive for biomedical applications. In physiological conditions, AuNPs exhibit high stability, superior biocompatibility, and low immunogenicity, with minimal side effects. Additionally, the surface of AuNPs can be functionalized with various biomolecules, enhancing their specificity and efficacy. The properties of AuNPs, including size, shape, charge, reducing agent used in synthesis, environmental conditions, and cell type, influence their biological activity. Studies have also indicated that the intracellular localization of functionalized AuNPs varies depending on their surface modifications [29–31].

Considering the importance of mitochondria in CRC progression and the potential of AuNPs in cancer therapy, this study aims to investigate the effects of AuNPs on mitochondrial function in human colorectal adenocarcinoma HT-29 cells. Specifically, we will assess cell viability, mitochondrial membrane potential, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and the expression of genes involved in the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. Understanding the interaction between AuNPs and mitochondrial dynamics may provide valuable insights into novel therapeutic approaches for CRC treatment.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

Chitosan (75–85% deacetylated, molecular weight: 300,000 g/mol), hydrochloroauric acid (HAuCl₄), MTT, Rhodamine 123, DCFH-DA, and Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). DMEM cell culture medium, trypsin-EDTA, penicillin/streptomycin, and FBS were obtained from Bioidea (Iran). The RNA extraction kit was purchased from Favorgen Biotech Corporation (Taiwan), and the cDNA synthesis kit was obtained from Yekta Tajhiz Azma (Iran). All other chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade and obtained from standard commercial sources.

Synthesis of chitosan-coated gold nanoparticles

Chitosan-coated gold nanoparticles (CH-AuNPs) were synthesized using the Turkevich method. Chitosan (75–85% deacetylated, molecular weight: 300,000 g/mol) was dissolved in 0.4 M acetic acid at a 2% (w/w) ratio. Hydrochloroauric acid (HAuCl₄) (Merck, Germany) was then added to the chitosan solution to achieve a final concentration of 2 mM. The mixture was homogenized at room temperature for 15 min using a magnetic stirrer at 80–90 rpm, then heated to 100 °C until a burgundy-red color developed, indicating nanoparticle formation. The final nanoparticle concentration was 2 mM. It should be noted that the reported concentration (2 mM) refers to the initial concentration of HAuCl₄ precursor prior to the reduction reaction, which is a standard parameter in the Turkevich method for gold nanoparticle synthesis.

Particle size distribution analysis by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS)

The hydrodynamic diameter and size distribution of nanoparticles were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) in the range of 1 nm to 10 μm. The DLS method relies on the Brownian motion of particles in suspension, where fluctuations in scattered light intensity correspond to particle size. A Qudix Scatteroscope1 nanoparticle size analyzer was used for measurements, and size distributions were recorded by analyzing the scattering peaks of smaller and larger particles (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) measurement by UV-Vis spectroscopy

SPR was analyzed using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Photonix Ar 2015, Pishroh Rayeh Electron Engineering Company). The system, equipped with a tungsten halogen lamp (visible range) and deuterium lamp (UV range), provides real-time spectral displays. Absorption spectra were measured from 200 to 800 nm, with a characteristic peak at approximately 520 nm for non-aggregated gold nanoparticles. Additionally, a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) was used for high-precision absorption measurements within the 190–840 nm range (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Nanoparticle imaging by Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was employed to visualize nanoparticles using a NanoWizard II AFM (JPK, Germany). After the nanoparticle solution was diluted in distilled water, a portion was deposited onto a mica sheet and allowed to dry. The AFM was used to generate phase and topographic images, as well as 3D reconstructions to measure nanoparticle size (Supplementary Fig. 3). It should be noted that DLS measures the hydrodynamic diameter of particles in suspension including the hydration layer, while AFM measures the dry particle size after dehydration. Therefore, DLS-derived values are typically larger than AFM-measured values.

Cell culture

HT-29 (Pasture Institute, Iran), a colon cancer cell line, was cultured under sterile conditions. Cells were incubated in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 90% humidity. After reaching ~ 80% confluence, cells were passaged using trypsin-EDTA, resuspended in DMEM (Bioidea, Iran) with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and seeded for further experiments.

MTT assay

Cells (10,000 per well) were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h. HT-29 cells were treated with serial concentrations of CH-AuNPs (12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 µmol/L, corresponding to approximately 2.5, 4.9, 9.8, 19.7, and 39.4 µg/mL, respectively) for 24 h. After treatment, MTT solution (0.5 mg/ml) (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) was added to the wells, and after 4 h of incubation, formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO (Sigma–Aldrich, USA). Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA reader, and viability was calculated by comparing the absorbance values to the control group. A 24-hour treatment duration was selected for all cytotoxicity and apoptosis assays (MTT, mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS, and flow cytometry) to maintain consistency across experiments and to capture early apoptotic events induced by CH-AuNPs.

Clonogenicity assay

Cells were seeded at 10,000/µl in a 6-well plate, incubated for 24 h, and then exposed to the IC50 concentration of gold nanoparticles. After replacing the medium, cells were allowed to form colonies for one week. Colonies were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and analyzed using OpenCFU software after imaging. In contrast to the 24-hour treatment used for other assays, the clonogenic assay required a longer duration (7 days) to allow for colony formation, which is standard for assessing long-term proliferative capacity after nanoparticle exposure.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (△ψm) measurement with rhodamine 123

Rhodamine 123 (Sigma–Aldrich, USA), a fluorescent cationic dye, accumulates in mitochondria with high membrane potential (polarized state) and decreases in fluorescence when the membrane potential is lost (depolarized state). After 24-hour treatment with gold nanoparticles, cells were washed with PBS, incubated with 5 µM Rhodamine 123 for 30 min in the dark at 37 °C, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) assay with DCFH-DA

DCFH-DA (Sigma Aldrich, USA) was used to detect intracellular ROS levels. After culturing 10,000 cells in a 96-well plate for 24 h, the treatment group was exposed to gold nanoparticles at the IC50 concentration for 24 h. Cells were then incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min, washed with PBS, and analyzed using flow cytometry. Fluorescence microscopy was also used to examine ROS levels.

Apoptosis assay by flow cytometry

Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI (Sigma Aldrich, USA) apoptosis detection kit and analyzed by flow cytometry. Cells were trypsinized (Bioidea, Iran), counted, and seeded in 6-well plates, followed by treatment with the desired substances for 48 h. After treatment, cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC to identify early apoptotic cells and Propidium Iodide (PI) to identify late apoptotic or necrotic cells. The stained cells were incubated and then analyzed using the BD FACSCalibur system, with data processed using CellQuest™ Pro and FlowJo software. A total of 10,000 events were recorded for each sample, with Annexin V-FITC readings in the FL1 channel and PI readings in the FL2 channel.

Purification of RNA, cDNA preparation, and real-time PCR

RNA extraction, cDNA preparation, and real-time PCR were carried out as follows: RNA was isolated using the FavorPrep Blood/Cultured Cell Total RNA Mini kit (Favorgen Biotech Corporation, Taiwan), with cells trypsinized, counted, and treated with the desired substances. The RNA was purified and its concentration and quality were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. 4). cDNA synthesis was performed using the Yekta Tajhiz Azma kit Iran, with reverse transcription of 1000 ng of RNA. The synthesized cDNA was stored for gene expression analysis. eal-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR® Green Master Mix (Ampliqon, Denmark) on a Quant Studio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s and 60 °C for 60 s. A melt-curve analysis was performed after amplification to confirm product specificity, with conditions of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 60 s, and gradual heating from 60 °C to 95 °C at 0.15 °C/s. GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene. Primer sequences, accession numbers, amplicon sizes, and melting temperatures are provided in Supplementary Table 1. PCR efficiencies were calculated using LinRegPCR software (version 2021.2) and are reported in Supplementary Table 2. The expression levels of target genes (BAX, BCL2, Caspase-3, and CYT C) were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method. All reactions were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 25. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times (minimum level of replication), and data were expressed as Mean ± SD. The significance of differences between two groups was evaluated using the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (One-Way ANOVA) was applied, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. P values less than 0.001 are reported as P < 0.001; P values between 0.001 and 0.05 are reported as exact values (e.g., P = 0.023).

Results

Characterization of chitosan-coated gold nanoparticles (CH-AuNPs)

The synthesized CH-AuNPs were characterized using DLS, UV-Vis spectroscopy, and AFM. DLS analysis revealed a monodisperse size distribution with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 12.3 nm. UV-Vis spectroscopy showed a characteristic SPR peak at approximately 520 nm, confirming the successful synthesis of spherical gold nanoparticles. AFM imaging further confirmed the spherical morphology and uniform size distribution of the nanoparticles, with an average diameter consistent with the DLS results. These characterization results collectively confirm the successful synthesis of CH-AuNPs with desired physicochemical properties for subsequent biological experiments.

Cell viability assay using the MTT method

The results of the MTT assay demonstrated a concentration-dependent decrease in the metabolic activity of HT-29 cells following treatment with serial concentrations of chitosan-coated gold nanoparticles (CH-AuNPs) for 24 h. The cells were exposed to concentrations of 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 µmol/L of CH-AuNPs. As the concentration of nanoparticles increased, cell viability progressively decreased. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for CH-AuNPs in HT-29 cells was calculated to be 33.3 µmol/L (approximately 6.5 µg/mL based on gold atom concentration), with a 95% confidence interval of 28.5–38.2 µM, indicating the concentration at which the gold nanoparticles significantly inhibited cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Clonogenic assay

The results of the clonogenic assay, examining the effect of treatment with the IC50 concentration of CH-AuNPs for 24 h, showed a significant reduction in the colony-forming ability of HT-29 cells in the CH-AuNPs-treated group (P = 0.0001) compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Measuring mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) with Rhodamine 123

The results of assessing mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) in HT-29 cells treated with the IC50 concentration of CH-AuNPs for 24 h revealed a significant decrease in Δψm in the CH-AuNPs-treated group (P = 0.0004) compared to the control group. This suggests that CH-AuNPs treatment notably disrupts mitochondrial membrane integrity in HT-29 cells (Fig. 1).

Validation of qRT-PCR assays

Prior to expression analysis, PCR efficiency and specificity were validated for all genes (GAPDH, BAX, BCL2, Caspase-3, and CYT C). Melt-curve analysis showed single, sharp peaks for each amplicon, confirming the absence of non-specific amplification or primer-dimer formation (Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Table 3). PCR efficiencies were calculated using LinRegPCR software (version 2021.2) based on standard curve analysis. As shown in Supplementary Table 2, all efficiencies ranged from 1.735 to 1.854, which falls within the acceptable range of 90–100%. GAPDH Ct values remained stable across control and CH-AuNP-treated groups (Ct variation < 0.5 cycles), and its amplification efficiency was consistent (1.735 ± 0.024), confirming its suitability as a reference gene under our experimental conditions, including mitochondrial dysfunction.

Examining the relative expression of BAX, Bcl-2, Caspase 3 and CYT C gene

The relative expression levels of BAX, BCL-2, Caspase 3, and Cyt-c genes in HT-29 cells treated with the IC50 concentration of CH-AuNPs for 24 hours were assessed using qRT-PCR. GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization of target gene expression. Conversely, the expression of BCL-2, an anti-apoptotic gene, significantly decreased (P = 0.0002), suggesting a shift toward apoptosis induction (Fig. 3B). Additionally, the expression of Caspase 3 and CYT C, key apoptotic markers, showed significant upregulation (P = 0.0004 and P = 0.0003, respectively) following CH-AuNP exposure, further confirming the activation of the apoptotic pathway in HT-29 cells (Fig. 3C and D).

Evaluation of apoptosis via annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry

The effect of CH-AuNPs on apoptosis induction in HT-29 cells was assessed using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining followed by flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 4). In the untreated control group, the majority of cells remained viable (84.4% in the Q4 quadrant), with negligible necrosis (1.42%), early apoptosis of 2.19%, and late apoptosis of 12.0%. Following treatment with the IC50 concentration (33 µM) of CH-AuNPs for 24 h, the percentage of cells in early apoptosis was 2.93%, late apoptosis was 38.8%, and necrosis was 6.20%. Discussion

Main findings and comparison with literature

AuNPs have gained significant attention in biomedical research due to their unique properties, including high bioavailability, stability, and tunable surface chemistry. Studies have demonstrated their potential efficacy in various cancer types, including colorectal cancer [32]. In this study, chitosan was employed as a reducing and stabilizing agent for AuNP synthesis via a chemical method, resulting in CH-AuNPs with a spherical morphology and an average size of 12.3 nm. Their surface plasmon resonance was observed at approximately 520 nm, indicating successful synthesis. The 24-hour exposure time was chosen based on preliminary time-course experiments (data not shown) indicating that significant cytotoxic effects were detectable at this time point, while longer exposures (48–72 h) led to excessive cell death that complicated the assessment of early apoptotic events. The consistency of treatment duration across MTT, mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS, and flow cytometry assays allows for direct comparison of the observed biological effects.

The stabilization of gold nanoparticles is a crucial factor influencing their bioactivity. Polymer-based stabilization, such as with chitosan, enhances nanoparticle stability and cellular uptake [33]. The physicochemical characteristics of AuNPs, including size, shape, surface charge, and coating, are integral to their mechanism of action and apoptotic potential. Previous studies have indicated that AuNPs synthesized using citrate as a reducing agent do not induce apoptosis in certain cancer cell lines [34]. In contrast, positively charged nanoparticles exhibit greater cytotoxicity due to their electrostatic interactions with negatively charged cell membranes, facilitating enhanced cellular uptake [34, 35]. The uptake mechanism is influenced by particle size, shape, and the mode of endocytosis, which varies among different cell types [36]. Notably, evidence suggests that chitosan-functionalized AuNPs preferentially accumulate in mitochondria, with chitosan itself enhancing nanoparticle uptake and bioactivity (Fig. 5) [35].

Graphical abstract. Chitosan-coated gold nanoparticles (CH-AuNPs) induce apoptosis in HT-29 colorectal cancer cells via mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), cytochrome c release, and caspase-3 activation, along with upregulation of BAX, Caspase-3, and CYT C and downregulation of BCL-2, in a ROS-independent manner

The present study aimed to assess the apoptotic effects of CH-AuNPs on HT-29 colorectal cancer cells. Using Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry, we observed a significant increase in apoptosis following 24-hour treatment with the IC50 concentration of CH-AuNPs compared to untreated controls. Similar apoptotic induction has been reported in other studies; for instance, A.C. Martínez et al. demonstrated CH-AuNP-induced apoptosis in MCF-7, HeLa, CEM, and K562 cancer cell lines [34, 37]. Additionally, tannic acid-synthesized AuNPs have been shown to trigger phosphatidylserine externalization and apoptosis in HCT116 colorectal carcinoma cells. Our results further corroborate that AuNPs exhibit a dose-dependent cytotoxic effect, wherein increasing nanoparticle concentration correlates with decreased metabolic activity and cell viability in HT-29 cells. This finding aligns with previous reports that AuNPs exert cell type-specific cytotoxicity, highlighting the variability in apoptotic mechanisms across different cancer models [34].

Given their promising anticancer properties, AuNPs offer a multi-faceted approach to cancer treatment, including selective cytotoxicity, efficient cellular uptake, and minimal impact on normal cells. In this study, CH-AuNPs significantly inhibited HT-29 cell clonogenicity, supporting their potential anti-metastatic properties. Consistently, previous studies have demonstrated similar anti-clonogenic effects of CH-AuNPs in HeLa and MCF-7 cells [34]. Clonogenic potential is a key determinant of cancer progression and metastasis; thus, our findings suggest that CH-AuNPs may contribute to limiting colorectal cancer invasiveness.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of apoptosis, and our study revealed that CH-AuNP treatment led to a significant loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) in HT-29 cells. Comparable findings have been reported in studies using tannic acid-derived AuNPs in HCT116 cells [38] and green-synthesized AuNPs from Albizia lebbeck in HCT-116 cells [38]. CH-AuNPs are believed to induce mitochondrial damage through two primary mechanisms: interference with Complex I, disrupting the proton gradient, and interaction with Complex IV, leading to cytochrome c (CYT-C) release and apoptosis activation [39]. Mitochondrial damage not only impairs proliferative and metastatic potential in cancer cells but also triggers intrinsic apoptotic pathways.

Interestingly, our study found that CH-AuNP treatment did not significantly increase ROS production in HT-29 cells. This observation contrasts with other reports where AuNP-induced apoptosis was associated with ROS generation [38, 40, 41]. The disparity in findings may be attributed to differences in nanoparticle physicochemical properties, exposure concentrations, and treatment durations. The absence of ROS elevation in our study suggests that CH-AuNP-induced apoptosis in HT-29 cells may occur independently of oxidative stress-mediated cytotoxicity. The selectivity of CH-AuNPs toward HT-29 cells may be attributed to the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect in cancer cells, combined with the positive charge of chitosan facilitating greater uptake in negatively charged cancer cell membranes. Additionally, cancer cells exhibit higher mitochondrial activity, making them more susceptible to mitochondrial disruption by AuNPs [42, 43].

Further mechanistic investigations revealed that CH-AuNP treatment upregulated the expression of pro-apoptotic genes Caspase-3, BAX, and CYT-C while downregulating the anti-apoptotic gene BCL-2. These findings indicate that CH-AuNPs activate the intrinsic apoptosis pathway, primarily through mitochondrial perturbation. Prior studies have established that AuNP-induced apoptosis is caspase-dependent [37], reinforcing the role of intrinsic apoptotic mechanisms in CH-AuNP cytotoxicity. Similar trends have been observed in other nanoparticle studies, where caspase activation and mitochondrial dysfunction contribute to cancer cell apoptosis [38, 40].

Overall, our findings emphasize that gold nanoparticle-induced apoptosis is influenced by multiple cellular processes, including mitochondrial dysfunction, caspase activation, and cellular uptake efficiency. The extent of apoptosis induction depends on nanoparticle design, exposure parameters, and cell type specificity. While ROS production has been implicated in AuNP-induced cytotoxicity in some studies, our results suggest that CH-AuNP-mediated apoptosis in HT-29 cells occurs independently of oxidative stress.

Alternative ROS-independent apoptotic mechanisms

An interesting finding of this study is that CH‑AuNP treatment induced significant mitochondrial membrane depolarization and upregulation of pro‑apoptotic genes (BAX, Caspase‑3, and CYT C) without a concomitant significant increase in ROS levels. While ROS overproduction is a well‑established mediator of mitochondrial apoptosis, our results suggest that CH‑AuNPs may trigger the intrinsic apoptotic pathway through ROS‑independent mechanisms. Several possibilities could explain this observation. First, CH‑AuNPs may directly interact with the mitochondrial membrane, particularly with cardiolipin, a phospholipid enriched in the inner mitochondrial membrane, leading to membrane permeabilization and cytochrome c release without requiring ROS generation. Second, chitosan‑coated nanoparticles may accumulate preferentially in mitochondria due to their positive surface charge, potentially causing direct physical disruption of electron transport chain complexes or opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) in an ROS‑independent manner. Third, CH‑AuNPs might directly modulate the expression or activity of BCL‑2 family proteins, shifting the balance toward pro‑apoptotic signaling independently of oxidative stress. Similar ROS‑independent mitochondrial apoptosis has been reported for other nanoparticles, including certain gold nanoparticle formulations and cationic polymers. We acknowledge that additional experiments, such as measuring mPTP opening, assessing cardiolipin peroxidation, or using specific ROS scavengers, would be required to fully elucidate the precise mechanism. Nevertheless, our data clearly demonstrate that CH‑AuNPs effectively induce mitochondrial apoptosis in HT‑29 cells, and this effect can occur through pathways that are at least partially independent of ROS elevation.

Limitations of the study

The authors acknowledge several limitations in the present study that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, while the successful synthesis of CH-AuNPs was confirmed by DLS, UV-Vis spectroscopy, and AFM, complementary physicochemical characterization techniques such as Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) to confirm chitosan coating on the nanoparticle surface and X-ray diffraction (XRD) to analyze the crystalline structure were not performed. Second, this study focused on a single colorectal cancer cell line (HT-29); therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to other colorectal cancer cell lines or other cancer types. Third, the apoptotic effects of CH-AuNPs were evaluated only in vitro, and in vivo studies are necessary to confirm their efficacy and safety in animal models. Fourth, a comparative study with uncoated gold nanoparticles was not conducted, which would have provided valuable insights into the specific role of chitosan coating in enhancing cellular uptake and cytotoxicity. Fifth, the long-term effects and potential resistance mechanisms to CH-AuNP treatment were not investigated. A major limitation of this study is the absence of critical experimental controls necessary to dissect the source of the observed biological effects. Specifically, the study did not include control experiments with (i) free chitosan alone, (ii) gold salt (HAuCl₄) alone, or (iii) size-matched uncoated gold nanoparticles. Without these controls, it is not possible to determine whether the cytotoxic and apoptotic effects observed are attributable to the gold core, the chitosan coating, residual ionic gold species, or a synergistic combination thereof. Consequently, the interpretation of the mechanism of action remains restricted, and definitive conclusions regarding the specific contribution of each component cannot be drawn. Future studies should incorporate these essential controls to fully elucidate the respective roles of the gold core and chitosan coating in mediating the anticancer effects of CH-AuNPs. Future studies should address these limitations by including additional characterization techniques (FTIR, XRD, zeta potential, stability testing in biological media), testing multiple cell lines including non-malignant colon epithelial controls, conducting in vivo experiments, performing comparative analyses with uncoated nanoparticles, and evaluating long-term treatment outcomes. Despite these limitations, the authors believe that the robust biological data presented herein provide valid and reproducible evidence supporting the potential of CH-AuNPs for further investigation in colorectal cancer therapy.

Cancer cells exhibit varying degrees of dependence on survival pathways such as proliferation, cell cycle progression, and metastasis. Therefore, understanding how AuNPs interact with different cancer cell lines is crucial for optimizing nanoparticle design, therapeutic dosing, and combinational strategies with other anticancer agents. Future studies should explore the integration of CH-AuNPs with chemotherapeutic drugs to enhance their therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that CH-AuNPs induce cytotoxic and apoptotic effects in HT-29 human colorectal adenocarcinoma cells under in vitro conditions, as evidenced by reduced cell viability, decreased colony-forming ability, mitochondrial membrane potential loss, upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes (BAX, Caspase-3, and CYT C), and downregulation of the anti-apoptotic gene BCL2. These findings are exploratory and cell‑line‑specific, providing a preliminary basis for further investigation. However, additional studies—including validation in multiple colorectal cancer cell lines, assessment of selectivity using non‑malignant colon epithelial cells (e.g., CCD-841 CoN or NCM460), and in vivo evaluation—are necessary before any therapeutic application can be considered.
