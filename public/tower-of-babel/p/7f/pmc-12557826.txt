“Gives us peace of mind for keeping our hands clean”: targeted handwashing intervention increases water use and hand hygiene in rural Alaska
Authors: Amanda Hansen, Gretchen Day, Marla Wehrli, Laura Eichelberger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLack of in-home water service remains a persistent challenge in rural Alaska due to underfunding, high construction and operating costs, extreme cold, and environmental change. Alaska has the highest proportion of households in the U.S. lacking complete plumbing, resulting in self-hauling of water and waste, reuse of limited supplies, and higher rates of water-washed disease than in plumbed homes. During the COVID-19 response, the Alaska Native Tribal Health Consortium (ANTHC) and Silverline LLC developed and installed the Miniature Portable Alternative Sanitation System (Mini-PASS) in 14 unplumbed Alaska Native communities. To evaluate its impact on hand hygiene, water-hauling behaviors, and system use, we conducted seasonal interviews with 147 households. Among 139 households reporting handwashing methods, 63% (n=93) used the in-home handwashing station (HWS) as their primary method. Longer ownership was associated with greater HWS use and increased water hauling. Analysis of 163 interviews from 52 households showed a monthly rise of 0.08 gal/c/d (0.30 L/c/d) in water use following installation. These findings indicate that in-home HWS interventions such as the Mini-PASS improve hand hygiene, reduce wash-basin water reuse, and modestly increase household water use over time. Longitudinal monitoring is critical to assess sustained behavioral and WASH outcomes in unpiped communities.Keywords: Alaska, WASH, rural health, Alaska native, water quantity, hand hygiene, handwashing station

Introduction

Access to clean, free-flowing water and sanitation is a basic human right, yet nearly half of the world’s population (approximately 3.6 billion people) lack access to adequate water and sanitation services [1]. In 2019, more than 2.3 billion people (30% of the world’s population) did not have access to adequate handwashing facilities including soap and water [2,3]. The United States has more than 2 million residents living without running water and basic indoor plumbing, with many more living without sanitation services [4].

The state of Alaska has the lowest rate of in-home plumbing in the nation, with over 3,300 homes across 37 remote communities who lack complete in-home plumbing [5,6]. In addition, an unknown number of households with piped water lose service due to interruptions, cost, and repair needs, and others are stuck with old, outdated, and failing systems [7,8]. Alaska’s rugged and remote landscape often faces subzero temperatures, adding to the complex challenges of the building and maintenance of sufficient water and sanitation services. The construction and upkeep of water and sewer infrastructure in rural Alaska comes at a high cost due, in part, to its remoteness, and it is estimated that the deficit between funds available and infrastructure needed is nearly $2 billion dollars [8].

The quantity of water available to and used by households is imperative for health and required for proper hygiene behaviors. Insufficient quantities of safe water can lead to water access-related disease and contribute to the consumption of contaminated water- leading to waterborne diseases [9]. Having a sufficient supply of water available as well as the ability to store water in a protected area is known to reduce exposure to some vectors as well as reduce the potential exposure and spread of various water-washed infections [10,11]. Expert opinion suggests that 5.3 gallons per person per day (20 lpcd) is enough water for drinking, cooking, food hygiene, handwashing, and face washing, but not other practices [9]. Although this quantity is not considered enough when demands for water are increased during increased hand hygiene needs, for example, in response to infectious disease outbreaks (e.g. COVID-19) [9,12].

In Alaska, many unpiped and underserved households collect and haul their own water- a labor-intensive process that requires time, physical effort, reliable transportation, fuel, and adequate containers for collection and storage. When households lack dependable transportation-obtaining enough water becomes significantly more difficult. These households often rely on support networks (e.g. friends and family), hire a water hauler, or travel by foot, which typically involves multiple trips to gather enough water for their household [13–17]. For many of these communities' water comes from a variety of sources such as rainwater, ice, rivers, lakes, and treated water from local watering points called “Washeterias.” Washeterias are facilities that provide critical water and sanitation infrastructure to communities in Alaska without in-home piped water and sewer systems. They typically provide treated water for collection, laundry services, showers, restrooms, and designated areas for honey bucket waste disposal (with a range of user fees). Multiple factors can influence a household’s ability to haul from different water sources including availability, accessibility, and quantity that can be reliably obtained. Seasonal variation, environmental conditions, water quality, tradition, convenience, physical ability, preferences of natural sources versus treated water (usually related to taste), and extreme weather events that shut down critical public infrastructure can all affect the utilization of different water sources. A household’s inability to haul and store enough water often results in limited quantities of water, prompting the reuse of water in handwash basins, which is commonly practiced within many of these water-insecure communities [18–20].

Handwashing is recognized as one of the most effective methods for preventing the transmission and spread of diarrheal diseases, foodborne illness, and respiratory infections [21]. However, in rural unpiped Alaska communities the reuse of water in handwash basins is a common strategy to conserve limited amounts of water available within the home, creating difficulty maintaining adequate hygiene [17–19,22]. Not surprisingly, these communities experience disproportionately higher rates of acute respiratory illnesses, gastrointestinal, and skin infections [17,23–25].

The miniature portable alternative sanitation system

The COVID-19 pandemic underscored the critical role of proper hand hygiene in disease prevention, creating an urgent need for rapid access to handwashing facilities [3,21,26]. Early pandemic data revealed that residents of unplumbed ANAI communities experienced the highest rates of COVID-19 infections nationwide [27], intensifying ongoing concerns about persistent WASH and health disparities in Remote Alaska. In response, the Alaska Native Tribal Health Consortium (ANTHC) received funding from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Foundation (CDCF) and collaborated with the Alaska-based engineering company Silverline LLC., to design, develop and install portable handwashing stations to address the urgent hygiene needs in unplumbed Alaska Native communities.

The Miniature Portable Alternative Sanitation System (Mini-PASS) was developed during the COVID−19 pandemic as a targeted intervention to increase in-home access to free-flowing water, expand water storage capacity, and encourage frequent handwashing. (For a detailed description of the PASS and Mini-PASS, see [19,28]. Adapted from the more resource-intensive Portable Alternative Sanitation System (PASS), the Mini-PASS includes two components: a handwashing station (HWS) (Figure 1), and a vented honey bucket (VHB). The HWS features a sealed 20-gallon tank that feeds a gravity-fed faucet and sink, a dual-sediment filter (25-micron and 1-micron) to remove larger particulates, and a 5-gallon wastewater collection bucket equipped with an overflow alarm. All components are contained within a compact cabinet for ease of use and space efficiency. An initial 100 Mini-PASS units were installed in late 2020 and in communities without in-home running water and sanitation. These units were well-received by households. With additional CDCF funding, installations expanded, and by 2023, 373 handwashing stations and 349 vented honey buckets have been installed in fourteen communities served by the Norton Sound Health Corporation in Northwest Alaska (3 communities), the Tanana Chiefs Conference in Interior Alaska (8 communities), and the Yukon Kuskokwim Health Corporation (3 communities).

The Miniature Portable Alternative Sanitation System (Mini-PASS) handwashing station.

To assess the system’s effectiveness and acceptability, ANTHC conducted a two-year evaluation (April 2021–March 2023) examining water use and quantity, hygiene and handwashing practices, user satisfaction, perceived health impacts, and reported reductions in skin, gastrointestinal, and respiratory symptoms among children aged 12 years and younger [28]. For this paper, we report our findings on the utilization of the Mini-PASS over time and the impact the systems had on handwashing behaviors, as well as water quantity hauled and brought into the home measured in gallons per capita per day (g/c/d).

Methods

“The handwashing station has been a life changing thing for me, it's not like running water but it is because I don’t wash up in the basin and dump it out into a bucket and all that. This is an upgrade from using the basin where six people just wash up in the same water and wash hands in the same water that’s how H. pylori and that kind of thing goes around so thank you very much for the Mini-PASS.” (A quote shared by a 61-year-old female who is a Tribal Council member, living in a village in Interior Alaska).

Recruitment and eligibility

All 373 households with Mini-PASS systems installed were contacted during each data collection period. Due to COVID-19 pandemic restrictions, most outreach was conducted via phone, and households without a number on record were reached through their Tribal Council office. Households without phone service, those unavailable, and those that declined participation were excluded from the evaluation. The research team made up to three contact attempts per household, leaving messages, and callback numbers when possible. Participants were counted as those who answered the phone, gave informed verbal consent, and were a part of at least one interview. Participants had to be at least 18 years of age and self-identified as being knowledgeable about water use, sanitation, and health in their home. All participants were compensated with a $50 gift card for any completed interview. Permission to conduct this research and publish these results were obtained from the Tribal Councils, regional health organizations, the Alaska Area Institutional Review Board (#2018-03-009) as well as the Alaska Native Tribal Health Consortium Health Research Reviewee Committee, the Yukon Kuskokwim Health Corporation, and Norton Sound Health Corporation.

Data collection

We conducted seasonal semi-structured interviews with individuals from 147 households where each recruited household participated in 1 to 5 interviews across the different follow-up periods defined by time-since-installation (pre-installation, 3−5 months, 6−9 months, 11−14 months, and 15−23 months). We attempted to align interview timing with seasons known to affect water use, availability, and quality. Phone interviews lasted 30−45 minutes, with responses manually recorded in Excel or REDCap during the call to ensure data collection accuracy. Not all households completed an interview at every timepoint. The urgency of improving water access during the COVID−19 pandemic prompted the prioritization of the intervention over the research. Therefore, only 41 households completed pre-installation interviews. Although every household was contacted at each of the post-installation follow-up periods, some households participated in a 3-month post-installation interview, for example, and then again at 15 months.

Interview question topics included: water quantity and source, changes in water use, handwashing and other domestic hygiene practices, perceived health impacts, child symptoms related to skin and respiratory infections, perceptions of the system, problems encountered, adaptations made to their systems, suggestions for improvement and affordability. For this paper, we analyzed interview questions related to water quantity measured in gallons per capita per day (g/c/d), sources of hauled water, and changes to household water use, as well as handwashing, and hygiene practices. Here we aim to answer two questions by conducting two different analyses with two separate subpopulations: (1) Are households using the handwashing station? (analysis 1: n = 139 households), and (2) Does the additional storage capacity of the Mini-PASS influence the volume of water hauled and used within a home? (analysis 2: n = 52 households). For analysis 1, we included interview data from households who had the Mini-PASS for longer than one month, and for analysis 2, we included data from households who had been interviewed more than once.

Analysis 1: handwashing station utilization

Interview data, including participants’ handwashing practices, were recorded for all households receiving the Mini-PASS. For this analysis, we included the most recent interview data from 139 households (95% of all participating households with a Mini-PASS installed) who had the Mini-PASS for more than one month. Since our primary outcome was whether individuals were currently using the handwashing station (HWS), if the only interview conducted with a household occurred before the installation of the HWS or within one-month post-installation, the household was excluded from the analysis. Clearly, participants could not respond to questions regarding HWS usage without access to the station.

Direct quotes from interviews were extracted and categorized into three distinct responses: “HWS,” “Basin,” or “Sanitizer.” If a participant said they used only the HWS or the HWS and Sanitizer, they were categorized as HWS users. All other responses were grouped as Basin users. Participants who reported using a HWS and a Basin were categorized as Basin users for two main reasons. First, from a health perspective, the combination of HWS and Basin usage may involve reusing water, which is known to facilitate disease transmission. Second, there were concerns that participants knew that the interviewer was connected to the Mini-PASS project and therefore might feel compelled to claim to use the HWS intermittently while predominantly relying on the Basin for handwashing.

In addition to number and percentage of households using HWS, our descriptive analysis also examined season of interview, sex of interviewee, location of HWS in the home (bathroom vs other location), whether the community had 10 or more Mini-PASS units, time since installation of the HWS (time since installation was included as a continuous variable, ranging from 0 to 23 months), number of residents in home, percentage of vulnerable persons in the household (e.g. number of children under 12 and adults over 65 divided by the total number of residents), and total gallons of water per person per day used in the household. We calculated means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges for linear variables and counts and percentages for categorical variables.

We conducted univariate analysis of factors related to handwashing, comparing HWS use to Basin use. T-tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare linear variables, and chi square test of independence to compare categorical variables. For normally distributed variables (e.g. “time since installation” and “total residents”, we reported means and standard deviations and compared HWS-users to Basin-users using t-tests. For nonparametric variables (“percentage vulnerable in household”, “total gallons of water per person per day”, we reported medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) and tested differences using Kruskal-Wallis test. A logistic regression was used to analyze the relationship between the outcome variable, (use of HWS) and the independent variable (time since installation of the HWS), controlling for other characteristics of the household. Only “gender of interviewee” was independently associated with “use of HWS” and included in the model. (The variable, location of the HWS, had too many missing responses to include in the regression). Differences were considered significant if p-values were 0.05 or less. All analysis was done using R Statistical Software, version 4.1.3 [29].

Analysis 2: changes in household water use gallons per capita per day

To assess whether the Mini-PASS affected household water use, we examined the relationship between the time since installation and household’s self-reported g/c/d. We analyzed a total of 163 interviews from 52 unique households, representing 35% of all participating households with a Mini-PASS installed (n = 147). Each household contributed between two and five interviews. Those with fewer than two interviews were excluded from this analysis.

Participants were asked to describe their primary, secondary, and tertiary water sources; frequency of hauling; quantity collected each trip; total household water storage capacity; and the last time they filled up all the available household water storage—including the 20-gallon Mini-PASS tank—to full capacity. We also inquired about the mode of transportation to various water sources (e.g. on foot or sled, or vehicle), as well as the total time required for each trip. To estimate time, participants were asked to consider the full duration of hauling water—from preparing (e.g. dressing for the weather, loading containers, fueling and warming up vehicles) to traveling, collecting water, returning home, and transferring hauled water into household storage. We asked participants to describe each of the containers they used to collect water (in gallons) and quantity each time they hauled water. Total g/c/d was calculated by adding up the total volumes from all water sources to obtain a total weekly collection amount, divided by the total quantity by the total number of residents, and then divided by seven to obtain the total volume of water per resident per day. Time since installation was included as a continuous variable, ranging from 0 to 23 months.

We used descriptive statistics such as measures of central tendency (mean, median, and mode) and measures of dispersion or variation (range, standard deviation, variance, and quantile ranks) to describe the characteristics of households. In our descriptive analysis we examined: the total number of interviews completed, repeated interviews, interviews per community, median gallons per capita per day (g/c/d) at each installation interval, and the total volume of water placed into the 20-gallon Mini-PASS tank after each hauling event. We evaluated potential covariates that could influence g/c/d, including the number of vulnerable residents in the household, interviewee age and gender, Washeteria closures during the past month, and the season and year of the interview. Community location was one factor of interest, as the study sites represented three distinct environmental settings—coastal, permafrost, and tundra. Seasonality was also considered important, given that the availability of public water infrastructure and natural water sources in Alaska varies throughout the year, affecting water use and hauling behaviors.

Statistical approaches for repeated measures data commonly include random effects (RE) models, fixed effects (FE) models, and generalized estimating equations (GEE) [30]. Generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) build on GEEs by integrating both fixed and random effects, allowing researchers to examine how predictors affect individual subjects, whereas GEEs focus on estimating average effects at the population level [31,32]. Based on household being a repeated unit in this analysis with the overall objective to measure changes to the outcome variable over time, we utilized a GEE approach. We conducted a Hausman test and determined a random effects model for this analysis. Differences were considered significant if p-values were 0.05 or less. All analysis was done using R Statistical Software, version 4.1.3 [29].

“We still get water the same, except for now we’re not putting our pitchers into the bucket to put it into a basin. It’s less work and we get more water, and we do it a lot less than before. Before we were just using 5-gallon buckets at a time and getting water more often. Now that we have more in-home water storage, we fill it up every other week.” (A 57-year-old grandmother of 9, and a life-long resident of an Interior community, 5 months’ post-installation).

Description of population

In order to analyze changes in household water use, we only included households who participated in more than one interview (n = 52, Table 1). Seventy percent (n = 36) of households completed between 2 and 3 interviews at various time points between 0 and 23 months’ post-installation of the handwashing station. The remaining 16 (30%) of households completed between 4−5 interviews post-installation.

Table 1 shows a breakdown of the total number of interviews and when they occurred post-installation. The majority of interviews were conducted during the Winter season (n = 98, 60%), followed by Summer (n = 50, 30%), with fewer conducted in the Fall (n = 12, 7%) and Spring (n = 3, 2%). A total of 78% of interviews were conducted in 2021 and 2022 (n = 63; n = 64, ~39% in each year), while the remaining 22% were conducted in 2023 (n = 36). More than half of the respondents identified as female (n = 93, 57%), one-fifth were between the ages of 18−30 years old (n = 33, 20%), half were between 31 and 64 years old (n = 80,49%), and almost a third (n = 49, 30%) were over the age of 65 years old.

Results

We evaluated for the potential effect of season on gallons per capita per day (g/c/d). Due to the concentration of interviews in Winter, seasons were aggregated into three categories: Fall/Spring, Winter, and Summer. Analysis using a random effects model indicated a statistically significant association (p = 0.0289) to changes to g/c/d during the Winter months. Although seasonal variation is expected to influence water access and use in Alaska—given fluctuations in the availability of public water infrastructure and natural sources—our ability to detect such effects is limited by the disproportionate representation of Winter interviews as well as the overall small sample size. Consequently, no definitive conclusions regarding seasonal effects on g/c/d can be drawn from the present analysis. Nonetheless, these results underscore the importance of future research explicitly designed to capture seasonal variation in household water use and hauling behaviors.

A GEE model of the data examining the relationship between the time since installation and gallons per capita per day showed an increase of 0.08 g/c/d (0.303 lpcd) on average each month after installation occurred (p-value = 0.056) (95% CI: −0.74 to 0.90). In other words, after 12 months post-installation, a household with (e.g. with 6 residents) would on average see an increase of 0.96 g/c/d (3.6 lpcd) which would translate to an overall increase of 5.8 g/c/d (22 lpcd) (0.08 × 12 months x 6 residents). We evaluated for other potential covariates that could influence g/c/d: the number of vulnerable residents in the household, interviewee age and gender, Washeteria closures during the past month, and the season and year of the interview. We found no other significant impact on g/c/d from any other variable.

Discussion

In this section, we discuss our findings in comparison to the sparse global health literature on handwashing interventions. Most existing literature on hand hygiene interventions focuses on increasing hand hygiene within institutions- or communal settings such as (e.g. schools, or hospitals, and primarily in other medical settings) mainly within low-income countries. Many of these studies have evaluated equipment-based interventions such as a handwashing station in a communal location (e.g. schools) paired with an educational campaign or hygiene curriculum [33–39], whereas others have investigated behavior changes through identifying social determinants of handwashing (e.g social influence, knowledge, intention) [40–43] or barriers and facilitators to communal handwashing [44]. Several of our findings indicate contextual and demographic factors that may influence HWS and water use, but these are limited by our small sample size. In our univariate analysis we found a positive association between the location of the HWS and its use. Although the association was not included in our logistic regression, due to missing values, other scholars have found similar associations between location of the handwashing station and water use [34,36,45]. A recent nonrandomized trial conducted in Bangladesh introduced handwashing stations with soap located adjacent to homes, with evidence that showed more than a five-fold increase in handwashing with soap and water [46]. While most of these studies are not directly comparable to ours, together these results indicate that conveniently placed handwashing stations may improve hand hygiene practices.

In our study, we found that the majority (n = 91, 65%) of Mini-PASS households reported sustained primary use of the system over time, a behavioral pattern suggesting households quickly accepted and consistently utilized the Mini-PASS. Time-since-installation had the most significant positive association with HWS use for handwashing: the longer household had the HWS, the more likely there were to report using it as their primary hand hygiene method (p < 0.02). Our results also indicate that households with the handwashing stations steadily increased the amount of water they hauled and used over time (p = 0.056; 95% CI: −0.74 to 0.90). Households reported increasing their per capita daily water use by an average of 0.08 g/c/d (0.303 lpcd) per month following installation. To illustrate the potential cumulative effect, after 12 months post-installation, a household (e.g. with six residents) would experience an average increase of 0.96 gallons of water per person per day, resulting in an overall increase of 5.8 gallons of water per person per day (0.08 g/c/d × 12 months × 6 residents). We surmise that the increased water use indicates a decrease in washbasin water reuse and correlates with the reported increased handwashing in free-flowing water using the HWS. The additional storage capacity of the Mini-PASS indicates an influence on the amount of water households can haul and store over time. However, more research is needed to determine the longer term impacts this additional storage can have for households and the ways in which this additional water can impact use and health within the home.

These findings fit into trends seen in other studies showing increased household water use with infrastructure improvements. Using self-reported logs and post-installation monthly water meters, Thomas et al. [17] found that the installation of in-home piped water significantly increased household water use. Pre-installation data (n = 191) showed an average of 1.5 g/c/d (5.7 lcpd), with post installation average of 25.7 g/c/d (97.3 lcpd) (ranging from 9.2 g/c/d to 37.9 g/c/d (34.8 lcpd−149.08 lcpd)), varying by each community. Such increases in in-home water use indicate significant health benefits, as demonstrated by their data showing an overall decline in clinic visits for respiratory infections (−16%) (95% CI: 11−21%), skin infections (−20%) (95% CI: 10−30%), and gastrointestinal illness (−38%) (95% CI: 13−55%). These findings demonstrate that improvements in household water infrastructure are consistently associated with substantial increases in water use and corresponding reductions in infectious disease, underscoring the profound health benefits of adequate and reliable in-home water service.

For communities still awaiting the investment and infrastructure required for piped running water, interim solutions can help provide partial relief and provide potential health and hygiene benefits. One study conducted in a remote subarctic community in Canada, evaluated domestic rainwater harvesting by installing rainwater collection units in seven households. They collected weekly surveys on water consumption amounts, water use, costs, volumes of rainwater harvested, and hours spent collecting water, and by conducting pre- and post-project focus group discussions [47]. Similar to our findings, the rainwater units were well-received and increased the amount of water available within the home. On average, families harvested an additional ~19.07 gallons per week, increasing the overall water available within the home by 17%. [47]. Taken together, both studies demonstrate that small-scale, context appropriate interventions can generate health and well-being benefits by enabling efficient use of limited water resources. Consistent with our results, this project illustrates that even modest incremental additions to household water supplies-whether potable or not, can support improved hygiene practices within the home. Yet, while such interventions offer important short-term relief, they cannot substitute for the basic human right to safe, accessible, and sufficient water in the home. A recent study in rural Greenland, found that roughly 80% of households still lack in-home piped water, with daily use averaging only 3 gallons per person per day (13 lpcd) in unpiped homes compared to 15 gallons per person per day (55 lpcd) in piped households [48]. Small-scale interventions can mitigate some of the health and psychosocial burdens of water insecurity, but without equitable investment in infrastructure, Arctic and subarctic communities will continue to face systemic water inequities that no stopgap solution can fully resolve.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective cohort study in Alaska to evaluate a household-based hand hygiene intervention. One of the strengths of our study methods is that we did not use fixed recall periods [49]. Instead, our “last-collection date” approach accommodated real-world variability: households reported intervals from 1 day to 1 month, with the majority hauling water every few days to once a week—depending on routines, household size, mobility challenges, and other factors. In contrast to studies that directly measured volumes with scales or graduated containers [50–53], or those relying on known or estimated vessel sizes [49,54–59], we relied on detailed descriptions from participants, probing questions, and cross-checks against prior interviews to approximate container volumes.

This study has several limitations, many of which stem from the challenges of conducting research during the height of pandemic, when many travel restrictions were implemented. Our conclusions are limited by the overall small sample size (52 households) and missing values needed for comparative analyses. The urgency of the COVID-19 pandemic prompted the prioritization of the intervention over the research, which not only limited the number of pre-installation interviews (n = 41) available for baseline data but also likely affected participation at various post-installation timepoints as households faced lockdowns, changes in phone numbers or service, and other disruptions that made consistent follow-up challenging. This limited our longitudinal analysis and ultimately impacted the generalizability of our results. During the pandemic, there was an increased emphasis on public health messaging that encouraged frequent handwashing to reduce disease transmission and spread. For this study, we collected minimal evidence to draw any conclusions on how the pandemic and mitigation strategies used during this time influenced water use and hauling behaviors, however, it was a constant variable during our study timeline and may have influenced results in unforeseeable ways.

Like many previous studies, we relied on self-reported data, which is subject to recall bias, and to limit this, other scholars have relied on water access proxies such as distance to and time for water collection [9,60–65]. In order to address this potential limitation of self-reported data, we asked detailed questions around hauling of each water source (e.g. method of hauling, size and number of containers, distance, and time). In the future, we plan to expand on our findings and address some of these limitations by recruiting a larger sample size from a cohort of households in rural Alaska utilizing various types of water and sewer services and systems (i.e. the Portable Alternative Sanitation System (PASS), the Mini-PASS, piped, and unpiped households). One piped community will serve as a control, and these households will be interviewed across multiple seasons to assess household water storage, use, water reliability, health indicators, and the prevalence of symptoms of water-washed diseases to better understand how each of these variables differ by water and sanitation service type and/or lack thereof. Collecting this information will help to better inform public health and tribal organizations on the impact water and sanitation service types have on overall health and wellbeing.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the limited number of studies that have explored hand hygiene and water use in Alaska, and to the sparse global health literature on the utilization of handwashing stations. Our findings indicate that in-home handwashing stations facilitate household handwashing practices. Although our findings show that the increased per capita water use still does not meet the recommended 50 lpcd, based on the reported reduction in respiratory symptoms among children ≤12 years old in Mini-PASS households [28], we believe that the Mini-PASS HWS facilitates efficient use of small amounts of water in ways that protect household health. Together, these findings highlight the important role that an affordable and decentralized water system solution, such as the Mini-PASS, can play in helping households transition from the widespread use of wash basins to utilizing free-flowing water for handwashing, with the potential of influence on water hauling capacity with the additional water storage tank provided by the units.

Importantly, the Mini-PASS is not intended to replace piped water, which remains the most critical and preferred solution for achieving water security and health equity. Instead, the Mini-PASS should be understood as a complementary tool—an affordable, decentralized, and interim strategy that can improve access to free-flowing water while communities await permanent infrastructure investments. By reducing reliance on shared wash basins and promoting more frequent handwashing, the Mini-PASS can help mitigate risks of water-washed diseases and support the wellbeing of Alaska Native households in underserved regions.
