Personalized risk prediction of lateral lymph node metastasis in male papillary thyroid cancer: a sex-specific model leveraging central lymph node ratio
Authors: Xue Zhang, Yuan Qin, Niuniu Hou, Yunjiao Zhang, Changjiao Yan, Xi Chen, Chutuo Liu, Chong Chen, Rui Ling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsNotable sex-based disparities exist in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), necessitating male-specific models for predicting lateral lymph node metastasis (LLNM) to guide treatment.MethodsWe developed and validated two nomograms, based on central lymph node ratio (LNR) and preoperative ultrasound (US), respectively, using data from 433 male PTC patients who underwent total thyroidectomy (TT) with central and lateral neck lymph node dissection. Multivariable analysis identified key risk factors. Model performance was evaluated using ROC curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) across training and validation sets.ResultsMale patients had 1.33-fold higher odds of central lymph node metastasis (CLNM) and 1.51-fold higher odds of LLNM than female patients (p < 0.05). Multivariable analysis identified multifocality, tumor size >20 mm, preoperative US nodal status, and an elevated LNR as independent risk factors for LLNM in males (p < 0.05). The LNR-based nomogram demonstrated superior predictive accuracy, with AUCs of 0.828 (training), 0.812 (internal validation), and 0.832 (external validation), outperforming the US-based model. Risk stratification using nomogram scores effectively categorized patients into three distinct groups with significantly different LLNM rates.ConclusionsThe nomogram based on the central LNR provides a precise and clinically valuable tool for the individualized prediction of LLNM in male PTC patients.KEYWORDS: Lateral lymph node metastasis, papillary thyroid carcinoma, lymph node ratio, nomogram, male

Introduction

Thyroid cancer represents the most prevalent malignant tumor within the endocrine system. According to the latest GLOBOCAN estimates (2022), thyroid carcinoma ranks as the seventh most common cancer globally, with 830,000 new cases reported, which has become the fastest-growing among all malignancies worldwide [1,2]. Notably, papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) constitutes the predominant histopathological type, accounting for approximately 90% of all thyroid cancers [3].

PTC exhibits a marked gender disparity, with a female-to-male incidence ratio of 3:1 to 4:1 [4]. Although male patients constitute only about one-third of the total cases, research indicate that distinct clinicopathological characteristics are present in male patients compared to female patients. Specifically, male PTC patients tend to present with significantly larger primary tumor diameters, a higher prevalence of extrathyroidal extension (ETE) [5], and increased rates of cervical lymph node and distant metastasis, suggesting more aggressive disease progression [6]. These sex-based clinicopathological differences correlate with a higher risk of disease recurrence and reduced 5-year survival rates [7].

Although most PTCs exhibit indolent growth characteristics, postoperative pathology reveals that lymph node metastasis occurs in 30%–80% of cases [8,9]. These metastases are categorized into central lymph node metastasis (CLNM) and lateral lymph node metastasis (LLNM) [10]. A positive correlation exists between the number of metastatic lymph nodes and recurrence risk. In particular, LLNM is associated with reduced disease-specific survival (DSS) and overall survival (OS) [11,12]. Currently, preoperative assessment of lymph node metastasis (LNM) remains a challenging clinical problem. Imaging modalities including ultrasound (US), computed tomography (CT), and fine-needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB) are routinely employed for evaluating suspicious malignant nodes and LNM. However, their diagnostic accuracy is limited by factors such as operator-dependent experience, lesion size, and the quality of the specimens obtained [13,14]. Lymph node ratio (LNR), defined as the proportion of metastasis-positive lymph nodes to the total number of lymph nodes dissected, provides a more comprehensive prognostic metric compared to the number of metastatic lymph nodes alone. LNR has been correlated with prognosis and TNM stage in multiple malignancies, including gastric and colorectal cancers [15,16]. In PTC with CLNM, combining LNR with metastatic lymph node size offers a valuable parameter for risk stratification and prognosis [17].

Because of the significant risks associated with LLNM dissection, such as recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and permanent hypoparathyroidism, the American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines [18] recommend that therapeutic dissection be performed only for N1b (defined as unilateral, bilateral, or contralateral cervical lymph node metastasis or retropharyngeal lymph node metastasis) confirmed by preoperative evaluation or intraoperative pathology. However, the omission of dissection for occult metastasis frequently results in locoregional recurrence, necessitating reoperation [12]. Given limited studies on LNM in male PTC specifically, this research aimed to identify risk factors for LLNM. Except for rare cases of “jumping metastasis,” the metastatic progression of PTC generally adheres to a sequential pattern, which usually occurs in CLNM first and then LLNM [19]. Consequently, this study developed a predictive model for LLNM incorporating the central LNR with other clinicopathological features and conducted a comparative analysis with the conventional model based on preoperative US. The comparative analysis guides clinicians in surgical planning, postoperative monitoring, and adjuvant treatment for male patients with PTC.

Materials and methods

Research subjects

A retrospective analysis was conducted on 9,105 patients diagnosed with PTC and treated at the Department of Thyroid, Breast and Vascular Surgery, Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University from January 2010 to December 2023. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (1) patients who underwent initial surgery and postoperative pathology consistent with PTC; (2) those who received total thyroidectomy (TT)/lobectomy accompanied by cervical lymph node dissection, with comprehensive pathological reports documenting lymph node dissection and metastasis; (3) individuals aged 18 years or older; and (4) patients had complete medical records. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) coexistence of PTC with other pathological types; (2) incomplete clinicopathological information; (3) history of prior thyroid surgery; (4) missing lymph node dissection records; and (5) patients who underwent US-guided thyroid radiofrequency ablation. TT with central and lateral cervical lymph node dissection was performed on patients under the following conditions: (1) Presence of LLNM suspected or confirmed by any preoperative examination: This included US findings indicative of malignancy, such as round or quasi-round shapes, microcalcifications, or cystic degeneration, as detailed in Methods 2.2; CT revealing radiological signs highly suggestive of LLNM; FNAC providing cytological confirmation of malignancy in lateral lymph node. (2) Intraoperative identification of significant tumor burden in the central compartment: The presence of extensive CLNM discovered during intraoperative exploration, which heightened the suspicion of occult LLNM. This decision was made by the surgeon based on factors such as a high number of involved nodes, large metastatic foci, or extracapsular extension in the central compartment. Patients treated with lobectomy were excluded from the modeling cohort, ensuring the analysis focused specifically on the high-risk population relevant to our prediction objective.

After screening by inclusion and exclusion criteria, we identified 5457 eligible PTC patients (4031 females; 1426 males). In this study, we analyzed a cohort of 433 male patients who underwent TT with both central (defined as having ≥3 central lymph nodes examined) and lateral lymph node dissection. These cases were randomized into groups according to a ratio of 7:3, with the training set (n = 303) and internal validation set (n = 130). Additionally, 110 male patients from the department of General Surgery, Xijing 986 Hospital from January 2022 to June 2025, were collected as the external validation set (Figure 1).

Clinicopathological properties

The study analyzed comprehensive clinicopathological characteristics including demographic characteristics (gender, marital status, age stratified as <55 vs. ≥55 years) [20], concurrent diseases (coexistence with other malignancies, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension), family history of thyroid cancer, follow-up duration (categorized as <24 vs. ≥24 months), preoperative thyroid function [thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH: 0.27–4.20μIU/mL) and thyroglobulin (Tg: 3.5–77.0 ng/mL)], pathological features [Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT), tumor location (unilaterality/bilaterality), tumor number (unifocality/multifocality), tumor diameter (categorized as <20 mm vs. ≥20 mm) [20], quantitative lymph node metrics (including the number of dissected and metastatic lymph nodes in both central and lateral regions, LNR), and preoperative US nodal status.

Follow-up duration was defined as the time between detection of the lesion and surgery. The diagnosis of HT was based on postoperative paraffin pathology and preoperative serum immunoelectrochemical detection that TgAb ≥115IU/ml and TPO-Ab ≥34IU/ml. Tumor diameter was defined as the largest tumor in the lesions. LNR defined as the proportion of metastasis-positive lymph nodes to the total number of lymph nodes dissected. The cutoff was established at 0.5 based on previous studies [21]. Preoperative US nodal status assessment Criteria [22]: If preoperative US demonstrates any of the following malignant features, lateral lymph node (LLN) is classified as suspicious for metastasis (cN1b): round or quasi-round (long-to-short axis ratio <2), microcalcification (punctate hyperechoic foci), cystic degeneration, hypoechoic areas, irregular cortical thickening ( > 2–3 mm), disappearance of hilar structure, unclear boundary between cortex and medulla, abnormal vascular patterns (peripheral or diffuse increased blood flow). If preoperative US finds preservation of normal lymph node architecture and features of reactive hyperplasia, the LLN is deemed as negative for metastasis.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 and R software (version 4.3.3). Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p < 0.05. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (%), and compared using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test. Significant predictors for LLNM were identified through univariable/multivariable analyses. These predictors were subsequently incorporated into two predictive nomograms based on central LNR or preoperative US using the rms package. Model performance was assessed by: (1) discrimination: evaluated by the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC) (pROC package), the Area Under the Curve (AUC), and 95% confidence intervals (CI); (2) calibration was assessed through Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit testing (p > 0.05 indicating adequate fit) and calibration curves using the Hare-Jansen estimation and bootstrap method, and (3) clinical utility was verified through decision curve analysis (DCA) across training, internal and external validation sets.

Results

Comparison of clinicopathological characteristics between male and female PTC patients

The 5457 eligible PTC patients demonstrate significant gender disparity with 4,031 (73.9%) female and 1,426 (26.1%) male patients (female-to-male ratio ≈3:1). These findings collectively indicate that male PTC patients present with advanced disease features despite lower overall incidence. In addition, marital status, coexistence with other malignancies, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, TSH, and Tg levels were significantly different between the two groups (p < 0.05).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of the above factors identified four independent predictors of LLNM in male PTC patients: tumor multifocality (OR = 1.911, 95% CI: 1.015–3.598, p = 0.045), larger tumor diameter (> 20 mm; OR = 5.270, 95% CI: 1.609–17.263, p = 0.006), suspicious metastasis (cN1b) by preoperative US (OR = 2.687, 95% CI: 1.245–5.799, p = 0.012) and central LNR. The LNR demonstrated a strong association with LLNM risk. Patients with 0 < LNR < 0.5 had a 7.706-fold increased LLNM risk (95% CI: 2.857–20.785, p < 0.001) and those with LNR ≥0.5 exhibiting 17.729-fold higher risk (95% CI: 7.652–41.080, p < 0.001) compared to node-negative cases. Construction of individualized prediction model

Based on the results of the multivariate analysis, we developed two distinct nomogram models in male patients, named Nomogram A (Figure 2(A)) and Nomogram B (Figure 2(B)). The models were constructed using R software (version 4.3.3). The length of each variable segment directly reflected its weighted contribution to the predictive model. The total score corresponding to LLNM was calculated by adding the individual scores of the four predictors. Based on this scoring system, the probability of LLNM predicted by the two nomograms ranged from 0.5 to 0.95.

Two nomograms predicting the risk of LLNM in male with PTC. A Nomogram A based on central LNR; B Nomogram B based on preoperative US.

Comparison of the performance of two nomograms

The discriminative ability of the two predictive models was evaluated using ROC analysis in the training set (Figure 3(a–b)). Nomogram A demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy with an AUC of 0.828 (95% CI: 0.775–0.881) compared to Nomogram B (AUC = 0.725, 95% CI: 0.667–0.783). Hosmer–Lemeshow testing (Nomogram A: χ2 = 4.521, p = 0.71; Nomogram B: χ2 = 7.775, p = 0.169) and calibration curves (Figure 3(e–f)) revealed good model fit for both nomograms. DCA curve (Figure 3(i-j)) demonstrated that predictive model had a higher clinical benefit than the extreme model curve across probability thresholds of 0.02–0.85 for Nomogram A and 0.14–0.84 for Nomogram B. However, Nomogram A showed significantly superior predictive performance compared to Nomogram B, highlighting the significant superiority of central LNR for LLNM prediction.

Internal and external validation of the nomogram A

To further verify the performance of Nomogram A, the AUC values of this model in the internal and external validation sets are 0.812 (95% CI 0.734–0.889) and 0.832 (95% CI 0.733–0.930), respectively, and the ROC curves are shown in Figure 3(c–d). Calibration curves (Figure 3(g–h)) show excellent consistency between predicted and observed outcomes in both cohorts. The DCA results show (Figure 3(k–l)) that the model has good clinical utility in predicting LLNM in male PTC patients, with clinical benefit observed at threshold probability ranges of 8%–82% for the internal validation set and 3%–60% for the external validation set.

Novel risk stratification based on the predictive nomogram

Based on total risk scores derived from Nomogram A, we selected appropriate cutoff values and categorized patients into three subgroups as follows: (1) low-risk group, (2) moderate-risk group, and (3) high-risk group for LLNM. The observed LLNM rates demonstrated significant differences across risk groups: low-risk (21.9%, 28.6%, 33.3%), medium-risk (54.7%, 53.1%, 60.7%), and high-risk cohorts (87.9%, 85.7%, 94.0%), with consistent gradients in training, internal and external validation sets (Figure 4).

Discussion

This population-based study revealed four principal observations: First, male PTC patients demonstrated significantly more aggressive tumor progression compared to female counterparts (p < 0.001). Second, multivariate analysis identified five independent predictors of LLNM in male PTC patients, including age <55 years, tumor diameter >20 mm, multifocality, suspicious metastasis as indicated by preoperative US, and central LNR. Third, these parameters were integrated into two validated nomograms. The model based on central LNR demonstrated robust predictive accuracy (AUC: 0.81–0.83) and was compared with the conventional model based on preoperative US. Finally, risk stratification based on nomogram scores effectively categorized patients into distinct prognostic groups with significantly different LLNM rates.

Recent advancements in physical examination have contributed to a progressive increase in disease detection rates, with thyroid carcinoma now representing the most rapidly rising malignancy worldwide. Consistent with previous reports [23], our analysis revealed significant gender disparities in PTC. While male patients exhibited lower overall incidence compared to females, they presented with more aggressive clinicopathological features, including larger primary tumor diameter and significantly higher rates of LNM. In this study, CLNM and LLNM were 1.33 and 1.51 times more likely in male patients than in female patients (p < 0.05). These findings support the notion that gender is an independent factor influencing tumorigenesis and metastatic potential [24]. Although the exact mechanisms remain incompletely understood, growing evidence suggests that gender-based disparities may be closely associated with hormonal, genetic, and tumor microenvironmental factors [25]. The androgen receptor (AR) can promote tumor invasiveness by activating epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathways, thereby enhancing metastatic potential in male patients [26]. An imbalance between estrogen receptor (ER) α and β isoforms influences thyroid cell proliferation and differentiation, with reduced ER-β expression associated with more aggressive disease in women [27]. Genomic studies have further revealed that male PTC exhibits a higher prevalence of BRAF V600E or TERT promoter mutations compared to females [28]. Moreover, single-cell transcriptomic profiling revealed gender-specific differences in B-cell populations, including distinct clustering patterns, differentiation trajectories, and dominant gene signatures that may modulate the tumor immune microenvironment in a sex-dependent manner [29]. While Sokouti et al. reported significantly higher recurrence and mortality rates in male versus female PTC patients [30], a subsequent propensity score matching (PSM) analysis by Park et al. found no statistically significant gender differences in recurrence rates or disease-free survival (DFS) among male and female with differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) [31]. Although the prognostic impact of gender in thyroid cancer remains controversial, the presence and location of lymph node metastases in the cervical region remain major independent risk factors for local recurrence and poor prognosis in PTC patients [12,32]. Missing nodal dissection increases risk of local recurrence and metastasis, which may pose a threat to survival. Although successful secondary surgery can mitigate survival risk, it carries significantly higher complication rates including lymphatic leakage, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, and permanent hypoparathyroidism, adversely affecting quality of life [33,34].

Existing predictive models for LLNM are primarily based on studies of the general population, with limited gender-specific analyses [35,36]. Wu et al. attempted to address this gap by developing distinct preoperative and postoperative models specifically for male patients with PTC. However, their research was constrained by a small single-center cohort (n = 216) and the absence of external validation [37]. Considering the gender differences in morbidity and clinicopathological characteristics, gender-specific predictive models are essential for more accurate management. Consequently, this study systematically evaluates risk factors associated with LLNM in male-specific PTC and develops validated predictive models to guide surgical extent determination, postoperative monitoring, and adjuvant treatment.

Surgeons are faced with the critical challenge of balancing the risks of missed metastatic lymph nodes against overtreatment. Thus, there is a pressing need for more objective and precise methods to predict LLNM. Conventional preoperative examinations such as US and CT demonstrate limited sensitivity due to the complex anatomical structure of cervical lymph nodes and operator-dependent variability, particularly in the assessment of LLNM [38,39]. Furthermore, the accuracy of FNAC is influenced by factors such as poor sampling quality and lesion size. Recent cytopathological data indicate that approximately 1–3% of nodules classified as Bethesda II nodules (benign) on preoperative FNA are ultimately diagnosed as malignant, whereas about 20% Bethesda III nodules (atypia of undetermined significance or follicular lesion of undetermined significance, AUS/FLUS) exhibit a final malignant diagnosis [40,41]. These statistics highlight the inherent limitations of preoperative FNA, particularly in cases with indeterminate cytology. Patrik et al. showed that FNAC had a diagnostic sensitivity of 86.5% for lesions >1 cm and 61.5% for lesions <1 cm [42]. Therefore, patients with insufficient preoperative evidence of LNM may be evaluated intraoperatively. The ATA guidelines recognize LNR as a critical risk factor for disease recurrence [43]. As in previous studies, we also found a positive correlation between central LNR and the risk of LLNM [44]. Building on this evidence, we developed an integrated predictive model that incorporates LNR alongside key clinicopathological characteristics to enhance LLNM prediction accuracy.

This retrospective analysis identified significant predictors of LLNM in male patients through univariate analysis. The multivariate logistic model excluding central LNR revealed age <55 years, tumor diameter >20 mm, and preoperative US nodal status (cN1b) as independent risk factors. The subsequent inclusion of central LNR resulted in an enhanced predictive model. We developed and validated two distinct nomograms: Nomogram A (LNR-based) demonstrated superior performance compared to Nomogram B (US-based). The C index is 0.828 (training set), 0.812 (internal validation set), and 0.831 (external validation set). Nomogram A showed significantly improved discriminative ability, providing an accurate model for LLNM risk in male PTC patients.

The findings have important implications for managing male PTC patients. We recommend routine intraoperative frozen section analysis for suspected CLNM and, when frozen pathology shows a high LNR or the model predicts a high probability of LLNM, TT with central and lateral neck dissection. Using the risk stratification from Nomogram A, patients are divided into three LLNM risk groups: High-risk (LLNM 85.7–94.0%) – comprehensive therapeutic lateral neck dissection, higher dose radioiodine, strict TSH suppression, and intensive cervical US surveillance. Low-risk (LLNM 21.9–33.3%) – a more conservative surgical approach, reduced adjuvant therapy, and less intensive follow-up. moderate-risk (LLNM 53.1–60.7%) – intraoperative frozen section of suspicious lateral nodes to guide the final extent of dissection. The nomogram provides a visual estimate of individual LLNM risk, facilitating clear communication with patients and supporting shared decision-making regarding surgical options and postoperative management.

Compared to subtotal thyroidectomy (STT), TT is associated with a higher incidence of early complications, particularly hypocalcemia [45,46] and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury [45,47]. However, as shown in Table 2 and Results 3.2, male PTC patients in our cohort exhibited more aggressive clinicopathological features than females, including 60% bilateral lobe involvement, 50% multifocal disease, and 80% preoperative US suspicion of LLNM. Current guidelines classify these patients as high risk, recommending TT. Furthermore, our objective was to develop a predictive model for LLNM in male patients. Accurate staging necessitates comprehensive lateral neck dissection, which is routinely performed together with TT. Although TT carries a higher risk of early complications, the cohort’s heavy disease burden (73.9% LLNM in the training set) makes the oncologic advantage of TT outweigh the incremental surgical risk. Although this study has many advantages in predicting LLNM in male PTC patients, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias due to the exclusion of cases with incomplete clinicopathological data. Second, the occurrence of “lymph node skip metastasis” [48,49] in some patients may lead to false-negative predictions when relying on solely central LNR. Third, the analysis of lymph nodes was restricted to broader central and lateral cervical regions rather than detailed compartments (levels I-V). Finally, LNR dependence on intraoperative assessment makes it difficult to assess the LLNM status in advance through preoperative examinations. However, the majority of patients with PTC maintain a preserved performance status, which allows for a thorough intraoperative frozen section evaluation to inform precise surgical management.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study successfully addresses the critical need for gender-specific predictive tools in PTC management by developing and validating a novel nomogram specifically for male patients. The model, which integrates the LNR with key clinicopathological factors such as tumor multifocality, size, and preoperative US status, demonstrates robust and consistent performance. Its superior predictive accuracy over the conventional US-based model underscores the significant clinical value of incorporating quantitative intraoperative pathologic metrics like LNR. Moreover, the model’s capacity for effective risk stratification into low-risk, moderate-risk, and high-risk categories provides a practical framework for customizing surgical aggressiveness and postoperative surveillance. This approach aims to optimize patient outcomes by minimizing both overtreatment and the risk of undetected metastases. Consequently, this tool represents a significant advancement toward personalized, evidence-based surgical decision-making for male PTC patients.
