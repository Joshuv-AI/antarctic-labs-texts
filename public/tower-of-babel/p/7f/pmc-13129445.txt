Keratinocytes: A key player in skin immunity and their emerging roles in secondary lymphedema
Authors: Xizhao Chen, Kevin Kuonqui, Sarit Pal, Jinyeon Shin, Annica Stull-Lane, Gopika Ashokan, Geoffrey Hespe, Babak J. Mehrara, Raghu P. Kataru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Keratinocytes, once viewed as inert epidermal scaffolds, are now recognized as active participants in the cutaneous immunity. They orchestrate local immune responses by releasing cytokines, chemokines, and alarmins that shape innate and adaptive inflammation. Dysregulated keratinocyte-immune interactions underline chronic inflammatory skin disorders, such as atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, and cutaneous lupus erythematosus. More recently, keratinocytes have been identified as key contributors to secondary lymphedema, a chronic inflammatory and fibrotic condition historically attributed to lymphatic dysfunction. This review revises our understanding of keratinocytes as immunomodulatory cells, while focusing primarily on their emerging role in the pathogenesis of secondary lymphedema. We highlight and appraise recent evidence that lymphatic stasis reprograms keratinocyte differentiation, induces type 2 helper T cells-skewed inflammatory responses, and perpetuates chronic inflammation and fibrosis. Understanding this keratinocyte-immune-lymphatic axis may unveil novel therapeutic targets beyond traditional lymphangiogenic approaches.

Introduction

Keratinocytes (KCs) comprise 80–90% of the epidermis and form the skin’s first physical and immunological barrier (Losquadro, 2017). Originating from basal progenitor cells, they undergo terminal differentiation through the stratum spinosum, granulosum, and corneum to form the cornified envelope that prevents water loss and microbial invasion (Chan, 2023; Losquadro, 2017). Epidermal renewal occurs approximately every three weeks through tightly coordinated cycles of keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation, a process governed by transcription factors such as p63 and AP-2γ (Candi et al, 2006; Koster et al, 2006; Lee et al, 2017; Rochat and Barrandon, 2009). This dynamic balance between regeneration and differentiation preserves epidermal integrity and enables rapid repair following injury.

Historically, keratinocytes were regarded as passive structural components of the skin barrier. This paradigm has shifted dramatically in the past two decades. Keratinocytes are now recognized as immunologically active sentinels that sense environmental stress, microbial products, and tissue damage, subsequently orchestrating local immune responses (Boulanger and Lenormand, 2018; Nestle et al, 2009). They express diverse pattern recognition receptors—including toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors, and C-type lectins—that detect pathogen- and damage-associated molecular patterns (Bianchi, 2007; Nestle et al, 2009). In response, KCs release antimicrobial peptides (Boulanger and Lenormand, 2018; Clausen and Agner, 2016; Morizane et al, 2010; Rademacher et al, 2019), secrete cytokines and chemokines that regulate both innate and adaptive immunity (Du et al, 2016; Hasegawa et al, 2022), and, under inflammatory conditions, can even present antigen to CD4+ T cells through major histocompatibility complex class II molecules (Meister et al, 2015).

The recognition of KCs as immune effector cells has been reinforced by their established roles in chronic inflammatory dermatoses, including atopic dermatitis (AD), psoriasis, and cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE). In these conditions, KC-derived cytokines and alarmins initiate, amplify, and sustain inflammation, demonstrating that the epidermis functions as an active immune organ rather than a passive target of immune attack. These discoveries have redefined KCs as central regulators of cutaneous immunity—cells capable of maintaining homeostasis in a steady state yet driving inflammation when dysregulated.

Despite these advances, the role of KCs in disorders beyond classical dermatoses remains underexplored. In particular, diseases involving chronic inflammation secondary to lymphatic dysfunction, such as secondary lymphedema, have historically been conceptualized as mechanical disorders of lymphatic obstruction. However, emerging evidence suggests that lymphedema is a chronic inflammatory and fibrotic disease driven by persistent immune dysregulation rather than fluid accumulation alone (Avraham et al, 2013; Li et al, 2020; Ly et al, 2019; Park et al, 20231).

Recent transcriptomic and histological studies have identified striking epidermal changes in lymphedematous skin: hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis, aberrant keratin expression, and altered barrier function (Campbell et al, 2025a; Campisi et al, 2019; Domaszewska-Szostek et al, 2016; Park et al, 2025; Torrisi et al, 2015; Wolf et al, 2022). These findings suggest that KCs, much like in inflammatory dermatoses, may respond to cytokine-rich interstitial fluid and mechanical stress from lymphatic stasis by releasing alarmins and profibrotic mediators (Qiao et al, 2019). This epidermal-immune-lymphatic axis may perpetuate local inflammation, impair lymphangiogenesis, and drive chronic tissue remodeling.

Understanding KC contributions to lymphedema reframes the disease as more than a failure of lymphatic drainage—it may represent an epidermally sustained immunoinflammatory condition. This review examines the evolving role of KCs as immunomodulatory cells in both health and disease, with a particular focus on their emerging functions in the pathogenesis and progression of secondary lymphedema. By integrating insights from epidermal immunobiology, fibrosis research, and lymphatic biology, we aim to highlight how targeting KC-immune crosstalk could inform the development of new, disease-modifying therapies.

Keratinocytes in Skin Immunity

KCs, once viewed as passive, are now recognized as active regulators of skin immunity (Figure 1) (Boulanger and Lenormand, 2018; Ye and Lai, 2025). As frontline barrier cells, they sense injury or pathogens releasing damage-associated molecular patterns and pathogen-associated molecular patterns, which initiate inflammation and recruit immune cells (Bianchi, 2007; Nestle et al, 2009). Under IFN-γ stimulation, they upregulate major histocompatibility complex class II to present antigens to CD4+ T cells (Meister et al, 2015; Zima et al, 2024). They also generate numerous antimicrobial peptides, such as human β-defensins (Boulanger and Lenormand, 2018), cathelicidin (Supp et al, 2004), calcium-binding psoriasin, and RNase 7, that shape immune responses, including T helper type 2 (Th2) signaling (Clausen and Agner, 2016; Rademacher et al, 2019). Furthermore, KCs secrete immunomodulatory molecules, including eicosanoids (Rosenbach et al, 1990), ROS (Bickers and Athar, 2006), and complement-regulating proteins (Dovezenski et al, 1992; Modlin et al, 2012). KCs, together with mast cells, Langerhans cells, macrophages, and innate lymphoid cells, are key components of the skin’s innate immune system. They constitutively produce cytokines such as IL-1, IL-7, and TGF-β to regulate Langerhans cells and generate inactive precursors of proinflammatory cytokines like pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 (Morizane et al, 2023). After tissue damage, KCs detect danger signals through pattern-recognition receptors—including TLRs, NOD-like receptors, and C-type lectins—and produce IL-1, TNF-α, IL-6, CCL3, CCL20, CCL27, CXCL9, and CXCL10 (Coates et al, 2018; Lebre et al, 2007). They also secrete IL-15 and IL-18 to activate T cells, type I IFNs and IL-12 to promote Th1 responses, IL-23 for Th17 responses, and IL-10 for regulatory T cell (Treg) induction, underscoring their context-dependent immunoregulatory roles (Boulanger and Lenormand, 2018; Di Meglio et al, 2011).

KCs also support immunosuppression, which is essential for skin homeostasis. TLR2 activation by non-inflammatory commensals like Staphylococcus epidermidis induces human β-defensins and suppresses TLR3-driven responses, highlighting microbiome-KC crosstalk in local immunity (Lai et al, 2009). KC-intrinsic major histocompatibility complex-II further maintains homeostatic type 1 responses by limiting commensal-induced Th1 accumulation (Tamoutounour et al, 2019). KCs can also regulate Treg cells through TSLP signaling and dendritic cells (DCs) to sustain immune tolerance (Kashiwagi et al, 2017; Tanaka et al, 2023). Together, these interactions underscore the central role of KCs in balancing cutaneous immune activation and homeostasis.

Keratins (Krt) produced by KCs also play active roles in skin immunity and inflammation beyond their traditional structural functions (Wang et al, 2016). Basal KC stem cells primarily express Krt5, Krt14, and low levels of Krt15, while differentiating KCs switch to Krt1 and Krt10 (Byrne, 1997; Fuchs and Green, 1980; Woodcock-Mitchell et al, 1982). Several Krt directly modulate inflammatory responses: Krt6 impairs KC migration during wound healing by inhibiting Src kinase (Rotty and Coulombe, 2012); Krt16 drives epidermal inflammation in psoriasis and cancer (Lessard et al, 2013); and Krt17 supports KC survival by dampening TNF-α signaling and promoting protein kinase B/mTOR activation (Kim et al, 2006; Tong and Coulombe, 2006). In dermatitis models, Krt17 shifts Th2 toward Th1/Th17 inflammation and amplifies innate and adaptive immune activation, whereas its loss reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 (DePianto et al, 2010). Together, these findings establish Krt as key regulators of epidermal inflammation, immune responses, and tissue repair (Zhang et al, 2019).

Keratinocyte-Immune Interactions in Inflammatory Skin Disease

KC-immune interactions drive hallmark features of inflammatory skin disorders, including pruritus, hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and fibrosis (Lin et al, 2024; Niehues et al, 2022). Acting as both sensors and amplifiers of immune activity, KCs integrate signals from cytokines, microbial products, and environmental stressors to orchestrate local inflammation. The following three conditions exemplify the central immunologic functions of KCs, demonstrating how distinct disease triggers converge on shared epithelial signaling pathways. This understanding not only clarifies their contribution to cutaneous homeostasis but also suggests similar novel therapeutic targets across inflammatory and autoimmune skin diseases (Ham et al, 2022; Hasegawa et al, 2022; Simmons and Gallo, 2024).

In AD, KCs initiate Th2-skewed inflammation through the release of epithelial alarmins such as IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP (Figure 2a) (Chieosilapatham et al, 2021; Hasegawa et al, 2022; Rizzo et al, 2016). These cytokines activate DCs and innate lymphoid cells to promote IL-4 and IL-13 secretion, impair barrier lipid synthesis, and enhance itch through IL-31 receptor signaling (Bilsborough et al, 2006; Dillon et al, 2004; Nakashima et al, 2018). In psoriasis, KCs act as amplifiers of Th17-driven inflammation (Figure 2b) (Albanesi and Pastore, 2010; Albanesi et al, 2007). Nucleic acids released from damaged KCs form complexes with the antimicrobial peptide LL-37, activating DCs through TLR7 and TLR9 (Albanesi et al, 2009; Ganguly et al, 2009; Lai et al, 2009; Lande et al, 2007; Li et al, 2014; Zhang et al, 2016). The resulting IL-23/IL-17/IL-22 cytokine cascade perpetuates KC hyperproliferation and aberrant differentiation, driving acanthosis and the thickened epidermis characteristic of psoriatic plaques (Conrad and Gilliet, 2012; Kamata and Tada, 2023; Madonna et al, 2012; Palombo et al, 2016; Sano et al, 2005; Sestito et al, 2011; Tian and Lai, 2022; Witte et al, 2014).

In CLE, KCs are both targets and initiators of autoimmunity (Figure 2c). CLE affects most patients with systemic lupus erythematosus and often persists despite adequate control of the systemic disease, reflecting complex immunopathogenesis (Chasset et al, 2016). CLE features epidermal atrophy, KC apoptosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration (Nasir and Mak, 2025). UV radiation induces KC apoptosis and nucleic acid release, triggering type I IFN production and immune cell recruitment (Hile and Kahlenberg, 2021; Köck et al, 1990; Niebel et al, 2023; Sontheimer et al, 2017; Takashima and Bergstresser, 1996). Type I IFNs are central to CLE pathogenesis (Psarras et al, 2022; Sarkar et al, 2018; Stannard et al, 2017): persistently elevated IFN-κ expression primes surrounding cells toward inflammatory activation, sustaining lesion chronicity and tissue injury. The cumulative result is a self-perpetuating inflammatory loop wherein KCs perpetuate immune activation and barrier loss even after the initial insult resolves.

Together, these cutaneous inflammatory diseases illustrate a unifying paradigm in which KCs act as key immune sentinels—initiating, amplifying, and sustaining cutaneous inflammation. Rather than passive bystanders, they function as active regulators whose dysregulation underlies diverse inflammatory phenotypes of the skin.

The discoveries described above have firmly established KCs as central regulators of inflammatory signaling in classical dermatoses. However, recent studies suggest that their immunologic functions extend beyond these well-characterized contexts to include disorders not traditionally classified as primary skin diseases, such as secondary lymphedema, a chronic, progressive disorder resulting from lymphatic injury or obstruction. Although historically viewed through the lens of impaired fluid transport and lymphangiogenic failure, emerging evidence indicates that sustained epidermal-immune activation is a key driver of its inflammation and fibrosis. Morphologic and transcriptomic analyses of lymphedematous skin now reveal epidermal hyperplasia, abnormal keratin expression, and elevated cytokine signaling—features reminiscent of chronic inflammatory dermatoses such as AD and psoriasis. These parallels suggest that KCs, through the same cytokine and alarmin pathways operative in other cutaneous diseases, may actively perpetuate inflammation and tissue remodeling in lymphedema. This evolving concept reframes the condition as an immune-epithelial disorder, setting the stage for the discussion that follows.

Emerging Roles of Keratinocytes in Secondary Lymphedema

Lymphedema is a chronic, debilitating disease estimated to affect hundreds of millions of people globally and approximately one in every 1000 persons in the United States (Rockson and Rivera, 2008). It is characterized by impaired lymphatic drainage, leading to chronic swelling, inflammation, immune dysregulation, and tissue remodeling, including fibrosis. Based on its etiology, lymphedema can be classified as primary or secondary. Primary lymphedema is often hereditary, congenital, or genetic (Duhon et al, 2022; Warren et al, 2007). Secondary lymphedema, in contrast, is acquired and typically arises from damage to the lymphatic system caused by external factors such as surgery, radiation, infection, or trauma (Lee and Kim, 2024). Globally, the most common cause of secondary lymphedema is filariasis secondary to infection with Wuchereria bancrofti, affecting more than 90 million people (Warren et al, 2007). In the Western world, secondary lymphedema most commonly occurs after cancer-related treatment (Wu et al, 2025). Based on the meta-analysis of DiSipio et al in 2013, it was estimated that the incidence of unilateral lymphedema can be as high as one in every five female breast cancer survivors (DiSipio et al, 2013). Despite its high prevalence, available treatments for secondary lymphedema are primarily palliative (Cheville et al, 2025; Ciamarra et al, 2025; León-Díaz and Medina-Otero, 2025), highlighting the incomplete understanding of the cellular and molecular mechanisms driving disease progression.

Redefining lymphedema pathogenesis

Although traditionally attributed to dysregulated lymphangiogenesis—particularly impaired vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 3 signaling (Brown et al, 2022a; Kuonqui et al, 2023)—the pathogenesis of secondary lymphedema remains incompletely understood. Pro-regenerative strategies show promise: APOA1-binding protein enhances VEGF-C-driven lymphangiogenesis (Kim et al, 2025), and Apelin, combined with VEGF-C, will begin phase I testing after reversing fibrosis and dermal backflow in preclinical models (Creff et al, 2024). However, conflicting findings, including elevated VEGF-C in patients (Jensen et al, 2015) and worsened lymphedema in mice with VEGF-C overexpression (Gousopoulos et al, 2017), underscore the complexity of targeting lymphangiogenesis.

Growing evidence indicates that chronic inflammation—particularly Th2 activity—is a major driver of secondary lymphedema. Th2 cytokines exert strong anti-lymphangiogenic effects and blocking them enhances inflammatory lymphangiogenesis without affecting angiogenesis in a mouse cornea suture model (Savetsky et al, 2015). Another group also reported the negative effects of Th2 cells and their cytokines (IL-4 and IL-13) on lymphatic vessel formations in a mouse asthma model (Shin et al, 2015). In mouse tail models and human biopsies, lymphatic stasis promotes T-cell inflammation and Th2 differentiation; IL-4 or IL-13 blockade prevents disease progression, reduces fibrosis, and improves lymphatic function independent of lymphangiogenic factors (Avraham et al, 2013). Ly et al then utilized transgenic mice deficient in Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation to confirm that Th2 differentiation is the essential step for lymphedema development after injury (Ly et al, 2019). Tregs also contribute to the pathogenesis; in transgenic mice, their depletion worsens disease, while adoptive transfer of Tregs can reverse it (García Nores et al, 2018b; Gousopoulos et al, 2016), and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 therapy protected against lymphedema by expanding Tregs in humans and mice (Wolf et al, 2024). Although animal models incompletely reflect chronic human disease (Padera et al, 2016), human biopsies show increased CD4+ infiltration, strengthening the translatability of the in vivo results, although a larger clinical study is warranted for further validation (Wolf et al, 2022). These insights support emerging anti-inflammatory treatments, including topical tacrolimus (Gardenier et al, 2017), topical captopril (Brown et al, 2023), metformin (Rubin et al, 2025), doxycycline (Brown et al, 2022b), pirfenidone (Baik et al, 2022), and IL-4 inhibitors (Mehrara et al, 2021), with topical tacrolimus showing the strongest early clinical promise (Gulmark Hansen et al, 2023; Hansen et al, 2025).

Despite recent advances in understanding and treating secondary lymphedema, the upstream molecular and cellular triggers of chronic inflammation and fibrosis—particularly the factors driving CD4+ T-cell infiltration—remain poorly defined.

Keratinocyte activation under lymphatic stasis

Recent evidence suggests that epidermal and dermal inflammation contribute to the pathogenesis of lymphedema (Dayan et al, 2018). Breast cancer-related lymphedema patients often exhibit skin changes, swelling, and fibrosis (Campbell et al, 2025a) (Figure 3a), with loss of basal KC polarity and increased Krt14 expression (Figure 3b and c). Histologic findings—immune cell infiltration, hyperkeratosis, fibrosis, and adipose deposition in the epidermis and dermis—further support skin involvement and the effectiveness of topical therapies (Li et al, 2020). Hyperkeratosis may arise from cytokine-rich interstitial fluid rather than intrinsic KC defects; blocking IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, KGF, and their receptors reduces KC mitosis (Domaszewska-Szostek et al, 2016). Th2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-13, elevated in lymphedema skin, also alter differentiation: lymphedema arm fluid increases Krt14 in vitro, while IL-4/IL-13 blockade restores Krt10 and reduces Krt14 (Campbell et al, 2025a). Summary and Critical Appraisal of Original Studies Investigating Keratinocyte Involvement in Secondary Lymphedema

From 2016 to 2025, research has progressively defined the immunologic and fibrotic roles of keratinocytes in lymphedema—from early cytokine-based observations to advanced transcriptomic, in vivo, and interventional studies. Despite compelling evidence implicating PAR2, STAT3, and TGF-β pathways, limitations such as reliance on murine or in vitro models and incomplete lineage tracing underscore the need for longitudinal human mechanistic investigations.

Abbreviations: BCRL, breast cancer–related lymphedema; EMT, epithelial-mesenchymal transition; KGF, keratinocyte growth factor; LC; Krt, keratins; PAR2, protease-activated receptor 2; STAT3, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3; Th, t helper; TSLP, thymic stromal lymphopoietin.

KCs may contribute to Th2 differentiation in lymphedema through early protease-activated receptor 2 upregulation after lymphatic injury in the mouse tail model (Park et al, 20231). Protease-activated receptor 2 is a known regulator of Th2-inducing cytokines in AD, and its blockade improves disease in mice (Jeon et al, 2025). Consistent with this framework, protease-activated receptor 2-deficient mice exhibit reduced Th2 inflammation and attenuated lymphedema. Additionally, inhibition of KC proliferation with topical teriflunomide is associated with decreased epidermal alterations and reduced disease severity (Park et al, 20231), suggesting a possible contribution of KCs to the inflammatory environment. However, it is important to note that these findings derive largely from preclinical models, and the data from Park et al (2023) remain preliminary at the time of this writing. Therefore, while these observations support a potential role for KCs in modulating early inflammatory responses, they do not yet establish KCs as definitive upstream drivers of lymphedema pathogenesis. Further peer-reviewed and mechanistic studies are required to clarify causality and temporal relationships in human disease.

Taken together, these observations suggest a possible role for KCs in shaping the early inflammatory environment of lymphedema; however, their precise position within the pathogenic hierarchy remains to be fully defined. In this context, KCs have emerged as potential contributors to the inflammatory milieu in lymphedema, representing an important conceptual advance in understanding disease progression. Nevertheless, the molecular and cellular mechanisms that trigger their activation—and the extent to which they sustain chronic inflammation and fibrosis—remain poorly defined. Although KCs respond to lymphatic fluid in vitro (Park et al, 20231), in vivo studies are needed to establish temporality and identify initiating signals. Equally lacking are studies clarifying how KC-derived mediators link early inflammation to the later Th2-driven response. Th2-polarizing cytokines released by KCs, including IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP, represent key candidates for future investigation (Figure 3d).

In parallel with their inflammatory functions, KCs may also contribute directly to the fibrotic remodeling observed in chronic lymphedema. KCs have been shown to contribute to other fibrotic conditions, such as hypertrophic scars, keloids, and systemic sclerosis (Dufour et al, 2020; McCoy et al, 2017; Russo et al, 2022, 2020; Zhong et al, 2016). Recent studies have shown that sustained exposure to TGF-β, IL-6, and mechanical stress can induce EMT in KCs, characterized by the loss of E-cadherin, upregulation of vimentin and α-SMA, and acquisition of migratory and collagen-producing properties (Baik et al, 2022; Park et al, 2025). This phenotypic shift suggests that epidermal cells may not only signal to fibroblasts but also actively participate in extracellular matrix deposition and dermal fibrosis. However, these findings largely derive from in vitro and small-scale murine studies, which may overrepresent the extent or permanence of EMT in human disease. Moreover, most investigations rely on gene or protein expression markers without direct lineage-tracing confirmation, leaving uncertainty as to whether KCs undergo full transdifferentiation or adopt a transient, fibrogenic phenotype. Future in vivo studies incorporating spatial transcriptomics and fate mapping will be essential to clarify the true contribution of KC-derived EMT to fibrotic remodeling in secondary lymphedema. Lastly, the precise role of KCs in driving versus sustaining fibrosis in secondary lymphedema remains incompletely defined. Clarifying whether these cells act as initiators of matrix remodeling or amplifiers of ongoing inflammation will be essential for understanding disease progression and identifying new therapeutic targets. At present, available evidence more strongly supports the role of KCs as amplifiers and sustainers of established inflammation rather than definitive upstream initiators of disease.

Keratinocyte-immune-lymphatic crosstalk

The interplay among KCs, immune cells, and lymphatic endothelium forms a complex signaling network that drives disease persistence. KC-derived alarmins such as IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP amplify Th2 polarization by activating DCs and innate lymphoid cells, which secrete IL-4 and IL-13. These Th2 cytokines, in turn, suppress lymphangiogenesis and exacerbate fibrosis, closing a pathogenic feedback loop (Avraham et al, 2013; Hasegawa et al, 2022).

Concomitantly, the loss of barrier integrity facilitates antigen penetration and microbial dysbiosis, perpetuating inflammation similar to the “outside-in” hypothesis in AD (Chieosilapatham et al, 2021). Crosstalk between KCs and fibroblasts further enhances fibrosis through reciprocal paracrine signaling: TGF-β and KGF from fibroblasts promote KC proliferation, whereas KC-derived oncostatin M and IL-6 activate fibroblast signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 pathways to sustain extracellular matrix deposition (Canady et al, 2013; Peng et al, 2019).

Building upon this framework of barrier disruption and epithelial-stromal signaling, emerging evidence also implicates microbiome dysregulation in the pathogenesis of secondary lymphedema. Recent studies have reported altered microbial diversity and compositional shifts in lymphedematous skin, suggesting that impaired barrier integrity may facilitate microbial imbalance. In patients with filarial lymphedema, Manavalan et al identified notable differences in bacterial flora compared with healthy controls, with Staphylococcus dominating both groups but rare genera such as Eremococcus and Facklamia detected selectively in affected individuals (Dharmalingam et al, 2025; Manavalan et al, 2025). However, the pathogenic significance of these compositional changes and their direct contribution to disease progression remain unclear.

Experimental data further suggest that lymphatic dysfunction may alter host responses to otherwise commensal bacteria. In a rat model, interruption of lymph flow via surgical ligation of lymphatic channels alone did not markedly increase cutaneous immune infiltrates but reduced CD4+ T cells and monocytes in draining lymph nodes. However, when edema was combined with local saprophytic Staphylococcus epidermidis exposure, a pronounced inflammatory response developed in the skin, characterized by dense CD68+ macrophage and antigen-presenting cell infiltrates, alongside impaired B-cell trafficking to regional lymph nodes. These findings indicate that lymphatic stasis may exacerbate inflammatory responses to commensal microbes while simultaneously disrupting coordinated immune surveillance (Cąkała-Jakimowicz et al, 2023).

In contrast, analysis of the skin microbiome in patients with unilateral upper-extremity secondary lymphedema revealed no significant differences in overall microbial diversity between affected and unaffected limbs (Campbell et al, 2023). Nevertheless, increased variability in specific genera, including Propionibacterium and Streptococcus, was observed between paired samples. Collectively, these findings underscore the heterogeneity of microbiome alterations across lymphedema subtypes and highlight the need for further investigation into host-microbial interactions in disease pathogenesis and its complications. Given the established role of KCs in regulating commensal interactions through TLR signaling and antimicrobial peptide production, disrupted KC-microbiome crosstalk may contribute to persistent inflammation. Whether microbial dysbiosis is a primary driver or a secondary consequence of lymphatic stasis remains to be determined; however, it represents an additional layer of immune-epidermal interaction relevant to disease progression.

Finally, lymphatic endothelial dysfunction secondary to lymphatic injury alters interstitial pressure and extracellular matrix stiffness, which may trigger mechanosensitive KC pathways such as yes-associated protein signaling (Laly et al, 2021; Uttagomol et al, 2019), and the opening of the Piezo family channels (Piezo1 and Piezo2), which have been associated with inflammatory responses (Atcha et al, 2021; Aykut et al, 2020; Geng et al, 2021; Solis et al, 2019). Although these mechano-transduction mechanisms remain to be fully defined in the context of secondary lymphedema, they provide a plausible link between lymphatic failure and epidermal activation. Together, these interactions create a feed-forward inflammatory circuit in which lymphatic injury induces KC activation, Th2 cytokine release, and progressive fibrosis, as illustrated in Figure 3d.

Keratinocyte-independent pathways in Th2 skewing and fibrosis

Although emerging evidence suggests that KCs play important inflammatory roles in the pathogenesis of secondary lymphedema, it is equally important to consider alternative and potentially parallel mechanisms. Beyond KC-mediated amplification, substantial evidence supports KC-independent pathways in the initiation of Th2-driven lymphedema pathology. Mechanistic studies demonstrate that in murine models, CD4+ T cells can be primed within skin-draining lymph nodes following antigen presentation and subsequently migrate to the skin to mediate disease progression (García Nores et al, 2018a). Notably, these studies did not implicate KCs directly in the activation of antigen-presenting cells; rather, lymphatic injury was shown to promote the migration of immature DCs to the site of injury, where they undergo activation.

More recently, using a murine model complemented by in vitro analyses of human lymphedematous skin, Campbell et al (2025b) reported antigen-driven oligoclonal expansion of CD4+ T cells—potentially linked to insulin-related antigens—in affected tissue, supporting a model in which adaptive immune activation may precede or occur independently of KC signaling. These findings further support a model in which adaptive immune activation may precede, or occur independently of, KC signaling. However, this study was limited by the absence of healthy control cohorts and by a lack of stratification for pre-existing metabolic disease before the development of secondary lymphedema in breast cancer patients.

In addition to immune and epidermal contributions, stromal compartments may also participate in early Th2 polarization during secondary lymphedema. IL-33 induction within dermal fibroblasts and stromal cells has been reported in post-surgical mouse tail lymphedema models, accompanied by upregulation of its receptor ST2/IL1RL1 (Uemura et al, 2025). These findings raise the possibility that stromal-derived alarmins in the context of lymphatic injury could contribute to local immune activation independently of direct epidermal signaling. However, the attribution of IL-33 primarily to fibroblasts in this study was based largely on co-localization with fibroblast markers, and definitive lineage-tracing evidence was not provided. Moreover, KCs and other resident cell types are also well-recognized sources of IL-33 in response to tissue injury (Qiu et al, 2022; Schmitz et al, 2005). Therefore, the relative cellular source and functional contribution of IL-33 during the early stages of secondary lymphedema remain incompletely defined and warrant further investigation.

Collectively, these data support a multifactorial model of secondary lymphedema in which KCs likely function within a broader inflammatory network as recruiters and/or amplifiers of immune responses rather than as exclusive upstream initiators. While emerging evidence highlights their potential importance in shaping the inflammatory milieu, it remains essential to consider alternative and KC-independent pathways—including lymph node-mediated adaptive priming, stromal alarmin signaling, and immune cell-intrinsic mechanisms—that may initiate or propagate disease. Maintaining this broader perspective will be critical for accurately defining pathogenic hierarchies and for guiding the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Translational and therapeutic implications

Recognition of KCs as an inflammatory driver in lymphedema introduces the avenue for new therapeutic approaches. Topical tacrolimus, which inhibits KC cytokine production, has demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models and early clinical trials for secondary lymphedema (Gardenier et al, 2017; Gulmark Hansen et al, 2023). Additional anti-inflammatory strategies—such as topical captopril, metformin, doxycycline, and IL-4 inhibitors—also show promise in mitigating fibrosis and edema (Brown et al, 2023; Mehrara et al, 2021; Rubin et al, 2025). Emerging therapies may focus directly on KC signaling. Protease-activated receptor 2 inhibitors like punicalagin analogs could suppress Th2 cytokine induction (Jeon et al, 2025), while modulation of the TGF-β pathway could prevent KC EMT and downstream fibrosis (Baik et al, 2022). Novel regenerative approaches, such as topical or gene-based delivery of VEGF-C/Apelin mRNA, may combine lymphangiogenic repair with KC immunomodulation (Creff et al, 2024).

An equally transformative prospect is the use of KC-derived biomarkers for early diagnosis and monitoring. Noninvasive skin tape-stripping has already identified epidermal cytokine signatures in AD (Lyubchenko et al, 2021) and could similarly detect early KC activation in post-surgical patients at risk for lymphedema. This approach may facilitate preemptive interventions before irreversible fibrosis occurs.

Collectively, these advances mark a conceptual shift from mechanical fluid management toward molecularly targeted therapy. Although mounting experimental evidence suggests that KCs influence the inflammatory and fibrotic milieu of secondary lymphedema, their causal role in human disease remains to be definitively established. Current findings are largely derived from preclinical or correlative studies, and clinical validation is still in its infancy. Consequently, while KC-directed interventions hold theoretical promise for restoring epidermal-immune-lymphatic balance, such strategies should presently be viewed as emerging research avenues rather than established therapeutic targets. Rigorous mechanistic and translational studies in human cohorts will be essential to determine whether modulating KC function can meaningfully alter the course of secondary lymphedema.

Summary

The recognition of KCs as active contributors to the pathogenesis of secondary lymphedema represents a significant conceptual advance. Once considered downstream responders to inflammation, KCs are increasingly recognized as active participants within the inflammatory cascade. Despite this progress, the precise molecular and cellular mechanisms by which these cells are activated under lymphatic stasis—and how they perpetuate the chronic inflammatory and fibrotic response—remain incompletely understood. Although in vitro studies have demonstrated KC activation following exposure to lymphatic fluid (Park et al, 20231), establishing temporality, causality, and the nature of the inciting stimuli in physiologically relevant in vivo models remains a critical next step. Moreover, the downstream pathways linking early KC activation to the adaptive Th2-dominated immune response have yet to be fully defined. Future investigations should delineate how Th2-differentiating cytokines released by KCs, including IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP, integrate into the broader immune network that sustains inflammation and fibrosis in lymphedema.

Collectively, the studies summarized above have reshaped our understanding of lymphedema as a multifaceted immune-inflammatory disease rather than a purely mechanical disorder of lymphatic flow. This emerging paradigm highlights a complex interplay between lymphatic dysfunction, immune activation, and epidermal remodeling. Nonetheless, much of the current evidence derives from small-scale murine models, necessitating further validation in larger animal models and human cohorts to establish clinical relevance and translatability. Building upon the established roles of KCs in other inflammatory skin disorders, future research is poised to uncover deeper mechanistic insight into their contribution to lymphedema. Such efforts are likely to inform the development of novel, targeted, and patient-centered therapeutic strategies that address not only lymphatic insufficiency but also the inflammatory and fibrotic processes sustained by KC-immune crosstalk.

Conclusions and Future Perspectives

KCs are integral not only to epidermal barrier integrity but also to immune and lymphatic regulation. Their emerging role in secondary lymphedema reframes the disease as a chronic inflammatory condition rooted in epidermal-immune crosstalk rather than purely lymphatic failure. Future research should define the initiating stimuli that activate KCs under lymphatic stasis, delineate temporal links between epidermal inflammation and fibrotic remodeling, and evaluate targeted therapies that disrupt KC cytokine signaling. By integrating KC biology into the broader framework of lymphatic disease, we may advance from symptomatic management toward true immunomodulatory and regenerative interventions for lymphedema.
