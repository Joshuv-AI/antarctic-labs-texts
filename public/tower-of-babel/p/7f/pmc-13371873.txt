Post-vaccination HPV prevalence, genotype distribution, and associated factors among vaccinated women in China: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Yuwei Shao, Chonggao Hu, Yulong Liang, Yue Yang, Shangyan Han, Huakun Lv, Yu Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to evaluate human papillomavirus (HPV) prevalence and genotype distribution among vaccinated women and to identify the factors associated with post-vaccination HPV infection. Women who received HPV vaccine and had the thin-layer liquid-based cytology test (TCT) and HPV genotype testing before HPV vaccination were recruited. Each participant was required to complete an online questionnaire, and cervical specimen was collected for TCT and HPV genotype testing. The epidemiology of HPV infection and genotype was described. The factors associated with HPV infection post-vaccination were identified through multivariate analysis, presenting the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with its 95% confidence interval (95%CI). A total of 893 eligible women participated. The prevalence of HPV post-vaccination was 8.51%. The common high-risk HPV (hr-HPV) were HPV52, HPV59, HPV58 and the common low-risk HPV (lr-HPV) was HPV53. Factors associated with HPV infection post-vaccination included aged ≥40y (AOR = 2.92), married status (AOR = 1.56), receiving the first dose of HPV vaccine after sexual debut (OR = 2.57), lr-HPV positive pre-vaccination (AOR = 2.35) and hr-HPV positive pre-vaccination (AOR = 5.72). The prevalence of HPV post-vaccination was lower than that of pre-vaccination and the common genotypes changed to those not covered by HPV vaccine administrated. Age, marital status, timing of HPV vaccination and prior HPV infection before HPV vaccination were identified as the factors associated with HPV infection post-vaccination. Receiving HPV vaccination before sexual debut and ongoing HPV screening after vaccination were recommended.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths among women, posing a significant health threat worldwide. In 2022, the global incidence of cervical cancer was approximately 660,000 cases (13.83 per 100000 women), and the number of deaths was close to 350,000 (5.13 per 100000 women), ranking 8th and 9th in terms of mobility and mortality of all types of cancer.1,2 The main etiological factor for cervical cancer is the persistent infection with human papillomavirus (HPV), especially for the high-risk HPV (hr-HPV). Approximately 200 genotypes of HPV have been identified, of which 13 genotypes are recognized as hr-HPV (HPV16, HPV18, HPV31, HPV33, HPV35, HPV39, HPV45, HPV51, HPV52, HPV56, HPV58, HPV59, HPV68). The most common low-risk HPV (lr-HPV) genotypes include HPV6 and HPV11.3 Previous studies have elucidated that hr-HPV infection is associated with premalignant and malignant of penile, vaginal, anal, vulvar, oropharyngeal and lr-HPV is implicated in anal genital warts and recurrent respiratory papillomatosis.4,5

Cervical cancer is currently considered as the sole tumor with a well-defined etiology that can be prevented through vaccination and screening.6 In 2023, China issued the Action Plan for Accelerated Elimination of Cervical Cancer (2023–2030), which proposes an expected HPV vaccination coverage of 90% by age 15 among girls and the screening coverage of 70% of women aged 35–65 y.7 HPV vaccines have been introduced to prevent HPV infection and HPV-related diseases in China. Currently, available HPV vaccines include the 2-valent HPV vaccine (2vHPV) targeting HPV16 and HPV18, 4-valent HPV vaccine (4vHPV) targeting HPV16, HPV18, HPV6, and HPV11, and 9-valent HPV vaccine (9vHPV) targeting HPV16, HPV18, HPV6, HPV11, HPV31, HPV33, HPV45, HPV52, and HPV58. HPV DNA test and cervical cytology tests such as thin-layer liquid-based cytology test (TCT) had been proven as an effective screening method in reducing the incidence of cervical cancer.8

Clinical trials had indicated the vaccine efficacy of HPV vaccine, with a cumulative vaccine efficacy of 97.4% against HPV 16/18-related cervical cancer.9 Furthermore, based on the international experience, national HPV vaccination programs had effectively reduced the prevalence of HPV infections and subsequently reduced the incidence of cervical cancer.10,11 However, previous reports had demonstrated that hr-HPV infection and cervical cancer would still occur after HPV vaccination,6 emphasizing the importance of exploring the epidemiological characteristics of HPV infection post-vaccination and the relevant factors. Although World Health Organization (WHO) does not recommend screening of HPV infection prior to HPV vaccination,5 the protection of HPV vaccine among women with preexisting HPV infection remains controversial worldwide.12 Furthermore, pre-vaccination HPV infection status, vaccination timing in relation to sexual debut, and the time interval following HPV vaccination had been identified as the factors influencing vaccine protective effect in the previous reports.13,14 Given the relatively short time period for all HPV vaccines licensed in China, further study on HPV infection post-vaccination in a real-world among Chinese women is warranted. Previous studies indicated the alteration in the distribution of HPV genotypes induced by massive HPV vaccination.15,16 The prevalence of HPV genotypes covered by the vaccine reduced, while the infection rates of non-vaccine‑covered genotypes increased. Nevertheless, the prevalence of HPV genotypes not covered by the HPV vaccine were rarely studied in China. Although many studies had already described HPV prevalence and the change of genotype distribution among vaccinated women, similar data was never reported in Zhejiang province. It is necessary to evaluate HPV prevalence among vaccinated women and to identify the factors associated with post-vaccination HPV infection in Zhejiang province, as well as to pay close attention to the trends in HPV genotype alteration in the post-vaccine era. These local findings would help to improve the HPV vaccination and the cervical cancer screening strategies, and would also benefit the development of new HPV vaccine.

This study aimed to evaluate HPV prevalence and genotype distribution among vaccinated women and to identify the factors associated with post-vaccination HPV infection in Zhejiang province, China, thereby furnishing a scientific foundation for optimizing vaccination strategies.

Methods

Study design and subjects

This study was a cross-sectional design to examine the HPV infectious status among women who had received the full-series of HPV vaccine (three-dose schedule). Eligible women from gynecological clinic or health examination center were recruited from June 25, 2024 to December 31, 2025 at Changxing maternity & child health care hospital, Zhejiang province, China. Each participant was required to finish a questionnaire and complete the cervical specimen collection. The inclusion criteria for recruitment were: (1) female aged ≥18 y; (2) received the full-series of HPV vaccine but not limited to vaccine types; and (3) received TCT, HPV DNA test and typing before the first dose of HPV vaccine for any purpose, such as health examination or gynecological diseases diagnosis. Exclusion criteria were: (1) any history of diseases such as cervical cancer, total hysterectomy, cervical resection, autoimmune disease and other cancers; (2) incompetent to complete the investigation; and (3) drop-out of HPV vaccination.

Sample size

The sampling size was estimated on the probability of hr-HPV infection rate among women with full-series of HPV vaccination and the formula for the calculation was as follow: Nmin=deff×z1−α/22×p×1−pd2. Based on the surveillance report from China and the vaccine effectiveness of HPV vaccine, we assumed a conservative estimate of 7% for HPV infection rate among women with full-series of HPV vaccination.17 Other parameters were set as follow: a two-tailed α error of 5%, a permissible error (d) of 0.02, a design effect (deff) of 2 and the inflating ratio of 10%. Finally, we aimed for at least 612 participants for this study.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire for investigation among participants was developed by experts from Zhejiang Provincial Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The content included the demographic characteristics of the surveyed women, living habits, knowledge on HPV infection and prevention, timing of HPV vaccination, pregnancy after HPV vaccination, HPV infection and TCT status prior to HPV vaccination, type of HPV vaccine received. We listed seven items on the aspect of knowledge on HPV infection and prevention (Items No. 14–20 in Table S1) in the questionnaire and assigned 1 point for the correct/complete response for each item while assigned 0 for wrong/incomplete response, with a maximum of 7 points for each participant. Then, we defined a variable of “knowledge scores on HPV” based on the total score of these seven items (0–7 points) and categorized it into high (≥the mean of knowledge score) or low (<the mean of knowledge score) level. All items in the questionnaire were designated as mandatory fields to ensure the data quality. The identification number of the participant served as the unique identification code and questionnaires with duplicated identification number were excluded. The complete questionnaire was presented in the supplementary materials (Table S1).

Cervical specimen collection

Each participant was required to collect the cervical specimen. A specialized cervical exfoliative cell collector was applied in this study. We used a cytobrush to collect the cervical exfoliated cells, which was placed in a small bottle containing preserving fluid after the specimen collection. Then, the specimen was promptly stored at 4°C and subsequently sent to the clinical laboratory at Changxing Maternity & Child Health Care Hospital within 24 hr for HPV analysis. The time interval between the last dose of HPV vaccine and specimen collection was calculated, which was defined as the variable of “time interval between the last dose of HPV vaccine and specimen collection.”

Laboratory testing

Each cervical specimen collected in this study underwent TCT and HPV DNA detection and typing. TCT was performed using a HQLCT-48 processor (Cytyc Corporation, HQBIOTEC). Following the Bethesda system,18 specimens were categorized as negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM), atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASCUS), atypical glandular cells (AGC), low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL), and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL).

Multiplex fluorescent PCR kits, including extraction and amplification (Bioperfectus Ltd, Jiangsu, P.R. China), were applied following the manufacturer’s protocol. Genotyping tests could identify 13 hr-HPV types (HPV16, HPV18, HPV31, HPV33, HPV35, HPV39, HPV45, HPV51, HPV52, HPV56, HPV58, HPV59, HPV68) and 8 lr-HPV types (HPV6, HPV11, HPV26, HPV53, HPV66, HPV73, HPV81, HPV82).

Study procedures

First, the electronic medical records and health examination record of Changxing Maternity & Child Health Care Hospital were reviewed to identify the women who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Second, based on the convenience sampling rules, trained researchers invited the selected eligible women to participate in this study through phone calls. Meanwhile, the research purpose, questionnaire content, and process of specimen collection were informed. Third, woman who was interested in participating in this study, would be scheduled a time for the questionnaire survey and cervical specimen collection. Fourth, woman who voluntarily participated was required to completed an online questionnaire by scanning a QR code, after she signed the electronic informed consent. For the items of HPV infection and TCT status prior to HPV vaccination, type of HPV vaccine received in the questionnaire, the previous test repots and vaccination records of each participant were provided for reference. Fifth, a woman who finished the online questionnaire was invited to gynecological examination room for the cervical specimen collection on the same day. If the participant was currently in menstrual period, an appointment would be made.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (Chicago, USA) statistical software, and the significance level was set at two-tailed of .05. Descriptive analysis was conducted. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. The HPV genotypes covered by the vaccine or not were categorized on the types of HPV vaccine administrated. It meant that the same HPV genotype of infection would be classified into different categories according to the type of HPV vaccine that woman received. TCT status, HPV DNA test and typing before/after HPV vaccination were described.

The main outcomes included the post-vaccination infection of HPV and hr-HPV. To identify the factors of influencing the main outcomes, we conducted univariate analysis between main outcomes and demographic characteristics, HPV vaccine types, HPV infection and TCT result before HPV vaccination, time interval between the last dose of HPV vaccine and specimen collection, etc. Factors showing statistical significance (p < .1) in the univariate analysis (Chi-square test) were subsequently included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, taking the post-vaccination infection of HPV infection and hr-HPV as the dependent variable, respectively. Odds ratio (OR) with its 95% confidence interval (95%CI) was calculated for each factor.

Discussion

This study was the first population-based research in Changxing county to assess the prevalence of HPV infection in women shortly after HPV vaccination. In our study, the infection rates of HPV and hr-HPV were both lower than those previously reported in a summative analysis from the 2127 medical institutions in China (17.70% for overall HPV and 13.12% for hr-HPV)19 and other cross-sectional reports prior or subsequent to mass HPV vaccination in China.20–24 These differences would be attributed to the disparities in the study population, demographic and socioeconomic background, as well as women‘s health awareness of the study areas. For example, the prevalence of HPV and abnormality of TCT pre-vaccination were also low. As such, continuous monitoring of HPV and good accessibility of screening should be implemented in a long-term for the cervical cancer prevention and treatment.

The distribution of genotypes of HPV varied across different regions. The common hr-HPV genotypes identified in this study were HPV52, HPV59 and HPV58, while the common lr-HPV genotypes was HPV53, indicating the highest prevalence in Changxing County. Additionally, the genotypes identified in the majority of the HPV‑infected women were not covered by the vaccines administrated. However, the dominant HPV genotypes were different in other countries. The dominant genotype was HPV16 worldwide, accounting for almost 60% of the highest risk for high-grade cervical lesion and invasive cancer.25,26 Additionally, HPV18 was the second common hr-HPV globally from a meta-analysis among the cytologically normal women in east and southeast Asia.5 Our results contrasted with these previous reports, suggesting that HPV52, HPV58 might surpass HPV16, HPV18 as the dominant genotypes. It was similar to the recent results from China,19,24 which found the top prevalent genotypes were HPV52, HPV58 for hr-HPV and HPV53 for lr-HPV. Notably, the probability of cervical cancer solely attributed to HPV52 or HPV58 infection was low as existing reports revealing the rarity of these two genotypes in cervical cancer in China.27 We assumed that the prevalence of HPV16 and HPV18 experienced a decline while other hr-HPV genotypes not covered by HPV vaccine might emerge as the dominant genotypes, since HPV16 was the most commonly detected genotype present with a prevalence of 4.4% in 2014,28 before HPV vaccine licensed in Zhejiang Province. These observations would be attributed to the widespread HPV vaccination since 2017. Our findings indicated that prevalence of HPV would be mitigated through the massive usage of HPV vaccine. Moreover, these observations would guide the development of vaccine with more genotypes included into HPV vaccine.

Additionally, the cross-protection of HPV vaccine might not be significant in this study, which was consistent with previous clinical trials and surveillance studies, showing a genotype-restricted characteristics.29,30 For instance, the cross-protection was only observed for HPV31 and HPV33 among women vaccinated with 2vHPV, and no trend for protection against HPV45, HPV52 or HPV58.30 However, several previous surveillance reports indicated the cross-protection for HPV31, HPV33, HPV45 among girls aged 17–19 y.30,31 Since there may be still controversy regarding the cross-protection of HPV vaccine currently, it is necessary to address this issue in the future study with the rigorous design.

Among 36 women with hr-HPV positive both pre- and post-vaccination, only five women’s infection genotypes were covered by the HPV vaccine administrated. This finding aligned with the previous reports, which also demonstrated the effectiveness on preventing recurrence in women administrated with 2vHPV and 4vHPV.32–34 For example, a study from Korea observed the recurrence incidence was 2.5% in the vaccination group, while it was 7.2% in the non-vaccination group.34 The possible mechanism was the antibody induced by HPV vaccine could prevent the HPV from entering the basal layer cell and avoid the infection of the regenerating tissues. However, the study applied a cross-sectional design, which could not measure a rigorous estimate on the effectiveness on preventing HPV recurrence.

In this study, several associated factors were identified for HPV or hr-HPV infection post-vaccination. First, older age was indicated as an independent factor associated with both overall HPV and hr-HPV infection. Previous reports had indicated that age would significantly influence the incidence of cervical cancer and HPV infection, presenting a “bimodal” distribution of age-specific HPV prevalence in China.35–37 Younger women are more likely to be infected with HPV as the sexual activity is relatively frequent, but most of them can clear HPV through their stronger immune system, while only a few become persistent infection.38 It would be an explanation for the lower risk of infection among younger women in our study. On the other hand, the incidence of HPV infection in older women decreases as the frequency of sexual behavior declines. However, the risk of persistent infection of HPV would increase since the function of immune system has gradually weakened and the ability to clear HPV is not sufficient. Second, the unmarried women showed a higher incidence of HPV or hr-HPV infection than the married, which was consistent with a meta-analysis from China.39 Marital status can indirectly reflect the sexual behaviors, such as the number of sexual partners and unstable marital relationships may lead to new sexual partners. Besides, the number of sexual partners through lifetime was significantly related to the risk of HPV infection.40 Third, our results revealed that HPV vaccination before sexual debut would have a lower risk for HPV infection post-vaccination, which was consistent with two previous reports from Japan and Colombia.14,15 It was assumed that the protection of HPV vaccination would be influenced by the time of HPV vaccination. Specifically, the vaccine effectiveness was 91.5% in women vaccinated before sexual debut, while it was 36.2% in women vaccinated after sexual debut.15 This finding suggested that HPV vaccination should be initiated before 14 y of age and also support the recommendations of HPV vaccination mainly target girls aged 13 y in the Chinese National Immunization Program since 2025. At last, having a history of hr-HPV infection pre-vaccination was identified as the associated factor of both HPV and hr-HPV infection post-vaccination, which was in line with previous reports from Ethiopia and India.41,42 For women with a prior history of HPV infection, the probability of HPV infection post-vaccination would be higher than those without such a history. The inference might be attributed to the suppression of immune function following the prior HPV infection as well as the risky sexual behavior associated with the occurrence of the prior HPV infection. Notably, the immune suppression can weaken the epithelial tissue, leading the virus easier to enter through the mucosal barrier and speed up the development of the high-grade cervical lesion.43 Furthermore, the variable of interval between the last dose of HPV vaccine and HPV detection was not associated with HPV infection post-vaccination. We assumed that the potential influence might not be detected due to the relatively short follow-up period in this study.

This study provided the valuable insights into the current prevalence of HPV among women who received the HPV vaccination, and the association between HPV infection post-vaccination and the impact factors on the demographic and gynecological health. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, since the participants were recruited from one medical institution in a county using convenience sampling, the selection bias could not be avoided and the findings would not be applicable to the general female population. Second, the items on HPV knowledge were not validated or psychometrically evaluated, which might affect the stability and validation of the results. Third, several variables on sexual behavior that could affect HPV infection were not included in the questionnaire, such as the number of sexual partners, frequency of sexual activity, and consistency of condom use, which could influence the integrity of our findings. Last, only women who had both pre-vaccination HPV testing and completed HPV vaccination were recruited in this study, and the selection bias could not be avoided. It would underestimate the HPV prevalence since the participants had better health awareness and behaviors.

Conclusions

In summary, our study found the prevalence of HPV post-vaccination was lower than that for pre-vaccination. The common HPV genotypes included HPV52, HPV59, HPV58, HPV53 after HPV vaccination. Factors associated with HPV infection post-vaccination included age, marital status, timing of HPV vaccination and prior HPV infection before HPV vaccination. This study offered valuable insights into the improvement of HPV vaccination strategy and emphasized the importance of HPV vaccination for women with high risk. Receiving HPV vaccination before sexual debut and ongoing HPV screening after vaccination were recommended.
