Constructing biomimetic microenvironments for liver regeneration
Authors: Yawen Zhu, Wanqi Yang, Zhongxia Wang, Dayu Chen, Jinglin Wang, Haozhen Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver regeneration is a sophisticated biological process influenced by a complex microenvironment that becomes profoundly altered in various pathological conditions. Current therapeutic approaches, including liver transplantation and pharmacological interventions, face significant limitations such as donor shortages, high costs, immune rejection, and insufficient functional recovery. Thus, alternative and innovative strategies are urgently needed. Biomimetic microenvironments constructed through tissue engineering have emerged as promising platforms, capable of recapitulating the liver’s natural architecture and supporting hepatic cell functions. This review outlines key pathological features and the biological basis underlying liver regeneration, highlighting cellular plasticity, inflammation, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and immune interactions. It further discusses advanced biomimetic strategies, including 3D cell cultures, decellularized ECM hydrogels, bioprinting technologies, and dynamic culture systems like hollow fiber, fluidized-bed, and microcarrier bioreactors. These innovations facilitate accurate modeling of hepatic functions, maintain cellular differentiation, and enhance regeneration. Despite significant advancements, challenges remain in optimizing microenvironmental fidelity, ensuring clinical scalability, and translating laboratory breakthroughs into effective therapies. Keywords: Liver regeneration, Biomimetic microenvironment, Tissue engineering, Biomaterials, Extracellular matrix, Liver diseases

Introduction

The liver is one of the most essential organs in the human body, responsible for a wide range of critical physiological functions, such as metabolism, detoxification, protein synthesis, and the storage of glycogen and cholesterol [1–5]. Its unique anatomical and physiological structures enable the liver to efficiently perform these complex tasks [6–9]. However, the liver’s ability to repair and regenerate itself is severely limited, particularly when faced with chronic liver diseases, cirrhosis, or acute liver failure (ALF) [10–14]. As a result, restoring liver function becomes a significant challenge in treating these conditions [15]. While liver transplantation and pharmacological treatments are the primary therapeutic options, liver transplantation faces several obstacles, including a shortage of organ donors, immune rejection, and the high costs associated with the procedure [16–19]. On the other hand, drug treatments may alleviate symptoms and slow disease progression but cannot fundamentally restore liver function [20–23]. Consequently, there is a pressing need for alternative and innovative therapeutic strategies that can address the underlying causes of liver disease [24–26]. In this context, the construction of biomimetic microenvironments has emerged as a promising solution, offering the potential to recreate the liver’s natural structure and functions to support liver cell growth, proliferation, and functional maintenance. This approach holds great promise for liver function restoration and could provide a new avenue for more effective liver disease treatments.

The rapid advancement of tissue engineering technologies has further fueled the potential of biomimetic microenvironments as a viable solution for liver disease therapy [27–29]. By combining biological scaffolds, liver cells, and bioactive factors, tissue engineering can recreate the complex physical, chemical, and biological characteristics of the liver [30–33]. This allows the creation of a 3D environment that closely resembles the liver’s native microstructure, providing an ideal setting for liver cell culture [34–37]. This engineering environment mimics the structure of the liver and promotes the survival and proliferation of liver cells [38–40]. By creating this optimal environment, tissue engineering technologies hold great promise for maintaining liver cell function over time, offering a significant advantage in both liver disease modeling and the development of liver cell-based artificial liver systems. The potential of biomimetic microenvironments to restore liver function through tissue regeneration offers a new and exciting possibility for liver disease therapy [41–44]. However, the challenge lies in designing a microenvironment that can sustainably support liver cell survival and maintain liver function over extended periods. This challenge is multifaceted and involves careful consideration of cellular interactions, scaffold materials, and biophysical conditions that need to be optimized for maximum effectiveness.

This review delineates the research milestones in the field of bionic microenvironments, with a particular emphasis on their applications in liver regeneration (Fig. 1). Initially, we review the pathological features of liver damage, including hepatitis virus infection, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcoholic liver disease (ALD), cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), highlighting their impact on liver function and regeneration. Subsequently, we explore the mechanisms of liver regeneration, emphasizing the roles of parenchymal and non-parenchymal cells, and critically appraise cutting-edge biomimetic modalities that enable precise recapitulation of hepatic function. Moreover, we critically assess the impediments to implementing these paradigms, namely scalability for clinical translation, nuanced microenvironmental refinement, and the rigorous extrapolation of benchtop discoveries into bedside therapeutics. In conclusion, we survey the vanguard of hepatic tissue engineering, elucidating how these breakthroughs promise to transcend extant therapeutic frontiers in liver disease.

Main pathological features of liver damage

Hepatic virus infection

Acute viral hepatitis (types A to E) is primarily characterized by acute inflammatory lesions in liver tissue, including hepatocellular degeneration, necrosis, and infiltration of various inflammatory cells, accompanied by significant hepatocyte regeneration [45]. Some hepatocytes may exhibit vacuolar degeneration or ballooning degeneration, leading to hepatocyte swelling, and apoptotic bodies may form, resulting in scattered focal necrosis [46]. Chronic hepatitis B mainly manifests as significant inflammatory responses in the portal areas, with a large number of inflammatory cell infiltrates, primarily lymphocytes, along with some plasma cells and macrophages [47–50]. Acute infections often present with jaundice and abdominal pain, yet most infected individuals can control the virus and recover within a few weeks [51–53]. However, for some patients, especially those infected in infancy or early childhood, the virus may develop into a chronic infection, often accompanied by chronic liver tissue damage and fibrosis, potentially leading to cirrhosis or even HCC [54].

As research into hepatitis B virus (HBV) biology and its immune response mechanisms deepens, scientists have gradually come to understand the complexity of chronic infections, focusing on the role of adaptive immunity [55, 56]. HBV can effectively evade the host’s innate immune response, allowing liver cells to remain infected without significant cytopathic effects. In contrast, the adaptive immune response is crucial for clearing the virus and its associated pathology [57]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of how HBV interacts with the host’s immune system is not only vital for comprehending the virus’s pathogenic mechanisms but also provides a theoretical basis for seeking new clinical approaches. In addition to HBV, other types of viral hepatitis, also pose significant threats to global public health [58]. Each viral infection has its unique transmission routes, clinical manifestations, and health intervention measures. Hepatitis A virus is more prevalent in the Mediterranean and North America regions and is typically transmitted via contaminated water or food, often presenting as an acute, self-limiting disease, with vaccination significantly reducing infection risks [59–61]. Hepatitis C, on the other hand, is primarily transmitted through blood, and its chronic infection can lead to long-term liver disease risks [62–64]. Effective antiviral therapies, such as direct-acting antiviral agents, have improved the mortality rate of hepatitis C [65, 66]. Moreover, hepatitis D is a dependent virus that co-infects with HBV, further complicating and exacerbating the disease. Hepatotropic viral infection invariably incites a triad of fatty degeneration, fibrogenesis, and inflammation whose interplay dictates disease trajectory and therapeutic responsiveness. Investigation of the HBV life cycle has long been impeded by the absence of in vitro platforms capable of recapitulating its entire intracellular chronology. Addressing this deficit, a recent study employed a 3D microfluidic hepatic culture as a physiologically relevant preclinical model of HBV infection [67]. The platform faithfully reconstitutes all phases of the viral life cycle and thereby affords mechanistic dissection of HBV immune evasion strategies that confound clinical intervention.

MASLD

MASLD is a progressively worsening liver condition characterized by pathological processes including fat accumulation, cell death, inflammation, and fibrosis within liver cells [68–70]. Therefore, after suffering from MASLD for a long time, liver fibrosis and cirrhosis will occur, eventually leading to the occurrence of cancer. The tissue regeneration during chronic diseases remains a controversial topic for many human organs [71–74]. In the case of MASLD, the liver exhibits a phenomenon of transdifferentiation between hepatocytes and cholangiocytes. This indicates that cellular plasticity plays a crucial role in the progression of the disease [75–77]. As MASLD advances, significant alterations occur in the cell types and structures within the liver microenvironment [78–80]. During metabolism and bile transport, parenchymal cells are the main functional cells of the liver. The cell types and tissue structures of the microenvironment directly affect the functions and regenerative capabilities of these cells [81, 82].

Different types of injuries can lead to significant changes in the microenvironment, thereby profoundly affecting the proliferation and regeneration of hepatocytes (Fig. 2A-B). In ALF, cellular proliferation is activated; however, in chronic liver diseases like MASLD, this proliferative capacity may be inhibited. The altered microenvironment in MASLD encourages various cell types to exhibit plasticity (Fig. 2C). Single-nucleus RNA sequencing analyses of MASLD progression reveal that cholangiocytes can transdifferentiate into hepatocytes and vice versa (Fig. 2D-F) [83]. This intercellular plasticity is particularly vital in the progression of MASLD, underscoring the influence of the microenvironment on liver regeneration processes.

Within MASLD research, the remodeling of the liver microenvironment may activate stem or progenitor cells, leading to differentiation into either hepatocytes or cholangiocytes, or allow hepatocytes and cholangiocytes to revert to a developmental progenitor state to restore cellular zonation [84–86]. The realization of these mechanisms is closely linked to changes in the microenvironment. Studying the alterations in the microenvironment associated with MASLD can illuminate its role in cellular regeneration [87–89]. This understanding lays the foundation for grasping the natural history of chronic liver diseases and seeking new treatment methods. However, constructing a truly representative microenvironment that simulates MASLD and assessing its functionalities present challenges, including the selection of biological materials, diversity in cell sources, and physiological adaptability [90–92]. In a MASLD investigation, investigators deployed organoid models coupled with transcriptomic profiling to quantify inflammatory and fibrotic signature [93]. Leveraging clinical MASLD gene-expression trajectories, they stratified organoid injury across discrete induction regimens. The findings underscore the imperative to calibrate MASLD organoids against clinical disease gradients, furnishing a single-cell benchmark for forthcoming damage assays.

ALD

Alcohol consumption is highly likely to cause fatty liver disease to develop into hepatocellular carcinoma [46]. The metabolism of alcohol primarily occurs in hepatocytes, where various key enzymes, convert ethanol into acetaldehyde, which is further metabolized to acetate [94–96]. This metabolic process can produce harmful by-products such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby leading to oxidative stress-related side effects [97–99]. Alcohol metabolism also reduces the NAD+/NADH ratio and impairs mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, which can induce hepatic steatosis [100, 101]. Additionally, ROS generated from alcohol breakdown stimulate the release of HMGB1 and mtDNA. These molecules act as danger signals, triggering liver inflammation and attracting immune cells to damaged areas. In cases of severe alcoholic hepatitis, hepatocytes may undergo dedifferentiation, assuming a cholangiocyte-like phenotype, which correlates with poorer clinical outcomes [102]. The emulate liver chip, faithfully mirroring human hepatic architecture, function, and multi-omic signatures, recapitulated early alcoholic liver disease by perfusing alcohol at patient-equivalent concentrations [103]. In this model, hepatocytes adopted a biliary-like phenotype congruent with human ALD histology. Cessation of alcohol exposure elicited demonstrable recovery, whereas concomitant challenge with ethanol and bacterial endotoxin precipitated aggravated injury, attesting to the platform’s fidelity in modelling both regression and progression of early-stage ALD.

The relationship between ALD and its microenvironment is complex and dynamic, characterized by interactions among various parenchymal and non-parenchymal cells (Fig. 3) [104]. Hepatocytes produce hazardous metabolites in the process of alcohol metabolism, leading to cellular injury and alterations in the microenvironment that trigger a cascade of immune responses. Liver non-parenchymal cells, including liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), Kupffer cells (KCs), and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of ALD. LSECs are responsible for the filtration of blood components and the regulation of metabolic enzyme activity, influencing the clearance of alcohol and the overall composition of the microenvironment. Meanwhile, KCs contribute to inflammation by clearing cellular debris and releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines. Upon activation, HSCs transform into myofibroblasts, producing excessive collagen and driving the progression of liver fibrosis.

Interorgan communication in ALD. Alcohol and its metabolites impair the gut barrier, allowing pathogen-associated molecules (PAMPs) to enter and activate immune responses, leading to inflammation. In ALD, levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) decrease, worsening gut permeability. Alcohol also disrupts gut microbiota, reduces beneficial bacteria like Akkermansia muciniphila, and lowers intestinal dendritic cells. It impairs FXR signaling, affecting bile acid regulation. In white adipose tissue, alcohol promotes fat breakdown, releasing fatty acids that accumulate in the liver as triglycerides, contributing to steatosis and inflammation. Furthermore, immune cells, including neutrophils、macrophages, exhibit dual roles in the ALD microenvironment [105, 106]. These immune cells respond to damage-associated molecular patterns released by hepatocytes, initiating inflammatory cascades and facilitating the recruitment of additional immune cells. Neutrophils, while contributing to liver injury through ROS production and cytokine release, also play a role in tissue repair to some extent [107]. T cells, particularly Th17 cells, enhance local inflammation, whereas regulatory T cells seek to suppress excessive immune activation [108, 109]. The imbalance between these immune cell populations in patients with ALD frequently results in exacerbated inflammation and liver damage. Consequently, the development of ALD is intricately linked to its microenvironment, and understanding this relationship is essential for advancing therapeutic strategies aimed at managing ALD [110].

ALF

ALF results from complex pathological conditions leading to acute liver cell damage, apoptosis, and decreased regenerative capacity. The microenvironment of liver injury is influenced by various factors, including changes in the ECM, the release of inflammatory mediators, and the involvement of other cells around the liver. Particularly, liver macrophages and stellate cells are pivotal in driving inflammatory cascades and fibrotic progression. Dysregulation of the gut-liver axis may impair intestinal barrier function, facilitating the translocation of microbial components and metabolic products to the liver via the portal circulation, thereby exacerbating hepatic harmful reactions. While existing studies have enhanced the understanding of the gut-liver axis’s importance in the pathogenesis, there has yet to be a comprehensive high-resolution characterization of intestinal cell features associated with ALF, and relevant therapeutic targets remain underexplored. The research utilized LPS-induced mouse model of ALF and employed single-cell RNA sequencing analysis to uncover the cellular landscape of intestinal cells during ALF (Fig. 4A) [111]. The study found that specific intestinal immune cell subsets, such as macrophages, and neutrophils, exhibited unique transcriptional profiles under pathological conditions. Additionally, intestinal epithelial cells displayed heterogeneity, with ALF-induced intestinal stem cells showing cell cycle arrest, impacting the gut’s protective and regenerative functions (Fig. 4B-J). These findings indicate that ALF’s effects on the gut extend beyond local responses, potentially triggering systemic physiological changes.

Liver cirrhosis and HCC

Persistent hepatic inflammation drives pathological ECM accumulation, promoting fibrosis and impairing liver repair in chronic liver disease. Cirrhosis develops as an advancing hepatic disorder resulting from multiple etiologies, characterized by significant changes in liver architecture and gradual loss of liver function [113, 114]. The pathological mechanism typically involves a fibrotic process following chronic liver injury, accompanied by abnormal deposition and remodeling of ECM components. In the pathological process of cirrhosis, increased cross-linking of the ECM is considered an important pathological feature [115]. This cross-linking not only enhances the rigidity of the ECM but also affects the interactions between cells and the matrix, altering cellular transcription programs and promoting macrophage activation and abnormal immune responses. Research by Yanan Du and colleagues revealed the critical role of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) in liver cirrhosis, indicating a significant correlation between increased AGE-mediated cross-linking and disease progression (Fig. 5A) [116]. These findings suggest that structural changes in the ECM could be a driving factor in the progression of liver cirrhosis. The study emphasizes the importance of AGE cross-linking in the pathogenesis of cirrhosis and points to potential therapeutic means targeting AGE-mediated cross-linking (Fig. 5B). Prolonged inflammation and fibrogenesis in cirrhosis intensify extracellular-matrix crosslinking, inflict recurrent parenchymal injury, and provoke aberrant regenerative proliferation. Within this milieu, iterative cycles of damage and repair foster genomic instability, while declining metabolic competence permits accumulation of carcinogens. The confluence of mutagenic stress and impaired detoxification triggers oncogene activation and tumor-suppressor silencing, culminating in the malignant evolution to HCC [117].

Exploring the tumor immune microenvironment is an important aspect in the treatment of HCC [118]. LSECs release TNF, IFN-γ, and ROS to kill tumor cells. KC contribute through phagocytosis and T cell recruitment. The immune response against tumors involves participation from B cells and NK cells. Conversely, T cells, immature dendritic cells, inhibit tumor-specific T cell function, suppressing immune surveillance. Cancer-associated fibroblasts enhance immune suppression and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, while NK cells, and tumor cells promote angiogenesis and immune evasion (Fig. 6) [119]. Additionally, metabolic reprogramming in immune cells can lead to functional abnormalities. In the HCC microenvironment, macrophages undergo M2 polarization, a phenotype associated with enhanced fatty acid oxidation capabilities and tumor cell migration. Metabolic reprogramming also affects T cell anti-tumor activity; in the HCC microenvironment, the increase in regulatory T cells is associated with their ability to utilize energy substrates such as lipids and glucose [120]. To surmount the clinical impasse of early hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance in cirrhosis, investigators devised a triphasic-CT-based deep-learning radiomics algorithm capable of pre-emptive HCC prediction [121]. Unlike conventional ultrasonography, whose sensitivity for incipient lesions remains limited and which often necessitates invasive biopsy, this integrative model fuses convolutionally extracted imaging signatures with clinical covariates to deliver high-precision risk stratification. Prospective embedding of the tool within clinical workflows promises individualized monitoring and timely interception of the cirrhosis-to-HCC transition [122].

Key regulatory mechanisms in liver regeneration

The liver’s regenerative capacity depends on tightly synchronized interactions among diverse cell types and molecular networks. The liver’s ability to rapidly restore its mass and function allows it to recover swiftly after toxic injury, surgical resection, or other severe damage. Emerging studies are elucidating the crosstalk between diverse cell populations, dynamic signaling pathway interactions, and microenvironmental regulation of liver regeneration. This section discusses these key mechanisms and explores their clinical applications.

Structure of the liver and regenerative capacity

Functionally and anatomically, the liver is organized into hexagonal units called hepatic lobules, which serve as its primary building blocks. The classic hepatic lobule displays portal triads at its peripheral angles and a single central vein in its geometric center (Fig. 7A) [123]. The hepatic parenchyma primarily consists of hepatocytes, the key functional cells that perform vital processes such as metabolic regulation, detoxification, and protein biosynthesis. In addition to hepatocytes, the liver also contains some biliary epithelial cells (BECs), which are responsible for modifying and transporting bile produced by hepatocytes. A subset of bipotent BECs resides in the canals of Hering, which can differentiate into either hepatocytes or BECs following liver injury, playing a critical role in liver regeneration. The biliary system itself functions as an exocrine conduit, facilitating bile excretion from the liver to the intestine.

The liver also contains various non-parenchymal cell types, including HSCs、 LSECs and a variety of non-resident immune cells. Notably, LSECs are uniquely characterized by fenestrations and the absence of a conventional basement membrane, which can promote the effective exchange of small molecules and proteins in the blood. The adult liver possesses remarkable regenerative capacity. Following ALF, hepatocytes and BECs rapidly proliferate to restore liver rebuild liver structure. Even in the absence of injury, low-level hepatocyte proliferation maintains liver homeostasis. Classic studies in rodents have demonstrated that the liver can fully regain its original size within days following partial hepatectomy, highlighting the organ’s regenerative potential.

However, during chronic liver diseases caused by factors such as viral infections, toxins, or metabolic disorders, the regenerative ability of hepatocytes and BECs gradually diminishes. These cells may become apoptotic or senescent, leading to impaired tissue repair. This dysfunction is often accompanied by progressive deposition of fibrotic tissue, which can evolve into cirrhosis and ultimately HCC. The resulting loss of liver function in cirrhosis is life-threatening, and the only curative option currently available is orthotopic liver transplantation. Unfortunately, the scarcity of donor organs and transplantation-related complications significantly limit its clinical application. Consequently, there is a growing demand for novel therapeutic strategies, such as cell and gene therapies, to address chronic liver diseases. Although numerous clinical trials have explored different protocols to generate hepatocytes for cell therapy, no long-term effective solution has yet been achieved. Therefore, ongoing research is focused on uncovering the biological mechanisms underlying liver regeneration to identify alternative and more effective cellular sources for therapeutic applications.

Cellular players in liver regeneration

Role of non-parenchymal cells in liver regeneration

Under homeostatic conditions, BECs undergo self-renewal, while HSCs remain largely quiescent. Resident macrophages perform essential immune surveillance by clearing cellular debris, as well as preventing the translocation of pathogens from the gut (Fig. 7B) [123]. During ALF, hepatocytes and BECs are upregulated to improve the injured area. Macrophages actively to eliminate necrotic cellular debris while undergoing phenotypic polarization toward a pro-regenerative state characterized by increased expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines and tissue-repair mediators (Fig. 7C) [123]. Chronic liver damage is characterized by widespread hepatocyte senescence. Under these conditions, the residual non-senescent hepatocytes exhibit compromised proliferative potential that fails to meet the regenerative demands of the chronically injured parenchyma (Fig. 7D) [123]. Although macrophages are still activated, the balance of macrophage subtypes shifts, favoring the accumulation of pro-inflammatory subsets. This leads to sustained immune activation and the stimulation of HSCs. Activated HSCs contribute to ECM deposition. Thus, persistent liver damage is marked by hepatocyte senescence and fibrogenesis.

In healthy livers, most hepatocytes are quiescent. Rodent lineage-tracing studies have shown that 84.1% of hepatocytes do not divide over a 9.5 to 9.7-month period [124–126]. Among those that do proliferate, the majority undergo only a single cell division, with a small fraction dividing multiple times. Whether hepatocyte self-renewal occurs uniformly across all liver zones or is restricted to a specialized subpopulation remains controversial. AXIN2 expression in perivenous hepatocytes functionally regulates Wnt signaling activity to maintain hepatic homeostasis and renewal capacity. However, more recent studies using genetically modified mouse models have failed to confirm these findings, suggesting that methodological differences may underlie the discrepancies. If AXIN2-dependent effects are validated, it may point to a critical regulatory role for AXIN2 in liver regeneration [127].

Under homeostatic conditions, hepatocyte proliferation is minimal. However, upon injury, hepatocytes rapidly re-enter the cell cycle to support regeneration. Studies show that residual hepatocytes can regain their original size within days following partial hepatectomy. This regeneration reaction involves the direct coordinated action of hepatocytes, BECs, HSCs, KC and LSECs.

Role of parenchymal cells in liver regeneration after ALF

ALF is encompassing various liver injuries that typically resolve within a short time. Common models for studying liver regeneration after acute injury include partial hepatectomy and excessive acetaminophen [128]. Partial hepatectomy serves as a clean model for studying liver regeneration without widespread necrosis or associated inflammation. Studies show that after 2/3 partial hepatectomy, remaining liver lobes expand to restore original liver volume within a week [129, 130]. The majority of residual liver cells re-initiate proliferation, with a subset undergoing additional rounds of division to fully restore the original hepatocyte population. Following surgical resection, hepatocyte apoptosis occurs 60 h after mitosis. In the partial hepatectomy experimental system, hepatocyte proliferation initiates in the perivenous region before propagating centripetally toward the portal triad. Single-cell sequencing in murine partial hepatectomy models reveals dynamic transcriptional reprogramming: while zonal gene expression gradients attenuate, hepatocyte heterogeneity persists with distinct subpopulations maintaining metabolic functions or adopting fetal-like states for regeneration. Wnt/β-catenin signaling emerges as the central regulator, mediated by injury-induced upregulation of LGR4/5 receptors and AXIN2 in hepatocytes. This on-demand activation of canonical Wnt signaling coordinates the regenerative response.

Biomimetic microenvironment construction strategy

Traditional 2D cell culture systems, such as petri dishes and multi-well plates, have long been used for in vitro studies. However, these models present significant limitations as they fail to replicate the complexity of the in vivo microenvironment [131]. The lack of ECM support in 2D cultures leads to cell depolarization, increased apoptosis, and reduced metabolic activity and functionality [132]. Additionally, 2D cultures lack the physiologically relevant cell-cell and cell- ECM interactions. This results in altered cell morphology, gene expression, and drug response, making it difficult to translate in vitro findings to in vivo applications. While animal models provide a more physiologically relevant environment, they come with ethical concerns, species differences, and high costs. The recent shift in regulatory policies, such as the FDA’s decision to remove the requirement for animal testing before human drug trials, further underscores the urgent need for more advanced and reliable in vitro models that better mimic the in vivo environment (Table 1).

Strategies for biomimetic 3D cell co-culture

Recent advancements in biomimetic 3D cell co-culture systems have led to the development of both scaffold-based and scaffold-free approaches. Scaffold-based methods utilize natural or synthetic matrices to offer structural support, facilitating cell adhesion, migration, and differentiation [139]. These scaffolds can be customized in terms of porosity, stiffness, and biochemical composition to regulate cell behavior effectively. However, challenges such as limited cell density and difficulty in achieving precise spatial organization persist [140]. In contrast, scaffold-free approaches, including spheroids, organoids, and cellular aggregates, allow cells to self-organize into 3D structures, facilitating more natural cell-cell and cell-ECM interactions [141]. A fundamental limitation of conventional culture systems is the diffusion constraint that intensifies with increasing construct size, impairing nutrient and oxygen delivery to the core and precipitating central necrosis. Consequently, replicating the tissue microenvironment necessitates meticulous regulation of parameters such as size, nutrient diffusion, and heterogeneity [142]. Multiple in vivo tissues circumvent this obstacle through densely vascularized networks that optimize microcirculation. Inspired by this biological principle, numerous porous materials and microchannel architectures have been engineered for 3D co-culture applications [143]. In addition, some emerging technologies, including 3D bioprinting and microfluidic organ-on-a-chip systems, offer innovative solutions by enabling precise control over spatial organization, mechanical forces, and biochemical gradients. These technologies are paving the way for more accurate and scalable in vitro models, with significant potential for applications in drug testing, personalized medicine.

One example of this advancement is the development of a method for creating liver lobule-like microtissues using a bioprinting system (Fig. 8A) [144]. This bioprinting system, which includes a preset cartridge and microfluidic emulsification system, successfully produces 45 uniformly sized multicellular microtissues per minute. The liver cells and endothelial cells in these tissues form structures resembling liver lobules and maintain high cell activity and structural integrity over time, outperforming unstructured spheroids. The structured microtissues also exhibit high expression of MRP2, albumin, and CD31. Another study found that structured microtissues were more stable than unstructured ones when implanted subcutaneously (Fig. 8B-D) [145]. Their research has shown that hydrogel-based bio-printed systems can control hepatocyte polarization in 3D cultures of human fetal liver organoids [146]. When the organoids were embedded in Matrigel and printed into HCC-gelatin hydrogel structures, the organoids maintained their integrity and morphology. Interestingly, the organoids polarized significantly in response to the printed HCC-gelatin columns, showing expression of MRP2 and ZO-1 within the organoid, while β4-integrin remained localized at the basal layer. These findings highlight the potential of 3D bioprinting to control cellular behavior and organoid development in a dynamic, biomimetic environment.

Furthermore, the construction of hierarchically inverse opal porous scaffolds has opened new avenues for 3D cell co-culture systems [142]. These scaffolds, fabricated through a simple microfluidic method that combines emulsion droplet templating with inert polymerization, effectively ensure nutrient transfer during cultivation and permit high-density cellular assembly. Sequential inoculation of different cell types into these structures has yielded co-cultures that emulate functional tissue characteristics, with experimental data confirming the maintenance of hepatocyte-specific functions in vivo. This innovative scaffold design provides a versatile platform for optimizing 3D co-culture techniques and tissue engineering. In parallel, efforts to mimic native vasculature have led to the creation of vascularized liver lobule–like organs using a 3D hydrogel system [147]. This approach employs gelatin methacryloyl hydrogel combined with sustained-release systems of vascular endothelial growth factor and human umbilical vein endothelial cells. The resulting organoids exhibit superior in vitro functionality and promote angiogenesis, maintaining activity and enhancing vascularization during in situ transplantation. These breakthroughs offer promising insights into in situ tissue reconstruction, vascular development, and therapeutic applications for liver organoids [148].

Decellularized materials for biomimetic scaffolds

In the fields of tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, decellularized materials have garnered significant interest due to their excellent biocompatibility and inherent biomimetic properties. These materials are produced through specialized physical, chemical, or biological processing methods that remove all cellular components from native tissues or organs [149]. The goal is to preserve the complex architecture and the bioactive molecules of the ECM, which play critical roles in supporting cell attachment, proliferation, differentiation, and tissue-specific functions.

Decellularized materials can be broadly classified into two main categories based on their morphology and application strategies. The first category comprises whole organ decellularized dECM scaffolds, which retain the native 3D structure of the organ, including its vasculature, ECM composition, and microarchitecture [150]. These scaffolds serve as natural templates for recellularization, providing an organ-specific microenvironment that closely mimics in vivo conditions. Their applications are extensive in organ regeneration, where they are seeded with relevant cell types and subjected to bioreactor culture systems for functional tissue development. The second category consists of solubilized ECM hydrogels, which are derived by further processing the decellularized matrix into a gel-like form [151]. This involves digesting the dECM using enzymes such as pepsin, resulting in a solubilized form that can be easily handled and injected. ECM hydrogels exhibit shear-thinning properties, making them suitable for minimally invasive delivery, 3D bioprinting, or coating surfaces to promote tissue regeneration. They are particularly useful for creating cell-laden scaffolds, modeling tissue niches in vitro, and delivering bioactive factors [152].

These two types of decellularized materials have distinct characteristics and are suited to different applications. Whole organ dECM scaffolds provide a highly biomimetic and structurally relevant environment, making them ideal for organ reconstruction and complex tissue regeneration [153]. In contrast, ECM hydrogels offer versatility for injectable therapies, 3D bioprinting, and high-throughput in vitro studies. Both approaches leverage the natural ECM’s ability to promote tissue-specific cellular responses, making them invaluable tools in advancing regenerative strategies, developing disease models, and studying tissue development and repair mechanisms [154].

Whole organ dECM scaffolds

Whole organ dECM scaffolds refer to the comprehensive, intact 3D framework derived from native tissues or organs after cellular components have been removed. These scaffolds preserve the organ’s original macrostructure, including its complex vascular network, tissue-specific architecture, and ECM composition, providing an authentic biological template for regenerative applications [155]. As a macro-scale biomimetic framework, whole organ dECM scaffolds retain the inherent spatial organization and biochemical cues critical for guiding cell attachment, growth, and functional maturation [156]. This approach aims to replicate the native organ’s structural hierarchy at a macro level, creating a conducive environment for recellularization and tissue regeneration. In liver tissue engineering, such scaffolds serve as the foundation for reconstructing functional liver tissue in vitro, enabling the development of transplantable organs or advanced models for studying liver physiology, drug metabolism, and disease mechanisms [157]. Their large-scale, organ-level architecture makes them particularly suitable for bridging the gap between experimental models and clinical transplantation, although challenges remain in ensuring uniform recellularization and maintaining vascular integrity.

Advances in tissue engineering technologies have provided new ideas for constructing in vitro liver models. dECM technology removes cellular components through physical, chemical, or enzymatic methods while preserving the ECM structure and bioactive molecules of the original tissue, offering an ideal scaffold material for liver cell regeneration and functional recovery. dECM-based liver scaffolds can effectively mimic the 3D structure of the liver, and when seeded with human fetal liver cells or co-cultured with hepatocytes and endothelial cell lines, they form highly organized 3D human liver models. These models exhibit significant liver differentiation and metabolic activity during a culture period of 7 to 14 days, providing a new experimental platform for drug metabolism and toxicity studies. Kang’s group involves modifying the biomechanical properties of decellularized liver scaffolds using nanoscale graphene oxide particles (NGOs) (Fig. 9A-G) [158]. This approach successfully achieved efficient regeneration of damaged liver tissue, extended the survival time of allogeneic transplants, and improved the therapeutic effect of bioengineered livers, providing a novel solution for organ donation. Although the dECM scaffold technology shows great potential for constructing in vitro liver models, there are still several challenges. For instance, how to minimize the immunogenicity of the scaffold, optimize recellularization strategies to improve cell survival rates, and simulate in vivo blood flow dynamics to enhance liver function are still key research problems. Furthermore, while dECM-based liver models have high potential in drug-induced liver injury research, their predictive capability needs further optimization to more comprehensively recapitulate the liver regeneration process and pathological responses.

Solubilized dECM hydrogel

dECM hydrogels are biomaterials that predominantly rely on the self-assembly process of collagen fibers, which can be regulated by glycosaminoglycans and other ECM proteins. At a macro level, these hydrogels form a distinct class of biomimetic scaffolds, characterized by their tunable physical and chemical properties that closely mimic the native ECM environment [159]. The formation process involves two key stages: first, dECM materials are dissociated into their constituent protein monomers under specific conditions, such as enzymatic digestion or chemical treatment [160]. Second, by precisely controlling factors like temperature or pH, these monomers undergo reorganization through intermolecular interactions, resulting in the spontaneous formation of a homogeneous gel with a 3D network structure. The most widely used method for preparing dECM hydrogels involves pepsin digestion, which effectively solubilizes dECM powders by breaking down complex proteins into soluble constituents [161]. These solubilized dECM hydrogels retain numerous bioactive factors inherent to the native ECM, providing a biologically rich environment that promotes cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation. Their adaptability in adjusting physical properties makes them particularly advantageous for soft tissue repair, wound healing, and regenerative applications. Currently, dECM hydrogels are extensively used in cartilage repair, skin regeneration, and other tissue engineering fields, demonstrating their significant regenerative potential [162].

Udagawa et al. employed liver-derived dECM hydrogel as a scaffold to augment engraftment and functional performance of mature human hepatocytes within fibrotic tissue [163]. Hepatocytes suspended in the solubilized hydrogel were allowed to gel at 37 °C, yielding cohesive aggregates exhibiting markedly superior intercellular crosstalk and angiogenic potential relative to conventional collagen matrices. Subsequent analyses confirmed robust engraftment and sustained hepatic function. Sun and co-workers subsequently engineered an ECM-mimetic hydrogel via polyethylene glycol–polysaccharide phase separation, yielding a highly porous architecture with tunable mechanics [164]. This scaffold substantially enhanced hepatocyte adhesion and viability while preserving long-term homeostasis and hepatic functionality. Mechanistically, hepatocytes within the hydrogel recapitulated the Notch and TNF signaling profiles of freshly isolated counterparts, underscoring its promise as a tissue-specific platform for liver bioengineering.

However, native dECM hydrogels are constrained by the stochastic assembly of collagen fibrils, which complicates precise tuning of physicochemical attributes. To surmount this limitation, a covalently cross-linked hydrogel was engineered whose Young’s modulus could be predictably modulated across the physiological and fibrotic spectrum by altering polymer concentration, while preserving organotypic bioactivity [165]. In particular, the click-cross-linked dECM hydrogel markedly elevated hepatocyte metabolic output, albumin secretion, CYP1A2 and CYP3A4 activities, and excretory capacity. Chen et al. further advanced the field by fabricating a bioactive dECM platform embedding dihydrolipoic acid–stabilised Pt nanoclusters doped with Cu nanozymes within cell-laden microgels [149]. Co-injected through porcine liver-derived dECM hydrogel for in situ delivery, this construct effectively resolved acute liver failure via synergistic antioxidant protection and enhanced regenerative signaling [46, 166].

Vascularization of microenvironment

Constructing an in vitro three-dimensional model that accurately replicates the physiological functions of a healthy human liver holds tremendous significance for enhancing experimental precision, advancing drug development, and facilitating clinical applications [90]. Given the liver’s intricate vascular environment and complex architecture, developing a sophisticated microvascular network in vitro along with ensuring adequate nutrient supply is essential for supporting cell growth, mimicking the heterogeneous hepatic structure, and recreating the cellular microenvironment [167]. The generation of vascularized liver tissue has thus emerged as a pivotal focus within regenerative medicine and organ engineering [168].

From a cellular origin perspective, two primary approaches are evident. The first involves stem cells with differentiation potential, often utilizing scaffold-free methods to produce spheroid or organoid structures lacking fixed architecture [169]. Initially, organoids are induced to differentiate in vitro to form primordial blood vessels, which are subsequently implanted into the liver scaffold to further promote vascular development [148]. Such cultivation techniques can spontaneously generate micro-liver tissues endowed with primary vascular networks, recapitulating embryonic vascularization processes. Post-transplantation, these tissues can integrate with the host’s vasculature via angiogenesis, and the addition of angiogenic factors like VEGF can markedly enhance vascularization efficiency [170]. The second approach employs mature, non-differentiated cells. These cells typically construct liver lobule-like tissues with embedded vascular structures through 3D bioprinting technologies. Owing to their limited differentiation capacity, mature cells benefit from the precise spatial control afforded by 3D bioprinting at micrometer scales, enabling the fabrication of microvascular networks aligned with pre-designed patterns, thereby preserving tissue architecture and facilitating cell-cell interactions [147]. Additionally, liver-on-a-chip platforms utilizing microfluidic channels offer an alternative strategy that simulates vascular distribution within liver lobules and allows for the integration of sensors for real-time functional monitoring.

The development of an artificial biological liver with a perfusable vascular network marks a significant step forward in liver bioengineering. To promote vascular regeneration, anti-CD31 aptamers were coated within the vascular cavity, facilitating the regeneration of vascular endothelium. Experimental results showed that this highly functional vascular system could activate the integrin-Akt signaling pathway, which notably improved liver cell function and survival rates in vitro [171]. Importantly, the organized vascular network was able to sustain liver function levels after transplantation in vivo and reduce the risk of vascular embolism, highlighting the potential clinical applicability of this vascularized artificial liver [172].

Building on the importance of vascularization, another approach involved creating highly vascularized liver cell spheroids using a pro-angiogenic strategy in vitro. This was achieved by integrating VEGF secreted by fibroblasts into a microfluidic device, which enhanced endothelial cell migration and vascular formation [173]. Functional assessments revealed significant improvements in key liver functions such as albumin secretion and urea synthesis, resulting in a 3D liver tissue model that more closely mimics physiological states, exhibits greater stability, and has strong potential for expansion. Such technology provides a solid foundation for scalable liver tissue construction in regenerative medicine.

Another innovative technique employed embedded printing with a cross-linkable, bimodal, cell-loaded material embedded in a yield-stress, self-repairing medium. Rheological tests demonstrated that by adjusting printing speed, researchers could precisely control the diameter of blood vessels and successfully construct complex vascular networks [174]. Embedding endothelial cell-loaded sacrificial ink into this medium enabled endothelial cells to form continuous vascular structures that closely adhered to liver cells. The resulting vascularized liver tissue showed superior performance in protein synthesis, metabolic functions, and expression of liver injury markers compared to non-vascularized models, underscoring the importance of vascular networks for functional tissue engineering [175].

The advancement of biomimetic cell culture

Bioengineered cell culture is a promising approach that overcomes the limitations of traditional 2D cultures by recreating the natural cellular microenvironment in vitro. The key principle of bioengineered culture is to recapitulate the tripartite nature of native cellular microenvironments through precise spatial organization, biochemical signaling, and biophysical cues. Spatially, cells in vivo exist within a complex 3D ECM, which provides structural support and regulates cell interactions [176, 177]. The evolution of in vitro liver models has transitioned from 2D cell cultures to 3D cell models, incorporating various technologies such as micro-patterned cell arrays, spheroids, organoids, and microfluidic systems. These models provide valuable insights into liver physiology, pathophysiology, and drug responses [178]. However, traditional 2D cell cultures lack a tissue-specific microenvironment, and early 3D cell models, while improving cell interactions, still struggle to fully replicate the complex structure and function of the liver. As a result, the next generation of hepatic bioengineered liver microphysiological systems (HBL-MPS) has gradually developed, offering a more comprehensive simulation of human liver functions by introducing physiological fluid dynamics, intercellular communication, and appropriate matrix materials.

The goal of HBL-MPS is to accurately represent the structure and function of the hepatic lobule, including oxygen gradients, zonation, vascularization, and cell signaling (Fig. 10A) [179]. Key technologies include: microfluidic devices, which provide physiologically relevant shear stress and fluid dynamics to simulate the liver’s blood flow environment; bioprinting and engineered matrix materials, which precisely control the spatial distribution to construct highly organized 3D structures. The next-generation patient-derived structured MPS and/or organoid MPS are essentially non-invasive, as cells from patients can be derived from blood or superficial skin cell collections. Furthermore, these platforms are suitable for controlled variations in experimental parameters such as controlled changes in time and disease drivers, complementing patient-derived, tissue-based ex vivo studies. With the integration of various imaging modalities, metabolomics, and real-time cellular functional readouts, the data generated by MPS could correlate with disease progression, enabling the identification and validation of non-invasive prognostic biomarkers.

Patient-specific iPSC-based structured MPS and/or organoid MPS can address the challenge of enrolling high-risk patients in large-scale long-term studies, which may fail and exclude other potentially beneficial treatments. These personalized MPS platforms can be “recruited” into preclinical trials for parallel testing of multiple single drugs and/or combinations. These MPS platforms reveal which patient cohorts respond to experimental treatments, optimizing clinical trial design, reducing variability in pharmacological responses, and minimizing the impact of disease heterogeneity, ultimately increasing the likelihood of drug approval. Given the steady technological progress made by global research teams, MPS-driven preclinical trials are expected to gain clinically meaningful validation within the next five years, supporting their role as a key component of clinical trial design. However, it is essential to demonstrate that adult PSC-derived structured MPS and organoid MPS possess critical physiological and clinical functions before they can be considered truly relevant for clinical applications (Fig. 10B).

In vitro dynamic culture system for maintaining liver-specific functions

Extracorporeal artificial liver support systems have been developed over recent decades to bridge patients to liver transplantation or facilitate native liver recovery. These systems are broadly categorized into two types: artificial and bioartificial liver support devices [180, 181]. Artificial liver systems utilize physicochemical approaches, such as plasma exchange, hemoperfusion and blood filtration, to eliminate toxins and metabolic waste from blood or plasma [182, 183]. The clinically established artificial liver support systems, including the Molecular adsorbent recirculation system, the Double plasma Molecular Adsorption System, the separated plasma adsorption system, and the single albumin dialysis, have been proven effective in treating patients with liver failure [184]. While artificial liver systems can temporarily remove toxins, they lack the capacity to synthesize liver-specific proteins or metabolize substances, limiting their ability to significantly improve survival rates in ALF patients. In contrast, bioartificial liver (BAL) systems incorporate functional hepatocytes within bioreactors, enabling not only detoxification but also critical metabolic and synthetic functions. This makes BAL a more promising therapeutic approach for ALF.

Bioreactors, as the core components of bioartificial liver systems, determine the cell growth environment, metabolic activity, and ultimately the therapeutic outcome [185, 186]. Therefore, the design of bioreactors must take into account the biological needs of cell culture and the engineering requirements of the system to achieve efficient material exchange, maintain cell function, and maximize product synthesis. In this section, we will delve into the types, structural characteristics, design principles, and material selection of bioreactors. The type of bioreactor is typically closely related to the way cells grow, the maintenance of metabolic activity, and the secretion capability of products [187]. Different types of reactors exhibit significant differences in terms of cell culture conditions, material exchange rates, operational simplicity, and scalability potential. Common reactor types include plate reactors, hollow fiber reactors, fluidized bed reactors, and microcarrier reactors.

Hollow fiber bioreactor

The hollow fiber bioreactor has significant application value in bioartificial liver systems due to its unique structural design. This reactor consists of a series of hollow fibers with micropores, where cells are typically cultured on both the inner and outer surfaces of the fibers [188, 189]. The design of the hollow fibers is highly similar to the capillary network in the liver, enabling effective simulation of the material exchange process that occurs in the liver. In this reactor, the medium surrounding the cells exchanges materials with the simulated blood flowing inside the fibers (usually a nutrient solution) via diffusion. This design not only provides cells with efficient nutrient supply but also effectively removes metabolic waste, significantly improving cell survival rates and metabolic activity [190]. The structural characteristics of the hollow fiber reactor allow for a high surface area in a limited space, enabling the cultivation of a large number of liver cells. Especially in terms of gas exchange, the hollow fiber reactor has a distinct advantage [191]. Gas exchange occurs through the micropore network in the inner and outer walls of the fibers, meeting the oxygen and carbon dioxide demands of liver cells and ensuring efficient cell metabolism. Furthermore, the fluid flow patterns inside the reactor dictate the metabolic activity of the cells by ensuring uniform distribution of essential nutrients and oxygen while promoting effective waste removal.

While considerable advances have been achieved in human MPS development during the past decade, liver-on-chip models that adhere to the 3Rs-complied (animal component-free), especially for drug metabolism and trans-epithelial transport, remain an unmet need in preclinical pharmacokinetics, absorption, distribution and metabolism. In this context, researchers have filled this gap by developing an innovative microfluidic liver-like system (Fig. 11A) [192]. This system utilizes organoid-derived polarized epithelial monolayers cultured on recombinant human laminin-332-coated hollow fiber membranes (HFM) (Fig. 11B-D). This platform uniquely supports dual-directional compound tracking-quantifying initial absorption from the blood-mimicking basal side while monitoring metabolite secretion into the tubular-like apical compartment or systemic return. Compared to conventional donor-matched organoid cultures, the system exhibits better hepatocyte-like maturation and functionality, similar to the previous model based on bile duct-on-chip HFM. Studies have found that the monolayers grown on differentiated HFM commonly express or enrich representative membrane proteins involved in basal uptake, efflux (Fig. 11E-G). This system enables precise measurement of key pharmacokinetic parameters for both parent drugs and metabolites during sustained 3-hour perfusion time, a significant improvement compared to previous perfusion times of only 10–13 min. However, there are also operational challenges associated with hollow fiber bioreactors. Due to the micropores and complex structure within the fibers, the reactor is prone to clogging, especially after prolonged use, as the accumulation of cellular metabolic waste may block the micropores, thus affecting the efficiency of material exchange.

Fluidized bed bioreactor

The concept of fluidized bed bioreactor (FBB) originates from thermal engineering, where solid particles are suspended in a liquid flow when the flow rate exceeds a certain threshold, creating a dynamic “fluidized” state. This principle was adapted for the field of bioengineering, where it has evolved into a 3D culture platform suitable for various cell culture and tissue engineering applications. In BAL systems, FBBs work similarly by continuously perfusing culture medium or plasma to provide a dynamic, suspended, 3D culture environment for encapsulated liver cells or spheroids, promoting efficient material exchange [193, 194]. Currently, most BAL systems utilize cell encapsulation technology in FBB structures, with some systems designed for spheroid cultivation using spheroid reservoirs.

Cell encapsulation technology has emerged as a pivotal strategy in liver tissue engineering and BAL system development during the past decade. Hepatocytes are typically cultured in 3D spheroid forms, which have been shown to not only improve the long-term survival of primary hepatocytes in vitro but also enhance their functional expression. The cells in spheroids form a polarized structure through tight cell-to-cell junctions, exhibiting enhanced liver-specific phenotypes and metabolic activity [195]. Additionally, the high-density aggregation of spheroids facilitates the scalability of BAL systems, enhancing their clinical potential. Traditional spheroid preparation methods include dynamic suspension systems such as rotating bottles and NASA rotating cell culture devices, which are simple to operate, suitable for long-term culture, and capable of batch production of uniformly structured spheroids. However, in these systems, spheroids are directly exposed to flowing medium or plasma, making them susceptible to shear stress damage, leading to structural disruption, loss of function, and even cell death.

To mitigate the negative effects of shear stress, researchers have developed various FBB optimization strategies [196]. One such approach is the directed-flow modified fluidized bed bioreactor (DMFBB), which offers an optimized alternative to traditional FBBs. By structuring the internal flow field of the reactor, DMFBB significantly reduces bed expansion and void volume, improving the effective contact area between cells and perfusate. The shear stress experienced by the microcapsules in DMFBB is much lower than in conventional FBBs, reducing the risk of structural rupture, improving the transport efficiency of nutrients and metabolites, and enhancing hepatocyte metabolic function expression. This optimization provides a more suitable fluid dynamics environment for cell growth and function maintenance while maintaining good fluidization.

Microcarrier bioreactor

In the research of bioartificial liver systems, microcarrier reactors are widely used due to their ability to provide a 3D cell culture environment. By allowing cells to attach to small, porous carrier particles, these reactors offer a large surface area, supporting the attachment and expansion of a large number of cells. However, microcarrier reactors still face challenges in long-term stable operation, such as insufficient cell function maintenance, accumulation of metabolic waste, and uneven nutrient supply, limiting their further development in clinical translation [197].

To address these limitations, contemporary research has biomimetically replicated hepatic lobule microarchitecture to engineer an innovative bioartificial liver system. This system features semi-permeable microtubes as the central structure, surrounded by functional micro-particles carrying hepatocytes derived from iPSC, fabricated using microfluidic droplet technology (Fig. 12A). This design mimics the organization of the hepatic lobule with a “central vein-radial arrangement of hepatocytes.” Microfluidic technology offers high controllability, enabling the batch production of uniform, structurally adjustable cell-loaded micro-particles, providing ideal scaffolding materials for constructing functional liver tissue units (Fig. 12B) [198]. Furthermore, the semi-permeable microtubes in the system function similarly to dialysis membranes, allowing for the removal of toxins and the exchange of metabolites in body fluids, playing a key role in maintaining cell function and system stability. This design integrates 3D spatial structure simulation, functional cell carriers, and material exchange pathways, offering new insights for the construction of next-generation bioartificial liver devices.

Some studies have also focused on the micro-scale and simulated the arrangement of bile canaliculi to create a directional cell culture microenvironment. For instance, researchers have used microfluidic technology to prepare hollow silk fibers, which are then arranged into a tubular network. After embedding hepatocytes between the microtubes, they observed significant improvements in cell polarity and bile secretion function. This approach, combining structural and functional biomimicry, provides new ideas for developing fully functional bioartificial livers. In traditional microcarrier reactors, cells typically attach to the surface of the microcarriers, and the reactor uses stirring to achieve uniform mixing and exchange of oxygen and nutrients. However, these systems face several challenges in large-scale applications. For example, stirring speed and gas exchange conditions must be precisely controlled, as improper adjustments can lead to cell detachment or aggregation, affecting cell distribution and function. Therefore, one of the core challenges for artificial liver systems is how to construct structurally stable and functionally complete 3D cell structures while maintaining cell viability. Building upon the native architecture of hepatic micro-tissues, a novel spatially ordered multicellular micro-lobule-like-fibers (MCMF) was created by using continuous microfluidic rotation technology for transplantation therapy in ALF (Fig. 12C-E) [199]. Hepatocytes and non-parenchymal cells in a single liver lobule are typically arranged radially around the central vein, a structure that not only provides a stable spatial scaffold for cells but also facilitates efficient oxygen and nutrient transport, maintaining metabolic function. Therefore, mimicking this structural feature to construct miniaturized liver lobules is of great significance for enhancing the performance of bioreactors.

Liver-on-a-chip

Anticipating drug efficacy prior to clinical entry remains the cornerstone of preclinical screening. The convergence of microfabrication and tissue engineering has propelled the evolution of organ-on-a-chip technology, offering a sophisticated alternative to conventional preclinical models [200]. Liver-on-a-chip systems reconstruct the liver’s minimal functional unit, achieving high-fidelity microscale emulation of hepatic physiology [180]. This biomimetic fidelity is essential for elucidating drug effects and refining safety and efficacy testing. Key determinants include device architecture, biomaterial selection, surface functionalization, cellular sourcing, sensor integration, and artificial intelligence (AI)-driven optimization [201]. Cell source

Primary human hepatocytes remain the gold standard for liver-on-a-chip applications, delivering physiological CYP activity and clinically relevant drug responses. Hendriks et al. leveraged 3D spheroids of primary hepatocytes to establish a CYP3A4 induction assay; screening twenty-five compounds at physiologic concentrations yielded complete sensitivity and specificity [211]. Deng and colleagues integrated primary hepatocytes into a 3D bioprinted hepatic construct; sustained oxygen and nutrient perfusion promoted rapid proliferation, restored hepatic function, and extended survival in murine liver-failure models [212]. When primary cells are scarce, transformed hepatic lines provide an accessible, cost-effective alternative. HepG2 and related carcinoma cells proliferate indefinitely, facilitate high-throughput screening, and adopt organotypic traits when cultured as 3D spheroids, including increased ECM deposition, cytoskeletal reorganization, and up-regulation of adhesion molecules. Pallavi et al. profiled HepG2 spheroids and documented time-dependent attenuation of proliferation coupled with enhanced expression of albumin, CYP450 enzymes, and other hepatospecific markers [213]. Only spheroids cultured at optimal density sustained balanced growth and metabolic competence, satisfying the stringent requirements for genotoxicity and hepatotoxicity testing [214].

iPSC-derived hepatocytes offer a renewable, patient-specific alternative. These progeny secrete essential plasma proteins, detoxify ammonia, and regulate glycogen and lipid metabolism, positioning them at the forefront of personalized liver models [215]. Although they initially display fetal characteristics—including low CYP activity and elevated α-fetoprotein—maturation cocktails and 3-D cues can drive adult-like function. Coupling iPSC-derived hepatic organoids with 3-D bioprinting further amplifies viability and performance. Li et al. cultured human chemically induced iPSC-hepatic organoids in an oxygen-permeable microporous device, then bioprinted spheroidal constructs; in-vitro and in-vivo readouts revealed superior cellular fitness and preserved hepatic competence [216]. Multi-cellular co-cultures that integrate hepatocytes with Kupffer cells, stellate cells, or mesenchymal stem cells markedly enhance physiological realism. Such configurations dissect paracrine regulation of drug-induced injury and inflammation while sustaining albumin output and metabolic flux. Microfluidic orchestration is now feasible: Prodanov et al. engineered a sinusoid-mimetic chip co-culturing hepatocytes, LX-2 stellate cells, and U937 macrophages under controlled perfusion, faithfully recapitulating the hepatic sinusoidal niche [217].

Device design and material selection

Flat-plate microsystems constitute the foundational tier of liver-on-a-chip technology. Perforated cultureware, often functionalised with vertically aligned graphene oxide or micropatterned extracellular-matrix proteins, supports high-throughput screening while preserving phenotypic fidelity. Brenton et al. co-cultured murine embryonic fibroblasts with hepatocytes on collagen-micropatterned plates, achieving superior CYP450 stability, diminished keratin-19 expression, and maximal per-cell albumin output relative to conventional monolayers [218]. Chip-based microfluidic platforms transcend the diffusive constraints of planar culture by recapitulating the dynamic hepatic microenvironment. Fabricated predominantly from PDMS, these devices afford precise modulation of shear stress, oxygen tension, and nutrient flux. Du et al. engineered a bichannel PDMS sinusoid-on-a-chip separated by a porous polyethylene membrane, whose fenestrated geometry emulates the liver’s endothelial barrier; functional assays confirmed sustained albumin secretion and preserved hepatocellular competence [219]. Multi-organ coupling systems integrate discrete microphysiological units via fluidic circuitry to model systemic crosstalk. Tao et al. interfaced hiPSC-derived hepatic and pancreatic islet organoids within a unified microfluidic framework, faithfully reconstructing the human liver-islet axis under both euglycaemic and type-2-diabetic conditions. This platform captures the synergistic regulation of glucose metabolism and pharmacological responses across organs, offering a powerful paradigm for interrogating multi-organ pathophysiology [220, 221].

AI-assisted system optimization

AI, grounded in advanced computational modelling and bioinformatics, is redefining precision in hepatic medicine [222]. Deep-learning algorithms now detect subtle parenchymal lesions, tumors, fibrotic septa, microvascular aberrations, that escape unaided human scrutiny, and iterative training continuously refines model parameters. Multiparametric MRI further enriches this pipeline, offering volumetric quantification of lesions alongside quantitative maps of soft-tissue contrast, signal intensity, and contrast-agent kinetics, thereby furnishing comprehensive anatomical and functional signatures. Beyond imaging, AI is dismantling the constraints of classical histopathology by fusing digital slide analytics with multi-omic datasets. This integrative approach deciphers the spatial architecture of the tumor microenvironment, delineating the precise topography and intercellular crosstalk of malignant and immune populations [223]. In HCC, such analyses expose an intricate regulatory circuitry linking innate immune signaling to mutational landscapes. Notably, Jia et al. leveraged whole-slide imaging (WSI) and AI-driven spatial statistics to interrogate NK cell distribution within HCC tissue, revealing that SPON2, a pivotal NK-cell regulator, augments recurrence risk stratification beyond conventional clinical indices [224]. To recapitulate these microenvironmental dynamics experimentally, the same group engineered a tumor-endothelial migration chip that emulates vascular invasion during metastasis [225]. Employing a KNN algorithm, they correlated multiplexed biomarker signatures with tumor-cell invasive capacity, achieving accurate prediction of exosome-mediated metastatic patterns.

Moreover, AI systems now quantify the full histopathological spectrum of liver injury, including steatosis, ballooning, lobular inflammation and fibrosis, providing an integrated portrait of disease severity. Janani et al. introduced an AI driven scoring instrument for MASH histology whose assessments of necrosis, inflammatory grade and fibrosis stage were concordant with consensus pathologist evaluations [226]. Relative to expert review, the algorithm matched mean interobserver performance while accelerating throughput by orders of magnitude through automated deep learning image processing. Beyond histology, AI is resolving the assembly complexity and analytical bottlenecks inherent to organoid cultures. Emerging AI organs interfaced with adaptive hardware promise to compress development timelines and expedite clinical translation [227]. Tan et al. reported the first bright field image based AI model for liver organoids [228]. Twelve microfluidic chips, each hosting 3 D hepatic organoids, were challenged with thirty reference compounds; time lapse bright field microscopy captured morphological responses non invasively, and convolutional networks translated these signatures into toxicity scores. The platform delivered 82.34% classification accuracy, establishing a high throughput label free pipeline for hepatotoxicity screening.

Potential applications

Animal surrogates inadequately replicate human hepatic physiology and pathology; liver-on-a-chip disease models therefore provide an indispensable translational platform for mechanistic investigation, therapeutic development, and toxicological evaluation [229]. Complex multifactorial disorders such as NAFLD and ALD remain mechanistically opaque, underscoring the urgent need for faithful in vitro models. Janna et al. engineered an ALD liver chip that accurately recapitulates recovery upon alcohol withdrawal and deterioration under a dual-hit alcohol-plus-endotoxin challenge [103]. By refining the ECM scaffold to generate a biomimetic biliary network, the authors further identified ethanol-induced ductal injury as a sensitive early marker of hepatic toxicity.

The absence of in vitro platforms capable of recapitulating the complete HBV life cycle has long impeded mechanistic and therapeutic studies. Microfluidic liver-on-a-chip now fills this gap. A 3D perfused organoid system sustains HBV replication for more than 40 days, supports all viral life-cycle steps, and elicits innate immune responses and cytokine profiles that mirror patient data, enabling dissection of immune-evasion pathways and biomarker validation. Prieto et al. employed a single-channel chip seeded with primary rat hepatocytes and demonstrated efficient infection by adenovirus-delivered HBV genomes, with subsequent release of viral DNA into the medium [67]. Sodunke et al. co-cultured primary human hepatocytes and bovine aortic endothelial cells on opposite faces of a porous membrane to create a stable hepatic-sinusoid chip, enabling chronic HBV infection studies for extended periods [230].

HCC remains a leading global cause of cancer-related death, and inter-individual variability in drug response continues to limit chemotherapy efficacy. Engineered MPS are now addressing this unmet need. Zhu et al. fabricated a hierarchically ordered hydrogel, micropores arrayed in a lattice interlaced with aligned nanopores, via template replication. This architecture yielded uniformly sized tumor spheroids with superior viability, providing a robust scaffold for anti-cancer drug screening [231]. Shen et al. leveraged microfluidic HCC chips to interrogate the role of HSCs in tumor angiogenesis and to screen for sorafenib resistance [232]. Such tumor-on-chip configurations faithfully recreate tumor–stromal interactions and open new avenues for dissecting therapeutic sensitivity in a patient-relevant context.

Future challenges and development directions

Challenges in addressing the pathological features of liver damage

Liver damage often leads to various pathological features, including inflammation, fibrosis, and necrosis, all of which severely compromise liver function. While much has been done to understand the molecular pathways that mediate liver injury, a deeper understanding of how these features progress in response to different etiologies is needed. Current research is focused on identifying biomarkers that both quantify existing hepatocyte damage and anticipate fibrotic transformation risks [233]. However, the complexity of liver pathophysiology, particularly the multifactorial causes of damage and the different stages of fibrosis, still poses a challenge to accurately assessing the degree of damage and formulating effective therapeutic interventions [234, 235]. One of the significant challenges lies in modeling liver damage, existing models often fail to accurately recapitulate the complex 3D architecture and cellular heterogeneity of the liver. Although animal models are valuable, they are not always reliable predictors of human liver function and disease. More advanced models, such as human-derived organoids or liver-on-chip platforms, are needed to improve the ability to study liver damage in real-time and screen for potential therapeutic agents.

Bionic microenvironment construction strategy for liver regeneration

A key strategy for improving liver regeneration and restoring liver function involves the construction of a bionic microenvironment that mimics the natural liver tissue. This microenvironment aims to provide the necessary biochemical, mechanical, and cellular cues to sustain hepatic cell viability and maintain physiological functionality. The liver’s native ECM provides structural support and biochemical signals necessary for cellular differentiation, migration, and function. However, recreating this microenvironment in vitro remains a challenge. To develop a functional bionic microenvironment for liver regeneration, researchers are focusing on the incorporation of bioengineered scaffolds, hydrogels, and decellularized liver matrices that can provide structural and biochemical support for liver cells. The integration of bioreactors that mimic the flow dynamics of blood and bile in the liver, coupled with controlled delivery of growth factors and oxygen, is another promising avenue for improving liver regeneration. Nevertheless, creating a truly functional bionic environment that allows for long-term survival and proper tissue organization remains a significant hurdle.

Furthermore, precise tuning of scaffold biomechanical characteristics is crucial for mimicking the liver’s unique biomechanical environment. This includes adjusting the stiffness and elasticity of the materials used to create a supportive substrate for liver cells. Current research is focused on developing smart materials that respond to physiological conditions, allowing for dynamic adjustment of the mechanical properties of the bionic microenvironment. These materials may help overcome some of the limitations of static scaffolds that fail to adequately replicate the liver’s natural biomechanical behavior.

Challenges in developing in vitro dynamic culture systems for maintaining liver-specific functions

Maintaining liver-specific functions in vitro is essential for the successful application of engineered liver systems in therapeutic settings. Traditional 2D cultures do not accurately recapitulate the complex architecture and functional properties of the liver, which are necessary for maintaining hepatocyte function, drug metabolism, and protein synthesis. Thus, the development of 3D culture systems has become a critical area of focus. Dynamic culture systems that simulate the in vivo conditions of blood and bile flow are essential for maintaining liver-specific functions. A major challenge in creating these systems is the design of bioreactors that can provide controlled perfusion of culture media to ensure the continuous supply of nutrients and oxygen, while also removing metabolic waste products. Additionally, the ability to simulate the mechanical forces exerted by blood flow in vivo, such as shear stress, is critical for maintaining the integrity of liver cells and tissues. Advancements in microfluidic technology have led to the development of liver-on-chip models that can simulate the dynamic interactions between liver cells, endothelial cells, and other key cell types in a more physiologically relevant manner. These systems hold great potential for drug screening, toxicity testing, and disease modeling. However, challenges remain in scaling these systems for high-throughput screening, as well as in integrating the complex interactions between liver cells and other tissues such as the immune system and vasculature.

Promoting large-scale production and clinical translation

In the realm of HCC diagnosis and treatment, the clinical application of liver biomimetic microenvironments has witnessed substantial advancements. A pivotal step toward HCC cure involves elucidating the correlation between the tumor microenvironment and the intestinal microbiota. An ongoing trial has developed HCC organoids and investigated their integration with host intestinal microbiota and peripheral blood mononuclear cells, assessing tumor cell viability by probing the tumor microenvironment and in vitro treatments (NCT06355700). Similarly, another clinical trial is evaluating the genomic and transcriptomic alterations resulting from the integration of HCC organoids with patient-derived intestinal microbiota and host immune cells (NCT06929845). It examines the efficacy of systemic therapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) using organoids and identifies potential modifier factors. Additionally, patient-derived organoids are used in co-culture systems with tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and patient-derived organoid-type tissue spheres to simulate the tumor microenvironment of cancer patients. The relevant clinical trial (NCT05913141) has demonstrated that this culture system can be utilized for preclinical validation and drug screening, capable of treating sensitive populations and providing personalized treatment for HCC patients. These studies underscore the potential of biomimetic microenvironments in exploring HCC-related mechanisms and personalized diagnosis and treatment, offering novel strategies for HCC discovery and prognosis.

For liver regeneration therapies to achieve widespread clinical application, scalable production methods for both liver cells and engineered liver tissues are imperative. Current techniques for generating large quantities of hepatocytes or liver tissue constructs are labor-intensive and not yet commercially viable. Optimizing cell culture protocols, scaling up bioreactor systems, and enhancing cell expansion techniques are crucial for translating liver regeneration technologies into clinical practice. Moreover, the clinical translation of these therapies necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the regulatory pathways involved in cell-based therapies and tissue engineering. Rigorous testing, standardization, and quality control are essential to ensure the safety, efficacy, and reproducibility of these treatments. The development of innovative bioreactor designs, improved biomaterials, and advanced culture systems capable of supporting large-scale production of functional liver tissues will be vital to meet clinical demands.

Conclusion

Engineered liver models, such as 3D biopharmaceutical tissues, dECM scaffolds, and MPS, provide a platform for closely mimicking the natural structure and function of the liver, thereby improving drug screening, disease modeling, and personalized medicine. These systems overcome the limitations of traditional 2D cultures and animal models by replicating the spatial, chemical and mechanical cues of the liver, thereby enhancing the survival, polarization and metabolic activity of hepatocytes. Clinically, BAL systems, including hollow fibers and fluidized bed bioreactors, have shown hope of bridging transplantation or restoring patients by temporarily restoring detoxification and synthetic functions. Despite significant progress, there are still challenges in scalability, microenvironment optimization and clinical translation. The future opportunities lie in improving in vitro models, strengthening the design of bioreactors, and integrating patient-specific cells for personalized treatment. However, the key challenges include achieving long-term functional maintenance of the engineered liver tissue, ensuring vascularization of nutrient and oxygen supply, and addressing the complexity of immune responses in the transplanted structure. Furthermore, the large-scale production and standardization of these technologies are crucial for clinical applications. The integration of tissue engineering, stem cell biology and advanced biomaterials offers transformative potential for liver regeneration, but interdisciplinary collaboration and rigorous clinical validation are needed to overcome current limitations. In conclusion, although challenges still exist, the integration of bionic strategies, regenerative medicine and dynamic culture systems holds transformative potential in the treatment of liver diseases. By addressing scalability, functionality and clinical feasibility, these advancements could revolutionize liver treatment and bring hope to millions of patients worldwide.
