Large Right Atrial Myxoma With Endocrine Abnormalities as a Possible Case of a Syndromic Disease: A Case Report
Authors: Rozita Jalalian, Hannaneh Yousefi‐Koma, Soheil Azizi, Babak Bagheri, Rahman Ghafari, Yas Yousefi‐Koma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe late onset of significant symptoms in this case provides valuable insight for future research on multiple neoplasia and endocrine syndromes, emphasizing the challenges of timely recognition. As syndromic features may regress over time, careful attention to detailed clinical history and subtle past manifestations remains essential for diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Primary cardiac tumors (PCTs) are rare, with myxomas accounting for 75%–80% of cases at an incidence of 0.5–1 per million persons per year. Although myxomas may arise in any cardiac chamber, the left atrium, particularly the fossa ovalis region, is the most common site [1, 2, 3]. Despite their histopathological benignity, myxomas can be life‐threatening due to obstructive and thromboembolic complications. Primary cardiac myxoma may be idiopathic, familial, or part of a genetic syndrome. Carney complex (CNC) is a rare autosomal dominant multiple neoplasia syndrome characterized by endocrine tumors, mucocutaneous lentigines, and myxomas, including cardiac myxomas [4].

Hyperparathyroidism (HPT) and hypothyroidism (HT) are prevalent endocrine disorders. HT is the second most frequent endocrine abnormality, while HPT ranks third, with a prevalence of around 7 per 1000 individuals. Both occur more often in women, with risk increasing with age. Women have a higher prevalence of HPT (up to 4 times) and HT (5–10 times) than men, which increases with age [5]. Here, we present a rare case of Carney complex diagnosed in midlife, in which fading cutaneous features masked the underlying syndrome. This case highlights the importance of revisiting clinical history and subtle physical findings when multiple benign tumors coexist, as early recognition of CNC has direct implications for surveillance, genetic counseling, and long‐term management.

Case Presentation

A 49‐year‐old woman presented with progressive dyspnea and generalized edema for 1 month. She denied hemoptysis or prior significant illness. On examination, jugular venous pressure was elevated, edema was present, and a small thyroid nodule was palpable. ECG was unremarkable. It was revealed that our patient has a history of cutaneous lesions similar to lentiginous lesions on her forehead, cheeks, and, to some extent, her genitals. Her facial lesions have completely faded away, but her genital lesions have been persistent. She complained that she first noticed her facial lesions around her teenage years and assumed them to be a part of the natural course of her puberty. Genital lesions appeared a few years later but did not raise any suspicion.

Investigations and Treatment

Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography revealed a large encapsulated, mobile hyperechogenic mass with irregular borders at the superior vena cava‐right atrium (SVC‐RA) junction (Figure 1D). The mass partially obstructed the flow and caused moderate to severe tricuspid regurgitation (Figure 1A). It also extended toward the inferior vena cava (Figure 1B,C; Videos S1–, S4).

Given the patient's symptoms and tumor size, surgical excision was performed in line with international guidelines [5]. Histopathology confirmed an atrial myxoma, measuring 4.5 × 4 × 3.5 cm and weighing 53 g (Figure 2). At a postoperative follow‐up, the patient complained of fatigue. Examination revealed enlargement of the left thyroid lobe. Laboratory tests showed hypercalcemia (Ca: 11 mg/dL), elevated iPTH (89.4 pg/mL), and elevated TSH (5.5 mIU/L), consistent with hyperparathyroidism and hypothyroidism. Although the laboratory diagnosis of hypercalcemia was approved, there were no electrocardiographic changes. Neck ultrasonography identified a hypovascular hypoechoic lesion inferior to the right thyroid lobe, suggestive of parathyroid adenoma, and a 7‐mm solid‐cystic nodule in the left thyroid lobe. A Tc‐99m sestamibi single‐photon emission computerized tomography (SPECT) scan confirmed focal uptake consistent with parathyroid adenoma (Figure 3). The patient underwent parathyroidectomy due to neuromuscular and neuropsychiatric symptoms and signs. The patient underwent parathyroidectomy for symptomatic hyperparathyroidism; histopathology confirmed adenoma. Levothyroxine (0.5 mg/day) was initiated for hypothyroidism.

Differential Diagnosis

The coexistence of multiple benign tumors, cardiac myxoma, parathyroid adenoma, and thyroid nodule prompted evaluation for a syndromic condition. Differential diagnoses included CNC, multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN1, MEN2, MEN4), and related disorders [6, 7]. Detailed history and examination pointed strongly toward CNC [8].

Results and Follow‐Up

The patient was diagnosed with probable CNC. She was monitored closely during the first postoperative year and transitioned to annual follow‐ups. At 3 years, she remained asymptomatic with no evidence of recurrence on echocardiography.

Discussion

This case illustrates the coexistence of cardiac myxoma with hyperparathyroidism and hypothyroidism in a middle‐aged woman, consistent with CNC. Sporadic myxomas usually occur in the left atrium, but syndromic cases can involve any chamber. The unusual right atrial‐SVC location raised suspicion for an underlying syndrome [8]. Although the excised mass was proven to be a benign myxoma, both the location and attachment site of the tumor (located in the RA–SVC junction and attachment to the atrial wall) were in favor of either a malignant tumor or a sign of a syndromic disease [9]. HPT and HT are common endocrine disorders and typically occur sporadically. However, their coexistence with cardiac myxoma should raise suspicion of CNC or related syndromes. Recognition is important, as some genetic mutations associated with these syndromes carry carcinogenic potential and may influence surgical and management decisions. Therefore, in suspicion of familial or genetic syndromes, genetic testing is done to determine the necessity of surgery [6, 10].

Regarding our patient's symptoms, we assumed the most related syndrome to be CNC. Its most common manifestations are myxoma, endocrine overactivity, and spotty pigmentations (lentigines) [7]. Therefore, a summary of the molecular genetics of this known syndrome could be helpful for a better evidence‐based investigation of this patient. In about 70% of CNC patients with genetic tests, different mutations of the PRKAR1A were noted, which result in the inactivation of the gene [4]. Approximately 70% of CNC patients harbor mutations in the PRKAR1A gene, a tumor suppressor encoding the type 1α regulatory subunit of protein kinase A. More than 120 mutations have been identified, though genotype–phenotype correlations remain unclear [7].

Although genetic testing is recommended in such cases [1, 3, 4], it was unavailable in our center. Careful history‐taking revealed previously overlooked lentiginous lesions, supporting CNC as the most probable diagnosis despite late presentation. However, we reevaluated our patient's history by asking more focused questions about the differential diagnoses. The history of cutaneous lesions that have faded away over time helped us to come up with the diagnosis of CNC as the most probable cause, even with the unusual age of diagnosis.

Yearly follow‐up visits are scheduled in case of further severe disease occurrences. Now, after 4 years, the patient remains stable with no recurrence.

Limitations

Our main limitation was the lack of access to genetic testing. Additionally, some clinical features had regressed with age, complicating the diagnosis.

Conclusion

CNC is a rare genetic syndrome typically diagnosed in youth but, as in this case, may present later in life. Coexistence of seemingly benign conditions, cardiac myxoma, thyroid nodules, and parathyroid adenoma, should prompt thorough evaluation for underlying syndromes, especially when subtle historical features (e.g., lentiginous lesions) are present. Reporting such atypical presentations may aid clinicians in recognizing and managing rare disorders.
