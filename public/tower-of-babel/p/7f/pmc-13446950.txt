Adrenal Pseudocyst Mimicking Cystic Neuroblastoma in a 6-month-old Infant
Authors: Aiswarya Raveendran Pillai, Liya Joseph, Jolhf Koshy Mathai, C. S. Aravind, G. M. Asok Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdrenal pseudocysts are rare in children and may mimic cystic adrenal tumors. We report a 6-month-old female with a left suprarenal cyst initially suspected as neuroblastoma. Surgical excision and histopathology confirmed an adrenal pseudocyst, highlighting the need to consider this entity in infants with cystic suprarenal lesions.

INTRODUCTION

Adrenal cysts are rare, with an incidence ranging from 0.06% to 0.18% in autopsy studies. They are classified into endothelial, pseudocyst, epithelial, and parasitic types.[1] Among these, adrenal pseudocysts constitute a nonneoplastic variant characterized by a fibrous capsule devoid of epithelial or endothelial lining.[2] While most cases are described in adults, adrenal pseudocysts are exceedingly uncommon in the pediatric age group. Owing to their rarity and nonspecific clinical presentation, they often mimic cystic neoplasms such as cystic neuroblastoma, adrenal cortical carcinoma, or renal cysts. This report describes a rare case of adrenal pseudocyst in an infant and discusses its clinical, radiological, and histopathological features, along with a brief review of the literature.

CASE REPORT

A 6-month-old female child, born of a nonconsanguineous marriage with an uneventful antenatal and perinatal history, was brought to the outpatient department with complaints of swelling over the left side of the abdomen. The mother had noticed the swelling incidentally the day prior while bathing the baby. There was a history of occasional nonbilious vomiting, but no loss of weight, loss of appetite, or jaundice.

On examination, the child was active and well-nourished. Abdominal examination revealed a firm, well-defined, nontender mass in the left hypochondrium and lumbar regions, extending toward the umbilicus. The mass was bimanually palpable, smooth, and had restricted mobility. There was no hepatosplenomegaly or lymphadenopathy.

A provisional clinical diagnosis of renal mass was considered. Ultrasonography of the abdomen showed a well-defined cystic lesion in the left suprarenal region; there was no dilation of pelvicalyceal system and no intrarenal cysts. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen revealed a 9 cm × 8 cm × 8 cm unilocular cystic lesion with thin walls and internal septations, displacing the left kidney posteroinferiorly and the pancreas medially. There were no calcifications or solid-enhancing components. A radiological impression of cystic neuroblastoma was made [Figure 1a]. Biochemical evaluation, including catecholamine analysis and relevant serum tumor markers, was performed in this patient and yielded normal results.

(a) Coronal contrast-enhanced computed tomography image of the abdomen showing a large, well-defined cystic lesion arising from the left adrenal region, displacing the left kidney inferiorly and medially. The lesion has a thin enhancing wall and internal fluid attenuation, initially suggestive of a cystic neuroblastoma. (b) Gross specimen of the excised adrenal cyst showing a well-encapsulated, spherical lesion with a smooth outer surface and areas of hemorrhage

The child underwent exploratory laparotomy through a left subcostal incision. Intraoperatively, a tense cystic lesion of size 10 cm × 8 cm × 8 cm arising from the left adrenal gland was identified, displacing the left kidney inferiorly. The adrenal gland was not separately visualized. The cyst was completely excised along with the adjacent splayed tissue [Figure 1b]. The postoperative recovery was uneventful.

Histopathological examination revealed a fibrous-walled cyst lacking epithelial or endothelial lining, with areas of old hemorrhage, necrotic debris, and hemosiderin-laden macrophages, confirming the diagnosis of an adrenal pseudocyst. The diagnostic challenges in such cases include preoperative diagnostic uncertainty, the need for complete excision while preserving adjacent structures, and the absence of standardized postoperative surveillance guidelines.

DISCUSSION

Adrenal pseudocysts are nonneoplastic cystic lesions of the adrenal gland, usually developing from organized adrenal hemorrhage that becomes encapsulated over time.[1] Clinically, they are often asymptomatic but may present with abdominal pain, distension, or a palpable mass when large, while endocrine function typically remains normal.[3]

Imaging features of adrenal pseudocysts are variable, often showing a well-defined cystic lesion with internal echoes or hemorrhagic components on ultrasound, higher-attenuation fluid or wall calcification on CT, and signal changes reflecting blood products on magnetic resonance imaging, making distinction from cystic adrenal tumors difficult.[4] In our case, the imaging features suggested cystic neuroblastoma, underscoring the diagnostic challenge. Pathologically, it consists of a fibrous, nonepithelial wall containing organized hemorrhage, hemosiderin-laden macrophages, cholesterol clefts, and occasional calcification, reflecting their origin from encapsulated adrenal hemorrhage.[3]

In the pediatric population, there are no established guidelines for the management of incidentally detected nonfunctional adrenal masses. Other differential diagnoses for a cystic suprarenal mass in infancy include adrenal cysts, adrenal hemorrhage, retroperitoneal lymphatic malformations, and, rarely, extrapulmonary–bronchopulmonary sequestration and retroperitoneal enteric duplication cysts. Distinction among these entities is facilitated by characteristic imaging features, such as the presence of a feeding vessel suggestive of extralobar pulmonary sequestration, signal characteristics consistent with hemorrhage in adrenal hemorrhage, thin-walled anechoic lesions in adrenal cysts, and a multiloculated cystic appearance in lymphatic malformations. In most cases, imaging enables a confident diagnosis and guides management. Given the higher incidence of functional and malignant adrenal tumors in children, most surgeons recommend surgical excision even for clinically asymptomatic lesions that are radiologically suspicious or remain indeterminate despite imaging evaluation. Complete surgical excision is indicated for all adrenal cystic lesions that are large, symptomatic, or cannot be confidently diagnosed as benign preoperatively.[5]

Surgical excision provides both definitive diagnosis and cure. The prognosis after complete excision is excellent, and recurrence is extremely rare.

CONCLUSION

Adrenal pseudocysts are exceedingly rare in the pediatric population, and their nonspecific imaging features frequently mimic malignant suprarenal tumors, posing significant diagnostic and management challenges. Reporting this case adds valuable clinical insight by highlighting the potential for radiological misdiagnosis, reinforcing the importance of considering pseudocyst in the differential diagnosis, and emphasizing the role of surgical excision and histopathology in achieving definitive diagnosis. This case contributes to the limited pediatric literature and may aid clinicians in better recognizing and managing similar presentations.
