Translation and psychometric validation of the Escala de Impactos da Mucosite Oral em Pacientes Oncológicos (EIMOPO) in Chinese patients with cancer
Authors: Xuebing Jing, Yuanyuan Li, Lingyan Sun, Yuanhua Liu, Yuping Cheng, Lin Wang, Minghui Zhou, Shanshan Zhang, Hanfei Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to translate the Escala de Impactos da Mucosite Oral em Pacientes Oncológicos (EIMOPO) into Chinese and to assess the psychometric properties of the cross-culturally adapted version, thereby ensuring its reliability and validity.MethodsA total of 473 patients with cancer who developed oral mucositis following radiotherapy/chemotherapy were recruited from two Grade A tertiary hospitals in Shandong Province between December 2024 and April 2025 using convenience sampling, with the sample size determined based on Kendall's estimation method. The original EIMOPO scale was translated and culturally adapted following Brislin' s guidelines. Validity was evaluated through item analysis and structural validity, while reliability was assessed using internal consistency test, test-retest reliability analysis and the split-half reliability. Data analyses were performed with SPSS and AMOS.ResultsThe Chinese version of the 21-item scale exhibited strong psychometric properties. Exploratory factor analysis identified a four-factor structure, accounting for 66.883% of the total variance. Confirmatory factor analysis further supported the model, demonstrating excellent fit: χ2/df ​= ​1.298, RMSEA ​= ​0.035, SRMR ​= ​0.042, GFI ​= ​0.914, CFI ​= ​0.982, IFI ​= ​0.982, and TLI ​= ​0.979. Reliability measures were also robust, with an overall Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.923, the test-retest reliability ranged from 0.783 to 0.916, and the split-half reliability coefficient was 0.815.ConclusionsThe Chinese version of the EIMOPO exhibits robust psychometric properties, establishing it as a reliable and appropriate instrument for assessing quality of life related with radiotherapy/chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis in patients with cancer within the Chinese cultural context.Keywords: Radiotherapy/chemotherapy, Oral mucositis, Quality of life, Cross-cultural adaptation

Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2022 report released by the World Health Organization (WHO) International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) team in 2024,1 there were nearly 20 million new cancer cases and approximately 9.7 million cancer-related deaths globally in 2022, with the global cancer burden expected to continue increasing over the next 30 years. Cancer epidemiology in China presents a particularly grave situation, with 4.82 million new cases and 2.57 million deaths reported in 2022, where both incidence and mortality rates are significantly higher than the global average.2 Notably, with advancements in cancer diagnosis and treatment technologies, the standardized 5-year relative survival rate among Chinese patients with cancer has reached 43.7%.3 However, alongside extended survival, patients commonly face symptom clusters induced by anti-cancer treatments, among which fatigue, pain, and Radiotherapy/Chemotherapy induced oral mucositis (RIOM/CIOM) severely impact patients' quality of life (QoL).4, 5, 6, 7

Oral Mucositis (OM) is a frequent complication of radiotherapy and chemotherapy in cancer treatment. Studies indicate that the incidence of OM in Chinese patients undergoing radiotherapy/chemotherapy ranges from 15% to 100%, being highest in head and neck patients with cancer and lowest in those receiving chemotherapy alone.4,5 OM not only causes severe oral pain, dysphagia, bleeding, and malnutrition but also increases the risk of local or systemic infections, significantly affecting their QoL.8 For patients with RIOM/CIOM, QoL involves life and spiritual domains affected by their disease and treatment, which are risk factors for future adverse stress events.9 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop measurement tools that can accurately assess these subjective experiences. The European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) is a generic QoL assessment tool for patients with cancer. While it reflects the overall QoL of patients with cancer, it lacks specific dimensions for evaluating OM-specific symptoms.10, 11, 12 The Chinese version of the Oral Mucositis Daily Questionnaire (OMDQ), originally developed by Elting et al.,13 is applicable in Appendix A; however, it places greater emphasis on symptom severity and offers limited assessment of social functioning and psychological impact.14 At present, there is no validated and reliable instrument in China specifically designed to evaluate QoL in patients with RIOM/CIOM This underscores an urgent need to develop or introduce targeted tools for comprehensive assessment in this patient population.

The EIMOPO was developed in 2023 by Professor Pereira's team at the University of São Paulo, Brazil,15 with the primary objective of evaluating QoL in patients with RIOM/CIOM. The original scale consists of 24 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, scores for each item ranging from 0 ("Never") to 4 ("Always"). The total score is calculated by summing all item responses, yielding a possible range from 0 to 96. The higher scores indicating lower QoL levels in patients with RIOM/CIOM. And the EIMOPO is a specific assessment tool developed based on the Patient-Reported Outcome (PRO) theoretical framework. It systematically evaluates OM-related symptoms-such as pain, dysphagia-and their impact on patients' daily functions and emotional states. It captures QoL related to treatment-induced OM across physiological, psychological, and social functional domains.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to translate the EIMOPO into Chinese and assess its validity and acceptability as an instrument for assessing the impact of QoL levels in patients with RIOM/CIOM in China.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study consisted of two phases: (1) translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the EIMOPO into Chinese to better reflect the QoL levels of Chinese oncology patients with RIOM/CIOM; and (2) evaluation of the reliability and validity of the adapted EIMOPO among patients with cancer with RIOM/CIOM in China.

Phase 1: translation and cross-cultural adaptation procedures

Our research team contacted the original authors via email to request the EIMOPO scales and its accompanying usage instructions (Note: The original scale development study has been approved by the local ethical review committee).15 Upon receiving authorization, the translation process was conducted in accordance with the Brislin model, utilizing a forward-backward translation procedure.16

Forward translation and synthesis. As the original version was in Portuguese, upon communication and authorization from the original author, the research team first translated the Portuguese original into English using professional translation software. The original author verified the conceptual meaning of the English version to facilitate the subsequent sinicization process. Two native Chinese translators with nursing degrees and proficiency in English independently translated the EIMOPO into Chinese (A1 and A2 versions). The translators had a master's degree. The two translators and research team analyzed and compared the two translated versions with the original instrument, discussed, and made modifications to obtain the forward translation (Version A).

Back-Translation. Two independent translators, who had not been involved in the initial translation and had not previously seen the original English version of the scale, were invited to perform back-translation (B1 and B2 versions). One English teacher who had not previously seen the English version of the scale (Master's in English, Associate Professor, had been a visiting scholar in the UK for one year) and one clinical oncologist (PhD, Associate Chief Physician, CET-6 passed). Team members and the two back-translators compared and modified B1 and B2 to produce a consolidated back-translated version B. Through further discussion, a pre-final Chinese version (Version C) was developed.

Expert panel discussion and cross-cultural adaptation. In accordance with established guidelines for the cross-cultural adaptation of scales,17 a panel of 10 experts from Shandong, Guangdong, and Zhejiang provinces in China was invited to participate in an expert consultation. The research team sent expert consultation forms via email or WeChat. The inclusion criteria for experts were as follows: (1) at least 10 years of working experience in oncology or hematology; (2) expertise in RIOM/CIOM; (3) holding a bachelor's degree or higher; (4) possessing an intermediate professional title or above; (5) familiarity with the development or adaptation process of psychometric tools; and (6) willingness to participate voluntarily. The expert panel comprised 1 hematologist, 2 oncologists, 1 radiation oncologist, 4 oncology specialist nurses, 1 head nurse of oncology, and 1 head nurse of hematology; years of experience ranged from 14 to 26 (22.50 ​± ​3.85) years; 4 had intermediate titles, 6 had associate senior titles or higher; 6 held master's degrees, 4 held bachelor's degrees. Based on their professional knowledge and clinical experience, the experts evaluated the scale items in terms of readability, conceptual clarity, linguistic appropriateness, item relevance, and cultural suitability.

Pilot study. A pilot study involving 20 eligible patients was conducted to evaluate the fluency and comprehensibility of the scale items. Face-to-face surveys were administered, during which the purpose, significance, and method of completing the questionnaire were explained to the patients. All questionnaires were collected on-site, and feedback regarding item comprehension and ease of completion was solicited. The pre-test demonstrated that the questionnaire could be completed within 5 minutes. No further revisions were required following the pilot study. The inclusion and exclusion criteria of 20 patients were the same as above, consisting of Lung Tumor 2, Gastric Tumor 2, Intestinal Tumor 3, Laryngeal Cancer 1, Breast Tumor 3, Malignant Lymphoma 3, Esophageal Tumor 3, Nasopharyngeal Tumor 2, Oral Tumor 1. The age range was 22–75 years, 9 women and 11 men.

Phase 2: validation field study

Participants and sample size. Patients hospitalized with cancer in the hematology, oncology, and radiation oncology departments of two Grade A tertiary hospitals in Shandong Province were recruited via convenience sampling from the beginning of December 2024 to the end of April 2025.

The EIMOPO consists of 22 items. The sample size used for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was 5–10 times of the items.18 To account for potential participant dropout, the initial sample size was increased by 20%. For confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), a minimum of 200 cases was required. A total of 500 questionnaires were distributed, and 473 were returned, resulting in an effective response rate of 94.60%. After data collection, the responses were randomly divided into two groups: one group of 230 for EFA and another group of 243 for CFA.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged ≥ 18 years; (2) meeting the diagnostic criteria for oral mucositis as defined by the Multinational Society for Cancer Support Therapy/International Society of Oral Oncology (MASCC/ISOO) in the Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Treatment of Mucositis in Cancer;19 (3) were suffered from RIOM/CIOM; and (4) informed of their diagnosis and willing to participate voluntarily. The exclusion criteria included: (1) cognitive or mental impairment that hindered effective communication; and (2) severe hearing loss that prevented adequate communication.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and IBM AMOS version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the sample, with continuous variables expressed as mean ​± ​standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages [n (%)].

Item analysis

Critical ratio (CR) method:20 the total score of EIMOPO was sorted from high to low, then the data were divided into 2 subgroups by their scores. And an independent samples t test was employed to analyze the high subgroups in the top 27% compared to the low subgroups in the bottom 27%. The critical ratio (CR) was calculated for each item, and items with a CR value less than 3 were considered for deletion; (2) Correlative coefficient method: the correlation coefficient was used to indicate the correlation between each item and the total questionnaire scores. A correlation coefficient greater than 0.3 was considered indicative of adequate item–total correlation.

Content validity

The content validity index (CVI) was applied to evaluate the content relevance, including the scale level (S-CVI) and the item level (I-CVI). An expert panel rated the relevance, clarity, and cultural adaptability of each item and the overall questionnaire on a four-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated "strongly disagree", 2 indicated "must be modified or disagree", 3 indicated "agree after minor modification", and 4 indicated "strongly agree". I-CVI values were above > 0.780 and S-CVI values were above > 0.900.21

Construct validity

EFA was performed by principal components analysis with varimax rotation to obtain the factor structure. Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin's (KMO) measure and Bartlett's sphericity were verified before the EFA; a KMO value ​> ​0.800 and significant Bartlett's test P ​< ​0.05 were considered qualified. The factor loadings of each item were > 0.400.21

CFA verified the internal structure of the scale and whether the structural model fit the actual data. The parameters of the model were estimated using the maximum likelihood estimation method. A model with good fitness should meet the following criteria: χ2/df ​< ​3; comparative fit index, incremental fit index, and Tucker–Lewis index > 0.900; standardized root mean square residual and root-mean-square error of approximation < 0.08. The goodness of fit index (GFI) values should be greater than 0.900, which are relatively good.21

Reliability

(1)Internal consistency reliability: Reliability was evaluated by internal consistency reliability and test-retest reliability. The internal consistency of the EIMOPO was tested by Cronbach's α coefficient. Internal consistency reliability: The Cronbach's α coefficient is used for evaluation. A Cronbach's α coefficient ≥ 0.700 indicates good reliability, while ≥ 0.60 suggests acceptable reliability.22 Forty randomly selected re-surveys were conducted in the total sample after 2 weeks, and the correlation between the two surveys was assessed to determine the re-test reliability of the questionnaire > 0.700 indicating good stability of the scale across time.22

The internal consistency of the EIMOPO was tested by Cronbach's α coefficient. Internal consistency reliability: The Cronbach's α coefficient is used for evaluation. (2)Half-reliability: The Chinese version of EIMOPO items are divided into two halves based on their sequence numbers using the odd–even grouping method. The correlation coefficient between the two halves' scores is calculated. A half-reliability closer to 1 indicates stronger internal consistency. Items with half-reliability < 0.700 should be considered for deletion.22

Results

Phase 1: translation and cross-cultural adaptation

According to the results of expert review and pre-survey interview results of the target population, item 16 (Are you irritated/impatient because of oral mucositis?) and item 17 (Are you sad/depressed because of oral mucositis?) were merged to form item 16 (Has oral mucositis affected your emotions?), and item 22 (Have you ever had or worried about cancer treatment affecting your teeth?) and item 23 (Have you ever had or worried about cancer treatment affecting your tongue, lips, and/or gums) were merged to form item 21 (Have you ever had or worried about cancer treatment affecting your teeth, tongue, lips, and/or gums?). The preliminary survey version D of Chinese was formed (including 22 items).

Item analysis

A total of 230 questionnaires were submitted for item analysis. The results showed the CR value of each item was > 3 (P ​< ​0.05), indicating a favorable differentiation. The Pearson correlation analysis showed that the correlation coefficient between the Chinese version of EIMOPO item score and total score was 0.439–0.747 (P ​< ​0.05), so all items were retained.

Validity

Content validity: 10 experts with more than 10 years' experience and held senior titles of deputy or above were invited to assess the I-CVI and S-CVI. As a result of a Delphi expert consultation, the expert response rate was 100%, I-CVI ranged from 0.846 to 1.000, and S-CVI/Ave was 0.969.

Construct validity: EFA results showed that the KMO value of the EIMOPO was 0.921 and Bartlett's test was χ2 ​= ​2778.383, P ​< ​0.001, confirming suitability for factor analysis. After removing item 18 with a load value ​< ​0.450, a second exploration was performed. The revised scale demonstrated a KMO value of 0.925 and Bartlett's test results (χ2 ​= ​2748.927, P ​< ​0.001), with all 21 items showing factor loadings between 0.638 and 0.848 (> 0.400). Orthogonal rotation was performed using both principal component analysis method and variance maximum method. It can be seen from the Scree Plot (Fig. 1) that the slope tends to be flat after the fourth factor, so four factors are extracted for principal component analysis, and the cumulative variance contribution rate of these four factors reaches 66.883%. The Chinese version of EIMOPO included 4 dimensions (21 items). According to the specific content of each item, the four factors were defined as oral symptoms (Q4,Q7, Q8,Q11, Q12,Q13,Q14,Q15),oral function ((physiological impact) (Q1,Q2, Q3,Q9,Q10)), general QoL ((psychological impact) (Q5,Q6,Q17,Q19,Q21))and general QoL ((other impact) (Q16,Q18,Q20)) in order (Table 2). CFA results were good: χ2/df ​= ​1.298, RMSEA ​= ​0.035 (90% CI ​= ​0.021, 0.047), SRMR ​= ​0.042, GFI ​= ​0.914, CFI ​= ​0.982, IFI ​= ​0.982, and TLI ​= ​0.979, which indicated that the four-factor model was fit (Fig. 2). The factor loading values range between 0.709 and 0.886, with all factor loadings exceeding 0.600. The composite reliability (CR) values for the factors are 0.897, 0.932, 0.791, and 0.871, all of which are greater than 0.700. The average variance extracted (AVE) values are 0.635, 0.632, 0.558, and 0.574, all exceeding 0.500. The √AVE values are 0.797, 0.795, 0.747, and 0.758, and the square root of the AVE for each factor is greater than its correlation with other factors. Therefore, both convergent and discriminant validity are met.

Reliability

The Cronbach's ɑ coefficients of the scale were 0.923 (total scale) and 0.781–0.932 (subscale). A total of 40 patients completed and returned the retest survey. Pearson's correlation coefficient ranged from 0.783 to 0.916, showing an acceptable test-retest reliability. The split-half reliability were 0.815 (total scale) and 0.728–0.926 (subscale).

Discussion

The practicality of the scale

OM is a common and clinically significant toxic reaction following radiotherapy/chemotherapy,23 often clinically manifested as thinning of the oral mucosa, breakdown, dryness, erythema, erosion, ulceration, necrosis, bleeding, etc.,24 leading to secondary dry mouth, pain, difficulty opening the mouth, and in severe cases, affecting eating and causing malnutrition. This increases patients' psychological and economic burdens, potentially causing treatment delays or interruptions, and constitutes a risk factor for future adverse stress events,9 severely impacting treatment efficacy and QoL. Studies show that severe OM can increase the risk of treatment interruption by 3–5 times and significantly prolong hospitalization,17 becoming a major negative factor affecting cancer treatment outcomes. Currently, the assessment of QoL in patients with RIOM/CIOM still relies predominantly on generic instruments such as the EORTC QLQ-C30. These tools lack specific evaluation of the unique impacts of RIOM/CIOM-related symptoms—such as dysphagia and taste alterations—on patients' social functioning. This assessment gap may result in delayed or imprecise clinical interventions.

The Chinese version of the EIMOPO scale consists of 21 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with scores for each item ranging from 0 ("Never") to 4 ("Always"). The total score is calculated by summing all item responses, yielding a possible range from 0 to 84, with higher scores indicating lower QoL levels in patients with RIOM/CIOM. The translated scale introduced in this study enables targeted evaluation of typical symptoms associated with treatment-induced OM—including pain, dry mouth, and taste changes—and provides a comprehensive assessment of the disease's effect on QoL across physiological, psychological, and social functional domains. Moreover, the scale demonstrates strong clinical applicability, with an average completion time of under five minutes, making it highly suitable for routine administration in busy clinical environments. It enables rapid yet accurate assessment of the impact of oral mucositis on patients' dietary intake, social interactions, and psychological well-being, thereby supporting the formulation of individualized intervention strategies. The promotion and adoption of this instrument are expected to advance evidence-based practice in supportive oncology care globally, offering clinicians a reliable means for standardized assessment and dynamic monitoring of QoL in patients with OM.

During the scale development process, we strictly adhered to the Brislin translation model to ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence with the original instrument. This was achieved through a rigorous procedure involving forward translation, back-translation, expert consultation, and pre-testing. Such methodological stringency provides a foundation for data comparability in future international multi-center studies and supports the advancement of global evidence-based practice in supportive cancer care. It should be emphasized that the use of this scale is explicitly limited to patients with cancer who have developed OM as a result of radiotherapy or chemotherapy. In practical applications, researchers must clearly define the assessment time frame according to the study design—for example, specifying whether it refers to the period since the initiation of radiotherapy/chemotherapy, the last treatment cycle, the past week, or since beginning treatment at the institution.

The scientificity of the scale

Validity refers to the extent to which a research instrument reflects the concept it intends to measure.25 In this study, content validity was evaluated by a panel of 10 experts with extensive clinical experience and solid professional expertise, selected based on strict criteria including years of experience, educational background, and professional title to ensure authoritative input. The I-CVI ranged from 0.846 to 1.000, and the S-CVI was 0.969, indicating excellent content validity of the Chinese version EIMOPO. EFA extracted 4 factors, explaining 66.883% of the cumulative variance. Factor loadings for each item ranged from 0.638 to 0.848. The extracted 4 factors retained the main content of the original scale, and the results were largely consistent with the original scale's structure, indicating good structural stability.

Reliability refers to the consistency and stability of an instrument in measuring the target construct, encompassing internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and dependability. Internal consistency reliability, which indicates the degree of inter-relatedness among items within the scale,26 was evaluated for the Chinese version of the EIMOPO. The overall Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.923, while the values for the individual dimensions ranged from 0.781 to 0.932. The test-retest reliability of each subscale ranged from 0.783 to 0.916, which was good. The test-retest survey was completed by 40 patients with an interval of 14 days, indicating good temporal stability of the questionnaire. Split-half reliability coefficients ranged from 0.728 to 0.926. These results demonstrate good reliability and stability of the Chinese version of the EIMOPO.

Feasibility in clinical practice

Pre-test results showed that the items of the Chinese version EIMOPO were clearly and understandably phrased. For instance, "Did you leave or stop socializing or attending appointments due to oral mucositis?" was adapted to "Did oral mucositis affect your social interactions?"; "Have you experienced or had financial difficulties due to receiving cancer treatment?" was simplified to "Have you faced financial difficulties due to cancer treatment?"; "Did you prefer eating smoothies or liquid foods?" was refined to "Did you prefer eating ice-cold, cool, or liquid foods?". These modifications enhanced the scale's alignment with Chinese linguistic habits and patients' comprehension levels. Feedback from clinical nursing staff during the pre-test phase highlighted that, compared to previous instruments, this version incorporates additional dimensions such as economic burden and psychosocial impacts, enabling a more comprehensive evaluation of QoL in patients with RIOM/CIOM. Its specificity offers valuable support for developing personalized care plans—including nutritional support, pain management, and psychosocial interventions.

Limitations

The EIMOPO scale utilized in this study was originally developed in Portuguese in 2023 by Professor Pereira's team. Following communication and formal authorization from the original author, the research team first translated the Portuguese version into English using professional translation software. The English translation was then reviewed and conceptually validated by the original authors. Based on this confirmed English version, the research team strictly followed cross-cultural adaptation guidelines for systematic sinicization. It should be noted that, as the research team lacked Portuguese language professionals, the English translation might have contained some semantic deviations, which could potentially affect the conceptual accuracy of the adapted Chinese version. Regarding the study participants, this research did not conduct stratified analysis of QoL based on different severity levels of oral mucositis (e.g., WHO grade III-IV vs. I-II), particularly for head and neck patients with cancer undergoing radiotherapy/chemotherapy, a high-risk group for OM. Whether the damage to QoL is specific needs to be verified by follow-up studies. A key limitation of this study is the absence of an analysis of criterion validity. While the internal structure of the scale is robust, we did not correlate scores with an established external criterion. Thus, future studies are needed to assess the scale's relationship with clinical outcomes or other validated instruments. And it didn't assess the scale's sensitivity to the dynamic changes of OM (e.g., symptom evolution fluctuating with radiotherapy/chemotherapy cycles) nor its efficacy in evaluating clinical intervention outcomes. Future studies are recommended to adopt longitudinal repeated-measures designs and further validate the generalizability and clinical applicability of the scale results through multi-center collaboration.

Conclusions

This study translated the EIMOPO into Chinese and evaluated its good reliability and validity among 473 Chinese patients with cancer. The study confirmed the EIMOPO suitability for assessing the QoL of RIOM/CIOM patients. The contents and items of the EIMOPO were easily understandable and convenient to complete. However, future studies could employ longitudinal or randomized controlled designs to further validate the EIMOPO's sensitivity to clinical changes in RIOM/CIOM symptoms. Such research could contribute to optimizing patient management strategies and establishing more comprehensive QoL assessment standards for oropharyngeal patients with cancer undergoing different treatment modalities.
