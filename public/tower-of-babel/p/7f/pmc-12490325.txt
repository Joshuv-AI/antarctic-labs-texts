The effect of caffeine use in the relationship between gaming and sleep in adolescents: A mediation analysis
Authors: Jennifer J Park, Xuewei Han, Marc N Potenza, Yihong Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsCaffeine is the most commonly used substance during gaming sessions. Despite health guidelines to avoid caffeine before adulthood, many adolescents use caffeine to compensate for lost sleep or prolong wakefulness to enhance gaming performance. The relationship between gaming and sleep is well-established, but the role of caffeine has been under-explored. This study investigated the potential mediating effect of caffeine use on the relationship between gaming duration/problems and sleep duration/difficulties in young adolescents.MethodsThe Years 2, 3, and 4 follow-up data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study were analyzed (N = 2,749, Mage = 11.9 at Year 2). Data from questionnaires on daily gaming duration and problematic gaming (Year 2), weekly caffeine use (Year 3), and daily sleep duration and sleep difficulties (Year 4) were examined. Analyses were conducted using an R package for causal mediation analysis.ResultsCaffeine use partially mediated the positive relationship between gaming hours/problems and sleep difficulties and the negative relationship between gaming hours/problems and sleep duration.ConclusionThe mediating effect of caffeine use was small but statistically significant in the relationship between gaming duration/problems and sleep duration/difficulties in young adolescents. Given the widespread use of caffeine among adolescents who game and the potential impact of modifying consumption during development, caffeine use could potentially be targeted to reduce sleep-related harm and other associated health issues.Keywords: addictive behaviors, compulsive behaviors, Internet addiction, gaming, caffeine use, sleep

Introduction

Caffeine is the most commonly used substance while gaming. Although no studies have exclusively examined adolescents, existing research reports that 33%–80% of adolescents and adults who game consume caffeine concurrently (Carey, Delfabbro, & King, 2022; Ip et al., 2021; Matias et al., 2023; Ream, Elliott, & Dunlap, 2011; Škařupová, Blinka, & Ťápal, 2018). In the wider gaming community, caffeine appears to be most frequently consumed for its general stimulant effects, including enhanced focus, increased enjoyment, and prolonged wakefulness (Roscoe, Pu, Castellanos, & Dalton, 2023; Škařupová et al., 2018). However, some individuals intentionally use caffeine as a performance-enhancing drug to improve gaming-specific skills, such as reaction time and hit accuracy (e.g., percentage of shots that hit the target in a shooter game) (Ribeiro & Poínhos, 2023), although the evidence around its effectiveness remains inconclusive (Lopez Frias, 2021; Thomas, Rothschild, Earnest, & Blaisdell, 2019). Caffeine use among adolescents who game is especially concerning, as this population not only represents the primary group consuming caffeinated sodas and energy drinks (Drewnowski & Rehm, 2016), but also exhibits the highest rates of problematic gaming (Kim et al., 2022). Increased caffeine intake may exacerbate negative consequences associated with problematic gaming within this population, such as sleep difficulties and fatigue (King & Delfabbro, 2018; Kristensen, Pallesen, King, Hysing, & Erevik, 2021; Männikkö, Billieux, & Kääriäinen, 2015). Among youth, increased caffeine intake has also been associated with sensation-seeking, risk-taking behaviors such as nicotine and alcohol use, and psychopathology (e.g., anxiety and depression) (Akanni, 2017; Jones & Lejuez, 2005; Kristjansson et al., 2011, 2018; Marmorstein, 2016; Richards & Smith, 2015; Temple, Ziegler, Graczyk, & Crandall, 2017).

Caffeinated products, particularly energy drinks, are heavily promoted via online games and social media platforms that are frequented by younger populations (Buchanan, Yeatman, Kelly, & Kariippanon, 2018; Edwards et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022). Current marketing strategies aim to integrate caffeine into gaming cultures via sponsorship of gaming events, esports teams, streaming sites, and figures viewed as credible within gaming communities (Edwards et al., 2022; Gallimberti et al., 2016; Roscoe et al., 2023; Visram, Cheetham, Riby, Crossley, & Lake, 2016). These youth-oriented strategies may reinforce adolescents' emerging social identities as gamers and perceptions that energy drinks enhance gaming performance, which sets a potentially concerning precedent for long-term health behaviors and the normalization of caffeine use within and beyond the context of gaming (Roscoe et al., 2023; Slater, 2015). An estimated 44%–46% of youth in Canada (Hammond & Reid, 2018; Wiggers, Asbridge, Baskerville, Reid, & Hammond, 2019) and 75% of adolescents in Taiwan (Yang et al., 2022) report exposure to advertisements for energy drinks online, yet public health education and health promotion efforts regarding safe caffeine consumption remain insufficient globally. Several studies indicate that adolescents are often unaware or misinformed about the caffeine content in beverages and health risks associated with high caffeine intake (Costa, Hayley, & Miller, 2014; McCrory et al., 2017; Roscoe et al., 2023; Thakre et al., 2015; Visram et al., 2016). Overall, the current marketing practices appear to work against the recommendations of public health agencies around the world (e.g., the American Academy of Paediatrics) to avoid caffeine consumption before adulthood due to various health risks, particularly those related to sleep disturbance (Schneider, Benjamin, & Committee on Nutrition and the Council on Sports Medicine Fitness, 2011).

Given the well-established negative relationship between gaming and sleep in developing youth, caffeine use should be considered a potential contributing factor (Kristensen et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2024). The consumption of caffeine among gaming youth and adults may serve to compensate for lost sleep, but it could also be a deliberate choice to avoid sleep and enhance gaming performance (Leonard et al., 2023; Sainz, Collado-Mateo, & Del Coso, 2020; Schwager et al., 2024). Multiple studies have found associations between caffeine use and longer gaming duration in adolescents (Bradbury, Turel, & Morrison, 2019; Larson, DeWolfe, Story, & Neumark-Sztainer, 2014; Škařupová et al., 2018). For example, Larson et al. (2014) reported that adolescent males who regularly consumed energy drinks (defined as consuming at least one energy drink per week) spent approximately 4.5 more hours gaming per week compared to boys with lower consumption. Similarly, adolescent females in this category spent around 2.5 additional hours gaming per week compared to their peers with lower consumption. Furthermore, a similar positive relationship has been demonstrated in the literature around problematic use, where problematic gaming has been linked to excessive caffeine consumption (Porter, Starcevic, Berle, & Fenech, 2010; Ream, Elliott, & Dunlap, 2013; Yang et al., 2022). Both behaviors may contribute to habits that disrupt sleep in adolescents.

To the best of our knowledge, the role of caffeine in documented relationships between gaming and sleep has not been investigated, particularly in large, longitudinal samples. This study aimed to examine the mediating effect of caffeine use on the relationship between gaming duration/problems and sleep duration/difficulties using Years 2, 3, and 4 follow-up data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study. We hypothesized that caffeine use at Year 3 would mediate the relationship between (i) problematic gaming at Year 2 and sleep difficulties at Year 4, (ii) problematic gaming at Year 2 and sleep duration at Year 4, (iii) gaming duration at Year 2 and sleep difficulties at Year 4, and (iv) gaming duration at Year 2 and sleep duration at Year 4.

Methods

Participants

This study analyzed the Years 2, 3, and 4 follow-up data from the ABCD study (5.1 release), which is an ongoing longitudinal project that aims to characterize neurobiological and psychological changes from childhood to young adulthood. The ABCD study website (https://abcdstudy.org/) and Garavan et al. (2018) have provided comprehensive details on recruitment and data collection procedures. In summary, the ABCD study recruited a baseline cohort of 11,878 children aged 9–10 years from 21 research sites across the United States. Behavioral data were/are collected annually and neuroimaging data were/are collected biennially. In the present study, participants were excluded from analysis based on the following criteria: (i) no self-reported engagement with single-player or online multiplayer video games, or (ii) missing data on problematic gaming, gaming duration, caffeine use, sleep difficulties, sleep duration, and basic demographic variables.

Measures

Gaming duration and problematic gaming (Year 2)

Average daily gaming duration was assessed using self-reported hours of gaming per day over the past 12 months, considering both weekdays and weekends. Participants who reported any hours of gaming were prompted to complete the Video Game Addiction Questionnaire (VGAQ), which is a 6-item measure of problematic gaming. Adapted from the Bergen Facebook Addiction Scale, the VGAQ is rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = never to 6 = very often) and renders a total score ranging from 6 to 36 (Andreassen, Torsheim, Brunborg, & Pallesen, 2012; Bagot et al., 2022). Higher scores reflect greater problematic-gaming severity. A total summary score was used in the present study due to the absence of a suggested cut-off point, consistent with recommendations for scales that demonstrate strong reliability (Sullivan & Artino, 2013; Warmbrod, 2014). The VGAQ has previously demonstrated good internal consistency reliability (McDonald's ω = 0.90) (Bagot et al., 2022).

Caffeine use (Year 3)

The average weekly number of caffeinated drinks consumed per week was assessed. Considering the past 6 months, participants self-reported the number of coffee, espresso drinks, energy drinks, sodas with caffeine, and teas with caffeine consumed per week. The typical serving size for each drink type was defined as follows: 8 oz for coffee, 1 shot for espresso, 8 oz for energy drinks, 12 oz for soda, and 8 oz for tea.

Sleep duration and difficulties (Year 4)

Hours of sleep and sleep difficulties were assessed using the 7-item Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep (DIMS) subscale, which is part of the 27-item Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC) (Bruni et al., 1996). The SDSC is a parent-reported questionnaire that assesses sleep disturbance in children over the past 6 months. The sleep duration question within the DIMS subscale was originally coded on a 5-point Likert scale as 1 = 9–11 h; 2 = 8–9 h; 3 = 7–8 h; 4 = 5–7 h; 5 = less than 5 h. In the present study, sleep duration was reverse-coded, such that higher values indicated longer sleep duration. Similar to methods reported by Zhao et al. (2024), the score was calculated as the sum of the remaining 6 items of DIMS to differentiate sleep difficulties and sleep duration. The level of internal consistency for the DIMS subscale has been found to be satisfactory (α = 0.80) (Ferreira et al., 2009).

Statistical analysis

Group differences between participants included in and excluded from the analysis on demographic characteristics, problematic gaming (VGAQ), and gaming duration were assessed using Linear Mixed Models (LMMs) with family as a random effect for continuous measures and Chi-square tests for categorical measures, respectively.

We conducted four mediation analyses. Caffeine use at Year 3 served as the mediator, while gaming duration and problematic gaming (VGAQ) at Year 2 were the primary exposure variables. Sleep duration and sleep difficulty (DIMS) at Year 4 were the primary outcomes. Variables were left unstandardized, as the study was interested in statistical significance and the proportion mediated. LMMs with family as a random effect were also used to assess the relationships between the exposure variables and the mediator, as well as between the mediator and the outcomes. In the mediation analysis, we adjusted for covariates including age, gender, race (categorized into four levels), family income, marital status of the parents, caffeine use at Year 1, and baseline values of the outcome measures at Year 1. The mediation analyses were implemented using the lme4 and mediation packages in R. Parameters in the mixed models were estimated via maximum likelihood estimation, and the indirect, direct, and total effects in the mediation analysis were estimated using 5,000 bootstrap simulations. All tests were two-sided, and the significance level was controlled at a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.05 to account for multiple comparisons. As a sensitivity analysis, we re-ran this procedure to examine specific types of caffeine consumed (i.e., coffee, espresso, tea, soda, or energy drinks) and meaningful combinations (i.e., coffee, espresso, and tea; coffee and tea; soda and energy drinks) as separate mediators.

Ethics

IRB approval and informed consent from a parent or guardian were obtained at participating ABCD sites. Adolescents who were capable of assent confirmed their willingness to participate in the ABCD study. The present study analyzed de-identified data and was exempted by the Yale IRB and the Yale Human Investigation Committee.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The demographic characteristics of participants at Year 2 follow-up (Table 1) show that 2,749 participants were included in analyses. Participants were 11.9 years old on average (SD = 0.65) and predominantly male (n = 1,671, 60.8%) and White (n = 1,567, 57.0%). There were statistically significant differences between the included and excluded groups across all variables except for family income. Compared to the excluded group, the included group was slightly younger, consisted of more male and White participants, and had more participants with parents who were married and had higher education levels. Furthermore, the included group had a lower average VGAQ score and higher average daily gaming duration.

Mediation effects

Figure 1 presents the results of the mediation analyses. Caffeine use significantly partially mediated the relationship between problematic gaming and sleep difficulties (indirect effect = 0.023, 95% CI [0.004, 0.049]; direct effect = 0.036, 95% CI [0.016, 0.055]; proportion mediated (PM) = 3.97%), problematic gaming and sleep duration (indirect effect = −0.006, 95% CI [−0.014, −0.001]; direct effect = −0.008, 95% CI [−0.013, −0.003]; PM = 4.69%), gaming duration and sleep difficulties (indirect effect = 0.044, 95% CI [0.012, 0.081], direct effect = 0.060, 95% CI [0.022, 0.102]; PM = 9.26%), and gaming duration and sleep duration (indirect effect = −0.012, 95% CI [−0.022, −0.003], direct effect = −0.029, 95% CI [−0.039, −0.018]; PM = 5.31%).

Sensitivity analysis

The consumption of energy drinks significantly partially mediated the relationship between gaming duration and sleep difficulties (indirect effect = 0.014, 95% CI [0.002, 0.03]; direct effect = 0.066, 95% CI [0.031, 0.104]; PM = 2.82%). Additionally, the combination of energy drinks and soda significantly partially mediated the relationship between problematic gaming and sleep duration (indirect effect = −0.005, 95% CI [−0.012, 0.000]; direct effect = −0.008, 95% [−0.013, −0.003]; PM = 3.93%). Other types of caffeine, whether examined individually or in combination with other caffeinated beverages, did not reach statistical significance in any of the assessed hypotheses. See the Appendix Table A1 and Fig. A1 for further information.

Discussion

This is the first study to investigate the mediating effect of caffeine use on the relationship between gaming and sleep in adolescents. The study employed data from a large longitudinal sample in the US. Findings indicate that the mediating effect of caffeine use was small (around 4%–9.5%) but statistically significant in the relationships between (i) problematic gaming and sleep difficulties, (ii) problematic gaming and sleep duration, (iii) gaming duration and sleep difficulties, and (iv) gaming duration and sleep duration. These findings support the wider literature that has consistently reported a positive relationship between gaming and caffeine use (Bradbury et al., 2019; Larson et al., 2014; Porter et al., 2010; Ream et al., 2013; Škařupová et al., 2018) and a negative relationship between gaming and sleep in adolescents (Kristensen et al., 2021). Sensitivity analyses indicated that, in specific cases, the dominant component of the mediated effect was the consumption of energy drinks or the combination of energy drinks and soda (in the relationship between gaming duration and sleep difficulties and between problematic gaming and sleep duration, respectively). Caffeinated energy drinks and soda may be appealing to adolescents due to palatability, peer influences, and youth-targeted marketing, compared to more “traditional” sources of caffeine, such as coffee and tea (Bryant Ludden & Wolfson, 2010; Edwards et al., 2022; Visram et al., 2016). Considering the higher consumption of energy drinks and soda in boys (Bryant Ludden & Wolfson, 2010; Visram et al., 2016), sex differences in caffeine-mediated associations between gaming behaviors and sleep outcomes warrant further investigation. Overall, the results of the present study suggest that caffeine use serves as a pathway through which gaming behaviors may impact sleep in adolescents, potentially exacerbating sleep difficulties and reducing overall sleep duration. However, given that caffeine use mediated only a small proportion of the total effect, other potential mediators may also be relevant (e.g., emotional dysregulation and internalizing psychopathology) (Richard et al., 2022; Schettler, Thomasius, & Paschke, 2024).

The statistically significant association between gaming and caffeine use, which formed the first path of the mediation, aligns with the current literature on these behaviors. Most games demand motor skills, mental agility, and processing speed (Bányai, Griffiths, Király, & Demetrovics, 2019; Yin et al., 2020), which may explain why the effects of caffeine are so frequently sought by adolescents despite (or because of) its negative impact on sleep. While other substances, including alcohol, tobacco, and marijuana, are also used by people who game (Le et al., 2024; Ream et al., 2011), caffeine appears to be unique due to its normalization and perceived practical use. Unlike other substances that are often linked to compulsive or recreational behaviors, the consumption of coffee and energy drinks during gaming is typically framed as a deliberate choice to improve gaming performance or facilitate extended gaming sessions through sleep avoidance, enhanced concentration, improved tension management, and hunger suppression (Roscoe et al., 2023; Škařupová et al., 2018). In a study of individuals who played more competitive and addictive games, such as multiplayer online battle arena games (Rehbein, King, Staudt, Hayer, & Rumpf, 2021; T’ng, Ho, & Pau, 2023), caffeine was reported as the most commonly used substance during gaming (Škařupová et al., 2018). Individuals who consumed caffeine during gaming sessions reported an additional 3.8 h of play per week compared to individuals who did not use any substances (Škařupová et al., 2018).

Caffeine consumption among people who game may also occur for reasons unrelated to gaming, such as habitual or dependent use (Roscoe et al., 2023). This suggests that some individuals may exhibit comorbidity between problematic gaming and caffeine use disorder, which is listed as a “condition for further study” in the DSM-5 (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). A few studies have investigated and found a co-occurrence of problematic gaming and excessive caffeine use (Porter et al., 2010; Ream et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2022), similar to evidence of gaming disorder co-occurring with other substance use disorders (e.g., alcohol use disorder) (Burleigh, Griffiths, Sumich, Stavropoulos, & Kuss, 2019; Na, Lee, Choi, & Kim, 2017). However, the co-occurrence of caffeine use disorder remains relatively underexplored in the context of problematic gaming compared to other substance use disorders. Given that youth who game may be particularly vulnerable to caffeine use disorder due to targeted marketing strategies and the integration of caffeine into gaming cultures (Gallimberti et al., 2016; Roscoe et al., 2023; Visram et al., 2016), it is important to conduct further research to mitigate sleep-related harm and other health issues within this population. Such research may also inform policy responses (e.g., regulating the accessibility and marketing of caffeine to adolescents), public health approaches (e.g., prevention, harm reduction, and health promotion), and the development of problematic gaming treatment guidelines that account for the associations between gaming, caffeine use, and sleep.

The present study showed a small but statistically significant direct effect between gaming and sleep, with the mediating role of caffeine use accounting for a small proportion of that relationship. However, the potential impact of changing caffeine use behaviors during development should be highlighted, as the positive health consequences of reducing problematic behaviors adopted during adolescence can persist into adulthood (Mytton et al., 2024). This is especially relevant in the context of caffeine use among adolescents who game, as caffeine may potentially act as a gateway drug to other addictive substances (Galimov et al., 2019; 2020; Yang et al., 2022). For example, several longitudinal studies have indicated that caffeine consumption in early adolescence is associated with an increased likelihood of alcohol use later in life (Arria, Caldeira, Bugbee, Vincent, & O’Grady, 2017; Brunborg, Raninen, & Andreas, 2022; Choi et al., 2016; Galimov et al., 2020; Kristjansson et al., 2022; Marmorstein, 2019; Miyake & Marmorstein, 2015; Patrick & Maggs, 2014). One study found that adolescents who consume energy drinks and experience difficulties falling asleep (i.e., initial insomnia) may be at a particularly high risk for alcohol use (Marmorstein, 2017). Caffeine-related risks may be heightened when gaming becomes harmful, as problematic gaming has also been frequently associated with increased and problematic substance use (e.g., alcohol, nicotine, cannabis) (Di Carlo et al., 2023; Erevik et al., 2019; Konkolÿ Thege, Hodgins, & Wild, 2016; Na et al., 2017; Van Rooij et al., 2014). Turel and Bechara (2019) reported that fewer gaming hours may be protective against substance use, while greater hours may increase substance use. Overall, substance use appears to be linked to both caffeine use and problematic gaming in adolescents, potentially perpetuating cycles of disrupted sleep and daytime sleepiness (Owens et al., 2014). Future research should investigate both caffeine use and problematic gaming as risk factors for substance use disorders (e.g., alcohol use disorder), which have the potential to jointly impact sleep.

Further supporting the discussion that caffeine use during adolescence may contribute to vulnerability to other forms of substance use, there is evidence suggesting that polysubstance use involving caffeine is prevalent among young individuals who game (Di Carlo et al., 2023). The concomitant use of caffeinated energy drinks and alcohol is concerning (Mallett, Scaglione, Reavy, & Turrisi, 2015; Patrick, Veliz, Linden-Carmichael, & Terry-McElrath, 2018; Pollard, McStay, & Meng, 2015), as an estimated 37% of university students mix energy drinks with alcohol (De Giorgi et al., 2022) and 13% of emergency department visits related to energy drinks involve alcohol (Bonar et al., 2015; Mattson, 2013). Such mixtures have been termed “AmED” (Alcohol Mixed with Energy Drinks), and some researchers have proposed that caffeine may mask the perception of alcohol intoxication while delaying sleep and prolonging drinking episodes (Bonar et al., 2015). A similar mechanism may apply to gaming, where caffeine use extends gaming sessions while diminishing awareness of problematic gaming behaviors. Though AmED consumption is most commonly associated with jitteriness and trouble sleeping, AmED use has also been linked to increased alcohol consumption, problematic alcohol use, and risky behaviors, such as intentions to drive home intoxicated, unprotected sex, and use of other substances (e.g., marijuana, ecstasy, and cocaine use) (Berger, Fendrich, & Fuhrmann, 2013; O’Brien, McCoy, Rhodes, Wagoner, & Wolfson, 2008; Snipes & Benotsch, 2013; Thombs et al., 2010). In the context of individuals who game, it is important to consider that different combinations of substances may have unique effects, use expectations, and contexts of use (Ream et al., 2011; Sussman et al., 2011). These differences should be considered in future research as they may influence the development of specific patterns of addictive behaviors involving caffeine during adolescence and their subsequent impact on sleep and other health factors in adulthood.

This study has limitations that should be considered. All participants were youth from the United States, which potentially limits the generalizability of the results to other cultural and demographic contexts. Variations in caffeine use and gaming behaviors may be influenced by cultural norms, societal attitudes, and regulatory environments, which vary across countries (Quadra et al., 2020; Wham, Rowe, Ali, & Rutherford-Markwick, 2017). Thus, future research should examine the replicability of the results in international populations. Another limitation is that VGAQ scores from the ABCD study were used, which is a relatively infrequently used measure compared to some others. However, the development of a standardized international tool for assessing gaming-related harms is ongoing, and no single measure has yet been established as the gold standard for assessing problematic gaming (King et al., 2020; Park, King, Wilkinson-Meyers, & Rodda, 2023). Despite this limitation, the VGAQ aligns closely with DSM-5 criteria for internet gaming disorder, a “condition for further study” (American Psychiatric Association, 2013), supporting its suitability for use in the present study.

Conclusion

Caffeine use mediated a small proportion of the relationship between gaming duration/problems and sleep duration/difficulties in adolescents. Given the widespread use of caffeine among adolescents that game and the potential impact of modifying consumption during development, caffeine use could potentially be targeted to reduce sleep-related harm and other associated health issues. Study findings could guide policies around the accessibility and marketing of caffeine to adolescents and inform problematic gaming treatment guidelines.
