In vitro ovicidal and adulticidal activities of crude extracts and fractions of Rosa abyssinica Lindley and Justicia schimperiana (Hochst. Ex Nees) T. Anders against Fasciola species
Authors: Melat Solomon, Yalemtsehay Mekonnen, Amelework Eyado, Mujib Abdulkadir Abdurahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing emergence of triclabendazole resistance in Fasciola species necessitates the exploration of alternative control strategies. This study evaluated the in vitro ovicidal and adulticidal activity of crude extract (80% methanol) and solvent fractions (aqueous, ethyl acetate and n-hexane) of the leaves of Rosa abyssinica Lindley and Justicia schimperiana (Hochst. Ex Nees) against Fasciola species. Egg hatch inhibition and adult worm motility assays were performed at different concentrations, and preliminary phytochemical screening was conducted to identify major secondary metabolites. The results demonstrated that all tested phytochemicals were present in the crude extracts and most fractions of both plants, except phenolics and glycosides, which were not detected in the ethyl acetate (JSEF) and n-hexane (JSNF) fractions of J. schimperiana. Moreover, in both the egg hatch inhibition and adult worm motility assays, all extracts exhibited varying degrees of activity. The highest efficacy was observed in the R. abyssinica aqueous fraction (RAAF), which achieved 76.4% egg hatch inhibition (at 8 mg/mL) and 100% worm motility inhibition (death) at 240 min (at 15 mg/mL). In addition, egg hatch assay observations also indicated that the RAAF induced egg degeneration, complete developmental inhibition and delayed embryonic development. In conclusion, these findings demonstrate that R. abyssinica and J. schimperiana have activity against Fasciola species, with RAAF revealing the most potent effects. Therefore, the observed anthelmintic potential suggests that these plants warrant further investigation for the possible development of plant-based fasciolicidal agents.Keywords: Rosa abyssinica, Justicia schimperiana, Fasciola, Egg hatch, Motility

Introduction

Fasciolosis is a zoonotic parasitic disease caused by liver flukes of the genus Fasciola. This disease is of major veterinary importance and causes an estimated annual economic loss of about 3 billion US dollars (Rehman et al., 2023). Nowadays, it is also becoming a significant public health concern by causing an estimated 17 million infections and putting 180 million more at risk worldwide (Massamba et al., 2026).

Fascioliasis represents a major constraint to livestock production worldwide and is increasingly recognized as a significant public health concern. Globally, an estimated 2.4–17 million people are infected, with approximately one billion at risk of infection (Ezeta-Miranda et al., 2024). In Ethiopia, Fasciola hepatica is the most prevalent liver fluke, followed by F. gigantica. Besides, mixed infection with F. hepatica and F. gigantica is also reported to be common. Abattoir surveys from different parts of Ethiopia reported 47–91% of bovine fasciolosis. Additionally, 2.5% to 9.8% prevalence of fasciolosis was estimated from Ethiopian school-aged children (Abaya et al., 2023).

Control of fasciolosis relies predominantly on the administration of triclabendazole (TCBZ), the drug of choice against both immature and adult stages of Fasciola species. However, increasing reports of reduced susceptibility and resistance to TCBZ have raised serious concerns regarding its long-term efficacy (Choi et al., 2025; Tripathi, 2024). These challenges underscore the urgent need to explore alternative and sustainable fasciolosis control strategies.

Medicinal plants constitute a valuable source of bioactive compounds with diverse pharmacological properties, including anthelmintic activity. Their effects are often attributed to secondary metabolites such as phenolics, flavonoids, tannins, saponins, and terpenoids, which may interfere with parasite physiology and development (Boyko & Brygadyrenko, 2025).). Rosa abyssinica and Justicia schimperiana are medicinal plants traditionally used in Ethiopia for the management of helminthic infections. Ethnobotanical reports indicate the use of R. abyssinica in the treatment of ascaris, tapeworm infections, and abdominal cramps, while J. schimperiana has been used against tapeworms and other intestinal parasites (Abebe & Teferi, 2021; Taye et al., 2022). Additionally, the use of J. schimperiana for the treatment of tapeworm and other abdominal and intestinal parasites was reported (Taye et al., 2022).

Previous studies have reported the presence of various bioactive constituents, including flavonoids, phenolics, alkaloids, tannins, saponins, glycosides and other major phytochemicals from R. abyssinica and J. schimperiana (Abdu et al., 2024; G/giorgis et al., 2022). Specific compounds identified from J. schimperiana, such as eucalyptol, β-sitosterol, and kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside, among the plant's major constituents, have been reported to have anthelmintic activity against different helminthic species (García et al., 2023; Jegnie et al., 2026; Thais et al., 2025; Villaseñor et al., 2002). In addition, R. abyssinica has been reported to contain compounds such as caryophyllene and furfural (Al-rehaily et al., 2003; Moustafa & Alrumman, 2011), both of which have demonstrated gastroprotective and anthelmintic potentials (Gyrdymova & Rubtsova, 2021; Sánchez-mendoza et al., 2014; Thais et al., 2025). These previous works provide a phytochemical basis for investigating the anthelmintic activity of these plants.

Although molluscicidal activity of R. abyssinica fruits has been reported (Basha et al., 2024) there is limited experimental evidence regarding the direct anthelmintic activity of the leaves of these plants against Fasciola species. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the in vitro ovicidal and adulticidal activity of crude extracts and solvent fractions of R. abyssinica and J. schimperiana leaves against Fasciola species and to characterize their preliminary phytochemical constituents.

Materials and methods

Plant collection and authentication

Fresh leaves of R. abyssinica and J. schimperiana were collected from Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, at coordinates of 9° 4′13.13"N: 38°46′28.69"E and 9° 2′30.55"N: 38°46′37.53"E, respectively. Botanical identification was performed at the National Herbarium, Addis Ababa University, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Voucher specimens were deposited with the specimen numbers MS001 and MS002 for R. abyssinica and J. schimperiana, respectively.

Plant leaves extraction and fractionation

Approximately 3 and 3.8 kg of fresh leaves of R. abyssinica and J. schimperiana, respectively, were collected. The leaves were shade-dried at room temperature and subsequently powdered using a grinder. To prepare the crude extract, the powder (600 g) was macerated with 80% methanol in a 1:5 (w/v) ratio on a shaker for four consecutive days. The mixture was subsequently filtered with Whatman filter paper (No. 1) followed by concentration under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator at 40 °C and stored at -4 °C until use. The 80% methanol crude extracts of R. abyssinica and J. schimperinana were coded as RA and JS, respectively.

Fractionation of the crude extracts was performed following the solvent partitioning method described by Ayalew et al. (2022). Briefly of 70 g of crude extract was dissolved in 280 ml of distilled water, followed by the addition of 280 ml of n-hexane. Subsequently, the resulting solution was transferred into a separatory funnel and allowed to stand with occasional stirring. After a clear layer was formed, the n-hexane (upper) layer was carefully removed. The addition of n-hexane to the residue was repeated twice, followed by collecting the upper layer. All the n-hexane layers were combined and taken as the n-hexane fraction (NF).

Following the n-hexane extraction, the residue (aqueous layer) was subjected to a similar extraction process using 280 ml of ethyl acetate. After standing and separation, the ethyl acetate layer was collected as the ethyl acetate fraction (EF) and while the residual aqueous layer was retained as the aqueous fraction (AF). The n-hexane and ethyl acetate fractions were concentrated using a rotary evaporator and any residual solvents were removed by drying in the oven at 40 °C. The aqueous fraction was lyophilized. All the fractions (NF, EF and AF) were stored at -4 °C until use.

Phytochemical analysis

Phenolic test

Five milligrams of extract were dissolved in 3 mL of distilled water. From this solution, 2 mL was transferred into a clean test tube. Subsequently, 5 mL of 5% ferric chloride (FeCl3) solution was added dropwise, and the mixture was gently shaken. The development of dark green/bluish black color indicated the presence of phenolic compounds (Shaikh, 2020).

Flavonoids

Two hundred milligrams of the extract were dissolved in 2 mL of ethanol directly in a test tube. A few drops of 1% lead acetate solution were then added. The formation of a yellow precipitate predicts the presence of flavonoids in the extract (Singh & Kumar, 2017).

Saponin

A 0.25 g of extract was diluted with 5 mL of distilled water in a test tube and vigorously shaken for 30 seconds. The mixture was then allowed to stand for a few Minutes. Persistent foam formation indicated the presence of saponin (Yirdaw & Kassa, 2023).

Terpenoids

A 0.2g of extract were mixed thoroughly with 2 mL of chloroform. Subsequently, 1 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) was carefully added along the side of the test tube to form a separate layer. The appearance of reddish- brown coloration at the interface indicated the presence of terpenoid (María et al., 2018).

Tannin

A hundred milligrams of the extract were dissolved in 5 mL of distilled water. Two mL of the extract was transferred to a test tube, then a few drops of 1% lead acetate was added. The formation of a yellowish precipitate indicated the presence of tannin (Devi et al., 2023).

Glycosides

The extract (0.5 mg) was dissolved in 1 mL of distilled water. Then, 40% sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was added drop by drop. The appearance of yellow color in the solution indicated the presence of a glycoside compound (Jagessar, 2017).

The extract activity against liver fluke

In vitro egg hatch assay

The egg hatch assay experiment was conducted according to the method used by Nwofor et al. (2018) with a slight modification. The Fasciola egg was obtained from the bile of cattle naturally infected with Fasciola. After the infection was confirmed in the liver, the bile was taken to isolate the eggs. The bile was centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was removed and the sedimented eggs were cleaned with distilled water repeatedly by centrifugation (2500 rpm for 5 min) and stored at 4 °C. Then the egg suspension was evaluated under a microscope and was diluted to obtain approximately 100 eggs in 2 μl. The experiment was performed on 96-well plates. About 2 μL of egg suspension was planted in the wells. The treatment group was filled with 300 μl of crude extracts and fractions in concentrations of 2, 4 and 8 mg/mL. Triclabendazole was used in the same amount as a positive control and 300 μl tween 80 (5 %) was used as a negative control. The experiment was performed in a triplicate manner. The eggs were incubated in a dark place at room temperature (26–28 °C). After a 24 h incubation, the extracts were carefully removed and replaced with distilled water. Incubation was then continued for a further 15 days for embryonation to take place (a total of 16 days). The eggs were then placed under a 60-watt artificial heat lamp for 5 h to induce hatching. Then the ovicidal activity was determined based on the percentage of eggs that failed to hatch.

In vitro adult worm motility assay

The adult flukes were collected from the infected livers of cattle obtained from Addis Ababa Abattoirs Enterprise. The morphological identification of worms was done by Dr. Abay Eniyew (Doctor of Veterinary Medicine). Both Fasciola hepatica and Fasciola gigantic worms mixed infection was identified in the livers. Further identification was performed by Amelework Eyado (Biomedical researcher). The adult worm motility assay was performed according to the method described by Nurlaelasari et al. (2023), with slight modifications. The fluke worms were rinsed in Hanks' saline (HBSS), pH 7.4 and then placed randomly in separate Petri dishes (5 worms/group) in 10 Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium with plant extracts and fractions at a concentration of 30, 15 and 7.5 mg/ml. A similar concentration of triclabendazole to the extracts was used as a positive control and the negative control group received 5% tween 80 as a vehicle control.

Worm motility was measured under a dissecting microscope (40x) after 30, 60, 120, 240 and 480 min using the Stepek et al. (2005) described motility scale of 0-5 Where 0 indicates total loss of motility, 1 indicates movement only when prodded, 2 indicates movement just at the ends of the body, 3 indicates reduced movement overall, 4 indicates active worms and 5 indicates highly motile worms. In the absence of the test substances, the motility of the control worms was observed.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Review Board (IRB) of the College of Natural and Computational Sciences, Addis Ababa University, before the commencement of the experiment (Minute No: IRB/06/2015/2023).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was done using JMP Pro 14 software. Differences among different groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey–Kramer honestly significant difference (HSD) test for multiple comparisons of mean hatched egg numbers. GraphPad Prism 11 software was used to generate graphs of adult worm motility activity and to determine the half maximal effective time (Et50) and effective time 90 (Et90). Statistical significance was accepted at p ≤ 0.05. The experiments were done in triplicate.

Results

Phytochemical analysis

The R. abyssinica crude extract (RA) and its fractions (RAAF, RAEF and RANF) illustrated the presence of phenolics, flavonoids, glycosides, saponins, tannins and terpenoids. Similarly, the crude extract of J. schimperiana (JS) contained all six tested phytochemical groups. Among its solvent fraction, JSAF was found to contain all six tested phytochemical groups. However, only flavonoids, saponins, tannins and terpenoids were detected in JSEF and JSNF (Table 1).

Observation

Therefore, the following observations describe the earliest egg developmental stages mostly observed from the groups treated with 8 mg/mL concentration at day 16.

The negative control (NC) mostly contained a hatched egg through an open operculum, as well as actively moving eye stage embryos. The positive control (Triclabendazole (TCBZ)) contained a few stained eggs (pink), un-embryonated eggs, eggs at early developmental stages and eggs with complete developmental inhibition (Fig. 1).

In vitro adult worm motility

In the motility assay experiment for the adult flukes, the negative control group remained unaffected, with no decrease in motility observed throughout the experiment. The antimotility activity of the crude extracts, as well as their fraction, was observed to be time and concentration-dependent (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4).

Discussion

The increasing ineffectiveness of triclabendazole (TCBZ), the drug of choice for the treatment of fascioliasis, has become a major concern in the control of Fasciola species. Reports of reduced susceptibility and resistance have compelled researchers to explore alternative control strategies. In this context, natural herbal products have gained considerable attention, as they represent a rich reservoir of bioactive compounds with diverse medical properties and potential anthelmintic activity (Allahyari et al., 2024).

In the present study, qualitative phytochemical screening of the crude extract of R. abyssinica (RA) revealed the presence of phenolics, flavonoids, glycosides, saponins, tannins and terpenoids (Table 1). GC-MS analysis by Moustafa & Alrumman (2011) confirmed the presence of various phenolic, glycosidic, and terpenoid compounds in the leaves, supporting the current findings. Furthermore, previous phytochemical screening of the fruit of R. abyssinica indicated the presence of phenolics, flavonoids, saponins, tannins and terpenoids, with the exception of glycosides (Wassie & Tsegay, 2025). The existence of similar classes of secondary metabolites in both fruits and leaves suggests that these compounds are widely distributed throughout the plant.

The crude extract of J. schimperiana (JS) revealed the presence of phenolics, flavonoids, glycosides, saponins, tannins and terpenoids (Table 1). These findings are in agreement with the report of G/giorgis et al. (2022), who similarly documented the presence of all these major phytochemical classes in the methanolic leaf extract of J. schimperiana . However, partial discrepancies were observed when compared with other studies. Eyasu et al. (2024) reported the presence of phenolics, flavonoids, saponins and tannins, but did not detect terpenoids. Similarly, Gizaw et al. (2022) identified the same phytochemical classes, while glycosides were not detected. These variations may be attributed to differences in extraction methods, harvesting season, geographical origin and environmental conditions (Alberto et al., 2025).

This study evaluated the effect of the crude extracts and fractions of the leaves of R. abyssinica and J. schimperiana against the adult worms and the eggs of Fasciola species. Evaluating the anthelmintic potential of plant extracts across more than one parasite developmental stage is essential for establishing a comprehensive profile of their efficacy against Fasciola species (Nurlaelasari et al., 2023).

To evaluate the egg hatch assay, Fasciola eggs were incubated for 16 days at 25–28 °C, which corresponds to the upper limit of the reported embryonation period for F. hepatica and F. gigantica (Hussein et al., 2010). Under these conditions, the negative control eggs demonstrated normal development and miracidial hatching after 5 hours of light exposure on day 16, with a hatch rate of 62.68%. All tested crude extracts and fractions of both plants revealed egg hatch inhibition and adult worm motility inhibition in a concentration-dependent manner. However, the level of activity varied among the different extracts. Among all extracts obtained from both plants, RAAF showed the highest egg hatch inhibition (76.4%), followed by RAEF (71.51%) and the RA 80% methanol extract (68.38%), highlighting the better anthelmintic potential of R. abyssinica compared to J. schimperiana (Table 2).

Based on the observations, the extracts were able to delay, inhibit and degenerate the development of Fasciola eggs to varying degrees. The degenerated eggs, appearing dark was observed in the group treated with RAAF (Fig. 1). This result suggests that the extract exerted a direct ovicidal effect, possibly through severe disruption of embryonic development and distortion of egg structure (Shanawany et al., 2019). This could be attributed to the strong protein-binding properties of tannins, leading to interactions with egg shell and embryonic structural proteins and resulting in protein aggregation and clumping (Hagerman, 1989).

Eggs treated with JSAF and JSNF fractions predominantly exhibited hatching accompanied by extensive eggshell rupture rather than normal opercular opening (Fig. 1). This hatching pattern suggests that these fractions weakened eggshell integrity, likely through the action of phytochemicals such as saponin, phenolics and tannin, which can disrupt eggshell structural proteins and increase permeability, leading to loss of mechanical strength and shell rupture during miracidial hatching (Francis et al., 2002; Molan et al., 2002).

In the adult worm motility assay, all tested extracts demonstrated anthelmintic potential, ranging from reduced motility to complete worm death. Among the plant treatments, R. abyssinica’s 80% methanol crude extract and its RAAF and RAEF fractions exhibited pronounced activity, achieving complete inhibition (death) more rapidly (at 240 min) than the extracts of J. schimperiana for which the JSEF fraction produced a comparable effect. Notably, RAAF revealed a clear concentration-dependent efficacy and maintained complete mortality even at the lowest concentration (7.5 mg/mL at 480 min), where TCBZ failed to achieve a similar effect (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4). This enhanced activity at a reduced dose suggests the intrinsic anthelmintic potential of the RAAF fraction.

The Et50 and Et90 analyses showed that JS exhibited the lowest Et50, followed by RAAF at 30 mg/mL. In contrast, the lowest Et90 was observed for RAAF, followed by RAEF at 30 mg/mL (Table 3). These results indicate that JS has a rapid initial effect with high early efficacy, which decreases over time. RAAF demonstrates a more sustained and consistent activity across time, whereas RAEF showed a slower onset of activity initially, followed by increased efficacy at later time points. Additionally, the observed better performance of JS compared to its fractions may suggest that the effect arises from the synergistic interaction among multiple phytochemical constituents (Abebe et al., 2022). Consistent with this, the observed activity across both crude extracts and fractions suggests that the anthelmintic effect is likely associated with multiple phytochemical constituents distributed across fractions rather than a single active compound. Although all fractions exhibited activity, differences in efficacy likely reflect variations in the concentration and composition of bioactive metabolites rather than uniform potency. Furthermore, the comparable or higher activity observed in some crude extracts may indicate that synergistic interactions among phytochemicals contribute to the overall effect, which could be partially reduced upon fractionation. However, in the absence of chromatographic profiling or bioassay-guided fractionation, it is not possible to definitively attribute the observed activity to specific fractions. Therefore, these findings should be considered preliminary, and further studies using advanced analytical techniques such as HPLC or LC–MS are recommended to identify and characterize the active constituents.

Previous studies have shown that leaf extract of Artemisia vulgaris (96% ethanol) and essential oils of Lavandula angustifolia and Ceratonia siliqua L. exhibited significant inhibitory effects on adult worms and egg hatching of Fasciola species in a concentration-dependent manner (Allahyari et al., 2024; Nurlaelasari et al., 2023). Increased concentration was associated with greater reductions in adult worm motility and higher egg-hatch inhibition. Therefore, these previous findings are consistent with the present results, further supporting the potential of plant-derived extracts and essential oils as effective agents against multiple developmental stages of Fasciola species.

In vitro study by Zulqarnain et al. (2026) reported that Artemisia absinthium aerial aqueous-methanolic extract and standard drug-treated Fasciola eggs exhibited delayed development, unembryonated eggs and damaged egg shells, findings that are consistent with results observed in the present study (Fig. 1). These results indicate a strong inhibitory effect on egg development.

The observed fasciolicidal activity may be associated with the presence of the aforementioned bioactive phytochemicals. Saponins are known to increase calcium ion influx, leading to excessive stimulation of worm muscle activity and subsequent paralysis. In contrast, tannins exert their anthelmintic effect by disrupting cell membrane integrity through strong interaction with membrane protein, ultimately causing membrane damage and lysis of the worm cell (Fahlevi et al., 2021). Additionally, terpenes may contribute to the observed activity by inducing tegumental membrane disintegration, owing to their lipophilic nature, they can penetrate and disrupt membrane integrity, thereby disturbing normal biochemical and physiological processes of the parasite (Wiles et al., 2025).

The observed biological activity may also be attributed to specific compounds such as caryophyllene and furfural identified in R. abyssinica (Al-rehaily et al., 2003; Moustafa & Alrumman, 2011), as well as eucalyptol, β-sitosterol, and kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside reported from J. schimperiana (Jegnie et al., 2026). These metabolites have been associated with anthelmintic activities and gastroprotective effects (García et al., 2023; Gyrdymova & Rubtsova, 2021; Sánchez-mendoza et al., 2014; Thais et al., 2025).

In general, the activity of R. abyssinica crude extract and its fractions, particularly RAAF and RAEF was observed to be higher in both egg hatch and adult worm motility assays compared to J. schimperiana crude extract and fractions. From previous work, R. abyssinica leaves were reported to contain higher total phenolic, flavonoid and alkaloid contents, along with broader and more intense fourier transform infrared (FTIR) peaks and stretching bands compared to J. schimperiana (Solomon et al., 2025). This previously reported phytochemical richness and chemical complexity may partly explain the superior anthelmintic activity observed.

To date, there are no documented studies specifically investigating the anthelmintic activity of R. abyssinica and J. schimperiana using standard in vitro or in vivo assays. In this context, the present study provides initial experimental evidence and may serve as a reference point for future investigations into the anthelmintic potential of these medicinal plants, particularly against Fasciola species. Nonetheless, comprehensive phytochemical analysis using advanced analytical approaches is necessary to identify, isolate and characterize the compounds underlying the observed biological activity.

Conclusion

R. abyssinica and J. schimperiana exhibited measurable anthelmintic activity against Fasciola species. R. abyssinica demonstrated comparatively strong efficacy, with its aqueous fraction (RAAF) revealing the highest inhibitory activity in both egg hatch and adult worm -motility assays. The crude extract of J. schimperiana also showed more promising activity in both assays than its fractions. The observed egg degeneration, delayed development and complete inhibition of embryogenesis highlight the potential of RAAF and other extracts to interfere with the life cycle of Fasciola species, which is critical for effective parasite control. This enhanced activity is likely associated with the presence of important bioactive phytochemicals detected in the present study. However, these findings are preliminary and based on in vitro evidence. Therefore, further studies involving advanced phytochemical characterization, compound isolation, and in vivo validation are necessary to confirm efficacy and to better understand the mechanisms underlying the observed anthelmintic effects.
