‘We Feel Invisible a Lot of the Time’: Insights Into the Roles of Alcohol and Other Drug Lived and Living Experience Workers
Authors: Ariel D Roxburgh, Shalini Arunogiri, Lauren Findlay, Victoria Manning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe employment of alcohol and other drug lived and living experience (LLE) workers has increased. Although there is a growing literature on challenges faced by LLE workers, there remains a gap in understanding the unique challenges and facilitators of frontline workers in diverse settings. Additionally, it is unclear what unique barriers are faced by living experience workers.MethodSeven focus groups were recorded and transcribed with a total of 16 LLE worker participants. Themes from focus groups were analysed using thematic analysis. Two themes were identified: (i) experiences of validation; and (ii) supporting the workforce.ResultsWhile most participants felt valued by clients, many spoke about experiences of stigmatisation by colleagues, which was evident for workers in hospital settings, possibly reflecting organisational models of care and their maturity in employing LLE workers. Living experience workers spoke about experiencing stigma that was underpinned by discriminatory policies and laws.Discussion and ConclusionsThe findings and discussion highlight the challenges faced by the LLE workforce that may be underpinned by in‐group and out‐group identity, lack of support structures, a lack of role clarity, and several other factors. Addressing these issues is important for any organisation that employs or intends to employ alcohol and other drug lived or living experience workers.Keywords: addiction, peer recovery support services, peer support specialist, peer work, qualitative

Introduction

A frontline alcohol and other drug (AOD) lived and living experience (LLE) worker refers to a worker who utilises their own past (lived) or current (living) experience of alcohol and other drug use, or associated experiences (e.g., criminal/justice, risk of overdose), to help others [1]. These workers also use skills learned in formal training and discipline specific frameworks [2]. The AOD LLE workforce is expanding, especially in clinical settings such as hospitals [3, 4]. This growth is expected to continue in Australia, driven by major reforms across jurisdictions, such as those implemented after recommendations from the Royal Commission into Victoria's Mental Health System [5]. This momentum reflects the growing evidence that engagement with LLE workers has been shown to improve treatment engagement, effectiveness, and satisfaction [1, 6, 7, 8]. However, the LLE workforce faces unique challenges in their roles [9, 10, 11].

While research on the challenges faced by AOD LLE workers is growing [9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15], specific experiences of these workers are often blurred by the inclusion of mental health worker experiences. For instance, du Plessis and colleagues [9] conducted a systematic review on LLE roles within substance use treatment settings, but had to broaden the scope to include mental health settings, citing the high co‐occurrence of substance use and mental health disorders and the limited availability of studies focused solely on AOD LLE workers.

This underscores the need for more targeted research that explores the distinct challenges faced by AOD LLE workers. Nevertheless, the broader literature on mental health LLE workers (which encompasses AOD roles) offers insights into the challenges AOD LLE workers might experience. These include: (i) LLE work being poorly defined—which leads to under‐utilisation of LLEs, LLEs being asked to do non‐LLE work, and LLEs feeling overwhelmed without clearly defined tasks; (ii) a lack of training and supervision; (iii) issues around remuneration and career progression; and (iv) being employed alone without other LLEs in the organisation [9, 11, 12, 14, 16]. More research is needed to fully elucidate the challenges faced by the AOD LLE workforce in Australia especially in subgroups and settings that remain unexplored. For example, AOD LLE roles are less established in hospital settings, having been previously concentrated in community and residential rehabilitation settings [17, 18]. This lower maturity of the role within hospital settings suggests that its integration may present distinct challenges. In addition to exploring unique settings, more research is needed to understand the experience of those identifying as living experience workers.

Living Experience

AOD living experience workers are people who use their current experience of drug use and/or overdose to help others, playing an important role in harm reduction initiatives such as needle and syringe exchanges and overdose prevention [8]. In reality, the distinction between lived and living experience of substance use is fluid. For example, people may have intermittent periods of abstinence and substance use, may no longer meet criteria for a substance use disorder but still engage in substance use, or may be abstinent from one substance but continue to consume another. Because people who use drugs are often criminalised, stigmatised and feel unsafe when receiving healthcare, living experience workers help create safety and trust [19]. Evidence shows this workforce improves treatment satisfaction, medication adherence, and mental health while reducing blood‐borne virus transmission [8, 20]. Despite these benefits, only one Australian study has examined the unique barriers faced by this workforce [21]. The study highlighted a tension in societal attitudes, where abstinence is celebrated but ongoing use is stigmatised, and highlighted employment barriers shaped by legal and organisational structures. Overall, the evidence on workforce challenges remains limited, and further research is needed to explore both the distinct and overlapping challenges faced by living and lived experience workers, along with their perspectives on what might address these challenges.

The current study aims to understand the factors that support and impede AOD LLE workers in their roles, and to examine whether these experiences differ for workers who identify as having living experience and those in unique settings.

Method

Participants

This study was conducted in Victoria, Australia. Participants were recruited through the mailing list of the state's peak body for AOD services, as well as through snowball sampling approach, where individuals who learned about the study could share the information with others. Inclusion criteria were: (i) currently or previously employed in a Victorian AOD LLE role; and (ii) over 18 years. Non‐AOD LLE workers, people working in LLE volunteer roles, non‐frontline workers (e.g., managers, educators and trainers), and non‐Victorian LLE workers were excluded. A total of 16 participants (Table 1) provided informed consent to participate and attended 1 of 7 focus groups.

Measures

Age, gender, type of service, workplace and LLE worker type were collected where lived experience referred to individuals with past substance use, and living experience referred to those currently using substances. Peer was intentionally ambiguous to allow participants the flexibility to withhold details about their substance use history if they chose to, or to provide an option acknowledging that the boundaries between lived and living are blurred (e.g., living for one substance and lived for another). For ‘type of service’, service options were included with local examples of these services provided next to each option to aid LLEs in selection. Service types included: non‐government organisations (NGO), community health (state‐funded primary healthcare for people with higher social and economic need and at greater risk of poorer health outcomes), hospital, residential treatment (e.g., therapeutic community), and harm reduction (e.g., needle and syringe program).

Data Collection

In‐depth, semi‐structured focus groups were conducted with participants. The focus group guide included questions around experiences of working as an LLE in the AOD sector, including perceived challenges and benefits. Focus groups also explored experiences of current supports and views on what could be changed. Focus groups were run by two people with lived experience of working as an AOD LLE. Facilitators began by introducing themselves and highlighting their own experience as AOD LLE workers to help reduce anxiety, create cultural safety and encourage participant openness. After questions were answered, facilitators invited others to contribute or prompted participants to further elaborate. Consent was obtained prior to the interviews, either verbally or by signing a consent form (when in‐person). Focus groups were conducted in‐person or via Zoom, took approximately 50 min to complete, and were audio recorded. Participants were reimbursed with an AUD $40 voucher. Ethics approval for this study was granted by the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee.

Data Analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed by an external transcription company. Transcripts were imported into NVivo 14 [22], and analysed using thematic analysis [23]. Thematic analysis was conducted following Braun and Clarke's [23] six‐phase procedure for conducting thematic analysis. In practice, the process was iterative and collaborative, using both inductive and reflective processes. The lead author (who has lived experience as an AOD LLE worker and who facilitated the focus groups) initially familiarised themselves with the data through repeated reading of transcripts and developed a preliminary set of thematic categories, forming an initial coding framework. Subsequently, the lead author and a second researcher (without lived experience and of a different gender—ensuring diverse perspectives) used NVivo 14 [22] and the preliminary coding framework to code text segments line‐byline into preliminary categories. As coding progressed, the coding framework was iteratively refined: new themes were generated where necessary, existing themes were revised, merged, or reorganised, categorised into higher order themes, and sub‐themes were further specified to better capture the nuances of the data. These meetings and refinement (e.g., discussing interpretations of quotes and grouping of data), resulted in a shared understanding of the data with multiple perspectives. A final set of themes and sub‐themes was agreed upon, supported by illustrative quotes. These themes were further reviewed and refined with input from the wider author team to ensure clarity and coherence in the final analysis. All participants were de‐identified.

Results

Two interconnected themes were identified through the focus group analysis (see Table 2 for themes and sub‐themes). The themes highlighted both experiences of lived and living experience workers in different workplace settings. The first theme, Experiences of Validation, captured participants' accounts of feeling valued in their roles as well as moments where they perceived a lack of validation. The second theme, Supporting the Workforce, captured the conditions that enable effective LLE work. Across both themes, two distinct factors were identified. Firstly, the setting—participants in hospitals had emotive accounts of their experiences in the workplace. This was principally evident within the sub‐theme ‘valued by clients, co‐workers, and the organisation’, with many feeling unvalued by co‐workers. Secondly, those identifying as living experience also recounted emotive experiences. This was captured in the theme of not being valued by clients, co‐workers, and the organisation, with many feeling unvalued by organisations and society in general.

Experiences of Validation

This theme refers to the acknowledgment that the way of working, thoughts, feelings, efforts or identity of LLE workers are meaningful, important and accepted. Experiences of validation were identified as a key theme because it underpinned many of the experiences discussed by participants such as feeling a lack of respect, not feeling seen or acknowledged, and not feeling valued. Participants sometimes implied that these experiences of invalidation (often from colleagues or society) could be driven by a poor understanding of LLE roles. However, LLEs said validation was often provided by clients, and that many aspects of their roles provided fulfilment.

Respecting Diversity

As illustrated in Table 2, participants' experiences of validation were highlighted by several sub‐themes identified during analysis. Some felt that the diversity in ways of working brought by LLEs inappropriately conformed to other models of support, which conveyed a lack of respect for the diversity brought by LLEs.

They asked us to wear badges … if I'm out there on the streets talking to people on drugs, if they see a badge, it automatically puts a barrier up … (Michael, lived experience worker, NGO).

This was particularly relevant to hospitals where participants sometimes felt their diverse experiences could be better utilised to inform care. Participants often described how their ways of working were not understood, respected or even allowed to be integrated within a medical model, which includes rigid clinical policies.

… when someone's about to go off, I'd love to just take them out, have a cigarette you know, go for a walk. But we can't do that in a hospital. If they start to escalate, then a code grey gets called and then certain procedures have to follow and peer workers are not part of a code grey procedure … the medical model and all this risk … it blocks peer workers from being involved. (Melissa, peer worker, hospital).

However, some participants from non‐hospital settings felt that their workplace respected and valued the diversity brought by LLE workers.

I think sometimes being around people who have lots of study under their belt and then you've got your lived experience … they're very open to listening. (Emma, peer worker, residential treatment).

Lack of Referrals

One theme, illustrated by many examples from participants in hospital settings, was that co‐workers were not making referrals to LLE workers. Participants noted that a lack of referrals not only restricted patient access to LLE support but also reinforced feelings that their role was undervalued within multidisciplinary teams. In contrast, appropriate referral practices were viewed as an important form of recognition, reinforcing the legitimacy and usefulness of LLE work. As Nicole explained, persistent efforts to clarify their role did not always translate into referrals from senior clinicians:

How do you make a senior consultant … understand that I am there to do a job too and that it's actually his job to utilise me and to offer my service to his patient … I just don't know how much more I can explain my role, send emails, reminders … (Nicole, peer worker, hospital).

Melissa further contextualised this issue, suggesting that low referral rates were closely tied to limited awareness and understanding of what peer work involves and the scope of support LLE workers can provide:

Because a lot of people aren't aware about what peer workers do and what we can help patients with, we often don't get as many referrals as we should be getting. (Melissa, peer worker, hospital).

This example of problems arising from a lack of understanding of LLE work was a common issue underpinning many of the other subthemes. The understanding of what an LLE role is or involves is known as role clarity.

Role Clarity

Participants described a pervasive lack of role clarity, characterised by limited understanding of what LLE workers do. This lack of understanding was evident across multiple levels, including LLE workers themselves, their co‐workers, the organisation, and within the LLE discipline itself. Participants suggested that this absence of clear role definition contributed to a lack of validation and feelings of inadequacy. For example, one participant said,

I remember when I started, within the team, I just felt completely lost. I didn't know where to be and what to do … Because [LLE work] was new to the organisation and new to everyone, no one sort of knew what to do … like I felt like a complete fraud. (Nathan, lived experience worker, hospital).

This account illustrates how insufficient role clarity can be experienced simultaneously at individual and organisational levels, particularly when LLE roles are newly introduced or poorly embedded within services.

Valued by Clients, Co‐Workers and the Organisation

This subtheme focuses on participants' experiences of feeling either valued or unvalued by clients, co‐workers, their organisation and society. Participants generally felt valued by clients, often through strong rapport, positive feedback and the sense that their work made a difference.

They know you know how hard it is … I love the relationship I form with people who have gone through the similar kind of experiences, I suppose. (Emma, peer worker, residential treatment).

While participants' experiences of clients were positive, their experiences with colleagues were mixed. The most frequently identified topic from the focus groups was around feeling unvalued by non‐LLE co‐workers.

I'm not always involved in the conversations they [non‐LLE co‐workers] have when they're talking about clients. That kind of bugs me a little bit, because I don't understand the full story and don't really know what to look out for. (Chris, lived experience worker, NGO).

This quote highlights the consequences of exclusion, which include limited understanding of client histories, decreased ability to provide support, and a sense of marginalisation.

Experiences of feeling unvalued were especially pronounced in the quotes of participants from hospital settings, where many described hierarchical workplace cultures and instances of dismissiveness or microaggressions from non‐LLE staff or other interactions that left them feeling disregarded.

I have never had a problem with a consumer [patient] and I really mean ever in two years. People are rude, people are having a psychotic episode, people are withdrawing, whatever, but the rudeness of a consultant not replying to a good morning, that really hurts. We're invisible. We feel invisible a lot of the time. (Lisa, lived experience worker, hospital).

In contrast, some participants in other organisations described more positive organisational cultures, providing accounts that demonstrated they felt valued by their co‐workers.

[workers, managers and the organisation] kind of get – they get me. (Daniel, peer worker, NGO).

Many participants spoke of feeling valued or not valued by the organisation and society more broadly. This was driven by experiences of being ‘tokenised’, not receiving the same privileges as other workers, and the broader devaluation of LLE roles.

I guess my experience with peer work would be not a great one. I think I was brought in as a token. (Emma, peer worker, residential treatment).

The theme of not being valued by co‐workers, organisations and society was also commonly identified in the experience of living experience workers, who stated that current substance use is criminalised and is more stigmatised than past substance use. One participant highlighted a distinction between ‘acceptable’ and ‘unacceptable’ forms of lived experience.

It's this idea; good peer, bad peer – you're a good peer as long as you want to conform to social norms and you want to be part of mainstream society and you want to distance yourself from bad behaviours like being mentally unwell or taking drugs. (Sean, living experience worker, community health).

The participant continued by describing a system that facilitates discrimination against those who use drugs—including living experience workers. Another participant indicated that they feel stigma and discrimination coming from their organisation rather than co‐workers or clients.

I've experienced a lot of stigma in my roles … I do not get triggered by working with the people that I'm working with, at all. The only triggers in work for me are always organisational triggers. (Ruth, lived and living experience, community health).

Pay

Participants spoke about the experiences of pay, with many expressing concerns that their pay did not reflect the value or complexity of their work. For several participants, perceived pay inequity contributed to broader feelings of being undervalued within their organisations.

I don't feel like what I do is any less than anyone else, but I definitely get paid a lot less. (Chris, lived experience worker, NGO).

In contrast, some participants reported satisfaction with their pay and conditions, highlighting variability across services and employment contexts within the sector.

Holidays are good, pay rate's good. (Daniel, peer worker, NGO).

Overall, pay was seen not only as a financial issue but also as a marker of organisational recognition. Lower pay was often interpreted as a lack of value placed on LLE work compared to other roles and was linked to broader pressures such as high workloads, stress, and concerns about role sustainability.

Organisational Policies

Living experience workers described instances where organisational policies were perceived as discriminatory and contributing to a sense of ongoing scrutiny. In particular, policies related to drug and alcohol use were seen as punitive and disproportionately impacting those with living experience.

It's common for a workplace in any sector to have policy around drug use or substance use and often that policy is quite punitive. I've picked up on one [policy] … it was saying things like, if you think a worker's using drugs, then you send them off for urine tests … We can't have that as policy. (Sean, living experience worker, community health).

This highlights that organisational policies are an important pathway to providing validation to LLE workers.

Fulfilment From Work

Nevertheless, many participants spoke about the connection and social benefits of their work through supportive connections with co‐workers, and the meaning that comes from helping others. Others spoke of the benefits to their own mental health of being actively engaged in something.

I enjoy being looked up to and being able to provide my experience. (Chris, lived experience worker, NGO).

I really enjoyed working in an area where I felt comfortable … Around people who I've spent time with over the years … I felt embraced. (Jessie, living experience worker, NGO).

The theme of experiences of validation was underpinned by both validating and invalidating experiences. Generally, participants felt valued by their clients but sometimes felt unvalued by non‐LLE co‐workers.

Supporting the Workforce

Linked to the theme of validation is the theme of support, which highlights the mechanisms that facilitate effective LLE work. Participants regularly spoke about the type of support they were receiving or would like to receive and how this could improve their effectiveness and wellbeing. Participants' experiences of support were reflected across several sub‐themes.

Supervision and Clinical Support

Participants described supervision as an important form of reflective practice and a space where they could feel valued. However, they also highlighted the need for more immediate and accessible support, with one indicating that a lack of on‐call clinical support had a negative impact on their wellbeing.

I might speak to a coordinator once every few weeks for an hour, and that's great but that's more of a personal chat … it doesn't feel like there's anything in place when I have profoundly suicidal people on the phone … I take call after call like that and I don't have anyone to debrief with, and it's profoundly impacting my own mental health. (Lisa, lived experience worker, hospital).

Participants highlighted the importance of external supervision to support confidentiality while others highlighted the value of supervision being provided by supervisors with lived experience themselves, which would offer greater understanding and reduce judgement.

I think it's really important to have a supervisor as a lived experience person themselves … There's no judgement. (Michael, lived experience, NGO).

Within the living experience workforce, one participant indicated that external supervision was likely the only place to find a supervisor with shared current living experience (i.e., current experience of substance use).

[There needs to be] more people trained as external supervisors … people who have current living experience … It's really hard to find people to talk to if you're currently using drugs. (Eva, living experience worker, harm reduction).

Training

Many participants also spoke about another form of support, training. Training was seen to enhance role clarity, confidence, and capability and was considered important not only for LLE workers, but also for non‐LLE co‐workers and managers to improve collaboration and understanding. Participants emphasised that training needs to be relevant to LLE work, with some referencing generalised LLE training such as intentional peer support and others highlighting the value of more practical, skills‐based training.

I did a couple of modules for emergency response training, dealing with aggressive people and – yeah, really good. (Taylor, peer worker, community health).

However, some reported limited benefit from the training they received, or noted that it was not relevant to their roles, suggesting a need for further refinement in training content and delivery for LLE workers.

Job Security

Job security was also cited as important for supporting the workforce. Participants discussed a perceived lack of job security, with short contracts, discriminatory policies, changing opinions about the value of LLE work, and unstable funding. Job security was discussed in relation to being able to safely share previous or current drug use without fear of losing work.

Our drug and alcohol stream was going to be chopped off on 30 June. It's always hanging over people's heads. (Taylor, peer worker, community health).

Access to LLE Community

Another area of support was the ability to connect with other LLEs. Participants spoke about the importance of having access to other LLE workers as co‐workers and through communities of practice. Many spoke about the benefit and support provided by other LLE workers, fostering a sense of community and belonging.

… there always should be more than one [LLE] so they can buddy up … debrief with the other … they have similar needs as yourself and can understand the challenges that you may have. (Stephanie, peer worker, residential AOD treatment service).

Similarly, some spoke about the isolation of being the only LLE in a workplace or the desire to have other LLE workers in their workplace:

My issue there is I work within three programs but I'm the only person … I'm the only AOD peer support worker in all of [name redacted]. (Ruth, lived and living experience, community health).

Stress and Workload

Participants described ways they could be better supported to manage work related stress, including more mental health and bereavement leave days, better work‐life balance, and greater emphasis on self‐care.

I find that the standard amount of mental health days is not enough for the work I do … (Pamela, Lived experience, community health).

This topic was particularly relevant in the accounts of living experience workers. One participant highlighted the greater need for bereavement leave for living experience workers, given the higher likelihood of premature death within their networks, among people who use drugs.

… I think I've used all of my sick leave now, for days of grief and funerals and this kind of stuff … (Jennifer, living experience worker, NGO).

Overall, participants described how work‐related stressors, alongside available supports, shaped their experiences of wellbeing, validation and sustainability in LLE roles.

Discussion

The study aimed to understand the experiences of the frontline AOD LLE workforce, including living experience workers, and those in unique settings. Two themes were identified during thematic analysis; Experiences of Validation—participants discussed experiences that signal perceived validation of their roles and lack of validation—and Supporting the Workforce—participants discussed their experiences of factors that support LLE work.

Experiences of Validation

While most participants felt valued by clients, many felt unvalued by their non‐LLE co‐workers. This difference in validation from LLE peers compared to non‐LLE co‐workers may be attributed to social identity theory [24]. The theory posits that people categorise themselves and others into social groups, referred to as in‐groups (i.e., ‘us’) and out‐groups (i.e., ‘them’). The lack of respect or validation may arise from in‐group bias, where individuals favour their own group (e.g., non‐LLE professionals) while devaluing or stereotyping other groups (e.g., LLE workers). Mistreatment and stigmatisation often arise when ‘out‐groups’ are misunderstood or perceived as less competent, important or worthy. One way to address this is by improving non‐LLE colleagues' understanding of LLE roles and their value. The role‐clarity sub‐theme suggested that misunderstandings about the LLE role contributed to several issues raised, including the lack of referrals. Because misinformation is a key driver of stigma and often emerges when people lack access to accurate information [25], providing accurate information about LLE roles and their benefits may be one way of reducing stigma.

Social comparison is also strengthened by social identification [25]. This may explain why the experiences of LLE's in hospitals was particularly negative. Group identification (e.g., as a doctor, nurse, etc.) is likely stronger in the hierarchical setting of a hospital compared to community health settings [18]. Further, the in‐group vs. out‐group dynamic is likely strengthened by policies and practices that separate these workforces such as the example of a participant not being allowed to attend clinical reviews. Adjusting policies and practices to reduce social comparison may improve experiences of validation in the workplace for LLE workers. One area of diversity identified in the LLE worker focus groups, was the experiences of those employed in hospitals.

Lived and Living Experience in Hospital Settings

Participants employed in hospital settings stated that they felt colleagues did not understand what LLE workers do, did not refer patients to LLE workers, and sometimes used stigmatising language or behaviours. In the United States, AOD LLE workers have been predominantly employed in therapeutic communities and other non‐medical organisations for many years [17]. Similarly, in Australia, LLE workers have been employed in therapeutic communities, harm reduction, and other community settings for decades [26]. However, the employment of LLE workers within large multidisciplinary organisations that follow a medical model (e.g., hospitals) is relatively new and increasing, which brings unique challenges [1, 27]. Englander et al. [18] advocated for the adoption of LLE workers in hospital settings, but noted the issues of intense and hierarchical hospital environments. The current study supports this. Hospital participants often spoke of feeling unvalued or misunderstood by co‐workers. This may be due to three factors: (i) hospitals having little experience and immature structures and policies to support LLEs; (ii) hospitals may follow a medical model of care that does not prioritise or integrate LLE support; (iii) hospitals hierarchical environments may contrast with the equality in relationships underpinning much of the LLE philosophies and training [18] and may serve to reinforce social comparison [24]. This social comparison may also be relevant to living experience workers.

‘Living’ Experience Workers

Living experience worker participants spoke about the unique challenges of ongoing substance use. Drug use is highly stigmatised [28], and research suggests that while some people who no longer use drugs still report feelings of shame about their past AOD use, such feelings are more common among those who currently use drugs [29]. Importantly, those who no longer used drugs are viewed as conforming to societal norms. In their influential work Link and Phelan [30] argue that stigma and discrimination arise when differences (e.g., attributes or behaviours) are ‘labelled’—a process where the differences are linked to undesirable characteristics. Stafford and Scott [31, 32] argue that behaviours become stigmatised when they are outside of the ‘norm’, where the ‘norm’ is a shared belief about how someone ‘ought’ to behave. Viewed through these frameworks, the quote from a living experience worker (Sean) that ‘good peers’ are those who ‘conform to social norms’, reflects how ongoing substance use is seen as a deviation from societal norms. In this way, drug use becomes a ‘labelled’ behaviour, associated with undesirable characteristics; thus, engendering stigma. In addition, unlike lived experience workers whose behaviour is not criminalised, living experience workers remain exposed to systemic harms, such as criminalisation, workplace surveillance and professional risk.

Living experience participants also spoke about workplace policies that discriminate against workers who used drugs, for example, staff urine drug screening. The findings also point to how institutional systems selectively legitimise some forms of experiential knowledge while marginalising others, and how the acceptability of lived experience is often contingent upon abstinence, recovery‐oriented narratives, and organisational perceptions of reduced risk. The findings on living experience workers suggest several things: (i) drug reform is needed to ensure that disclosure of current substance use is safe and to reduce stigma from social comparison; (ii) workplace policy needs to consider living experience workers.

Supporting the Workforce

Participants discussed the importance of discipline‐specific supervision and training for LLE workers. These findings were consistent with the findings of previous literature on best practice for employing mental health LLE workers [33] and on barriers faced by LLE workers [9, 11, 12, 14]. Participants argued that supervision needs to be delivered by someone who is themselves an LLE.

Supervision and training are important mechanisms that can be utilised to strengthen role clarity. A lack of role clarity is a commonly identified problem in the LLE workplace [9, 11, 33]. Greater role clarity is associated with better job performance [34] and job satisfaction [35]. However, many participants spoke about a lack of clarity in their roles, in their profession, and from others. Du Plessis et al.'s [9] systematic review of the LLE worker literature found that a lack of role clarity was the most prominent organisational challenge faced by LLE workers, arguing that a lack of role clarity can impede a worker's confidence and perceptions of their competence. Further, Hurley et al. [36] found that a lack of role clarity impacted some mental health LLE workers' own wellbeing. Given the importance of role clarity [34, 35], creating role clarity should be a primary concern of those employing LLEs. du Plessis et al. [9] and Byrne et al. [33] both advocated for discipline specific supervision, conducted by LLEs, as a way to increase role clarity, which was supported by the results of the current study. Finally, the organisational literature more generally advocates for capability frameworks and role descriptions as ways to improve role clarity, create career pathways, and provide foundations for training [37].

Many other supportive practices were discussed by participants including ensuring LLE workers have access to other LLEs, training, fair pay, and greater job security. These practices are consistent with previous literature on best practices in the mental health LLE field [33]. Participants in hospitals spoke about a lack of LLE‐delivered supervision, a lack of referrals, and a poor understanding of LLE work by non‐LLE colleagues. Some hospital participants described how their workplaces had negatively impacted their mental health. Researching the mental health LLE workforce in a multidisciplinary organisation, Byrne et al. [33] suggested that LLEs may be more effective if they were employed by LLE‐run agencies and then assigned to multidisciplinary organisations. Such a proposal may also work for the AOD LLE workforce. Though consideration should be taken to ensure this does not increase social comparisons between LLE and non‐LLE workforces.

The distinction between living and lived experience was not always clear in the study, with some participants identifying as both lived and living or selecting ‘peer’. Indeed, the distinction is not always clear; for example, a worker may identify as having lived experience for one substance but living experience for another substance, they may have had a recent relapse or may prefer not to disclose current drug use. Another limitation is that recruitment relied on a mailing list that may not have reached all AOD LLE workers, although most Victorian AOD services are included on this list and could disseminate the study information to their staff. Additionally, because this study reflects the contemporary context in Victoria, the findings may not be generalisable to other regions within Australia or internationally. Future research could address these limitations by adopting more targeted sampling strategies to ensure representation across diverse service types and geographical locations. Nevertheless, the current study has highlighted that many of the barriers faced by lived experience workers are similar to those cited by living; and has highlighted that feeling unvalued by society and discriminatory policies are commonly cited by living experience workers.

Conclusion

The current study highlights the challenges faced by the LLE workforce that may be underpinned by in‐group and out‐group identity, lack of support structures, a lack of role clarity, and several other factors. Addressing these issues is important for any organisation that employs or intends to employ AOD lived or living experience workers.
