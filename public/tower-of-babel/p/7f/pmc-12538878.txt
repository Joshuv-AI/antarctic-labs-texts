Pre-exposure prophylaxis use and discontinuation among Brazilian gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men: examining the role of internalized homonegativity and discrimination
Authors: Karine M. Barreto, Thiago S. Torres, Lucilene Freitas, Brenda Hoagland, Emília M. Jalil, Valdilea G. Veloso, Beatriz Grinsztejn, Paula M. Luz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a highly effective HIV prevention strategy yet use and persistence among populations at high vulnerability to HIV in Brazil remains suboptimal. We sought to quantify internalized homonegativity and discrimination using multi-item validated instruments and to estimate their associations with PrEP use and discontinuation among gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (MSM).MethodsA cross-sectional, web-based survey was conducted from November 2021 to January 2022, recruiting a convenience sample of MSM through dating apps and social media. The survey assessed sociodemographic characteristics, sexual behavior, substance use, HIV transmission knowledge (using the HIV Knowledge Assessment tool), internalized homonegativity (using the Reactions to Homosexuality Scale), and experiences of discrimination (using the Explicit Discrimination Scale). PrEP use was assessed with response options “I have never used PrEP” (henceforth “never used”), “I am currently using PrEP” (henceforth “current use”), and “I used PrEP in the past but stopped” (henceforth “discontinuation”). Unadjusted and adjusted associations between internalized homonegativity and discrimination and the outcomes a) “current use” of PrEP compared to “never use”, and b) “discontinuation” compared to “current use”, were estimated using logistic regression models.ResultsThe analytic sample consisted of 2,840 participants, with PrEP use patterns distributed as follows: 71.3% had never used PrEP, 22.8% were current users, and 5.9% had discontinued use. Unadjusted analyses indicated negative associations between internalized homonegativity and discrimination scores with current PrEP use (with higher scores leading to decreased odds of “current use”), and positive associations with PrEP discontinuation (higher scores leading to increased odds of “discontinuation”). After adjusting for potential confounders, higher internalized homonegativity was associated with decreased odds of “current use” (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.88, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.78–0.99) per 1 standard deviation [SD] increase in score) and more experiences of discrimination was associated with increased odds of “discontinuation” (aOR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.03–1.50 per 1 SD increase in score).DiscussionOur findings support the hypothesis that psychological distress from self-stigmatization and discrimination undermines PrEP use and persistence. Public health strategies need to integrate stigma-reduction and culturally competent, LGBTQIAPN+-affirming services to support PrEP as an effective prevention strategy.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11742-4.

Background

Tenofovir-based oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is highly effective in preventing HIV among several populations, including gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (MSM). A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that oral PrEP with tenofovir 300 mg plus emtricitabine 200 mg (TDF/FTC) is effective for MSM, with a rate ratio of 0.25, indicating a 75% reduction in HIV incidence compared to placebo or no treatment, and a rate ratio of 0.14 among those with high adherence [1]. Beyond the landmark clinical trial, the iPrEx trial, which first showed a reduction in HIV incidence among MSM and transgender women using daily oral TDF/FTC compared to placebo [2], observational studies have also shown population-level impact [3–5], including in Latin America [6–8]. Long-acting PrEP formulations have also been shown to be effective for MSM and transgender and non-binary persons [9, 10], serving as an important option for those who have difficulty adhering to oral PrEP.

In Brazil, the PrEP Brasil Demonstration Project showed high oral PrEP uptake and adherence among MSM and transgender women, with 74% of participants achieving protective drug levels at week 48, indicating successful delivery within the Brazilian Public Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde [SUS]) [7, 11]. The ImPrEP study, the largest PrEP implementation study in Latin America, corroborated these findings, showing that same day delivery of oral PrEP was feasible with good PrEP persistence [6, 8]. Modeling studies have suggested that oral PrEP is a cost-effective strategy, with a significant reduction in lifetime HIV acquisition risk and an increase in life expectancy among MSM and transgender women at higher vulnerability to HIV [12]. However, since implementation of PrEP as a public health strategy in 2018, national statistics of PrEP use from January 2018 to August 2025 show that 33% of those who received at least one PrEP refill in the past 12 months had discontinued PrEP [13]. Increasing PrEP use could significantly reduce HIV incidence, with a potential 29.7% decrease in incidence if 60% uptake was achieved within 24 months in urban centers like Rio de Janeiro [14]. Thus, while PrEP is effective if taken correctly, PrEP use and persistence are crucial for maximizing the individual and populational protective benefits.

The use of oral PrEP among MSM faces multifaceted challenges spanning individual, interpersonal, social, and structural domains [15–20]. At the individual level, foundational barriers include insufficient awareness and knowledge regarding this prevention tool [21–23]. Furthermore, a range of individual factors influence PrEP uptake, from socio-demographic characteristics (such as age, sexual orientation, race, education level, and income), to attitudes and beliefs (e.g., concerns about PrEP effectiveness), and the perceived attributes of PrEP itself (including actual or anticipated side effects, potential drug interactions, the demands of long-term adherence, and its limitation to HIV prevention without protection against other sexually transmitted infections) [19, 24]. Individual behaviors, notably sexual practices and substance use have also been identified as significant correlates of PrEP engagement [24].

Interpersonal and social barriers frequently manifest through various forms of stigma. Individuals may fear stigmatization from others, driven by perceptions that PrEP use could imply promiscuity or perpetuate existing stigmas associated with homosexuality or HIV. At the structural level, barriers primarily revolve around access. These include the financial cost of PrEP, complexities with insurance coverage, and broader logistical challenges in obtaining the medication. For instance, despite PrEP being provided free-of-charge in over 1230 healthcare settings across Brazil [13], a 2020 web-based survey among MSM and transgender and non-binary persons revealed that a substantial proportion cited lack of knowledge regarding access points or difficulty accessing PrEP as primary reasons for never initiating (one-third of participants) or discontinuing (one-fourth of participants) its use [24].

Corroborating and expanding the interpersonal and social factors cited above, the Minority Stress Model [25, 26] provides a theoretical framework for understanding how stressors arising from social stigma and discrimination disproportionately impact individuals belonging to marginalized groups. Specifically, the model posits that distal stressors such as discrimination and proximal stress processes such as internalized homonegativity may undermine engagement in preventive health behaviors. Internalized homonegativity, defined as the internalization of negative societal attitudes and beliefs about homosexuality, can lead to self-stigmatization and negatively impact mental health and health-seeking behaviors, including PrEP use [24, 27]. Moreover, the stigma associated with HIV and PrEP frequently translates into fears about attending HIV clinics, thereby deterring access to care [28]. Consistent with this framework, experiences of discrimination have also been cited as barriers to PrEP use [17, 18, 29, 30].

Guided by the Minority Stress Model, we investigated whether higher levels of internalized homonegativity or more experiences of discrimination were associated with (a) lower PrEP use and (b) greater PrEP discontinuation. We hypothesized that higher internalized homonegativity and greater exposure to discrimination would be negatively associated with PrEP use and positively associated with PrEP discontinuation. To test these hypotheses, we quantified internalized homonegativity and discrimination using multi-item validated instruments and estimated their associations with PrEP use and discontinuation in regression models while adjusting for the most important factors associated with these outcomes as suggested by current literature.

Methods

Study design

A convenience sample of MSM recruited through dating apps (Grindr, Hornet and Scruff) and social media (Facebook and Instagram) from November 2021 to January 2022 completed a cross-sectional, web-based survey. The open survey was administered via Alchemer® (https://www.alchemer.com/). Requests for voluntary survey completion were sent through direct message inbox for Hornet, banners for Scruff and Grindr, and boosted posts for Facebook and Instagram, following approaches conducted in prior studies [23, 24, 31]. The study instrument was written in Portuguese (an English translation is provided in the Supplementary Material). Respondents were able to change/review answers and did not receive compensation for participation, as per Brazilian regulations. Usability and technical functionality in different personal computers and mobile phones’ operating systems were checked before survey administration. Only one response per internet protocol address was allowed.

Study population

Upon providing electronic informed consent, participants were asked to verify their age as 18 years or older before proceeding to the study’s questionnaire. Inclusion criteria included (a) identification as cisgender man, transgender woman or man, or non‑binary person, (b) report of sex with men, and (c) residence in Brazil. Exclusion criteria were (a) currently living with HIV, (b) no male sexual partner in the past year, (c) lack of awareness about oral PrEP, (d) an incorrect answer to any attention questions [32], and (e) duplicated I. P. address (the first participation was retained). We also applied additional exclusion criteria in order to target this analysis to persons who would have been eligible for PrEP based on Brazilian guidelines at the time of the study (report of any of the following within the past six months: a) condomless anal intercourse, b) male sexual partner with HIV, c) engagement in transactional sex, or d) diagnosis of a sexually transmitted infection). It is noteworthy that in mid-2022, Brazilian guidelines were updated to recommend that PrEP eligibility and HIV exposure assessments be individualized, considering personal sexual practices, partnerships, social dynamics, and contexts of increased vulnerability. Lastly, for this analysis, because the instrument we used to measure internalized homonegativity, the Reactions to Homosexuality Scale, was validated among gay, bisexual, and other cisgender men who have sex with man [33], we excluded participants not identifying as cisgender man.

Variables

Outcome variables: Prep use and prep discontinuation

Participants were first asked about PrEP awareness (yes, no). Among those reporting awareness, PrEP use was assessed with the possible response options “I have never used PrEP” (henceforth “never used”), “I am currently using PrEP” (henceforth “current use”), and “I used PrEP in the past but stopped” (henceforth “discontinuation”). Additionally, participants reporting “never used” and participants reporting “discontinuation” were asked about their reasons for never using and stopping, respectively.

Main exposure variables

Internalized homonegativity

Internalized homonegativity was measured using the Reactions to Homosexuality Scale [34], which was validated among Brazilian gay, bisexual, and other cisgender men who have sex with men [33] (please refer to [33] for a discussion of the scale’s unidimensionality). It includes seven items (example items include: “I feel comfortable discussing homosexuality in a public situation” and “Homosexuality is as natural as heterosexuality”) that are measured on a 7-point Likert scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Total scores range from 0 − 42. Higher scores indicate higher internalized homonegativity. The Reactions to Homosexuality Scale showed good internal consistency in the present sample (k = 7 items, n = 2,840, α = 0.80).

Experiences of discrimination

Experiences of discrimination were measured using the unidimensional 18-items Explicit Discrimination Scale (EDS), which measures experiences of day-to-day discrimination [35]. The EDS assesses perceived discrimination in 18 different circumstances and exhibits robust psychometric properties [35–37]. For each of the 18 circumstances, participants responded to four questions. Question 1 inquired participants about experiences of negative differential treatment, without defining a recall period. For example, item 1 asks: “Have you ever been mistaken for an employee of an establishment, when you were actually a customer?” [36]. Response options across the 18 items were as follows (response [coding]): never [0], occasionally [1], frequently [2], always [3]. Questions 2 and 3 address the primary and other reasons attributed to the negative differential treatment (including gender, sexual orientation, social class, weight, among others). Question 4 asked participants to indicate if the negative differential treatment was perceived by them as discrimination (yes, no). If the participant answered yes to Question 4, responses to question 1, across the 18-items, were summed up yielding the participant’s discrimination score, which could range from 0 to 54; higher scores indicated more experiences of discrimination. The EDS showed good internal consistency in the present sample (k = 18 items, n = 2840, α = 0.86).

Other variables

Sociodemographic characteristics

Participants were asked about sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, sexual orientation, race, education level, income, and Brazilian state of residence and if the city of residence was a state’s capital or metropolitan region. Gender was collected with the following response options: man, cisgender man, transgender man, woman, cisgender woman, transgender woman, travesti, and non-binary. Options “man” and “cisgender man” were grouped together as “cisgender man”, likewise “woman” and “cisgender woman” were grouped together as “cisgender woman”. Travesti is a term used in Brazil that mostly refers to individuals assigned as male at birth and have feminine gender expression. Sexual orientation was collected as gay or homosexual, bisexual, heterosexual and other (e.g., pansexual, asexual, or demisexual). Race was classified according to the categories defined by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (Instituto Brasileiro de Geografia e Estatística, IBGE) as Asian or of Asian descent, Black or of African descent, Indigenous, Pardo which includes people who identify as mixed race or multiracial, and White or of European descent. Education level was categorized as primary, secondary, tertiary, and higher than tertiary. Monthly income was asked in relation to the number of minimum wages, one minimum wage was BRL1,212 in 2021, equivalent to USD 212. Brazilian state of residence was collected and grouped according to the country’s administrative region.

Sexual behavior

Sexual behavior questions referred to the prior 6 months and included number of partners (1–5, 6–10, 11–30, 31–100, > 100), condomless anal sex (yes, no), and number of partners living with HIV (0, 1, 2 or more). Participants were asked if they had been diagnosed with syphilis, urethral or anal gonorrhea and/or chlamydia in the past six months (yes, no). Participants were also asked about engagement in transactional sex (defined as having sex in exchange for money or other goods, e.g. gifts, housing, etc.) in the past six months. Lastly, participants were asked if they had a steady partner (yes, no).

Substance use

Chemsex was defined as the self-reported use of any of the following substances before or during sexual activity: cocaine; crack, basic paste, or oxycodone; marijuana, hashish, or skunk; ecstasy; methamphetamines (crystal or speed); gamma hydroxybutyrate or gamma butyrolactone (GHB/GBL); poppers; other inhalants; mephedrone; or hallucinogens (e.g., lysergic acid diethylamide [LSD], mushroom tea). Participants were also asked about the specific use of alcohol or poppers before or during sex (yes/no). Binge drinking was assessed through the third question of the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT-C), with participants indicating having consumed six or more drinks at once (yes, no). All substance use questions referred to the preceding six months.

Knowledge of HIV transmission, prevention and treatment

HIV knowledge was measured using the HIV knowledge assessment tool [21, 38, 39], which contains 12 statements about the transmission and prevention of HIV, with response options “true,” “false,” and, “I don’t know.” Correct responses were scored as 1 and incorrect or “I don’t know” responses as 0, with a total score range of 0–12. Higher scores indicate higher levels of HIV knowledge.

Statistical analysis

The study population was described overall and by PrEP use, categorized into three mutually exclusive groups (never used, current use, and discontinuation), using counts and percentages. Group differences were assessed with Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables and, for continuous variables, groups’ means were compared with one-way ANOVA.

To examine the role of internalized homonegativity and discrimination on PrEP use and PrEP discontinuation, we first estimated their unadjusted associations with the outcomes and then included both variables in an adjusted model that also included the other explanatory variables identified through literature as important correlates for PrEP use or discontinuation. Two regression analyses were undertaken. One logistic regression model considered the outcome “current use” of PrEP compared to “never used”, for this analysis, participants who reported PrEP “discontinuation” were excluded. The other logistic regression model considered the outcome “discontinuation” compared to “current use”, for this analysis, participants who reported “never used” were excluded.

The sociodemographic variables included in the adjusted analyses were age, sexual orientation, race, education level and income. Sexual behavior-related variables were having a steady partner, number of partners, unprotected anal sex, having partners living with HIV, STI diagnosis and transactional sex. Variables related to substance use included binge drinking, chemsex, use of alcohol and use of poppers during sex. Finally, living in a capital city or metropolitan region and the HIV knowledge score were also included. The continuous variables (HIV knowledge score, internalized homonegativity score and discrimination score) were standardized to z-scores for inclusion in regression models. All analyses were performed using R project version 4.3.2.

Ethics

This study received approval from the human subjects’ ethics committee at the Instituto Nacional de Infectologia Evandro Chagas of the Fundação Oswaldo Cruz (#CAAE 01777918.0.0000.5262) and was conducted according to the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki. All study participants provided electronic informed consent before survey initiation. No personally identifiable information was collected, except for I.P. address.

Results

A total of 9,527 people provided electronic informed consent, reported as 18 years of age or older and were directed to the questionnaire (Fig. 1). Five hundred and thirty-nine did not meet inclusion criteria (most, N = 515, due to never having had sex with men). Four thousand and ninety-one were excluded, most because they reported living with HIV (N = 1,927), had no male sexual partner in the prior year (N = 568), or incorrectly answered any of the three attention questions (N = 1,240). Out of 4,897 eligible participants for the present analysis, 1,988 (40.6%) did not meet PrEP eligibility criteria and 69 participants did not identify as cisgender man. Finally, 2,840 participants were included in the analysis, of which 2,026 (71.3%) had never used PrEP, 648 (22.8%) were currently using PrEP, and 166 (5.9%) had discontinued PrEP.

When comparing the three study groups defined according to PrEP use (“never used”, “current use”, and “discontinuation”) differences were observed for some sociodemographic and behavioral variables (Table 1). Compared to the other two groups, “current users” were older, more likely to report White race, to have post-tertiary education, and to earn more than 10 minimum wages per month (20.8% compared to 11.5% and 15.7% of “never used” and “discontinuation”, respectively). “Current users” reported more partners in the past six months, whereas the frequency of condomless anal sex was very similar across groups. “Current users” were less likely to report transactional sex in the past six month and more likely to report chemsex or use of poppers in the same period compared to the other two groups. Binge drinking was similar across groups. Internalized homonegativity and discrimination scores were lower among “current users” compared to the other two groups.

Among participants who reported “never used” PrEP (N = 2,026), the most cited reasons for not using were “I do not know where I can get PrEP” (N = 742, 36.6%), “I worry about the side effects of PrEP” (N = 375, 18.5%), and “I use other substances (licit or illicit drugs, alcohol, hormones)” (N = 271, 13.4%). Among participants who reported PrEP “discontinuation” (N = 166), the two most cited reasons for discontinuing were “I was not correctly taking PrEP” (N = 42, 25.3%) and “I feared that PrEP would not work because I use alcohol or drugs” (N = 42, 25.3%).

Outcome variable: Current use of PrEP

In unadjusted analyses, internalized homonegativity score was associated with a significant decreased odds of “current use” of PrEP (unadjusted odds ratio [uOR] per 1 standard deviation [SD] increase in score 0.73, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.66–0.81) (Table 2). Likewise, higher discrimination score was also associated with decreased the odds of “current use” of PrEP (uOR per 1 SD increase 0.88, 95%CI 0.80–0.97). After adjusting for other variables, the effect size of both variables weakened and only internalized homonegativity score remained significantly associated with “current use” of PrEP (adjusted OR [aOR] per 1 SD increase 0.88, 95%CI 0.78–0.99).

Among the adjustment variables, participants aged 35 to 44 years (compared to participants aged 30 to 34 years), having tertiary education (compared to less than tertiary education), and living in a capital city or metropolitan region (versus not) were associated with increased odds of “current use” of PrEP. Outcome variable: PrEP discontinuation

In unadjusted analyses, internalized homonegativity score was associated with a significant increased odds of “discontinuation” of PrEP (uOR per 1 SD increase 1.29, 95%CI 1.09–1.54) as was the discrimination score (uOR per 1 SD increase 1.26, 95%CI 1.09–1.46) (Table 2). After adjusting for other variables, the effect size of the internalized homonegativity score weakened and was no longer statistically significant: internalized homonegativity score (aOR per 1 SD increase 1.23, 95%CI 1.00-1.52). In contrast, higher discrimination score was significantly associated with increased odds of “discontinuation” of PrEP (aOR per 1 SD increase 1.24, 95%CI 1.01–1.47) (Table 2).

Among the adjustment variables, participants aged 35 to 44 years (compared to participants aged 30 to 34 years) and living in a capital city or metropolitan region (versus not) were associated with decreased odds of “discontinuation” of PrEP. Additionally, higher number of sexual partners and use of poppers significantly decreased the odds of “discontinuation” of PrEP.

Discussion

We investigated whether higher levels of internalized homonegativity or more experiences of discrimination were associated with (a) lower PrEP use and (b) greater PrEP discontinuation. Though higher internalized homonegativity and more experiences of discrimination were negatively associated with PrEP use in unadjusted analyses, in the adjusted analysis only internalized homonegativity remained significantly associated with decreased odds of PrEP use. We also found that higher internalized homonegativity and more experiences of discrimination were associated with increased “discontinuation” of PrEP in unadjusted analysis though in the adjusted analysis, only more experiences of discrimination were associated with increased odds of discontinuation of PrEP.

The association of internalized homonegativity scores with “current use” of PrEP were consistent in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses. These findings support the notion that internalized negative beliefs about homosexuality can act as a barrier to engaging with HIV prevention strategies [40]. Internalized homonegativity can affect the willingness to engage with HIV prevention strategies, including PrEP, due to feelings of shame or fear of being judged. It can also lead to self-stigmatization and negatively impact mental health and health-seeking behaviors [41]. A 2021 qualitative study conducted among Brazilian gay men who had recently discovered to be living with HIV, cited that participants commonly perceived widespread serophobia and homophobia in the public and among health professionals, including staff in Brazilian services dedicated to caring for people living with HIV [42]. Individual-level interventions, such as support groups and counseling, and population-level interventions promoting social inclusion may be beneficial in promoting PrEP use and persistence. Addressing and combating homophobia can significantly decrease stigma, fostering a more inclusive and supportive environment that may improve the overall well-being and quality of life for LGBTQIAPN + individuals.

We found that more experiences of discrimination were associated with increased odds of “discontinuation” of PrEP. In Brazil, LGBTQIAPN + populations systematically suffer from intersectional discrimination, in which factors like sexuality, gender identity, race, and socioeconomic status intertwine to exacerbate exclusion and stigma [43]. Experiences of discrimination, whether in healthcare settings or daily life, can create significant barriers to accessing and adhering to PrEP by discouraging individuals from using PrEP due to a reluctance to engage with systems perceived as discriminatory or for fear of further stigmatization [16, 44]. Indeed, prior studies form Brazil have shown that racial groups are systematically discriminated in the healthcare setting, with Black persons reporting higher perceived difficulty in accessing health services [45, 46]. Other important research focused on people living with HIV found that HIV-specific healthcare settings were not free from stigma, especially among key populations [47]. In a study among people living with HIV, 50% of MSM reported experiences of discrimination in health care settings compared to 29% of heterosexual men [48]. Taken together, these findings suggest that even among individuals who manage to access health services, experiences of discrimination lived in health-care settings may hinder persistent engagement. Considering that adult men are significantly less likely to seek health care or to have a medical consultation [49], access to PrEP may well have been their first encounter with the healthcare setting – meaning that they were never previously exposed to discriminatory experiences occurring in the healthcare setting. However, engagement with the PrEP program requires repeated visits to healthcare services and may, consequently, expose these individuals to discriminatory experiences which lead them to discontinue PrEP. Future longitudinal studies among PrEP users could investigate this hypothesis by measuring the timing and location of discriminatory experiences and the likelihood of subsequent discontinuation of PrEP.

Corroborating a previous study conducted among MSM from Brazil [24], higher HIV knowledge scores were associated with increased odds of “current use” of PrEP. This finding emphasizes the importance of education and awareness campaigns. Individuals with a better understanding of HIV transmission, prevention, and treatment are more likely to recognize their risk and take proactive steps to protect themselves. Though PrEP has been provided in Brazil within its National Health System since 2018, our findings suggest that there is a need for targeted educational interventions that address misconceptions about HIV and PrEP and provide accurate information about the benefits and risks of PrEP. Sexual education should be promoted at early age at schools in Brazil, as the number of new HIV cases in Brazil continue to rise among youth, especially adolescent and young men [50–52]. Our results showed that one of the main reasons cited for not using and for discontinuing PrEP was the use of other substances (licit and illicit), evidencing the need to educate those who seek and use PrEP that the use of substances, though not encouraged, is not a contraindication.

Our results also corroborate prior literature on the association of sexual behavior and substance use variables and the use or discontinuation of PrEP. The association between a higher number of sexual partners and increased odds of “current use” and decreased odds of “discontinuation” likely reflects a heightened perception of HIV risk among individuals with more frequent sexual encounters [53, 54], motivating them to seek PrEP. Conversely, the observed link between transactional sex and decreased “current use” may indicate a complex interplay of factors, including socioeconomic vulnerabilities, limited access to healthcare, and potentially, a lower perceived self-efficacy in managing sexual health risks. The positive association between chemsex and use of poppers with “current use” suggests that individuals engaging in these behaviors may be more aware of their vulnerability to HIV and thus actively seek PrEP to reduce their risk, a finding already reported in prior studies [55, 56]. Furthermore, use of poppers was also strongly associated with decreased odds of PrEP “discontinuation”. Together, these findings suggest that rather than indicating a propensity for not using or abandoning PrEP use, participants reporting use of poppers may have a heightened health consciousness prompting them to actively seek and consistently maintain PrEP as a harm reduction strategy. In other words, use of poppers seems to be associated with a proactive approach to sexual health, indicating that individuals who use them are highly engaged in their health management.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional study design limits our ability to assess temporality between exposures and the outcomes. Second, the use of a convenience sample recruited through dating apps and social media may limit the generalizability of the findings. Our population was predominantly composed of gay, bisexual, and other cisgender men who have sex with men which hindered our ability to address the particularities of the lived experiences of other sexual and gender minorities. Qualitative studies could provide valuable insights into the similarities and differences of the lived experiences of MSM and transgender and non-binary persons regarding PrEP use and discontinuation. Third, the reliance on self-reported data may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of the responses. Fourth, the study population was limited to participants who were eligible for PrEP as per Brazilian guidelines at the time of the study recruitment, which may further limit the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

This study examined the specific roles of internalized homonegativity and experiences of discrimination in the dynamics of PrEP engagement. We found that higher internalized homonegativity was associated with decreased odds of “current use” and that experiences of discrimination were associated with increased odds “discontinuation”, thereby supporting the hypothesis that psychological distress stemming from self-stigmatization and exposure to discrimination impacts an individual’s ability to engage with this preventive health intervention. The analysis also indicated that a higher number of sexual partners and the use of poppers were associated with increased odds of “current use” and decreased odds of “discontinuation”, suggesting that certain lifestyle factors might act as facilitators for continued engagement in a PrEP program. From a public health perspective, these findings indicate that PrEP implementation strategies need integrated interventions that extend beyond merely addressing barriers to access. Community-level interventions aimed at reducing societal stigma and discrimination against LGBTQIAPN + individuals, fostering environments of greater acceptance and inclusion, particularly in healthcare settings, are imperative for cultivating culturally competent and stigma-free services. A deeper exploration into the specific role of healthcare providers and the micro-level interactions within healthcare settings is essential to understand how these dynamics could facilitate rather than impede PrEP persistence.
