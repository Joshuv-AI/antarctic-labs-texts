Sirenomelia and the Spectrum of Caudal Anomalies: Clinical Reflections From a Preterm Termination
Authors: Sajjad Ahmed Khan, Bibisa Bhandari, Saurav Jha, Aron Neupane, Kshitiz Parajuli, Sweta Singh, Aditya Duhan, Fahad Farooq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSevere oligohydramnios with fetal anomalies, like sirenomelia, requires early diagnosis to prevent maternal complications such as infection or hemorrhage from delayed detection. Challenges in resource‐limited settings hinder timely intervention, emphasizing the need for improved screening and counseling to optimize outcomes and future pregnancy planning.

Introduction

Sirenomelia, alternatively known as mermaid syndrome, is a congenital anomaly characterized by apparent fusion of the legs into a single lower limb, which is associated with a variety of visceral abnormalities incompatible with life [1]. It is an extremely uncommon occurrence with a prevalence of 0.8–1 in 100,000 neonates. About 300 cases of this lethal congenital malformation have been reported to date. The affected infant resembles a mermaid with a human‐like head and trunk and a fish‐like tail [2]. Without any known etiology, failure of the extremity bud to separate from the primordial cells leading to inability to rotate during the organogenesis period is deemed a possible mechanism. The defects develop in the early period of vascular development primarily due to a developmental anomaly in the veins feeding the lower extremities and an anomaly in mesodermal cell migration [3]. More than half of sirenomelia cases end with stillbirth, and those born alive usually die within a day or two of birth owing to complications associated with a genitourinary, cardiac, respiratory, neurologic, or gastrointestinal‐associated malformation [4]. We report a case of a singleton baby with sirenomelia, that is, mermaid syndrome, born to a 25‐year‐old mother from Western Nepal.

Case Presentation

Case History/Examination

A 25‐year‐old primigravida presented to the emergency department of Manipal Teaching Hospital on 24th March 2021 at 34+ weeks of gestation by last menstrual period, with amenorrhea for 8 months. She had been a booked case at another private hospital and was referred to this center for pregnancy termination due to a reduced amniotic fluid index (AFI) of 1 cm, indicating severe oligohydramnios. The patient reported perceiving adequate fetal movements and had no significant complaints throughout her pregnancy. There was no history of per vaginal (PV) bleeding, PV discharge, PV leakage, lower abdominal pain, headache, blurring of vision, or epigastric pain.

During the course of her pregnancy, she had been taking iron and calcium tablets as advised and had received two doses of Tetanus Toxoid vaccination. She had regular menstrual cycles of 28 days, lasting 3 to 4 days, with a history of dysmenorrhea. The patient had no history of Diabetes Mellitus, Hypertension, thyroid disorders, or any other significant past medical or surgical history. There was no history of exposure to drugs or teratogens during pregnancy. She was a nonsmoker and did not consume alcohol. Additionally, there was no family history of congenital birth defects or history of consanguinity. There was also no relevant occupational or environmental exposure, including contact with heavy metals such as lead or mercury, that could have contributed to the anomaly.

Her general examination was normal, with stable vital signs and a normal systemic examination. Abdominal examination revealed a uterus consistent with a size of 26 weeks, in a longitudinal lie with cephalic presentation. The fetal heart rate was 144 bpm, measured with Doppler. Per vaginal examination showed a closed internal os, with a firm, central, and uneffaced cervix.

Ultrasonography (USG) revealed a single live fetus with a calculated gestational age of 29 weeks and 2 days, in cephalic presentation with posterior placentation. It also showed an AFI of 1 cm (severe oligohydramnios) and a cystic lesion measuring 3.4 × 1.6 cm in the coccygeal region, suggestive of a teratoma or meningocele. The estimated fetal weight (EFW) was 1300 g. Together with that, classic features of sirenomelia were observed, including fused bilateral lower limbs and absence of genitalia. Additionally, the ultrasound showed abnormal abdominal vasculature consistent with a single umbilical artery and likely renal agenesis, which contributed to the severe oligohydramnios.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Given the clinical presentation and ultrasound findings, a comprehensive diagnostic approach was undertaken to evaluate the fetus and determine appropriate management. The primary concern was the severe oligohydramnios, indicated by an AFI of 1 cm, which significantly limits the diagnostic accuracy of USG but is also a known feature associated with certain congenital anomalies and poor fetal outcomes. A cystic lesion measuring 3.4 × 1.6 cm in the coccygeal region raised suspicions of either a sacrococcygeal teratoma or meningocele. However, considering the presence of severe oligohydramnios and abnormal fetal morphology, a broader range of differential diagnoses was considered, including sirenomelia, caudal regression syndrome, limb–body wall complex, VACTERL association, congenital limb deficiencies, Potter sequence, and congenital talipes equinovarus.

The diagnostic process included a detailed sonographic evaluation, which revealed a single live fetus in cephalic presentation with posterior placentation and a gestational age corresponding to 29 weeks and 2 days. Despite the reduced amniotic fluid limiting sonographic clarity, key abnormalities were identified, including fusion of the lower limbs and the presence of a cystic mass. No genitalia were visualized, and fetal growth restriction was suspected with an EFW of 1300 g. These findings were communicated clearly and compassionately to the patient and her family. Genetic counseling and a discussion of the possible underlying syndromes and poor prognosis were provided. The potential for fetal non‐viability and postnatal complications, even in the event of a live birth, was thoroughly discussed.

After extensive counseling and with informed consent, the decision was made to proceed with the medical termination of pregnancy. The patient was administered Mifepristone followed by Misoprostol in accordance with standard obstetric protocols for second‐trimester termination. The regimen involved the administration of two doses of Misoprostol over the course of 2 days. Close monitoring was carried out throughout the induction process to observe maternal vital signs and uterine activity, and to ensure patient comfort and safety.

Following the induction process, the patient delivered vaginally a preterm fetus weighing 1.5 kg with an APGAR score of 0 at 1 and 5 min, confirming intrauterine fetal demise or immediate neonatal death. Results and Conclusion (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient underwent termination of pregnancy using Mifepristone followed by Misoprostol. After two doses of Misoprostol over the course of 2 days, she delivered vaginally a fetus with a birth weight of 1.5 kg. The APGAR score was 0/10 at 1 min and 0/10 at 5 min, indicating poor fetal viability and immediate death after birth. A detailed fetal examination revealed multiple congenital anomalies, including absent genitalia, features suggestive of Potter facies (low‐set ears, a beaked nose, and prominent epicanthic folds), and fused bilateral lower limbs and feet, with a distinct plane of fusion (Symelia dipus). The fused feet were rotated, with a total of 10 toes, aligned abnormally and resembling fins. This led to the final diagnosis of sirenomelia.

The mother was monitored for 2 days postpartum. At the time of discharge, her general condition was fair, vital signs were stable, and her abdomen was soft and nontender. She was prescribed antibiotics and advised to follow up in 6 weeks. Additionally, she was instructed to monitor her temperature, pulse, and respiratory rate twice daily during the puerperium, to use sterile vulval pads, and to be alert for any PV bleeding, seeking immediate medical attention if any complications arose.

Discussion

Sirenomelia, also called the mermaid syndrome, is due to a caudal blastemal defect with the persistence of the vitelline artery [5, 6]. Vascular steal hypothesis, defective blastogenesis, and the pressure wave theory are some hypothesized causes. It is the most severe form of Caudal Regression Syndrome [7]. This condition is more common in monozygotic twins and males, with approximately 300 cases recorded in the literature so far [4]. Sirenomelia is a rare and lethal congenital deformity with an incidence of one in 60,000–70,000 pregnancies affecting multiple systems. Maternal age below 20 years and above 40 years are some of the important risk factors for this anomaly, along with maternal diabetes and heavy metal exposure [8, 9].

Methylergonovine maleate, phenobarbital, antibiotic trimethoprim, and misoprostol are the teratogens associated with the condition. Successful induction of sirenomelia by administration of cadmium and lead in the golden hamster was also reported in experimental studies [8]. Sirenomelia can be identified as soon as the first trimester using USG by careful examination of lower limbs. Amniotic fluid is enough in the first trimester to allow any detection of abnormal lower limbs, undetermined external genitalia, anorectal atresia, and lumbosacral agenesis.

Oligohydraminos is a sonographic marker of absent or nonfunctioning kidneys from the second half onwards and, along with bilateral renal agenesis, malformed lower limbs, and a single umbilical artery, can help in early diagnosis.

Infantogram after birth reveals bony defects and helps in the classification. Postmortem autopsy establishes absence or presence of abnormalities of internal organs [10]. In our case, the diagnosis was made postnatally following a clinical examination of the newborn.

Intrauterine diagnosis of sirenomelia is possible as early as week 22 of pregnancy. Color Doppler imaging during pregnancy is useful because it can help with diagnosis, particularly when bilateral renal agenesis is present. The Doppler flow image shows the umbilical cord with two vessels alone [11].

Early diagnosis helps to terminate the pregnancy at an early age with least discomfort to the mother as it is always a fatal condition with deaths occurring within 1–2 days after birth [12].

In Nepal, few cases have been documented, highlighting the rare occurrence and poor prognosis of this condition. An 18‐year‐old primigravida at 34 weeks and 5 days of gestation underwent preterm vaginal delivery and delivered a live baby with a weight of 1250 g, with multiple congenital anomalies like Potter facies, dolichocephaly, single umbilical artery, absent external genitalia, imperforate anus, and fused lower limbs and fused feet, and a poor Apgar score leading to death after 7 h of life [12]. Another case from the Far‐Western part of Nepal involved a sirenomelia baby born to a 21‐year‐old female at 41 weeks of gestation with features of caudal dysgenesis, having fusion of lower limbs and no toes at all, with no identifiable external genitalia and anus. The neonate died 3 h after delivery due to respiratory distress [13].

In a few exceptional cases, infants have been able to survive and live for many years. The longest living documented case of sirenomelia survival belongs to Tiffany Yorks, who lived for 27 years. She successfully separated her legs via surgery before her first birthday. On another note, Shiloh Pepin, who had her lower extremities fused and lacked a bladder, uterus, and rectum, lived 10 years without any surgical interventions to separate her limbs and possessed only a quarter of a kidney and one ovary. Milagros Cerron, the “Mermaid lady” from Peru, has existed for more than 14 years now and has undergone limb separation in two surgical procedures [12].

Delayed diagnosis of sirenomelia can lead to significant maternal complications, both physical and psychological. Physically, continuing a pregnancy with a lethal anomaly may increase risks such as uterine overdistension, intrauterine infection, prolonged labor, postpartum hemorrhage, and the need for surgical intervention. Psychologically, late diagnosis often causes emotional trauma, including anxiety, guilt, grief, and even post‐traumatic stress, particularly when families are unprepared for the diagnosis or outcome. In this case, the diagnosis was made only after referral for severe oligohydramnios in the third trimester, limiting the window for timely counseling and intervention. From a systemic perspective, several barriers may have contributed to the delayed diagnosis. These include limited availability of anomaly scans in the first trimester, inadequate training in detecting rare fetal anomalies, socioeconomic constraints preventing early or frequent antenatal visits, and fragmented referral systems between private and public healthcare sectors. In many low‐resource settings, reliance on basic USG and inconsistent follow‐up further impedes timely diagnosis. To address these challenges, the authors suggest strengthening early antenatal screening programs, integrating low‐cost training modules on rare congenital anomalies for primary‐level providers, and improving referral pathways through standardized national guidelines. Enhancing public health education and subsidizing first‐trimester scans for at‐risk populations may also help reduce delays in diagnosis and mitigate maternal morbidity and emotional distress.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of early antenatal detection of sirenomelia, a rare and fatal congenital anomaly. Delayed diagnosis, as seen here where the condition was identified post‐termination for severe oligohydramnios, can lead to increased maternal morbidity and emotional distress. In low‐resource settings, limited access to detailed prenatal imaging remains a challenge. Therefore, strengthening early screening protocols and improving diagnostic capabilities—especially in the first trimester—are essential to enable timely intervention, informed decision‐making, and reduce the physical and psychological burden on affected families.
