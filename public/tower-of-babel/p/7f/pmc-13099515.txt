Optimizing landing mechanics to modulate patellar tendon loading: An individualized moment arm analysis
Authors: Bojie Xuan, Yang Song, Dong Sun, Fengping Li, Shunxiang Gao, Qiaolin Zhang, Xuanzhen Cen, Liangliang Xiang, Zixiang Gao, Bálint Kovács, Gusztáv Fekete, Yaodong Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySingle-leg landing (SL) imposes substantial mechanical demand on the patellar tendon, with peak patellar tendon force (PPTF) serving as a key metric for characterizing the internal mechanical environment of the tendon. This study integrates 3D modeling with high-resolution in vivo kinematics to quantify the patellar tendon moment arm (PTMA) and the PPTF, examining their biomechanical correlations and neuromuscular features. Minimal sex-related PTMA differences suggest comparable anatomical leverage during knee flexion across both sexes. In both sexes, PPTF was significantly positively correlated with the knee flexion angle at initial contact (IC) and significantly negatively correlated with the knee range of motion (ROM). Muscle network analysis showed lower clustering coefficients in high-frequency versus low-frequency bands. Reduced IC knee flexion and increased ROM attenuate patellar tendon mechanical demand. By incorporating individualized moment-arm analysis, this study provides a biomechanical basis for understanding patellar tendon loading during landing.Subject areas: biomechanics, sports medicine

Introduction

Patellar tendon injuries, particularly patellar tendinopathy, are among the most prevalent overuse injuries in sports that involve repetitive jumping and landing, such as basketball and volleyball.1,2 These conditions often lead to chronic pain, prolonged functional impairment, and even premature retirement from competitive sport.3 Single-leg landing (SL) is a common yet critical maneuver in these sports and requires precise neuromuscular control to achieve effective impact attenuation and efficient load transfer through the kinetic chain.4 Inadequate control during this task can increase mechanical loading across lower-limb joints. Specifically, a greater knee flexion angle at initial contact (IC) during SL has been associated with increased patellar tendon loading during landing.5,6

Within the sequential impact-transmission pathway from the foot to the ankle, knee, and hip, the knee serves as a pivotal site for energy absorption and moment distribution.7 When neuromuscular control is deficient or coordination among the knee extensor muscles is suboptimal, the efficiency of energy dissipation declines.8 This decline can increase tension in the quadriceps and patellar tendons and elevate patellofemoral joint reaction force. Consequently, greater mechanical stress is imposed on the patellar tendon, increasing tendon loading demand. Furthermore, insufficient neuromuscular control may compromise effective load distribution across the lower limb, which can further amplify this localized stress, particularly during high-intensity or repetitive landing activities.9 To objectively quantify this demand, peak patellar tendon force (PPTF) has been established as a key biomechanical metric for evaluating landing mechanics. Accurate quantification of PPTF is crucial, since excessive mechanical loading has been widely discussed as an important factor associated with tendon pathology.10 Therefore, characterizing the tendon mechanical environment can provide crucial insights into biomechanical conditions that may be relevant to injury development.

From a biomechanical perspective, PPTF is governed by the net sagittal knee moment and the patellar tendon moment arm (PTMA), which represents the mechanical leverage of the knee extensor mechanism.11 Current approaches for estimating PTMA, however, often rely on population-averaged curves or cadaveric data; these approaches fail to account for individual variation in anatomy and dynamic posture and may therefore introduce systematic bias in PPTF and mechanical loading estimates. Although methods such as the instantaneous helical axis have improved PTMA measurement accuracy, substantial inter-individual differences in femoral/tibial geometry and tendon morphology persist.12 Dandridge et al. achieved high-precision PTMA measurements across the full flexion-extension range in cadaveric knees (root mean square [RMS] error: 0.2 mm; maximum error: 0.4 mm), and showed that PTMA was significantly longer in males and in individuals with larger knee-joint volumes, underscoring the importance of personalized modeling.13 Gray et al. further reported that, within the same subject, PTMA remained relatively stable across different functional tasks in the 0°–70° flexion range; yet inter-subject variability could still reach 13% even after normalization for bone size.14 Collectively, these findings indicate that PTMA may be extrapolated across tasks for a given individual. Nevertheless, robust cross-subject comparisons require individualized assessment. By incorporating personalized PTMA evaluation, the present study can provide a more accurate estimation of PPTF, thereby improving the precision of biomechanical load evaluation and strengthening biomechanical interpretation of injury-relevant loading patterns.

PPTF is modulated by multiple biomechanical factors, including sagittal-plane IC angles of the ankle, knee, and hip, joint range of motion (ROM), knee flexion angular velocity, peak joint moments, and peak vertical ground reaction force (PVGRF).15 While previous studies have addressed selected aspects of these parameters, the systematic relationships between PPTF and the above-mentioned set of variables, particularly when inter-individual variability in PTMA is considered, have not been comprehensively investigated. A more integrated analysis of these associations, while accounting for subject-specific PTMA differences, could offer important insights into fundamental distinctions in joint-level control strategies during landing. Furthermore, neuromuscular regulation may play a critical role in controlling mechanical loading on the musculoskeletal system, such as patellar tendon loading, by regulating muscle forces. Although this regulatory mechanism has been recognized across diverse motor tasks, network-level neuromuscular analyses under conditions associated with high patellar tendon demand remain scarce.

Our study integrates computed tomography (CT)/ magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based 3D reconstruction with a dual fluoroscopic imaging system (DFIS) to generate subject-specific femur-patella-tendon models and to capture in vivo knee kinematics at sub-millimeter resolution on a frame-by-frame basis. This combined approach enables more precise determination of PTMA, thereby improving the accuracy of PPTF estimation during SL. In addition, we systematically examine the relationships between PPTF and key lower-limb biomechanical variables. Building on this biomechanical foundation, we further construct functional muscle networks to investigate whether elevated patellar tendon loading is associated with reduced global network efficiency and clustering, thereby probing potential underlying mechanisms from a neuromuscular coordination perspective. Through this multi-level integrated analysis, the study will contribute to the understanding of how patellar tendon loading interacts with movement control across both mechanical and neuromuscular domains.

Accordingly, we address two primary research questions: (1) how do lower-limb biomechanical variables relate to PPTF during SL? and (2) how do inter-individual differences in PTMA influence PPTF? In relation to the second question, it is also an important question whether individualized PTMA explains PPTF more effectively than population-averaged PTMA. Based on these aims, we propose the following hypotheses: (1) PPTF is positively associated with knee flexion angle at IC and with peak joint moments and negatively associated with knee ROM; and (2) within the same individual, PTMA remains consistent across tasks, while personalized PTMA provides stronger explanatory power for PPTF than population-averaged PTMA. Testing these hypotheses is important for understanding biomechanical loading patterns related to patellar tendon injury risk: hypothesis 1 identifies high-loading movement patterns associated with mechanical overload, whereas hypothesis 2 evaluates whether subject-specific anatomical modeling improves quantification of tendon loading demand. Overall, this multi-level framework provides a robust basis for assessing and optimizing landing mechanics.

Relationship between mean PTMA and knee flexion angle

Table 1 summarizes the mean PTMA measured across incremental knee flexion angles. Sex-related differences in PTMA were minimal, indicating similar leverage trends during knee flexion in both female and male participants. Compared with the values of Gray et al.,14 PTMA was generally higher by approximately 1.5–2.3 mm in the present study, with the discrepancy becoming more pronounced at flexion angles exceeding 5°. Across the flexion spectrum, PTMA followed a characteristic pattern: it increased progressively from near extension, reached a peak plateau within the moderate flexion range (45°–55°), then declined slightly (Table 1). Overall, PTMA ranged from 46.2 to 48.5 mm across the defined flexion intervals.

Relationship between PPTF, IC joint angles, and ROM

In females, PPTF showed a significant positive correlation with the IC knee flexion angle (r = 0.55, p < 0.001) (Figure 2A). With respect to ROM, PPTF was positively correlated with ankle ROM (r = 0.49, p = 0.002) and negatively correlated with knee ROM (r = −0.35, p = 0.038) and hip ROM (r = −0.73, p < 0.001). In males, PPTF was significantly positively correlated with the IC knee flexion angle (r = 0.45, p = 0.028) (Figure 2B). Regarding ROM, PPTF was negatively correlated with knee ROM (r = −0.42, p = 0.039).

Relationship between PPTF and lower-limb joint moments

In both females and males, PPTF was significantly positively correlated with peak ankle joint moment (females: r = 0.40, p = 0.017; males: r = 0.70, p < 0.001; Figures 2A and 2B, respectively) and very strongly positively correlated with peak knee joint moment (females: r = 0.94, p < 0.001; males: r = 0.95, p < 0.001).

Relationship between PPTF, knee dynamics, and ground reaction force

PPTF was significantly negatively correlated with PVGRF in both sexes (females: r = −0.62, p < 0.001, Figure 2A; males: r = −0.74, p < 0.001; Figure 2B). In addition, in males, PPTF showed a significant positive correlation with peak knee flexion angular velocity (r = 0.52, p = 0.009, Figure 2B).

Functional muscle network analysis

In the low-frequency band (0–25 Hz), the muscle network displayed robust synchronous connectivity, particularly among VM, rectus femoris (RF), and gluteus medius (GMed) (Figure 3A). The color mapping in Figure 3A reflects this pattern, with darker hues indicating higher spectral coherence and tighter coupling between these muscles. In contrast, in the high-frequency band (25–50 Hz), intermuscular connectivity weakened, the network became more decentralized, and muscle activity shifted toward greater functional independence. Consistent with this pattern, the color distribution in the high-frequency band appeared sparser, with lower coherence values indicating weaker intermuscular connections. In Figure 3B, the low-frequency network (0–25 Hz) exhibited a densely interconnected architecture, characterized by thicker and brighter connecting lines that indicate pronounced neuromuscular synergy. By contrast, in the high-frequency range (25–50 Hz), the connections became noticeably thinner and more dispersed, indicating reduced network-wide synchronization and a shift toward greater functional independence among muscles.

With respect to sex differences, muscle networks in females and males followed similar trends across the low- and high-frequency bands. Significant sex differences (p < 0.001) were observed in the clustering coefficient in both frequency bands. Furthermore, regardless of sex, clustering coefficients in the high-frequency band were substantially lower than those in the low-frequency band (Figure 3C). This pattern suggests looser intermuscular connectivity at higher frequencies, along with a reduction in tightly coupled local subgroups. In contrast, no significant differences (p > 0.05) were detected in global efficiency or network density between the two frequency bands.

Discussion

This study employed an integrated methodological framework that combined subject-specific 3D modeling with high-resolution in vivo kinematics to estimate patellar tendon loading during a dynamic landing task (Figure 1). The primary aims were to quantify this loading and to examine its associations with lower-limb biomechanics and concomitant alterations in neuromuscular coordination, as reflected by functional muscle network topology. Overall, the findings were broadly consistent with our hypotheses. Across both sexes, PPTF was positively associated with the knee flexion angle at IC and with peak ankle and knee joint moments, but negatively associated with knee ROM. These results suggest that, in our cohort, reducing the knee flexion angle at IC and increasing knee joint ROM during the SL phase may help reduce PPTF. Moreover, PTMA showed substantial inter-subject variability. Accordingly, incorporating individualized PTMA captures this critical anatomical variation, thereby providing a more geometrically robust estimation of PPTF compared with using a static population-averaged value.

Regarding PTMA, our findings corroborate those of Gray et al., indicating that within a moderate range of knee flexion, PTMA remains relatively stable for a given individual, yet displays substantial variability across individuals.14 This inter-individual variability underscores the necessity of personalized PTMA assessment for improving the accuracy of joint force estimations derived from inverse dynamics. Furthermore, as Im et al. demonstrated, PTMA may vary dynamically by 3%–26% depending on contraction patterns, and precise measurement of the effective quadriceps moment arm (EQma) substantially enhances the mapping between joint force and joint moment.16 Taken together, these observations reinforce the importance of using both personalized and dynamically informed moment-arm parameters during in vivo investigations of musculoskeletal loading. Our results further indicate that, during knee flexion, PTMA follows a characteristic “increase-peak-decrease” pattern, with mean values ranging from 46.2 to 48.5 mm. Starting from a near-extended position, PTMA gradually increases, peaks at approximately 45°–55° of knee flexion and then declines slightly. Although this pattern aligns with the observations of Gray et al., absolute PTMA values in the present study were consistently 1.5–2.4 mm higher; this discrepancy may stem from differences in participant anatomy, measurement methodologies, or experimental protocols.14 Notably, the consistency in peak position and curve shape supports the notion that, for a given individual, the anatomical PTMA profile is highly robust, whereas its absolute magnitude shows greater inter-individual variability and is also influenced by contextual factors. This further highlights the critical role of personalized PTMA assessment in enhancing the fidelity of biomechanical models in both research and clinical applications. Nevertheless, it is important to emphasize that, although precisely measured PTMA improves the accuracy of patellar tendon force estimation, the resultant PPTF represents a biomechanical demand rather than a direct measure or predictor of tissue pathology.

A significant positive correlation was observed between PPTF and knee flexion angle at IC in both sexes, suggesting that a greater flexion angle at touchdown may increase initial patellar tendon tension. As the knee joint flexes, changes in the patellofemoral joint alignment likely necessitate greater force in the patellar tendon to maintain joint stability, thereby contributing to elevated PPTF.17,18 Conversely, knee ROM was negatively correlated with PPTF in both sexes. This finding indicates that a greater degree of knee flexion during the landing phase (i.e., a softer landing strategy) may reduce the peak load on the patellar tendon, potentially via enhanced shock absorption and more effective load distribution across the joint.19 Our results also revealed sex-specific associations. In females, PPTF was positively correlated with ankle ROM, suggesting that greater ankle mobility may be linked to higher patellar tendon load. This relationship could be attributed to increased demands on knee musculature to control the additional motion at the ankle. In contrast, PPTF was negatively correlated with hip ROM in females, indicating that greater hip flexibility may help redirect a portion of the mechanical load away from the knee and thereby reduce stress on the patellar tendon during landing.20 Collectively, these results highlight the potential for both proximal and distal joint mechanics to influence localized tendon loading, particularly among female participants.

Regardless of sex, PPTF was positively correlated with peak ankle and knee joint moments. Higher peak moments indicate greater net muscular demand during dynamic tasks. To generate these larger moments, the quadriceps must produce greater force, which is transmitted through the patellar tendon and thereby elevates PPTF.21 With respect to PVGRF, a positive correlation with PPTF would be biomechanically expected, because greater impact forces typically require greater quadriceps force to stabilize the knee joint.22 However, the negative correlation observed in our data suggests the involvement of compensatory neuromuscular or kinematic strategies. Specifically, individuals who generate higher PVGRF may concurrently adopt altered movement patterns (e.g., proximal/distal load shifting) that effectively redistribute load away from the patellar tendon.23 Thus, the negative association likely reflects not a direct causal effect of PVGRF magnitude, but rather the influence of an underlying movement strategy that simultaneously modulates both external ground reaction force (GRF) and internal tendon loading.24 This finding may underscore the complexity of the relationship between external forces and internal tissue loads.

From a neuromuscular perspective, distinct patterns were observed across frequency bands. In the low-frequency range (0–25 Hz), the muscle network exhibited robust synchronous connectivity, particularly among key muscles such as the VM, RF, and GMed. This suggests that, at lower frequencies, muscle activation is characterized by greater inter-muscular coordination, with multiple muscle groups acting in concert to support smooth and sustained movement patterns.25 The elevated synchronicity and tightly integrated network structure (reflected by high clustering coefficients) observed in the low-frequency band are commonly linked to low-intensity, continuous motor tasks that rely heavily on reflexive responses and automated control mechanisms.26 During the high-demand SL task, such coordinated activation may enhance movement efficiency and reduce metabolic cost by providing a stable postural foundation. In this operational mode, synergistic activation of muscles such as the quadriceps and the GMed likely contributes to maintaining knee joint stability, thereby potentially optimizing impact-force distribution and modulating the mechanical environment of the patellar tendon. Conversely, in the high-frequency range (25–50 Hz), network synchronization was markedly reduced, the network became more decentralized, and muscular activity shifted toward greater functional independence. This shift is closely associated with the demands of high-intensity, rapid movements. At higher frequencies, neuromuscular control appears to rely more on localized, task-specific modulation than on broad network-wide coordination, thereby facilitating quick movement adjustments.27 Physiologically, this decentralized control mode prioritizes fast reflex pathways and precise, fine-tuned motor unit recruitment to regulate individual muscles in response to the demands of high-velocity and high-power activities.28 This contrasts sharply with the tightly coupled, synergistic cooperation observed in the low-frequency domain, where muscle activation supports continuous joint stabilization and global postural control. Our results further revealed that muscle network organization followed parallel trends in males and females across frequency bands. Specifically, clustering coefficients were significantly lower in the high-frequency range than in the low-frequency range for both sexes,29 reflecting a general loosening of network connectivity and a reduction in tightly clustered functional modules at higher frequencies. This shift from global synergistic coordination to localized task-specific modulation provides further support for frequency-dependent neuromuscular control strategies in managing dynamic joint loading.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, regarding sample characteristics, the cohort was limited to healthy young adults, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations, such as older individuals or those with a history of musculoskeletal injury. Second, regarding experimental design, although standardized laboratory tasks are necessary for experimental control, they may not fully capture the dynamic complexity and variability of real-world athletic movements, potentially limiting the study’s ecological validity. Third, and most critically, this study focuses exclusively on mechanical loading and does not include clinical assessments of tendon pathology or longitudinal injury tracking. Consequently, although the calculated PPTF is based on the concept of mechanical demand, a direct theoretical or clinical link to patellar tendon injury has not been established. The results should therefore be interpreted as an analysis of mechanical demand rather than as a clinical diagnosis or prediction of injury risk. Fourth, from a methodological standpoint, representing the patellar tendon as a single line of action may introduce bias, given that the tendon consists of multiple fiber bundles and likely experiences non-uniform load distribution. Nevertheless, considering the tendon’s relatively limited thickness in the sagittal plane and the established precedent for this modeling approach in the literature, this simplification remains justifiable for the research questions addressed.30 Fifth, due to the cross-sectional nature of the experimental data, the long-term adaptive effects of training on patellar tendon loading remain unclear.

These limitations highlight several important directions for future research. Longitudinal studies incorporating clinical outcomes are needed to determine whether the mechanical demand quantified in this study can lead to patellar tendinopathy risk. In addition, future work should extend these findings to more diverse populations and to more ecologically valid movement paradigms to enhance generalizability. The long-term adaptive effects of training on tendon loading warrant investigation through longitudinal intervention studies. Finally, advanced modeling approaches may provide deeper insight into regional load distribution within the patellar tendon, complementing the whole-tendon force estimates reported in this study.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study participant details

A total of 60 healthy volunteers (24 males and 36 females) were recruited. Participant anthropometrics stratified by sex were as follows: females (age, 23.8 ± 4.2 years; height, 162.1 ± 6.4 cm; body mass, 55.2 ± 8.1 kg) and males (age, 25.1 ± 5.0 years; height, 175.6 ± 7.2 cm; body mass, 73.5 ± 12.4 kg). Knee joint structural integrity was evaluated using MRI and confirmed as normal by a certified orthopedic specialist. Inclusion criteria were: (1) no neurological, muscular, or skeletal disorders; (2) no history of knee injury within the past 6 months; (3) normal lower-limb strength and flexibility; and (4) the ability to successfully perform SL tasks. Before enrollment, participants received a detailed explanation of the study aims, requirements, and procedures, and provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningbo University (RAGH20240105).

Method details

Data collection

For each participant, CT and MRI scans of the dominant knee were acquired to construct participant-specific high-fidelity anatomical models. CT images were acquired using a Somatom Sensation 16-slice spiral CT scanner with the following parameters: 0.5-mm slice thickness, 120-kV tube voltage, and 250–300-mA tube current, to capture high-resolution osseous structures.31 MRI scans were performed using a GE Discovery-sim 750 3.0-T system with a body coil. A sagittal T1-weighted sequence was used (1-mm slice thickness, no interslice gap) to precisely identify the origin and insertion of the patellar tendon.32 Imaging data were reconstructed using MIMICS 20.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) to generate subject-specific femur, tibia, and patella models (Figure 1A), providing a precise anatomical basis for subsequent kinematic and biomechanical analyses.

A (DFIS; Ti-WISH-II, Ti-Motion, China) was used to record 3D knee kinematics in the sagittal and coronal planes. The system comprised two X-ray units positioned at fixed angles and operated at 30 Hz with a pulse width of 1.5 ms and an image resolution of 1020 × 1020 pixels. Exposure parameters were set to 110 kVp and 200 mA, and radiation dose was maintained below 0.14 mSv.33 Before data collection, spatial calibration and image-distortion correction were performed to ensure measurement accuracy. A 3D motion capture system (Vicon, Metrics Ltd., UK), consisting of eight infrared cameras operating at 200 Hz, was simultaneously used to track the trajectories of 38 reflective markers placed on anatomical landmarks based on the generic 2392 musculoskeletal model.

Surface EMG (sEMG) was recorded using a wireless system (Trigno EMG, Delsys, USA) at 2000 Hz to monitor activity of the rectus femoris (RF), vastus lateralis (VL), vastus medialis (VM), biceps femoris (BF), semitendinosus (ST), gluteus maximus (GMax), and gluteus medius (GMed) (Figure 1B).34 Before electrode placement, the skin was cleansed with alcohol to remove oils and keratin. Two trained investigators positioned electrodes over each muscle belly according to anatomical landmarks and aligned them with the muscle fiber direction. To minimize electrode displacement during movement, electrodes were secured with adhesive patches and further reinforced with elastic bandages to maintain stable skin contact.35

The GRF was recorded using two force plates (AMTI, Watertown, USA) at 1000 Hz to quantify participant–ground interactions and to identify IC during landing, defined as the instant when vertical GRF exceeded 10 N.36

Experimental procedure

The experimental protocol for SL biomechanical test is illustrated in Figure 1B. Before formal testing, all participants completed a 3-min warm-up by walking on a treadmill at a self-selected pace and then familiarized themselves with the landing task.

During testing, a 30-cm-high platform was placed directly in front of the force plates, and participants stood on the platform with their hands on their hips.37 On the examiner’s verbal cue(“start”), participants moved the dominant leg forward, leaned the trunk slightly forward, and performed a vertical drop landing from the platform with zero initial velocity. To ensure zero initial velocity, participants paused for approximately 0.5 s after leaning forward. The absence of noticeable displacement of the reflective markers on the medial and lateral malleoli over 100 consecutive frames (0.5 s) was verified in real time using Vicon Nexus software.

During landing, participants were instructed to contact the center of the force plate using only the dominant leg and to maintain balance for at least 3 s. A trial was considered successful if the participant did not fall and did not exhibit instability. Each participant completed five valid SL trials, with at least 2 min of rest between trials to minimize fatigue.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data processing

To quantify knee joint motion, local coordinate systems for the femur and tibia were defined based on the 3D reconstructed models. For the femur, the transverse axis was defined as the transepicondylar axis (TEA) (Figure 1C), connecting the most prominent points of the medial and lateral epicondyles. The longitudinal axis was obtained by fitting the centers of equidistant cross-sections along the femoral shaft using a linear least-squares approach. The anteroposterior axis was defined as orthogonal to the other two axes, and the origin was located at their intersection. For the tibia, the mediolateral axis (MLT) (Figure 1C) was defined by the centers of circles fitted to the medial and lateral tibial plateaus. The longitudinal axis was similarly obtained by fitting cross-sectional centers along the tibial shaft, and the anteroposterior axis was defined as orthogonal to the other two axes.38 Right-handed Cartesian coordinate systems were then established for both bones, and all kinematic parameters were expressed as femoral motion relative to the tibia.

The CT-derived 3D bone models were imported into Autoscoper V2 (Brown University, USA) to generate digitally reconstructed radiographs (DRRs). Semi-automated 2D–3D registration was then performed to track bony segments frame by frame by mapping the 3D models onto the 2D fluoroscopic projections and extracting the corresponding spatial transformation matrices (Figure 1D).39 After registration, kinematic data were normalized to 0–100% of the landing phase and smoothed using a fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 6 Hz to reduce high-frequency noise and registration error. The analysis window extended from initial foot contact (defined as VGRF >10 N) to the instant of peak knee flexion.40

Kinematic and kinetic data were preprocessed in Vicon Nexus, including marker labeling and calibration, interpolation of missing trajectories, and removal of redundant or erroneous markers. The resulting C3D files were imported into Visual3D 6.7.3 (C-Motion Inc., Germantown, USA) for subject-specific musculoskeletal modeling. Anatomical reference frames were established from marker positions, and rigid-body segment parameters were scaled according to each participant’s height and body mass. Joint angles and angular velocities were computed using Visual3D’s built-in algorithms, and inverse dynamics, combined with synchronized GRF data, were used to calculate joint moments. To improve the signal-to-noise ratio, kinematic and kinetic data were low-pass filtered using a fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth filter with cutoff frequencies of 10 Hz and 20 Hz, respectively. All joint-angle, angular-velocity, and joint-moment variables analyzed in this study were restricted to the sagittal plane.

Based on this framework, PTMA and patellar tendon force (PTF) were calculated. Using individualized femur–tibia–patella models, the line connecting the patellar apex and tibial tuberosity was approximated as the patellar tendon line of action (Figure 1E). Combined with six-degrees-of-freedom tibiofemoral kinematics obtained from DFIS, the instantaneous knee flexion–extension axis was determined frame by frame using the finite helical axis (FHA) method. The shortest distance between this axis and the tendon line of action was defined as PTMA at each time point, yielding a time series of PTMA(t).14 To suppress noise, PTMA(t) was low-pass filtered with a 6-Hz zero-phase Butterworth filter, and frames with unstable FHA estimation or insufficient angular displacement were excluded. For each participant, the mean value across five valid trials was taken as the individual PTMA, and repeatability was assessed using the coefficient of variation. For inter-individual comparisons, PTMA was normalized to either femoral condylar width or the anteroposterior dimension of the tibial plateau.

The net knee extension moment represents the total internal moment that must be generated by the quadriceps to counteract the external moment induced by GRF. PTMA represents the mechanical leverage of the patellar tendon; therefore, PTF reflects the tensile demand required to generate this internal moment. A smaller PTMA indicates a mechanical disadvantage, such that higher tendon tension is required to achieve the same joint moment. This calculation captures the dynamic interplay between joint kinetics and individualized anatomy, and the resulting PTF reflects the localized mechanical stress imposed on tendon tissue throughout the landing phase. The PTF(t) series was smoothed in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) using a fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 6 Hz to reduce high-frequency noise while avoiding phase delay. From this, the PPTF and its timing were then extracted, and PPTF was normalized to body weight (PPTF/BW) for group comparisons. All scalar indices were temporally aligned with kinematic and kinetic variables for subsequent correlation and regression analyses.

sEMG processing and functional muscle network analysis were performed in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Raw sEMG signals were first band-pass filtered using a fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth filter (20–450 Hz) and notch-filtered at 50 Hz and its harmonics (±1 Hz) to remove power-line interference. The signals were then full-wave rectified and demeaned to remove drift.42,43 All trials were time-aligned to the IC event. After manual inspection and artifact removal, valid trials from the same participant under identical conditions were concatenated to satisfy the time-length requirements for spectral analysis while preserving trial independence.

Two stable and interpretable frequency bands were identified: a low-frequency band (0–25 Hz) and a high-frequency band (25–50 Hz).

For each frequency band and experimental condition, weighted undirected functional muscle networks were constructed. In these networks, nodes represented muscles, while edge weights corresponded to the coupling strength between muscle pairs. For visualization and computation, a threshold of 30% of the maximum edge weight within each frequency band was applied, with edge strength classified as low (0–33%), medium (34–66%), or high (67–100%). Three global topological metrics were calculated: global efficiency, which measures the overall effectiveness of information transfer within the network; the weighted clustering coefficient, which indicates how strongly nodes tend to form tightly connected subgroups; and network density, which represents the ratio of actual connections to the maximum possible connections at the specified threshold. From a neuromuscular perspective, these metrics quantify how the central nervous system coordinates muscle groups to maintain joint stability. Global efficiency (Equation 3) reflects the capacity for rapid and integrated information transfer, representing the system’s ability to operate as a synchronized unit to attenuate landing impacts. It was calculated as the average of the inverse shortest path length dij between nodes. A higher global efficiency value indicates an enhanced capability for instantaneous coordination during high-demand tasks such as landing. The clustering coefficient (Equation 4) measures the degree of functional specialization, elucidating how specific muscles form synergistic “cliques” to provide localized joint protection. It was derived from the relationship between the number of triangles ti around node i and its degree ki, reflecting the modularity of neuromuscular control. Network density (Equation 5) was utilized to quantify the overall saturation of neuromuscular coupling, defined as the ratio of the actual functional connections E to the theoretical maximum possible connections N(N-1)/2. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corporation, NY, USA). Prior to formal analysis, Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests were performed to assess data normality. The results confirmed that all variables followed a normal distribution.

To explore the relationships between PPTF and lower-limb biomechanical parameters, Pearson correlation analyses were performed for the following variables: PVGRF, peak knee angular velocity, peak moments of the ankle, knee, and hip joints, and sagittal-plane IC angles and ROM for the three joints. Linear regression models were then applied to confirm the correlations, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) ranges from −1 to 1, where r > 0 indicates a positive correlation, r < 0 indicates a negative correlation, r = 0 indicates no significant linear relationship, and |r| = 1 indicates a perfect linear relationship.44

For the functional muscle network analysis, three global topological metrics-global efficiency, weighted clustering coefficient, and network density (based on a 30% threshold)-were computed separately for the low-frequency (0–25 Hz) and high-frequency (25–50 Hz) components. Paired sample t-tests were conducted for within-subject comparisons between the two frequency bands, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.
