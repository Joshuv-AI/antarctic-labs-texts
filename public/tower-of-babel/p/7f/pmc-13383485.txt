Husbands’ smoking and risk of diabetes in reproductive-aged Chinese women: a nationwide population-based cohort study
Authors: Min Jin Zhang, Long Wang, Sijing Ding, Ziyi Cheng, Zheheng Liu, Qianru Wu, Yuyan Wu, Jihong Xu, Yuan He, Yuanyuan Wang, Ya Zhang, Hongguang Zhang, Ying Yang, Xu Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreventing impaired fasting glucose (IFG) and diabetes mellitus (DM) among reproductive-age women is important for both maternal and infant health. We included non-smoking women aged 20-49 years without pre-diagnosed DM and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) <7.0 mmol/L at baseline. Among 2,475,950 women with baseline normal glycemia, we examined incident IFG (FPG 5.6-6.9 mmol/L) and DM (FPG ≥7.0 mmol/L or self-reported). Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a leading cause of global mortality and morbidity, affecting 529 million people and causing 6.7 million deaths annually, with health expenditure reaching 966 million USD [1, 2]. Among reproductive-age women, DM prevalence ranges from 1.2% to 7.2% across populations [3–5]. Preventing both impaired fasting glucose (IFG) and DM in reproductive-age women is important for both maternal and infants. A population-based study of 6.4 million Chinese women found that preconception DM increased risks of spontaneous abortion (11%), preterm birth (17%), macrosomia (13%), and perinatal death (59%), while even IFG increased these risks by 2–8% [5]. Furthermore, maternal hyperglycemia increases risks for metabolic and cardiovascular complications in offspring later in life [6].

While smoking is a well-established modifiable risk factor for DM through insulin resistance and impaired glucose metabolism [7, 8], secondhand smoke (SHS) exposure may similarly increase DM risk due to its toxic components, which can be as concentrated as those in smoking [9]. Two systematic reviews of prospective studies found that nonsmokers exposed to SHS have 22% to 27% higher DM risk compared with unexposed nonsmokers [10, 11]. However, these studies likely underestimate the true association as most studies relied on self-reported exposure despite evidence that 70% of nonsmokers are unaware of their SHS exposure [12], and 44.7% of adults with DM remain undiagnosed globally [2]. Besides, the relationship between SHS and glycemic dysfunction is dose-dependent, with higher intensity and longer duration of SHS exposure increasing DM risk [13]. Both childhood and adult exposures independently increase DM risk [14], and trajectory analyses showed that individuals who reduce their exposure from high to low still have 90% higher odds of DM than those with consistently low exposure [15], indicating persistent metabolic effects of SHS. Although the association between SHS exposure and DM is well studied, evidence on glycemic impact from a single predominant household source remains limited. In populations with marked sex disparities in smoking prevalence, spousal smoking often constitutes women’s primary exposure source [16, 17]. Identifying the relationship between spousal smoking and wives’ DM risk, including time-varying effects on glycemia, would inform targeted household interventions and family-based cessation strategies.

China has the largest number of tobacco users globally, with 50.6% of men and 2.1% of women aged 15 years and older smoking [18]. This sex disparity leads to 65.1% of reproductive-age non-smoking women reporting SHS exposure at home [19]. Our previous cross-sectional analysis showed dose-response associations between husbands’ smoking intensity with wives’ risks of IFG and DM [20]. This prospective study further examined temporal associations of husband smoking status, intensity, and cumulative smoking with incident IFG and DM among initially normal glycemia reproductive-age women. We also examined whether changes in husbands’ smoking status were associated with glycemic progression among women with pre-existing IFG.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective cohort study included reproductive-age couples participating in the National Free Prepregnancy Checkups Project (NFPCP) in China. The NFPCP provides free preconception health examinations for couples planning pregnancy within 6 months, initially targeting rural couples in 220 counties in 2010 and expanded to urban areas across 2,907 counties by 2013 [20–25]. At each examination, trained health professionals collected sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical data through standardized questionnaires and physical examinations during face-to-face counseling. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the National Research Institute for Family Planning in Beijing, China, and written consent was obtained from all participants. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) to report this study. The median inter-examination interval was 2.12 years (interquartile range (IQR): 1.18–3.29), with a total of 6,658,579 person-years of follow-up.

Following implementation of China’s two-child policy in 2015 [26], couples planning a second child were encouraged to participate in repeat NFPCP examinations. Of 2,913,834 couples (wives aged 20 to 49) who completed two preconception examinations between January 2010 and April 2020, we excluded 85,290 couples for the following reasons: wives who currently smoked, wives with missing husband smoking data, wives with pre-diagnosed DM, or wives with fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L at baseline. The final analytic sample included 2,828,544 couples (Fig. 1).

Exposure assessment

At each examination, husbands were asked “Do you currently smoke?” Those answering “yes” were further asked about the average number of cigarettes smoked per day (CPD) during the past 12 months. Based on smoking status at both examinations, husbands were classified as consistently smoking (smoking at both), consistently non-smoking (nonsmoking at both), ex-smoking (smoking at baseline but not follow-up), or newly smoking (nonsmoking at baseline but smoking at follow-up).

Among consistently smoking husbands, smoking intensity was quantified as the mean CPD across two examinations and categorized as 1–5, 6–10, 11–15, 16–20, and ≥ 21 CPD. Cumulative exposure was calculated as pack-years (inter-examination interval in years mean CPD/20) and categorized as 0.1-2.0, 2.1-4.0, 4.1-6.0, and ≥ 6.1 pack-years.

Outcome assessment

The outcomes were wives’ glycemia status at follow-up. Blood samples were collected after overnight fasting (≥ 8 h) and analyzed using either the glucose oxidase or hexokinase method at local laboratories, adhering to the National Guide to Clinical Laboratory Procedures [27]. Quality assurance was maintained through biannual external assessments by the National Center of Clinical Laboratories for Quality Inspection and Detection. Women were categorized as: (1) normal glycemia (FPG < 5.6 mmol/L without self-reported DM); (2) IFG (FPG 5.6–6.9 mmol/L without self-reported DM); and (3) DM (FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or self-reported DM) [28].

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on established confounders of the association between secondhand smoke exposure and diabetes risk, as identified in a recent systematic review of prospective cohort studies and our previous cross-sectional analysis of this cohort [10, 20].Wives’ age was recorded at each examination, and the mean of the two values was used for analysis, categorized as 19–24, 25–29, 30–34, and 35–49 years. Education level was assessed using eight categories (illiterate, primary school, junior high school, senior high school, vocational school, college, master’s degree, and doctoral degree) and categorized as junior high school and below or senior high school and above. Ethnicity was self-reported and categorized as Han (the majority ethnic group of China’s population) or others (including 55 ethnic minority groups). Residential setting was categorized as rural or urban based on household registration type. Occupation was assessed using seven predefined categories (farmer, worker, service industry, business, housework, teacher/civil servant/staff, and other) and categorized as farmers, workers, or others for analysis. Alcohol consumption was self-reported, those who reported drinking further reported average daily intake in milliliters, categorized as non-drinking, 1–10 ml/day, 11–50 ml/day, and > 50 ml/day. Height and weight were measured at each examination, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²), and categorized as underweight (< 18.5), normal (18.5–23.9), overweight (24.0-27.9), and obese (≥ 28.0) [29]. All covariates were collected at both examinations. Because age, BMI and alcohol consumption could vary between the two examinations, the average of the two measurements was used in the analyses.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages while continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations. Differences between groups for categorical variables were assessed using the χ² test, and continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test.

The first analysis included 2,475,950 couples with wives having normal glycemia at baseline. Multivariable multinomial logistic regression examined associations between husbands’ smoking status (consistently non-smoking (reference), consistently smoking, ex-smoking, newly smoking) and wives’ incident glycemic outcomes (normal glycemia (reference), IFG, DM) at follow-up. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% CIs were calculated.

To examine dose-response relationships, we restricted analyses to wives with baseline normal glycemia whose husbands were consistently non-smoking (reference) or consistently smoking. We assessed associations with smoking intensity (CPD) and cumulative exposure (pack-years), testing for linear trend using category medians as continuous variables. Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted by wives’ age, education level, ethnicity, residential setting, occupation, BMI, and alcohol consumption, including interaction terms to assess effect modification. To account for multiple comparisons across seven subgroup variables, a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of 0.007 (0.05/7) was used for interaction tests.

The secondary analysis of 352,594 couples with wives having baseline IFG examined glycemic progression or regression by husbands’ smoking status, with consistently smoking husbands as the reference exposure.

All models adjusted for wives’ age, education level, ethnicity, residential setting, occupation, BMI, and alcohol consumption. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the analyses of husbands’ smoking status patterns with additional adjustment for the inter-examination interval (continuous, in years) to examine whether differential follow-up duration across exposure groups biased the estimated associations. For the dose–response and subgroup analyses, which were restricted to consistently non-smoking and consistently smoking husbands, the inter-examination interval was not additionally adjusted because (1) the median intervals were nearly identical between these two groups (2.06 vs. 2.05 years) and (2) for the pack-years analysis, the interval is a component of the exposure calculation. The proportion of missing data for all covariates ranged from 0.0006% to 2.7%. Given the low percentage of missing data (< 5%), complete case analysis was performed without imputation. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Among 2,828,544 couples included in the study, 2,475,950 wives (87.5%) had normal glycemia and 352,594 (12.5%) had IFG at baseline. Among husbands, 62.7% were consistently non-smoking, 16.2% consistently smoking, 11.5% ex-smoking, and 9.6% newly smoking. Table 1 shows wives with consistently smoking husbands were more likely to be aged 19–24 years (26.8% vs. 24.7%), have lower education levels (junior high school or below: 59.0% vs. 56.4%), and report alcohol consumption (3.5% vs. 1.4%) compared to those with consistently non-smoking husbands. They also had higher prevalence of underweight (11.1% vs. 7.5%), overweight (15.0% vs. 12.1%), and obesity (3.5% vs. 2.2%) compared to those with consistently non-smoking husbands. Similar patterns were observed for women with ex-smoking or newly smoking spouses.

Incident dysglycemia among women with baseline normal glycemia

Table 2 shows of 2,475,950 women with normal glycemia at baseline, 250,717 (10.1%) developed IFG and 24,419 (1.0%) developed DM at follow-up. Compared with wives of consistently non-smoking husbands, those with ex-smoking husbands had increased odds of IFG (AOR 1.07, 95% CI 1.05–1.08) and DM (AOR 1.12, 95% CI 1.08–1.17). Higher odds were observed for wives with newly smoking husbands (IFG: AOR 1.11, 95% CI 1.09–1.12; DM: AOR 1.17, 95% CI 1.12–1.22). Wives with consistently smoking husbands showed increased odds of DM (AOR 1.07, 95% CI 1.03–1.11) but not IFG. Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for the inter-examination interval yielded consistent results (eTable 1).

Among wives with consistently smoking husbands and those with consistently non-smoking husbands, the odds of DM increased with smoking intensity (P trend < 0.001; eTable 2). The AORs were 0.84 (95% CI 0.76–0.93) for 1–5 CPD, 1.01 (95% CI 0.96–1.06) for 6–10 CPD, 1.20 (95% CI 1.13–1.28) for 11–15 CPD, 1.14 (95% CI 1.07–1.22) for 16–20 CPD, and 1.30 (95% CI 1.09–1.56) for ≥ 21 CPD. The odds of IFG showed a similar pattern (P trend = 0.048), with AORs of 0.95 (95% CI 0.92–0.97), 0.99 (95% CI 0.97–1.01), 1.04 (95% CI 1.02–1.06), 1.00 (95% CI 0.97–1.02), and 1.12 (95% CI 1.05–1.20) across the same intensity categories. For cumulative exposure, a dose-response relationship was observed. The odds of DM increased progressively with AORs of 1.02 (95% CI 0.98–1.07) for 0.1-2.0 pack-years, 1.24 (95% CI 1.16–1.33) for 2.1-4.0 pack-years, 1.55 (95% CI 1.35–1.77) for 4.1-6.0 pack-years, and 1.88 (95% CI 1.43–2.47) for ≥ 6.1 pack-years compared to wives with consistently non-smoking husbands (P trend < 0.001; eTable 3). The odds of IFG showed a similar dose-response pattern with AORs of 1.00 (95% CI 0.99–1.02), 1.10 (95% CI 1.07–1.13), 1.10 (95% CI 1.04–1.16), and 1.18 (95% CI 1.05–1.33) for the same pack-year categories (P trend < 0.001; eTable 3).

Exploratory subgroup analyses, applying a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of 0.007, found effect modification by ethnicity for smoking intensity (P for interaction < 0.001, eTable 4) and by ethnicity and BMI for cumulative exposure (both P for interaction < 0.001) (eTable 5). Associations with cumulative pack-years tended to be stronger among non-Han ethnicity and women with higher BMI.

Glycemic progression among women with baseline IFG

Table 3 shows among women with IFG at baseline, those with consistently non-smoking husbands had lower odds of persistent IFG (AOR 0.89, 95% CI 0.87–0.91) and progression to DM (AOR 0.85, 95% CI 0.80–0.90) compared with those with consistently smoking husbands. Reduced odds were also observed for women with ex-smoking husbands (persistent IFG: AOR 0.85, 95% CI 0.82–0.87; DM progression: AOR 0.91, 95% CI 0.84–0.99) and newly smoking husbands (persistent IFG: AOR 0.84, 95% CI 0.81–0.87; DM progression: AOR 0.89, 95% CI 0.81–0.98). These findings remained consistent in sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for the inter-examination interval (eTable 6).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first large prospective cohort study of over 2.8 million Chinese couples to establish temporal relationships between husbands’ smoking and glycemia outcomes in reproductive-aged women. We found that women with consistently smoking husbands had a 7% higher odds of DM compared to wives with consistently non-smoking husbands, while those with ex-smoking (12%) or newly smoking (17%) husbands had even higher risks. We found dose-response relationships for cumulative pack-years. Among women with baseline IFG, husbands’ smoking cessation was associated with an 9% reduced odds of DM progression compared to women with consistently smoking husbands.

Previous meta-analyses found that SHS exposure increases DM risk by 27% to 42%, higher than 7% in women with consistently smoking husbands in our study [10, 11]. This discrepancy might be due to previous studies capturing SHS from multiple sources including workplaces and public settings, whereas our study assessed only husbands’ smoking. Additionally, actual exposure from husbands’ smoking may also depend on unmeasured factors such as whether smoking occurred indoors, household ventilation, and cohabitation time. Besides, 54.5% of consistently smoking husbands in our cohort were light smokers (1–10 CPD), which might dilute the overall effect. Prior findings have been inconsistent, as a prospective study of never-smoking women found no significant association between husbands’ smoking and DM risk [30], likely because it relied on self-reported DM. Cross-sectional studies using laboratory-confirmed glycemia have consistently shown positive associations between husbands’ smoking with women’s IFG and DM risks [20, 31]. This study extends previous findings by confirming temporal relationships and dose-response relationships for cumulative pack-year exposure on incident IFG and DM, providing stronger evidence for causality between husbands’ smoking and women’s glucose metabolism dysfunction. These associations were robust to additional adjustment for the inter-examination interval.

Women with ex-smoking or newly smoking husbands had higher odds of DM than those with consistently smoking spouses. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as several potential explanations may apply. Firstly, the median inter-examination interval of 2.12 years may be insufficient to observe the long-term benefits of husbands’ smoking cessation, as study of active smokers has found ex-smokers DM risk remains higher than consistently active smokers for up to 5 years post-cessation, with normalization requiring 10 years [32]. Second, we assessed smoking status only at two examinations and could not determine the exact timing of cessation or initiation. Husbands who quit may have done so in response to their own or family health concerns, and may have had heavier prior smoking histories than those who continued, resulting in greater cumulative exposure for their wives before cessation. Third, for women with newly smoking husbands, the higher odds may reflect acute metabolic effects, as smoking acutely impairs glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity [33, 34]. Fourth, the relatively lower odds observed in women with consistently smoking husbands may be due to selection bias, as our study excluded women with DM at first preconception examination, potentially selecting for those less susceptible to the effects of husbands’ smoking. Fifth, the ex-smoking and newly smoking groups had longer inter-examination intervals (median 2.36 and 2.30 years) than the consistently smoking group (2.05 years), though sensitivity analyses adjusting for this difference yielded consistent results (eTable 1).

The lower odds of both IFG and DM at 1–5 CPD was inconsistent with the cumulative exposure analysis, where even minimal exposure (0.1-2.0 pack-years) showed no protective association. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, husbands reporting 1–5 CPD may include occasional or social smokers who rarely smoke at home, resulting in negligible exposure for their wives, whereas heavier smokers with greater nicotine dependence are less likely to restrict their smoking to outdoor settings. Second, some husbands in the non-smoking reference group may have underreported their smoking status, as self-reported smoking has been shown to underestimate actual smoking prevalence [35], which would dilute the reference group and attenuate the estimated contrast with the 1–5 CPD category. Third, the 1–5 CPD category had a relatively small sample size (n = 53,194) compared with the reference group (n = 1,555,878), which may contribute to instability in this estimate. Finally, residual confounding cannot be excluded, as husbands who smoke lightly may differ from non-smokers and heavy smokers in unmeasured characteristics. The consistent dose-response relationships observed with cumulative pack-years, which integrate both intensity and duration, suggest that this apparent protective association at 1–5 CPD does not reflect a true biological effect.

Several biological mechanisms may underlie the observed association between husbands’ smoking and women’s glycemic status. Wives of smoking husbands are likely exposed to tobacco smoke through SHS, thirdhand smoke that persists on indoor surfaces [36], and shared household environments. SHS contains similar toxic compounds as smoking [37]. Tobacco smoke generates reactive oxygen species that disrupt redox homeostasis and activate pro-inflammatory pathways [38], leading to chronic low-grade inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and insulin resistance [39–42]. Recent prospective cohort studies have reported that endogenous antioxidant biomarkers, including bilirubin, albumin, uric acid, and creatinine, are associated with the risk of smoking-related cancers in a dose- and sex-dependent manner [38, 43–46], further supporting the role of oxidative stress in smoking-related chronic disease. In addition, nicotine decreases insulin sensitivity, as diabetic smokers require higher insulin doses than non-smoking diabetic patients to achieve similar glycemic control [47, 48]. Nicotine also reduces the release of insulin through its effects on nicotinic receptors present in pancreatic islet cells and β-cells [49].

Among women with IFG, those whose husbands quit smoking had significantly lower odds of DM progression compared to women with consistently smoking husbands. This extends previous research showing that SHS worsens glycemic control in DM patients by demonstrating that reduction in household tobacco smoke exposure, through spousal smoking cessation, may prevent progression from IFG to DM [50]. The finding suggests that for reproductive-aged women with IFG, spousal smoking cessation is a modifiable intervention target.

Our findings have several implications, particularly in China where smoke-free policies mainly focus on indoor public places and some outdoor areas, leaving people unprotected in private areas. Healthcare education during preconception examination should be considered as a teachable moment for couples with both normal glycemia and IFG women, emphasizing the harms of continued and newly initiated husbands’ smoking alongside the benefits of cessation to promote household smoking cessation. The dose-response relationships between husbands’ smoking and women’s IFG and DM risks highlighted the need for comprehensive tobacco control interventions aligned with WHO MPOWER recommendations, including extending smoke-free policies to private environments, implementing family-based smoking cessation interventions, and increasing tobacco taxation to reduce smoking.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we assessed husbands’ smoking status and intensity but did not capture contextual factors that may modify the degree to which husbands’ smoking affects wives’ health, such as whether smoking occurred inside the home, household ventilation, cohabitation time and smoking behavior of other household members. We also did not assess wives’ tobacco smoke exposure from workplaces or other settings, which may bias our estimates. Second, we could not distinguish between the relative contributions of SHS and thirdhand smoke to the observed associations, as thirdhand smoke persists on indoor surfaces for weeks to months [36], and may independently affect metabolic health. Third, the lack of exact smoking cessation timing for ex-smoking husbands increased heterogeneity within this group, potentially combining husbands who quit long before follow-up with recent quitters, thus biasing our estimates of the true association between ex-smoking husbands with women’s IFG and DM risks. Fourth, although we adjusted for wives’ sociodemographic characteristics, household-level factors such as income, dietary patterns, physical activity, psychosocial stress, and broader health behaviors were not directly measured. Husbands’ smoking behavior may be correlated with these factors, which could independently influence wives’ metabolic health and represent potential sources of residual confounding. Fifth, as multinomial logistic regression yields odds ratios rather than relative risks, the estimated associations for IFG (incidence 10.1%) may overestimate the true relative risk. For DM (incidence 1.0%), the rare disease assumption is satisfied and odds ratios closely approximate relative risks. Sixth, the subgroup and interaction analyses involved multiple statistical tests, increasing the possibility of chance findings. These results should be interpreted as exploratory, and we applied a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of 0.007 to the interaction tests to partially address this concern. Seventh, the NFPCP dataset does not include measurements of endogenous antioxidant biomarkers such as bilirubin, albumin, uric acid, or creatinine. Although external evidence supports the role of oxidative stress in smoking-related chronic disease [38, 43–46], we could not directly assess this pathway in our study. Future studies with available biomarker data could further clarify the biological mechanisms underlying the association between spousal smoking and glycemic outcomes. Finally, our reliance on FPG, without oral glucose tolerance tests, HbA1c, or postprandial blood glucose measurements, could lead to an underestimation of IFG and DM participants. Additionally, fasting glucose measurements were obtained from multiple local laboratories using either glucose oxidase or hexokinase methods, and despite standardized procedures and biannual external quality assessments, inter-laboratory variability may introduce some degree of misclassification of glycemic status, though such variability is unlikely to differ systematically by husbands’ smoking status and would not be expected to produce spurious associations.

Conclusions

In this large prospective cohort study of over 2.8 million Chinese reproductive-age couples, husbands’ smoking was associated with increased odds of IFG and DM in women, with clear dose-response relationships for cumulative pack-years, but not smoking intensity. Smoking cessation among husbands of women with IFG reduced DM progression risk. These findings suggest that expanding smoke-free policies to private settings and providing family-based smoking cessation interventions during preconception examination may be effective strategies for DM prevention in women.
