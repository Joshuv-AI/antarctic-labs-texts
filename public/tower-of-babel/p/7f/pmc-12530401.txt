Integration of precision medicine into routine cancer care—protocol for the Precision Care Initiative: a research programme of effectiveness-implementation hybrid trials
Authors: Shuang Liang, Skye McKay, Frank Lin, Milita Zaheed, April Morrow, Bridget Douglas, Jeffery Chan, Helen Monaghan, Priscilla Chan, Elizabeth Kennedy, Elijah Tyedmers, Stephanie Walker, Kathryn Leaney, Christine E Napier, Sandy Middleton, Phyllis Butow, Rachel Williams, Bonny Parkinson, Mandy L Ballinger, Katherine Tucker, David Goldstein, David Thomas, Natalie Taylor

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGenomic diagnostics have accelerated therapeutic and preventative breakthroughs in oncology and cancer genetics. Despite increased access, the implementation of genomics-based care faces serious fragmentation and scalability issues due to a lack of system support. The Precision Care Initiative aims to develop a novel and scalable Precision Care Clinic (PCC). It is designed to coordinate precision medicine in oncology and streamline decision support for referring oncologists and geneticists. The PCC will enhance quality of care through multifaceted, patient-centred communication. It will also improve translational capacity by integrating team expertise in precision oncology, implementation science, clinical informatics, cancer genetics, health economics and patient-reported measures.Methods and analysisThis programme uses a type I and type II hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial design sequentially. The complex clinical intervention is precision oncology—matching the targeted treatment or risk management strategy to the right patient, based on their genomic, cancer staging, environmental, lifestyle and biological characteristics, etc. The service intervention is the PCC, providing centralised multidisciplinary review to facilitate shared decision-making with clinicians for the provision of optimal precision oncology care for their patients. The implementation intervention is the co-designed implementation platform—applying evidence-based implementation approaches and Learning Health System principles to enhance feasibility and sustainability. All adult patients across Australia referred to the PCC (n=est. 100–150/year), and healthcare professional interest holders involved in the delivery of precision oncology services, are eligible to participate. Over the study course, phase I involves using a mixed-methods approach to inform iterative co-design and pilot testing of the first PCC with an accompanying implementation platform, and a suite of outcome measures to assess effectiveness; phase II (hybrid type I) includes the implementation of the PCC and evaluation of the outcome measures designed in phase I; phase III (hybrid type II) involves a co-design of local adaptations and testing the effectiveness of the PCC model nationally.Ethics and disseminationThe study received ethical approval from the St Vincent’s Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (2023/ETH00373). Study results will be presented at relevant conferences and published in peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration number NCT06077110 Keywords: Cancer genetics, Health Services, Clinical Trial

Introduction

Precision medicine, which matches the right treatment to the right patient at the right time, is a rapidly evolving field that holds great promise for enhanced clinical efficacy, reduced safety concerns and decreased economic burden.1,3 This field has seen substantial scientific advances continually revolutionising our understanding of the biology underlying disease development and innovating clinical trial conduct.4,8 However, many innovations remain stuck in the research translational pipeline and struggle to penetrate healthcare systems, leading to significant research waste and missed opportunities that could potentially save lives.4 5

One area where these translational challenges are evident is adult oncology.9 In Australia, cancer incidence has significantly increased, with current estimates around 1 69 000 cases. This number is projected to exceed 200 000 within the next decade.10 Additionally, the projected cancer-related healthcare costs are expected to rise by 42% per capita by 2050.11 Precision oncology, which involves targeted anticancer therapies and preventative strategies informed by molecular tumour profiling, continues to enhance our understanding of cancer heterogeneity. The rapid advancements in molecular technologies and cancer genomics are accelerating the inevitable paradigm shift from traditional cancer care to precision oncology, offering invaluable potential across the entire spectrum of cancer care.12

Precision care models currently rely on research and clinical care expertise, facilitated by a variety of organisations, including public, private and research-based entities. This is essential for patient management and data curation; however, it often leads to inconsistencies in practices, processes, priorities, education, training and technical skills.13 As cancer patient populations become more stratified, achieving the necessary sample size for traditional trial designs becomes less feasible, resulting in a scarcity of orthodox clinical evidence.14

Global efforts have been undertaken to promote the precision oncology approach, spanning policy, research, data sharing, education and other areas, highlighting a collective recognition of its potential benefits.4 Despite this progress, various challenges are encountered and addressed by different interest holders, leading to fragmented evidence accumulation, and increasing variations in practices and processes.4 Most patients with cancer are still unable to access precision care as part of standard healthcare service.2 At present, patients primarily access precision oncology through private or non-profit medical research settings. Even with national health insurance systems operating a centralised precision oncology programme in some parts of the world (eg, Japan) access to targeted drugs is problematic.15 Moreover, regardless of country or health system type, historically marginalised populations are disproportionally affected.16

Compounded challenges exist in the effective implementation of precision oncology.17,19 Figure 1 illustrates the complexities of integrating precision oncology into already overburdened healthcare systems. The process of evidence-based variant interpretation and therapy selection is resource-intensive. The next hurdle is the implementation of clinical decisions, as access to existing and emerging medicines is not universally available. Patients are further confronted with miscommunication, confusion and disappointment due to insufficient infrastructure support, poor care coordination and inadequately managed expectations.20 Healthcare professionals have identified the lack of multidisciplinary care coordination as a barrier to the effective delivery of precision oncology care.13 17 Although original research exploring precision oncology care and patient experiences is limited,9 available studies have reported treatment delays, confusion and increased anxiety due to the current fragmented nature of precision oncology service delivery.17 Integrated and collaborative approaches are needed to overcome these barriers reported by both patients and healthcare professionals to deliver precision care.5 21 22 It is important to ensure appropriate and sufficient counselling for patients and their families, including informing them of the risks and benefits of tumour molecular profiling.2 23 To ensure patient safety and privacy, ethical guidelines should be adhered to and are expected to evolve in tandem with the field.23,26

Gaps that Precision Care Initiative intended to address.

Omico is a nationwide network of research and treatment centres that facilitates, supports and promotes clinical trials in genomic cancer medicine. Through Omico, four key Australian medical oncology and cancer genetics precision medicine programmes are currently underway at many hospital sites, including the Prince of Wales Hospital (POWH)—a major hospital in Australia. These four programmes are MoST (Molecular Screening and Therapeutics study); CaSP (Cancer Screening Programme); RisC (Genetic Cancer Risk in the Young study) and SMOC+ (Surveillance study in Multi-Organ Cancer prone syndromes).

An opportunity exists to make routine precision oncology care a reality through proactive implementation efforts.27 28 Leveraging our combined multidisciplinary expertise in medical oncology, cancer genetics, implementation science, data science and health economics, we are working to co-create and implement a novel care model that will be optimised for scaling up nationally. The Precision Care Initiative is establishing a model of care (figure 2) to (1) provide the supporting infrastructure to deliver well-coordinated precision medicine services as part of routine public healthcare29; (2) harmonise research and clinical care priorities and practices across Australian healthcare contexts30 and (3) use clinical informatics—that is, capture data at the clinical encounter, and use those data to embed and support knowledge generation processes for rapid adoption of research into clinical care and continuous care improvement.31

Aim

The Precision Care Initiative will drive the integration of precision medicine practice and research into routine healthcare, providing streamlined services and improved translation in genomically informed cancer care. The initiative will consist of three key core service domains: (1) core clinical services; (2) research development and auditing (Learning Health System); and (3) training, education and workforce development; operating as a flexible approach to support the precision medicine patient journey. The multidisciplinary team (MDT) will work to achieve the following research objectives:

Co-design and pilot a Precision Care Clinic (PCC), and develop an accompanying implementation platform (strategies and resources developed to overcome identified barriers and maximise existing facilitators for optimising evidence-based innovation implementation) and suite of outcome measures (phase I).

Patient and public involvement

Consumer representatives have been involved in the design and conduct of this research. They were engaged from the grant application stage as chief investigators. They participate in planning and execute meetings, review patient-oriented materials and contribute to public presentations. Patient consultations provided valuable insights into diverse healthcare experiences, shaping the patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) to be used to evaluate the success of the new service. Their continued involvement will be ensured throughout the programme. Our consumer partner is a valued co-author on this publication.

Recruitment and consent

Study site recruitment

Study phases I and II will be conducted at the POWH. Upon completion, further partners will be invited to participate in phase III scale-up activities, which may include implementation of the precision oncology model of care in various contexts, with or without a physical site.

Interest holder recruitment

Precision oncology interest holders, who are representatives of those influencing and/or impacted by the model of precision care, will be invited to participate in various study activities as detailed below in the ‘Study outline’ section. These will enable understanding of current precision oncology practices, perceptions about existing barriers and facilitators to precision oncology implementation and experiences of the PCC at various phases of implementation. Interest holders will include clinicians (eg, oncologists, nurses, geneticists and genetic counsellors), precision medicine research personnel, management, administration and IT staff.

A purposeful sampling approach will be used, leveraging the professional networks of the research team across oncology, genetics and clinical trials. While the first PCC is based at the POWH, interest holders will be recruited nationally. Study invitation letters will be emailed by members of the research team to known professional interest holders, with snowballing strategies to enable wider distribution. In addition, referring clinicians will also be sent study invitation letters by mail or email.

Interested participants will be invited to contact the research team for further information and/or to schedule an interview or focus group time. Participants will be provided with a Participant Information Sheet (PIS), and consent will be obtained prior to involvement in research activities. An online version of the consent form will also be available via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap).

In addition, MDT observation as detailed in the ‘Study outline’ will be conducted. The MDT observer may attend the hybrid MDT in-person or virtually. The PCC MDT coordinator will circulate a PIS to all MDT attendees via email. Attendees will be informed that participation is voluntary, and they are able to opt out of MDT observation either via email or verbally prior to commencing the MDT meeting. For attendees who do not consent to observation, the MDT observer will not make specific notes about the attendee or the case discussion for patients they present. Identifiable information from either a patient or MDT participants will remain confidential and will not be recorded.

Patient recruitment

Referral and participation in the PCC will take place through existing clinical referral channels at POWH. Patients are considered eligible for participation in the accompanying research if they (1) have been referred to the PCC at POWH and (2) are aged 18 years or over and (3) are able to provide informed consent. Patients are not eligible if they are (1) aged under 18 years or (2) are unable to provide informed consent. Given a key goal is to ensure equity of access to precision oncology, non-English-speaking patients will be accommodated for using formal (service provided) or informal (eg, family member or carer) interpreters.

Upon receipt of the referral to the PCC, potential patient participants will be contacted by the POWH site study coordinator, who will confirm eligibility, explain the research study and answer any questions. For all patients referred to the PCC, informed consent will be sought for participation in study activities (eg, patient experience surveys and interviews). A waiver of consent is sought for access to de-identified clinical information collected on the pilot cohort of patients referred to the clinic during phase I.

Patients will be informed that participation is completely voluntary, and they can withdraw at any stage. Interested participants will be sent a PIS and consent form, either by mail or email. As noted in the PIS, participants will be made aware that they will not be reimbursed or compensated for their participation in study activities. Data collection will commence only once written consent is obtained. An online consent version of the consent form will also be available via REDCap. With each survey that is administered, participants will be reminded of the voluntary nature of their participation and their ability to opt out of the research at any stage without affecting their clinical care or relationship with staff at the POWH. Participants who wish to withdraw from the study will be asked to sign a Form for Withdrawal of Participation.

Participants who do not complete the surveys within 2 weeks will be sent a reminder email by the study coordinator. The management of adverse events will adhere to the standardised procedures established at the recruitment sites.

Omico programmes and data sharing

Most of the initial referrals to PCC will include those received from the Omico programmes, with the referral pathways expected to evolve as the PCC service continues to develop. Ethics approvals are in place to enable sharing of routinely collected data between these research programmes, subject to individual patient consent. Existing informed consent processes and procedures for individual research programmes such as MoST, CaSP, RisC and SMOC+ are fully approved and operationalised. This includes specific patient consent for future Human Research Ethics Committee-approved research studies.

Study outline

Phase I: co-design and pilot a PCC and develop an accompanying implementation platform and suite of outcome measures

Activity 1a: co-design and pilot PCC functions, practices and processes

In the early introductory phases of the PCC, interest holders who have consented to participate in implementation consultation activities will be invited to participate in a clinical practice process mapping activity. Semi-structured interviews will be conducted with precision oncology trial personnel, clinical and non-clinical cancer care professionals to map initial processes introduced for the PCC for the delivery of precision medicine trials and subsequent patient journeys through the health system. Process map-supported interview schedules will aim to identify information relating to the PCC infrastructure, resources required, available data, factors affecting and strategies to support optimal implementation. Interviews will be conducted by a member of the University of New South Wales (UNSW) research team either by phone, videoconferencing or in-person, and will be audio-recorded, transcribed and coded for thematic analyses. Any identifiable information obtained from the interviews will be removed from any reporting.

Transcripts will be coded line-by-line using a mixed inductive/deductive qualitative analysis approach. Content analysis will be used to identify key steps in the existing and PCC precision medicine care pathway and inform the development of visual, software-generated process maps (using Microsoft PowerPoint). Where possible, data will also be coded to relevant implementation science frameworks, for example, Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) 2.032 and Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF),33 while also allowing generation of themes outside of these frameworks.

The PCC will become operational during phase I to better understand the context for implementation and elicit early implementation outcomes such as acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility. Following completion of the pilot testing, feasibility and clinical impact data will be used to further inform the phase II effectiveness evaluation activities.

Activity 1b: audit/resource use data collection

Clinical data will be extracted from both precision medicine trial patient records and health system databases. This will serve two inter-related purposes. First, implementation scientists will match the data to relevant components of the process maps to verify and contextualise gaps, bottlenecks, inefficiencies and patient outcomes, and to inform the economic evaluation. Second, the data will inform the design and operationalisation of a sustainable self-learning portal capable of retrieving and analysing precision medicine data captured through the trial and the clinical databases illustrating patient journeys through the health system.31

Activity 1c: survey of familial cancer service interest holders

Using the interest holder recruitment strategy detailed previously, cancer genetics healthcare professionals will be invited to complete a short survey exploring preferences surrounding germline clinical validation processes. Survey items will assess current factors influencing decision-making practices surrounding germline clinical validations, as well as perceived benefits and limitations of a centralised review and testing service, as proposed in the PCC. The survey will inform germline validation protocols for the PCCs and identify implementation needs. The survey will be administered online via REDCap during study phase I.

Activity 1d: co-design the PCC implementation platform

Triangulating the information gathered from 1 a–1c, the research team will design an implementation platform to optimise the PCC. Using ‘implementation mapping’ methodology34 and informed by the combined use of the CFIR 2.0,32 Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategies35 and Behaviour Change Techniques,36 the platform and resources will be designed to overcome identified barriers and maximise existing facilitators for optimising evidence-based intervention implementation.37 Crucially, existing initiatives facilitating the implementation of existing approaches to integrated care (eg, NSW Integrated Care Initiative, the Australian Rare Cancer Portal) will be explored to ensure new practices can be harmoniously incorporated into clinical workflows.

As part of the PCC implementation platform, Learning Health System principles31 will be applied to incorporate data-sharing capabilities and approvals, decision support technology and formalised multidisciplinary/multisite clinical teams. The end goal is to establish a formal Learning Health System platform specifically primed for use by health and implementation professionals in the routine hospital setting. The proposed Learning Health System will be uniquely designed to rapidly retrieve relevant patient cases and assist clinical decision-making. It will do so through the collection of data from MDT cases, published case reports and clinical trials for analysis and synthesis, to engage more efficient changes in precision oncology. A web application will be co-designed to provide interfaces for users to query and extract the relevant knowledge based on user inputs. This approach maintains the knowledge from unique patient cases and facilitates the rapid retrieval and integration of the evidence-based knowledge into practice.

Activity 1e: develop outcomes framework

Based on information obtained through activities 1a–1d, we will co-design a suite of secondary outcomes to appropriately capture the intended benefits of the PCC at the patient, clinician and health service levels. The approach will involve consideration of the flow of intended change and subsequent benefits initiated by the implementation platform to support the operationalisation of a generalisable integrated PCC at scale. Interview and focus group transcripts will be analysed and coded against the categories of implementation, service and patient outcomes used in implementation research, as shown in figure 4. Interest holders will be consulted to prioritise and co-design outcome measures for the PCC effectiveness-implementation trial, delivered in phase II. Given the fast-moving field of precision oncology, it is anticipated that additional valuable secondary outcomes may be identified throughout the project and approval for any new measurement tools will be sought via an ethics amendment.

Phase II: test the clinical, service and cost-effectiveness of the PCC and pilot the implementation platform

Activity 2a: test clinical and service effectiveness of the PCC

Design

Type I hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial, with mixed-methods data collection approaches to assess the effectiveness of the PCC.

Population

All eligible patients (and their family members, where relevant/appropriate) referred to the PCC and their referring clinicians.

Service intervention

PCC implemented at POWH. The PCC is a novel quaternary-level service that integrates molecular and genetic expertise into hospital-based oncology practice to provide comprehensive decision support for complex cancer cases, comprising two core components: (1) molecular MDT and (2) precision clinic.

Primary outcomes

Service effectiveness (referring clinician)

The primary outcome of the trial is effectiveness of the precision care molecular MDT, in terms of decision support value to referring clinicians. This will be demonstrated by the number of clinicians reporting at least one of eight dimensions of service value constructs being met. These eight dimensions are specific treatment recommendations, specific clinical trial recommendations, prioritisation of treatment options, de-prioritisation of low-value care, decision reinforcement, practical support to execute options, diagnostic review and/or revision and external specialist liaison and consultation.

Secondary outcomes

Service effectiveness (referring clinician)

This includes (1) impact on referring clinician time, defined as the proportion of patient cases for which the referring clinician reports that the PCC service either increased or decreased time spent investigating therapeutic options, compared with their usual clinical workflow, that is, without PCC input; and (2) impact on clinical decision-making outcomes, defined as the proportion of patient cases for which the referring clinician reports they were able to implement one or more of the PCC recommendations.

Clinical effectiveness (patient)

A combination of PROMs38 and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) informed by phase 1d co-design activities will be adopted to the precision medicine context. To assess effectiveness at a patient level, the following secondary outcome measures will be explored: Satisfaction with Care (PATSAT-C33),39 views and attitudes (patient-perceived benefits and drawbacks of process and care model); Perceptions of Cancer Care Coordination, Shared Decision Making (SDM-Q-9), Decisional Conflict (SURE), Decisional Regret, views on informing relatives (genetics patients only), Perception of Uncertainties in Genomics (PUGS) (genetics patients only), altruism, Herth Hope Index (HHI short) and Health-related Quality of Life (EQ-5D-5L).40 Patient treatment, surveillance and status outcomes will also be explored via the patient and clinician surveys or via routine follow-up.

Exploratory outcomes

Service effectiveness

Effectiveness (eg, delivery and uptake of therapeutic recommendations, including novel treatments and clinical trials; delivery and uptake of genetic recommendations, including appropriate germline testing and cascade testing in family members); efficiency (eg, quality assessment of MDT case discussions, including work-up time and activities, specialty input, number of consultations/MDT required per case); equity (eg, service reach to rural, regional and remote patients) and timeliness (eg, time from referral to recommendation dissemination).

Data collection

A mixture of surveys, interviews and process evaluation data will be collected to assess effectiveness.

Clinician surveys will be used to measure the primary and secondary effectiveness outcomes. For each patient case referred to the molecular MDT, the referring clinician will complete a structured online survey at referral and post-MDT discussion to assess the perceived decision support value of the MDT service across eight predefined dimensions. The primary outcome is met if the referring physician answers ‘Yes’ to at least one of the eight dimensions. Referring clinicians will be able to opt in to additional online survey questions administered at baseline, 3 months, 6 months and 18 months from the timepoint of patient referral.

Patient surveys will be administered at baseline (ie, at referral), 3 months and 6 months. An additional survey will be administered to patients who attend a PCC consultation for a post-consult timepoint. A link to the online survey will be emailed to participants or participants can opt to complete a paper version sent via post or provided in clinic. Given that the majority of participants will be affected by cancer and undergoing active treatment, they will be given the option of nominating a proxy (eg, carer, family member) to complete surveys on their behalf—either with the participant present or in place of the participant. Participants who score poorly on the EQ-5D-5L measure (eg, level 4 or level 5 index score on any of the five quality-of-life domains) will be flagged for discussion with the PCC clinical team, who will be responsible for notifying the referring clinician.

Semi-structured interviews will be carried out with both interest holders and patients to provide in-depth qualitative data on the experiences of the PCC.

Observations of the PCC MDT will be conducted by members of the UNSW implementation research team (with necessary site contingent worker status approvals). The researcher will complete a de-identified semi-structured observation checklist for each PCC patient that is presented, including MDT work-up activities, areas of discussion and specialty input, discordant discussions and MDT outcomes.

Service-level outcomes data will be extracted from the hospital database to provide indicators of clinical effectiveness, including consultation and MDT information and billing. Timestamped data will be used to monitor intervals, including time from research result to PCC referral, time from referral to recommendation dissemination, time from identification of germline research result to clinical confirmation result. Patient demographics and the uptake of PCC telehealth services will be used to assess reach and equity of the service provided.

Sample size and data analysis

The primary outcome—proportion of patient cases discussed at the molecular MDT for whom the referring clinician reports at least one decision support dimension of service value being met—is a binary outcome (true if ≥1 service value is met; false if none were met). The null hypothesis (H0) states that P ≤ P0, meaning the MDT provides value in 30% or fewer cases. The alternative hypothesis (H1) states that P > P0, meaning the MDT provides value in more than 30% of cases. We aim to detect a true proportion of value (P1) of 0.50. A sample size of 67 achieves 95.678% power to detect a superiority proportion (P0) of 0.3000 using a one-sided exact binomial test with a target significance level of 0.050. The actual significance level achieved by this test is 0.047. To account for potential attrition in response to clinician surveys (10%), a total recruitment of 75 patients is expected.

Interim analysis is planned for both futility and efficacy when 50% of the planned evaluable patients have had the primary outcome assessed, that is, after 34 patients. A two-stage group sequential design will be employed using an O'Brien–Fleming type alpha-spending function to preserve the overall one-sided type I error rate at α=0.05, with a planned interim analysis at 50% information time (t1=0.5) and final analysis at t2=1.0. The Lan–DeMets alpha-spending approach will be used to approximate the O'Brien–Fleming boundaries, which are characteristically conservative at early interim looks to minimise the risk of premature trial termination. The corresponding efficacy boundaries are defined by approximate p value thresholds of p=0.0067814 at the interim analysis and p=0.0432186 at the final analysis, with the trial stopping for efficacy if either boundary is crossed. This design maintains statistical rigour while allowing for early demonstration of treatment benefit, with boundary crossing probabilities and expected sample size calculations based on the assumption that any boundary crossing results in trial termination.

Efficacy boundary at interim

The study will demonstrate early strong evidence of effectiveness if the observed proportion at interim analysis (Pinterim) is ≥0.70 and the one-sided p value testing Pinterim against P0=0.30 is less than the O’Brien–Fleming adjusted α (p<0.0067814). With Ninterim=34 and P0=0.30, the critical threshold requires Sinterim≥24 successes to satisfy both the proportion requirement (24/34=0.706 ≥ 0.70) and the statistical significance criterion under the sequential design boundaries.

Approaches to analyses of the secondary and exploratory clinic and service effectiveness outcome data will include qualitative, quantitative and, where appropriate, triangulation of data to corroborate patterns within the findings.

Activity 2b: pilot the implementation platform

Design

Implementation support strategies designed in phase I will be delivered at POWH to support the integration of the PCC into routine clinical practice. To assess the implementation effectiveness of the PCC implementation platform, as well as precursor implementation outcomes related to the service and clinical effectiveness, mixed-methods data collection approaches will be used.

Population

Health organisations, healthcare delivery professionals, healthcare management/administration professionals and precision medicine research personnel.

Intervention

The PCC implementation platform will be applied as part of the effectiveness-implementation trial.

Outcomes

The implementation strategies will be designed to integrate the PCC into clinical practice using Proctor’s ‘implementation outcomes’ as an initial guiding framework. These outcomes include acceptability (ie, satisfaction); appropriateness (ie, perceived fit); feasibility (ie, actual fit)41; adoption (ie, intention to try); fidelity (ie, intervention delivered as intended); penetration (ie, level of service access) and sustainability (ie, sustained use).

Data collection

Pre, mid and post the trial of the PCC, a mixture of service-level data, surveys, interviews, focus groups, project logs and process evaluation data will be collected to assess implementation outcomes.

Service-level data will be extracted from the hospital database to provide indicators of implementation effectiveness, including number and type of PCC referrals (ie, adoption), number of appropriate referrals and MDT discussions (ie, fidelity), referring clinician geographic location and specialty (ie, penetration).

Referring clinician surveys will incorporate validated implementation scales to assess the implementation outcomes of acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility (ie, AIM, IAM, FIM).41

Semi-structured interviews (guided by the CFIR 2.032 and Proctor42 frameworks) will be conducted with both interest holders and patients during the implementation phase to provide in-depth qualitative data on the implementation of the PCC and indicate implementation outcomes such as acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility. Interviews will be audio recorded, transcribed and de-identified for analysis.

Focus groups will be held with members of the PCC clinical and research teams to further assess implementation outcomes, track adaptations to the model of care, iteratively refine the process map developed in phase I and identify additional implementation support needs. Specific implementation expertise will be used to assess, using implementation logic models as a foundation and tracking system for adaptations, key mechanisms of action behind implementation success. Focus groups will be audio recorded and transcribed for analysis.

Project logs will be completed to monitor study progress and track key implementation outcomes, such as adoption (eg, number of staff attendees at clinical meetings), fidelity (eg, adaptations to the PCC) and cost (eg, resources, staff time).

Sample size and data analysis

All healthcare delivery and management/administration, IT staff and precision medicine research personnel involved in current or future delivery of precision medicine trials, cancer patient care and/or infrastructure in oncology will be invited to participate. Approaches to analyses of the service level and implementation outcome data will include qualitative (for interview/focus group and some observational data), quantitative (eg, pre-post comparison of validated implementation outcomes scales; costs of implementation strategies) and where appropriate triangulation of data to corroborate patterns within the findings (eg, patient interviews can be compared with experiences of healthcare professionals in terms of adoption and delivery of the PCC which could eventually lead to the design of approaches for adaptation). As in phase I, qualitative analyses will be guided by the implementation frameworks: CFIR 2.0,32 TDF and Proctor’s implementation outcomes taxonomy.42 Adaptations will be captured using the model for adaptation design and impact,43 which incorporates constructs from the Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications-Enhanced.44 An example logic model demonstrating the relationships between implementation, service and patient outcomes is provided in figure 5.

Activity 2c: test cost-effectiveness of the PCC

Design

Within-trial analysis that will synthesise costs and outcomes using cost-effectiveness and cost-consequences analysis.

Population, intervention, comparator and outcomes

As per 2a/2b.

Data collection

A process map will be constructed to identify the required resources to implement the clinic. The resources used to implement the clinic, including the time spent conducting research and MDT workup, will be captured using tracking logs completed by the clinician, genetic counsellor and administration teams. The time spent discussing patients during MDT meetings will be captured through the MDT observation template. Members of the research team will then work to ensure unit costs are collected according to plan. Cost logs will be kept, including unit costs for hospital staff which will be determined by their role and the average salary per hour, and invoices retained for consumables (eg, travel costs, stationery costs and printing costs). Medicare Benefits Schedule items (if) claimed and the costs of additional testing (eg, germline testing) will be collected through hospital systems.

Outcomes

Outcomes will be captured using the clinic referral form, and the referring clinician and patient surveys. Clinician survey asks about the average length of time spent on various tasks before the clinic (eg, interpreting genomic results and researching therapies and, at 3 months, changes in the planned clinical management and task efficiency). Patient survey assesses satisfaction, quality of life, psychological and decisional outcomes. Additionally, costs associated with the implementation platform will be documented during development and testing.

Data analysis

A within-trial analysis approach will be conducted, where only the health outcomes and costs observed during the trial will be included. Modelling of long-term health outcomes will be infeasible due to the wide variety of cancers involved. The impact of the PCC on outcomes (decision support, clinical management, time spent conducting research, patient satisfaction, utilities, psychological and decisional outcomes) will be assessed using descriptive statistics and maximum-likelihood linear mixed-effects models. Costs, consequences and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios will be estimated. Non-parametric bootstrapping will be used to estimate incremental cost-effectiveness ratios. The economic evaluation will be conducted according to the best-practice guidelines.45 46

Phase III: develop and pilot test a precision care scale-up model and toolkit

The final research phase involves designing a scale-up trial to test the effectiveness of the PCC scale-up framework and toolkit in achieving adoption of the model of care. While initial planning will leverage off the existing Omico site partnerships and relationships established through this project, the specific approach to piloting the framework and toolkit will remain flexible. Depending on how the scale-up model evolves as a result of phase I and II effort, this may or may not involve additional physical hospital sites. Phase III will employ a type II hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial design, with activities informed by the findings from phases I and II. A phase III research protocol will be submitted for Human Research Ethics Committee review in a future ethics amendment.

Discussion

The Precision Care Initiative, being uniquely designed to incorporate co-design from its inception, integrate research into clinical decision-making in real time, and use an evidence-driven implementation platform, is likely the first of its kind to address the complexity of wholistic precision medicine and integrated support for managing complex cases within routine oncology practice. POWH is ideally positioned for piloting the PCC, with a familial cancer clinic embedded and existing referral mechanisms from the tumour sequencing research programmes. The POWH has confirmed participation in the development, implementation and testing of the PCC. Upon completion, phase III scale-up activities will be developed and will be subject to subsequent ethics amendment and governance approvals.

When designing and evaluating a health system intervention to be initially tested in a real-world setting, identifying ways to separate and specify the extent to which the intervention—versus its implementation—has impacted outcomes for patients is crucial. Hybrid effectiveness-implementation trials are a novel approach to promote rapid translation of clinically effective interventions into practice, blending efficacy and effectiveness stages of clinical and implementation studies to evaluate strategies and explore factors affecting implementation effects simultaneously.47 While the PCC will be tested for effectiveness as part of this programme of work in a real-world setting, the design and evaluation of a corresponding implementation strategy is crucial to success. Furthermore, for health system managers and policymakers to justify the allocation of resources for new interventions and implementation strategies to optimise care, there is a need not only to establish the clinical and service level effectiveness of the intervention but also the cost-effectiveness of the resources required to implement it.48 49

Through real-time collection of electronic data about implementation and patient outcomes with rapid research synthesis and analysis, the proposed Learning Health System will be uniquely designed to rapidly identify challenges and opportunities during the implementation and operation of the PCCs to engage more efficient changes in precision oncology. The platform will provide infrastructure for embedding precision-oncology research into practice, thereby enhancing the long-term sustainability of the PCC. This vision is depicted in figure 6.

Vision of the Precision Care Clinic.

Implementation logic models50 will be designed to specify the inputs, activities, outputs, moderators and expected short-term, medium-term and long-term outcomes flowing from implementation, service and clinical activities and outcomes. As shown in figure 4, implementation outcomes and their antecedent assessments (eg, feasibility, acceptability, adoption, fidelity) are necessary precursors for changes in service outcomes, and ultimately patient (clinical) outcomes.42 Therefore, to follow this required order of events for patient outcomes to be achieved, our research activities will focus on pilot testing the implementation effectiveness of the PCC and on clinical effectiveness of the PCC. The cost-effectiveness of both the PCC and its implementation will be presented third.

The implementation strategies designed to support the PCC in practice will depend on the target behavioural and system changes needed and the associated barriers identified.51 Accordingly, resources required for each strategy will also vary depending on start-up and ongoing costs (including recurrent and capital items) and the organisational level(s) and department(s) involved. For example, educational materials for patients and clinicians to improve genomic knowledge. This would involve costing the initial start-up resources needed to develop and deliver the educational material, and any staff time for attendance at educational sessions, after which there would be no anticipated ongoing costs. In contrast, changes to the reporting of test results would require software modifications.

We will engage with key interest holders to drive the co-design of the PCC and its implementation platform, enabling knowledge production and translation to support wider uptake and sustainability. Co-design is an effective approach that facilitates dialogue between different interest holders in developing, tailoring and implementing interventions to ensure rapid translation to practice.52 Establishing new complex pathways of care requires—at a system and practice level—an understanding of existing underlying processes, variation in approaches and influences on implementation. Process mapping is commonly used to map clinical pathways with interest holder consultation and can be a useful tool to identify clinical or patient behaviours that could be targeted for change53,55 and to co-design interventions and accompanying implementation strategies to optimise practice.53

Combining precision medicine, implementation science, clinical informatics, cancer genetics, oncology, patient-reported measures and health economics, the Precision Care Initiative will be a leading innovative transformational research programme that supports the rapid adoption, optimisation, scale-up and sustainment of precision medicine practice and research into routine care across multiple health services in Australia.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval has been obtained from the St Vincent’s Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (2023/ETH00373), along with site-specific governance approvals from the South Eastern Sydney Local Health District Board (2023/STE00574). Written informed consent will be obtained from study participants, where applicable. All personal information will be de-identified to maintain confidentiality when reporting data and findings. Study data will be securely stored in accordance with ethical and institutional policies for a minimum of 15 years following publication.

Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, scientific conferences and relevant media channels. All research outputs will report aggregated results, and no individual participant will be identifiable.
