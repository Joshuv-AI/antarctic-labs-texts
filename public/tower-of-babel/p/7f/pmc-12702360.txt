A Rare Metastatic Fibrous Tumor: Review of Literature With a Case Report
Authors: Gowri Swaminathan, Jonathan Muratori, Faateh Rauf, Santino Patrizi, Theo Trandafirescu, Larry A Sonna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) can be either localized or metastatic. We present a rare case of a 69-year-old patient with a metastatic solitary fibrous tumor in uncommon anatomical locations, treated with a short course of pazopanib. While surgical resection remains the primary treatment for localized SFT, chemotherapy has been used for advanced cases. Our unique case highlights the need for more research into treatment options to improve patient outcomes. Since both benign and malignant SFTs can metastasize, mortality is mainly due to thoracic metastases; further studies are needed to understand the prognostic factors that influence disease spread versus local growth.Keywords: Solitary fibrous tumor, metastases, Intrathoracic, Intraabdominal, Benign, Malignant, Case report

Introduction

Solitary fibrous tumor (SFT) is a rare and ubiquitous mesenchymal tumor with an annual incidence of one new case per million. It primarily affects adults aged 20 to 70 years. The metastatic risk is approximately 35% to 45%, with the abdominal cavity accounting for 31%, the limbs for 29%, the pleura for 22%, the trunk for 11%, and other sites, including the head and neck, for 7%.1 We present a unique case report of a 69-year-old woman with SFT that metastasized to atypical anatomical sites.

Case Presentation

A 69-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with syncope and two episodes of vomiting. The patient had a significant past medical history of coronary artery disease, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and type II diabetes mellitus for 42 years, in addition to a left below-knee amputation with phantom leg syndrome and right second-digit amputation. Computed tomography (CT) of the head and neck revealed bilateral carotid artery stenosis, which was attributed to the syncope; it also coincidentally revealed bilateral pulmonary nodules in the apical lungs. CT of the chest revealed a left lower lobe versus posterior mediastinal mass lesion (Fig. 1). CT of the abdomen demonstrated a large, necrotic mass measuring 7.2 × 7.1 cm in the left lower lobe of the lung and a lesion in the left liver lobe measuring approximately 2.3 cm at its largest diameter (Fig. 2).

Coronal section of computed tomography chest revealing bilateral multiple pulmonary nodules.

Axial section of computed tomography abdomen revealing the left hepatic lobe lesion.

After 3 months, the patient presented to the pulmonary clinic with a dry cough, exertional dyspnea, decreased exercise tolerance, and unintentional weight loss. A chest radiograph (Fig. 3) revealed disease progression, characterized by an interval increase in pulmonary nodules across multiple lobes. She was not a known smoker. The positron emission tomography scan (Fig. 4) revealed multiple metabolically active lesions: (Fig. 4A) a 2.6-cm right thyroid lobe nodule, (Fig. 4B) a mass at the left lung base and left hepatic lobe, (Fig. 4C) numerous pulmonary nodules, and (Fig. 4D) at the right posterior ilium. The specimen from the CT-guided percutaneous left lung biopsy was primarily hypocellular, with areas of hypercellularity consisting of short-spindled cells (Fig. 5D). Immunostains were positive for STAT6 (Fig. 5A) and CD34 (Fig. 5B) and negative for AE1/AE3, cytokeratin, CD31, S100, and desmin. A Ki-67 proliferative index of approximately 40% was observed in the hypercellular areas (Fig. 5C). Beta-catenin immunostaining revealed a membranous expression pattern, with no mitotic figures, consistent with a diagnosis of solitary fibrous tumor.

Chest radiograph revealing an interval increase in the number of pulmonary nodules across multilobar segments.

Positron emission tomography–computed tomography scan revealing multiple metabolically active lesions (indicated with blue and orange arrows) in the (A) right thyroid lobe nodule (blue), (B) left lung base (blue) and left hepatic lobe mass (orange), (C) numerous pulmonary nodules (blue), and (D) right posterior ilium (blue).

(Clockwise from top left to bottom left) (A) STAT6 positive, (B) CD34 positive on immunohistochemistry stains, (C) Ki-67 proliferative index of 40%, and (D) hypercellular area with spindled tumor cells with focal cytologic atypia.

Chest imaging (Fig. 6) revealed the presence of growing pulmonary nodules against the backdrop of worsening symptoms in the following weeks. An image-guided thyroid and liver biopsy was conducted. Histopathology confirmed the spread of a metastatic solitary fibrous tumor to the lung, liver, thyroid, and two left level IV lymph nodes. The patient was started on pazopanib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor that targets VEGFR, PDGFR, and Kit receptors. Her clinical course was complicated by prolonged hospitalization due to pulmonary embolism, respiratory failure, and stroke; she passed away a few months later after a cardiac arrest.

Discussion

SFT is a rare type of mesenchymal neoplasm that can develop in various locations throughout the body, though they are most frequently found in the thoracic cavity. In 1942, Stout and Murray described them as “solitary mesothelioma of the pleura.” They also referred to SFT as hemangiopericytoma, a term now considered obsolete according to the recent WHO classification of thoracic tumors.2 Stout and Hamidi later renamed these tumors “solitary fibrous tumors” in 1951.3

Epidemiology

SFTs represent a broad spectrum of fibrous neoplasms that are rarely malignant and have low metastatic potential. They can remain asymptomatic for an extended period, creating a diagnostic conundrum. These fibrous tumors can manifest almost anywhere in the body,1 most often in the thorax, followed by the meninges and abdomen.4,5 SFTs have an age-adjusted yearly incidence of one new case per million people.1 The pleura has been identified as the most common site6; other frequently involved locations include the meninges (27%), abdominal cavity (20%), trunk (10%), extremities (8%), and head and neck (5%).7 Typically occurring in adults aged between 20 and 70 years, SFTs have a peak incidence in the fifth and sixth decades of life.8 Our literature review does not indicate any sex predilection.9,10

The risk of metastasis has been observed to be as high as 35% to 45%, or even higher, in case series with longer follow-up periods. One series reported a 5-year metastasis-free rate of 74%, whereas the 10-year metastasis-free rate decreased by 55%.11,12 Recurrences were noted after 10 years in 10% of the patients with SFT.13 Therefore, extended surveillance is essential in managing patients with SFTs.

Etiopathogenesis

Research to date has suggested that these tumors originate from mesenchymal cells,9 although there is no consensus on the precise type of cells from which they arise.14 To date, no clear environmental factors have been identified that increase the risk of SFT.9 The recurrent fusion of the NAB2 and STAT6 genes, located on chromosome 12q13, has been proposed as one of the key genetic alterations playing a crucial role in the pathogenesis of SFT.3 Research has revealed that the underlying transcript between NGFI-A binding protein 2 (EGR1 binding protein 2) (NAB2) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) is formed after at least one repressor domain of NAB2 is replaced by a transactivation domain of STAT6, leading to the overexpression of EGR1, the primary target of the NAB2 gene. The STAT6 gene encodes a cytoplasmic protein functioning as a transcription factor, whereas NAB2, which is localized to the nucleus, acts as a transcriptional repressor by interacting with the EGR1 target. The transcript NAB2–STAT6 serves as a transcriptional activator, promoting the expression of EGR1 target genes. Interestingly, this transcript translocates to the nucleus, resulting in higher levels of NAB2 expression (also a target of EGR1) compared with other tumors or normal tissues. This phenomenon explains the characteristic nuclear STAT6 immunostaining, which is highly beneficial for diagnosing SFT.15 Notably, STAT6 can induce fibrosis within the interactive network involved in wound healing, which includes macrophages, CD4+ (TH2) cells, and fibroblasts, through signaling by IL-13. In any case, it seems prudent to consider STAT6 as a potential key protein in the pathogenesis of SFT as well. The fact that EGR1 is a target of both NAB2 as a transcriptional repressor and STAT6 as a transcription factor creates an intriguing intersection, even when an external factor beyond the canonical IL-13/STAT6 pathway—which induces phosphorylation and subsequent dimerization of STAT6 to enter the nucleus—is necessary. Furthermore, as NAB2 and STAT6 are involved in regulating inflammation, collagen production, fibroblast activation, and vessel formation, a more in-depth study of the microenvironment in SFT is essential to unravel the mechanisms underlying each of these processes.1

Clinical Presentation

SFTs have been reported in various anatomical sites, though they seem to arise preferentially in serosal membranes. Most SFTs appear histologically benign (80%) but can still exhibit malignant behavior, especially when tumor sizes are more than or equal to 10 cm. Notably, a retrospective study of 139 patients with pathologically proven SFT reported that 49 developed metastases; the researchers found that malignant pathology and a mitotic count more than or equal to 4 per 10 high-power fields (HPFs) were associated with metastatic disease in a multivariate analysis (p = 0.01; HR, 0.22; 95% CI, 0.05–0.73).12 Malignant SFTs have been defined as hypercellular with increased mitotic activity, characterized by more than or equal to 4 mitoses per 10 HPFs,16, 17, 18, 19 typically associated with cytologic atypia, tumor necrosis, or infiltrative margins.20,21 Advanced age has been reported as a prognostic factor for SFT; however, it remains unclear whether this applies to benign or malignant cases.11,22 The patient reported here exhibited low mitotic activity, consistent with a benign SFT.

Intrathoracic SFTs are the most common location, followed by intra-abdominal SFTs.16,23,24 Symptoms depend on the site of SFTs.15,22,23 Approximately 50% to 80% of intrathoracic SFTs present as asymptomatic masses, typically discovered incidentally on chest imaging, as in our patient.16,25,26 The principal mode of metastasis is through hematogenous dissemination, whereas lymphatic spread is considered uncommon.12 Patients with histologically benign SFTs can also develop metastatic disease, and patients with histologically malignant SFTs may not experience recurrence or metastatic disease.13,27, 28, 29 Higher rates of metastatic disease have been reported in extrathoracic SFTs than in thoracic SFTs.20,30 Metastatic disease in the lungs has been identified as a primary cause of mortality associated with SFTs.12 It is plausible that our patient had a primary SFT in the liver that metastasized to other sites such as the thyroid, ilium, and lung parenchyma over the years, ultimately leading to her death.

Paraneoplastic syndromes occur in less than 10% of pleural SFT.1 The two paraneoplastic manifestations associated with these SFTs are paraneoplastic osteoarthropathy (also known as pulmonary osteoarthropathy or Pierre-Marie-Bamberg syndrome) and, more rarely, paraneoplastic hypoglycemia (also known as Doege-Potter syndrome).25,31 Hypoglycemia is most closely associated with malignant SFT and may be related to the production of insulin-like growth factors.14

Diagnosis

Imaging with CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is essential for localizing the tumor and characterizing its features. Metastatic disease can be detected using 18F-FDG positron emission tomography/CT, as demonstrated in our patient, who demonstrated the spread of SFT to the liver, lungs, thyroid, and ilium bone.

Diagnosis is confirmed based on histopathologic characteristics and immunohistochemistry. A typical SFT consists of randomly arranged cells with a spindle or ovoid shape within a collagenous stroma, intermixed with blood vessels that exhibit a characteristic staghorn shape and perivascular fibrosis. This arrangement of cellular and stromal components is referred to as a “patternless pattern,” and the spectrum of this combination can vary between paucicellular, characterized by spindle-shaped cells within a prominent fibrous stroma, and hypercellular, where the stroma is barely detectable. Classic SFTs demonstrate low mitotic activity with low counts,32 whereas, as previously mentioned, malignant SFTs are characterized by high mitotic activity and features of cellular atypia.

Immunohistochemically, a strong nuclear staining of STAT6 is characteristic of SFT. The genes NAB2 and STAT6 are involved in the regulation of inflammation, collagen production, fibroblast activation, and vessel formation. The transcript NAB2-STAT6 acts as a transcriptional activator, inducing the expression of EGR1 target genes, which is a transcription factor involved in various cellular processes, including growth, differentiation, apoptosis, and wound healing.1 Additional studies have identified the GRIA2 gene and aldehyde dehydrogenase I as promising new diagnostic markers for SFT.3

Potential Mimics

Termed as the “greatest simulator” of all soft tissue neoplasms, SFTs can pose a diagnostic challenge and require clinical suspicion, histopathologic and immunohistochemical confirmation, along with molecular features.33 Depending on the anatomical location of the SFT, it can resemble other soft tissue tumors from different origins, including, but not limited to, schwannoma, spindle-cell lipoma, dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, liposarcoma, gastrointestinal stromal tumor, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, and synovial sarcoma.3 Intrapulmonary tumors, such as those in our patient, need to be distinguished from sarcomatoid carcinoma. Infrequently, squamous cell carcinoma, carcinoid tumor, and malignant mesothelioma can mimic SFT.34

Management

Surgical resection of SFT remains the cornerstone for both localized disease and situations with limited metastatic disease.1 For pleural SFTs, pulmonary wedge resection is often used due to the tumor’s pedunculated nature. Other often-performed resection methods for pleural SFTs include isolated parietal pleural resection, chest wall resections, or pulmonary wedge resections.35 Video-assisted thoracic surgery has been successfully conducted on SFTs measuring less than 10 cm.36 Embolization is an effective preoperative strategy for controlling intraoperative bleeding with sessile SFTs.37 Both intracranial and intraabdominal SFTs often lend themselves to piecemeal resections; however, prior studies with a follow-up period of 43 months do not indicate a prognostic difference between en bloc and piecemeal resections.38 Adjuvant radiotherapy can play a role in managing SFT, especially for localized SFT or oligometastatic disease with piecemeal resection. Notably, 5-year local control rates in approximately 89 patients with SFT who received postoperative radiotherapy were 90% versus 60% (p value = 0.052), thus demonstrating a clear benefit when radiotherapy is added in the adjuvant setting.39

Chemotherapy is frequently used for advanced or metastatic SFTs, as in our patient. Most available evidence regarding chemotherapy pertains to anthracycline-based regimens; the progression-free survival (PFS) has been noted to be 3 to 5 months, with an overall response rate ranging from 10.5% to 20%.40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45 Dacarbazine and temozolomide have also demonstrated positive results in SFT.1 Trabectedin is an approved treatment for pretreated metastatic soft-tissue sarcomas.46 The STRADA trial is currently assessing the efficacy of adriamycin combined with dacarbazine compared with trabectedin alone in a phase II randomized study (https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT03023124). In addition, a phase II trial involving eribulin, a microtubule inhibitor, is underway in Japan (https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT03023124).

SFTs are believed to be highly vascular tumors with elevated expression of platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR).47 The combination of temozolomide and bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody that targets VEGF, was among the first regimens to incorporate an anti-angiogenic agent in SFT.48 Other anti-angiogenic agents currently under investigation for use in SFTs include sunitinib, a multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitor that affects VEGFR1-3, PDGFRβ, Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 (Flt3), KIT, and rearranged during transfection (RET),49 and sorafenib, another multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitor that targets VEGFR 1–3, PDGFRβ, FGFR, and RAF.50

Maruzzo et al.51 reported data from a single-institution experience with pazopanib, a multi-targeted receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor that targets VEGFR 1–3, PDGFR, and KIT, revealing modest activity against fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) 1–3. This was the medication administered to our patient. Pazopanib has been tested in two cohorts of SFTs: low-aggressive or typical and malignant or dedifferentiated. In the aggressive SFT cohort (malignant and dedifferentiated SFT), 36 patients were enrolled across 16 centers in Italy, France, and Spain. Furthermore, of the 35 patients evaluated, there were two with partial response (PR) (6%), 21 with stable disease (SD) (60%), and 12 with progression of disease (PD) (34%), according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors, with a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 5.57 months. However, the enrollment of dedifferentiated cases was halted due to rapid disease progression with pazopanib.32 In the low-aggressive cohort, among the 31 patients evaluated, according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors criteria, there were two with PR (6%) and 29 with SD (94%).52 The median progression-free survival (PFS) was assessed at 9.8 months, making pazopanib one of the first-line medications in this cohort, based on both its toxicity profile and efficacy results from a phase II trial.32,52 The authors suggest a possible contribution to the longer PFS in the low-aggressive cohort due to indolent biology, which may still be maintained even when metastases develop.1 Our patient developed venous thromboembolism, possibly due to the hypercoagulable state from SFT itself, the reported increase in venous thrombosis caused by pazopanib, or as a result of functional decline from the disease's burden.

Prognosis

The median survival of all 139 patients with SFT was 212 (interquartile range, 71–288) months, whereas the median overall survival of patients with SFT with metastatic disease was 125 (interquartile range 22–152) months (p < 0.001).12 Nonetheless, the overall prognosis of SFTs is generally favorable, with a wide spectrum of histopathologic variation, as most tumors are benign, despite exhibiting locally aggressive behavior, and only a small subset of patients displays metastatic behavior. As we have already learned, patients with histologically benign SFT can develop metastatic disease, and patients with histologically malignant SFT might not experience recurrence or metastatic disease13,27, 28, 29; therefore, mortality has been attributed to the thoracic metastases.52

Conclusion

Given what we know about the natural history of SFTs overall, a paradigm shift is necessary. Our case report and literature review indicate that the initial histopathology of SFT does not predict the tumor's metastatic potential or determine the clinical outcome. Because the morbidity and mortality linked to SFTs seem to stem from their metastatic potential, future studies focused on identifying tumor markers specific to this potential are vital for improving outcomes. Thus, further research is needed to evaluate management strategies to find whether early surgical removal provides any advantages than standard “lung nodule surveillance.” There may also be value in studying the optimal interval for follow-up imaging if early surgical intervention is not performed. However, the rarity of these tumors presents a significant obstacle for future research.
