A Rare Monogenic Metabolic Syndrome From a DYRK1B Variant: Management Challenges and Insights
Authors: Somdatta Giri, Ayan Roy, Atanu Kumar Dutta, Shinjan Patra, Shaik Mohammad Tahaseen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic syndrome is typically polygenic; however, rare monogenic forms such as DYRK1B-associated abdominal obesity-metabolic syndrome 3 (AOMS 3) exist. Clinical Relevance

This case underscores the clinical importance of recognizing monogenic forms of metabolic syndrome, particularly in young patients presenting with early-onset, atypical, or treatment-resistant features that may mimic more common conditions such as polycystic ovarian syndrome. Identification of a DYRK1B variant expands the phenotypic spectrum of abdominal obesity-metabolic syndrome 3 and highlights the role of detailed metabolic profiling and genetic testing in appropriate clinical contexts, especially when there is familial clustering. Importantly, the observed improvement with sodium glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor therapy suggests a potential targeted therapeutic strategy in such patients, emphasizing the value of precision medicine in optimizing outcomes beyond conventional treatment approaches.

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome is traditionally regarded as a multifactorial disorder resulting from complex interactions between polygenic susceptibility and environmental factors.1 Advances in next-generation sequencing have, however, identified rare monogenic contributors that provide mechanistic insight into metabolic disease. Variants in DYRK1B are associated with an autosomal dominant form of metabolic syndrome, termed abdominal obesity–metabolic syndrome 3 (AOMS3).2 Up to 1.3% of individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes may carry a DYRK1B variant, indicating potential under-recognition of monogenic metabolic disease.3 We report a young female who was initially diagnosed with polycystic ovarian syndrome with diabetes, but was subsequently found to have a monogenic disorder due to DYRK1B variant. This case highlights a single-gene variant as a potential cause of metabolic syndrome, offering new insights into its molecular pathogenesis. We report a young woman initially diagnosed with polycystic ovarian syndrome and diabetes who was subsequently found to carry a heterozygous DYRK1B variant. Although previously classified as functionally benign, we found this variant to have significant clinical manifestation. Comprehensive dynamic metabolic phenotyping was undertaken to characterize the disease process. While DYRK1B variants are known to exhibit age-dependent and typically full penetrance, the patient’s mother—who carries the same variant—remains only modestly metabolically affected in her fifth decade, whereas the index case manifested a full phenotype in her twenties. Additional distinctive features included a hyperandrogenic phenotype, severe hypertriglyceridemia, and absence of prominent adipogenesis, features not previously described in DYRK1B-related disease. This case broadens the phenotypic spectrum of DYRK1B-associated metabolic disease and highlights its clinical heterogeneity. There are limited data on treatment response too. Longitudinal metabolic improvement following SGLT2 inhibitor therapy provides further clinical insight and warrants future investigation.

Investigations

The patient underwent standard anthropometric assessment, and evaluation of hyperandrogenism using the modified Ferriman–Gallwey score. A 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test was performed with glucose and insulin measured at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. Insulin resistance and β-cell function were assessed using the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance, the Matsuda Index, and the Insulin Secretion–Sensitivity Index-2 (ISSI-2). The insulin tolerance test (ITT) was performed with intravenous insulin at 0.15 IU/kg. Log-transformed plasma glucose values were plotted against time to derive the rate constant for plasma glucose disappearance (KITT); a KITT <1%/min was considered indicative of severe insulin resistance. Hepatic steatosis was assessed using transient elastography (FibroScan, Echosens), and body composition was evaluated by whole-body dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry in Hologic Horizon Wi.

Whole-exome sequencing was performed on genomic DNA extracted from peripheral blood using a custom target-enrichment capture kit. Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina next-generation sequencing platform, achieving a mean on-target coverage of >80–100×, with the variant of interest covered at 34× depth. Sequence reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using the BWA aligner, and variant calling was performed following GATK best-practice workflows implemented via Sentieon, including duplicate removal, base quality recalibration, and indel realignment. Variant calling was performed using the Sentieon HaplotypeCaller. Identified variants were annotated using the VariMAT pipeline, with gene annotation performed using Ensembl variant effect predictor against Ensembl release 104. Copy number variants were assessed from targeted sequencing data using the ExomeDepth algorithm. Variant interpretation incorporated multiple disease and population databases, including ClinVar, OMIM, HGMD, LOVD, DECIPHER, SwissVar, gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, TOPMed, and an internal Indian population database (MedVarDb v4.0). Functional impact of nonsynonymous variants was predicted using PolyPhen-2, SIFT, MutationTaster2, and LRT.

Exon 6 of the DYRK1B gene was polymerase chain reaction-amplified, and the product was sequenced by Sanger sequencing, which has an analytical detection limit of ∼12% for variant detection. Sequences were aligned to the reference transcript ENST00000323039.10 using standard variant-analysis software. The variant was not confirmed by Sanger in the proband. However, since the mother had the variant, it is indirectly confirmed. Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient. A waiver of ethics committee approval was granted by the institutional authority.

Case Presentation

A 21-year-old female presented with oligomenorrhoea and clinical features of hyperandrogenism, including progressive hirsutism over the past 7 years. Menarche occurred at age 12 years, subsequently she developed oligomenorrhea. During evaluation, she was incidentally diagnosed with diabetes mellitus. On physical examination, she exhibited central obesity (waist circumference 92 cm, normal range <80 cm; BMI 25.6 kg/m2, overweight 23-27.9 kg/m2), acanthosis nigricans, and had a modified FGS of 8/36, suggestive of mild hirsutism (Fig. 1). Initial investigations revealed elevated fasting (179 mg/dL) and postprandial (329 mg/dL) glucose levels, with a HbA1c of 10.6%, severe hypertriglyceridemia (1517 mg/dL), and evidence of grade II hepatic steatosis (6.0 KPa -F0-F1, CAP- 277 dB/m) with hepatomegaly (18.8 cm) on abdominal ultrasonography. An OGTT revealed marked insulin resistance accompanied by inadequate beta-cell compensation, consistent with a type 2 diabetes mellitus phenotype (Table). This was further supported by the ITT, which showed a low insulin sensitivity index; the rate constant for plasma glucose disappearance (KITT) was 0.45%/min, indicating severe insulin resistance (Fig. 2). DXA body composition analysis showed a normal fat mass index but elevated visceral adipose tissue area, along with increased android-to-gynoid fat ratio and trunk-to-limb fat mass ratio. She had proteinuria (urine ACR 164 mg/g); but no retinopathy or peripheral neuropathy. She was initiated on metformin, glimepiride, and pioglitazone, but her glycaemic control remained suboptimal, with HbA1c decreasing to 9.1% after 6 months of this initial therapy (Fig. 3). She underwent a short course of insulin infusion (requiring 48 units insulin over 48 h), followed by the introduction of dapagliflozin, a sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor in place of glimepiride. The patient showed an excellent clinical response – not only did glycaemic parameters improve (HbA1c 7.3% after 6 months), but her blood pressure normalized (previously uncontrolled despite amlodipine 5 mg for 3 months), serum triglyceride declined from 1517 mg/dl to 192 mg/dl, and serum testosterone levels dropped significantly from 89 ng/dL to 25 ng/dL after 3 months of therapy. However, she was also on statin and fenofibrate. She experienced no therapy related adverse effects following dapagliflozin initiation. Figure 4 shows post treatment clinical image of the patient.

Discussion

The DYRK1B gene encodes the dual-specificity tyrosine-phosphorylation-regulated kinase 1B, a member of the evolutionarily conserved family of serine/threonine protein kinases involved in adipogenesis and glucose metabolism.4 DYRK1B contains a characteristic tyrosine-X-tyrosine motif within its activation loop, where autophosphorylation of the second tyrosine is essential for kinase activation and function.5 Variants in DYRK1B have been associated with an autosomal dominant form of metabolic syndrome termed abdominal obesity–metabolic syndrome 3 (AOMS3; OMIM #615812).2 These pathogenic variants are thought to inhibit the Sonic Hedgehog and WNT signaling pathways, leading to increased expression of adipogenic transcription factors such as C/EBP-α and PPAR-γ, ultimately promoting adipogenesis and insulin resistance.6 Earlier reports described 2 missense DYRK1B variants, p.R102C and p.H90P, in families of Iranian and European descent, both of which act as potent inhibitors of the WNT signaling pathway, thereby promoting adipogenesis. These variants also enhanced glucose-6-phosphatase expression – suggesting increased hepatic gluconeogenesis. Subsequent investigations identified 65 additional rare heterozygous variants, predicted to be deleterious by in silico analyses and found in families with AOMS. Among these functional analysis revealed that 6 variants (encoding p.G120V, p.H179L, p.D259N, p.P282L, p.R349 W, and p.R358) showed a fully inhibitory impact on WNT signaling.3 These null variants have stronger effect on obesity and diabetes risk. These variants also enhance de novo lipogenesis by activating mTORC2, resulting in elevated diacylglycerol levels in the plasma membrane. This, in turn, activates protein kinase C (PKCε), which phosphorylates threonine residues of the insulin receptor kinase, thereby inhibiting its activity and impairing hepatic insulin signaling.7 The consequent reduction in insulin action promotes increased hepatic glucose production.8 The hepatic steatosis and hypertriglyceridemia observed in our patient may be explained by this mechanism of increased hepatic lipogenesis. Interestingly, the patient's metabolic parameters improved with an SGLT2 inhibitor, prompting the question of whether the observed benefits were solely due to insulin-independent glucose lowering, or if they also involved modulation of hepatic lipid metabolism. However, the observed metabolic improvements following dapagliflozin initiation are associative, particularly in the context of a single case, and should be confirmed in future studies before any definitive conclusion. DYRK1B is also expressed in pancreatic beta cells, where it promotes beta cell apoptosis. This is particularly noteworthy in our patient and in her mother, who exhibited both severe insulin resistance and beta cell dysfunction, as evidenced by a low ISSI-2. This dual impairment raises the possibility that selective DYRK1B inhibition might represent a novel therapeutic strategy to improve both insulin action and secretion.9

A study by Mendoza-Caamal et al identified the p.Arg252His variant in DYRK1B as a causative mutation in patients initially diagnosed with type 2 diabetes.10 In silico modelling suggested that the substitution of arginine with histidine at position 252 disrupted hydrogen bonding with Pro552 and Ser554, likely leading to conformational changes in the catalytic domain and loss of function. Later, Detro-Dassen et al conducted a functional study on the R252H variant and found that this substitution did not impair the catalytic activity of DYRK1B.11 This raises the question of how the R252H variant contributes to disease pathogenesis despite preserved catalytic activity, particularly in our patient who exhibited a florid clinical phenotype. It is therefore likely that the DYRK1B variant exerts its effects through a kinase-independent mechanism, as demonstrated by Bhat et al.7

DYRK1B variants are known to exhibit age-dependent penetrance and variable expressivity. However, in our case, the patient's mother – who carries the same variant – remains metabolically modestly affected in her fifties. This observation points toward incomplete penetrance and highlights the possibility of protective genetic or environmental modifiers. Another notable aspect of this case is the absence of prominent adipogenesis along with the presence of a hyperandrogenic phenotype, which has not been previously described. DYRK1B-related hyperandrogenism has been rarely reported in the literature. We hypothesize that the hyperandrogenic state in this condition is largely a manifestation of severe insulin resistance. In contrast, hyperandrogenism in classic PCOS is typically of mixed ovarian and adrenal origin. In DYRK1B-related disease, the precise source of androgen excess remains uncertain and would require a detailed hormonal and dynamic evaluation to delineate its origin. These phenotypic discrepancies underscore the complexity of DYRK1B-related disease expression and the need for further functional studies to elucidate the modifiers that influence disease onset and severity.

Glucotoxicity likely contributed to the initial suboptimal response to oral antidiabetic therapy. Short-term insulin infusion was used to mitigate glucotoxicity; however, insulin was not continued beyond 48 h. The patient was subsequently maintained on dapagliflozin in addition to her existing oral antidiabetic agents and adherence to a diabetic diet. Over the ensuing months, a progressive and sustained improvement in glycemic control was observed. Although the initial insulin infusion likely facilitated reversal of glucotoxicity, the sustained glycemic improvement occurred in the setting of ongoing combination therapy, including dapagliflozin. We acknowledge that alternative explanations may have contributed to the observed metabolic improvements, including regression to the mean given the markedly abnormal baseline values; however, the magnitude of change exceeded what would be expected from measurement variability alone. Variability related to the timing of biochemical assessments is unlikely, as standardized dynamic testing and departmental protocols were followed. With respect to lipid parameters, improvements were influenced by concurrent lipid-lowering therapy; discontinuation of fenofibrate was followed by a rise in triglycerides, indicating that lipid changes could not be attributed solely to improved glycemic control or SGLT2 inhibition.

This report has several important limitations. First, the findings are derived from a single case, limiting generalizability and precluding causal inference regarding genotype-phenotype relationships or therapeutic effects. Second, segregation analysis is incomplete, as comprehensive genetic and metabolic evaluation could not be performed in family members beyond the patient and her parents. Sanger sequencing confirmation of the identified DYRK1B variant in the proband could have provided stronger molecular confirmation of the genetic finding. Third, although DYRK1B variants are known to exhibit age-dependent penetrance, the variable expressivity observed within this family underscores the complexity of penetrance and suggests the influence of modifying genetic or environmental factors, warranting further investigation. Fourth, functional validation of the identified DYRK1B variant was not performed in this proband, and conclusions therefore rely on clinical correlation and previously published functional data.

Future studies should focus on targeted functional assays to clarify the molecular impact of this variant, comprehensive family-based segregation analyses to better define penetrance and expressivity, and systematic phenotyping of additional cases with DYRK1B variants. Prospective studies evaluating responses to different therapeutic agents, including insulin sensitizers and SGLT2 inhibitors, in genetically characterized patients with AOMS3-like phenotypes would further inform disease mechanisms and personalized management strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case illustrates a rare monogenic cause of metabolic syndrome due to a DYRK1B variant and expands its spectrum. The observed clinical response to an SGLT2 inhibitor provides a potential therapeutic avenue, although further research is necessary to validate its efficacy and uncover its molecular basis. This case also reinforces the importance of genetic evaluation in early-onset presentations of metabolic syndrome and calls for deeper investigation into the factors influencing penetrance and phenotype variability in monogenic metabolic disorders. Such insights are crucial for personalized disease management, informed family counselling, and the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.
