Impact of increasing post-filter ionised calcium target on regional citrate anticoagulation efficacy in ICU continuous renal replacement therapy: the non-inferiority randomised controlled Ca-CIBLE protocol
Authors: Mona Assefi, Rayan Braik, Arthur James, Noémie Clavieras, Elodie Baron, Florian Blanchard, Jean-Michel Constantin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionContinuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is a critical therapeutic intervention for patients with severe acute kidney injury in intensive care. However, premature filter clotting remains a significant challenge during CRRT, impacting treatment efficacy, costs and patient outcomes. Anticoagulation is essential to maintain circuit patency, with regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) emerging as a preferred strategy due to its favourable bleeding profile. The standard target for post-filter ionised calcium (iCa) concentration during RCA-CRRT is set between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L, although evidence supporting this range is limited. We hypothesise that a higher post-filter iCa target (0.35–0.45 mmol/L) can provide comparable circuit patency while potentially reducing adverse effects associated with citrate administration.Methods and analysisThis multicentre randomised controlled non-inferiority trial will compare a low post-filter iCa target (0.25–0.35 mmol/L) with a higher post-filter iCa target (0.35–0.45 mmol/L) in patients undergoing RCA-CRRT in the intensive care unit. A total of 412 CRRT sessions will be randomised with a 1:1 ratio into these two groups. The primary outcome is the incidence of filter clotting. Secondary outcomes include filter lifespan, post-filter iCa levels, citrate infusion rates, the occurrence of metabolic adverse effects, financial costs and blood loss.Ethics and disseminationThe study has obtained approval from the ethics committee (Ethics Committee Est III, Nancy, France) and patients will be included after providing informed consent. The results will be disseminated at academic conferences and in peer-reviewed publications. Introduction

Renal replacement therapy is a crucial therapeutic intervention for supporting kidney function in critically ill patients suffering from severe acute kidney injury. Among available techniques, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is favoured over intermittent methods due to its ability to maintain haemodynamic stability, volume control and acid-base balance.1 However, an important obstacle encountered during CRRT is the premature loss of the dialysis filter caused by clotting. This complication not only impairs the efficacy and duration of treatment but also escalates treatment costs, workload and the risk of blood loss.2 3 Consequently, ensuring circuit patency is an indispensable clinical objective, with anticoagulation of the extracorporeal blood forming a crucial component of this strategy.4

The gold standard of anticoagulation for CRRT is regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA),1 which offers circuit patency with reduced bleeding complications.59 RCA involves the infusion of citrate into the blood at the onset of the extracorporeal circuit. Citrate functions by chelating ionised calcium (iCa), a pivotal cofactor in the clotting cascade, thereby providing effective anticoagulation. Post-filter iCa is the surrogate used to assess the level of regional anticoagulation. The typical target range for post-filter iCa is set between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L.911 However, the evidence supporting this specific target remains limited and primarily derives from underpowered trials.12 13 One such study, conducted in 2004, evaluated healthy volunteers and reported an anticoagulant effect at iCa levels of 0.56 mmol/L in serum and below 0.33 mmol/L at maximum.13 Yet, extrapolating these findings to critically ill patients is challenging, further compounded by the variability in iCa measurement depending on the blood gas analysers used.14

Despite the advantages of citrate administration as an anticoagulant, potential side effects, especially in cases of liver failure and/or tissue dysoxia, warrant caution and contraindication in severely patients. Among the rare yet life-threatening complications associated with citrate administration is citrate accumulation. This occurs when the body surpasses its capacity to metabolise citrate, leading to elevated levels of citrate–calcium complexes in the blood and a total-to-iCa ratio higher than 2.5.15 This condition may manifest with hypocalcaemia, high anion gap metabolic acidosis and increased serum lactate levels due to primary impairment of the tricarboxylic acid cycle. Careful differentiation is necessary between citrate accumulation and net citrate overload, the latter being a benign complication resulting from excessive citrate administration or insufficient hemofilter clearance without impairment of citrate metabolism, leading to plasma alkalosis through an increase in the strong ion difference.16 Additionally, inadequate trisodium citrate delivery may occur when the administered alkalotic load is insufficient to compensate for the acidosis associated with acute renal failure, resulting in metabolic acidosis. Furthermore, citrate acts as a chelator of magnesium, potentially contributing to hypomagnesemia. Therefore, a thorough assessment of the benefits and risks of citrate administration during CRRT is vital, particularly in critically ill patients.

To address these concerns, our study aimed to explore the hypothesis that increasing the post-filter iCa target in RCA may limit the dose of citrate and, consequently, mitigate adverse effects without compromising the filter lifespan. Based on our retrospective data analysis, elevating the post-filter iCa target from 0.25 to 0.35 to 0.30–0.40 mmol/L did not result in a decrease in filter lifespan until clotting occurred.17 Our hypothesis is that a higher post-filter iCa target does not compromise the effectiveness of anticoagulation, and therefore would not increase the incidence of filter clotting. This multicentre randomised controlled trial is designed to assess the non-inferiority of anticoagulation effectiveness with a post-filter iCa target between 0.35 and 0.45 mmol/L vs 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L during RCA-CRRT sessions in intensive care unit (ICU).

Methods and analysis

The study protocol was drafted in accordance with the ‘Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials’ recommendations and checklist (see online supplemental appendix 1).

Aims of the study

The primary objective of this study is to compare the incidence of filter clotting between a post-filter iCa target of 0.35–0.45 mmol/L and 0.25–0.35 mmol/L during RCA-CRRT in ICU.

Trial design

The Ca-CIBLE study is a prospective, multicentre, randomised, controlled, non-inferiority, open trial. Patients will be randomly assigned to one of two groups: a post-filter iCa target between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L or 0.35 and 0.45 mmol/L with a 1:1 ratio.

Trial setting

The Ca-CIBLE study is taking place in seven ICUs in France.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Eligible patients must be 18 years of age or older, hospitalised in ICU with an indication for RCA-CRRT, covered by a social security regimen and have provided written consent. If the patient is unable to consent and is accompanied, written consent must be obtained from a legal representative or a close relative. In cases where the patient is unable to consent and is not accompanied, due to the urgency of the procedure, they may be included based on the investigator’s decision (inclusion procedure in an emergency situation with subsequent necessity to sign a consent to prosecute).

Exclusion criteria

Patients fulfilling one of the following criteria will not be included: those receiving curative systemic anticoagulation, individuals with contraindications to the use of citrate (hypersensitivity to Regiocit (Gambro–Baxter, Deerfield, Illinois, USA)), patients with contraindications to the administration of the ancillary drugs Phoxilium (Gambro–Baxter) and calcium chloride, patients with an absolute contraindication to the use of citrate due to a lack of metabolism in the Krebs cycle, and therefore a major risk of accumulation (severe liver impairment with prothrombin rate<30% and lactates>3 mmol/L, severe tissue dysoxia in uncontrolled shock with lactic acidosis and lactates>4 mmol/L, or drug intoxication such as metformin, paracetamol, propofol, ciclosporine), pregnant woman, individuals under legal protection measures (guardianship or safeguard measures), those deprived of liberty by a judicial or administrative decision, individuals who decline to participate or those participating in another interventional study that may impact the evaluation criteria of this study.

Intervention

RCA-CRRT will be ordered based on clinical indications and performed according to a standardised protocol (see online supplemental appendix 2). The CRRT will be conducted in continuous venovenous haemofiltration mode using the Prismaflex system (Gambro–Baxter, Deerfield, Illinois, USA) and a 0.9 m2 high-flux AN69 membrane. A double-lumen high-performance dialysis catheter will be used, and blood flow will be maintained between 120 and 180 mL/min based on the patient’s ideal body weight. The prescribed filtration dose will be 30 mL/kg/hour to achieve a delivered dose of 20–25 mL/kg/hour, following Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines.1 A citrated replacement solution (Regiocit), containing 18.0 mmol/L of citrate, will be continuously delivered to the blood before the filter of the extracorporeal circuit. The infusion rate of predilution replacement flow will be coupled to the blood flow, aiming for a stable citrate concentration in the extracorporeal circuit. The initial citrate dose will be 3.0 mmol/L of blood, and then the citrate flow rate will be adjusted to achieve the post-filter iCa target according to the protocol. Post-filter iCa will be measured using the ABL90 FLEX PLUS (Radiometer Medical, Copenhagen, Denmark) blood gas analyser 15 min after any change in dose and then every 6 hours. Calcium chloride will be administered to the patient through a central line to maintain systemic iCa within 1.00–1.30 mmol/L. Fluid removal rates will be left to the discretion of the attending physician to achieve optimal fluid balance. Additionally, metabolic monitoring will be conducted with a blood ionogram every 12 hours.

Interventional group

The interventional group will have a high post-filter iCa target, set between 0.35 and 0.45 mmol/L. Citrate doses will be adjusted according to the standardised protocol to be within the correct range (see online supplemental appendix 2).

Control group

The control group will have a low post-filter iCa target, set between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L. Citrate doses will be adjusted according to the standardised protocol to be within the correct range (see online supplemental appendix 2).

Duration of treatment

The duration of treatment for each CRRT session in both groups will be a maximum of 72 hours (figure 1). After that, according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, the CRRT session should be stopped and the circuit blood should be returned.

Allocation and sequence intervention

Each CRRT session, defined by the use of one filter, will be treated as an isolated entity, as a single patient may undergo multiple CRRT sessions during their ICU stay. Each RCA-CRRT session will be randomised (maximum four per patient). A computer-generated randomisation will be performed with stratification according to centre and cardiovascular Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) Score (0–2 vs 3–4) in a 1:1 ratio, using a centralised web-based management system (CleanWeb). After randomisation, the strategy assigned to the patient (post-filter iCa target between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L or 0.35 and 0.45 mmol/L) will be initiated immediately.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the incidence of filter clotting. Filter clotting will be defined by one of the following criteria: increased transmembrane pressure greater than 300 mm Hg; visible thrombus in circuit or filter; inability to rotate the blood pump due to an obstructing thrombus.6

All causes of CRRT stoppage will be independently reevaluated by two clinicians. The session will be attributed to filter thrombosis if both clinician’s evaluations are concordant.

The lifespan of each filter until filter clotting and overall filter lifespan, including all causes of stoppage, measured in hours. Sample size

Estimating the filter clotting rates for the reference group (post-filter iCa target between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L) is challenging due to wide heterogeneity found in the literature, ranging from 9.5%18 to 57.9%.6 Therefore, we conducted a retrospective analysis of data from our centre between January 2016 and April 2018.17 The observed incidence of filter clotting was 18.5% out of a total of 473 CRRT sessions analysed, with a post-filter iCa target between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L. We thus consider it clinically acceptable to conclude non-inferiority if the intervention group (post-filter iCa target between 0.35 and 0.45 mmol/L) has a filter clotting incidence of up to 21%, representing a difference of 2.5 percentage points, with a non-inferiority margin set at 9%. Therefore, it will be necessary to include 412 analysable RCA-CRRT sessions in the study.

Data collection and management

Data will be collected on an electronic case report form (e-CRF) by a trained investigator or research assistant at each centre. At the time of inclusion, the following data will be collected: informed consent, demographic characteristics, vital signs, indication for CRRT and biological data. Daily, for 72 hours, the biological data with the occurrence of metabolic events will be recorded, as well as the early interruption of the CRRT session with the cause of the stoppage. All parameters of the CRRT session will be collected on the memory card of the monitors. Each person responsible for completion of the e-CRF (investigator, research staff) will have a ‘user’ account with specific computer rights linked to their role (eg, the right to enter or modify data or to lock, monitor or sign a page of the e-CRF). The data will be compiled directly from the e-CRF into the database hosted on a dedicated server with controlled access (account/password) according to the user’s role. Any addition, modification or deletion of data will be recorded in a non-editable electronic file (the audit trail). All investigators will archive all of the study data for at least 15 years after completion of the study. All investigators will commit to keeping the identities of the individuals who participate in the study confidential by assigning them a code.

Statistical description and statistical analysis methods

All analyses will be performed by the study statistician according to a predefined statistical analysis plan using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed p value less than 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Descriptive analysis of patient groups at baseline

Quantitative parameters will be presented as median and IQR. Comparisons will be made using either the Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on whether the assumptions of the t-test are met. Categorical data will be reported as the number and percentage (%) and will be compared using Fisher’s exact test or χ2 test, as appropriate. The analysis will be conducted on a per-protocol basis as the primary analysis (the most conservative approach in a non-inferiority study) and on an intention-to-treat basis. No imputation for missing values will be performed.

Primary outcome analysis

The incidence of filter clotting will be expressed in absolute values (n) and percentage (%). Comparisons between groups will be performed using Pearson’s χ2 test.

Secondary outcomes analysis

Filter lifespan and filter lifespan until clotting curves according to the post-filter iCa target will be plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. The total dose of citrate infused, the occurrence of metabolic adverse effects, financial costs and blood loss will be compared between the two groups using the Student’s t-test (or Mann-Whitney test if necessary).

Predetermined subgroup analysis

Randomisation is stratified according to cardiovascular SOFA Score (0–2 vs 3–4) to ensure a homogeneous distribution of patients with haemodynamic failure in each group. A subgroup analysis will therefore be performed for the main and secondary evaluation criteria in this subgroup of patients.

Data monitoring

An investigator at each centre will be responsible for daily patient screening, enrolling patients in the study, ensuring adherence to the protocol and completing the e-CRF. Research assistants will regularly monitor all the centres on-site to check adherence to the protocol and accuracy of the data recorded. The study does not have a data monitoring committee.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Ethics and dissemination

The Good Clinical Practice Guideline and the Declaration of Helsinki will be followed throughout this trial. The protocol has been approved by the central ethics committee (Ethics Committee Est III, Nancy, France) with the registration number 2022-003678-22 on 25 January 2023. Patients will be included after verification of eligibility criteria and after providing written consent to the investigator (available as online supplemental appendix 3), in accordance with the central ethics committee’s decision. For patients not able to provide informed consent, their next-of-kin will be contacted following the same procedure. Patients will be informed as soon as possible by the investigator of their participation in the study, and their written consent to continue participating in the study will be obtained. All consent forms were approved by the ethics committee.

Results of this trial will be disseminated at academic conferences and published in a peer-reviewed journal. Additional documents (statistical analysis plan) may be available. Authorship will be granted to those who make significant contributions.

Discussion

The challenge of RCA during CRRT lies in balancing efficacy and safety. Our retrospective study, which examined the filter clotting rate with a post-filter iCa between 0.25 and 0.35 mmol/L, demonstrated that increasing the post-filter iCa target range to 0.30–0.40 mmol/L did not compromise efficacy, as evidenced by comparable filter lifespan until clotting between the two groups (HR, 1.020 (0.703; 1.481); p=0.92).17 However, we did not observe a reduction in citrate-related metabolic complications despite a significant decrease in citrate doses. Building on this initial retrospective analysis, we hypothesise that adopting an even higher post-filter iCa target (0.35–0.45 mmol/L) could offer an optimal compromise between anticoagulation efficacy and citrate-related adverse effects. To date, no prospective study has addressed the optimal citrate dosing or post-filter iCa target, despite it being the recommended anticoagulation strategy.1

Our primary objective is to evaluate anticoagulation efficacy and ensure that an increase in the post-filter iCa target does not result in an increased filter clotting rate, which serves as our primary outcome. Our protocol involves independent assessments by two clinicians for each CRRT stoppage, enhancing the precision of causal attribution. Assessing the filter lifespan until clotting could also serve as a relevant secondary evaluation criterion. Preventing premature filter loss is a significant clinical challenge for ensuring an effective clearance, minimising nursing workload and costs. We aim to mitigate the impact of confounding factors through randomisation and stratification based on centre and cardiovascular SOFA Score. Additionally, the standardisation of the CRRT protocol with the same machines and blood analysers across all participating centres ensures the consistency and reproducibility of our intervention, reinforcing the robustness of our study design. Nevertheless, this standardisation may potentially limit the generalizability of results to more diverse clinical contexts and different CRRT protocols. Finally, our adherence to a predefined statistical analysis plan further enhances the transparent and objective interpretation of study results.

While demonstrating the absence of reduced efficacy with a higher post-filter iCa target is our primary aim, it’s important to assess potential secondary benefits in terms of reduced citrate dosages, citrate-related adverse effects and associated costs. This comprehensive approach aims to determine if expanding the post-filter iCa target range could offer tangible clinical or economic advantages. Furthermore, it is also interesting to consider that an increase in the post-filter iCa target, which typically leads to a decrease in citrate doses, might make it possible to expand citrate’s use to patients with relative contraindications to citrate due to impaired citrate clearance (hepatocellular insufficiency or tissue hypoxia).
