Congenital Absence of the Left Atrial Appendage: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Nicole Wuzynski, Osama Smettei, Asad Naveed, Yasser Alsaleh, Sarah Woolridge, Rami M Abazid
Published: 2026

Congenital absence of the left atrial appendage (CALAA) is an extremely rare congenital anomaly of the left atrium that is typically identified incidentally during cardiac imaging. This report describes a rare case of CALAA, in a patient aged 43 years, detected on cardiac computed tomography. Additionally, we reviewed 28 reports describing 31 cases of CALAA.

Background

The left atrial appendage (LAA) is a small muscular extension arising from the anterolateral wall of the left atrium (LA). The LAA contributes to atrial pressure regulation and plays an endocrine and hemodynamic role in cardiovascular physiology.1 During the third to fourth weeks of gestation, the LAA develops from the primordial LA and retains a trabeculated morphology that differs from the smooth-walled LA components formed through incorporation of the pulmonary veins.1

Clinically, the LAA represents the principal target for pharmacologic and mechanical stroke prevention strategies because it accounts for > 90% of cardiac thrombi in patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation (AF).2 Several morphologic subtypes have been described, including cauliflower, chicken wing, windsock, and cactus morphologies, each associated with varying thromboembolic risk profiles3 (Fig. 1).

CALAA is a rare anatomic variant that is typically detected incidentally on advanced cardiac imaging. Owing to its asymptomatic nature and the absence of routine screening, the true prevalence remains unknown. Although CALAA has been hypothesized to reduce thromboembolic risk, particularly through the absence of a thrombus-prone appendage, its clinical significance remains uncertain, especially in patients without AF.4

We report a rare case of incidentally detected CALAA in a 43-year-old female patient without a prior history of AF identified on coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA). Additionally, we reviewed 28 reports to evaluate the clinical presentation, diagnostic approach, and management of CALAA.

Case Presentation

A 43-year-old female patient was referred to the cardiology clinic with a 3-month history of atypical chest pain. Her medical history was significant only for hypertension, without additional coronary artery disease risk factors. She had no previous history of AF or other cardiac disorders.

Vital signs were within normal limits: blood pressure, 127/76 mm Hg; heart rate, 65 beats per minute; respiratory rate, 15 breaths per minute; and oxygen saturation, 99% on room air. Physical examination findings were otherwise unremarkable.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal left ventricular size and systolic function, normal right ventricular size and function, normal biatrial size, and no significant valvular abnormalities. CCTA revealed normal coronary arteries and CALAA (Fig.1). The patient was reassured that the chest pain was noncardiac and that the incidental finding of CALAA had no established clinical significance.

Methods

A focused literature search was performed in PubMed, to identify reports of congenital absence or agenesis of the LAA published between January 1990 and August 2025. The following search terms and combinations were used: “left atrial appendage,” “left atrium appendage,” “congenital,” “agenesis,” “absent,” “absence,” “hypoplasia,” and “anomaly.” Only full-text English-language articles describing adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) were included. Eligible case reports and relevant clinical studies were reviewed. Extracted variables included age, sex, diagnostic modality, clinical presentation, management, and outcomes. The collected data were synthesized to summarize the major clinical findings.

Diagnostic workup

The most common indication for diagnostic evaluation was pre-catheter ablation assessment, in 13 patients (41.9%), followed by pre-cardioversion evaluation in 6 patients (19.4%). Diagnosis of CALAA was established using multimodality imaging in 24 patients (77.4%), whereas 7 patients (22.6%) were diagnosed using a single advanced imaging modality. Discussion

CALAA is an exceptionally rare congenital anomaly that is generally identified incidentally during cardiac imaging performed for unrelated indications, most commonly during planning for pulmonary vein isolation. In this systematic review of 31 patients with a mean age of 65 years, a higher proportion of patients were male. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and CCTA were the most frequently used diagnostic modalities.

The LAA develops early during embryogenesis and maintains a trabeculated structure distinct from the smooth-walled LA body. The pathogenesis of CALAA remains unclear. Proposed mechanisms include failed embryologic development or early involution of the LAA during LA morphogenesis. In patients with AF, the LAA is the predominant site of thrombus formation and accounts for approximately 90% of intracardiac thrombi.2 Consequently, the LAA is a major target for mechanical exclusion strategies intended to reduce cerebrovascular events. Theoretically, CALAA may provide a similar protective effect. However, current evidence is limited to isolated case reports, and data are insufficient regarding the thromboembolic risk profile or anticoagulation benefit in patients with CALAA.5 Therefore, anticoagulation decisions should be individualized, particularly in patients with low bleeding risk, until additional evidence becomes available.

In our literature review, palpitations were the most frequently reported symptom, occurring in 55% of patients with CALAA. However, 81% of patients underwent cardiac evaluation before pulmonary vein isolation for AF, suggesting that symptoms were more likely attributable to AF than to CALAA itself. Importantly, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated limited utility for LAA assessment, and no reviewed cases were diagnosed using this modality. Instead, advanced cardiac imaging (TEE or CCTA) typically was performed before cardioversion or catheter ablation, indicating substantial detection bias.

Because CALAA is often detected incidentally during AF workup, and diagnosis depends largely on pre-procedural imaging, substantial selection bias is likely. This bias may explain the apparent predominance of male patients and the observed association with AF. Therefore, these findings likely reflect a highly selected population undergoing advanced cardiac imaging rather than the true epidemiology of CALAA. Accordingly, CALAA is likely underdiagnosed, and its prevalence in the general population is probably underestimated.

Among reported patients with CALAA and AF, anticoagulation strategies were guided primarily by thromboembolic risk assessment using the CHA2DS2-VASc score rather than by the congenital anomaly or CALAA itself.3

An important differential diagnosis of CALAA is complete thrombotic occlusion of the LAA. Misdiagnosis of a thrombosed LAA as CALAA may result in inappropriate management decisions, including cancellation of cardioversion or discontinuation of anticoagulation therapy. Therefore, invasive LA angiography demonstrating absent appendage filling and electroanatomic voltage mapping confirming electrical silence may be necessary in selected cases to exclude thrombotic occlusion.

This report has several limitations. Most available data on CALAA derive from isolated case reports or small case series, and asymptomatic individuals are likely underrepresented. Furthermore, long-term outcomes remain poorly characterized.

CALAA is an extremely rare congenital anomaly that appears to occur more commonly in male patients and is most frequently identified incidentally during AF evaluation. Multimodality imaging is essential for accurate diagnosis of CALAA and for differentiating true CALAA from thrombotic LAA occlusion.
