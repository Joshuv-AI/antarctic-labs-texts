Mapping of geographic inequality in mental health care facilities and psychiatrists distribution across seven districts of indore division: A geospatial analysis from central India
Authors: Vijay Niranjan, Ashvin Chouhan, Ankit Kushwaha, Riya Gangwal, Minakshi Verma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:India faces a severe mental health workforce shortage, with Madhya Pradesh (MP) having only 0.05 psychiatrists per 100,000 population—far below the World Health Organization (WHO) benchmark of 3. Granular data on psychiatrist distribution are needed to address inequities in care.Aim:To map psychiatrist distribution across the seven districts of Indore division, MP, to assess geographic inequalities and estimate resource requirements for achieving WHO staffing norms.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional geospatial survey (Jan–Mar 2025) identified actively practicing psychiatrists via snowball sampling, global positioning system (GPS)mapping, and inequality indices (Gini, Theil). Spatial clustering was analyzed using the nearest-neighbor index (NNI). Regression models assessed infrastructure-psychiatrist associations.Results:Among 71 psychiatrists, 88% clustered in Indore city, yielding a regional density of 0.54 per 100,000. Three districts had no psychiatrists. High inequality (Gini = 0.78) and urban clustering (NNI = 0.38) were observed. Infrastructure availability strongly predicted psychiatrist presence (β =0.98). Meeting WHO targets requires 429 additional psychiatrists (annual investment: INR 514.8 million).Conclusion:Severe urban–rural disparities exist in psychiatrist distribution. Targeted investments in workforce expansion, telepsychiatry (e.g. Tele-MANAS), and rural infrastructure are urgently needed to bridge the treatment gap.

Mental disorders contribute 7.7% of all disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in India—nearly double their burden in 1990—yet the country’s mental health workforce remains critically understaffed.[1] With only 0.75 psychiatrists per 100,000 population, India falls far short of the World Health Organization’s (WHO) minimum recommended threshold of three per 100,000.[2] In Madhya Pradesh (MP), the shortfall is even more alarming: a mere 0.05 psychiatrists per 100,000, coupled with a mental health treatment gap estimated between 70% and 92% according to the National Mental Health Survey (NMHS).[3] This systemic shortage leaves millions without access to timely and appropriate psychiatric care.

The inequity is not just quantitative but geographic. Existing mapping studies, including recent work by Sutar et al.,[4] show that psychiatrists in MP are heavily concentrated in a few urban centers—primarily Indore, Bhopal, and Gwalior—while nearly half of the state’s 54 districts are left without a single mental health specialist. A similar pattern of urban clustering has been noted across India, including in North Indian districts where services are saturated in cities and sparse in tribal and rural belts.[5]

This imbalance is especially stark in the Indore division, which encompasses seven districts: Indore, Dhar, Khargone, Barwani, Alirajpur, Jhabua, and Khandwa. While Indore city hosts multiple medical colleges and corporate hospitals, its neighboring tribal and rural districts struggle with unfilled specialist posts, minimal infrastructure, and high unmet needs. These local disparities often remain hidden beneath state-level averages. In a setting where district authorities make health budgeting decisions, fine-grained data are crucial to guide targeted deployment of resources, build health infrastructure, and plan appropriate links to referral services.

Geospatial mapping has emerged as a powerful and low-cost decision-support tool in public health, endorsed by the WHO Mental Health Atlas for assessing service equity.[6] Such techniques allow us not only to quantify workforce distribution but also to visualize spatial clustering, highlight underserved areas, and generate metrics like the Gini coefficient or nearest-neighbor index (NNI). When combined with regression modeling and treatment gap estimation, these data can inform actionable planning.

Against this backdrop, our study aimed to map psychiatrists and mental health facilities across the Indore division; analyze patterns of spatial clustering and inequality; and estimate the psychiatrist shortfall and cost implications for achieving WHO staffing norms. Beyond headcounts, we also discuss evidence-based policy solutions—including task-sharing, rural service incentives, and digital mental health platforms like Tele-MANAS—to bridge these disparities and make equitable mental health care a reality in central India.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cross-sectional geospatial study was conducted between January and March 2025 across the seven districts of the Indore division in Madhya Pradesh, a region with a combined population of approximately 13.2 million as per the 2011 Census.[3] The primary objective was to identify and map all psychiatrists and mental health care facilities—across both public and private sectors—to assess their distribution and geographic inequality. We defined eligible participants as having a postgraduate qualification in psychiatry and currently registered with the Medical Council and practicing in the Indore division. Those pursuing postgraduate training, retired, or who had migrated out of the region were excluded. Mental health care facilities included were medical colleges, district hospitals, other government hospitals, private clinics, private hospitals running psychiatric outpatient department, addiction treatment centers, and mental health institutions (MHIs).

A snowball sampling strategy was employed to locate psychiatrists and mental healthcare facilities, starting with names drawn from multiple sources: the Indian Psychiatric Society–Madhya Pradesh Chapter (IPS-MP) directory, medical college staff directories, and records from the District Mental Health Programme (DMHP). Each identified psychiatrist was then asked to refer others practising in the area. Recruitment continued until two successive referral waves yielded no new contacts, indicating saturation of the available pool—an approach consistent with peer-network-based recruitment frameworks described by Biernacki and Waldorf.[7]

A structured Google Form, adapted from Debnath et al.,[5] served as the primary data collection tool. It captured key variables such as facility type, full address, and GPS location via a Google Maps pin. Facility types were coded into categories—medical college (M), district hospital (D), other government hospital (G), private clinic (P), addiction treatment centers (A), and MHI—mirroring classifications used in previous mental health mapping studies.[4] Initially, we explored a range of electronic sources, including free-to-use websites such as Google, Find My Doctor, and WebMD, as well as mobile applications like Practo and Justdial and of those practitioners and facilities whose location was not available were asked to share their GPS location through electronic media or were physically visited to acquire the geo location.

Coordinates were cleaned to remove duplicate or out-of-scope entries. Cleaned location data were then exported as comma-separated values (CSV) files and mapped using Google My Maps, with district boundaries overlaid for spatial referencing. These layers were exported in keyhole markup language (KML) format for visualization and comparison across districts.[89] We calculated psychiatrist density per 100,000 people, as well as inequality indices including the Gini coefficient and Theil index, to quantify distributional imbalance.

The Gini coefficient is a measure of inequality ranging from 0 (complete equality) to 1 (complete inequality), widely used to assess the evenness of resource distribution across a population. The Theil index is an entropy-based statistic that similarly measures inequality, with higher values indicating greater disparity. Both indices help objectively quantify how psychiatrists are distributed across districts, allowing direct comparison of workforce distributional equity.

The dataset was subsequently imported into IBM Corp. (2020). IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp. for statistical analysis. We calculated psychiatrist density per 100,000 people, as well as inequality indices including the Gini coefficient and Theil index, to quantify distributional imbalance. To evaluate spatial clustering, we applied Euclidean nearest-neighbour analysis and performed a Z-test for complete spatial randomness, using macro code adapted from Willington et al.[10] Additionally, linear regression was used to assess the association between the number of mental health service points and the presence of psychiatrists in each district.

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Human Ethics Committee. All participants provided consent before data submission, and no personally identifiable information other than that available in the public domain was collected in the published dataset.

RESULTS

A total of 71 psychiatrists were identified as actively practising across the seven districts of the Indore division, distributed over 72 distinct service locations. This translates to an average psychiatrist density of 0.54 per 100,000 population, based on the 2011 Census figures.[3] While this figure is more than double the Madhya Pradesh state average of 0.21 reported by Sutar et al.,[4] it remains well below the WHO’s recommended benchmark of 3 per 100,000.[2]

However, the distribution was far from equitable. Indore district alone accounted for 63 of the 71 psychiatrists, giving it a striking density of 1.92 per 100,000—nearly ten times that of any other district. In contrast, Burhanpur, Jhabua, and Alirajpur had no psychiatrists at all. Khandwa, the next-best-served district after Indore, had only four psychiatrists for a population of over 1.3 million, resulting in a modest density of 0.31 per 100,000. The inequality in distribution was quantified using the Gini coefficient (0.779) and the Theil index (0.411), both indicating severe workforce disparity [Figure 1]. A Chi-square test further confirmed that the distribution of psychiatrists did not reflect population proportions, highlighting the disproportionate concentration in Indore. Estimates based on the NMHS 2015–2016 suggest that roughly 10.6% of the population may be experiencing a diagnosable mental health disorder at any given time.[3] Across the Indore division, this translates to approximately 1.39 million individuals potentially in need of psychiatric care. Yet only a fraction of this need is currently being met. Based on WHO norms of one psychiatrist per 40,000 population, the division requires a total of 526 psychiatrists—429 more than are presently available. If the state has to address this shortfall of 429 psychiatrists and achieve the WHO standard, the study projects a required recurring annual investment of INR 514.8 million, assuming an average salary of INR 1.2 million per psychiatrist as per the NHM Madhya Pradesh norms.[11] Under a phased recruitment plan, hiring 25 psychiatrists annually would take 18 years; hiring 50 would take 9 years; and hiring 100 per year would close the gap in just 5 years.

DISCUSSION

This geospatial mapping study uncovered stark urban–rural disparities in psychiatrist distribution across the Indore division of Madhya Pradesh. Although the regional average of 0.54 psychiatrists per 100,000 population appears modestly better than the state average of 0.21 reported by Sutar et al.,[4] it remains significantly below the WHO benchmark of 3 per 100,000.[2] More importantly, this average conceals an extreme concentration of resources: nearly 90% of the region’s psychiatrists are clustered in Indore city alone. Districts like Burhanpur, Jhabua, and Alirajpur were found to have no psychiatrists available to serve.

This pattern of clustering is not unique to Indore. Similar findings have been documented in North Indian districts where urban zones host a disproportionate share of the mental health workforce,[5] and even in other low-resource settings globally, where healthcare tends to concentrate in areas of better infrastructure and private sector viability.[69] Our NNI (0.38) and high inequality coefficients (Gini = 0.779) statistically confirmed what the maps vividly depicted: psychiatrists are geographically concentrated where the demand is most profitable, not necessarily where the need is greatest.

The drivers of this urban bias are multifaceted. Indore’s concentration of postgraduate training seats, corporate hospitals, and private clientele creates powerful pull factors. Simultaneously, rural districts often present push factors such as infrastructural deficiencies, professional isolation, safety concerns, and limited academic opportunities—barriers that have long disincentivized rural service.[10] Surveys from similar Indian states show that many psychiatrists perceive rural postings as career detours unless accompanied by financial incentives or academic attachments.

This maldistribution has serious consequences. Using a conservative prevalence estimate, over 1.3 million people in the Indore division are likely living with untreated mental illness. If left unaddressed, this treatment gap is likely to widen further, especially as population growth outpaces mental health workforce expansion.[34]

Yet the findings also point to practical solutions.

Telepsychiatry is one promising frontier. The national Tele-MANAS program, launched in 2022, has already handled over two million calls across 53 centers and supports access in 20 languages via mobile apps.[12] In the Indore division, a hub-and-spoke model could be adopted, wherein Indore medical colleges serve as teleconsultation hubs while medications and follow-ups are handled at the district level. However, telepsychiatry alone is insufficient unless accompanied by local medicine supply chains and trained support staff.

To sustainably address psychiatrist shortages, multiple policy levers must be activated. Another strategy is to establish psychiatry units in every district hospital, which could simultaneously function as training sites for psychologists and psychiatric social workers. International data suggest that such “grow-your-own” models significantly improve rural workforce retention.[13]

Financially, the gap is bridgeable. The estimated annual cost of hiring the required 429 psychiatrists—about INR 514.8 million—is less than 0.2% of Madhya Pradesh’s current health budget. A phased plan hiring 100 psychiatrists annually could close the workforce gap within 5 years. This figure, though ambitious, is not unrealistic when weighed against the mental health burden faced by over a million underserved citizens.

The state can start diplomate of national board (DNB) and diploma programs in psychiatry at district hospitals and medical colleges, as already being done in other specialties, to develop manpower to bridge the gap and can motivate and incentivize in-service medical officers to enrol in it.

While our study offers granular, decision-grade evidence for the Indore division, it is not without limitations. Snowball sampling, while efficient for mapping professionals in fragmented systems, may miss isolated practitioners not embedded in peer networks. Additionally, the focus was limited to psychiatrists—future research should include allied professionals like clinical psychologists and social workers to present a more comprehensive workforce landscape.

Despite these constraints, the study demonstrates how low-cost, replicable geospatial tools—combined with district-level data—can help health systems plan more equitable mental health services. The evidence is clear: addressing geographic and infrastructural gaps is not only feasible but imperative for closing the mental health treatment gap in central India.
