Impact of staff turnover on extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients: a nationwide registry-based analysis in Japan
Authors: Kazuya Kikutani, Mitsuaki Nishikimi, Shinichiro Ohshimo, Nobuaki Shime
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We investigated the impact of physician staff turnover on outcomes in OHCA.•No significant difference in 30-day survival was found in overall OHCA cases.•Early April admission was associated with improved 30-day survival in ECPR patients.•Institutional readiness during staff turnover periods may improve outcomes in ECPR.

Introduction

Staff turnover in clinical settings has the potential to impact patient outcomes, possibly due to changes in provider experience and team dynamics. In the United States, this phenomenon is known as the “July Effect,” referring to a potential decline in the quality of care and worse outcomes during July, when newly graduated physicians begin clinical practice.1, 2 Similar turnover effects have also been reported in other countries; in the United Kingdom, it is colloquially referred to as the “killing season”.3 However, the existing evidence on the July Effect is mixed: some studies report worse outcomes associated with it,1, 4 others show no significant differences,5, 6 and a few even suggest improved outcomes during this period.7

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) represents a high-acuity clinical scenario in which optimal team performance and rapid decision-making are essential for patient survival. Given the complexity and urgency of these interventions, any disruption due to staff turnover—such as during the so-called July Effect—may have amplified consequences. Although prior studies have examined the July Effect in in-hospital cardiac arrest settings,8 to our knowledge, no study has specifically investigated this phenomenon in OHCA. Considering findings from a previous study suggesting that the July Effect may be more pronounced in high-risk patients,4 we hypothesized that this effect might have a greater impact on outcomes in patients with OHCA, particularly those who received extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR)—the most severely ill population requiring more complex and time-sensitive interventions in the emergency department. Given that clinical outcomes in high-risk interventions like ECPR are influenced by institutional learning curves and team familiarity,9, 10 focusing on ECPR cases offers a reasonable approach to evaluate the potential impact of workforce turnover.

Although the effects of physician turnover on patient outcomes have been explored in various healthcare settings, including in-hospital cardiac arrest,8 limited attention has been given to its potential impact in time-critical, high-acuity situations such as OHCA—particularly in patients receiving ECPR. Understanding whether workforce turnover is associated with differences in outcomes in such scenarios is crucial for informing institutional policies and ensuring high-quality resuscitation care throughout the year.

This study aimed to determine the potential impact of the annual physician turnover period—which, in Japan, occurs at the beginning of April rather than in July—on clinical outcomes in OHCA patients, both overall and among those who received ECPR.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study utilized data from the Japanese Association for Acute Medicine (JAAM) OHCA Registry, a nationwide database of OHCA patients in Japan. The registry includes prehospital information based on the Utstein style—such as witnessed arrest, bystander CPR, initial electrocardiographic rhythm, time of emergency call, and emergency medical services contact time—as well as detailed in-hospital treatment data, including airway management, administration of medications such as epinephrine, and the use of ECPR. The registry’s data collection protocol has been described in previous studies.11 As of December 2022, over 100 hospitals across Japan participated in the JAAM-OHCA Registry, covering the majority of tertiary emergency centers. In Japan, emergency medical services are operated by local fire departments under a nationwide system, and paramedics are authorized to provide advanced life support, including defibrillation and selected medications, at the scene. Patients are usually transported to the nearest designated emergency hospital capable of advanced resuscitation. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hiroshima University (E2025-0058). Because this study used de-identified registry data, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived.

Selection of participants

We analyzed data from patients registered in the JAAM-OHCA Registry between 1 June 2014 and 31 December 2022. Eligible patients were adults (aged ≥18 years) who experienced OHCA without return of spontaneous circulation upon hospital arrival, including those who underwent ECPR as part of resuscitation. Patients with missing data on the primary outcome (30-day survival) were excluded from the analysis. Patients who were initiated on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation after return of spontaneous circulation were also excluded.

Group definitions

Patients were categorized into two groups based on the date of hospital admission: the late March group (18–31 March) and the early April group (1–14 April).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 30-day survival. The secondary outcome was favorable neurological status at 30 days, defined as a Cerebral Performance Category score of 1 or 2.12

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed for both the overall OHCA cohort and the ECPR subgroup, as pre-specified in our study protocol given that ECPR patients represent a high-risk subgroup requiring complex interventions. A chi-square test was used to assess the association between group and 30-day survival. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between admission period (early April vs. late March) and 30-day survival. To account for potential confounding, the model was adjusted for age, presence or absence of bystander CPR, initial electrocardiographic rhythm upon hospital arrival, and time to hospital arrival.13, 14 The number of covariates included in the multivariable logistic regression was restricted to avoid overfitting given the sample size of the ECPR subgroup. Therefore, we selected age, bystander CPR, initial rhythm, and time to hospital arrival as key prognostic factors, which have been consistently reported as important predictors of OHCA outcomes in prior studies.13, 14 We opted for complete case analysis, given the relatively small proportion of patients with missing data—8.3 % (503/6036) in the OHCA cohort and 12.8 % (24/187) in the ECPR cohort.

To reflect changes in international resuscitation practices, data were grouped into three time periods based on the publication years of the American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines15, 16: 2014–2015 (pre-AHA 2015), 2016–2020 (post-AHA 2015), and 2021–2022 (post-AHA 2020). Although the Japan Resuscitation Council guidelines are widely used in Japan, they are largely adapted from the AHA and International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation recommendations. Therefore, AHA guideline publication years were used to define the temporal groups for international comparability.

To further explore the time-window effect, we performed a sensitivity analysis with alternative definitions: (i) a weekly comparison (March 25–31 vs. April 1–7) and (ii) a monthly comparison (March vs. April).

As an additional sensitivity analysis, we addressed missing data among the covariates used in the multivariable logistic regression model (age, shockable rhythm, time to hospital, and bystander CPR). Missing values were present only for time to hospital and bystander CPR. For time to hospital, missing values were imputed using the facility-specific median (or the overall median if facility-level data were unavailable), while missing values for bystander CPR were replaced with the overall mode.

All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Secondary outcome

Among all OHCA patients, the proportion with favorable neurological outcomes at 30 days did not differ significantly between late March and early April admissions (1.4 % vs. 1.8 %, p = 0.27). In the multivariable analysis, using the same adjustment variables as for the primary outcome, early April admission was not associated with significantly higher odds of a favorable neurological outcome (OR: 1.41, 95 % CI: 0.85–2.34, p = 0.18).

In the ECPR cohort, the unadjusted comparison also showed no significant difference in 30-day favorable neurological outcomes (11.5 % vs. 16.9 %, p = 0.40). Multivariable logistic regression yielded similar results (OR: 1.77, 95 % CI: 0.64–4.92, p = 0.27).

Sensitivity analyses

For the sensitivity analyses, we further investigated the effect of staff turnover by comparing outcomes during 1-week and 1-month periods before and after the start of the new academic year.

Patient characteristics for the 1-week comparison (25–31 March vs. 1–7 April) are presented in Supplementary Table 1. In this comparison, April cases again showed a significantly higher 30-day survival rate among ECPR patients (48.8 % vs. 21.7 %, p = 0.014), though not among all OHCA patients (5.3 % vs. 4.1 %, p = 0.16). In the overall OHCA cohort, multivariable analysis did not demonstrate a significant association between admission week and outcome (Supplementary Table 2). However, in the ECPR subgroup, the association remained significant (adjusted OR: 4.55, 95 % CI: 1.51–15.2, p = 0.009; Supplementary Table 3).

In contrast, as shown in Supplementary Table 4, in the full-month comparison (April vs March), no significant difference in 30-day survival was observed in either the overall OHCA cohort (4.0 % vs 4.0 %, p = 0.98) or the ECPR subgroup (27.7 % vs 23.4 %, p = 0.40). Consistently, multivariable analysis did not demonstrate a significant association between calendar month and outcome in either group (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6).

As another sensitivity analysis, we performed multivariable logistic regression using the imputed dataset for both the overall OHCA cohort (N = 6036) and the ECPR subgroup (N = 187). The results were consistent with the complete case analysis (overall OHCA: OR 1.18, 95 % CI 0.91–1.53, p = 0.21; ECPR subgroup: OR 2.05, 95 % CI 1.00–4.18, p = 0.05).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that patients undergoing ECPR in early April—immediately after the annual physician turnover—had better 30-day survival than those in late March. By contrast, no such effect was observed in the overall OHCA population. The consistency of the signal across both the 2-week and 1-week comparisons lends some support to the robustness of the findings. The absence of an effect in the full-month comparison suggests that any potential benefit associated with the April transition may be transient or attenuated when longer time frames are considered. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically explore the potential impact of staff turnover on outcomes in this high-risk group.

Given the extremely high baseline mortality associated with OHCA, it is possible that the influence of staff turnover was attenuated or masked, leaving little room for observable differences in outcomes attributable to physician experience or team transitions. In addition, because CPR is widely practiced and standardized across both prehospital and in-hospital settings, the impact of individual physician experience may be less pronounced in the overall OHCA population. This may partly explain why no significant effect was observed.

Compared with the overall OHCA cohort, patients who received ECPR typically exhibited a higher survival rate.17, 18 Several studies have suggested that clinical outcomes of ECPR may be influenced by team experience and institutional learning curves.9, 10 Given that ECPR is highly complex and time-sensitive, transient reductions in experience due to workforce turnover could plausibly impact survival. Interestingly, however, our findings showed that the prognosis of OHCA patients who received ECPR was better in early April, which corresponds to the typical period of physician staff turnover in Japan. One possible explanation for this counterintuitive finding is that Japanese hospitals may proactively strengthen supervision and team coordination during April to ensure the safety of critically ill patients. Unlike the “July Effect” described in the United States—where less-experienced physicians may be more heavily involved in frontline care during their first month—Japanese institutions often assign experienced staff to key roles at the start of the academic year. This institutional adaptation may help maintain or even enhance the quality of care despite the administrative transition. Furthermore, Japanese medical institutions typically exclude newly hired physicians from overnight and weekend duties during their initial training period. This practice may result in more experienced physicians being preferentially assigned to emergency cases during early April, potentially contributing to improved outcomes in high-acuity interventions such as ECPR. However, these are speculative explanations, and we did not collect institutional-level data to evaluate these practices directly. Future research is warranted to explore whether such structural adaptations influence outcomes during turnover periods.

Although early April admission was associated with higher 30-day survival among ECPR patients in this study, no significant difference was observed in neurologically favorable survival. This finding aligns with previous randomized controlled trials, which have suggested that ECPR may improve overall survival compared with conventional CPR,19 but have not consistently demonstrated a clear benefit in long-term neurological outcomes.19, 20

Overall, our findings suggest that with proper institutional preparation, periods of staff turnover do not necessarily compromise patient outcomes—even in the context of highly complex and time-sensitive interventions such as ECPR. Rather than posing a threat to care quality, workforce transitions may serve as an opportunity to reinforce system readiness and operational resilience.

This study has several limitations. First, although we utilized a large, nationwide registry, the number of eligible cases was limited because of our focus on a narrow time window surrounding the turnover period and the restriction to ECPR-treated patients. While this approach enhanced the internal validity of our analysis by targeting a clinically homogeneous and high-risk population, it inevitably reduced the overall sample size and statistical power. In particular, the ECPR subgroup comprised a relatively small number of cases, resulting in wide confidence intervals and a borderline p-value; therefore, the observed association may be unstable and should be interpreted with caution. Second, as with any observational study, there remains the possibility of residual confounding. Although we adjusted for key covariates such as age, presence or absence of bystander CPR, initial cardiac rhythm at hospital arrival, and time to hospital, other unmeasured factors—such as seasonal variation, severity of illness, staffing patterns, or institutional protocols—may have influenced the results. Specifically, hospital-level factors such as institutional ECPR case volume, staffing composition, or specific local protocols were not adjusted for in our analysis and may have contributed to residual confounding. Third, the decision to initiate ECPR and the specific protocols used were at the discretion of individual institutions, which may have introduced variability in patient selection and timing of intervention. Fourth, our findings may not be fully generalizable to other healthcare systems because they are derived from the Japanese context. In addition, our study population was restricted to patients who arrived at the hospital without return of spontaneous circulation, which further limits the generalizability of the findings to all OHCA patients. However, the insights may still be applicable to institutions aiming to strengthen preparedness during periods of workforce transition. Fifth, our registry did not capture objective information on the actual scale of physician staff turnover or the mitigation measures implemented by individual hospitals. To our knowledge, such data have not been reported in prior studies either. Therefore, we were unable to evaluate how institutional responses to staff transitions may have influenced patient outcomes. Finally, the observational nature of this study precludes causal inference. The associations observed should therefore be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating. In addition, we did not adjust for multiple testing across cohorts and outcomes; therefore, the findings, particularly in the ECPR subgroup, should be regarded as exploratory. Prospective studies may be needed to more definitively assess the impact of staff transitions on patient outcomes in critical care settings.

Conclusions

Although no significant effect was observed in the overall OHCA population, 30-day survival among patients with OHCA undergoing ECPR was higher immediately after the start of the new academic year, when physician turnover typically occurs, but these findings should be interpreted with caution given the observational nature and limited sample size.
