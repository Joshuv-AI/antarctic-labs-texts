DAM-IN: Comprehensive Dam Catchment Attributes for Dam Safety Studies in India
Authors: Mayank Tiwari, Saran Aadhar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Indian subcontinent has around 5715 dams across different rivers, which play a pivotal role in the growth of various economic sectors in the country. In this study, we developed a dataset of catchment attributes for 5715 dams over the Indian subcontinent using observed, reanalysis, and remote sensing data. The comprehensive dam dataset consists of six major attributes, including topography, climate, geology and groundwater, soil, LULC, and human-induced activities. Using the 30-m SRTM data, we delineated the watershed for each dam using the automatic outlet relocation algorithm and estimated various topographic attributes for each catchment. We developed geological, soil, and LULC attributes for each dam catchment, considering existing global and regional datasets. We also included human-induced activities datasets for each dam catchment, which can be useful in studying the impact of human activities on dam safety and water resources. Overall, the comprehensive dam dataset consists of high-quality catchment attributes for each dam, which can be used to conduct various scientific studies, dam safety analyses, and impact studies on Indian dams.Subject terms: Hydrology, Natural hazards, Water resources

Background & Summary

Dam infrastructures play an important role in the socio-economic development of any region by fulfilling the water demands of various key sectors such as agriculture, household, and industry. Dams enhance resilience in river discharge management during extreme hydrological conditions1–4. In many regions around the world, dam reservoirs not only fulfil the water demands of human society but are also used for recreational activities for visitors. In contrast to benefits, dams obstruct the natural flow of water, disrupting aquatic ecosystems and reducing sediment transport, which negatively impacts downstream agriculture and fisheries-dependent economies5. Rather than viewing dams solely in terms of advantages and disadvantages, it is important to recognize that dams in India serve multiple purposes, such as flood control, electricity generation, and food security. Despite the versatility and significance of dam infrastructures, they have a risk of massive damage to human society living downstream due to their failure6.

Increased human activities and climate change have posed several challenges to the dam infrastructure across the globe7–10. Due to climate change, the summer monsoon rainfall has become erratic in the last few decades over India and has led to severe and widespread hydrological extremes in the region11–14. The increased frequency of hydrological extremes during the observed climate has caused massive damage to dam infrastructure and water availability in the reservoirs15,16. For instance, the Tiware Dam in Maharashtra was breached in the year 2019 due to heavy flooding, which caused large-scale damage in seven downstream villages. Many other dams (such as the Machchhu Dam in Gujarat, Chungthang Dam in Sikkim, Malana Dam in Himachal Pradesh, etc.) have been damaged due to catastrophic flood events in the past17. Recently, the Central Water Commission (CWC) of India reported that around 44% of dam failure cases are due to hydrological extremes. These hydrological extremes are projected to increase further over the Indian Subcontinent14. Therefore, there is an urgent need to understand the risk of hydrological and hydraulic extremes on dam infrastructure due to climate change and human activities.

To perform the hydrological, hydraulic, and any other dam safety studies on dam infrastructure, the comprehensive dam catchment datasets are required, including static datasets (such as geology, soil type and topographical features, which remain constant over time) and dynamic datasets (such as precipitation, ground water levels, and evapotranspiration, which fluctuate over time for the dam catchment). Previous studies18–23 developed regional and global datasets for Dams, which include dam height, length, completion year, area, river basin, and reservoir capacity datasets. For instance, Lehner et al.22 developed the Global Dam Watch database, which catalogues 41,414 barriers (dams). Similarly, Zhang and Gu19 developed a Global Dam Tracker (GDAT) database documenting over 35000 dams with information on their location, catchment, completion and construction years, height and length, reservoir capacity and area, and purpose of the dam19,22. However, these global datasets do not include all the static and dynamic datasets required for dam catchments to conduct various hydrological and hydraulic studies. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop the various dam catchment attributes that can consider the physical and hydrometeorological characteristics of the dam, such as soil, topography, geology, climate, Land Use Land Cover and vegetation, groundwater, and human-induced activities to perform the hydrological and hydraulic studies on dam infrastructure.

In this study, we develop an open and comprehensive dataset for dam catchments of the Indian subcontinent. A total of 5715 dam catchments for 18 river basins is included in the dataset (Fig. 1, Figure S1). These dam catchment attributes are developed for six characteristics: topography, geology and groundwater, soil, Land Use and Land Cover and vegetation, climate, and human-induced activities. All these attributes are easily accessible and in a usable format. This large dataset can be used to perform various studies and to unravel the various unanswered questions about dams and dam safety in India. The developed dataset can also be used for reservoir management, water quantity studies, environmental risk assessment, and climate change risk analysis.

Methods

We estimated the six catchment characteristics (topography, geology and groundwater, soil, Land Use and Land Cover (LULC), climate, and human-induced activities) for each dam catchment in India using various reanalysis, observed, and remote-sensing data (Fig. 1). These characteristics collectively capture the essential physical, environmental, and anthropogenic factors that influence hydrological and hydraulic processes within a catchment. In this section, we discussed the source of various datasets used to develop the dam catchment attributes over the Indian Subcontinent. Moreover, we provided the details of various static and dynamic catchment attributes and discussed the methodology to evaluate these attributes in this section.

Watershed delineation and topographical attributes

First, we delineated the watershed for each dam to identify the catchment boundary to estimate the six catchment characteristics (topographic, Geological, Soil, Vegetation, Climate, and Human-induced activities) and their attributes for each dam catchment (Fig. 1b). To delineate the watershed, we used a Digital Elevation Map (DEM) from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM; https://www.usgs.gov/centers/eros/science/usgs-eros-archive-digital-elevation-shuttle-radar-topography-mission-srtm-non) at 30 m spatial resolution24. The DEM data from SRTM have been widely used in various remote sensing, hydrological, and other studies for watershed delineation25–29. Previous studies25,30 showed that the DEM from SRTM is more reliable and accurate than other DEM datasets.

Initially, we pre-processed the DEM data using the ArcGIS tool (Figure S2). The sinks in the DEM data were filled during the pre-processing step to avoid possible errors in the watershed delineation due to unreasonable peaks and depressions. After the sink filled in the DEM data, we estimated the flow direction for each grid of filled DEM data using the flow direction tool in ArcGIS. Later, the flow-direction data were used to delineate the catchment for all dams using the Automatic Outlet Relocation (AOR) algorithm30. The AOR algorithm was preferred over the manual allocation of the Snap Pour Point (as outlet) in ArcGIS for watershed delineation due to its accuracy and time to perform the task of delineation30. The dam locations (as outlet points) were obtained from the National Register of Large Dams (NRLD 2019) to delineate the watershed using the AOR algorithm.

Considering the catchment boundary, we estimated the various topographic attributes for each dam using DEM data and ArcGIS tools. The topographic attributes include mean elevation, slope, area, perimeter, and circularity ratio of the catchment. We estimated the mean elevation and slope of dam catchments considering the DEM data and dam catchment boundaries using the spatial analyst tools of ArcGIS. We also estimated the circularity ratio for dam catchments using delineated catchment boundaries. The topographic attributes of dam catchments are useful for understanding the behavior and characteristics of water flow31. Previous studies showed that the catchment topography (such as elevation, slope, and shape) significantly alters the discharge peaks, sediment yield, and erosion rates31,32.

Geological attributes

The geological condition of the catchment plays a significant role in the movement of water in various directions. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the geological features of the catchment. We used two comprehensive world datasets (Global Lithological Map and Global Hydrogeology Map) to develop the various geological attributes for all dams in India. The Global Lithological Map (GLiM29; https://www.geo.uni-hamburg.de/en/geologie/forschung/aquatische-geochemie/glim.html was used to identify the geology class of the dam catchment. In GLiM data, the lithology of any location is classified into three levels29. The first level shows 16 major lithological classes, and the subsequent second and third levels have 12 and 14 subclasses that explain more specific rock features33. To identify the topmost geology class of the dam catchment, we considered first-level GLiM data for the geological classification. We also evaluated the two most important hydrogeological attributes (permeability and subsurface porosity) to understand the behaviors of rocks toward water movement and retention. We used GLobal HYdrogeology MaPS (GLHYMPS30) data to evaluate the permeability and subsurface porosity of each dam in our dataset. The permeability and subsurface porosity in the GLHYMPS (https://borealisdata.ca/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi:10.5683/SP2/DLGXYO) data were estimated using the high-resolution global lithology map34. For each dam catchment, the area average porosity and permeability attributes were calculated using GLHYMPS data. The geological attributes are important features for hydrological and groundwater modeling. These can also be used to analyze the sediment deposition rate in the dam catchment and to understand the dynamics between groundwater and the land surface.

Our dataset also included the groundwater data (mean value and time series) for each dam catchment. The groundwater depth (low or high) significantly affects the hydrological conditions of the dam catchment. For instance, a high groundwater table reflected greater flood potential compared to a low groundwater depth in the recent studies35,36. Similarly, the low groundwater table in the catchment was associated with higher susceptibility to drought37. To estimate the mean groundwater level for each dam catchment, we obtained the groundwater data of more than 4900 stations from the Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) through the India Water Resources Information System (India-WRIS; https://indiawris.gov.in/wris/#/groundWater) for the period 1996 to 2020. Based on the availability of measurement wells in the dam catchment, we estimated the area-average groundwater depth for each dam. In case of unavailability of measurement wells in the catchment, we considered the nearest groundwater well data to estimate the groundwater attributes.

Soil attributes

Soil is a significant storage component in hydrological and land surface modeling38. In our comprehensive dataset, we identified eight primary soil attributes, which are useful for various hydrological, hydraulic, geoscience, and agroecological studies. These soil attributes are texture, organic carbon content, available water capacity, conductivity (saturated hydraulic conductivity, Table S1), depth, porosity, density, and maximum water content. We obtained soil texture, organic carbon content, and available water capacity from the Harmonised World Soil Database (HWSD, version 2.0; https://www.fao.org/soils-portal/data-hub/soil-maps-and-databases/harmonized-world-soil-database-v20/en/), a collaborative effort between the Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) and the International Institute for Applied System Analysis (IIASA). The HWSD database divides soil strata into different depth levels with intervals of 20 cm and 50 cm. These strata depths have different values of soil parameters. We used FAO data to obtain soil conductivity, porosity, bulk density, and maximum water content variables for dam catchments. Land Use Land Cover (LULC) and vegetation attributes

Rapid urbanization and intensive human activities have changed the LULC patterns in the last few decades. The changes in the LULC pose a significant impact on the hydrological and land surface processes39,40. In our dataset, we presented six LULC attributes, which are commonly used in the hydrological and land surface models to evaluate the runoff and other hydrological components in the watershed. These six attributes are the dominant type of LULC class, a fraction of build-up, agriculture, forest, scrub, and water cover in the watershed. We estimated the six attributes for each dam catchment using LULC data. The LULC data were obtained from the National Remote Sensing Centre (NRSC; https://bhuvan-app1.nrsc.gov.in/thematic/thematic/index.php) at a spatial resolution of 56 m for the year 2015–2016. NRSC-LULC data were created using multi-temporal terrain-corrected ortho-rectified AWiFS images processed through the NRC-AWiFS LULC project.

To evaluate the LULC attributes for the dam catchment dataset, the LULC data from NRSC were reclassified into five primary LULC classes, such as build-up, agriculture, forest, scrub, and water. We estimated the dominant LULC class and fraction of each LULC class for each dam catchment. The dominant LULC class was estimated as the most often occurring class in the catchment area. The fraction of build-up, agriculture, forest, scrub, and water cover was estimated based on the total catchment area and the area of each class.

Apart from LULC attributes, we also presented the seasonal variation in the vegetation cover over Indian dam catchments using a vegetation indicator. We used the seasonal Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) to evaluate the temporal variability in the vegetation cover over the catchment. The seasonal NDVI was evaluated for four seasons [June-August (JJA), September-November (SON), December-February (DJF), and March-May (MAM)] using Advanced Very High-Resolution Radiometer Surface Reflectance (AVHRR) data for the period 1981 to 2020 at 0.05° × 0.05° spatial resolution. Climatic attributes

Spatial and temporal variations in the climate attributes are essential for understanding any catchment’s atmospheric and possible hydrological behaviour. In the dam catchment dataset, we presented the daily area-average time series of the most commonly used climate variables (such as precipitation, maximum and minimum temperature, wind, and potential evapotranspiration) for each dam catchment for the period 1951 to 2019. Moreover, we included several other climatic attributes in the previous studies43–45, such as high and low precipitation frequency, season and spell, aridity, and seasonality in our dataset. These climatic attributes were defined under the project of the World Climate Research Programme (WCRP) on Climate and Ocean: Variability, Predictability, and Change46. We also presented the maximum precipitation value for each dam in the dam catchment dataset since the maximum precipitation value (that occurred during the observed period) is important for the dam safety studies (such as estimation of design flood).

To estimate the climate attributes for each dam catchment, we obtained the gridded dataset from the India Meteorological Department (IMD; https://www.imdpune.gov.in/) and the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) reanalysis product (ERA5; https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/reanalysis-era5-single-levels?tab = overview) at the spatial resolution of 0.25° for the period 1951–2019. Since IMD data is available only for the Indian region, we used ERA5 data for the catchment areas outside the Indian region. Mahto and Mishra (2019) evaluated various meteorological variables of ERA5 and found that ERA5 data provide the most reliable climate reanalysis data. ERA5 uses past observed datasets to simulate accurate and consistent meteorological variables47 across the globe. Recently, ERA5 data have been widely used in various hydrological and geoscience studies47–49.

Daily area-averaged time series of precipitation, minimum and maximum temperature, maximum wind, and evaporation (actual evapotranspiration) were evaluated from the gridded IMD and ERA5 data for each dam catchment. However, potential evapotranspiration (PET) was estimated using the Hargreaves-Samani method for the period 1951–2019 to construct a daily area-averaged time series of PET for each dam catchment50. We also evaluated the various other climatic attributes, such as high and low precipitation frequency, season, spell, and seasonality, for our dataset using area-averaged daily precipitation data from 1951 to 2019 for each dam catchment. To estimate the frequency of high precipitation days (per year) in each dam catchment, we calculated the average annual number of days with precipitation exceeding five times the mean daily precipitation during the period 1951 to 2019. Similarly, the frequency of low precipitation was estimated by calculating the average annual number of days with less than 1 mm of precipitation during the period 1951–2019. We also estimated the frequency of high and low precipitation days for each season [winter (January-February), pre-monsoon (March-May), monsoon (June-September), and post-monsoon (October-December)] to estimate the low and high precipitation seasons for each dam catchment. The high precipitation season is the season during which most high precipitation days (precipitation exceeding five times the mean daily precipitation) occur, while the low precipitation season is the season during which most dry days (with less than 1 mm of precipitation) occur. In addition to high and low precipitation frequency and season, we estimated the duration of low and high precipitation spells in each dam catchment. The high precipitation spell is defined as the average duration of high precipitation events (consecutive precipitation days exceeding five times the mean daily precipitation) for the period 1951 to 2019. However, the low precipitation spell is defined as the average duration of low precipitation events (continuous precipitation days with less than 1 mm of precipitation). We also calculated the seasonality of precipitation for each dam catchment using the area average precipitation over the period 1951–2019. Human-induced activities

Due to human-induced activities, the natural conditions of dam catchments are continuously changing in the largely populated Indian subcontinent. The anthropogenic activities can affect the various land surface fluxes, hydrological and hydraulic processes and parameters, and ecological parameters of the dam catchments. In the dam catchment dataset, we estimated four different types of human-induced activities for each dam catchment. These human-induced activities are road density, human footprint, night-time light, and population count. We obtained the road density data from the Worldwide Roads Inventory (https://www.globio.info/download-grip-dataset) Project at a spatial resolution of 5 arcmin52. The Worldwide Roads Inventory has collected and included more than 60 geographic datasets on road infrastructure52. We also used human footprint data, which measures the exploitation of Earth’s natural resources, from the Global Human Footprint (GHF) dataset from 1993 to 200953. To develop the GHF database, eight variables (population density, built environments, pasture, cropland, navigation waterways, railroads, highway, and electric infrastructure) were considered to quantify both indirect and direct human actions53. The range of GHF varies from 0 to 50 (https://gis.earthdata.nasa.gov/portal/home/item.html?id=e1f0028da5d84d0da724b6dc783f3f23).

In addition to road density and human footprint data, we obtained the night-time light data from the Defence Meteorological Satellite Program (DMSP) of the U.S. Air Force to estimate the human activities in the dam catchment54 The DMSP Operational Linescan System (DMSP-OLS) acquired the source emitted visible and near-infrared light during nighttime to provide high-resolution nigh-time light data at a spatial resolution of 30 arcsec for the period 1992 to 2013 (https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/dmsp-operational-linescan-system). In this study, we used the variable “avg_lights_x_pc” to estimate the cloud-free area average night-time light data for each dam catchment. Variable “avg_lights_x_pc” here refers to a stable light product from nighttime light data, and its DN value ranges from 0 (no lighting) to 63 (highest lighting)55. To estimate the population, count in the dataset, we obtained the Gridded Population of the World version 4.11 from the Centre for International Earth Science Information Network (CIESIN), Columbia University. The GPW (https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/projects/gpw) data were estimated at the interval of five years (namely, 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020) with a spatial resolution of 30 arcsec.

Data Records

The dataset is available as a zip folder (DAM_IN_Dataset.zip) on Zenodo56. This zip folder has 19 subfolders and 21 text/Excel files. Out of 19 subfolders, 18 correspond to individual river basins. Each river basin folder contains multiple dam folders, and each dam catchment folder includes four attribute folders along with two attribute text files.

Technical Validation

Our dam catchment attributes data are developed using the various widely used observed, reanalysis, and remote sensing data (mentioned in the previous section). To validate our dataset, we displayed the spatial distribution of each dam attribute on the map to confirm that the dam attributes capture spatial variation across the Indian Subcontinent, similar to the source dataset. For instance, Figure S3 shows elevation from STRM at 30 m spatial resolution and the mean elevation of the dam catchments.

Using the SRTM-DEM data, we delineated the dam catchments and estimated four primary topographic attributes (mean elevation, slope, area, and circularity ratio) for each dam in our dataset (Fig. 2). Similar to the source data, a large variation was observed in the mean elevation of dam catchments, which varies from 25 m to 1000 m across the Indian Subcontinent (Fig. 2a; Figure S3). As expected, our data also showed a low and mild slope in the dam catchments in central India compared to those in the coastal and Northern regions (Fig. 2b). The majority of dam catchments have low slopes (less than 7 m/km) in the Indian Subcontinent. The area of the catchment was also observed to be less than 500 km2 for the majority of the dams (Fig. 2c). We found that only 15% of dam catchments are more than 500 km2. The circularity ratio (Rc) is another important topographical attribute57, which can significantly affect the various hydrological and hydraulic processes in the dam catchment. We estimated the Rc value for each dam catchment. The majority of dam catchments have Rc values less than 0.5 (low Rc values), which represent the elongated catchment shape58. In addition to these four topographic attributes, we evaluated the minimum elevation, maximum elevation, and catchment perimeter for each dam catchment over the Indian Subcontinent (Figure S4). Overall, we developed seven area-averaged topographic attributes (area, perimeter, circularity ratio, mean elevation, maximum elevation, minimum elevation, and mean slope) for each dam catchment in our dataset.

The majority of the dam catchments (~61%) in central India have a shallow groundwater level between 3 and 7.5 m (Fig. 3d). However, dam catchments in the southern and western parts of the country have deep groundwater levels (Fig. 3d).

The dam catchments in the Indian Subcontinent rely on two monsoon seasons (the southwest monsoon and the northeast monsoon). The southwest monsoon (during July-September) is the predominant rainy season in India (except for a few southern states), during which a significant area of the nation receives more than 80% of annual precipitation. We observed the seasonality index value between 0.95 and 1.15 for dam catchments in India, which represents the majority occurrence of precipitation in three months (Table 2 and Fig. 9). Since the dam infrastructure is vulnerable to extreme precipitation events, we estimated the maximum precipitation for each dam catchment and found a large variation in the maximum precipitation values, ranging from 40 to 440 mm (Fig. 9). We also estimated high precipitation frequency, spell, and season for each dam catchment. The dam catchments in the Western Ghats and Gujarat show the frequent occurrence of extreme precipitation events for a longer duration (Fig. 9a–c). Similar to extreme precipitation, we estimated the low precipitation frequency, spell, and season for each dam catchment. The pre-monsoon season is the low precipitation season for the majority of dam catchments (Fig. 9). Our dataset provided the area-average climatic attributes for each dam catchment to represent the climatic condition.

Finally, we estimated human-induced activities (road density, human footprint, night-time light, and population count) for each dam catchment to evaluate the human influence in dam catchments. Based on the spatial distribution of four human-induced activities, we observed that human activities, such as road density and human footprint, are significant over the majority of the dam catchment. However, the population and stable light density are small in the majority of dam catchments. For instance, most dam catchments have road density in the range of 200–400 m km−2 and human footprint in the range of 12 to 20, while most dam catchments show a population of less than 10000 and light emission values of less than 1 (Fig. 10).

Usage Note

The presented dataset covers a wide range of catchment attributes (such as topography, geology, soil, LULC and Vegetation, climate, and human-induced activities) for 5715 dam catchments over the Indian Subcontinent. The comprehensive and high-quality dataset can be used for a wide range of studies, including hydrological, hydraulic, and environmental, for various Dam infrastructures in India. Moreover, the dataset will attract a lot of attention to conduct dam safety studies by various state water resources agencies and organizations across India. We believe that the dataset is useful in the surveillance, inspection, operation, and maintenance of vast dam infrastructure networks in India to ensure their safety and proper functioning. Moreover, the dataset can be used to conduct the dam break analysis for the Indian Dams to understand the downstream vulnerability in the case of dam failure. While the presented dataset offers significant benefits for hydrological and hydraulic analysis, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations arising from the use of different data sources for specific attributes. For LULC, we utilized a regionally developed LULC dataset (NRSC-based dataset) at a higher resolution to ensure greater accuracy and regional relevance. However, for the human-induced activities, we used the existing global human footprint (GHF) datasets due to the unavailability of a regional dataset. The GHF data were estimated considering different LULC datasets available at the global level. These inconsistencies in the dataset used to derive the dam catchment attributes may introduce additional uncertainty in the resultant outcome. Despite these limitations, we believe our datasets remain a valuable resource for regional studies. Overall, we are confident that the synthesis of various datasets into one dataset opens up many opportunities for engineers, researchers, scientists, and policymakers to test and develop various unique hypotheses in the field of hydrology, hydraulics, and the environment.
