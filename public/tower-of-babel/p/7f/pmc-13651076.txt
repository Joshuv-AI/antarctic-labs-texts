Eating behavior traits across thyroid function groups in Saudi adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hayat Alzahrani, Sahar G. Alshammari, Soaad Alsulami, Sahar Afeef, Nehal S. Alsharif, Sarah Althubeati, Sarah M. Arafsha, Amal Aloud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThyroid dysfunction is known to impact metabolic rate and body weight, yet its relationship with eating behavior remains underexplored in Saudi adults. This study explores the differences in appetitive traits across self-reported thyroid status groups using the validated Arabic version of the Adult Eating Behavior Questionnaire (AEBQ-Ar).MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 439 adults in Saudi Arabia (median age = 39 years, median BMI = 29.06 kg/m2, females = 59.9%). Participants self-reported their thyroid diagnosis status (normal, hypothyroid, or hyperthyroid), demographic data, and eating behaviors using AEBQ-Ar. General Linear Model regression analyses were used to assess differences in eating behavior subscales across self-reported thyroid groups, adjusting for age, sex, and BMI with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.ResultsAfter adjustment for covariates, both the self-reported hypothyroid and hyperthyroid groups reported significantly higher emotional overeating (β = 1.032 and β = 1.198, respectively; both p < 0.001), hunger (β = 0.696 and β = 0.780; both p < 0.001), satiety responsiveness (β = 0.464 and β = 0.761; both p < 0.001), and slowness in eating (β = 0.511 and β = 0.711; both p < 0.001) compared with individuals reporting normal thyroid function. Food fussiness was higher in the self-reported hypothyroid group compared with the normal group (β = 0.196, p = 0.006). No other traits differed significantly between the self-reported hypothyroid and hyperthyroid groups.ConclusionsSelf-reported thyroid dysfunction was associated with higher emotional overeating, hunger, satiety responsiveness, and slower eating. Because thyroid function tests and treatment data were not collected, these findings reflect associations with self-reported status rather than confirmed current function. Nevertheless, clinicians may consider assessing eating behaviors in patients with thyroid disorders. Future studies should include biochemical confirmation and treatment history.

Introduction

The thyroid is a vital endocrine gland that secretes thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), hormones that are essential for maintaining metabolic homeostasis [1]. These hormones regulate resting energy expenditure, thermogenesis, metabolism, and body weight [2, 3]. The production of thyroid hormones is regulated by the hypothalamic pituitary thyroid axis. Disruption in this regulatory system can lead to excessive or insufficient secretion of thyroid hormones, resulting in hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism, respectively [1]. Hyperthyroidism is characterised by a hypermetabolic state with increased energy expenditure and weight loss [4], while hypothyroidism is associated with reduced energy expenditure and weight gain [4].

In addition to their metabolic effects, thyroid hormones exert important central effects on appetite regulation [5]. Thyroid hormones have been shown to influence appetite by modulating the activity of appetite-regulating neurons in the arcuate nucleus within the hypothalamus [5]. Evidence suggests that thyroid dysfunction alters peripheral appetite-regulating hormones such as ghrelin and leptin, which play key roles in stimulating and suppressing appetite [6, 7]. These mechanisms suggest that disturbances in thyroid function may contribute to altered eating behaviors.

Emerging evidence indicates that individuals with thyroid dysfunction may exhibit differences in eating behavior traits. A study in obese women with hypothyroidism reported higher levels of shape concerns and disordered eating attitudes compared to those with normal thyroid function [8]. More recently, patients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis were found to have a greater prevalence of eating disorders compared to healthy controls [9]. The existing literature has used tools assessing eating disorders rather than validated measures of normal eating behavior traits. This highlights the need to investigate eating behavior traits in thyroid dysfunction using tools designed to detect daily appetite characteristics.

Despite the well-established metabolic effects of thyroid dysfunction, little is known about how eating behaviors differ between adults with normal thyroid function and those with thyroid dysfunction in Saudi Arabia. This is particularly important in Saudi Arabia, where thyroid dysfunction is prevalent and where cultural dietary patterns may influence behavioral responses. Clarifying these differences could help healthcare professionals tailor nutrition counseling and identify subgroups needing additional nutritional and psychological support. Therefore, this study aimed to compare eating behavior traits measured by the Arabic version of the Adult Eating Behaviors Questionnaire (AEBQ-Ar) between participants with normal thyroid function and those with thyroid dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Saudi Arabia to examine differences in eating behavior between adults with normal thyroid function and those with self-reported thyroid disorders (hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism). A convenience sampling approach was employed, and data were collected between [March-May/2025] using an anonymous online questionnaire created in Google Forms. The online questionnaire was distributed via social media platforms and word of mouth. Sample size estimation was performed a priori using G*Power (version 3.1) for multiple linear regression. Assuming a small-to-moderate effect size (f2 = 0.04), α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and five degrees of freedom (three thyroid status groups, age, sex and BMI), the required minimum sample size was 327 participants. Allowing for approximately 10% invalid or incomplete responses, the target sample size was set at 360 participants. The final sample included in the analysis was 394 participants. Eligible participants were adult men and women aged 18–65 years living in Saudi Arabia, while exclusion criteria comprised pregnancy or breastfeeding, chronic diseases other than thyroid disorders [e.g., diabetes (n = 12), hypertension (n = 13), other comorbidities (n = 9)], psychiatric or neurological conditions(n = 2), and use of medications that known to affect appetite or weight (other than thyroid medications, n = 9). Chronic diseases and thyroid dysfunction status were self-reports which were based on physician diagnoses. Self-reporting of thyroid status is commonly used in large epidemiologic cohorts, as medical record was not available. All participants provided electronic informed consent after being informed of the study objectives, protocol, and confidentiality safeguards. The ethics committee board of human and social studies at King Saud University, Saudi Arabia, approved the study (IRB: KSU-HE-25-012). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

Sociodemographic and health information

Data were collected using a self-administered online questionnaire, which consisted of two sections. The first section captured sociodemographic and health information, including age, sex, education level (illiterate/primary school/high school/graduate/postgraduate), marital status (single/married/divorced/widowed), employment status (employed/unemployed), residency region (central/north/south/east/west), and self-reported height and weight, which were used to calculate BMI (body weight in kg divided by height in meters squared). Participants were classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) Body Mass Index (BMI) classification: underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2) [10]. The use of indirect measures like BMI can be justified in large-scale public health studies because they are easy to apply, cost-effective, and provide valuable data for comparison across populations [11]. Participants were categorized into three thyroid status groups: normal, hypothyroid, and hyperthyroid based on self-reported diagnosis. Thyroid function tests and treatment data were not collected; therefore, these groups reflect self-reported thyroid status rather than biochemically confirmed current thyroid function. For readability, thyroid groups are referred to hereafter as normal thyroid status, hypothyroid, and hyperthyroid groups.

Adult eating behavior questionnaire (AEBQ)

The second section of the questionnaire assessed the participants’ eating behaviors using the Arabic version of the Adult Eating Behavior Questionnaire (AEBQ-Ar), which was translated and validated for use among the Saudi population [12]. The AEBQ-Ar is based on the original AEBQ developed by Hunot et al. (2016) and consists of 27 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree). The items cover six subscales, categorized as either food-approach behaviors (Hunger (items 8, 21, 25, and 27), Enjoyment of Food (items 1, 3, 4, 16, 18, and 26), Emotional Overeating (items 5, 7, 9, 13, and 15)) or food-avoidant behaviors (Satiety Responsiveness (items 10, 17, 23, and 24), Food Fussiness (items 2, 6, 11, and 14), Slowness in Eating (items 12, 19, 20, and 22)). Subscale scores were calculated by averaging the responses to the items within each subscale, with higher scores indicating greater expression of the corresponding eating behavior trait as described previously [12]. Food-approach traits are generally positively associated with BMI, while food-avoidant traits are negatively associated [12]. The AEBQ-Ar has demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.66 to 0.89 for individual subscales [12].

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test alongside visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Results indicated that both age and BMI were not normally distributed. Accordingly, descriptive statistics were used to summarize study variables as follows: categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages (n [%]), while continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Between-thyroid status groups comparisons for categorical variables (e.g., sex, marital status) were conducted using chi-square tests. For non-normally distributed continuous variables (i.e., age, BMI), the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to assess differences across thyroid status groups. To evaluate the independent association between thyroid status and appetitive traits, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted for each subscale of the AEBQ-Ar. Each model included thyroid status as the main independent variable and was adjusted for age, sex, and BMI as covariates. Results are reported as regression coefficients (β) with corresponding standard errors (SE). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all tests, with a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of p < 0.0083 (0.05/6) applied for regression analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp).

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that individuals with thyroid dysfunction could have altered eating behaviours compared to those with normal thyroid functions [9]. This cross-sectional study has compared eating behaviour traits between individuals with thyroid dysfunction and those with normal thyroid functions. Additionally, it investigated whether eating behaviour traits could be related with demographic and clinical characteristics, including BMI and age. Our study revealed higher scores of emotional overeating, enjoyment of food, slow-ness in eating, satiety responsiveness, and hunger scores in both thyroid dysfunction groups compared to the normal thyroid function group. These findings are aligned with a previous study by Çetinkaya Altuntaş, who found that patients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis had a greater prevalence of eating disorders compared to healthy controls [9].

These findings suggested that thyroid level imbalances may influence appetite regulation and eating behaviour through underlying neuroendocrine mechanisms. Previous studies have shown that high levels of thyroid hormones during hyperthyroidism can alter circulating levels of appetite-regulating hormones ghrelin and leptin [13, 7]. Ghrelin, a hormone released by the stomach, promotes appetite by stimulating the orexigenic neuropeptide Y (NPY) and agouti-related peptide (AgRP), while inhibiting the anorexigenic pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) pathway [14]. On the other hand, leptin, secreted from adipose tissue, suppresses food intake by inhibiting the orexigenic neuropeptides NPY and AgRP and stimulating the anorexigenic POMC neuropeptide [15].

Studies have reported that ghrelin levels tend to decrease during hyperthyroidism [7], while leptin levels may be normal [16] or reduced [13]. Conversely, hypothyroidism is associated with elevated leptin concentrations [8], likely reflecting increased adiposity and fat mass accompanying weight gain, whereas ghrelin levels reported to be normal [7] or slightly elevated [16]. These hormonal alterations suggest that thyroid dysfunction may influence appetite signalling pathways through both peripheral hormonal changes and central neuroendocrine mechanisms, which could partly explain the differences in eating behaviour observed in our study between participants with thyroid dysfunction and those with normal thyroid function.

However, despite the potential role of these hormonal alterations in appetite regulation, several studies have shown that leptin and ghrelin levels return to their normal levels after treating hyperthyroidism. This indicates that the persistence of energy imbalances after achieving euthyroidism is not likely to be solely explained by alterations in these appetite-regulating hormones [7, 17]. Instead, additional mechanisms could contribute, including psychological changes associated with the thyroid disease. Individuals with thyroid disorders usually experience mood disturbances such as depression and anxiety, which may predispose these individuals to emotional overeating as a maladaptive coping strategy [13, 14].

Moreover, other contributors may include residual adaptations within the central nervous system affecting hypothalamic appetite regulation, as well as alterations in body composition that persist beyond biochemical normalization. Furthermore, elevated thyroid hormone levels may induce longer-lasting changes in central metabolic pathways and energy regulation, which may persist even after thyroid function tests return to normal [3, 18].

In line with these potential effects of thyroid dysfunction on eating behaviours, we found higher scores for food fussiness in the hypothyroidism group compared to normal thyroid function group. However, no differences were observed between hyperthyroidism group and normal thyroid function group or between hyper-and hypothyroidism groups. One possible explanation for this observation is that hypothyroidism has been associated with alterations in taste and falvour perception, which could change food preferences and increase selective eating behaviour [19].

The descriptive data in this study revealed that age and BMI were significantly different between the thyroid dysfunction groups and the normal thyroid function group. These findings suggest that increasing age and BMI could be associated with thyroid dysfunction. This is consistent with previous studies that showed both ageing and obesity are associated with impaired thyroid dysfunction [20].

Overweight and obesity prevalence was greater among participants reporting a history of hyperthyroidism and, to a lesser extent, hypothyroidism compared to participants with normal thyroid function. Thyroid dysfunction has been clinically associated with weight alterations even after treatment and biochemical normalization of thyroid function. Previous studies have shown that individuals treated for hyperthyroidism usually experience weight re-gain and may later develop overweight or obesity after treatment [21, 22]. A number of explanations have been proposed, including longer-lasting changes in central metabolic pathways involved in energy balance, persistent alterations in appetite regulation, reductions in resting energy expenditure after treatment, and recovery of fat mass [3, 4]. Additionally, treatment-related factors may contribute to weight gain following treatment of hyperthyroidism. Torlinska et al. (2019) reported that radioactive iodine therapy and the development of hypothyroidism requiring levothyroxine replacement were associated with additional weight gain compared with antithyroid drug therapy alone.

Lifestyle and behavioural factors could also contribute to increased risk of overweight and obesity among individuals with thyroid dysfunction. Fatigue, anxiety, emotional distress, and impaired quality of life associated with thyroid disorders may contribute to reduced physical activity and poorer health outcomes, further promoting excess weight gain [23, 24]. These findings are consistent with previous literature showing a higher risk of overweight and obesity among individuals with thyroid disorders, particularly following treatment for hyperthyroidism [21, 22].

This study has a number of strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that compared eating behaviour traits between individuals who self-reported thyroid dysfunction and those with normal thyroid function in the kingdom of Saudi Arabia where research in this area is limited. It was conducted on a reasonable sample size and adjusted for key potential confounders, strengthening validity of the findings. It used the validated Arabic version of AEBQ which enhances reliability and cultural relevance of the findings. However, the study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design could limit the causality relationship between thyroid dysfunction and eating behaviour traits. Additionally, reliance on self-reported data of height, weight and thyroid status may introduce misclassification or recall bias of the findings. Also, Participants with chronic diseases, including diabetes mellitus and hypertension, were excluded based on self-reported. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design and recruitment process may be subject to selection bias, as reflected by the unexpected gender distribution in hyperthyroidism, and therefore our findings should be taken as relationships with self-reported history of thyroid disease, not with current thyroid dysfunction. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs and include objective measures of thyroid status and anthropometric parameters to further validate these findings.
