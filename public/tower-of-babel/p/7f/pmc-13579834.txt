Temporal improvements in prehospital systems-of-care for ST-elevation myocardial infarction patients in Victoria, Australia: a retrospective study
Authors: Andrew Bishop, Ziad Nehme, Dion Stub, Karen Murdoch, Ashanti Dantanarayana, Jesslyn Wijaya, Benjamin Meadley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrehospital care is central to outcomes in ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), yet how systems of care evolve over time remains incompletely described. This study aimed to evaluate the statewide temporal trends in prehospital ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) care in Victoria, Australia, examining changes in epidemiology, clinical management, time metrics, and adverse events.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis using data from the Victorian Ambulance STEMI Quality Initiative (VASQI). Adults with suspected STEMI treated by paramedics between January 2018 and December 2023 were included. Patient demographics, clinical presentation, and compliance with a prehospital bundle of care (aspirin administration, First Medical Contact [FMC] to ECG ≤ 10 min, hospital notification, and transport to percutaneous coronary intervention [PCI] facility) were analysed and further stratified into patients who received prehospital thrombolysis (PHT) or primary PCI (pPCI) reperfusion pathways. Descriptive statistics and nonparametric tests for trends were used.ResultsA total of 18,075 patients were included (median age 67 years, IQR 56–78; 69.7% men). The incidence of suspected STEMI was 58.2 cases per 100,000 person-years (≥ 18 years), increasing from 49.0 in 2018 to 59.5 in 2023 (p = 0.102). Bundle of care compliance was achieved in 69.1% of patients, increasing from 59.5% to 78.2% (+ 18.7% mean difference [MD], 95% CI 16.3–21.1; p for trend < 0.001). Improvements were observed in both PHT (MD, + 22.7%; 95% CI 13.9–31.4; p for trend < 0.001) and non-PHT reperfusion pathways (MD, + 18.4%; 95% CI 15.8–20.9; p for trend < 0.001). All bundle elements showed improvement: aspirin administration by 0.9% (95% CI 0.3–1.4; p for trend = 0.002), FMC to ECG ≤ 10 min by 8.7% (95% CI 6.6–10.8; p for trend < 0.001), hospital notification by 12.6% (95% CI 10.8–14.4; p for trend < 0.001), and direct transport to PCI by 0.9% (95% CI 0.0–1.9; p for trend = 0.046).ConclusionsParamedic compliance with the STEMI processes of care improved significantly over the study period. Although key clinical interventions for patients with STEMI have been delivered at high rates, opportunities remain to further optimise care, highlighting the importance of ongoing monitoring of clinical quality performance.Keywords: STEMI, Out of hospital, Quality registry, Bundle of Care, Myocardial Infarction

Background

ST-elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI) is a time-critical medical emergency where an acute occlusion of one or more coronary arteries occurs, reducing blood supply to the myocardium. If untreated, it can lead to complete infarction of myocardial tissue [1] and high morbidity and mortality [2]. In Australia, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), of which STEMI is the most high-risk subset, represents a substantial source of health service utilisation. Approximately 40,200 new ACS events in people ≥ 25 years occurred in 2018, equivalent to around 110 events per day [3], with STEMI representing 24% of non-fatal events. In Victoria, contemporary Ambulance Victoria (AV) data indicate that paramedics identified 2,874 patients with suspected STEMI between July 2024 and June 2025, with more than 200 patients receiving prehospital thrombolysis (PHT) [4].

STEMI is identified using a combination of clinical signs and symptoms, in addition to the presence of characteristic changes on a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG). Paramedics play an essential role in the early identification and initiation of management strategies, with Emergency Medical Services (EMS) being the first medical contact (FMC) in 74% of patients with STEMI in Victoria, Australia [5]. Previous evidence demonstrates that elements of prehospital care, including aspirin administration [6], rapid 12-lead ECG acquisition [7–9], hospital notification [10–12] and transport to a PCI facility [13] are associated with improved patient outcomes. In this study, these elements were combined into a single metric, referred to as the prehospital Bundle of Care (BOC).

STEMI management focuses on reperfusion, achieved either through direct transport to a hospital with Primary Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (pPCI) [10], or administration of PHT [14–17]. Rapid reperfusion is important, and is associated with reduced infarct size, mortality, and morbidity [18–20]. While pPCI is preferred due to its effectiveness and lower risk profile [21], where it cannot be performed rapidly, intravenous fibrinolysis followed by transfer to a PCI facility for ongoing management is recommended [22, 23]. PHT was introduced in Victoria in 2014, aiming to reduce reperfusion delays for rural STEMI patients without timely access to pPCI [16].

Advancing prehospital STEMI care is essential and requires a robust quality improvement process and assessment of core quality measures, such as the BOC, to highlight system strengths and identify gaps in patient care. In STEMI, delays or inconsistencies in early care can lead to missed opportunities for timely reperfusion, widen regional disparities, and undermine broader system efficiencies. Measuring and addressing these gaps is central to improving outcomes at both patient and system levels.

This study aimed to describe the statewide temporal changes in epidemiology, clinical management and prehospital BOC compliance for paramedic-suspected STEMI patients in Victoria, Australia, between 2018 and 2023.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of a statewide clinical registry of prehospital suspected STEMI patients who were treated by paramedics in Victoria, Australia, between January 2018 and December 2023. This study was approved by the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (Project ID: 46739).

Setting

Victoria, Australia, has a population of ~ 7 million people over 227,000 km2, with the capital, Melbourne, home to over 5.3 million. AV is the sole EMS provider in Victoria, attending to more than 1 million patients annually. Triple Zero (000) calls are centrally triaged, with clinical details and location determined, and emergency ambulance(s) dispatched based on proximity and patient presentation.

AV operates a two-tiered system comprising Advanced Life Support (ALS) and Mobile Intensive Care Ambulance (MICA) paramedics. ALS paramedics have completed a 3 or 4 year undergraduate degree and are trained in numerous procedures, including intravenous cannula insertion, medication administration, cardiac arrest management, and 12-lead ECG interpretation. MICA paramedics have additional skills, including endotracheal intubation, vasoactive medications, advanced ECG interpretation, and greater patient management autonomy. MICA paramedics have practiced at an ALS level for at least two years prior to completing a Graduate Diploma and internship program.

STEMI identification in Victoria is based on clinical assessment and the presence of standardised ECG criteria: ≥ 2.5 mm ST elevation in V2-3 leads V2-3 in men < 40 years of age (≥ 2 mm if ≥ 40 years), or ≤ 1.5 mm ST elevation in V2-3 in women or ≥ 1 mm in other leads [1], or new-onset left bundle branch block [24]. ALS paramedics are supported by the computerised interpretation of the 12-lead ECG (Zoll X series monitor-defibrillator, Zoll Medical Corporation, Chelmsford, Massachusetts, United States) when diagnostic uncertainty exists. MICA paramedics may override the computerised interpretation and make independent diagnostic decisions. Both ALS and MICA paramedics can transmit ECGs to the receiving hospital and can seek remote decision support via a senior paramedic or cardiologist if the diagnosis is unclear or for assistance with clinical decision making.

STEMI management follows AV clinical practice guidelines [25]. Guidelines recommend that patients within 60 min of pPCI facilities are directly transferred with ECG transmission and hospital notification. There are 17 hospitals throughout Victoria where AV transports STEMI patients for pPCI (13 public and 4 private).

If timely pPCI is not feasible and the patient is eligible, PHT is considered for patients with STEMI. ALS paramedics administer PHT following consultation with a senior AV paramedic or an on-call cardiologist, whereas MICA paramedics can administer PHT independently. The PHT pathway consists of intravenous tenecteplase administration, followed by rapid transfer to a PCI hospital, including air medical services if the transport time is prolonged.

All patients with STEMI received aspirin and heparin (unless contraindicated), hospital notification, and ECG transmission. Nitrates, opioid analgesia, haemodynamic support, and oxygen therapy are provided as required. Due to the potential for clinical deterioration, the standard practice is that when available, MICA paramedics transport all STEMI patients.

Participants

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were aged ≥ 18 years and attended by AV with paramedic-suspected STEMI with details entered into the Victorian Ambulance STEMI Quality Initiative (VASQI) registry [4, 26]. We included patients with documented evidence of ST elevation using standardised criteria on the prehospital 12-lead ECG, where paramedics have provisionally diagnosed STEMI in the patient care record and provided treatment in accordance with ACS guidelines. Cases were excluded if ST elevation was first identified after arrival at the hospital or if the case was a secondary interfacility transfer unless tenecteplase was administered by paramedics.

Variables

The primary outcome was paramedic compliance with the STEMI BOC, which required all components to be achieved.

Secondary outcomes focused on temporal changes among broader areas of clinical performance, including FMC to ECG, FMC to PHT, heparin administration, effective pain relief, and oxygen administration when indicated. Adverse events, such as death during AV care, were also described.

The analysed data included several time and management variables. The STEMI BOC is an established AV clinical quality metric, consistent with contemporary Australian guidelines [8] and comprises four management elements: (i) aspirin administration, (ii) FMC to ECG ≤ 10 min, (iii) hospital notification, and (iv) transport to a PCI hospital. Response interval was defined as the time from the 000 call to the arrival of the first crew at the scene. FMC to ECG was defined as the time from the first patient contact to the time of ECG acquisition and FMC to PHT was defined as FMC to time of tenecteplase administration. Effective pain relief was defined as a reduction in pain score of ≥ 2/10 among patients presenting with severe pain (≥ 8/10). English difficulty was identified via a binary checkbox field in the AV electronic patient care record (ePCR), recorded at the discretion of the attending paramedic. Appropriate oxygen administration was defined as oxygen administration in the setting of sustained SpO2 < 92% (excluding known chronic obstructive pulmonary disease). Lights and Sirens transport refers to the highest-priority emergency transport, involving expedited travel and the use of traffic law exemptions to minimise transport time.

Data sources

The VASQI is a statewide clinical registry that captures all paramedic-suspected STEMI cases in Victoria. Data were extracted from both written and ePCR using an automated search algorithm, with potential cases manually verified by trained registry staff against predefined eligibility criteria. Eligible cases were entered into the registry using standardised definitions. VASQI records patient-, treatment-, and system-level variables, including demographic characteristics, vital signs, case time intervals, medication administration, and hospital notification. Additional information was obtained from computer-aided dispatch logs, emergency call records, cardiac monitor/defibrillator downloads, and ECG tracings.

Bias

Potential sources of bias included reliance on paramedic-documented diagnoses, possible under-ascertainment of STEMI cases with atypical or subtle ECG findings, and incomplete documentation may have introduced uncertainty in interpreting aspects of care management. Potential bias was minimised through manual case verification by trained registry staff and standardised variable definitions.

Study size

As a population-level registry study, all eligible cases were included; therefore, no formal sample-size calculation was performed.

Statistical methods

The baseline characteristics and outcomes were summarised using descriptive statistics and stratified by fiscal year. Analyses were performed using complete-case data, excluding missing or implausible values for relevant variables, with no imputation performed. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as mean with standard deviations for normally distributed data or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. For the BOC, each individual component was coded as binary, with the composite calculated only for cases with complete data for all four components. Patients presenting in cardiac arrest were excluded from time-dependent and clinically non-applicable BOC component denominators. Denominators therefore varied by component according to missing, implausible, or non-applicable data.

The annual incidence of STEMI was calculated as the number of STEMI cases per calendar year divided by the mid-year Victorian adult population obtained from the Australian Bureau of Statistics [27]. Temporal changes in the incidence were assessed using simple linear regression. Temporal trends were assessed using Cuzick’s non-parametric tests for continuous and ordinal variables, and Cochran-Armitage for categorical variables. All p for trend values represents tests for temporal trend across study years based on these methods. Comparisons between the baseline (2018) and final (2023) years were performed using two-sample tests of proportions, for which the mean difference (MD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. No sensitivity analyses were performed.

Stata version 18 (Stata Corp., College Station, Texas, USA) was used for the data analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline [28] was used in the development and formatting of this paper.

After adjusting for population, the average incidence was 58.2 cases per 100,000 adult person-years, increasing from 49.0 in 2018 to 59.5 in 2023 (p = 0.102). The cohort was 69.7% male, with a median age of 67 years, increasing modestly from 66 years in 2018 (IQR 56–76) to 68 years in 2023 (IQR 56–78). Significant temporal differences were observed in several cardiovascular risk factors, including hyperlipidaemia (+ 3.1%; p for trend = 0.002), diabetes (+ 1.0%; p for trend = 0.003), known coronary artery disease (-2.8%; p for trend = 0.031), and smoking history (+ 1.6%; p for trend = 0.001).

Several physiological variables on the initial assessment also changed over time, including higher rates of tachycardia (+ 1.9%; p for trend = 0.014), hypoxaemia (+ 5.3%; p for trend < 0.001), and proportion of patients presenting with STEMI on the initial ECG (+ 4.1%; p for trend < 0.001). There were no significant trends in the initial pain scores of ≥ 7 (39.9%) or tachycardia. The median time from symptom onset to 000 calls was 54 min (IQR 19–184), with no statistically significant trend.

Primary outcome: Bundle of Care (BOC) compliance

The primary outcome was BOC compliance (Table 2). Overall compliance was achieved in 69.1% of the patients, with an 18.7% increase from 2018 to 2023 (95% CI 16.3–21.1; p for trend < 0.001). Compliance also improved significantly in both PHT (22.7%, 95% CI 13.9–31.4; p for trend < 0.001) and non-PHT subgroups (18.4%, 95% CI 15.8–20.9; p for trend < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Time metrics

The median response interval increased from 10 min (IQR, 7–13) to 11 min (IQR, 8–17) (p for trend < 0.001), with a corresponding decrease in the proportion of response intervals ≤ 15 min (81.4% to 70.2%; p for trend < 0.001). Scene times showed a statistically significant trend (p for trend < 0.001), with minimal year-to-year variation (median 27–30 min). FMC to hospital transport times increased slightly from 50 min (IQR 40–68) to 51 min (IQR 40–69) (p for trend = 0.004).

Clinical management

The proportion of patients receiving PHT was stable over the study period (8.3% overall; p for trend = 0.889), with the proportion being treated within 30 min of FMC increasing from 20.5% to 45.5% (p for trend < 0.001). Overall, 32.9% of the eligible patients received PHT within the recommended time frame.

Heparin administration declined from 59.6% to 55.4% (p for trend = 0.004), and effective pain relief remained high and stable throughout the study period (90.7%; p for trend = 0.165). The use of opioid analgesia showed a decreasing trend from 76.1% to 71.2% in 2023 (p for trend < 0.001), while nitrate use showed an increasing trend from 34.9% to 45.4% (p for trend < 0.001). Appropriate oxygen administration remained consistently high (≥ 80%), with oxygen administration showing a small statistically significant decline from 81.4% to 79.4% (p for trend < 0.001).

Lights and sirens transport remained high and unchanged over time (96.1% to 96.2%; p for trend = 0.097). Consultation with a senior AV paramedic or cardiologist increased modestly from 9.8% to 10.8% (p for trend = 0.031).

Adverse events

Major adverse events during the prehospital period were rare. The incidence of cardiac arrest in patients not presenting in arrest increased from 2018 to 2023 (2.8%–4.9%; p for trend < 0.001), and mortality in paramedic care remained low and stable (0.9%; p for trend = 0.940). Hypotension occurred in 25.8% of patients, with no significant temporal trend (p for trend = 0.852).

Among patients who received PHT, adverse events occurred in 2.6% of cases, with no significant temporal trends (p for trend = 0.236), with most adverse events related to minor bleeding.

Discussion

In this statewide analysis of paramedic-suspected STEMI, we observed an increasing incidence and significant improvement in the prehospital management of STEMI, as indicated by increased compliance with the BOC from 2018 to 2023. While most bundle components were delivered at high rates, FMC to ECG ≤ 10 min remained the lowest-performing element but did have significant improvement. Analysis of secondary outcomes showed largely consistent clinical management practices, with evolving patterns in analgesia use, greater consultation with senior clinicians, and significant improvements in FMC to PHT times.

The incidence of suspected STEMI observed in our cohort (58.2 per 100000 adult person-years) was similar to that reported in previous Australian studies [29, 30], with comparable sex distribution and slightly higher median age [17, 23, 31, 32]. Although there were some significant temporal changes in demographics and clinical presentation, the magnitude of these shifts was modest in this study. Previous studies have found that 11% to 20% of patients ultimately diagnosed with STEMI have a non-diagnostic ECG on initial assessment [33–37]. This is consistent with our findings, with 18.4% having an initial non-diagnostic ECG, underscoring the challenge of identifying early indicators of acute coronary occlusion on the ECG. These patients may be at risk of delayed recognition and management, representing an important area for further research, such as the emerging Occlusion Myocardial Infarction (OMI) paradigm [38–40].

Compliance with the BOC has increased from 60.0% to 78.6% over the study period, reflecting a system-wide improvement. This extended to both reperfusion pathways (pPCI and PHT), demonstrating that the observed improvements were not limited to a single patient cohort. Many EMS systems worldwide have implemented STEMI bundles or protocols [41–44], but their adoption and specific components vary by region and resources. Examination of individual BOC components suggests that improvements in hospital notification were the primary driver of overall BOC gains, increasing from 80.5% in 2018 to 93.1% in 2023. This likely reflects continued paramedic education and training around the importance of hospital notification, which has previously been shown to shorten time to reperfusion [10, 12]. FMC to ECG timeliness also contributed meaningfully, improving from 77.5% to 86.2%. Aspirin administration and transport to a PCI-capable facility were near ceiling throughout the study period, leaving limited room for further improvement in these components. While definitive causal attribution is not possible from registry data alone, the role of VASQI as an active quality improvement mechanism is likely to have contributed to observed improvements. Regional performance reports are provided to paramedics and clinical managers, enabling targeted education and feedback where adherence to specific care components is suboptimal. This feedback loop represents a plausible and clinically meaningful driver of the system-wide improvements observed over the study period and could be replicated in other time critical conditions such as cardiac arrest and sepsis.

There is significant variation in prehospital aspirin administration across regions. Reported rates include 52.5% and 58% in the US and Sweden [45, 46], 86% in Qatar [41], and 38.7% in Pakistan [47]. An Australian-based study from Tasmania demonstrated 90% administration [48]. In contrast, our cohort had consistently high rates (98.6%), reflecting long-term integration into standardised paramedic practice in Victoria. In our EMS, call takers may advise patients to self-administer aspirin before the ambulance arrives, which likely results in earlier aspirin administration, but does not influence the already high rate of overall adherence.

FMC to ECG ≤ 10 min represents the greatest potential for improving BOC compliance, with prior reporting indicating that this metric can be difficult to achieve in practice [49]. There are multiple potential causes of delayed FMC to ECG timing, including atypical ACS presentations, which more commonly affect women and older patients [50], and scene-level complexity not captured in registry data. Strategies for further improvement for FMC to ECG timeliness should focus on continued education and training emphasising ECG acquisition as the first clinical priority. The target is recommended in contemporary guidelines [1, 8, 51], and available data indicate that it is achievable and represents an excellent measure of EMS quality [48, 52–54]. Hospital notification rates were also consistently high and reflected a modest but consistent improvement over earlier Victorian data, which reported an overall notification rate of 86.4% [10]. In our cohort, the rates of direct transport of patients who received PHT to a PCI-capable facility were higher than those in previous studies [53], representing the strength of the current Victorian STEMI system of care.

Clinical management trends outside the BOC were mostly stable, although some patterns evolved. Opiate use declined, nitrate use increased, and consultation with senior clinicians became more frequent, reflecting changing clinician behaviour and a shift towards shared decision-making. Heparin administration at FMC is associated with increased rates of spontaneous reperfusion [55] and decreased mortality without an increase in bleeding complications [56]. In our cohort, only 54.6% of patients received heparin with a decreasing trend over the study period, highlighting an important focus for improving the care provided for STEMI patients.

The timing of PHT remains an area for improvement, with less than one-third of eligible patients receiving PHT within 30 min of FMC, despite significant temporal improvements. Other contemporary Australian studies reported median FMC to PHT times of 36.5 min [53] and STEMI-identification to PHT of 35 min [22] and 21 min [57] respectively, but did not report the proportion treated within 30 min, limiting direct comparison with our findings. Other studies have demonstrated wide disparities in timely PHT administration, with median times ranging from 25 to 52 min, with 20–61% of patients receiving PHT within the recommended 30-minute window [48, 58–60]. Response intervals increased slightly, and on scene times remained stable. In response to the potential for prolonged on-scene times delaying reperfusion, service improvements in our EMS have focused on streamlining consultation pathways to expedite decision-making and reduce time to transport.

Adverse events were low in this cohort, reinforcing the safety of both the pPCI and PHT pathways. The number of paramedic-witnessed cardiac arrests was low and consistent with the existing STEMI literature [61–64]. The observed upward trend in cardiac arrest during EMS care warrants monitoring, although it may be explained by an aging cohort with more comorbidities rather than a declining quality of care. The number of deaths in paramedic care was also low (0.9%).

Overall, these findings highlight sustained system-level improvement in STEMI care delivery over time. The implementation of a STEMI BOC, supported by clinical governance has been shown to improve the delivery of patient care. In Qatar, for example, the introduction of a structured BOC with defined performance metrics increased compliance from 39% to 76% within 12 months [41].

Statewide, ambulance-led registries play a central role in sustaining this type of improvement. Both VASQI and the Queensland STEMI registry [57] focus on continuous monitoring and system-wide feedback to support the delivery of high-quality prehospital STEMI care.

In contrast, research in New South Wales has examined reperfusion pathways and system performance [14, 17, 22, 29, 31], but no dedicated state-wide EMS STEMI registry currently exists, although regional data are captured through the NSW Ambulance PHT database [65].

Internationally, different models exist. In Europe, the German FITT-STEMI project has demonstrated the impact of timeliness of care on patient outcomes [19, 20]. In the United States, the National EMS Information System (NEMSIS) offers a large EMS dataset for research that has been used to examine STEMI processes, including aspirin and nitrate administration [45, 66]. However, as a broad EMS surveillance tool, it lacks detailed data on the specific reperfusion strategies used. Many EMS jurisdictions also rely on hospital-based cardiac registries [67, 68], which often lack comprehensive prehospital data and may not reinforce the importance of EMS performance on clinical outcomes.

The progressive improvement in BOC compliance observed in this study is likely driven by an organisational-wide commitment to research, evaluation and system improvement through mechanisms such as the establishment of VASQI. Further improvements will require maintaining the currently high-performing elements of care while addressing those with lower compliance, most notably FMC to ECG ≤ 10 min. Targeted education reinforcing the importance of obtaining an ECG early in the patient encounter may help reduce variation. As the other components of the BOC already show consistently high adherence, improving timely ECG acquisition represents the most realistic opportunity to move the overall compliance toward 90%.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis of VASQI data, the findings rely on the accuracy and completeness of paramedic documentation, and some variables may have been inconsistently reported. Second, the registry only includes cases where STEMI was identified by paramedics, potentially underestimating the true burden of STEMI and excluding patients with unrecognised STEMI. Third, case inclusion was based on paramedic diagnosis, and some cases may have been STEMI mimics rather than true acute coronary occlusion. Hospital-linkage data to formally validate prehospital STEMI diagnosis were not available for this cohort, and establishing diagnostic confirmation rates represents an important aim of future VASQI-linked research. Also, missing data for some variables may have introduced bias. Additionally, changes to dispatch protocols and their potential contribution to temporal trends in case volume was not assessed. Finally, this study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic period (2018–2023), which overlaps with documented reductions in cardiac emergency presentations in Victoria and internationally [69, 70]. This may have influenced STEMI incidence and presentation patterns during the study period, particularly in 2020–2021, and should be considered when interpreting temporal trends.

These findings are broadly generalisable to EMS systems with comparable STEMI care models and registry infrastructure, though differences in service delivery including rural access to PCI and paramedic skillsets may limit external validity.

Conclusion

This statewide analysis demonstrated improved paramedic adherence to the STEMI BOC. However, challenges remain, particularly in improving heparin administration rates and timely aspects of management, specifically ECG acquisition and prehospital thrombolysis administration. These findings reinforce the value of EMS-specific clinical quality performance monitoring in supporting clinical and system performance, ultimately contributing to high-quality prehospital STEMI care. Future research should explore the relationship between BOC compliance and clinical outcomes to strengthen the evidence for performance monitoring.
