Bespoke Three-Dimensional Modeling for Reconstruction of a Complex Poststernotomy Chest Wall Defect
Authors: Benjamin Wadowski, Rachel Medbery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNoninfectious chest wall defects after sternotomy are most commonly related to sternal malunion but encompass a heterogenous spectrum of abnormalities that may extend beyond the sternotomy itself. An individualized approach to reconstruction is essential. Historically this has required intraoperative anatomic assessment and decision-making, which in turn may compromise operative times and technical results. We present an unusual case of costochondral-sternal dissociation after routine sternotomy that was addressed with the aid of preoperative 3-dimensional modeling and custom plate design. We demonstrate that the use of this approach resulted in a first-attempt successful reconstruction with no perioperative complications.

Noninfectious osseous complications after median sternotomy are rare and most commonly involve sternal nonunion, which occurs at a rate of ∼0.4% to 1%.1,2 Rib fractures after sternotomy are more common, occurring at a rate of up to 35%,3 but rarely result in chest wall instability or late postoperative symptoms. Sternal-costochondral dissociation after median sternotomy in the absence of nonunion is exceedingly rare. Whereas treatment of sternal nonunion is known to involve a combination of débridement, rewiring, plating, and potentially bone grafting,4 the surgical management of chest wall complications that extend beyond the sternum demands an individualized approach. Here we present a unique case of multilevel sternal-costochondral dissociation with intact sternotomy after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), managed with the aid of preoperative 3-dimensional (3-D) modeling and custom-bent titanium plating.

A 57-year-old man with coronary disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, obesity, and type 2 diabetes underwent an elective CABG ×4 (including left internal mammary artery harvest) at another hospital. His sternum was closed with 3 steel wires and 3 sternal plates with circumferential titanium bands, and his short-term recovery was reportedly uneventful. Two computed tomography scans were performed at the referring institution for chest pain at 9 weeks and at 12 months postoperatively. Both reports describe the chest wall as unremarkable.

The patient was referred ∼16 months postoperatively with a sternal clicking sensation and feeling of instability with movement of the left upper extremity. Physical examination confirmed a palpable defect along the left sternal-costochondral junction. Computed tomography revealed an intact sternotomy with dissociation of the left sternal-costochondral junctions of ribs 3 through 6, with partial dehiscence of the xiphoid process (Figures 1A-1C, Video 1). On retrospective review, these findings were present on the 12-month scan. Images were not available from the 9-week scan.

Right-sided attachments of ribs 5 and 6 (5-R, 6-R) are shown with normal anatomy. (B, C) A 3-D rendering of patient’s chest wall in multiple projections demonstrates the defect as described in A. Multiple preoperative planning sessions were held during which a 3-D model of the patient’s chest wall was created (Figure 1D), and titanium plates were modeled, sized, and custom-bent to fit the patient’s anatomy. The patient’s midline incision was reopened with creation of bilateral subcutaneous and pectoralis flaps. Bilateral cryoanalgesia was performed, and all prior hardware was removed. Complete sternotomy healing was confirmed.

Internal fixation of the left ribs 3 through 6 to the sternum was performed using the prebent plates, and gaps beneath the plates were filled with demineralized bony matrix (Figure 2A). The pectoralis flaps were advanced, and the incision was closed in layers. Operative time was 195 minutes, with minimal blood loss.

(A) Intraoperative photograph demonstrates medial fixation of prebent titanium plates and complete filling of gaps using demineralized bony matrix. (B) Postoperative day 0 roentgenogram shows configuration of the plates with clear pleural spaces.

The patient was extubated in the operating room and managed with a protocolized multimodal pain regimen and early mobilization. He was discharged on postoperative day 4 on room air, with all drains removed (Figure 2B). He has since been seen in the clinic, and his symptoms have resolved. Chest radiography was performed 3 months postoperatively and demonstrated intact plates with stable position (Figure 3).

Comment

The etiology of the chest wall complication presented in this case is unknown. The use of asymmetric sternal retractors to aid in mammary harvest has been associated with an increased incidence of rib fractures,3 but we have found no cases of late dissociation of the ribs from the gladiolus absent sternal nonunion. Known risk factors for sternal nonunion include diabetes, obesity,4 and COPD1; we can extrapolate from their presence in this patient that he may have been at higher risk for this unusual complication as well.

This case presented several technical challenges. First, the placement of screws for sternal fixation after CABG required meticulous sizing to mitigate risk of retrosternal cardiac or vascular injury. This was facilitated by the 3-D modeling: the screw height to be used at different precise locations on the sternum was able to be determined preoperatively, which obviated the need for intraoperative sizing. The width of the sternal-costochondral gap resulted in a need for relatively long titanium plates, which extended laterally deep beneath the pectoralis flap. Bending these plates to each rib intraoperatively would likely have resulted in significantly increased operative time and an inferior contour match; thus, prebent plates proved beneficial.

Finally, the space beneath and between each plate required filling to provide long-term plate stability. Previous reports have used material such as autologous bone grafts to stabilize sternal nonunion.4 In this case, given the extent and irregularity of the unfilled space, we elected to use demineralized bone matrix, which is immunologically inert, instills as a paste, and hardens over a short period of time.

The use of 3-D modeling technology for treatment of chest wall defects is gaining favor, in particular in the treatment of pectus excavatum and carinatum.5 Benefits range from enhancing understanding of each patient’s unique anatomy, as in this case, to the creation of custom, printed bioprosthetics for implantation. The use of these techniques has expanded to include management of oncologic chest wall defects as well.6 Our experience with this patient may serve as a template to further expand the implementation of this technology. By distilling complex anatomic defects into discrete mechanical problems, solutions can be devised in the clinic to optimize technical and clinical outcomes in the operating room.
