CRISPR screen reveals SOX2 as a critical regulator of CD133 and cellular stress response in glioblastoma
Authors: Neil Savage, Etienne Danis, Chirayu R Chokshi, Stefan Custers, Muhammad Vaseem Shaikh, Petar Miletic, Chitra Venugopal, Kevin R Brown, Rajeev Vibhakar, Jason Moffat, Sheila K Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma (GBM) remains a formidable challenge in clinical settings due to limited treatments available. The surface protein CD133 marks glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs), cells capable of overcoming therapeutic pressures and correlate with more aggressiveness tumor phenotypes. In this study, we employed a CRISPR-Cas9 functional screen to deconvolute CD133 dynamics in tumors. This led us to establish that SOX2 is a key player in controlling the PROM1 gene, which in turn influences how cells react to stress factors, including those induced by chemoradiation treatment. The discoveries in this study shed light on the complex web of mechanisms that control the survival and resistance of GSCs, offering promising new avenues for targeting and potentially overcoming therapy resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20183-7.Subject terms: Neural stem cells, Cancer stem cells, CNS cancer

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is an aggressive form of brain cancer resistant to current therapies, making it difficult to treat and manage effectively1. In addition to cellular heterogeneity, infiltrative growth of the tumor, and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, the dismal prognosis for patients is partly attributed to glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs) which possess the ability to self-renew and ultimately contribute to tumor growth and recurrence2–4. GSCs can be identified by the surface marker CD133, a pentaspan-transmembrane glycoprotein, which drives the chemo- and radiotherapy resistance5,6. Therefore, CD133 has been considered a promising target for the development of novel therapies for GBM7,8.

One potential approach to improving patient outcomes is to first identify specific regulators of GSCs, as targeting their regulators may provide a means to selectively eliminate the cancer stem cells9. Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR) is a powerful genome editing tool to manipulate gene function in a precise manner to identify functions in biological processes10. Genome-wide CRISPR knockout screens provide a high throughput method to systematically perturb thousands of genes individually to deconvolute complex genetic networks in disease progression11. The results of these unbiased screens can provide unprecedented insights into underlying molecular pathways to help identify new therapeutic targets in cancer12.

In the current study, we performed CRISPR screening on CD133 + GSCs to identify regulators that dictate their expression. SRY (sex determining region Y)-box 2, commonly known as SOX2 was identified as a key regulator of CD133 expression in GSCs among additional proteins of interest. SOX2 is a transcription factor extensively studied in stem cell populations13. SOX2 is a member of the SOX family of transcription factors, which play key roles in embryonic development and stem cell maintenance13. In GSCs, SOX2 contributes to self-renewal, differentiation, and cell survival14. Studies have demonstrated that SOX2 is often overexpressed in GBM, and that increased expression correlates with a more aggressive tumor phenotype and a worse prognosis for patients15.

The tumorigenic transition of neural stem cells (NSCs) into GSCs is the result of aberrant and largely unidentified biological processes. While SOX2 tightly regulates neural development and homeostasis in the healthy setting, the increased presence of SOX2 contributes to the unchecked signaling pathways that allow cancers to become more tolerant of chemoradiation16. Understanding the role of SOX2 in CD133 + GSCs and the dynamics of SOX2-CD133 interactions holds promise for uncovering novel therapeutic targets and strategies for combating GBM.

Materials and methods

Generation of primary cell lines

Primary GBM samples were acquired from patients as approved by Hamilton Health Sciences and the McMaster Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (REB# 16078). All participants were provided informed consent during the study. Tumor specimens were treatment-naïve and processed using previously published protocols17. GBMs were then cultured in Neurocult Complete (NCC) media, a defined and commercially available serum-free neural stem cell medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat#05751), supplemented with human recombinant epidermal growth factor (20ng/mL: STEMCELL Technologies, Cat#78006), basic fibroblast growth factor (10ng/mL; STEMCELL Technologies Cat#78006), heparin (2 µg/mL 0.2% Heparin Sodium Salt in PBS; STEMCELL technologies, Cat#07980), antibiotic-antimycotic (1X; Wisent, Cat# 450-115-EL). Tumorspheres derived from these cultures were expanded on polyornithine-laminin coated plates for adherent growth until sufficient populations were acquired for experimental purposes. Passaging consisted of dissociating a 10 cm dish with 1 mL of TrypLE (ThermoFisher Cat. 25200056) for 5 min at 37 °C followed by collection with PBS and centrifugation at 300 g. Neural stem cells were derived and propagated in a similar fashion, as previously published18. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Flow cytometry analysis and cell sorting

Single cell GBM suspensions were generated from adherent cell cultures by dissociating with 3 mL of TrypLE on 15 cm dishes for 5 min at 37 °C followed by collection with PBS and centrifugation at 300 g. Cells were incubated with CD133/2 anti-human, PE-conjugated antibody (Miltenyi Cat. 130-113-186) or Isotype Control Antibody, mouse IgG2b, PE (Miltenyi Cat. 130-120-698 at manufacturer’s recommended concentration for 15 min, followed by PBS washing, centrifugation (repeat as before) and resuspension. The viability dye 7AAD (1:10; Beckman Coulter, A07704) was used to exclude dead cells. Cells were sorted using Beckman Coulter MoFlo XDP Cell Sorter with all flow plots were generated using FlowJo software (FlowJo 10.10.0).

Sphere formation assay

Single cells were plated in a 96-well plate at a density of 1,000 cells/200 µL per well and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for five days. Spheres were counted manually with spheres formed being defined as clusters of three or more cells with no clearly defined border.

Cell proliferation assay

Single cells were plated in a 96-well plate at a density of 1,000 cells/200 µL per well and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for five days. 20µL of Presto Blue (ThermoFisher, Cat.A13262), a fluorescent cell metabolism indicator, was added to each well four hours prior to reading out the assy. Fluorescence was measured using a FLUOstar Omega Fluorescence 556 Microplate reader (BMG LABTECH) with excitation and emission wavelengths of 544 nm and 590 nm respectively. Readings were then analyzed using Omega analysis software. Proliferation was calculated for each well by subtracting the average RFI of blank wells from the RFI of individual wells. Mean RFI was plotted for each well being tested as a side-by-side comparison of proliferation.

CRISPR SgRNA lentivirus library generation

The TKOv3 lentivirus was produced as previously described28. Briefly, HEK293T cells were seeded at 9 × 106 cells per 15 cm plate and incubated overnight. Transfection was conducted with a mixture of psPAX2 (4.8 µg; Addgene #12260), pMDG.2 (3.2 µg; Addgene #12259), TKOv3 plasmid library (8 µg) and X-tremeGENE 9 (48 µl; Roche Cat. 6365787001) in Opti-MEM (Gibco Cat.31985070). 24 h after transfection the media was changed to DMEM with 1% BSA and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Virus-containing medium was collected 48 h after transfection, centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min and stored at − 80 °C. Viral titres were determined by titration on HAP1 cells where 24 h after infection the media was replaced with puromycin-containing medium (1 µg ml−1) and incubated for 48 h. The multiplicity of infection (MOI) was determined 72 h after infection by comparing survival of infected cells with infected unselected and uninfected selected control cells.

Pooled Genome-Wide CRISPR screen in glioblastoma stem cells

The CRISPR screen was established, where 100 × 106 cells were transduced with the TKOv3 lentivirus library at an MOI of 0.3, representing more than 400-fold coverage per sgRNA after selection with puromycin. Media was changed 24 h after infection to puromycin-containing medium (1.2 µg/mL). 72 h after transduction, 30 × 106 puromycin-selected cells were collected for genomic DNA extraction at starting timepoint, and 90 × 106 cells were evenly divided into triplicates (30 × 106 cells each, 2.5 × 106 cells per 15 cm dish). Passaging subsequently occurred every 7 days (approximately 3 cell doublings), where 30 × 106 cells in each replicate were reseeded until the 12th doubling (T12) when sorting was performed. A representative portion of the final population of cells (bulk/unsorted) was collected for sequencing prior to staining for flow cytometry followed by the top and bottom 5th -percentiles of CD133 surface expression being collected in cooled flow tubes. Collected cells were centrifuged and stored at −80 °C until genomic extraction as described below.

Genomic DNA extraction and illumina sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from experimental cell pellets using Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega Cat. A1120). Sequencing libraries were prepared by amplifying sgRNA inserts using a two-step PCR reaction using primers that include Illumina TruSeq adapters with i5 and i7 indices. Resulting libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq2500 (RRID: SCR_016383) as previously published28. Each read was completed with standard primers for dual indexing with Rapid Run V1 reagents. The first 20 cycles of sequencing were dark cycles or base additions without imaging. The actual 26 bp read begins after the dark cycles and contains two index reads, reading the i7 first, followed by i5 sequences.

Screen data processing and quality control

Reads were trimmed by extracting the flanking 20 bp after each 8 bp anchor was found in barcoding primers. A 2 bp mismatch was allowed for the anchor search. After trimming, a quality-control alignment was performed using Bowtie v.0.12.8 (allowing for a maximum of 2 bp mismatches) and sgRNAs were quantified. Read counts for all samples in the screen were combined into a matrix (the percentage of recovered sgRNAs in each sample is provided below; recovered is defined as ≥ 1 raw read) and normalized by dividing each read count by the sum of all of the read counts in the sample and then multiplying by the expected read number (10 million). Fold change was calculated to a reference sample (T12 unsorted). Calculated fold changes were then used to generate normalized Z scores using drugZ (v.1.1.0.2). A gene was determined to be a hit if at least two of the four gRNAs were significantly enriched or depleted (FDR < 0.05) in either the CD133-high vs. CD133-low experimental arms.

Generation of validation knockout vectors

Individual lentiviral sgRNA constructs for knockout validations were generated as follows: Single-stranded sgRNA oligos (sequences provided below) were annealed using T4 polynucleotide kinase in T4 ligation buffer (NEB, Cat.B0202S) and ligated into BsmBI digested (NEB, Cat.R0580), phosphatase-treated (NEB, Cat.M0289S) and gel-purified modified pLCKO backbones using T4 DNA ligase (NEB, Cat.M0202T). All plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing and the virus was prepared as described above. GSCs were transduced with targeted or control lentiviruses by exposure for 24 h in suspension, followed by plating cells onto laminin-coated dished for an additional 24 h. 48 h after transduction, the used media was replaced with fresh NCC media containing puromycin to eliminate all uninfected cells for 72 h, at which point validation experiments were initiated.

Western blotting

Cells pellets were lysed in RIPA buffer (50mM Tris HCl pH 8.0, 150mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS) containing HALT™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (ThermoFisher Cat.78440) and placed on an active nutator in 4 °C for 1 h. Once complete, centrifugation took place at 14,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method (BioRad). 30 µg total protein was loaded in all lanes, and proteins were resolved on 4%–12% Novex Bis-Tris gradient gels (Invitrogen) at 100 V for 60 min and transferred to Immobilon-P PVDF membranes (Millipore) at 200 mA for 120 min. Membranes were blocked in TBS-T containing 5% BSA for 30 min at room temperature followed by the addition of primary antibodies to be incubated at 4 °C on a rocker at slow speeds. The following morning, membranes were washed repeatedly with TBS-T followed by incubation with secondary antibodies in TBS-T containing 5% BSA for 60 min. After repeated washes in PVDF membranes were imaged.

Antibodies were purchased and used at the following concentrations: CD13319 (Abcam, 19898, 1:5000), KEAP120 (ThermoFisher, 10503-2-AP, 1:2000), TADA121 (Santa Cruz, Anti-STAF42 sc-398787, 1:2000), SIAH122 (Novus Biologics, NBP2-20356, 1:1000), SOX223 (Cell Signaling Technology, D9B8N 23064, 1:5000), Beta-tubulin24 (ThermoFisher, MA5-16308, 1:5000), GAPDH25 (Abcam, ab8245, 1:5000), Beta-Actin (Cell Signaling Technologies, 13E5 Cat.4970, 1:5000), anti-Mouse IgG Secondary (LICOR, 926-68070, 1:10,000), anti-Rabbit IgG Secondary (LICOR, 926-32211, 1:10,000), Santa Cruz (m-IgG Fc BP-HRP sc-525409, 1:10,000). Cropped images were utilized to conserve space in Figures and improve contrast where necessary. Uncropped images of all blots are included in Supplementary. Protein levels were quantified prior to cropping.

Online databases

To incorporate publicly available data sets, the GEPIA2 interface was utilized to compare correlations of individual genes, or genome-wide gene expression profiles between GBM specimens of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and normal brain tissue of the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) portal as previously described26.

CUT&RUN

Patient-derived lines were acquired and propagated as previously described. CUT&RUN was performed using a magnetic bead-based protocol using the CUTANA Kit (EpiCypher Cat.14-1048) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The magnet was placed on ice for all steps. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (300 g for 3 min at room temperature in a swinging bucket rotor) and washed in ice cold Wash Buffer. One million cells were added to low-binding PCR tubes containing activated beads (in triplicate) for 10 min at room temperature. Cell Permeabilization Buffer consisted of Wash Buffer containing 0.01% Digitonin. Antibody Buffer consisted of Cell Permeabilization Buffer containing 0.5 M EDTA. 0.5ug of each antibody (SOX2-test Cell Signaling (D9B8N) Rabbit mAb #23064), H3K4me4-positive control EpiCypher 13–0057 Rabbit Monoclonal (2088-1F4), and IgG-negative control EpiCypher 13–0042 Rabbit Polyclonal) was added to respective tubes and placed on a nutator overnight at 4 °C for incubation. Extracted DNA fragments were quantified using a QubitTM fluorometer and stored at −20 °C until library preparation was initiated.

Library preparation, sequencing and analysis

Library preps were conducted using NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing libraries were prepared by amplifying DNA fragments using a 2-step PCR using primers as follows: R1: 5’-[insert]-AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCAC.−3’ and R2: 5’-[insert]-AGATCGGAAGAGCGTCGTGTAGGGAAAGAGTGT.−3’. The resulting libraries were subsequently sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq2500 (RRID: SCR_016383). Analysis was conducted using previously published pipelines36 and analysis code is publicly available at https://github.com/etiennedanis/Scripts_for_SLURM.

Results

Identifying regulators of CD133 surface expression in GSCs

Herein, we define CD133 + populations as cells with the detectable AC133 epitope27. Patient-derived GSC lines established in our lab (Supplementary Table 1) were assessed for CD133 surface expression using flow cytometry with three of the highest expressing lines (BT935, BT954 and MBT103) being expanded for functional assays (Fig. 1A). Each cell line was then sorted into CD133 + and CD133- subpopulations to determine the ability of each subpopulation to self-renew, or form secondary spheres (Fig. 1B, left) and proliferate (Fig. 1B, right). In all patient samples, the CD133 + population exhibited greater self-renewal capacity (p < 0.001) and proliferation (p < 0.0001) compared to the CD133- cells. As BT935 displayed the highest surface expression of CD133 it was expanded to conduct a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 dropout screen using the Toronto Knockout Library version 3 (TKOv3)28 (Fig. 1C). TKOv3 is designed to include four sgRNA per gene for approximately 18,000 protein-encoding genes of the human genome plus 142 genes as controls (ex. EGFP, LacZ). At the end of the screen, sorting populations using flow cytometry into the top and bottom 5th percentiles of CD133 MFI surface expression resulted in approximately 4–5 million cells being collected per group for analysis.

After successful alignment of reads, all genes deemed essential in BAGEL29 and/or PanCancer30 were quantified to ensure their lower abundance across the CD133-high, CD133-low and unsorted arms confirmed the success of the screen (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). This was determined with a high level of confidence for precision and recall (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Correlation among screen arms was quantified (Supplementary Fig. 1 C) with the greatest discrepancy being between the CD133-high vs. CD133-low populations (R = 0.64). DrugZ analysis was used to rank genes according to genotype-phenotype interactions and conclusively ranked PROM1 as the most direct regulator of CD133 surface expression as expected since PROM1 encodes the CD133 protein (Supplementary Fig. 1D).

Comparing the CD133-high population to the CD133-low population, twelve genes (PROM1, KEAP1, TADA1, TADA2B, FBXW7, SIAH1, SOX2, NF2, RBL1, MEN1, NDUFB11, APBB1) met a stringent FDR < 0.05 cutoff for positive interactions with relation to the CD133 surface expression (blue), while one gene (CUX1) was detected for negative interactions (yellow) (Fig. 1D). To validate our dataset further, we performed pathway enrichment analysis31 using all identified hits and found several morphogenic processes including netrin-activated signalling, glial differentiation, kidney epithelial differentiation and developmental cell division (Fig. 1E). Most of the genes identified in our screen are involved in general transcriptional regulation and SOX2, a well-defined transcription factor in GBM, already being described to be correlatively connected to CD133 expression in GSCs16. Of the twelve genes determined to be positive regulators of CD133, we investigated the top seven candidates using SOX2 as a rational cut-off because of its high expression and well-known role in GSCs (Supplementary Fig. 1E)3,16.

Validating SOX2 as a regulator of stemness and CD133 levels in GSCs

Two sgRNA sequences per gene and one AAVS1 control were taken from the TKOv3 library and individually ligated into plasmid backbones to validate their roles in regulating CD133 and stemness. CUX1 was also included because it was the only gene determined to be a negative regulator. After individual gene knockouts, the total proportion of CD133 + cells in GSC populations was measured in biological triplicate (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 2 A) along with the surface abundance (mean fluorescence intensity, MFI) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 2B). PROM1 knockouts displayed the greatest reduction in both phenotypes, followed by SOX2 knockouts as opposed to other genes ranked higher in the DrugZ ranking. This trend was consistent among all examined GSC lines.

Next, the effect of each gene knockout over whole cell CD133 protein levels was investigated in BT935 by western blotting (Fig. 2C left panel, Supplementary Fig. 7) and quantified by normalization to AAVS1 levels (Fig. 2C right panel, Supplementary Table 2). Knocking out PROM1 functionally led to the greatest decrease in total CD133 protein using either PROM1 sgRNA with SOX2 sgRNA also displaying significant reduction of CD133. Effects were consistent within other GSC lines BT954 and MBT103 (Supplementary Fig. 2 C, Supplementary Figs. 8–9, Supplementary Tables 3–4) suggesting further examination.

Finally, standard in vitro stem cell assays were conducted in all GSC lines to investigate the role of each gene relating to stemness. This was done by measuring the ability of GSCs to self-renew (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. 2D) and proliferate (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 2E). The effect of each gene knockout affecting experimental readouts varied among cell lines while target specificity was confirmed using commercial antibodies when possible (Supplementary Figs. 3,11). The greatest impairment in stemness and proliferative capacity were observed with SOX2, TADA1 and TADA2B knockouts across all GSC cell lines. KEAP1 knockouts exhibited reduced self-renewal and proliferation in BT935 and BT954 (p < 0.05) while similar effects were not found in MBT103. SIAH1 and FBXW7 knockouts showed significant ablation of self-renewal in BT935 and BT954 (p < 0.05) but not MBT103 despite marked reductions in proliferative capacities.

Mapping SOX2 binding sites in GSCs reveal regulatory networks

The overlapping biology of healthy NSCs with GSCs is of great interest in brain tumor research. SOX2 and CD133 are both markers for NSCs32–34. Our screen was conducted in the context of stem cell-enriching media without therapeutic pressures applied. Given the prominence of SOX2 as a top hit, we hypothesized that it may act upstream of a transcriptional program influencing GSC identity and stress adaptation, which prompted chromatin and transcriptomic analyses. Using publicly available data sets, expression levels of SOX2 were compared between GBM specimens of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and normal brain tissue of the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) portal as previously described26 where SOX2 showed higher expression in GBM relative to normal brain tissue (Fig. 3A). We next validated reduced CD133 protein as a direct result of SOX2 being knocked out across multiple cell lines (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 10).

The primary antibody used for western blotting is also compatible for CUT&RUN (i.e. Cleavage Under Target and Release Using Nuclease) in GSCs as well as NSCs. An improvement of CUT&RUN over ChIP-seq is that the global chromatin profiling provides higher resolution with a much higher dynamic range of useable DNA input35,36. Because the antibody is allowed to bind SOX2 in its native state without sheering the chromatin, targeted DNA sequences are identifiable with much less background noise (Fig. 3C). We decided to first determine genome-wide interactions of SOX2 in GSCs (Figs. 3D, E). As a positive control for our CUT&RUN, we used the H3K4me3 histone mark, which is found at developmentally regulated gene promoters36,37 and is commonly used as a positive control for CUT&RUN. Non-specific immunoglobins (IgG) was used as the negative control. Not surprisingly, we observed H3K4me3 in the promoter of PROM1 of all three GSC lines tested (Fig. 3F). SOX2 binding was also detected at proximity of PROM1 gene in the promoter and in the first intron (Fig. 3F). Interestingly, after normalization based on the total number of reads, the cell line with the highest expression of CD133 (PROM1) was also the cell line exhibiting the highest SOX2 binding activity indicating that either SOX2 is more present and stabilized at proximity of PROM1 or that more cells in the BT935 cell population have SOX2 binding at PROM1 gene proximity.

SOX2 preferentially binds stress response genes in GSCs compared to NSCs

We were then interested in looking at SOX2 binding in normal neural stem cells (NSCs). We performed SOX2, H3K4me3 and IgG CUT&RUN in NSC197 cells, a cell line generated from embryonic brain tissue as previously described18. We confirmed the presence of SOX2 binding at proximity of the PROM1 gene in NSCs (Supplementary Fig. 4 A) confirming that SOX2 directly interacts with PROM1 (CD133) in NSCs as well but with a differential SOX2 binding pattern, including additional peaks. Other genomic locations presented a stronger SOX2 binding in GSCs while very little SOX2 binding in NSCs, namely CDKN2D and TET2 (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Unfortunately, our CUT&RUN experiment performed using NSCs presented significantly less H3K4me3 peaks throughout the genomes and lower H3K4me3 signal at housekeeping genes looked at suggesting CUT&RUN conditions between our samples were not the same. We also observed a decrease in the number of SOX2 binding sites in NSC197 cells compared to GSC cell lines. This prevented us from conducting a direct differential binding analysis.

To characterize this potential dysregulation, we ranked the genes associated with SOX2 binding at their proximity based on their peak score (int(−10*log10(qvalue))) obtained after peak calling with MACS238. We then selected the genes with the highest levels of SOX2 binding (top 3,000 genes) compared to negative control for each cell line and with these gene lists, we ran a pathway enrichment analysis using Metascape39 (Supplementary Figs. 5&6). The top 3,000 peaks were selected to maximize the pathway enrichment analysis because Metascape has an upper limit of 3,000 genes to get significant pathway enrichment results. We then selected the top 50 pathways enriched in the genes regulated by SOX2 in each cell line and compared the pathways between the different cell lines. Pathways in common among GSCs and NSCs were involved in cell cycling and mitosis, as well as cell signalling through the Rho GTPase family (Fig. 4A). Among pathways identified in NSCs while absent in GSCs, several morphogenic and developmental pathways in the nervous system were detected (Fig. 4B). Genes of interest included ANO1, PCDHAC2 and GLI3 and the respective IGV profiles are provided (Supplementary Fig. 4 C).

Pathways of interest identified as unique to GSC lines included cellular responses to stress as well as stemness, namely negative regulation of cell differentiation (Fig. 4C). Looking at the IGV profiles of genes involved in the cellular response to stress (BRCA2, CDKN2D, BCL2L1, FOS, SESN2, TXNIP and NES), we found a much stronger SOX2 binding signal at proximity of these genes in GSCs compared to NSCs (Supplementary Fig. 4D). This cellular response to stress pathway may enhance the ability of cells to cope with various factors such as hypoxia, metabolic stress, DNA damage) prevalent in the tumor microenvironment. To validate this, a subset of genes involved in cellular responses to stress were investigated (FOS, SESN2, TXNIP and NES) for upregulation in GSCs compared to NSCs and indeed were found to be overexpressed (Fig. 4D). Together these findings underscore the different roles of SOX2 in promoting GBM survival and resistance through stress response pathways compared to in NSCs being associated with developmental and morphogenic pathways.

Discussion

Our study aimed to better understand the transcriptional regulation of GSC markers, which may inform future therapeutic strategies. Because the cell surface glycoprotein CD133 identifies treatment-resistant GSCs capable of seeding tumor recurrence, we examined the molecular drivers of this phenotype. We first examined its variable expression among patients in primary GSC lines without exposure to chemoradiation treatments. Each respective CD133 + GSC population exhibited a higher capacity for self-renewal and proliferation compared to their CD133- counterparts. Using our highest CD133 expressing GSC line we conducted a genome-wide CRISPR knockout screen to examine regulators of CD133 and stemness. Our screen yielded results with known stem cell regulatory genes (ex. SOX2) emerging as top hits, as well as previously understudied genes (ex. TADA1 and TADA2B).

Of our top candidate genes, the transcription factor SOX2 showed a direct interaction with PROM1, encoding CD133 protein. Total CD133 protein levels and cell surface expression decreased dramatically in response to SOX2 knockouts and cells exhibited a less proliferative phenotype. SOX2 is a heavily investigated transcription factor that can be active throughout the genome. The increased presence of SOX2 in GBM compared to normal tissue suggested the possibility of heightened SOX2 activity. To dissect molecular differences between healthy NSCs and GSCs, we used Cleavage Under Targets and Release Using Nuclease (CUT&RUN) workflows. This approach revealed that SOX2 was not only detected in the promoter region of PROM1 (coding for CD133) but also in the first intron. Moreover, the study suggests that the location of SOX2 may vary between GBM cells and normal cells, in particular for the regulation of stress response genes. This suggests a context-dependent function of SOX2 in GSCs. These differences in SOX2 gene regulation may influence how cells respond to stress factors, including those induced by chemoradiation treatment.

Previous research has demonstrated SOX2 promotes tumorigenesis by upregulating genes associated with stemness in cells14 and while it was shown SOX2 is broadly active in CD133 + cells, it was proposed SOX2 is a downstream target of CD133 signalling16. Conversely, we found SOX2 to be upstream of PROM1 leading to transcription regulation. Our data supports a direct regulatory relationship between SOX2 and PROM1 in GBM, consistent with and extending prior findings. It may also be possible that CD133 signalling in turn regulates SOX2 expression since a circulatory signalling cascade may be influential for the maintenance of GSC populations. CD133 expression is known to be dynamic and heterogeneous within GSC cultures, and its absence does not necessarily indicate terminal differentiation. The persistence of CD133- cells in this screen could reflect either plasticity within the GSC population or the presence of a parallel stem-like subpopulation with alternative markers (ex. CD44)40. The BT935 line used in the screen has high basal CD133 expression, which may reduce dynamic range for identifying negative regulators. Additionally, CD133 upregulation may involve more transcriptional activators than repressors in this context. Future screens using low CD133 GSC lines may better capture negative regulators.

Together these findings highlight the utility of complementary genomic approaches, such as CRISPR screens and CUT&RUN, in addressing the complexities of cell biology. The interdisciplinary nature of this study provides a valuable resource for the scientific community to further investigate novel biology in rare cell populations, namely healthy neural stem cells and glioblastoma stem cells.
