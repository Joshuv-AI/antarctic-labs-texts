Advances in understanding mitophagy's role in lung injury
Authors: Wenyu Du, Youjing Yang, Tao Zhang, Junyu Jiang, Dingyuan Du, Guangbin Huang, Shasha Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitophagy has emerged as a key regulator in lung injury, presenting new avenues for therapeutic intervention. Lung injury, often caused by infections, trauma, or inhalation of toxic gases, disrupts lung tissue integrity and function, frequently leading to pulmonary fibrosis. Mitophagy serves a dual purpose: it removes damaged mitochondria, reducing oxidative stress and preventing cell death, thereby offering protection in acute lung injury. However, excessive mitophagy can deplete mitochondria, impair energy metabolism, and aggravate tissue damage. Throughout the progression of lung injury, mitophagy finely tunes inflammation, immune responses, and cell survival, helping to modulate cytokine storms and delay fibrosis. Additionally, it influences metabolic reprogramming and intercellular communication, affecting critical cell types such as alveolar epithelial cells, macrophages, and fibroblasts, which are essential for tissue repair and regeneration. Although the precise molecular mechanisms remain under investigation, mitophagy is increasingly recognized as a promising therapeutic target for lung injury and fibrosis. The future challenge lies in achieving a precise balance in the regulation of mitophagy to maximize its protective effects while minimizing potential harm, thereby opening new pathways for innovative therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Lung injury is a pathological condition resulting from the structural damage and functional impairment of lung tissue, caused by both direct (intrapulmonary) and indirect (extrapulmonary) factors such as severe infection, trauma, or inhalation of toxic gases. As one of the most prevalent diseases globally, lung injury imposes a substantial health burden annually. A key pathological feature of lung injury is the loss of function in pulmonary capillary endothelial cells and alveolar epithelial cells, which often presents clinically as severe hypoxemia, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema, and respiratory distress. Lung injury is generally categorized as acute or chronic. Acute lung injury (ALI) is often caused by direct or indirect external trauma, such as damage to the skin, ribs, or chest muscles, while chronic lung injury typically results from long-term exposure to risk factors such as smoking, viral infections, or malignancies.

As ALI progresses, persistent inflammation and related processes may trigger aberrant tissue repair, leading to fibrotic lesions and progression to pulmonary fibrosis, a chronic interstitial lung disease. It is widely recognized that dysregulated pulmonary or systemic inflammatory responses constitute the primary pathogenic mechanism of lung injury. Therefore, the most effective therapeutic approach remains clinical intervention to mitigate inflammation, combined with mechanical ventilation support.

Mitochondria function not only as the cell's energy powerhouse and metabolic center but also as central hubs that regulate immune and inflammatory responses. The stability of mitochondrial quantity and quality is essential for proper cellular function. Under normal physiological conditions, mitochondria maintain homeostasis through continuous cycles of fission and fusion of their inner and outer membranes. However, upon exposure to external harmful stimuli, the balance between fission and fusion is disrupted, leading to mitochondrial structural alterations and dysfunction, which can trigger various diseases.1 Mitophagy, the selective degradation of dysfunctional mitochondria, plays a critical role in preventing the accumulation of abnormal mitochondria that generate excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can impair endothelial function, disrupt the vascular-epithelial barrier, and exacerbate lung injury. In addition to maintaining mitochondrial quality and homeostasis, mitophagy acts as a self-protective mechanism to prevent cellular damage and death.2,3 As such, it represents a potential therapeutic target for regulating the course of lung injury. Thus, understanding the regulatory role of mitophagy in the progression of lung injury is of paramount importance. This review explores the mechanisms underlying mitophagy, its role in the transition from ALI to pulmonary fibrosis, its functions and intercellular communication in lung cells, and potential therapeutic targets.

Mitophagy

Mitochondria are essential double-membraned organelles present in most eukaryotic cells, serving as the primary site for cellular aerobic respiration. Through oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondria generate adenosine triphosphate, which powers numerous physiological processes critical for maintaining cellular function and integrity, making them indispensable for cellular survival and metabolism. Beyond their role in energy production, mitochondria also participate in cell differentiation, lipid synthesis, and signal transduction, while regulating processes such as cell death, calcium homeostasis, and iron balance.4 As highly dynamic organelles, mitochondria continuously undergo fission and fusion in response to changes in the organism, organ systems, and intracellular environments—a process termed mitochondrial dynamics.5 Mitochondrial fusion is regulated by mitofusin (MFN)-1 and MFN2, located on the outer mitochondrial membrane, along with optic atrophy 1 on the inner membrane,6 while dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) mediates mitochondrial fission.7 Factors such as calcium concentration, adenosine triphosphate levels, ROS, membrane potential, and DNA mutations are tightly associated with mitochondrial dynamics. Given the lack of histone protection for mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), mitochondria are particularly susceptible to damage from factors such as increased ROS, inflammation, aging, nutrient deprivation, and ischemia-hypoxia, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction. Upon damage, mitochondria employ multiple mechanisms to restore their structural and functional homeostasis, including mitophagy, mitochondrial biogenesis, mitochondrial dynamics, and proteolysis.

Mitophagy is a critical mechanism that maintains cellular function and homeostasis by eliminating senescent or damaged mitochondria and promoting mitochondrial biogenesis. In response to external stimuli, mitochondria undergo asymmetric fission, yielding 2 daughter mitochondria with different membrane potentials: one becomes depolarized, while the other remains fully polarized. The depolarized mitochondrion subsequently triggers mitophagy. The activation of proteins such as phosphatase and tensin homolog-induced kinase 1 (PINK1) and Parkin facilitates the selective encapsulation of defective mitochondria by autophagosomes, which then fuse with lysosomes to degrade the dysfunctional mitochondria.

In 2008, Narendra et al.8 discovered that Parkin can be recruited to depolarized mitochondria, thereby promoting mitophagy. Since this landmark study, research on mitophagy has rapidly advanced, leading to the identification of multiple mitophagy pathways.

PINK1/parkin-mediated mitophagy

PINK1 is a serine/threonine kinase synthesized in the cytoplasm, and it works in synergy with Parkin, an E3 protein ligase encoded by the parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase gene, downstream of PINK1. Numerous studies have demonstrated that PINK1 and Parkin work synergistically in the mitophagy pathway.9, 10, 11 Structurally, PINK1 contains an N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence, which is recognized by the mitochondrial outer membrane translocase complex translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 22. This recognition directs PINK1 through the translocase of the outer membrane into the translocase of the inner membrane complex.12,13 Once inside, PINK1 is cleaved by mitochondrial processing peptidase and presenilin-associated rhomboid-like, generating an N-terminal truncated PINK1 that is subsequently released into the cytosol and degraded by the ubiquitin-proteasome system.3 As a result, under steady-state conditions, PINK1 expression remains low. However, when mitochondria are damaged by external stimuli, depolarization of the mitochondrial outer membrane blocks PINK1's import and degradation pathways, leading to its accumulation on the mitochondrial outer membrane. This accumulation allows PINK1 to form homodimers and to be activated through autophosphorylation at Ser228 and Ser420, thereby recruiting Parkin to the damaged mitochondrial outer membrane and amplifying the damage signal. Under physiological conditions, Parkin is widely distributed in the cytosol in an autoinhibited state. Upon mitochondrial damage, Parkin's Ser65 site is phosphorylated by PINK1, altering its conformation and triggering its E3 ligase activity, resulting in the ubiquitination of various mitochondrial outer membrane proteins such as MFN1 and MFN2.14,15 Additionally, PINK1 phosphorylates ubiquitin and polyubiquitin chains on the mitochondria, facilitating inactive Parkin's binding to phosphorylated ubiquitin, thereby indirectly activating Parkin.

Parkin “tags” aging or damaged mitochondria with ubiquitin (Ub) by attaching Ub to substrates or mitochondrial outer membrane proteins, forming ubiquitin chains. Through the ubiquitination of mitochondrial outer membrane proteins, Parkin regulates mitophagy. Ubiquitin chains act as substrates for PINK1, promoting the phosphorylation of ubiquitin by PINK1, which in turn facilitates the accumulation of Parkin on the mitochondrial outer membrane. Ubiquitin and ubiquitin chains serve as “eat-me” signals for damaged mitochondria, initiating the process of mitophagy. At the same time, Parkin amplifies mitophagy signaling through a feedforward regulation mechanism.16,17 Mitochondrial outer membrane proteins ubiquitinated by Parkin interact with autophagic receptor proteins such as sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1), nuclear dot protein 52 (NDP52), and optineurin (OPTN), all of which contain ubiquitin-binding domains. These receptors bridge ubiquitinated mitochondria to microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3), linking them to the autophagosome.15 Autophagic receptor proteins also mediate the formation of isolation membranes around damaged mitochondria, preventing further damage to other cellular components. Subsequently, the mitophagosome is delivered to lysosomes, where it matures into a mitophagolysosome and is degraded in the acidic environment.18

Non-PINK1/parkin-mediated mitophagy

In addition to the classical PINK1/Parkin pathway, other types of E3 ligases, such as Siah E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1, autocrine motility factor receptor, Ariadne RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1, and SMAD-specific E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1, also play a role in mitophagy. These ligases localize to the mitochondrial outer membrane, where they generate ubiquitin chains that recruit autophagic receptor proteins to induce mitophagy. PINK1 can also bypass Parkin by utilizing ubiquitin phosphorylation to directly recruit autophagic receptor proteins, such as OPTN and NDP52, to the mitochondria. This recruitment then facilitates the initiation of autophagy through autophagy-initiating factors, including UNC-51-like kinase 1, double FYVE domain-containing protein 1, and WD repeat domain phosphoinositide-interacting protein 1, mediating the formation and expansion of the autophagosome membrane.15 In addition, several autophagic receptor proteins located on the mitochondrial outer membrane contain LC3-interacting region motifs. These receptors can mediate mitophagy by directly binding to LC3 without requiring ubiquitination.

Although several mitophagy-related pathways have been identified, research indicates that these pathways exhibit parallelism, redundancy, and functional overlap. The relationship between lung injury and mitophagy

The lungs, as critical organs for mediating gas exchange in the human body, are highly susceptible to a variety of endogenous and exogenous stimuli due to their rich oxygen content and blood supply. Consequently, exposure to harmful factors can easily lead to ALI. As the disease progresses, alveolar epithelial cells, endothelial cells, alveolar macrophages, and fibroblasts collectively mediate inflammatory responses and reshape the immune microenvironment.19,20 This, in turn, induces abnormal tissue repair processes, ultimately leading to fibrotic foci and progression from ALI to pulmonary fibrosis. Based on these pathological characteristics, we will explore the regulatory role of mitophagy in lung injury across 3 stages: ALI, progression to fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis.

Mitophagy alleviates ALI

The pathogenesis of ALI remains unclear. Based on the histological characteristics of the lungs, the first cells affected following ALI are the capillary endothelial cells and alveolar epithelial cells. This leads to diffuse infiltration of inflammatory cells, triggering a cytokine storm. The body subsequently secretes large amounts of inflammatory mediators, ROS, and proteases, which disrupt the alveolar-capillary barrier. As a result, the alveolar space becomes filled with inflammatory exudates and cytotoxic substances, ultimately leading to diffuse pulmonary edema and the formation of hyaline membranes.

As highly sensitive organelles, mitochondria play a critical role in the progression of ALI and acute respiratory distress syndrome. The lipopolysaccharide (LPS) component of the cell walls of Gram-negative bacteria can be recognized by pattern recognition receptors on airway epithelial cells, triggering an inflammatory response, promoting oxidative stress, and causing mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby exacerbating disease progression and ultimately leading to organ failure. Therefore, the timely clearance of damaged mitochondria is crucial in the development and progression of ALI. Studies have shown that in an LPS-induced mouse model of ALI, cytoplasmic condensation of Parkin and microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3B in type II alveolar epithelial cells (AT2) increases 2 h after LPS administration, as observed via immunofluorescence staining. Further electron microscopy by Tang et al.21 revealed that 2 h after LPS administration, mitochondrial grayscale intensity increased, but significantly decreased after 24 h, indicating mitochondrial damage and the involvement of mitophagy in the ALI process. Mitochondrial damage leads to the release of mtDNA, which binds to cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and activates an immune response through its downstream adapter protein, stimulator of interferon genes (STING). Additionally, mtDNA activates the NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain-associated protein 3 (NLRP3) signaling pathway, which, in conjunction with the cGAS/STING pathway, influences cytokine production.22 Runt-related transcription factor 1 (RUNX1), a key transcription factor in hematopoietic stem cell formation, has been shown to increase in expression, along with SQSTM1 and BCL2 interacting protein 3-like, in transgenic mice expressing RUNX1 in AT2 cells following LPS administration. Meanwhile, protein levels of cGAS, STING, and NLRP3 significantly decreased, mitigating mitochondrial swelling and cristae damage. RNA sequencing of RUNX1-silenced A549 cells revealed that mitophagy-related gene expression was significantly altered, with 6 mitophagy adapter proteins consistently downregulated. These findings indicate that RUNX1 confers protection against LPS-induced lung injury by upregulating mitophagy adaptors such as SQSTM1 and BCL2 interacting protein 3-like, thereby activating the PINK1/Parkin pathway, suppressing hyperactivation of the cGAS/STING pathway, and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine release, including interferon-α, interferon-β, interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-18, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α.21 Consistently, histone deacetylase sirtuin 1, which is involved in inflammatory responses and regulates mitophagy, was reported to reverse sepsis-induced endothelial cell damage and inflammation in ALI by promoting mitophagy in pulmonary vascular endothelial cells, thereby inhibiting the mtDNA-cGAS-STING pathway and NLRP3 overactivation.23 Therefore, we can infer that mitophagy, as a protective mechanism, offers a new therapeutic option for ALI.

However, recent studies have reported that mitophagy plays a dual role in lung injury. Excessive activation of mitophagy can lead to a sharp reduction in mitochondrial numbers, subsequently decreasing the energy available to the body. Following LPS treatment, mitochondrial membrane potential decreases, initiating Parkin recruitment and increasing Drp1 expression, which enhances mitochondrial fission and PINK1/parkin-mediated mitophagy.24 Mitochondrial division inhibitor 1 has been shown to reduce apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation in LPS-treated rats by inhibiting mitophagy through the suppression of Drp1 expression.25 In such cases, enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis and maintaining the quality and quantity of mitochondria might protect lung tissue. According to a study by Luo et al.,25 sequential treatments of human mesenchymal stem cells with pioglitazone and magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (pioglitazone-Fe-human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells) promoted mitochondrial biogenesis and intercellular mitochondrial transfer, restoring mitochondrial homeostasis and preventing the progression of lung injury.26 Thus, how to regulate the balance between mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy may represent a new challenge for future research.

Mitophagy influences the transition from acute to chronic phases

As previously mentioned, the development of pulmonary fibrosis is primarily driven by uncontrolled inflammatory responses.27 Following the onset of ALI, large quantities of inflammatory cytokines are secreted, leading to the aggregation and infiltration of inflammatory cells into lung tissue. This creates a positive feedback loop that amplifies the inflammatory response, resulting in a cytokine storm and accelerating tissue damage.28 This process involves complex signaling pathways and intercellular communication, primarily affecting the nuclear factor-κB, mitogen-activated protein kinase, toll-like receptor, and Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription pathways.29 As the disease progresses, the immune microenvironment is reshaped, causing a shift from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory phenotype. This transition activates abnormal tissue repair pathways, leading to extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and the eventual development of pulmonary fibrosis. Mitophagy plays a critical role in the transition from lung injury to pulmonary fibrosis. During the ALI stage, external stimuli cause mitochondrial damage or stress, leading to excessive ROS production and inducing apoptosis.30 Previous studies have shown that the loss of PINK1 or Parkin genes increases the production of mitochondrial ROS (mtROS), which in turn activates pro-fibrotic signaling pathways, such as the transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1)/mothers against decapentaplegic homolog2/3 pathway, promoting fibroblast differentiation and the onset of pulmonary fibrosis.31 Therefore, persistent mitochondrial dysfunction and insufficient mitophagy exacerbate the inflammatory response and cell death, driving the progression of pulmonary fibrosis.

It is well known that the onset of ALI results in widespread cellular damage, with alveolar epithelial cell injury being the most representative. When type I alveolar epithelial cells (AT1) are damaged or die, tissue repair pathways are activated, inducing AT2 to function as stem cells and differentiate into AT1, promoting full repair of lung tissue. In aged mice, AT2 proliferation and differentiation are impaired, leading to abnormal immune cell activation and altered fibroblast activation signals. In contrast, in young mice, AT2 differentiation is not inhibited, and the inflamed lung tissue tends to undergo complete repair.32 This suggests that the differentiation process of AT2 cells is a key factor in the progression of ALI to pulmonary fibrosis, with age being a significant influencing factor. Consistently, epidemiological studies on idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) show that pulmonary fibrosis is more prevalent in elderly populations.33 Research indicates that during the regeneration of alveolar epithelial cells following bacterial pneumonia, downregulation of mtDNA (MICU1) in AT2 cells leads to reduced differentiation of AT2 into AT1.34 MICU1 is a key regulator of mitochondrial calcium uptake, modulating mitochondrial calcium through the mitochondrial calcium uniporter. Mitochondrial calcium uptake influences multiple processes involved in mitochondrial homeostasis, including mitochondrial respiration, mitophagy, and apoptosis. Misregulation of Ca2+ can also disrupt mitochondrial energy synthesis.35,36 Other studies have explored whether knockdown of mitochondrial pyruvate carrier 1 and MICU1 alters autophagic flux, showing that mitochondrial pyruvate carrier deficiency affects mitochondrial calcium uniporter activity through MICU1, thereby promoting autophagy signaling.37 Taken together, these findings suggest that MICU1 not only regulates AT2 cell differentiation but also has connections to mitophagy. According to research by Sanchez et al.38 the mitochondrial regulator single-minded 2s promotes breast epithelial cell differentiation via parkin-mediated mitophagy. However, it remains unclear whether mitophagy accelerates repair of alveolar epithelial cells, thereby reducing inflammatory cell infiltration and improving outcomes in pulmonary fibrosis. Nevertheless, based on these studies, we can infer that further exploration of the connections between MICU1, mitophagy, and AT2 differentiation may enhance our understanding of the relationship between mitophagy and lung injury, potentially leading to the development of novel therapeutic targets.

Mitophagy regulates the progression of pulmonary fibrosis

Following alveolar injury, excessive tissue repair and abnormal remodeling of the lungs are collectively referred to as pulmonary fibrosis. Pulmonary fibrosis is a chronic and progressive fibrotic interstitial lung disease that causes severe and irreversible lung function impairment. Among various types of pulmonary fibrosis, IPF progresses the fastest and has the highest mortality rate.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a key factor contributing to pulmonary fibrosis. Specifically, excessive production of mitochondrial ROS not only activates multiple pro-fibrotic signaling pathways but also disrupts mitochondrial-nuclear signaling, thereby affecting DNA transcription and gene expression. This, in turn, promotes the production of fibrosis-related proteins.39 As a crucial mechanism for maintaining mitochondrial function and cellular homeostasis, mitophagy holds promise as a potential therapeutic strategy for treating pulmonary fibrosis.

The effects of mitophagy on pulmonary fibrosis are heterogeneous across cell types.40 Specifically, Liu et al.41 used a co-culture model of human bronchial epithelioid cells (BEAS-2 B) and National Institutes of Health 3T3 cells (NIH-3T3) to study pulmonary fibrosis in vitro, revealing differences in the sensitivity of these cells to autophagy triggers and distinct outcomes after exposure to particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5). BEAS-2B cells exposed to PM2.5 exhibited mitochondrial damage, with Parkin's nuclear retention preventing its recruitment to mitochondria and thereby indirectly inhibiting mitophagy. In contrast, fibroblasts exhibited higher levels of autophagy, which may lead to recognition and encapsulation of PM2.5 as an exogenous stimulus, thereby further inducing abnormal fibroblast proliferation and migration. As a result, PM2.5 exposure increased the fibrotic phenotype in NIH-3T3 cells, characterized by elevated collagen deposition and increased α-actin expression.41 However, increased mitophagy in fibroblasts does not always accelerate pulmonary fibrosis. Some studies have shown that reduced mitophagy in lung fibroblasts from IPF patients leads to insufficient ROS clearance, which induces myofibroblast differentiation and the formation of fibrotic foci.42,43 For alveolar macrophages, upregulation of mitophagy during pulmonary fibrosis can reduce apoptosis. A previous study reported that adding conditioned medium from M2 macrophages to cultured human fetal lung fibroblast 1 cells, and inducing a fibrotic phenotype with bleomycin, revealed upregulation of the pro-fibrotic enzyme 8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase (OGG1) and downregulation of PINK1/Parkin, with macrophages becoming activated and polarizing into the M2 phenotype. Using the OGG1 inhibitor TH5487 or OGG1 knockout restored PINK1/Parkin expression and alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction in human fetal lung fibroblast 1 cells.44 Additionally, enhanced mitophagy in macrophages promotes the expression of macrophage-derived TGF-β1.45 Similarly, macrophage-derived TGF-β1 can induce ROS production in epithelial cells and suppress PINK1 expression in fibroblasts, inhibiting fibroblast mitophagy and increasing apoptosis.46,47 In summary, we can infer that mitophagy dysfunction in epithelial cells, macrophages, and fibroblasts promotes the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Therefore, mitophagy represents a potential therapeutic target for pulmonary fibrosis. However, uncontrolled mitophagy may accelerate cell death. Therefore, achieving precise regulation of mitophagy is critical to advancing its clinical translation.

The interplay between mitophagy and inflammatory responses

Mitophagy and immune responses engage in dynamic reciprocal regulation during tissue injury, modulating the progression of lung injury via bidirectional signaling pathways. Mitophagy serves an anti-inflammatory role by selectively eliminating damaged mitochondria, thereby preventing excessive immune activation. Mitochondrial damage results in the release of mtDNA, mtROS, and other damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and downstream inflammatory pathways, leading to excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β. By selectively degrading damaged mitochondria, mitophagy reduces cytosolic DAMP levels, thereby suppressing hyperinflammatory responses in macrophages and alveolar epithelial cells. However, excessive activation of mitophagy can disrupt immune cell metabolism and impair their effector functions; for instance, over-clearance of mitochondria in alveolar macrophages may weaken their phagocytic capacity, thereby increasing susceptibility to pulmonary infections.

Conversely, immune cells regulate mitophagy through diverse signaling mechanisms, forming a feedback loop between immunity and autophagy. Mitophagy influences immune cell metabolic reprogramming by clearing damaged mitochondria and facilitating macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Moreover, the IL-33/suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (ST2) axis activates the adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway, enhancing oxidative phosphorylation and promoting the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10. In contrast, macrophage-derived TGF-β1 inhibits mitophagy in fibroblasts, activating the platelet-derived growth factor receptor/phosphatidylinositol 3'-kinase/protein kinase B pathway and inducing myofibroblast activation, thereby driving pulmonary fibrosis progression. This process likely establishes an “inflammation–autophagy–fibrosis” positive feedback loop that exacerbates tissue injury and remodeling.

Mitophagy of different cells in lung injury

Alveolar epithelial cells

Alveolar epithelial cells are the primary components of the alveoli, with both AT1 and AT2 cells jointly forming the physical barrier of the alveolar epithelium, which is essential for maintaining the structural integrity and normal function of the lungs. Although AT1 cells are few, they cover over 90% of the alveolar surface area and facilitate gas exchange. Due to their cellular morphology, they are prone to damage and rupture. In contrast, AT2 cells secrete surfactant to reduce surface tension and prevent alveolar collapse. They also participate in pulmonary inflammatory and immune responses, differentiate into AT1 cells to promote tissue repair, and have the capacity to regenerate AT1 cells.

In recent years, research on ALI and inflammation has increasingly focused on cellular metabolism and mitophagy. Mitochondria are at the core of the cellular metabolic network, where processes such as fatty acid oxidation, amino acid metabolism, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle take place. Metabolic reprogramming influences mitophagy and the progression of various diseases. Acetyl-CoA synthetase short-chain family member 3, a key enzyme in short-chain fatty acid metabolism within epithelial cells, reduces fatty acid oxidation by downregulating the expression of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A and enhances anaerobic glycolysis. This metabolic reprogramming decreases mitochondrial fission and fusion, increases mtROS production, inhibits mitophagy, and leads to the inactivation of alveolar epithelial cells.48 Previous studies have reported a positive correlation between citrate concentration and mitochondrial dysfunction in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, revealing a potential link between citrate and mitochondrial impairment.49 Yang et al.50 demonstrated that the accumulation of mitochondrial citrate in alveolar epithelial cells in vitro led to citrate directly binding to the mitochondrial membrane protein FUN14 domain-containing protein 1, recruiting Drp1, and inducing excessive mitophagy. This triggered necroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells. Repeated injury and dysfunction of alveolar epithelial cells are pathological features of pulmonary fibrosis. Mitophagy has a protective role in these cells by reducing apoptosis, which presents a potential therapeutic target for alleviating pulmonary fibrosis.51 In lungs affected by IPF, reduced PINK1 expression in epithelial cells correlates with increased susceptibility to fibrosis. PINK1 deficiency leads to mitochondrial stress and the release of mitochondrial DNA, which activates toll-like receptor 9 and promotes the secretion of the profibrotic factor TGF-β.52 In a juvenile mouse model of PINK1 deficiency, accumulated damaged mitochondria suggest insufficient mitophagy, which increases apoptosis and mtROS production.47 Research by Yu et al.53 confirmed that thyroid hormone upregulates PINK1 expression, promotes mitochondrial biogenesis, and reduces apoptosis in alveolar epithelial cells. The antifibrotic mechanism may involve the restoration of mitochondrial function through enhanced mitophagy. These studies indicate that mitophagy acts as a protective mechanism against pulmonary fibrosis.

Research has shown that in lung fibrosis models with surfactant protein C gene mutations, such as SFTPCI73T, there is a significant decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential, leading to impaired mitophagy and consequently promoting the progression of IPF.54,55 Additionally, endoplasmic reticulum stress in AT2 cells causes a reduction in PINK1 levels, further exacerbating mitochondrial homeostasis dysregulation already present in IPF.56 These findings indicate that damaged mitophagy in AT2 cells is closely associated with lung injury and fibrosis.

The inactivation of SIRT1 and the reduction of autophagy exacerbate AT2 cell senescence induced by smoking, forming a positive feedback loop linked to mitochondrial oxidative stress, ultimately contributing to pulmonary fibrosis.57 consistently, AT1 exposed to ozone experience oxidative stress, activating the PINK1/Parkin signaling pathway, triggering mitophagy, and initiating pyroptosis via the NLRP3 inflammasome.58

It is worth noting that current research predominantly focuses on AT2 cells, with relatively few studies investigating AT1 cells. The role of AT1 cells in the mechanisms of lung injury and fibrosis remains to be further explored.

Alveolar macrophages

Alveolar macrophages play a critical role in immune surveillance and regulation, providing defense against inhaled particles and pathogens. They can directly phagocytose pathogenic microorganisms and secrete inflammatory and chemotactic factors to recruit neutrophils to clear pathogens, thereby initiating the innate immune response. Under stimulation from cytokines or metabolites released within the immune microenvironment, alveolar macrophages can adopt various phenotypes to carry out their functions effectively.59

Even in the presence of sufficient oxygen, the cellular metabolism of M1 macrophages shifts from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis in response to inflammatory stimulation. This shift promotes the activation of the pentose phosphate pathway, generating β-nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate, which in turn induces the production of reactive ROS and the synthesis of nitric oxide. Additionally, this process suppresses the TCA cycle by altering isocitrate dehydrogenase expression and by the accumulation of lipid metabolites.60 Therefore, a key metabolic feature of M1 macrophages is the disruption of the TCA cycle, leading to the accumulation of metabolites such as succinate, citrate, and itaconate. The resulting mtROS increases the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha, which subsequently activates transcription of key glycolytic genes, thereby sustaining glycolysis.61 In contrast, M2 macrophages maintain an intact TCA cycle, deriving energy through oxidative phosphorylation and expressing high levels of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma regulates lipid accumulation, promotes fatty acid oxidation, and sustains oxidative phosphorylation, providing the energy required for M2 macrophage function.62 As mentioned previously, macrophage polarization is closely linked to mitochondrial energy metabolism, such as ROS production and glycolipid metabolism. Mitochondria are thus considered key “controllers” in determining macrophage function.63

Mitochondria are crucial for macrophage function and the maintenance of lung homeostasis. When mitochondria are damaged, they release mitochondrial mtROS and mtDNA into the cytoplasm, where they act as endogenous DAMPs that activate the NLRP3 inflammasome. NLRP3 recruits apoptosis-associated speck-like protein to form an inflammasome complex, which mediates the activation of caspase-1. Activated caspase-1 then cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD), producing an N-terminal fragment (GSDMD-NT) with pore-forming activity, triggering the maturation and secretion of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18. GSDMD-NT translocates from the cytoplasm to the cell membrane, where it oligomerizes to form pore-like structures through which IL-1β and IL-18 are secreted into the extracellular space. This process initiates an inflammatory response and creates an inflammatory microenvironment, leading to macrophage pyroptosis.64, 65, 66 A study by Kuang et al.67 revealed that mitochondria containing GSDMD-NT are transferred from pyroptotic alveolar macrophages to neutrophils via microvesicles, accelerating the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps and enhancing ROS production, thereby promoting the progression of ALI.

Currently, the regulatory role of mitophagy in macrophages during disease progression remains to be further elucidated. AKT serine/threonine kinase 1, also known as protein kinase B, is a cytokine that can inhibit apoptosis. Studies have shown that AKT serine/threonine kinase 1 derived from alveolar macrophages induces the upregulation of mtROS, which mediates the occurrence of mitophagy, counteracts apoptosis, and promotes pulmonary fibrosis by increasing the expression of TGF-β1.45 In an in vitro lung injury model where macrophages were stimulated with LPS, transfection with NLRP3 lentivirus or overexpression of miR-138-5p alleviated LPS-induced macrophage pyroptosis and the resulting cytokine storm. Further investigation has revealed that this effect was achieved through mitophagy, which reduced cytosolic mtDNA levels, leading to demethylation of the miR-138-5p promoter. This upregulation of miR-138-5p expression inhibited NLRP3 activation, thereby mitigating inflammation.68 These studies demonstrate that mitophagy is a powerful mechanism for regulating macrophage pyroptosis and alleviating disease progression.

Pulmonary fibroblasts

Pulmonary fibroblasts play a crucial role in constructing and maintaining the ECM and are key cells in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Under the influence of direct or indirect factors, oxidative stress triggers chronic inflammation, leading to the death of alveolar epithelial cells. Subsequently, pulmonary fibroblasts abnormally proliferate and differentiate, secreting collagen and other ECM components, which results in ECM accumulation and tissue remodeling, thus driving the development of pulmonary fibrosis.

Aerobic glycolysis is an important metabolic process in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis, as it accelerates energy supply to the cells. The enhancement of glycolysis depends on the expression of glycolytic enzymes such as hexokinase 2 (HK2) and 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3). Inhibition of glycolysis by reducing the activity of HK2 and PFKFB3 can decrease fibroblast differentiation into myofibroblasts and alleviate pulmonary fibrosis. Transforming TGF-β promotes the activation and proliferation of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, enhancing ECM deposition while inhibiting its degradation, thus playing a central role in the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis. Glucose transporter 1 is involved in the facilitated diffusion of glucose. Upregulation of glucose transporter 1 increases glucose uptake and mediates TGF-β signaling to promote fibrosis.69, 70, 71 These studies highlight that glycolytic reprogramming is crucial for the development of pulmonary fibrosis.

Fibroblasts, as direct effector cells in pulmonary fibrosis, undergo senescence and insufficient mitophagy, contributing to their differentiation into myofibroblasts. Studies have shown that bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4), a secretory protein belonging to the transforming TGF-β superfamily, is downregulated in patients with IPF and in a bleomycin-induced mouse model. This suggests that reduced BMP4 expression is associated with fibrosis progression in IPF. In a subsequent study, using an adeno-associated virus 9-induced BMP4 overexpression model, it was confirmed that activation of the BMP4 signaling pathway can prevent fibroblast differentiation into myofibroblasts and reduce ECM production. However, silencing PINK1 weakened BMP4's ability to inhibit pulmonary fibrosis.72 This indicates that BMP4 mitigates fibroblast differentiation and alleviates fibrosis progression via PINK1-mediated mitophagy. Previously, research identified Parkin as a key regulator of mitophagy. In a mouse model of bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis, Parkin knockdown resulted in reduced mitophagy, increased ROS production, and impaired mitochondrial biogenesis. Accumulated ROS activated the platelet-derived growth factor receptor/phosphatidylinositol 3'-kinase/protein kinase B signaling pathway, promoting the differentiation and proliferation of myofibroblasts. In simple terms, low Parkin expression in IPF patients leads to insufficient mitophagy, thereby accelerating fibrosis. Proper mitophagy can inhibit excessive proliferation of ectopic fibroblasts. As reported by Wang et al.70 alanine (ALA) binds to MAS-related G-protein coupled receptor (MrgD), upregulating parkin/LC3-mediated mitophagy, reducing PFKFB3/HK2/lactate-mediated glycolysis, and inhibiting TGF-β1-induced fibroblast activation and pulmonary fibrosis. Notably, while this study proposed a novel mechanism by which mitophagy counteracts glycolytic reprogramming, the precise interplay between glycolysis and mitophagy as a therapeutic target requires further investigation.

Intercellular communication and mitophagy in lung injury

The pathogenesis of lung injury is highly complex, involving intricate intercellular communication among various cell types, including epithelial cells, endothelial cells, immune cells, and fibroblasts, which collectively regulate its progression. In the early stages of lung injury, damage to epithelial and endothelial cells primarily activates repair pathways in AT2 cells, prompting their differentiation into AT1 cells and subsequent regeneration of the alveolar epithelium.73, 74, 75, 76 The function of AT2 cells is regulated by Wnt, Notch, and Hippo/yes-associated protein signaling pathways and involves interaction with neighboring epithelial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, immune cells, as well as cytokines and growth factors that induce ECM production.75,77, 78, 79, 80 These factors promote AT2 cell proliferation and differentiation through paracrine, autocrine, and direct cell-to-cell contact signaling. Additionally, aging can impair the differentiation capacity of AT2 cells. Single-cell sequencing analysis of AT2 cells from young and aged mice revealed that the expression of AMPK pathway-related genes was significantly downregulated in the aged mice group. Serine/threonine kinase 11 (STK11) is a key upstream kinase that activates the AMPK pathway, and its specific deletion in AT2 cells impairs alveolar regeneration, demonstrating that STK11-mediated AMPK signaling is essential for AT2 differentiation.81 AMPK serves as a central sensor of cellular energy status and regulates various biological processes, including autophagy.82 It senses signals related to mitochondrial dynamics and transduces them to lysosomes, influencing autophagy flux.83 Previous studies have found that treating human kidney-2 cells pre-exposed to high glucose/high fatty acid conditions with the AMPK activator metformin induces AMPK phosphorylation, promotes PINK1 translocation to mitochondria, and enhances mitophagy, ultimately improving kidney fibrosis.84 Thus, we hypothesize that there may be multiple interactions between AMPK, mitophagy, and alveolar regeneration, which collectively regulate the repair process of lung injury.

Macrophages can be classified into alveolar macrophages, interstitial macrophages, and monocyte-derived macrophages (mo-Mac), all of which can initiate immune cascades influencing the progression of ALI. Current studies indicate that the signaling of IL-33 secreted following lung epithelial injury depends on recognition and interaction with its specific receptor, ST2. Upon binding, regulatory T cells secrete IL-13, which stimulates macrophages to release IL-10 and to activate Rac1, promoting phagocytosis.85,86 Additionally, regulatory T cells can modulate macrophage polarization, with mitophagy playing a contributory role. Lin et al.87 reported that IL-33 significantly reduces the secretion of M1 macrophage-associated cytokines C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 and TNF-α, while markedly increasing the expression of the M2 macrophage-associated cytokine C-C motif chemokine ligand 22. Mechanistic investigations revealed that IL-33 primarily regulates the expression of mitophagy-related proteins PINK1, Parkin, and LC3 via the AMPK pathway, thereby modulating the polarization phenotype of mo-Mac. Xu et al.88 emphasized that by regulating mitophagy levels, the IL-33/ST2 axis enhances oxidative phosphorylation in macrophage metabolic reprogramming, further promoting the expression of M2 polarization genes. These studies suggest that mitophagy influences the interplay between IL-33/ST2 signaling and macrophage polarization, thereby affecting the progression of lung injury. Moreover, increased mitophagy facilitates the transition of M1 macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, regulating macrophage polarization and contributing to lung injury repair. Hung et al.89 demonstrated that arsenic induces autophagy/mitophagy via LC3 II and phosphorylated Parkin protein markers. Treatment of arsenic-exposed M1 macrophages with the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine restored the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase, indicating that autophagy/mitophagy pathways induce M2 polarization of macrophages. If mitophagy levels decrease, macrophages polarize toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, exacerbating disease progression.90 In summary, mitophagy holds potential as a therapeutic strategy for lung injury, and the IL-33/ST2 pathway could represent a promising direction for future research breakthroughs.

If unresolved, persistent lung injury and immune system imbalance caused by excessive immune activation can easily lead to pulmonary fibrotic remodeling. Previous studies have shown that DAMPs released from injured alveolar epithelial cells recruit mo-Mac to the sites of injury. Macrophages release monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, and the injured epithelial cells communicate with mo-Mac via the monocyte chemoattractant protein-1/CCR2 signaling pathway, inducing their transition into a pro-fibrotic phenotype.91 During the progression of lung disease, mo-Mac exhibit dynamic changes in the expression of pro-fibrotic genes; they highly express inflammation-related genes such as TNF during the acute inflammatory phase and upregulate pro-fibrotic genes, including matrix metalloproteinase-12, during the fibrotic phase.92 Notably, macrophage-derived MMP12 plays a critical role in inducing endothelial cell dysfunction, and sustained endothelial injury forms a positive feedback loop that promotes fibrosis.93,94 Mitophagy plays a protective role in endothelial cells, serving as an essential mechanism for their response to oxidative stress and damage repair.95 Mai et al.96 emphasized that targeting the upregulation of mitophagy-related proteins, microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3B, Autophagy Related 5, and Autophagy Related 12, can improve mitochondrial membrane potential and enhance mitophagy levels in human umbilical vein endothelial cells in vitro, thereby alleviating cellular senescence.96 However, other studies have reported that the absence of endothelial cell mitochondrial uncoupling protein 2 can lead to excessive activation of PINK1-induced mitophagy, resulting in insufficient mitochondrial biogenesis and increased apoptosis of endothelial cells.97 Taken together, mitophagy may regulate endothelial Mitophagy regulatory targets for lung injury treatment

Mitochondrial dysfunction has been established as a contributing factor in various diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases, fibrosis, and tumors.98 In recent years, the role of mitochondrial autophagy at different molecular stages in the progression of lung injury has garnered increasing attention, and targeting the molecular mechanisms regulating mitochondrial autophagy is considered a potential therapeutic strategy for ALI and pulmonary fibrosis.

Damage to epithelial cell integrity caused by various pathological factors is a critical initiating event in lung injury, and protecting alveolar epithelial cells can effectively mitigate the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Pseudoprotodictyon alkaloid is a natural compound known for its anti-fibrotic properties. Further research has shown that this compound alleviates pulmonary fibrosis by upregulating PINK1/parkin-mediated mitochondrial autophagy, thereby inhibiting alveolar epithelial cell senescence and reducing fibroblast activation and collagen deposition.99 Conversely, IL-17A acts as a pro-fibrotic cytokine, with increased expression of IL-17RA observed in AT2 cells from patients with IPF. In a mouse model of bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis, IL-17A knockout mice exhibited reduced apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells, indicating that IL-17A accelerates apoptosis in AT2 cells, thereby promoting fibrosis progression. This mechanism is associated with IL-17A's inhibition of PINK1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy.100 In summary, mitochondrial autophagy plays a crucial regulatory role in the process of pulmonary fibrosis by influencing AT2 cell apoptosis and the release of pro-fibrotic factors.

Under normal conditions, mitochondria can serve as signaling platforms for various innate immune responses, including those mediated by the mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein. However, during mitochondrial damage, they can also serve as sources of DAMPs, mediating the damage response.101 Sestrin2 is a highly conserved stress-inducible protein that protects cells from damage caused by oxidative stress and hypoxia. Previous studies have reported that Sestrin2 mitigates NLRP3 activation in macrophages by promoting mitochondrial autophagy, thereby alleviating sepsis-related lung injury. Wu et al.102 found that endotoxin-induced ALI was more severe in Sestrin2-knockout mice, further demonstrating that Sestrin2 negatively regulates NLRP3 activation through PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitochondrial autophagy, thereby protecting alveolar macrophages and inhibiting ALI.

Beyond lung injury, the role of mitochondrial autophagy as a therapeutic target in other fibrotic diseases has been partially established. Research indicates that NADPH oxidase 4, a member of the β-Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase family, primarily catalyzes the formation of ROS. ALA can modulate autophagy induced by ROS generated through NADPH oxidase 4, alleviating liver fibrosis.103 This finding suggests that mitochondrial autophagy may also be a significant therapeutic target in the liver; thus, investigating whether this mechanism applies to pulmonary treatment is crucial. ALA and its receptor, MrgD, have been shown to protect against cardiac and renal fibrosis.104 Studies evaluating ALA's effects on pulmonary fibrosis demonstrate that ALA improves lung fibrosis by reducing oxidative stress and inducing autophagy. Further investigation into the ALA/MrgD axis revealed its role in alleviating ALI by modulating aerobic glycolysis and Parkin/LC3-mediated mitochondrial autophagy, thereby suppressing TGF-β1-induced fibroblast activation.105 These findings suggest that mitochondrial autophagy may serve as a therapeutic target in fibrotic diseases, potentially influencing the treatment of ALI and fibrosis. This provides a new avenue for improving clinical treatments for lung injuries.

Mitophagy-based therapeutic strategies

Future research could enhance experimental reliability by employing lung organoids or genetically humanized mouse models. It should also focus on investigating autophagy mechanisms in AT1 cells at the single-cell level and explore the molecular crosstalk between the gut–lung axis and mitophagy. These approaches will further advance drug development and the clinical application of mitophagy-based therapies. Moreover, mitophagy-targeted therapeutic strategies should involve stage-specific interventions based on disease progression and cell-type characteristics.

In terms of pharmacological treatment, during the ALI phase, therapies should be tailored to the properties of alveolar epithelial cells. For instance, acid-resistant nanocarriers can be developed to enable targeted delivery of the mitochondrial fission inhibitor mitochondrial division inhibitor 1, facilitating its release specifically in injured lung regions. This approach aims to selectively inhibit Drp1-mediated excessive mitochondrial fission, thereby reducing autophagy-associated apoptosis induced by hyperactivation of mitochondrial signaling pathways. Additionally, surfactant protein A-targeted nanocarriers could be used to achieve specific drug delivery, promoting the clearance of mtROS and interrupting the “injury–inflammation” cycle.

Furthermore, given the characteristics of alveolar macrophages, aerosolized lipid nanoparticles carrying miR-138-5p mimics could be developed. These could promote mitophagic degradation of cytosolic components, inhibit inflammasome activation and inflammatory cytokine secretion, and thereby alleviate acute inflammatory responses.

Chronic pulmonary fibrosis, a consequence of unresolved ALI, is primarily driven by fibroblast overactivation. Targeting mitochondrial matrix components with mitochondria-penetrating peptide-conjugated drugs—designed to match fibroblast properties—represents a promising precision treatment strategy. Such approaches can activate mitophagy pathways, promote the clearance of damaged mitochondria, suppress reprogramming of aerobic glycolysis, and block fibroblast differentiation. In the chronic phase, mitochondrial homeostasis may also be supported by preconditioning human mesenchymal stem cells with pioglitazone and magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, followed by infusion therapy.

Conclusion

In summary, mitochondrial autophagy serves as a quality-control mechanism that protects cellular function, mitigates inflammatory damage, and maintains mitochondrial and cellular homeostasis. Researching its role in lung injury can enhance the development of therapeutic strategies for this condition. Under varying physiological and pathological conditions, mitochondrial autophagy may yield different effects; it can protect cells by eliminating damaged mitochondria, but excessive activation may exacerbate cellular damage. Regulating mitochondrial autophagy can effectively slow the progression of lung injury and mitigate pulmonary fibrosis, although it may sometimes increase lung damage. Undoubtedly, mitochondrial autophagy has the potential to become a novel therapeutic target for lung injury. Currently, further research is needed to explore the application of mitochondrial autophagy in treating lung injury, and additional clinical trials are required to verify the safety and efficacy of identified targets that have shown significant therapeutic effects. Moreover, a deeper investigation into the mechanisms of intercellular communication during the pathogenesis and progression of lung injury will enhance our understanding of the complex immune microenvironment involved. Understanding the pathogenic mechanisms of lung injury can provide direction and evidence for the development of subsequent therapeutic agents. It is also worth considering whether the pathways regulating mitochondrial autophagy in other organ diseases can be applied to lung injury.
