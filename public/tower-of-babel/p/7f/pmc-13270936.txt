Combined catheter ablation and left atrial appendage occlusion for persistent atrial fibrillation with left atrial appendage thrombus

Introduction

Left atrial appendage (LAA) thrombus is generally regarded as a contraindication to LAA closure and catheter ablation. Conversely, for the specific subset of patients with LAA thrombus refractory to anticoagulant therapy, who are also complicated by severe heart failure and persistent atrial fibrillation (AF), implementation of these 2 interventions is often deemed clinically necessary.

Case report

We present a case of a patient who underwent successful combined LAA closure and radiofrequency catheter ablation for AF. The patient presented with complex comorbidities, including dilated cardiomyopathy, severe heart failure, and a previous cerebral infarction. Despite continuous oral anticoagulant (OAC) therapy, the LAA thrombus persisted and remained unresolved.

History of presentation

A 65-year-old man was hospitalized for recurrent episodes of palpitations and chest tightness. Electrocardiogram (ECG) and ambulatory ECG monitoring indicated persistent AF. Computed tomography angiography revealed no significant coronary artery stenosis. Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed dilated cardiomyopathy. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a left atrial diameter of 46 mm, a left ventricular diameter of 57 mm, and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 23%. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) indicated thrombus formation in the LAA. The patient was treated with oral rivaroxaban (15 mg per dose, twice daily) for anticoagulation and standard heart failure medications. After 50 days, follow-up TEE still revealed a thrombus measuring approximately 9 × 9 mm, which was located in the distal portion of the LAA (Figure 1, Supplemental Video 1).

Medical history

7 years previously, the patient underwent successful interventional thrombectomy for acute cerebral embolism. During hospitalization, he was first diagnosed as having paroxysmal AF, but he subsequently failed to adhere to long-term OAC therapy. 1 year ago, he developed heart failure symptoms related to AF.

Differential diagnosis

Based on the results of 2 TEE examinations performed before and after OAC therapy, the patient was still diagnosed as having LAA thrombus. Continuous monitoring with ambulatory ECG and heart rhythm monitoring confirmed the diagnosis of persistent AF.

Investigations

Preoperative computed tomography suggested that the LAA had multiple lobes, and large cerebral infarct lesions were present (Figure 2). TTE was used to measure the diameter of the LAA ostium. The diameters of the landing zone and the ostium were 23 and 26 mm, respectively (Figure 3). The blood N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide concentration was 2881 pg/mL.

Management

After successful induction of general anesthesia, dual cerebral protection devices (Emboshield NAV6, Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL) were deployed via bilateral femoral artery access, with placement in both internal carotid arteries (Supplemental Video 2). Under TEE guidance, transseptal puncture was performed uneventfully. Subsequent multiplanar measurements of LAA anatomic parameters informed the selection of a 28–34 mm (2–6 mm larger than the maximum measured diameter) LAmbre LAA closure device (LifeTech Scientific Corporation, Shenzhen, China), which incorporates a proprietary dual-disc design (umbrella and cover disc) to optimize anatomic conformity. The LAA closure delivery sheath with a 6F pigtail catheter was advanced to the LAA orifice under TEE guidance. The umbrella component of the LAmbre device was first released and partially deployed at the LAA orifice. Thereafter, the device and delivery sheath were pushed to the predetermined landing zone, before fully deploying the umbrella and then completing deployment of the LAmbre device’s cover. Both 2- and 3-dimensional TEE confirmed complete LAA occlusion, and the pull test was performed under radiograph and TEE guidance. After confirming its stability, the LAmbre device was fully released. Subsequent TEE also confirmed complete LAA occlusion (Figure 4, Supplemental Videos 3–4).

The process of LAA closure under TEE and radiograph guidance. A and D: The fixation disc was positioned in the landing zone. B and E: The disc successfully occluded the LAA. C and F: The pull test confirmed the stability of the occluder position. LAA = left atrial appendage; TEE = transesophageal echocardiography.

After successful atrial septal puncture, a PentaRay mapping catheter was advanced into the left atrium for 3-dimensional reconstruction via the CARTO 3 system (Biosense Webster Inc, Irvine, CA). Subsequently, a second transseptal puncture was performed, and a radiofrequency ablation catheter (SMARTTOUCH SF, Biosense Webster) was advanced into the left atrium for ablation. Ablation parameters were set as a posterior wall ablation index of 300–400, an anterior wall ablation index of ≥500, a power of 35–45 W, and a spot-to-spot distance of ≤6 mm, to ensure transmural and continuous lesion lines. Electrical isolation was first achieved for the pulmonary veins; then the left atrial posterior wall box and mitral/tricuspid isthmus line were ablated. After cardioversion, pacing confirmed bidirectional block at the isthmus lines, with electrical isolation verified for the pulmonary veins and left atrial posterior wall box. Finally, the cerebral protection devices were then removed, and no significant thromboembolism was observed. After awakening, the patient showed no new neurologic symptoms or signs.

Outcome and follow-up

3 months after the intervention, outpatient follow-up indicated a significant improvement in cardiac function (left ventricular ejection fraction 40%). TEE showed a marked reduction in left atrial diameter (40 mm) and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (50 mm). Ambulatory ECG confirmed sinus rhythm without AF recurrence.

Discussion

According to current guidelines for the management of AF1 and expert consensus on catheter ablation for AF2 and LAA closure,3 the presence of LAA thrombus is a contraindication to both catheter ablation and LAA closure. Although most cases of LAA thrombus resolve with intensive anticoagulation therapy, there are still a few patients in whom the thrombus does not resolve.4 The traditional treatment in such circumstances is surgical LAA ligation and surgical ablation. However, the patient in the present case had severe heart failure and large cerebral infarct lesions, so he was unable to tolerate open-heart surgery. Given that the thrombus was located distally within the LAA—a key factor for minimizing procedural risk—we attempted LAA closure, and the procedure proved successful. Some reported the safety and efficacy of LAA closure in patients with LAA thrombus.5,6 Thus, for patients with LAA thrombus refractory to anticoagulant therapy, LAA closure may be performed under cerebral protection as a minimally invasive alternative to surgical intervention.

The patient in the present case had persistent AF complicated by heart failure. Both current AF guidelines and expert consensus recommend catheter ablation for such patients to improve quality of life and reduce rehospitalization risk.1, 2, 3,7 Our previous research has also confirmed the safety and efficacy of the “one-stop” treatment approach combining LAA closure with catheter ablation.8 Therefore, after successful LAA closure, we performed catheter ablation for AF. The follow-up results confirmed the effectiveness and safety of this strategy.

Drawing on the favorable outcomes of this single case, we propose that performing LAA closure before catheter ablation could be a feasible and relatively safe treatment approach for patients presenting with distal LAA thrombus refractory to anticoagulant therapy.

Conclusion

For patients with AF with LAA thrombus, when standard anticoagulation therapy is ineffective, sequential LAA closure followed by catheter ablation for AF as a “one-stop” interventional treatment may represent a potentially safe and feasible therapeutic option.
