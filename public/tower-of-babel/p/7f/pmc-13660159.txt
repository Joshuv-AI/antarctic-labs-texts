Emergency department nurses’ self-reported triage decision-making abilities and associated factors: a cross-sectional study in three tertiary hospitals in Sichuan, China
Authors: Yingying Zhao, Juan Hu, Jing Zhao, Lan Wang, Qiu Jin, Humin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate emergency department (ED) nurses’ self-reported triage decision-making abilities and the associated factors, providing evidence for the development of a standardized triage training system.MethodsConvenience sampling was adopted. An online questionnaire survey was conducted among 549 ED nurses from three tertiary hospitals in Sichuan, China from May 6 to May 17, 2025. The survey covered general information about the hospitals, respondents’ demographic characteristics, and the Chinese version of the Triage Decision-Making Inventory (TDMI).ResultsThe ED nurses’ mean TDMI score was 182.54 ± 25.30, with mean item scores of 5.15 ± 0.71 for critical thinking, 5.07 ± 0.73 for cognitive behaviors, 4.78 ± 0.71 for triage experience and confidence, and 4.67 ± 0.84 for intuition. Univariate analysis showed that 13 factors—including hospital type, age, nurse level, years of work experience, and receipt of formal triage training—were associated with ED nurses’ TDMI scores (all P < 0.05). Multivariate linear regression analysis revealed that specialty hospital (β = −0.132), age (β = −0.150), nurse level (β = 0.157), receipt of formal triage training (β = 0.221), familiarity with adult triage criteria (β = 0.221), and inclusion of case-based discussions in triage training (β = 0.099) were independent factors associated with TDMI scores (all P < 0.05), accounting for 18.3% of the total variation (adjusted R² = 0.183).ConclusionsThe self-reported triage decision-making abilities of ED nurses in the three tertiary hospitals were at an upper-intermediate level, characterized by multidimensional imbalance and significant individual differences. Intuition may be a key area associated with improvement in self-reported triage decision-making abilities. It is recommended that a standardized triage training system be established, the application of triage criteria be strengthened, and case-based discussions be promoted during triage training. Background

Emergency department (ED) triage is the first step in the hospital emergency care system. It is directly associated with the efficiency of patient care and the rational allocation of healthcare resources [1, 2]. The number of ED visits in China has increased significantly over the past decade, rising from 51.9 million to 166.5 million [3], of which pediatric ED visits accounted for 35%-42% [4]. Beck et al. reported that 68.4% of pediatric ED visits to a clinic in Austria were non-urgent [5]. Wang et al. found that high-acuity cases accounted for 4.6%-5.5% of ED visits in Beijing, China [6]. ED overcrowding can lead to delayed treatment for critically ill patients and an increase in medical errors. Triage accuracy and efficiency are therefore crucial for the rational allocation of healthcare resources and for ensuring patient safety.

Triage is a complex clinical decision-making process that relies on triage nurses’ professional knowledge, abilities, and practical experience [7]. Global mainstream triage systems, such as the Manchester Triage System (MTS), Australasian Triage Scale (ATS), and Emergency Severity Index (ESI), provide standardized criteria for assigning triage levels [8]. Training and assessment programs for triage nurses have been implemented in several countries. For example, the Emergency Nurses Association in the United States provides standardized ESI training courses for triage nurses. In the United Kingdom, triage nurses are required to obtain national accreditation before using the MTS. In Australia, the assessment of ED nurses’ triage decision-making abilities is included in the ED quality monitoring system [9].

From a theoretical perspective, triage decision-making can be explained on the basis of dual-process theory and Benner’s novice-to-expert model. Dual-process theory proposes that human cognition and decision-making operate through two thinking systems: System 1 is fast and intuitive, whereas System 2 is rational and analytical. Triage nurses’ decision-making often relies on System 2, while experts can make efficient decisions by drawing on System 1 intuition [10, 11]. Benner’s novice-to-expert model emphasizes the role of clinical experience and structured training in the development of competence, and Benner’s theory of expert intuition has been shown to be more than merely a subjective feeling. The association between ability level and intuitive decision-making has also been empirically confirmed. Previous studies have found that nurses with higher self-reported ability and greater clinical experience have higher intuition scores, indicating that triage decision-making ability is associated with modifiable factors such as experience and training. This provides theoretical support for the selection of study variables [12]. Although there is theoretical and empirical support for the associations among experience, ability, and intuitive decision-making in routine nursing practice, the application of these frameworks to triage in China has not been fully explored. In particular, in the context of ED overcrowding and the unique challenges of pediatric EDs, the ways in which dual-process theory and Benner’s novice-to-expert model explain triage ability remain unclear.

Internationally recognized triage tools have achieved remarkable results in their respective countries and regions, but their direct application in China may face localization challenges due to differences in healthcare resource allocation, variations in disease spectra, and the lack of corresponding national training and accreditation systems. A three-step triage workflow has been standardized in China since the Expert Consensus on Emergency Pre-examination Triage was released in 2018 [13]. In 2020, the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine developed and interpreted pediatric and obstetric ED triage criteria [14, 15], thereby promoting the standardization of triage criteria in China. Regionally uniform ED triage criteria have been developed in cities such as Beijing and Shanghai [16, 17]. However, these criteria have deficiencies: (1) there is a lack of nationally uniform triage criteria and standard operating procedures, with triage nurses playing a key role in triage accuracy; (2) there is a lack of a standardized training system, as triage training is generally provided by ED staff without official standardized textbooks [18]; and (3) there is a lack of an authoritative triage competency certification or assessment system, with assessments for triage nurses generally focusing on theoretical knowledge. Previous studies have shown that ED nurses’ Triage Decision-Making Inventory (TDMI) scores are at an intermediate level [18]. However, multicenter studies assessing ED nurses’ triage decision-making abilities and the associated factors in specific regions remain scarce.

This study is the first to investigate the triage decision-making abilities of ED nurses in tertiary hospitals in southwestern China. Based on previous studies [18], this study used multivariate analysis to identify key factors, such as hospital type, receipt of training, and familiarity with triage criteria, associated with triage decision-making abilities. This study can provide evidence for the differentiated development of regional triage criteria across China. The findings can also provide a basis for developing a standardized, localized training system for triage nurses, promoting the unified implementation of triage criteria, and the establishing an ability certification system.

Methods

Participants

This was a multicenter cross-sectional study using convenience sampling. ED nurses from three tertiary Grade A hospitals in Sichuan, China were invited to participate in May 2025. Of the three hospitals, two were general hospitals located in Chengdu and Mianyang, respectively, and one was a specialty hospital for women and children located in Chengdu. All three hospitals had dedicated ED triage positions, with an average annual ED visits volume exceeding 300,000, establishing triage criteria and workflows, and an electronic triage system.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) being a registered ED nurse; (2) currently working at an ED triage position or having worked in such a position within the past year; and (3) being able to provide informed consent and voluntarily participating in the study.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) nurses on long-term leave (duration > 1 month) for reasons such as maternity leave, sick leave, or continuing education during the survey period; (2) newly recruited nurse (with < 3 months of ED work experience) who had not engaged in triage work; and (3) nurses who refused to participate or withdrew from the study.

Convenience sampling is a feasible strategy for rapidly obtaining samples for exploratory studies. Because ED nurses work in shift-based systems with heavy workloads, and because accessibility varies across hospitals, convenience sampling was used in this study. The three included tertiary Grade A hospitals (two general hospitals and one specialty hospital) were typical in terms of triage processes, electronic systems, and training resources, and included nurses with diverse professional levels and years of work experience. Therefore, they can partially represent the characteristics of similar hospitals. However, it should be noted that the training coverage rate and familiarity with triage criteria in the sample may have lead to an overestimation of overall triage decision-making abilities and an underestimation of differences in decision-making caused by insufficient resources among ED nurses in Sichuan, China. Therefore, the study results may be applicable to ED nurses in tertiary hospitals with standardized triage protocols, but may not be suitable for direct extrapolation to primary or secondary healthcare institutions.

Survey tools

A questionnaire comprising general information items and the Chinese version of the TDMI was used in this study (Supplementary file 1).

General information items

The general information items were designed by the researchers based on a literature review and covered the following aspects: (1) hospital characteristics, including hospital type (general or specialty), use of an electronic triage system, and implementation of triage criteria; (2) respondents’ demographic characteristics, including age, gender, nurse level, and educational attainment; (3) respondents’ job details, including years of work in the ED, years of work at a triage position, average weekly triage hours, and possession of emergency care-related certificates; and (4) training and assessments, including receipt of formal triage training, training frequency and methods (including whether case-based discussions were incorporated), familiarity with adult triage criteria and pediatric and obstetric triage criteria released by the National Health Commission of China in 2021 and the Expert Consensus on Emergency Pre-examination Triage, and post-training assessment frequency and methods.

Chinese version of the TDMI

The TDMI was developed by Cone [19] to assess nurses’ triage decision-making abilities. The Chinese version of the TDMI was translated and revised by Zhang [20] in 2014 and has demonstrated good reliability and validity in applications in China. The Chinese version consists of 37 items across four subscales: cognitive behaviors (8 items; maximum possible score 48), triage experience and confidence (10 items; maximum possible score 60), intuition (9 items; maximum possible score 54), and critical thinking (10 items; maximum possible score 60). Cognitive behaviors refer to the standardized operational behaviors of nurses who strictly follow the triage workflow. Triage experience and confidence refer to the work experience and situational response experience that nurses accumulate through long-term triage practice. Intuition refers to nurses’ rapid and automatic triage decision-making ability, which develops over time and is demonstrated under time constraints. Critical thinking refers to nurses’ rational decision-making process of analyzing, integrating, questioning, and verifying information related to a patient’s condition.

A 6-point Likert scale was used, with individual item scores ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree) and total scores ranging from 37 to 222. Higher TDMI scores were associated with stronger triage decision-making abilities. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the original TDMI was 0.96, with subscale coefficients ranging from 0.78 to 0.90. For the Chinese version, the Cronbach’s α was 0.93, and the content validity index was 0.89. The Cronbach’s α for the samples in this study was 0.93.

The TDMI subscales differ in the number of items and maximum possible scores. Therefore, this study converted each subscale’s raw total score into a mean item score to enable standardized comparisons among the subscales. The conversion formula was as follows: mean item score for a subscale = subscale total score / number of items in the subscale. The mean item scores ranged from 1 to 6, consistent with the 6-point Likert scale, thereby facilitating direct comparisons of mean item scores among the four subscales.

Questionnaire survey

The following measures were taken to ensure data quality of the electronic questionnaire survey:

(1) Pre-survey. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All research methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Ethical approval of this study was obtained from the Medical Research Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University [2025 Medical Scientific Research for Ethical Approval No. (119)]. Participants provided informed consent, were assured of data privacy, and informed that they could withdraw at any time. The electronic questionnaire was designed using Wenjuanxing, a widely used Chinese online survey platform. Research coordinators from the three hospitals received unified training on the study purpose, inclusion and exclusion criteria, questionnaire completion instructions, and key considerations. A small-scale pretest (n = 30) was conducted to evaluate the clarity of questionnaire items and the functionality of the electronic survey process. Minor adjustments were made to the wording of several items based on pretest feedback.

(2) Survey: The ED head nurses of the three hospitals distributed the questionnaire link and QR code via WeChat to nurses who met the inclusion criteria. Details of the invitation process are as follows: the ED head nurses at each hospital served as research coordinators. They briefly explained the purpose, content, and duration of the study, as well as the questionnaire completion instructions, to all nurses in their respective EDs, clearly informing them that this was an academic study with no commercial purpose and that the collected data would be used only for this study. The questionnaire link and QR code were then shared in the nurses’ WeChat groups and forwarded individually to nurses who had not checked the group messages in time, ensuring that every nurse who met the inclusion criteria received the invitation without omission. To minimize any perceived pressure arising from questionnaire distribution through the head nurses, the following measures were adopted: the head nurses were informed that they were responsible only for questionnaire distribution, invitation, and promotion, and were not permitted to urge, persuade, or imply participation in any form, nor to link participation with respondents’ work performance evaluations. The questionnaire was completed independently by the nurses, and the head nurses neither interfered with nor reviewed their responses, thereby minimizing respondents’ psychological stress arising from concern that the head nurses might know the details of their responses.

The first page of the questionnaire was an informed consent form that clearly explained the study purpose, content, principle of voluntary participation, and confidentiality. Respondents were allowed to proceed to the formal questionnaire only after ticking the “I agree” checkbox. This research strictly adhered to the principle of voluntary participation. All participants took part voluntarily, without any compulsory requirements. The informed consent form stated that participants could choose to participate in or withdraw from the study at any time, that no reason for withdrawal was required, and that participation would not have any adverse effects on their jobs, such as performance assessment. ED nurses who did not tick the “I agree” checkbox could not access the questionnaire completion page. There were no compulsory participation requirements at any stage of the process. The principal investigator’s contact information was provided so that ED nurses could raise any questions or concerns.

All questions were mandatory. Responses could be submitted only after all questions had been answered. The online questionnaire platform restricted responses to one submission per device. This was an anonymous survey. No personally identifiable information, such as names, employee identification numbers, or contact information, was collected. Only general information, such as age, nurse level, and years of work experience, was collected. Data exported from Wenjuanxing’s backend did not contain any personally identifiable information and were identified only by random numbers. All original data are stored by two research team members and kept strictly confidential. They were used for this study only. After the study is completed, the data will be destroyed in accordance with relevant requirements to ensure that respondents’ private information is not disclosed. This may reduce the influence of social desirability bias on the assessment of self-reported triage decision-making abilities.

(3) Post-survey quality control: The collected data were double-checked by two researchers, with a focus on the time required to complete the questionnaire. Responses with completion times of < 5 min or > 30 min were excluded. Responses showing regular answer patterns (e.g., the same response given to ≥ 90% of the questions, or the same answer recurring in a wave-like pattern) or logical errors (e.g., contradictions between age and years of work experience) were also excluded. The research database was established after independent checks by the two researchers.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 was used for data analysis. For descriptive statistics, measurement data (such as TDMI total score and mean item scores) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). Enumeration data (such as age groups and hospital types) were presented as frequency (percentage, n/%). For univariate analysis, normally distributed measurement data with two categories (e.g., TDMI total scores of ED nurses from specialty versus general hospitals) were compared using the independent-samples t-test. Normally distributed measurement data with multiple categories (e.g., TDMI total scores across different nurse levels) were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance, with least significant difference t-test used for pairwise comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test.

A repeated-measures analysis of variance was performed to compare the mean item scores across the four subscales. If the assumption of sphericity was not met, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. The Bonferroni correction was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Partial η² was reported as the effect size.

For multivariate analysis, TDMI total score was set as the dependent variable (continuous), and variables with P < 0.05 from univariate analysis were entered as independent variables. Dummy coding was used for categorical predictor variables, such as nurse level, hospital type, and familiarity with the adult triage criteria. Taking the nurse level as an example, this variable included four response options: nurse, nurse practitioner, supervising nurse, and co-chief nurse or above. In this study, “nurse” was set as the reference group, and three dummy variables were included in the regression model (coded as 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively, as shown in Table 1). For binary variables, “specialty hospital” and “no” were set as the reference groups for “hospital type” and “receipt of formal triage training”, respectively, and were coded as 0 and 1.

Assignments on independent variables

To select the appropriate regression method, this study first used the enter method to build the initial regression model, thereby including all variables identified in the univariate analysis. This allowed a comprehensive evaluation of the predictive effect of each variable on TDMI scores. Considering the exploratory nature of this study and the lack of mature theoretical guidance on the relative importance of each variable, the stepwise method was then adopted for variable selection (α-to-enter = 0.05, α-to-remove = 0.10) to identify the core combination of variables with independent predictive effects on TDMI scores.

For hypothesis testing in regression, this study conducted tests on the assumptions of multiple linear regression. Through residual and Q-Q plots and Shapiro-Wilk tests, it was observed that the residuals approximately followed a normal distribution (P > 0.05). The residual plots showed that residuals were randomly distributed around the zero line, with no clustering or divergent trends, satisfying the assumption of homoscedasticity. The measurement results for tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) showed that the tolerance values for each independent variable were greater than 0.1, and the VIF values were less than 10. This indicates no multicollinearity, ensuring the robustness of the regression model’s estimation.

Results

Response collection and general information

A total of 554 questionnaires were distributed, and 554 responses were received (100% response rate). After excluding five invalid responses due to excessively short completion times or regular answer patterns, 549 valid responses were included, yielding an effective response rate of 99.1% (549/554).

In China, nurse levels include nurse, nurse practitioner, supervising nurse, co-chief nurse, and chief-nurse. Of the 549 respondents, 247 (44.99%) were supervising nurses and 214 (38.98%) were nurse practitioners; 309 (56.28%) were from general hospitals and 240 (43.72%) were from the specialty hospital; 484 (88.2%) were female; 240 (43.72%) were aged 31–40 years; 363 (66.12%) had more than five years of ED work experience; 468 (85.25%) were currently working at a triage position; 506 (92.2%) had received formal triage training; 369 (67.21%) reported that triage training they received included case-based discussions;124 (22.59%) were very familiar with the adult triage criteria released by the National Health Commission of China in 2021; and 386 (70.31%) were somewhat familiar with these criteria.

ED nurses’ self-reported triage decision-making abilities

The mean TDMI total score for the 549 ED nurses was 182.54 ± 25.30, accounting for 82.2% of the maximum possible score of 222. Based on the relative distribution of TDMI scores reported in previous studies [18], the triage decision-making abilities observed in this study can be considered to be at an upper-intermediate level. This judgment is based on lateral comparison with previous studies rather than on an absolute standardized classification. Because the TDMI subscales differ in the number of items and maximum possible scores, direct comparisons of raw subscale scores may be misleading. Therefore, mean item scores were calculated to standardize the comparisons. Table 2 presents the descriptive statistics for the mean item scores, with critical thinking receiving the highest score (5.15 ± 0.71) and intuition receiving the lowest score (4.67 ± 0.84). This suggests that intuition may be a key area associated with improving self-reported triage decision-making abilities.

Discussion

Level of ED nurses’ triage decision-making abilities

ED nurses’ self-reported triage decision-making abilities were at an upper-intermediate level

The results of this study showed that the mean TDMI score for ED nurses was 182.54 ± 25.30, accounting for 82.2% of the maximum possible score of 222. According to the stratification criteria for TDMI scores used in the studies by Yang et al. [18], the ED nurses’ triage decision-making abilities observed in this study can be considered to be at an upper-intermediate level (this judgment is based on lateral comparison with previous studies rather than an absolute standardized classification), which was higher than the 166.50 ± 26.90 reported in a multicenter survey in northern China [18] and broadly similar to those of nurses in tertiary hospitals in economically developed eastern regions of China (such as Shanghai and Beijing) [21]. This may be attributed to the National Health Commission of China’s recent efforts to standardize ED triage through expert consensus [16, 17], as well as increased attention on triage in tertiary hospitals. All three hospitals included in this study were tertiary Grade A hospitals, each with an average annual ED volume exceeding 300,000. All had electronic triage systems and implemented standardized triage procedures. These factors provided a structured practice environment for triage nurses. In addition, 92.2% of the nurses had received formal triage training, a rate higher than the 68.3% reported in northern China [18]. This suggests that standardized practice combined with systematic training may be associated with improved self-reported triage decision-making abilities.

Triage decision-making abilities characterized by multidimensional imbalance

This study found that ED nurses’ self-reported triage decision-making abilities were characterized by multidimensional imbalance. The mean item score for the critical thinking subscale was the highest (5.15 ± 0.71), consistent with the view that triage is a clinical reasoning process, as proposed by Cone [19]. This suggests that Chinese triage nurses have developed solid foundational clinical judgment skills, possibly due to strengthened critical thinking training in recent nursing education in China and the incorporation of case-based discussions in triage training [22, 23].

Table 2 shows that the mean item score for the intuition subscale was 4.67 ± 0.84, which was significantly lower than those for the other subscales (all P < 0.001). This suggests that the intuition subscale was a weak point compared with the other three subscales, and that intuition may be a key area of weakness in triage decision-making abilities among ED nurses in the three tertiary Grade A hospitals. This is consistent with the TDMI definition of “intuition” as a rapid, nonintentional judgment ability developed by nurses on the basis of accumulated triage experience. This contrasts with international research emphasizing dual support from rational analysis and intuitive judgment in triage decision-making. Graff et al. and Price et al. described clinical decision-making through dual-process theory, highlighting the interdependence of analytical and intuitive reasoning [10, 11]. Global mainstream triage systems, such as ESI and MTS, incorporate intuitive training into their core curriculum, whereas triage training in China primarily focuses on rational skills, such as symptom recognition, memorization of triage criteria, and procedural execution, without emphasizing intuitive decision-making or including it in ability assessments. This results in insufficient attention to intuition. The results of Liu et al. have shown that greater clinical experience significantly enhances nurses’ professional intuition and clinical reasoning [24]. Soola et al. and Chen et al. found that intuitive decision-making abilities in nurses with extensive experience are usually associated with repeated clinical practice rather than being acquired solely through systematic training [25, 26]. Benner’s novice-to-expert theory further supports the idea that intuitive decision-making develops through long-term, accumulated experience [12]. In our study, a large proportion of nurses with greater work experience, including those holding the title of supervising nurse or above, were included; however, some had subsequently been promoted to management positions and therefore had fewer opportunities to make triage decisions directly. The development of intuitive ability is closely associated with the accumulation of experience in clinical practice, which is consistent with our study findings that practice frequency is associated with decision-making ability. Comparing mean item scores across the four subscales eliminated the influence of differences in the number of items per subscale, making comparisons among subscales more reliable.

Factors associated with triage decision-making abilities

Multivariate linear regression analysis revealed that six factors accounted for 18.3% of the total variation in the TDMI scores (adjusted R² = 0.183), indicating that the remaining approximately 82% of the total variation was not explained by the model. Therefore, our discussion of the associations between each associated factor and decision-making ability was based on the limited explanatory power and should be regarded as an exploratory analysis rather than a causal inference.

This study found that familiarity with triage criteria, age, nurse level, hospital type, receipt of formal triage training, and the inclusion of case-based discussions in triage training were independent factors associated with ED nurses’ self-reported triage decision-making abilities. However, these identified factors explained only a limited portion of the total variation because of the model’s relatively low explanatory power. Other possible factors, such as cognitive style, organizational culture, workload, and the practice environment, may also be predictors. The cross-sectional design precluded causal inference and did not account for moderating or mediating effects among variables. Therefore, all of the following discussion is based on associations. The practice suggestions are preliminary and need to be validated through longitudinal or intervention studies.

Familiarity with triage criteria

In this study, familiarity with triage criteria showed the strongest association with TDMI scores (β = 0.221), consistent with dual-process theory [10] and suggesting that explicit knowledge of triage criteria underlies decision-making. Only 22.59% of participants were very familiar with the criteria, a rate lower than those observed in hospitals in Changsha, Hunan, China [27]. This reflects regional differences in the promotion of the triage criteria. A uniform triage criterion had not been developed in Sichuan, China, leading to differences in familiarity with adult and pediatric triage criteria among ED nurses. The mean TDMI total score for nurses who were very familiar with triage criteria were significantly higher than those for nurses who were somewhat familiar or not at all familiar (P < 0.001), consistent with Zhu et al.’s results [28]. The implementation of triage criteria was associated with triage consistency (Kappa value increased from 0.42 to 0.68). Because familiarity with triage criteria showed the strongest association with TDMI scores (β = 0.221), the following exploratory directions could be considered: (1) establishing unified regional triage criteria to reduce regional differences; (2) incorporating mastery of triage criteria into the assessment of nurses’ triage decision-making abilities; and (3) developing a triage nurse accreditation system to promote improvements in triage quality. Given the model’s limited explanatory power, with only 18.3% of total variation explained, and the study’s cross-sectional design, the above suggestions should be regarded as preliminary hypotheses. Their validity needs to be confirmed through longitudinal studies or randomized controlled trials.

Age and nurse level

In this study, nurse level was positively correlated with TDMI scores (β = 0.157). The mean TDMI scores for supervising nurses and co-chief nurses or above were significantly higher than those for nurses and nurse practitioners (P < 0.001), consistent with the findings of Martínez-Segura et al. [29]. This may be because promotion to higher nurse levels requires professional knowledge and years of experience, and because supervising nurses were generally responsible for triage quality control and guidance in complex cases. However, because of the model’s limited explanatory power and because nurse level is a comprehensive indicator variable, its contribution could not be separated from those of other factors.

Notably, the mean TDMI score for co-chief nurses or above (183.08 ± 19.40) was slightly lower than that for supervising nurses, possibly because the 13 nurses who possessed the title of co-chief nurses or above had shifted to management or teaching positions and had decreased their direct participation in triage practice [30]. Because nurse level is a comprehensive indicator of experience and ability rather than an exogenous variable, this study was unable to determine whether nurse level itself or the accompanying accumulation of experience led to the observed differences in scores. ED nurses aged 30 years and older had higher mean TDMI scores (β = 0.128), which is in line with the pattern that clinical decision-making abilities are associated with experience accumulation [30]. Some studies have shown that nurses’ TDMI scores are not associated with professional title or age, whereas nurses with longer ED work experience have stronger self-reported triage decision-making abilities [31, 32]. This suggests that the accumulation of work experience is associated with triage decision-making abilities. However, this remains a speculative interpretation and does not establish a causal relationship.

Hospital type

The mean TDMI score for ED nurses in the specialty hospital were significantly lower than that in general hospitals (β = −0.132), consistent with previous studies [33]. This highlights limitations on triage decision-making abilities in specialty hospitals. ED nurses in general hospitals encounter a wider variety of diseases, providing more opportunities to develop comprehensive triage skills. In contrast, triage criteria and procedures in the specialty hospital were primarily tailored to specialized conditions, with limited mechanisms for managing interdisciplinary emergencies. This may reduce nurses’ confidence in handling complex scenarios. In this study, the specialty hospital was a women’s and children’s hospital, where more than 85% of patients were children. ED nurses performed well in pediatric emergencies but were more prone to errors in adult cases because of their lower exposure to such cases [34]. This suggests that the diversity of cases encountered by ED nurses is associated with TDMI scores, indicating that triage abilities may be influenced by multiple factors, such as patient type, specialty hospital characteristics, and work environment. Working in a specialized hospital may be associated with lower self-reported triage decision-making ability. The feasibility of tailored triage criteria and training programs suited to the characteristics of specialty hospitals could be explored in the future. However, this is only an exploratory suggestion, and its effectiveness needs to be validated through multicenter prospective studies.

Formal triage training

A higher mean TDMI score was observed among nurses who had received formal triage training (β = 0.135, P = 0.015), consistent with the studies conducted in Iran and Malaysia [25, 35]. In this study, the effectiveness of formal triage training was reflected in two aspects: (1) the training content covered the core elements of the triage criteria released by the National Health Commission of China in 2021; and (2) the training methods were diversified, with 67.2% including scenario simulation, which helped nurses convert theoretical knowledge into practical skills through immersive practice. Nurses without formal triage training mostly relied on mentorship and were more likely to develop nonstandard decision-making habits because of mentors’ personal biases, such as overreliance on subjective judgment [36]. This suggests that mentorship may be associated with decision-making habits and may affect TDMI scores.

Nurses whose triage training included case-based discussions had significantly higher TDMI scores than those whose training did not (β = 0.099, P = 0.029), consistent with the results of Sayar et al. [37]. Case-based discussions enhanced nurses’ sensitivity to triage scenarios, enabling quicker identification of hidden symptoms and more accurate decisions [25]. This aligns with the availability heuristic in dual-process theory, whereby nurses’ reference to similar cases to support decision-making is associated with TDMI scores [38]. Such discussions also strengthened professional knowledge, which plays a greater role in triage decision-making than experience alone [37]. Additionally, knowledge sharing involved in case-based discussions was also associated with nurses’ TDMI scores [37]. Therefore, case-based discussions are recommended for regular hospital training; however, this recommendation should also be regarded as exploratory.

Based on the associated factors identified in this study, we recommend the following improvements in training: ① Basic components. Typical cases should be used to explain the triage criteria. ② Specialized components. Training in complex case analysis and triage simulation exercises based on real cases is recommended to improve critical thinking and intuitive decision-making. The effectiveness of these measures need to be validated through intervention studies, such as randomized controlled trials, and evaluated using objective indicators, such as triage accuracy and assessment results from triage simulation exercises.

The regression model applied in this study explained only 18.3% of the total variation, suggesting that other important predictors may exist. For example, cognitive styles, including intuitive and analytical styles, and organizational culture have been shown to be independent factors associated with clinical decision-making behavior [39] and to be context-dependent. Owing to the complexity of measuring the construct of cognitive styles and their exclusion from the model, some effects may have been attributed to existing variables, thereby affecting the accuracy of the estimates. In the future, these variables could be examined using relevant scales to assess their incremental explanatory effects on triage decision-making ability.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional survey, it can explore associations between each factor and TDMI scores but cannot infer causal relationships. The study results, therefore, reflect associations rather than causation. Second, this study used convenience sampling, with ED nurses from three tertiary Grade A hospitals in a single province selected as the study subjects. The resources of these three hospitals were relatively superior to those of primary and secondary healthcare institutions. The coverage rate of triage training and familiarity with triage criteria among ED nurses in these three hospitals may have been higher than the average level in the province where the hospitals were located. This may have led to an overestimation of triage decision-making abilities among the investigated ED nurses. No multistage or stratified random sampling was used. Sample selection was based on convenience rather than randomness, which may have reduced the representativeness of the samples by excluding ED nurses from healthcare institutions of different levels, regions, and types, thereby introducing sampling biases. This may limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Third, the TDMI scale was used to measure ED nurses’ triage decision-making abilities. As a self-report measure, its results may be influenced by various subjective factors, potentially leading to self-report bias. Specifically, nurses’ self-perception, professional confidence, social desirability, and professional identity requirements may result in overestimation or underestimation of their triage decision-making abilities, causing discrepancies between the study findings and actual triage performance. Therefore, the scale may not fully or objectively reflect ED nurses’ actual triage decision-making abilities. Fourth, the study subjects were drawn from three tertiary Grade A hospitals. These hospitals had standardized triage criteria and large patient volumes. As a result, the findings may not be applicable to small hospitals, rural hospitals, or hospitals without electronic triage systems. Therefore, the study has regional limitations, which may make it difficult to generalize the findings to ED nurses in other provinces or regions of China. Fifth, the predictive factors included in this study explained only a limited proportion of the variation, and other factors may also affect decision-making abilities. Sixth, the questionnaires were distributed by head nurses. Although measures such as anonymity, informed consent, and a prohibition on urging nurses to respond were adopted, social desirability bias may still have persisted. The high response rate may have been associated with mobilization by head nurses and the relevance of the questionnaire topic, but it may also have introduced self-selection bias. Nurses who were more confident in their triage decision-making may have been more likely to participate in the study, leading to slightly higher TDMI scores than their actual level. Third-party recruitment could be adopted in future studies to reduce response and self-selection bias.

Future studies may adopt multistage random sampling to enhance sample representativeness, expand regional coverage, include healthcare institutions at different levels and in different regions, and take different triage conditions into account. A longitudinal design may be used to track dynamic changes in nurses’ decision-making abilities and to explore the mechanisms underlying the associations among variables. Objective indicators, such as triage accuracy rates and simulated triage performance, may be used to supplement self-report measures and reduce subjective bias. The range of independent variables may also be expanded to include additional potential associated factors. In addition, more stable hierarchical regression methods may be used to optimize the model and improve its explanatory power and stability, thereby enhancing the reliability and applicability of the findings.

Conclusions

The self-reported triage decision-making abilities of ED nurses in tertiary hospitals in Sichuan, China, were at an upper-intermediate level. This conclusion was based on relative comparison with previous studies rather than on standardized classification. Tables 2 and 3 show that the mean item score for the intuition subscale was significantly lower than those for the other subscales, suggesting a multidimensional imbalance. The study results indicated associations between various factors and TDMI scores, but did not establish causal relationships. Nursing managers should pay greater attention to intuition and its associated factors. It should be emphasized that this was an exploratory study. Establishing a standardized triage training system, strengthening the application of triage criteria, and promoting case-based discussions in triage training were positively associated with ED nurses’ self-reported triage decision-making abilities. However, these associations do not constitute causal evidence, and much of variation remains unexplained. Additional potential variables, such as cognitive styles and organizational cultures, should be included in future studies, and longitudinal or intervention studies are needed to validate the true effects of each factor and the effectiveness of training measures.
