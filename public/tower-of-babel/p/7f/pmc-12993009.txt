Cuproptosis-inducing photothermal nanotherapy coupled with ECM destabilization drives potent tumor regression and immune reawakening
Authors: Sathiyamoorthy Padmanaban, Omkar Sangabathula, Sahil Chahal, Amal Babu, Sree Samanvitha Kuppa, Adityanarayan Mohapatra, Giang Chau Dang, Shee Eun Lee, Sakthivel Gandhi, Hyun-Joo Woo, In-Kyu Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor challenges are posed by the dense extracellular matrix and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in the treatment of aggressive breast cancers such as triple-negative breast cancer. Conventional treatments usually fail to achieve durable outcomes because they poorly address ECM-mediated barriers that facilitate tumor cell migration, driving rapid post-therapy recurrence. We designed a hollow nanocomplex incorporating an extracellular matrix–disrupting agent to improve intratumoral penetration and potentiate immunogenic cell death. The system consisted of hollow copper sulfide nanoparticles loaded with 4-methylumbelliferone, modified with polymetformin, and surface-coated with hyaluronic acid to ensure targeted delivery via CD44 receptors. Upon 1064 nm near-infrared laser irradiation, hollow copper sulfide loaded with 4- methyl umberlliferone and coated with polymetformin and hyaluronic acid generates localized heat for photothermal ablation while releasing 4-MU to inhibit HAS2, disrupting the tumor matrix. Moreover, final group induced immunogenic cell death and downregulated HAS2 and CD44 in vitro along with copper mediated apoptosis. Meanwhile, the final group achieved complete tumor regression in vivo, with no recurrence and full prevention of lung metastasis observed in a 4T1 mouse model. Furthermore, immunostaining confirmed that apoptosis, macrophage repolarization, and immune activation were increased. This research presents a synergistic nanoplatform that integrates matrix remodeling, photothermal therapy (PTT), and immune reprogramming, offering a promising strategy for treating metastatic and immune-evasive breast tumors.Keywords: Hollow copper sulfide, 4-Methylumbelliferone, HAS-2 depletion, Cuproptosis, Matrix disruption

Introduction

Breast cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, particularly due to its high rate of recurrence, metastasis, and resistance to conventional therapies [[1], [2], [3]]. Among the known subtypes of breast cancer, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) presents a major clinical challenge, largely owing to the associated poor immunogenicity and lack of hormone receptors, which limit the efficacy of endocrine or targeted therapies [2,4,5]. Subsequently, an urgent need exists for multifunctional therapeutic strategies that can eliminate primary tumors and remodel the tumor microenvironment to prevent metastasis and promote long-term immune surveillance [6,7] (see Scheme 1).

Schematic representation of the synthesis of hollow copper sulfate nanoparticles incorporating 4-methylumbelliferone (4MU) and polymetformin, with an outer hyaluronic acid coating, and their proposed mechanism of action in mice combining photothermal therapy (PTT).

The scheme deals with three major problems. 1) Increase in the hyaluronan and CD44 leads to extensive tumor growth, 2) increase in HAS-2 levels in 4T1 tumors improves the hyaluronan levels and thereby surges the ECM deposition, and 3) intensification in the ECM deposition leads to impaired infiltration of the immune cells into the tumor microenvironment.

Recently, metal-based nanomaterials, particularly hollow copper sulfide (HCuS) nanoparticles [8], have garnered significant attention as photothermal agents due to their strong near-infrared (NIR) absorption [9], high photothermal conversion efficiency, and biocompatibility [10]. Moreover, the hollow architecture provides an ideal nanocarrier system, offering a large internal cavity for drug loading while maintaining a high surface area-to-volume ratio for surface modification [11,12]. Furthermore, copper-based nanoparticles inherently possess redox activity and can interact with intracellular glutathione and oxidative stress pathways, further enhancing the anticancer potency of these nanoparticles through reactive oxygen species (ROS)-related mechanisms [13].

Traditional photothermal therapy (PTT) primarily utilizes the NIR-I window (650–900 nm) [14]; however, PTT that employs the NIR-II windows (1000–1350 nm) is emerging as a superior alternative due to an association with deeper tissue penetration [15,16], reduced scattering, and higher maximum permissible exposure (MPE) [15,17]. Indeed, these expanded mechanisms allow for more efficient and safer thermal ablation of deep-seated tumors [18,19]. Notably, 1064 nm lasers, which were used in this study, fall within this NIR-II window and have exhibited increased therapeutic efficacy in both preclinical and translational studies [20,21]. Additionally, when combined with targeting ligands and controlled drug delivery, NIR-II-activated nanoplatforms offer immense potential for treatment in precision oncology [22,23].

Thus, this study incorporated 4-methylumbelliferone (4-MU), a small-molecule inhibitor known to downregulate hyaluronan synthase-2 (HAS2) [24], a key enzyme responsible for synthesizing hyaluronic acid (HA), to complement the photothermal component and enhance tumor microenvironment modulation. Interestingly, HA is a critical component in the tumor extracellular matrix (ECM), which facilitates tumor growth, invasion, and immune evasion through interactions with the CD44 receptor [25,26]. Meanwhile, HAS2 overexpression and excessive HA deposition contribute to increased interstitial fluid pressure, physical barriers to immune infiltration, and enhanced metastatic potential [27,28]. Therefore, by depleting HA through HAS2 inhibition, administering 4-MU serves to normalize the tumor matrix, disrupt HA–CD44 interactions, reduce tumor stiffness, and improve nanoparticle penetration and immune cell accessibility [29,30].

Hence, this study incorporated 4-MU into a hollow CuS core to deliver this matrix-modulating agent directly to the tumor site; meanwhile, surface modification with HA provided active targeting via CD44-mediated uptake, further enhancing tumor specificity. To amplify ROS generation and stress response, the particle was additionally modified with polymetformin, a polymeric form of the biguanide that promotes oxidative imbalance within tumor cells. Upon laser activation, this integrated nanoplatform—designated CuP-HAM—generates localized hyperthermia while simultaneously releasing 4-MU, amplifying ROS levels, and remodeling the tumor microenvironment.

This study presents an in-depth investigation of a rationally engineered nanotherapeutic system—CuP-HAM—designed to integrate photothermal therapy, tumor matrix remodeling, and immune modulation within a single platform. The nanoparticle was systematically characterized owing to the associated synthesis and physicochemical properties and biological behavior, including in vitro uptake, cytotoxicity, immunogenic cell death induction, and mechanistic impact on hyaluronan signaling. Subsequent in-vivo studies in a 4T1 orthotopic breast cancer model demonstrated the ability of the platform to achieve complete tumor ablation, suppress tumor recurrence, and effectively prevent pulmonary metastasis following NIR-II laser activation. We hypothesize that this multi-modal approach, which combines laser-triggered photothermal ablation, HAS2-targeted matrix disruption via 4-MU release, and polymetformin-induced oxidative stress, enhances tumor cell killing, promotes immunogenic cell death (ICD)—as evidenced by the upregulation of CRT and HMGB1—and reprograms the tumor microenvironment by facilitating macrophage polarization and downregulating CD44-mediated immune evasion pathways. This convergence of physical, biochemical, and immunological mechanisms offers a synergistic and durable therapeutic strategy capable of addressing both the primary tumor burden and the immunosuppressive landscape of metastatic breast cancer, particularly in hard-to-treat subtypes such as TNBC.

Experimental section

Materials

Copper (II) chloride, sodium hydroxide, and 4-MU were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Hydrazine hydrate, ethanol, and methanol were purchased from Duksan Pure chemicals, Republic of Korea.

Method

HCuS synthesis

Copper sulfide nanoparticles (CuS NPs) were synthesized following previously established methods [31]. In summary, 100 μL of 0.5 mol/L CuCl2 solution and 240 mg of PVP-K30 were introduced into 25 mL of deionized water under magnetic stirring at room temperature. Subsequently, 25 mL of a 0.01 mM NaOH solution (pH 9.0) was added, followed by the addition of 6.4 μL of hydrazine hydrate (N2H4·H2O) after 2 min. Five minutes later, 200 μL of Na2S solution (320 mg/mL) was introduced into the suspension. The reaction mixture was maintained at 75 °C for 2 h to allow the CuS NPs to form. The resulting nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and washed twice with deionized water.

Drug loading mechanism (4-MU in HCuS)

The 4-MU, which is highly hydrophobic, was loaded into the hollow parts of HCuS using the solvent evaporation method. Briefly, a 0.75:1 ratio of drug to nanoparticles was measured and dispersed in methanol. The mixture was stirred for 1 h, and the temperature was slowly increased to 50 °C. The solution was then allowed to evaporate. As the solution was reduced to a final volume of 10%, distilled water was slowly added to the mixture and stirred vigorously. After the methanol has been completely removed, the 4-MU transitions into the pores of HCuS to form 4-MU + HCuS. Next, the 4-MU + HCuS combination was centrifuged and dispersed in distilled water.

Polymetformin synthesis

To synthesize polymetformin (PolyMet), linear polyethylenimine (PEI) and dicyandiamide were allowed to react under heated, acidic conditions. Specifically, 0.2 g of linear PEI hydrochloride and 2 g of dicyandiamide were dissolved in 10 mL of 2 M hydrochloric acid. The mixture was maintained at 100 °C for 24 h to complete the reaction. Afterward, the product was purified using an ultrafiltration device to eliminate any remaining unreacted dicyandiamide, followed by two washes with deionized water. Then, the resulting material was lyophilized. The successful synthesis of PolyMet was confirmed using proton nuclear magnetic resonance (^1H NMR) spectroscopy [32].

CuP-HAM synthesis

To modify the surface charge of the 4-MU + HCuS nanoparticles, the nanoparticles were first coated with positively charged PolyMet. Specifically, PolyMet was added to the 4-MU + HCuS nanoparticle solution at a 2:1 mass ratio (PolyMet:4-MU + HCuS) and stirred at 40 °C overnight. Subsequently, positively charged intermediate nanoparticles, referred to as CuP-M, were formed. These CuP-M particles were then collected by centrifugation and washed three times with deionized water to remove any remaining unbound PolyMet.

Next, the CuP-M intermediate was coated with hyaluronic acid (HA; 480 kDa) to enhance biocompatibility and provide CD44-targeting functionality. HA was added at a 2:1 mass ratio (HA:CuP-M), and the mixture was stirred at 40 °C overnight to allow thorough coating, yielding the final nanoparticle formulation, CuP-HAM. This HA-coated product was again purified by centrifugation and washed three times with deionized water to remove any excess HA. The purified CuP-HAM nanoparticles were then freeze-dried and stored for subsequent analysis.

Characterization of the CuP-HAM nanoparticles

The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of HCuS, CuP-M, and CuP-HAM nanoparticles were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a Zetasizer Nano Z instrument (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). Each measurement was conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and assess variability.

Next, field-emission transmission electron microscopy (FE-TEM; JEM-2100F, USA) was employed to evaluate the morphology of nanoparticles. Additional material characterization was performed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses.

The photostability of the nanoparticles was assessed by irradiating the nanoparticle solutions with a 1064 nm near-infrared (NIR-II) laser for 5 min, during which the corresponding temperature profiles were recorded to generate heating curves.

Colloidal stability of nanoparticles

To evaluate the colloidal stability of the nanoparticles, the nanoparticles were dispersed in three different media: distilled water (D.W.), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and 10% serum. The particle size in each medium was monitored over 7 days to assess any changes in dispersion stability.

4-MU content, encapsulation efficiency, and in-vitro release profiles

Drug content was estimated using the High-Performance Liquid Chromatography technique. All experiments were performed in triplicate. The drug content was calculated using Equation (1).

The entrapment efficiency (EE) was assessed by keeping 1 mL of drug-loaded CuPHAM nanoparticles in a dialysis membrane bag (3.5 kDa MWCO) for 2 h. The amount of drug dialyzed was estimated using HPLC. The percentage of 4-MU entrapped was calculated using Equation (2).

The release of 4MU from CuS nanoparticles was carried out through dialysis method under sink conditions. CuPHAM nanoparticles equivalent to 10 mg of the drug were placed in a cellulose dialysis bag (molecular weight cut off, 3.5 kDa; Sigma-Aldrich) and sealed at both ends. The dialysis bag was immersed in a glass compartment containing 50 ml of phosphate buffer (pH 7.4, 6.5 (with and without laser irradiation)) as the dissolution medium at 37 ± 0.5 °C, and the media was agitated under magnetic stirring at 200 rpm to perform an in vitro release study. Aliquots of 1 ml were withdrawn at regular time intervals with the replacement of fresh PBS (1 ml) over a period of 72 h. The samples (n = 3) were then analyzed through HPLC.

In vitro cell studies

The 4T1 murine breast cancer cell line was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic solution. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 to ensure optimal growth conditions.

Calorimetric WST-1 cell viability assay

The 4T1 cells (1 × 104 cells per well) were seeded in a 96-well plate and cultured for 24 h. Following incubation, the culture medium was aspirated and replaced with fresh medium containing CuS, 4MU, and CuP-HAM at varying concentrations. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. To evaluate the cell viability, 10 μL of WST-1 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation for 1 h. Subsequently, a microplate reader was used to measure the absorbance at 440 nm.

Cell uptake studies

The 4T1 cells (5 × 106) were seeded in a culture dish and were allowed to adhere overnight. Then, the 4T1 cells were treated with coumarin (C6)-loaded nanoparticles with and without HA. These nanoparticles were treated for 1 h, 2 h, and 4 h. Subsequently, the nanoparticles were washed three times and were isolated using trypsin. Next, 2% PFA was added to the isolated pellet, and the mixture was stored at 4 °C in the dark before flow cytometry analysis to measure the cellular uptake of the nanoparticles. Similarly, the cells were processed in 8-well plates for a qualitative cell uptake process.

ICD Evaluation in vitro

For the in vitro assessment of DAMPs, 4T1 breast cancer cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded and treated with culture medium containing either CuS or CuP-HAM nanoparticles (at an equivalent CuS concentration of 50 μg/mL) or left untreated (blank control). After a 4-h incubation, the cells were subjected to NIR laser irradiation (1064 nm) for 5 min to induce photothermal activation. Following treatment, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies against calreticulin (CRT, D3E6) and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1, D3E5) at 4 °C for 12 h. Secondary antibody labeling was performed using Flamma® 594-conjugated secondary antibodies, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. During the staining procedure, all washing steps were performed with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST) to minimize nonspecific binding. Finally, fluorescence signals corresponding to DAMP exposure (CRT surface translocation and HMGB1 release) were visualized using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, LSM-700, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Western blotting assay

The 4T1 cells were treated with either CuS or CuP-HAM nanoparticles (at an equivalent CuS concentration of 50 μg/mL) for 4 h, followed by 1064 nm NIR laser irradiation at 1.5 W/cm2 for 5 min. After photothermal treatment, the cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. Culture supernatants were collected and stored for cytokine analysis, while the remaining adherent cells were lysed using RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and kept on ice for 30 min.

The lysates were then centrifuged at 13,300 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min to remove cellular debris. The resulting total protein content in the supernatants was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay kit. SDS-PAGE was employed to resolve equal protein concentrations, before the separated proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes and subjected to Western blotting. Membranes were blocked and incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. After thorough washing, the protein bands were visualized using a Thermo Fisher chemiluminescence imaging system.

Hemocompatibility testing of CuPHAM nanoparticles

The biocompatibility of NPs was evaluated through hemolysis assays using whole blood from BALB/c mice collected in heparin-coated tubes to prevent clotting. The samples were centrifuged (5000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C) to isolate red blood cells (RBCs), which were washed thoroughly with 1 × PBS to remove plasma and other components. The cleaned RBCs were then incubated with varying concentrations of CuPHAM NPs dispersed in PBS, with Triton X-100 (1 %) as the positive control (complete hemolysis) and PBS as the negative control. In Vivo Antitumor Efficiency of the CuP-HAM Nanoparticles.

All animal experiments were authorized by the Chonnam Institutional Law of Animal Care and the Committee of Chonnam National University Hospital (CNU IACUC-H-2024-23). Tumor reduction studies were conducted on six groups of BALB-C female mice bearing 4T1 TNBC. The tumors were injected subcutaneously (experiment started with n = 6). Different groups containing PBS (control), 4-MU (concentration of 275 μg/mouse), CuP-HA (CuS levels of 300 μg/mouse), CuP-HA + laser, CuP-HAM (4-MU-275 μg/mouse and CuS-300 μg/mouse), and CuP-HAM + laser were injected in 100 μL of DPBS into the mice via tail vein injection. The groups that included laser irradiation were irradiated at 1064 nm (after 24 h of sample injection) until the temperature reached 48 °C-50 °C. The increase in temperature was continuously monitored using a thermal camera. Tumor diameters and body weight were measured every other day (the former using the vernier calipers) starting from the first day of treatment. After monitoring for 21 days, the tumors were isolated and weighed.

In vivo immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analysis

Following deparaffinization, tissue sections were incubated in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 60 °C for 15 min to perform heat-mediated antigen retrieval. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against iNOS (inducible nitric oxide synthase; Abcam, cat# ab3523), CD206 (Abcam, cat# ab64693), and CD44 (Abcam, cat# ab157107) at 4 °C overnight. All antibodies were diluted in a blocking buffer containing 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBST. After three gentle washes with PBST, sections were incubated with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h; the antibodies had also been diluted in the same blocking buffer. For apoptotic cell detection, TUNEL staining was performed using the Dead End™ Fluorometric TUNEL System (Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Finally, fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) and analyzed accordingly.

Flow cytometry analysis

Tumor single cells were obtained by chopping and enzymatically digesting mixed tumor tissues. After isolation, the cells underwent red blood cell lysis using ACK lysis buffer. The isolated cells were then stained with antibodies targeting CD45, CD4, CD3, CD11b, CD11c, CD86, FOXP3, CD25, and CD80, following the manufacturer's protocols (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). After staining, the cells were fixed with 1 % paraformaldehyde. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer (BD Bioscience, CA, USA), and data was analyzed using FlowJo software (Treestar Inc., OR, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from 4T1 cells after treatment with PBS, 4MU, CuP-HA + Laser, CuP-HAM, CuP-HAM + Laser using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quantity and purity of RNA were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) by measuring absorbance at 260/280 nm. For each sample, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out using a SYBR Green detection system on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each 20 μL reaction mixture contained 10 μL of 2 × PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), 0.5 μM of each forward and reverse primer, 2 μL of diluted cDNA template, and nuclease-free water to the final volume. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. A melt curve analysis was performed at the end of each run to confirm the specificity of amplification.

The relative mRNA expression levels of cuproptosis-associated genes including FDX1, DLAT, LIAS, LIPT1, SLC31A1, and PDHB were quantified. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene for normalization. All reactions were performed in triplicate. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA) and are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). An unpaired t-test was used for two-group comparisons and one-way ANOVA for comparisons among multiple groups.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the nanoparticles

HCuS was synthesized by chemical precipitation using PVP as a surfactant. The bare CuS nanoparticles demonstrated an approximate average size of 150–160 nm (Fig. 1A). This represents the baseline size of the core material, with a lower PDI demonstrating highly dispersed mono-sized particles. The Zeta potential of the HCuS showed a negative surface charge of around −17 to −18 mV (Fig. 1B). Then, 4-MU was loaded into the pores of the HCuS using known solvent evaporation methods. The successful loading of 4 MU was analyzed using HPLC for measuring the drug loading and also the encapsulation efficiency. The encapsulation efficiency of 4MU in HCuS was found to be 76% whereas the drug loading was found to be 38% in the final CuP-HAM particles. Further, HCuS was coated with a highly positively charged PolyMet. The 1H NMR spectrum (400 MHz, d6-dimethylsulfoxide) displayed all characteristic proton signals attributable to the PolyMet moieties. Notably, proton resonances appearing in the range of δ 2.86–3.75 ppm, together with the distinct guanidinium proton signals observed at δ 5.80–6.40 ppm, confirmed the successful formation of the biguanide polymer (Fig. S7). This polymer was coated to provide a positive surface charge, yielding +20 mV, proving the successful coating of the polymer over the metal surface. The size of the CuP-M nanoparticles was slightly larger than that of the bare CuS, indicating that the nanoparticles had been successfully coated with PolyMet and that the 4-MU drug had likely been encapsulated. The size is around 170–180 nm. The CuP-HAM nanoparticles presented a further increase in size, reaching approximately 185–200 nm. This confirmed that the charge interaction had promoted the successful coating of the HA layer onto the PolyMet-coated nanoparticles. The slight increment in size at each step (CuS < CuP-M < CuP-HAM) was consistent with sequential surface modifications and drug loading. Cumulative data showing the size and the PDI is shown in Figure S1 (A-C). The CuP-HAM nanoparticles exhibited a shift back to a negative zeta potential, around −5 to −10 mV; however, these nanoparticles were less negative than the bare CuS. This confirmed the successful coating of HA, which is a negatively charged biopolymer, thereby masking the positive charge of the PolyMet layer and rendering the overall particle surface negatively charged or less positive. Indeed, this is more often preferred for systemic delivery since a negatively charged or less positive particle surface reduces the protein corona formation. The CuP-HAM nanoparticles remained stable over 7 days when suspended in PBS (Fig. 1C). Moreover, the size remained consistently around 200–220 nm throughout the observation period, proving the stability of the synthesized nanoparticles. Nanoparticle Tracking Analyzer (NTA) analysis exhibited the size of the CuP-HAM nanoparticles to be in the range of 160-165 nm in size. This analysis also proved that the concentration of the nanoparticles was found to be 4 × 108 nanoparticles per mg per mL of nanoparticles in water (Fig. S2). UV–Vis–NIR absorption spectra revealed that both CuS and CuP-HAM exhibited broad NIR absorption extending into the NIR-II region, with clear absorbance retained at 1064 nm. Notably, CuP-HAM maintained appreciable absorption at 1064 nm despite surface functionalization, indicating its suitability for NIR-II photothermal excitation (Fig. S3). The FE-TEM images clearly show the hollowness of the nanoparticles (Fig. 1D), also confirming that the size of these nanoparticles ranged between 90 and 120 nm. Further imaging of the CuP-HAM nanoparticles demonstrated a thin layer of the polymers that were coated on the surface of the HCuS, proving that HA had been successfully coated on the final nanoparticles (Fig. 1E and F). Further EDS spectra were recorded to confirm the composition of the synthesized nanoparticles. HCuS was found to have a copper by weight percentage of 57.30%, followed by oxygen at 24.88% and sulfur at 17.82%.

Subsequently, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to analyze the surface elemental composition and chemical valence states. The survey spectrum confirms the presence of copper (Cu), sulfur (S), carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O), with no detectable signals from foreign elements, thereby underscoring the purity of the sample surface. The signals pertaining to the C1s and O1s are likely attributable to adventitious carbon and surface-adsorbed oxygen or hydroxyl species. The high-resolution spectrum for the C 1s was deconvoluted into four components (Fig. 1H). The most prominent peak, centered at a binding energy of approximately 284.8 eV, was attributed to the C–N and/or C–S bonding, while the C–C (∼283.5 eV), C–H (∼281.5 eV), and C=O (∼286.2 eV) peaks were also observed. The high-resolution Cu 2p spectrum displayed two main peaks at ∼932.4 eV and ∼952.2 eV, corresponding to the Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 spin-orbit doublet of Cu2+. The presence of characteristic shake-up satellite features at ∼943 eV further confirmed the Cu2+ oxidation state, while the absence of the strong satellite peak typical of CuO corroborated the XRD phase purity findings. The S 2p spectrum is deconvoluted into S 2p3/2 (∼161.6 eV) and S 2p1/2 (∼162.8 eV) peaks, which are characteristic of the S2− species in the CuS lattice. The absence of signals at higher binding energies (>165 eV) indicates that sulfur is present in a reduced sulfide form, with no detectable sulfate or sulfite species. Similarly, the O 1s spectrum features a broad peak around ∼531.2 eV, which can be ascribed to surface hydroxyls or adsorbed molecular water, rather than lattice-bound oxygen, thereby aligning with the absence of oxides in the XRD profile.

Collectively, the XPS analysis confirms the presence of Cu2+ and S2− as the primary chemical states in the sulfide lattice. However, the significant contributions from C, N, and O species suggest the material is a CuS-based composite or has a modified surface, rather than being pure, stoichiometric CuS. The crystallographic structure and phase purity of the as-synthesized CuS were investigated further using XRD. Notably, the XRD pattern displayed distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 27.7°, 29.3°, 31.8°, 32.8°, 47.9°, and 59.4°. These peaks correspond to the (101), (102), (103), (006), (110), and (116) lattice planes of the hexagonal covellite CuS phase, consistent with JCPDS Card No. 06-0464, thereby confirming the formation of a hexagonal crystalline structure (Fig. S4). The sharpness and high intensity of the diffraction peaks suggest excellent crystallinity and absence of significant amorphous content or secondary phases. No impurity-related diffraction peaks were detected, which confirmed the high phase purity of the material and underscored the efficacy of the synthesis method. Notably, the absence of any diffraction signals from the copper oxides (e.g., CuO or Cu2O) further supports the formation of a pure CuS phase. Nanoparticle's thermal conversion efficiency was tested in response to 1064 nm wavelength laser for 5 min with various concentrations (10, 20, and 30 μg/mL). There was a steady spike in the temperature highly linear across all the concentrations as shown in Fig. S5A. Fig. S5B represents the thermal images of the tube test performed using three different concentrations of HCuS nanoparticles (10, 20, and 30 μg/mL) showing that there's a rapid rise in temperature in 5 min of irradiation. The step-by-step increase is shown in the graph (Fig. S5A). Thermal stability test was performed to analyze the repeatability effect of thermal conversion from laser irradiation for multiple ON/OFF cycles. 5 cycles were performed to measure the thermal conversion ability, and the conversion rate was found to be stable even after 5th cycle. This proves that the material is highly stable in generating heat from laser irradiation even after multiple cycles. Fig. S5C shows the stability of HCuS nanoparticles after multiple cycles of laser irradiation and also the photothermal conversion efficiency of the CuP-HAM nanoparticles were found to be 29.8% (Fig. S6). The photothermal conversion efficiency of CuP-HAM is comparable to or higher than that of commonly reported inorganic photothermal agents, including gold nanorods (≈22%) [33], Fe3O4 nanoparticles (≈25%) [34], and CuS-based systems (≈25–60%) [35], as summarized in previous studies. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements were performed to evaluate the textural properties of pristine CuS nanoparticles and 4-MU–loaded CuS (CuS+4-MU) (Fig. S8). Pristine CuS exhibited a characteristic adsorption–desorption isotherm with a specific surface area of 4.19 m2 g−1, indicative of a porous structure (Fig. S8A). Corresponding BJH pore size distribution analysis revealed a mean pore diameter of 30.45 nm, confirming the presence of mesoporous/hollow features within the CuS framework (Fig. S8B). Following 4-MU loading, CuS+4-MU displayed a reduced nitrogen uptake and a decreased specific surface area of 3.49 m2 g−1 (Fig. S8C). Consistently, the BJH pore size distribution showed a pronounced reduction in mean pore diameter to 18.12 nm (Fig. S8D). The associated decrease in surface area and pore diameter indicates partial occupation of the internal pores by 4-MU molecules rather than collapse of the nanoparticle structure. Collectively, these changes in surface area and pore size distribution provide direct evidence that 4-MU is successfully loaded within the hollow/mesoporous interior of the CuS nanoparticles, supporting the formation of the CuP-HAM nanoplatform.

The cumulative release profile of 4-MU from CuP-HAM (n = 3) was evaluated under physiological (pH 7.4), mildly acidic (pH 6.5), and pH 6.5 with laser irradiation conditions (Fig. S9). CuP-HAM exhibited a sustained release pattern over 72 h under all conditions, with slightly higher cumulative release observed at pH 6.5 compared with pH 7.4. Laser irradiation at pH 6.5 resulted in only a marginal increase in release relative to pH 6.5 alone, with no evidence of burst release.

The HCuS nanoparticles without laser irradiation (black bars) demonstrated minimal cytotoxicity across all tested concentrations, maintaining over 80% cell viability up to 50 μg/mL (Fig. 2A). However, significant cell death was observed in a dose-dependent manner upon 1064 nm laser irradiation (red bars), with viability dropping below 30% at 50 μg/mL. These data confirm the photothermal efficiency of the HCuS nanoparticles in generating localized heat sufficient to induce tumor cell death. Similarly, the CuP-HAM nanoparticles—which encapsulate 4-MU and are surface-modified with PolyMet and HA—exhibited comparable dose-dependent photothermal cytotoxicity upon laser irradiation (Fig. 2B). The presence of HA did not impede internalization or photothermal performance. Instead, the targeting functionality of HA and the ROS-enhancing properties of PolyMet may potentiate the cell-killing effect, as evidenced by a slightly greater cytotoxic response compared to HCuS alone at higher doses.

Therefore, to investigate the nanoparticle internalization efficiency, the 4T1 cells were incubated with coumarin-6 (C6)-labeled nanoparticle formulations for 4 h, and the internal fluorescence level was assessed (Fig. 2C and D). Fluorescence quantification (Fig. 2C) revealed negligible signals in untreated cells (negative control), moderate uptake with C6 alone, and significantly enhanced uptake with CuS + C6 and CuP-HA–C6, indicating that nanoparticle encapsulation facilitates cellular entry. Notably, the CuP-HA–C6 group exhibited the highest mean fluorescence intensity, likely due to CD44 receptor-mediated endocytosis, as the 4T1 cells are known to overexpress CD44. This was corroborated by confocal microscopy (Fig. 2D), where the green fluorescence within the cytoplasm was enhanced in the CuP-HA–C6-treated group. These results underscore the role of HA in facilitating active targeting and cellular internalization, critical mechanisms for maximizing therapeutic efficacy in tumor models.

Finally, to validate the photothermal-induced cytotoxic effects visually, a live/dead viability assay was performed across four groups: PBS (negative control), CuP-HAM, CuS + laser, and CuP-HAM + laser (Fig. 2E and S11). Consistent with the WST results, minimal red fluorescence (indicating dead cells) was observed in the PBS and CuP-HAM-only groups, confirming the low intrinsic toxicity of the nanoparticles in the absence of laser irradiation. Meanwhile, upon laser irradiation, both the CuS + laser and CuP-HAM + laser groups displayed prominent red fluorescence, with the CuP-HAM + laser group presenting the most pronounced cell death. This synergistic effect likely results from the combined impact of photothermal heating, 4-MU-mediated HAS-2 suppression, and metformin-enhanced ROS generation, leading to enhanced tumor cell apoptosis and necrosis. DCFDA staining revealed low basal ROS levels in control and free 4-MU– or CuP-HA–treated cells. CuP-HAM treatment increased intracellular ROS, with the highest DCF fluorescence observed in the CuP-HAM + laser group, indicating markedly enhanced ROS accumulation upon photothermal activation (Fig. S12). Further JC-1 staining was used to assess mitochondrial membrane potential following different treatments (Fig. S13). Control, free 4-MU, and CuP-HA–treated cells predominantly exhibited red JC-1 aggregate fluorescence, indicating intact mitochondrial membrane potential. In contrast, CuP-HA + L treatment resulted in an increased green JC-1 monomer signal, with the CuP-HAM + L group showing the most pronounced red-to-green fluorescence shift, indicating significant mitochondrial membrane depolarization. Quantitative analysis confirmed a marked reduction in the red/green fluorescence ratio in laser-treated CuP-HAM cells compared with all other groups.

Laser-triggered immunogenic cell death and tumor matrix modulation by CuP-HAM nanoparticles

To investigate the immunogenic cell death (ICD)-inducing capacity and tumor microenvironment modulation ability of the developed CuP-HAM nanoparticles, we analyzed the expression of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and key extracellular matrix markers in 4T1 breast cancer cells after various treatments.

Immunofluorescence staining of calreticulin (CRT) and HMGB1, two hallmark indicators of ICD, revealed distinct trends across treatment groups (Fig. 3A and B). Negligible levels of CRT and HMGB1 were observed in untreated cells (PBS group) and those treated with CuP-HA alone, suggesting that neither passive exposure to HA-coated CuS nanoparticles nor the absence of any active payload or photothermal effect is sufficient to induce ICD. Treatment with 4-MU alone resulted in a slight increase in CRT and HMGB1 expression, likely due to the inhibitory effect of this compound on hyaluronan synthase 2 (HAS2), which can alter the tumor extracellular matrix and partially disrupt immune-evasive mechanisms. Interestingly, the application of CuP-HA with NIR laser irradiation (CuP-HA + laser) led to a noticeable elevation in both ICD markers, underscoring the role of PTT in promoting endoplasmic reticulum stress and danger signal release. However, the most significant upregulation of both CRT and HMGB1 was observed in the CuP-HAM + laser group, where the combination of photothermal heating, 4-MU-mediated matrix disruption, ROS production via PolyMet, and CD44-targeted internalization via HA created a potent synergistic effect [36]. The quantitative analysis (Fig. 3C and D) confirmed this observation, revealing statistically significant increases in both CRT surface translocation and HMGB1 release in the CuP-HAM + laser group compared to all other treatments. These findings are consistent with previous studies that demonstrate enhanced ICD following PTT and ROS co-stimulation, particularly in aggressive tumors, such as 4T1, which are otherwise resistant to immunogenic conversion [37].

Meanwhile, the observed DAMP expression suggests that CuP-HAM + laser treatment may help convert the tumor into an immune-responsive phenotype, enhancing antigen presentation and favoring immune cell activation. Such effects are well-documented in the literature, where combinations of thermal damage and immunogenic stressors are shown to promote tumor-specific immunity.

In parallel, we examined the impact of these treatments on the HAS2 and CD44 protein levels via Western blotting (Fig. 3E). HAS2 is a key enzyme responsible for HA synthesis. CD44 is the primary receptor for HAS2 and is expressed on tumor cells, facilitating HA-mediated survival, migration, and immune suppression. As expected, 4-MU significantly downregulated HAS2 expression. More notably, both the CuP-HAM and CuP-HAM + laser treatments produced pronounced suppression of HAS2 and CD44, with the CuP-HAM + laser group exhibiting the strongest reduction. This dual suppression not only confirms the effective delivery of 4-MU into the tumor cells but also highlights the ability of the formulation to disrupt HA–CD44 signaling, which plays a central role in tumor progression, metastasis, and immunosuppression in breast cancer models. The ability of HA-based nanoparticle treatment to downregulate CD44 expression is particularly significant, as recent studies have shown that CD44 upregulation contributes to drug resistance, matrix-mediated immune exclusion, and metastatic seeding. Therefore, by disrupting this axis, CuP-HAM + laser irradiation potentially weakens the structural and signaling support provided by HA within the tumor microenvironment, further sensitizing tumor cells to PTT and immune attack [[38], [39], [40]]. Inhibition of HAS2 by 4-MU leads to a marked reduction in hyaluronan synthesis, which correspondingly decreases the population of CD44-high cancer stem-like cells—a relationship well-documented in breast cancer models [41,42]. Loss of HAS2 also disrupts the HA-rich tumor microenvironment critical for CD44-mediated interactions with tumor-associated macrophages and metastatic niche formation [42]. Clinically, high HAS2 expression is tightly correlated with elevated CD44 signaling and poor tumor prognoses in triple-negative breast cancer, underscoring the pathological importance of the HA–CD44 axis [43]. Mechanistically, CD44 engagement has been shown to drive YAP activity through RhoA activation; therefore, CD44 downregulation following HAS2 inhibition may also attenuate YAP-mediated pro-survival and pro-proliferative signaling [44].

Thus, these results suggest that CuP-HAM nanoparticles, especially under NIR laser irradiation successfully integrate matrix remodeling, tumor targeting, photothermal heating, and ICD induction. The combination of HAS2 and CD44 downregulation, coupled with robust CRT and HMGB1 expression, positions CuP-HAM + laser irradiation as a promising multi-mechanistic therapeutic approach capable of enhancing tumor immunogenicity and disrupting key pathways involved in tumor growth and immune escape.

In vivo antitumor and anti-metastatic efficacy of CuP-HAM nanoparticles in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice

The in vivo biodistribution of Cu was quantified to evaluate the effect of hyaluronic acid (HA) modification on tumor accumulation (Fig. S14). Compared with HA-free nanoparticles (CuP-M), CuP-HAM exhibited significantly higher Cu concentration within tumor tissues, demonstrating enhanced tumor localization upon HA functionalization. This increase in tumor-associated Cu content was consistently observed across biological replicates and reached statistical significance. While both CuP-M and CuP-HAM showed predominant accumulation in reticuloendothelial organs such as the liver, the tumor Cu levels were markedly elevated in the CuP-HAM group, indicating improved tumor selectivity rather than nonspecific systemic distribution. The enhanced tumor accumulation is attributed to HA-mediated CD44 targeting, which facilitates receptor-assisted cellular uptake and retention within the tumor microenvironment. In contrast, HA-free CuP-M nanoparticles exhibited lower tumor Cu levels, suggesting limited active targeting.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that HA surface modification significantly improves tumor accumulation of CuP-HAM, supporting the proposed role of HA in CD44-mediated tumor targeting and providing a mechanistic basis for the enhanced therapeutic efficacy observed in subsequent in vivo studies. Further, in future, long-term clearance of nanoparticles needs to be analyzed to get a clear outcome of biosafety.

The hemolytic activity of CuP-HAM was evaluated using a red blood cell (RBC) lysis assay across a wide concentration range (6.25–300 μg mL−1) (Fig. S21). Compared with the positive control, which induced substantial hemolysis, CuP-HAM caused negligible RBC lysis at all tested concentrations, with hemolysis levels comparable to the negative control. These results demonstrate the excellent hemocompatibility of CuP-HAM, supporting its suitability for systemic administration.

Next, a 21-day animal study was performed using a 4T1 breast tumor-bearing mouse model to evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy and anti-metastatic potential of CuP-HAM nanoparticles. Six groups were studied: PBS (control), 4-MU, CuP-HA, CuP-HA + laser, CuP-HAM, and CuP-HAM + laser.

The schematic of the treatment schedule is outlined in Fig. 4A. The first treatment was administered once the tumors reached ∼100 mm3. Nanoparticles were administered via intravenous injection, and PTT was applied on the following day only to the laser-designated groups using a 1064 nm laser. Tumor progression and systemic toxicity were monitored up to day 21, followed by tissue collection for endpoint analyses. As shown in Fig. 4B, the tumor volumes in the PBS, 4-MU, CuP-HA, and CuP-HA + laser groups exhibited progressive growth, with only moderate suppression in the CuP-HA + laser group. The CuP-HAM treatment alone resulted in partial tumor inhibition, highlighting the effect of 4-MU-mediated HAS2 inhibition and matrix remodeling. Remarkably, the CuP-HAM + laser group demonstrated complete tumor regression without any recurrence over the 21 days. This sharp therapeutic effect is attributed to the synergistic action of PTT-induced hyperthermia, ROS generation, HA-mediated tumor targeting, and 4-MU-driven HA synthesis suppression, which collectively disrupt tumor architecture and induce potent tumor cell death. The representative images of excised tumors from all groups on day 21 are presented in Fig. 4C. Visibly, the CuP-HAM + laser group had no detectable tumor nodules, confirming total regression; this was in contrast to the other groups. Correspondingly, Fig. 4D presents the tumor weights, where a significant reduction was observed in the CuP-HAM + laser group (p < 0.001), further supporting the superior therapeutic efficiency of this treatment combination.

To assess lung metastasis, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on lung tissue sections from all animals (Fig. 4E). Pronounced metastatic nodules were detected in the PBS, 4-MU, CuP-HA, and CuP-HA + laser groups, consistent with the highly metastatic nature of 4T1 tumors. The metastatic lesions in the CuP-HAM group were reduced but remained present. Strikingly, no metastatic foci were detected in the CuP-HAM + laser group, demonstrating a strong anti-metastatic efficacy by this treatment combination. This can be directly attributed to the downregulation of HAS2, which plays a key role in HA production and matrix-assisted tumor cell migration. The disruption in the HA–CD44 axis not only impairs tumor progression but also effectively prevents secondary seeding in distal organs such as the lungs. The same was also proved by India ink staining done in the lungs of the mice as shown in Fig. 4F. The same lungs were used to calculate metastatic nodules in the lungs as white lesions as shown in Fig. 4H. The efficacy of the nanoparticles was also proven by the ultrasound B- Mode imaging. Here the images depict that in PBS, 4-MU, CuP-HA, CuP-HAM groups show a clear hypoxic middle part as shown in Fig. 4G, which is the hallmark of tumor progression. On the other hand, in CuP-HA + Laser group shows a slight increase in the contrast showcasing the oxygen levels in the in tumor center. In the final group, hypoxia is found to be much more reduced due to the combined effect of 4-MU and laser relaxing the thick ECM which was the major cause of hypoxia. The same was quantified using ImageJ software and was plotted in Fig. 4I. Throughout the study, no significant differences in body weight were observed among the groups (Fig. 4J), indicating excellent biocompatibility and the absence of systemic toxicity from the nanoparticles or the laser treatment. The observed stable body masses also reflect the selective tumor ablation ability of the CuP-HAM system without generating off-target side effects.

To evaluate the effect of CuP-HAM on tumor ECM components, tumor sections collected after treatment were subjected to Alcian blue staining to visualize hyaluronic acid (HA)–rich glycosaminoglycans and Sirius Red staining to assess collagen deposition (Fig. S19). In control tumors, intense Alcian blue staining was observed, indicating abundant accumulation of HA-rich matrix within the tumor stroma. Similarly, Sirius Red staining revealed dense collagen fibers distributed throughout the tumor tissue, consistent with a compact and fibrotic extracellular matrix that is characteristic of desmoplastic tumor microenvironments. Treatment with free 4-MU or CuP-HA alone resulted in a no reduction in Alcian blue and Sirius Red staining, suggesting no inhibition of HA synthesis and collagen deposition. However, these effects were spatially heterogeneous and did not lead to substantial ECM depletion.

In contrast, tumors treated with CuP-HAM, particularly when combined with laser irradiation, exhibited a pronounced reduction in Alcian blue staining, indicating effective depletion of HA-rich matrix components. Concurrently, Sirius Red staining showed a marked decrease in collagen density and fiber organization, reflecting significant remodeling of the collagenous tumor stroma. The combined CuP-HAM + laser treatment consistently produced the most prominent reduction in both HA and collagen content among all groups.

These histological findings demonstrate that CuP-HAM effectively disrupts key structural components of the tumor ECM in vivo. The observed ECM remodeling is attributed to the 4-MU–mediated inhibition of hyaluronan synthesis, which weakens the HA-dominated matrix network, while photothermal activation further amplifies stromal disruption. Such ECM depletion is expected to alleviate physical barriers within the tumor microenvironment, thereby facilitating improved immune cell infiltration and enhancing the efficacy of photothermal and immunomodulatory therapies observed in subsequent analyses.

Collectively, these results highlight that the CuP-HAM nanoparticle system, upon NIR laser activation, not only achieves complete primary tumor ablation but also prevents metastatic spread, offering a dual-action therapeutic strategy. The combination of tumor matrix remodeling, PTT, immune modulation, and ROS amplification positions the CuP-HAM + laser treatment as a highly promising nanoplatform for aggressive and metastatic cancers. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) collected after 21 days of treatment revealed well-preserved tissue architecture across all experimental groups (Fig. S20). No obvious signs of inflammation, or structural abnormalities were observed in CuP-HAM–treated mice, including those receiving laser irradiation, compared with control groups. These findings indicate that CuP-HAM does not induce overt systemic toxicity under the applied therapeutic conditions, supporting its favorable in vivo biocompatibility and short-term safety profile.

CuP-HAM-mediated PTT induces immunogenic cell death, macrophage reprogramming, and tumor matrix remodeling in 4T1 tumors

To investigate the underlying mechanisms involved in tumor regression and the immune modulation observed following the CuP-HAM + laser treatment, we performed extensive immunohistochemical analyses of tumor sections collected on day 7, focusing on hallmarks of ICD, apoptosis, immune activation, and tumor matrix remodeling.

Fig. 5A presents immunofluorescent staining of six key markers across all six treatment groups: CRT, HMGB1, TUNEL, CD44, CD206, and iNOS. Meanwhile, Fig. 5B–G shows the corresponding quantification data. The CuP-HAM + laser group exhibited remarkably elevated expressions of CRT and HMGB1, indicative of robust ICD induction (Fig. 5A, B, and C). CRT, a well-known "eat me" signal, and HMGB1, a potent proinflammatory DAMP, were strongly expressed in this group, supporting the earlier in vitro findings and confirming the ability of laser-activated CuP-HAM to trigger immunogenic cell death in vivo. This release of DAMPs is expected to facilitate dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation, which represent critical steps in initiating systemic antitumor immunity. CD44, the HA receptor and a driver of tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and immune evasion, was drastically downregulated in the CuP-HAM + laser group (Fig. 5A and D). This supports the earlier Western blot results and confirms that HAS2 inhibition by 4-MU, combined with HA depletion, can effectively dismantle the HA–CD44 signaling axis, thus reducing tumor cell stemness, metastatic potential, and resistance to immune attack.

Macrophage polarization was assessed by staining for CD206 (M2-like) and iNOS (M1-like) markers (Fig. 5A–E, and F). The CuP-HAM + laser group showed a striking decrease in CD206 expression along with a marked increase in iNOS levels, suggesting a shift from immunosuppressive M2 macrophages to a proinflammatory M1 phenotype. This macrophage repolarization is critical in overcoming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and enhancing antitumor immunity [45,46]. Furthermore, the release of CRT and HMGB1 likely contributes to this reprogramming by stimulating innate immune signaling. TUNEL staining revealed extensive apoptotic DNA fragmentation in the CuP-HAM + laser group, significantly higher than in all other groups (Fig. 5A and G). This confirms that the observed tumor regression is not only due to necrotic ablation via photothermal therapy but also involves programmed cancer cell death, likely amplified by ROS stress and matrix disruption. The integration of PTT-induced heat, 4-MU-mediated matrix remodeling, and PolyMet-driven oxidative damage synergistically promotes cancer cell apoptosis at a profound level.

Quantitative analysis of all markers further validates these observations (Fig. 5B–G). Indeed, the CuP-HAM + laser group demonstrated the highest levels of CRT, HMGB1, iNOS, and TUNEL positivity, coupled with the lowest CD44 and CD206 expression, achieving statistical significance across all comparisons (p < 0.001). These molecular changes comprehensively explain the complete tumor regression and absence of recurrence or metastasis observed in vivo.

CuP-HAM modulates the tumor immune microenvironment

To evaluate the immunomodulatory effects of CuP-HAM, tumor-infiltrating immune populations were analyzed by flow cytometry following treatment with PBS, free 4-MU, CuP-HA, CuP-HA + L, CuP-HAM, and CuP-HAM with laser irradiation CuP-HAM + L (Fig. 6).

Tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) phenotypes were first evaluated based on CD86 (M1-like) and CD206 (M2-like) expression (Fig. 6A and S16). Representative dot plots revealed that PBS-treated tumors exhibited a predominance of CD206+ M2-like macrophages, consistent with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Treatment with free 4-MU or CuP-HA alone resulted in only modest changes in macrophage polarization. In contrast, CuP-HAM treatment led to a noticeable shift toward CD86 expression, indicating partial repolarization of TAMs toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Notably, CuP-HAM combined with laser irradiation (CuP-HAM + L) induced the most pronounced polarization shift, with a substantial increase in CD86+ cells and a concurrent reduction in CD206+ cells. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in the proportion of CD86+ macrophages and a decrease in CD206+ macrophages in the CuP-HAM + L group compared with all control and monotherapy groups (Fig. 6E and F). These findings demonstrate that the synergistic combination of hollow CuS-mediated photothermal therapy and 4-MU–mediated extracellular matrix remodeling effectively reprograms TAMs from an M2-like immunosuppressive state toward an M1-like immunostimulatory phenotype.

Given the importance of dendritic cells (DCs) in antigen presentation, we next examined CD11c+ DC populations within the tumor (Fig. 6B and S17). While PBS and free 4-MU treatments showed minimal DC infiltration, CuP-HAM treatment significantly increased the percentage of CD11c+ cells, an effect that was further amplified upon laser irradiation. To assess DC maturation status, expressions of the co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 was analyzed (Fig. 6C). CuP-HAM + L markedly increased the proportion of CD80+CD86+ mature DCs compared with all other groups, as quantified in Fig. 6G and H. These results indicate that photothermal activation of CuP-HAM not only promotes DC recruitment but also enhances their maturation, which is critical for effective antigen presentation and downstream adaptive immune activation.

Regulatory T cells (Tregs), identified as CD25+FOXP3+ cells, were analyzed to determine whether CuP-HAM treatment could alleviate immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 6D and S18). PBS-treated tumors displayed a high frequency of FOXP3+ Tregs, consistent with immune evasion. Free 4-MU or CuP-HA treatments resulted in only minor reductions in Treg populations. In contrast, CuP-HAM + L significantly reduced the proportion of FOXP3+ Tregs within the CD3+ T-cell population (Fig. 6I), suggesting effective suppression of tumor-associated immune tolerance.

Collectively, these flow cytometric analyses demonstrate that CuP-HAM, particularly when combined with NIR laser irradiation, orchestrates a multifaceted remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. The platform simultaneously promotes M1-like macrophage polarization, enhances dendritic cell maturation, and suppresses regulatory T-cell accumulation. This coordinated immune reprogramming is attributed to the combined effects of localized photothermal stress, reactive oxygen species generation, and hyaluronan synthesis inhibition by 4-MU, thereby overcoming key barriers associated with immune-evasive and metastatic breast tumors.

CuP-HAM mediated apoptosis via copper transfer channel in the mitochondria of 4T1 cancer cells

CuS-mediated photothermal treatment coupled to hyaluronan inhibition via 4-methylumbelliferone functions synergistically to elicit a robust cuproptotic response. qPCR profiling across five treatment groups-PBS, 4-MU, CuPHA + L, CuP-HAM, CuP-HAM + L-illustrates a clear, multi-axis breakdown in mitochondrial metabolism and integrity of the extracellular matrix. CuPHA + L alone reduces DLAT, FDX1, LIPT1, PDHB1, and LIAS significantly; hence, the lipoylation-dependent TCA cycle is directly impaired by the copper release during PTT (Fig. 7A–G). This agrees with the concept of irreversible cuproptotic commitment, at which point lipoylated protein aggregation and mitochondrial proteotoxicity overwhelm any cellular compensatory abilities for metabolic homeostasis. This effect becomes more pronounced with 4-MU. 4-MU itself inhibits not only HAS-1 and HAS-2 but also remodels the tumor matrix by reducing hyaluronan, which allows deeper penetration of nanoparticles and a more even spread of heat. HAS-1 did not show any significant change across the groups whereas HAS-2 levels have been found to be significantly modified in 4-MU and also in CuP-HAM + L group. SLC31A1 is significantly upregulated in copper-treated groups, especially in CuP-HAM + L, indicating increased intracellular copper absorption and heightened Cu+-dependent damage. The combination of CuP-HAM + L creates the most extreme transcriptional phenotype by shutting down the entire lipoylation machinery, raising copper import, and ceasing ECM production all at once. These changes in biochemical environment come together to provide a high-pressure push that drives the cell toward faster, irreversible cuproptosis.

In short, 4-MU transforms the tumor's structural and metabolic vulnerabilities at their root, enabling CuS-PTT to induce deep, system-wide metabolic collapse. The CuP-HAM + L condition presents itself as a strong, mechanism-based scaffold for the leveraging of copper metabolism and extracellular matrix biology toward the attainment of optimal cuproptotic cancer cell lethality.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and evaluated a multifunctional nanoplatform, CuP-HAM, composed of HCuS nanoparticles loaded with the HAS2 inhibitor 4-MU, surface-modified with PolyMet to enhance ROS generation, and finally cloaked with HA for CD44-targeted tumor delivery. It should be noted that, in this work, hyaluronic acid is employed in two mechanistically distinct contexts, exogenous HA is used transiently as a CD44-targeting ligand to enhance nanoparticle uptake, whereas endogenous HA synthesis-driven by HAS2 and responsible for sustained CD44-mediated tumor progression and immune evasion—is selectively inhibited by 4-MU. This unique nanocomposite system was designed to target the tumor microenvironment, induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), modulate immune responses, and prevent metastasis simultaneously, when activated by 1064 nm NIR PTT. Comprehensive characterization (Fig. 1) confirmed the successful synthesis of CuP-HAM nanoparticles with favorable physicochemical properties for systemic administration and photothermal conversion. The in-vitro assays (Fig. 2) revealed that the CuP-HAM nanoparticles were efficiently internalized by 4T1 breast cancer cells via CD44-mediated uptake and exhibited minimal cytotoxicity in the absence of laser irradiation. In contrast, NIR irradiation triggered potent cancer cell death. Live/dead staining and cell viability assays demonstrated photothermal-triggered cytotoxicity, while C6 uptake confirmed targeted nanoparticle delivery. Delving deeper into the mechanistic insights, the CuP-HAM + laser treatment significantly upregulated CRT and HMGB1—two canonical DAMPs—confirming strong ICD induction (Fig. 3). Western blotting further validated that the therapy promoted the downregulation of both HAS2 and CD44, indicating effective suppression of HA synthesis and matrix-related survival pathways. This dual targeting of structural and immunological barriers presents a strategic advantage in overcoming tumor progression. The in vivo therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 4) was profound: CuP-HAM + laser irradiation achieved complete tumor regression with no recurrence across 21 days, while all other groups showed progressive or partially controlled tumor growth. Tumor weights corroborated these findings, and importantly, the body weights of the mice remained stable across all groups, confirming biocompatibility. H&E staining of lung tissues demonstrated that only the CuP-HAM + laser group was free of metastases, strongly correlating the anti-metastatic effect to HAS2/CD44 axis disruption and enhanced ICD-driven immune surveillance. Finally, immunohistochemical analyses (Fig. 5) revealed a highly favorable immune microenvironment reprogramming in the CuP-HAM + laser group. This group exhibited the highest levels of CRT, HMGB1, and TUNEL positivity—indicating a combination of ICD and apoptosis—and simultaneously showed strong downregulation of CD44 and CD206 (M2-like macrophage marker) alongside elevated iNOS (M1-like macrophage marker) expression. These data collectively suggest that CuP-HAM not only eliminates tumors directly via PTT and apoptosis but also remodels the immune milieu to support durable antitumor immunity. Importantly, newly added flow cytometry analyses (Fig. 6) further substantiated the immunomodulatory capacity of CuP-HAM, revealing a pronounced increase in M1-like macrophages (CD86+), enhanced dendritic cell maturation (CD11c+CD80+CD86+), and elevated cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, alongside a marked reduction in immunosuppressive M2-like macrophages (CD206+) and regulatory T cells (CD25+FOXP3+). These quantitative immune profiling data provide robust cellular-level evidence that CuP-HAM combined with laser irradiation actively reshapes the tumor immune landscape toward an antitumor phenotype, complementing the histological and molecular findings (Fig. 7).

In summary, the CuP-HAM nanoplatform integrates matrix remodeling, targeted delivery, ROS amplification, and laser-responsive photothermal therapy to orchestrate a multi-pronged attack on aggressive breast tumors. By simultaneously eradicating the primary tumor, preventing metastatic spread, and reprogramming the tumor microenvironment, this system offers a compelling and clinically translatable strategy for treating metastatic and immunosuppressive cancers, such as TNBC.

Future studies will focus on evaluating the long-term durability of CuP-HAM–mediated antitumor immunity, including tumor rechallenge experiments in cured mice to directly assess immune memory formation. In addition, comprehensive pharmacokinetic profiling, long-term clearance analysis, and chronic toxicity studies will be conducted to further support translational potential. The modular design of CuP-HAM also enables future integration with immune checkpoint inhibitors or other immunotherapeutic modalities to further enhance systemic anti-tumor efficacy.
