Mental Health Emergency Department Visits and Hospitalizations Among Alabama Publicly Insured Youth During COVID‐19
Authors: Hao Zhang, Pradeep Sharma, Theodoros Giannouchos, Jillur Rahim, Itopa Jimoh, David Becker, Justin Blackburn, Ye Liu, Wanda Davis, Michael A. Morrisey, Julie McDougal, Bisakha Sen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIncreases in the proportion of pediatric emergency department (ED) visits due to mental illness (MI) or intentional self‐harm (ISH) in early months of the COVID‐19 pandemic have been documented. Less is known about hospitalizations, or trends during later months of the pandemic. This study assessed changes in MI/ISH‐related ED visits and hospitalizations among enrollees in Alabama's Children’s Health Insurance Program, ALL Kids, during the pandemic.MethodsAdministrative claims data from ALL Kids, were used for enrollees aged 6–17 from 2017 to 2022. The study‐period was divided into pre‐COVID (January 2017–March 2020), early COVID (April 2020–May 2021), and late COVID (June 2021–December 2022). Multivariate logistic regressions were employed to estimate the probabilities that ED visits and hospitalizations were MI/ISH related.ResultsThe study sample included 72,870 ED visits and 8205 hospitalizations for all causes. After adjusting for covariates, the proportion of MI/ISH‐related ED visits increased from 2.5% pre‐COVID to 4.1% in early COVID (p < 0.01), then declined to 2.8% in late COVID. MI/ISH‐related hospitalizations rose from 26.3% pre‐COVID to 31.3% in early COVID and remained elevated at 30.2% in late COVID. Increases were more pronounced among females and older enrollees.ConclusionsThe COVID‐19 pandemic was associated with increased MI/ISH‐related ED visits and hospitalizations among publicly‐insured youth, with hospitalizations remaining elevated later into the pandemic. Shifts in healthcare‐seeking behaviors or admission criteria during the pandemic may have impacted these findings.Relevance to Clinical PracticeFindings on pediatric mental health during the COVID‐19 pandemic can inform efficient health resources allocation for future public health crises. Our findings highlight the need for ensuring adequate access to health care for youth with severe mental health conditions during public health emergencies.

The COVID‐19 pandemic has had a profound impact on mental health globally, with evidence highlighting significant effects on children and adolescents under the age of 18 (1). This age group faced a multitude of challenges during the pandemic, including the abrupt closure of schools, limited social interaction, and disruptions to daily routines, all of which contributed to increased stress, anxiety, depression, and higher risk of intentional self‐harm (ISH) (2, 3, 4). Evidence from multiple studies has confirmed the association between the pandemic and adverse mental health outcomes in youth. A meta‐analysis pooling data from 29 studies suggested that 1 in 4 youth globally experienced symptoms of depression, while 1 in 5 experienced symptoms of anxiety during the pandemic (5). Additionally, rates of suicidal ideation, suicidal attempts, and ISH increased dramatically among children and adolescents, underscoring the pandemic's significant toll on youth mental health (6, 7).

Publicly insured youths represent an especially vulnerable population within this context, as they often face socioeconomic challenges and increased barriers to accessing healthcare services (8). Compared to their privately insured counterparts, publicly insured youths have been shown to experience higher rates of various mental health issues, in part due to structural and environmental risk factors such as financial strain and differential access to supportive resources (9). The COVID‐19 pandemic likely had a heavier impact on the mental health of this population, as national research suggested that youths that were minority or from lower socioeconomic backgrounds experienced greater impairments in mental health during the pandemic (10). While Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP) play a critical role in expanding access to health care in this population, mental health service utilization among youths covered by Medicaid/CHIP declined sharply during the COVID‐19 pandemic, even as mental health needs were increasing (11).

Despite these known vulnerabilities, few studies have used administrative claims data to assess the impact of COVID‐19 on mental health utilization among children and adolescents enrolled in Medicaid or CHIP. To address this gap, we conducted a study to examine trends in mental health‐related ED visits and hospitalizations during the COVID‐19 pandemic among youths enrolled in ALL Kids, the CHIP program in Alabama. This study makes two contributions to the existing literature. First, we examined mental health‐related hospitalizations in addition to ED visits. While numerous studies have examined mental health‐related ED visits during the pandemic (12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18), mental health‐related hospitalizations have received relatively little attention. Hospitalizations are particularly important since they represent the most severe manifestations of mental health conditions and often require significant healthcare resources. Including hospitalizations thus offers a more comprehensive view of both the prevalence and severity of mental health crises among youth during the pandemic. In addition, we used an extended window of observation to examine the mental health of children and adolescents. While existing studies have largely focused on trends in mental health ED visits immediately following the COVID‐19 outbreak (12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18), our data spanned from 2017 to 2022, allowing us to capture trends across different phases of the pandemic. A deeper understanding of the impact of COVID‐19 on pediatric mental health is crucial to assess the longer‐term implications of the pandemic and to promote efficient health resources allocation.

METHODS

Data and Study Population

This study used administrative claims data from Alabama's CHIP program (ALL Kids) from 2017 to 2022, which covers approximately 70,000 to 80,000 individuals each year. ALL Kids coverage is available in 12‐month enrollment periods to Alabama children aged 18 years and younger and with family incomes between 141% and 312% of the federal poverty level (FPL). Our study sample included all ED visits and hospitalizations for ALL Kids enrollees aged 6 to 17 years during the study period. We focused on enrollees aged 6 to 17 years since they represent school‐aged individuals who were more likely to be affected by school disruptions during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The University of Alabama at Birmingham institutional review board approved this study.

Measures

The two primary outcome variables of interest were binary indicators representing whether an ED visit or a hospitalization was related to mental illness (MI)/ISH. ED visits and hospitalizations were identified using a combination of procedure codes, place of service codes, and revenue center codes, consistent with the Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS) specifications (19). Hospitalization episodes were identified using inpatient claims, with two episodes being considered separate if the discharge and admission were at least one day apart. ED visits and hospitalizations were constructed to be not mutually exclusive. ED visits that ended up in hospitalizations were included in both outcome variables.

Since overall healthcare utilization declined during the early months of the pandemic (20), we followed the approach used in previous studies and assessed whether the proportion of ED visits or hospitalizations that were attributable to MI/ISH changed over time (14, 17, 21). MI and ISH related events were identified using the HEDIS Follow‐Up After Hospitalization for Mental Illness (FUH) measure (21, 22). The HEDIS measures are developed by the National Committee for Quality Assurance and include measures across various domains, such as effectiveness, access, and utilization of care. MI, as defined in the FUH measure, includes the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) diagnosis codes for various mental health conditions such as mood disorders, personality disorders, and anxiety disorders. ISH captures instances of suicide attempt or self‐harm through means such as poisoning, cutting, and other methods. Both ED visits and hospitalizations were coded as MI/ISH related if the ICD‐10 codes used by HEDIS for MI or ISH were present in the primary diagnosis field of the administrative claims.

We categorized the study period into three distinct periods: the pre‐COVID phase (January 2017–March 2020); the early‐COVID phase (April 2020–May 2021); and the late‐COVID phase (June 2021–December 2022) (23). The early‐COVID phase started with statewide school closure and captured the immediate shock of the pandemic. While school closure lasted for less than 3 months for most schools in Alabama, this phase was marked by hybrid learning models, intermittent quarantines, and broader social and healthcare system disruptions. In contrast, the late‐COVID phase represents the period when most public health restrictions had been lifted and schools were fully reopened. In the statistical analyses, we controlled for individual‐level covariates including age, race/ethnicity, sex, ALL Kids eligibility group and urban/rural residence. Race/ethnicity was categorized into three groups: non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, and other. ALL Kids eligibility has three categories: low fee group (141%–151% FPL), fee group—which includes those 152%–203% FPL and those 204%–312% FPL (the latter is referred to as the expansion group) and a no fee group, which is primarily comprised of Native Americans who are federally exempted from cost sharing. We combined the low fee and no fee groups (hereafter referred to as the no or low fee group) in the analysis due to the small sample sizes, while treating the expansion group separately from the fee group. Rural or urban residence was determined using the Rural Urban Commuting Area (24) designation based on the enrollee's ZIP code. We also included the Child Opportunity Index (COI) in our analyses. The COI is a composite measure developed to assess the geographic disparity in the neighborhood resources that affect children's well‐being, such as education, healthcare, housing, and environmental factors (25). To simplify the analysis, we categorized the state‐normed COI into two groups: high (including very high and high categories from the original classification) and low (including moderate, low, and very low).

Statistical Analysis

Multivariate logistic regression models were used, with binary indicators for MI/ISH‐related ED visits and hospitalizations as the outcome variables, and the COVID‐19 phase (pre‐COVID, early‐COVID, and late‐COVID) as the primary explanatory variable. The models controlled for individual demographic and socioeconomic factors, including age, sex, race, ALL Kids eligibility group, urban/rural residence, and COI. We also controlled for quarter fixed effects to account for seasonality change in the outcomes in all logistic models, as well as the number of months the patient was enrolled in the ALL Kids program in each calendar year to account for churn, coverage loss, and potential anticipatory behaviors which could affect ED and hospitalization use. The predicted probabilities of outcome measures at each phase were calculated using marginal effects, which indicated the adjusted, average difference in the predicted probability of the outcome for each COVID‐19 phase relative to the reference category (pre‐COVID). We further conducted interaction analysis to examine whether the impact of COVID‐19 on MI/ISH‐related ED visits and hospitalizations varied across sociodemographic characteristics.

We note that estimates from the multivariate logistic regression models might be biased due to pre‐existing trends in the outcomes. To assess the validity and sensitivity of our estimates, we conducted a single‐group interrupted time series analysis, as an alternative modeling strategy, which accounts for existing trends and changes in both the level and slope of the outcomes across different COVID‐19 phases.

Our study sample was constructed using SAS 9.4, and all statistical analyses were performed in Stata 18/SE. In all statistical models, we accounted for potential within‐group correlation by clustering the error terms at the ZIP code level. Statistical significance was determined using a threshold of p < 0.05.

RESULTS

The study sample consisted of 72,870 ED visits from 39,661 enrollees and 8205 hospitalizations from 5850 enrollees. The monthly count of ED visits and hospitalizations throughout the study period are shown in Figure 1, and the monthly count of unique enrollees are presented in Appendix Table S1. Compared to the pre‐COVID phase, ED visits during the early‐COVID and late‐COVID phases were more likely to involve individuals from the expansion groups (34.2%, 39.6%, and 42.9% for pre‐COVID, early COVID, and late COVID respectively, p < 0.01), and less likely to involve non‐Hispanic Blacks (27.9%, 22.9%, and 24.1%, p < 0.01). Hospitalizations during the early‐ and late‐COVID phases also had a higher representation of individuals from the expansion groups (38.6%, 43.1%, and 43.0%, p < 0.01), though differences in race were not significant. Notably, both ED visits (2.5%, 4.4%, and 2.8%, p < 0.01) and hospitalizations (26.1%, 32.1%, and 30.1%, p < 0.01) were more likely to be primarily related to MI/ISH in the early‐ and late‐COVID phases compared to pre‐COVID phase (Table 1).

After adjusting for covariates, multivariate logistic regression estimates revealed a significant increase in the proportion of ED visits related to MI/ISH during the early‐COVID phase, rising from 2.5% in the pre‐COVID phase to 4.1% (change in the predicted probability or “marginal effect” 1.6 percentage points, p < 0.01). However, this proportion declined to 2.8% in the late‐COVID phase, nearly returning to pre‐COVID levels but still statistically significantly higher (marginal effect 0.4 percentage points, p < 0.05). In contrast, the proportion of hospitalizations related to MI/ISH increased from 26.3% in the pre‐COVID phase to 31.3% in the early‐COVID (marginal effect 5.0 percentage points, p < 0.01), and remained elevated in late‐COVID phases (30.2%, marginal effect 3.9 percentage points, p < 0.01) (Figure 2). Throughout the study period, older age, female, and urban residence were associated with a higher likelihood of MI/ISH‐related ED visits and hospitalizations, while non‐Hispanic Black race/ethnicity was inversely associated with MI/ISH‐related cases in both settings (results not shown).

The analyses to assess potential differences across sociodemographic subgroups revealed that the COVID‐19 pandemic had a greater impact on MI/ISH‐related ED visits and hospitalizations among older teens, though this difference diminished during the late‐COVID phase (Figures 3 and 4). Additionally, female sex was associated with a greater increase in the proportion of both MI/ISH‐related ED visits and hospitalizations, but only hospitalization was this statistically significant. Rural residence and non‐Hispanic Black race/ethnicity were associated with a smaller increase in the proportion of MI/ISH‐related ED visits and hospitalizations, but these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Results from the interrupted time series models suggest that, for both ED visits and hospitalizations, there was an increasing trend in the proportion related to MI/ISH during the early COVID‐19 phase, which subsequently leveled off or began to decline in the late COVID phase (Appendix Figures S1 and S2).

DISCUSSION

While several studies have looked at MI/ISH‐related pediatric ED visits during the early months of the COVID19 pandemic, MI/ISH pediatric hospitalizations have received little attention. Further, trends in MI/ISH‐related ED and hospitalizations beyond the early months of the COVID19 pandemic are also largely unexplored. In this study, we contribute to the literature by examining the proportion of ED visits and hospitalizations related to MI/ISH throughout different phases of the COVID‐19 pandemic period among ALL Kids‐enrolled pediatric patients in Alabama. Study findings revealed that the early‐COVID phase was associated with a significant increase in the proportion of both MI/ISH‐related ED visits and hospitalizations. Although this proportion declined and approached pre‐pandemic levels in the late‐COVID phase for ED visits, the proportion of MI/ISH‐related hospitalizations remained significantly elevated in the late‐COVID phase. These results suggest that the pandemic was associated with sustained changes in youth mental health, particularly in terms of severe cases requiring hospitalization.

Our findings align with previous studies which have consistently reported a significant increase in the proportion of ED visits related to mental health conditions during the COVID‐19 pandemic. For example, one study using data from the National Syndromic Surveillance Program found that the proportion of ED visits related to mental health conditions increased significantly from 2% before COVID to over 2.5% after COVID among the general population (13). Similar findings were reported by another study focusing specifically on children, which showed that the proportion of mental health–related ED visits for children aged 5–11 and 12–17 years increased approximately 24% and 31%, respectively in 2020 compared to 2019 (17). Our study adds to the existing literature by demonstrating that the impact of COVID‐19 extended beyond ED utilization with the inclusion of hospitalizations in our analysis. Therefore, our findings suggest that the pandemic was associated with a greater burden of mental health problems. Further, the disruptions in usual care may have contributed to increased vulnerability among youth experiencing mental health problems. In a prior study using ALL Kids data we found that approximately 13% of enrollees used telehealth during the early COVID‐19 phase, with mental health services accounting for approximately one‐third of these telehealth claims (26). Further, among ALL Kids enrollees who received follow‐up care within 30 days of a mental health‐related hospitalization, almost a quarter was through telehealth (27). However, substantial barriers to accessing care via telehealth among pediatric patients in Alabama ‐ especially those from low‐income families ‐ had also been reported during COVID‐19 (28), along with significant disparities in telehealth use by race/ethnicity, family income and place of residence (26, 29). Given these challenges, the observed increase in the proportion of MI/ISH ED and inpatient episodes might have been driven by lack of access to alternate means of routine and timely care. Addressing barriers in access to telehealth services, such as expanding broadband access and increasing the number of telehealth providers, may help improve access to mental health services and reduce the needs for hospitalizations.

The proportion of MI/ISH‐related ED visits declined towards pre‐pandemic levels during the late‐COVID‐19 phase, when most schools in Alabama re‐opened with in‐person classes. While there is extensive research on the association between school closures and deteriorating mental health among children and adolescents, studies examining the impact of school reopening remain scarce and had mixed findings (30, 31). As stated earlier, our study uses school reopening as a proxy measure for normalizing of routines and lifting of restrictions. It is possible that such normalization helped alleviate some of the stress and isolation that led to increased MI/ISH needs during the early‐COVID phase. However, it is important to note that part of the decrease in the proportion of MI/ISH‐related ED visits may be a result of an increase in ED visits for other medical conditions as healthcare utilization bounced back in the latter months of the pandemic, as suggested by our findings.

In contrast to ED visits, the proportion of MI/ISH‐related hospitalizations remained significantly elevated throughout the late‐COVID phase. This suggests that COVID‐19 may have had a longer‐lasting impact on the mental health of individuals with severe manifestations of mental health conditions compared to those with milder symptoms. It is also possible that lifting of restrictions while the pandemic was still on‐going may have been less helpful—or indeed, even harmful—for those with most severe cases of MI (32). Further research is needed to understand the divergence in patterns of MI/ISH ED visits versus MI/ISH hospitalizations in the later months of the pandemic. Overall, our findings on the persistent increase in MI/ISH‐related hospitalizations during the pandemic highlight the need for health system preparedness and targeted planning. Health systems and policymakers could use these findings to inform health resource allocation and prioritize early interventions during future crises.

Consistent with previous studies (4, 14, 15, 17, 21, 33), we also found that ALL Kids enrollees who were female, and older experienced a more pronounced increase in the proportion of ED visits and hospitalizations associated with MI/ISH during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The heightened impact on female enrollees aligns with existing literature indicating that young females are more likely to experience anxiety and depression (34), which may manifest in acute crises requiring emergency care. Furthermore, compared to elementary school students, those in middle and high school face unique stressors, such as academic pressures, peer relationships, and social isolation, all of which contribute to their increased vulnerability to mental health issues. These findings suggest that associations between COVID‐19 and MI/ISH‐related healthcare utilization varied across demographic groups, highlighting the importance and need for targeted interventions. Implementing school‐based mental health services, particularly in middle and high schools, that can identify mental health issues early‐on while taking into account and tailored to the distinct needs of various student subgroups, may provide an important option to address the increasing mental health problems among older youths (35, 36).

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, we focused on the percentage of MI/ISH‐related ED visits and hospitalizations among all ED visits and hospitalizations to characterize the mental health burden of the pandemic and recognize that this measure is sensitive to changes in the overall utilization of healthcare services during the pandemic. Second, our analysis is limited by its observational nature, and the lack of a comparison group of patients who were not subject to the COVID‐19 pandemic, which restricts our ability to establish causal relationships between the COVID‐19 pandemic and changes in MI/ISH‐related ED visits or hospitalizations. Third, we did not require a baseline period to capture preexisting conditions. Hence while the literature suggests that the COVID‐19 pandemic had a disproportionately larger impact among individuals with preexisting severe mental health disorders (37, 38), we cannot decipher which patients had a prior history of mental health disorders. Unmeasured factors, such as regional variations in pandemic responses or healthcare access within Alabama, as well as shifts in healthcare‐seeking behavior and hospital admission criteria during the COVID‐19 pandemic, may also influence ED and hospitalization for MI/ISH or other causes. Fourth, we define the “late COVID‐19” period for the entire state using the most common timing of school reopening, and recognize that there can be other ways to define this period that yield somewhat different dates. Fifth, the low fee and no fee groups may face substantially different cost‐sharing for health services. For example, the copay for inpatient hospital stay is $200 for the low fee groups, compared to $0 for the no fee group. Therefore, combining these groups in the analyses may mask important differences. Finally, our data are limited to ALL Kids‐enrolled minors in Alabama, which may limit the generalizability of our findings, but at the same time these findings should be especially relevant for the several other states with similar standalone CHIP programs, including the southern states of Mississippi, Georgia, and Texas, which share many of Alabama's socio‐demographic‐cultural characteristics.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, our study demonstrates significant changes in MI/ISH‐related ED visits and hospitalizations during different phases of the COVID‐19 pandemic among ALL Kids‐enrolled youths in Alabama. While the proportion of ED visits for MI/ISH returned to pre‐pandemic levels in the late‐COVID phase, hospitalizations for these conditions remained elevated, suggesting a prolonged effect on cases with intensive use of health services. Our findings underscore the critical need for ongoing mental health support and interventions, particularly for those requiring inpatient care, as the long‐term effects of the pandemic continue to unfold. By identifying subpopulations that experienced the largest increases in MI/ISH‐related healthcare utilization during the pandemic period, this study provides a foundation for strategic resource planning.
