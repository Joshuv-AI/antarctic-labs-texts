Engineered CD4 T cells for in vivo delivery of therapeutic proteins
Authors: Harikrishnan Radhakrishnan, Sherri L. Newmyer, Harold S. Javitz, Parijat Bhatnagar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceWe’ve addressed the challenge of achieving effective disease treatment with safe doses of complex biologics with otherwise short half-lives. Unlike conventional methods that require frequent dosing of synthetic carriers and posing harm to healthy tissues through systemic distribution, we engineered CD4 T cells to express therapeutic proteins precisely at the disease site. The CD4 T cell–based delivery system, exemplified here with IFNβ for antitumor effects, outperforms other cells, such as CD8 T cells, in several aspects: superior transduction efficiency, accelerated expansion rates, and heightened therapeutic protein production. The reduced cytolytic activity of the CD4 T cells, compared to CD8 T cells opens the possibility of using a higher maximum recommended starting dose for clinical trials.

Current challenges that impair treatments for many diseases include our inability to deliver drugs at the exact time and location, prevent rapid clearance of drugs, prevent side effects from excess drug levels, and prevent drug resistance due to suboptimal drug levels. The status quo in delivery platforms entails packaging drugs into synthetic carriers (e.g., liposomes, nanoparticles, hydrogels, pegylated proteins) (1, 2). The doses are normalized to body weight or surface area and do not account for variability among patients or diffusion to the healthy tissues (3, 4). Their release follows first-order kinetics, i.e., concentration of the drug released decreases over time (5). Their bioavailability is limited by their short half-life and reduction in activity due to pegylation, and they require multiple infusions. Real-time monitoring and dosing of individual disease microenvironments is currently not possible (6, 7). Suboptimal drug levels have an inadequate effect on more aggressively growing disease cells allowing them to grow unchecked, acquiring mutations, and evolving into a drug-resistant disease over time (8, 9). The host-immune response (i.e., the adaptive and humoral immune responses toward synthetic carriers) may lead to a long-term immunological memory makes them ineffective for subsequent use. By contrast, overdosing, in an attempt to compensate, causes morbidity by diffusing into healthy tissues (10, 11). Current treatments are, therefore, governed by the negative impact of such therapies on healthy tissues rather than by their efficacy against the diseased cells. Calibrated delivery of drugs to individual disease microenvironments with spatiotemporal resolution is a challenge common to all cell-based diseases such as cancers, autoimmune disorders, and viral infections.

Our mission is to develop a drug-delivery platform, a zero-order delivery system, that will persist in the body for a long duration and deliver drugs specifically at the disease sites, proportionate to the disease mass, without affecting healthy tissues and can be readily redirected for treating a range of diseases. Toward this goal, we recently reported on engineering primary T cells for cell-mediated synthesis of protein-based biologics at the disease site (12, 13). When configured for drug delivery, T cells have a distinct biological advantage because they chemotactically extravasate to the disease site with cellular resolution and engage the target diseased cells with molecular specificity. At the single-cell level, a T cell activates in a binary (on/off) event when it encounters a threshold antigen density (14–17), on the surface of the target cell and executes a parallel program resulting in clonal expansion of the activated T cells (18, 19). This biology of the T cell enabled us to reprogram it into a cell-based platform that activates upon engaging disease cells in an antigen-specific manner, clonally expands, and synthesizes desired proteins proportionate to the disease burden and specifically at the disease site (13, 20, 21). The present report addresses two challenges for translating this technology into a clinically relevant system: 1) developing a process that can be scaled up for producing primary T cells engineered for delivering therapeutic proteins and 2) increasing the synthesis of desired protein from the primary T cell to reduce the number of cells required to produce the desired effect.

The foundation of our zero-order T cell–based delivery system is a synthetic pathway that includes an antigen-specific extracellular portion of the transmembrane chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) that, upon identifying the antigen on the target cell, mobilizes the intracellular nuclear factor of the activated T cell (NFAT) signaling pathway and induces the expression of the desired protein through the NFAT response element (NFAT-RE). While recent advances have shown the potential of utilizing the synthetic pathways for cell-mediated expression of desired proteins, this approach is primarily focused on using these proteins to enhance the effects of cells engineered to produce them (22–27). However, these engineered cells face limitations when it comes to exerting extracellular functions, primarily due to challenges related to limited expression of the synthetic proteins and the scalability of their manufacturing. To address these limitations, we hypothesized that employing helper CD4 T cells, as opposed to pan CD3 T cells, which include both CD4 and CD8 T cells, could offer a solution. The formulation of this hypothesis was based on the fact that phosphorylation of the 53SSPS56 motif in the NFAT transactivation domain in antigen-stimulated killer CD8 T cells is impaired and this limits the ability of NFAT transcriptional machinery (28) to signal through the NFAT-RE (Fig. 1). Additionally, initiating the process with the CD4 T cell population is crucial, as commencing with the pan CD3 T cell population, which contains a mixture of CD4 and CD8 T cells, eventually results in a predominance of CD8 T cells with compromised NFAT transcriptional machinery, as mentioned earlier.

Schematic of the T cell delivery platform. CD4 and CD8 T cells were engineered for the delivery function when activated by antigen-presenting cells.

This study effectively illustrates that CD4 T cells, when compared to their CD8 counterparts, possess an enhanced capacity for transporting specific proteins to sites of disease. Through incorporation of a CAR with the 4-1BB intracellular domain, while excluding the CD28 intracellular domain, CD4 T cells exhibit a threefold improvement in transduction efficiency, double the rate of expansion, and a fivefold increase in the expression of target proteins. We verified the efficacy of our CD4 T cell-based delivery system using a CAR that recognizes Folate Receptor alpha (FRα) as an antigenic target, prompting the cell to induce a bioluminescent reporter enzyme. As demonstrated in our prior work (20, 21, 29), the adaptable design of the CAR permits us to redirect the platform’s specificity to recognize alternative antigens. To validate our CD4 T cell-based approach for delivering functional therapeutics, we used it to deliver interferon-β (IFNβ) and exerted antitumor effects in vivo in intraperitoneally (i.p.) implanted ovarian cancer cells.

Results

The phenotype of the T cell used in the delivery platform directly influences its therapeutic outcome by affecting both the efficient production of a dosage suitable for clinical use and the synthesis of therapeutic proteins produced from the cell within the targeted location. We therefore explored the dynamics of CD4 and CD8 T cells in vitro for transduction, expansion, and antigen-induced delivery function (Fig. 2). The initial CD4 to CD8 ratio in freshly isolated primary CD3 T cells from peripheral blood strongly favors CD4 (CD4:CD8 = 7:1, representative data in SI Appendix, Fig. S1) and exhibits minimal shift until day 10. However, when expanded for longer durations that is generally required to obtain cell numbers for a clinically relevant dose and as documented in the literature (30, 31), a shift toward CD8 T cells is observed (32, 33). A similar observation was made by us when CD3 T cells were expanded for 25 d after transduction (CD4:CD8 = 1:2 on day 25, Fig. 2A). While this shift may not be a concern when aiming to maximize cytolytic CD8 T cell production, as is often the goal and a deterministic factor in the success of CAR T cell therapies (34), it may not be the ideal approach when engineering these cells for a delivery function that relies on the NFAT pathway. This is due to the impaired NFAT pathway in CD8 T cells, as previously mentioned, which limits their antigen-induced ability to express the engineered protein (28). Indeed, when starting with the CD3 T cell population (CD4:CD8 = 7:1 on day 0) from the same donor (P < 0.01, at all points beyond day 0), we only obtained ~7-fold expansion. By contrast, when culturing primary CD4 T cells alone, as shown in Fig. 2B, we observed a ~14-fold expansion in 8 d after transduction.

Our results comparing transduction efficiencies of CD4 and CD8 phenotypes (Fig. 2C) further support our choice of engineering the CD4 phenotype. The CD3 population was reconstructed by combining CD4 and CD8 T cells at a 1:1 ratio. Our results in Fig. 2C show that the transduction efficiency of the CD4 T cells was ~3-fold higher than that for CD8 T cells and ~1.5-fold higher than the representative CD3 population (CD4:CD8 = 1:1). To account for the variance due to human genetic diversity, we repeated the experiment three times with three human donors (represented by different symbols). All transductions were performed using the same process and the efficiency of transduction was determined after 5 d by assessing the percentage of engineered T cells. It is therefore noteworthy that compared to the CD8 T cells, the CD4 T cells not only expand faster (~2-fold, Fig. 2B) but also transduce more easily (~3-fold, Fig. 2C). We also observed that when compared to the CD8 T cells, CD4 T cells exhibit a stronger tendency to chemotactically migrate toward chemokines CCL5 and CCL17 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). These chemokines are naturally enriched in the tumor microenvironment and are known to play a major role in T cell recruitment to the tumor sites (35–39). This finding is in consensus with previous reports, where antigen-reactive CD4 T cells have been reported to efficiently infiltrate into immunologically cold tumors (40) and render the tumor microenvironment receptive to cytotoxic CD8 T cells, resulting in efficient tumor rejection (41).

Our experiments in Fig. 2 A–C initially used a 3rd-generation CAR that included the intracellular domains of CD28 and 4-1BB in addition to the CD3ζ domain. In the next steps, we assessed three combinations of the intracellular CAR domains that could improve performance of CD4 T cells and its potential to serve as a delivery platform. The results are documented in Fig. 2 D and E. The three CAR constructions included intracellular domains from i) CD28 only (28ζ), ii) 4-1BB only (BBζ), and iii) CD28 and 4-1BB in tandem (28-BBζ). Complete schematics of the individual CAR constructs are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S3. Our results indicate that, while transduction efficiency (Fig. 2D) of the CD4 T cell is independent of the combination of the intracellular domains used in the CAR, the expression level of the engineered protein (represented by a bioluminescent reporter enzyme, Fig. 2E), depends on these domains. Our findings showed that the BBζ CAR (i.e., the CAR that included the 4-1BB intracellular domain but not the CD28 intracellular domain), was more effective in inducing the engineered reporter enzyme in both CD4 and CD8 T cell delivery platforms. Kinetic reporter activity assay (spanning over 2 to 48 h after stimulation) comparing the 3 different CAR systems confirmed the superior delivery function (reporter activity) of the CD4 T cell delivery platform expressing BBζ CAR (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The other two CARs (i.e., 28ζ, 28-BBζ) induced the reporter activity to a similar but lesser extent. Moreover, the reporter enzyme activity induced by the BBζ CAR was ~5-fold more in the CD4 T cell delivery platform compared to that in the CD8 T cell delivery platform and was specific in response to the antigen-presenting target cell (FRα+OVCAR3 cell, in this case). This is interesting because, compared to the 28ζ CAR, the BBζ has also been reported to impart other desirable traits in the CAR T cells, such as increased persistence (42–44), reduced tonic signaling (45), and better toleration by patients in terms of cytokine release syndrome and cell therapy-associated neurotoxicity (46).

Next, we verified our hypothesis that the reduced expression of an engineered protein when using NFAT-RE transcriptional start sites in the CD8 T cell delivery platform was due to the impaired NFAT-based transcriptional machinery (28). Alternatively, this could have resulted from the reduced antigenic stimulation due to the cytolytic action of the FRα-specific CD8 T cell delivery platform against the antigen-presenting target cells (20, 21). To test this, we replaced the FRα-presenting target OVCAR3 cells with microbeads (2.8 μm diameter) functionalized with human FRα antigen and antihuman CD28 antibodies. Similar microbeads functionalized with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies served as the positive control, and unstimulated CD4 and CD8 T cells (engineered for delivery function) were used as negative controls. The use of microbeads in this assay eliminates the loss of stimulation due to cytolysis of the antigen-presenting target cells by the CD8 T cells. Our results (Fig. 2F) show ~5-fold higher activity (i.e., higher expression) of the bioluminescent reporter in the CD8 T cells and ~12-fold activity in the CD4 T cells. Our results show that while NFAT-regulated expression in CD8 T cells is present, such expression is about ~7-fold higher in the CD4 T cells. This validates that the CD4 T cell delivery platform has more pronounced NFAT-based transcriptional machinery required for the delivery function, which is impaired in the CD8 T cells.

The data shown in Fig. 2G verify that the CD4 T cells engineered for delivery function, using FRα specific CAR and Nluc bioluminescent reporter enzyme, exhibited minimum cytolytic activity. Compared to the CD8 T cells, they can thus be administered at an increased dose for a higher maximum recommended starting dose in first-in-human clinical trials. In this experiment, we incubated CD4 and CD8 T cells, both engineered for FRα specific CAR expression, with target cells engineered to present FRα [i.e., FRα+A2780cis cells, as described before (47)]. The target cells were also engineered to express the Luc2® enzyme, an ATP-dependent bioluminescent reporter that serves as a live-cell marker. Nonengineered primary CD3 T cells were used as a negative control. Our results confirm the significantly low cytolytic activity of the CD4 T cell delivery platform compared to the CD3 T cell delivery platform with specificity toward the same antigen (FRα) (CD4:CD8 = 1.5:1) (P < 0.01, at all E:T), and attest to our selection of the CD4 T cell as the suitable phenotype for use in our cell-based delivery system. The engineered CD4 T cells can therefore be delivered at an increased tolerated dose to robustly express the desired protein without exhibiting the undesired side effect of killing healthy cells that may be expressing basal levels of the target antigen.

To verify the engineered function of our CD4 T cell delivery platform in vivo, we used immunodeficient mice with i.p. tumors who were treated with our CD4 T cells engineered to express Nluc reporter enzyme to validate it for the delivery function (Fig. 3). An i.p. model using ovarian cancer cell line, A2780cis, with ectopic expression of target antigens was used 1) to study antigen specific delivery function and 2) to mirror the peritoneal metastasis commonly seen in ovarian cancer patients. The target specific delivery function, i.e., Nluc activity (Fig. 3B) was imaged and quantified (Fig. 3C) at baseline (day 0) as well as on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5.

The engineered CD4 T cells with specificity for FRα antigen (Fig. 3C), as previously demonstrated (13) showed enhanced delivery function. Additionally in Fig. 3C, we showed target-specific in situ Nluc reporter activity when stimulated by FRα+ tumors, compared to when stimulated by nontarget cells, i.e., FRαneg tumors. Additionally, by using negative controls [CD4 T cells with the same inducible delivery function but without a CAR (No CAR-Nluc); FRα-specific CD4 CAR (CAR-BBζ) T cells without the NFAT-RE inducible delivery function], we validated the antigen-specific in situ delivery function of our CD4 T cell delivery platform. An increase in the delivery function, as indicated by the increase in inducible reporter activity, was observed in the Nluc producing FRα-CAR+ CD4 T cells, compared to the control groups (Fig. 3 B and C). Indeed, our results indicate the potential for implementing our previously published cell-based antigen (48) and serology (49) testing in the CD4 T cell platform for in vivo sensing. The technology may therefore be used for monitoring disease progression, assessing therapeutic response, and delivering therapeutic proteins in situ.

In the next step, we verified our CD4 T cell delivery platform (Fig. 4) by exchanging the nucleotide sequence of the bioluminescent reporter enzyme with an antitumor cytokine, interferon-beta (IFNβ) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). Using microbeads functionalized with FRα antigen and anti-CD28 antibodies, we reconfirmed our previous observation that CD4 T cells can be used for synthesizing engineered therapeutic proteins via the NFAT transcriptional machinery (Fig. 4A). Compared to pan CD3 T cells and killer CD8 T cells, engineered for the delivery function, the helper CD4 T cell-based delivery platform produced twofold more IFNβ confirming its enhanced antigen-specific delivery function. The secretion of IFNβ from the respective negative controls (unstimulated CD3, CD4, and CD8 T cells, all engineered for IFNβ delivery) was minimal and is shown in Fig. 4A. The results in Fig. 4B show growth-inhibitory effect of IFNβ on various cell lines [Melanoma (SK-MEL-147, WM88, SEKI), Breast (CAL-51, SUM52PE), Ovarian (OVCAR3, A2780cis) cancer cell lines and noncancer cell line HEK293T/17], when treated with the supernatant of stimulated CD4 and CD8 T cells engineered to produce IFNβ. We observed the growth-inhibitory effects on five (SK-MEL-147, WM88, SUM52PE, A2780cis, and OVCAR3) out of eight cell lines, necessitating the need for prescreening the tumors for IFNβ sensitivity, prior to considering this approach as a therapeutic option.

Finally, to demonstrate the therapeutic utility of our IFNβ-producing CD4 T cell platform in vivo, we used them to challenge antigen positive (FRα+Luc2+A2780cis) tumors. The experiment schedule is detailed in Fig. 4C and the results are shown in Fig. 4 D–F. FRα+Luc2+A2780cis cells (2x106) were i.p. implanted in NSG mice. The 12-day-old xenograft tumors were i.p. treated with 5 × 106 FRα-CAR+ T cells (with NFAT-RE inducible IFNβ) daily for 6 d (day13 to day19). The therapeutic efficacy was assessed by imaging (Fig. 4D) and quantitatively comparing the tumor luminescence (Fig. 4E) with control groups [i.e., 5 × 106 FRα-CAR+ T cells without NFAT-RE inducible IFNβ; 0.25 µg recombinant human IFNβ (rhIFNβ)]. A significant reduction in tumor mass, as indicated by the reduction in luminescence, was observed in the IFNβ producing FRα-CAR+ T cells, compared to the control FRα-CAR+ T cells that did not produce IFNβ, indicating that our CD4 T cell delivery platform indeed produces functional therapeutics (Fig. 4E). See Appendix A in Supporting Information for our calculation indicated that the IFNβ dosage delivered by CD4 T cells was ~300-fold less than the directly administered rhIFNβ, yet more effective. The results in Fig. 4F show statistically significantly improved survival in mice treated with IFNβ producing FRα-CAR+ T cells, when compared to the direct injection of rhIFNβ and control FRα-CAR+ T cells that did not produce IFNβ. The rhIFNβ treatment did not show any survival advantage and all mice were euthanized (SRI Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee # 22001) as a result of weight loss or distress. No apparent toxicity, as indicated by the body weight, associated with the treatment was observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

Discussion

We have capitalized on the biology of CD4 T cells and transformed them into a cell-based platform that can assess the disease burden and mount a proportional response. Our data using reporter protein show the potential of using this technology for expressing engineered therapeutic proteins precisely at the disease site. We demonstrate that the helper CD4 T cell offers a clear competitive advantage over the killer CD8 T cell or a combined pool of CD4 and CD8 T cells (i.e., pan CD3 T cells) when engineered into cell-based delivery platforms. Overall, the correctly engineered CD4 T cells offer to bridge the two gaps that could prevent the clinical adoption of this technology, i.e., scaling up the cell production to generate a clinical dose and increased synthesis of the therapeutic protein from the cell so as to reduce the size of the clinical dose required to produce the desired effect.

Transducing primary T cells with two distinct lentivectors—one encoding the antigen-specific CAR and the other the CAR-inducible transgene expressed under NFAT-RE—has been previously documented (50). However, it has been challenging to attain a high lentivector titer with a single plasmid with genetic payload encoding both the CAR and the CAR-inducible transgene, enabling the transduction of immune cells in a single step (12). While achieving single-step transduction allows for clinical translation of the next generation of CAR T and CAR NK cells, with improved functions (e.g., enhanced persistence, reduced exhaustion, and mitigated safety concerns) (22–24, 27), the use of these cells to exert extracellular functions through the synthetic protein has been limited by their capacity to produce a sufficient amount of such proteins in vivo and the scalability of the ex vivo manufacturing process.

Previously, we reported on a scalable process for manufacturing lentivector particles with large genetic payload at a high titer (12) and a simple and efficient process to transduce primary T cells in a single step and expand their ex vivo culture (13). In this work, we have compared CD4 T cells with CD8 T cells, when engineered for the delivery function and this comparison favored CD4 T cells for multiple reasons. For example, the CD4 T cells engineered with a CAR that has 4-1BBζ intracellular domain but not the CD28 intracellular domain, compared to a similarly engineered CD8 CAR T cell, exhibits ~30-fold cumulative improved performance (~3-fold transduction, ~2-fold expansion, ~5-fold activity). Although it may be easy to presume that starting with pan CD3 T cells, i.e., combined population of CD4 and CD8 T cells, may still be relevant because it includes CD4 T cells, our results show this to be not true. When starting with this combined population, the final population after cell expansion consists mainly of CD8 T cells, a phenomenon also reported by others (32, 33) and have been linked to the therapeutic success of CAR T cell therapies.

The low cytolysis of CD4 T cell delivery system also offers a substantial advantage for our platform, i.e., the maximum tolerated dose of the CD4 T cell engineered for drug delivery can be high without exposure to the healthy tissues. Our primary hypothesis for selecting the CD4 T cells over the CD8 T cells has been the NFAT-based transcriptional machinery that is more productive in CD4 T cells (28). However, findings from other research groups also support other advantages that CD4 T cells offer. For example, the activation of NFAT pathway has recently been reported to be responsible for CD8 T cell exhaustion (51). CD4 T cells produce more Th1 cytokines and proliferate faster than CD8 T cells (52). Compared to pan CD3 CAR T cells with a noncurable dose, the same number of T cells with half from each subset (CD4, CD8) more effectively treats tumors (52). Helper CD4 T cells induce stemness in the CD8 T cells that then persists longer and increases the memory T cell pool for improved treatment outcome (53). Unlike CD8 T cells, the CD4 T cells extravasate into cold tumors (40) and recruit other CD8 T cells (54). The CD4 T cells also assist vascular normalization, attenuate hypoxia, and reduce metastasis (55). They have been found to persist for more than a decade (56–58). In fact, when passaged in vivo, they can outlive the host mouse species by four times and expand at least 1040-fold (58).

We expect that our cell-mediated drug delivery system will positively impact various medical conditions that require precise spatiotemporal drug administration. Indeed, in context of our current focus, we are leveraging it for solid tumors and viral infections. Building upon our experience with cell-based delivery of IFNβ for targeting viral infections (29, 59), and evidence of its success as an antitumor agent (60–63), we sought to explore the feasibility of antitumor efficacy by focusing the localized delivery of IFNβ through our CD4 T-cell delivery platform. Our data showed that in situ delivery of IFNβ using our CD4 T cell–based platform substantially outperformed the direct administration of rhIFNβ dose of 0.25 µg (70,000 IU) per day, a tolerable dose reported by others for use in mice (64, 65). We showed that daily i.p. delivery of 0.25 µg rhIFNβ (70,000 IU) for 6 d (a total combined dose of 420,000 IU) failed to demonstrate any significant therapeutic advantage. This is in consensus with previous reports where daily i.p. administration of rhIFNβ (10,000 IU) for 23 d (a total combined dose of 230,000 IU) failed to elicit any therapeutic response (66). Indeed, our calculations, detailed in Appendix A of Supporting Information, indicate that the IFNβ dosage delivered by CD4 T cells was approximately 300 times lower than that of directly administered rhIFNβ, yet it exhibited greater effectiveness. The increased therapeutic effect at reduced equivalent dose once again shows the potential of our cell-based platform to minimize any undesired side effects such as inflammation, tissue damage, and multiorgan failure (67–71), thereby offering the possibility to increase the Maximum Recommended Starting Dose during subsequent human trials of high-dose IFN (HDI) adjuvant therapy.

Continued development of this technology will therefore help us overcome the two major issues that have been observed with systemic IFNβ treatments (72–74)—severe toxicities to healthy tissues and immunosuppression in the local tumor microenvironment and use it to synergize with other antitumor agents such as chemotherapies (75, 76), radiotherapies (77), antibody-based immunotherapies (60, 61), as well as bridge the innate and adaptive immune responses (60). Furthermore, we expect that the ongoing developments in the field of CAR T cell therapies will continue to benefit our CD4 CAR T cell-mediated drug delivery platform. For example, new technologies for allogeneic CAR T cell therapies will enable us to improve affordability and accessibility of our platform and knock-out of TCRs could reduce nonspecific drug delivery due to TCR-mediated activation of these cells.

In conclusion, our CD4 T cell–based technology acts as a zero-order delivery system, providing a promising solution to the challenges posed by the first-order drug delivery systems with synthetic carriers. Unlike first-order systems, it focuses on the concentration of therapeutic biologics at the disease site proportionate to the disease mass (13), increasing efficacy while reducing concentration in healthy tissues, improving safety. Furthermore, the long-term persistence of CD4 T cells in the body, which can last for more than a decade, might hold promise in significantly reducing the frequency of reinfusions required. This would not only improve patient compliance but also, over the long term, alleviate the burden on the healthcare system and streamline treatment procedures.

Our ongoing efforts are focused on addressing the unanswered questions surrounding the translation of our CD4 T cell delivery platform. For example, our current efforts are on exploring cytokine sequences that can be expressed in CD4 T cells for improving their persistence and overcoming the need for repetitive dosing. Subsequently, we plan to test our CD4 T cell delivery system for potential safety issues that might arise due to cytokine release syndrome, graft-versus-host disease, macrophage-activation syndrome, cerebral edema, and neurotoxicity (78, 79). These concerns, which are relevant in CAR T cell therapies, may impact the clinical application of the CD4 T cell delivery system. An additional concern is the acquisition of cytolytic activity by the CD4 T cells (80, 81) that may be abrogated by the use of upcoming gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR. Given these considerations, our ongoing efforts aim to evaluate the safety of our CD4 T cell–based technology, which provides a comprehensive solution to overcome drug delivery challenges. By enhancing both efficacy and safety, this technology has the potential to improve patient outcomes.
