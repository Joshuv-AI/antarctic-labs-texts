Ex-Tregs: From controversial artifact to therapeutic target
Authors: Liping Li, Minghui Li, Xuyu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegulatory T cell (Treg) instability has evolved from a debated artifact into a central concept in immunology, though the mechanisms driving the transition from a stable suppressor to a pathogenic ex-regulatory T cell (ex-Treg) remain unclear. This review synthesizes findings from fate-mapping, epigenetic, and metabolic studies in mouse models and human systems, tracing the ex-Treg concept through three phases: controversy, epigenetic reconciliation, and current views. The Treg-specific demethylated region (TSDR) distinguishes truly committed Tregs from transient forkhead box P3 (FOXP3) expressers. Three interdependent factors—environmental stress (interleukin-1β [IL-1β], interleukin-6 [IL-6]), epigenetic erasure (ten-eleven translocation 2 [TET2] dysfunction leading to TSDR hypermethylation), and metabolic rewiring (mechanistic target of rapamycin [mTOR]-driven glycolysis)—cooperatively drive ex-Treg conversion. The “self vs non-self” hypothesis frames instability as an evolved checkpoint balancing tolerance with host defense. Ex-Tregs contribute to autoimmunity and aging but also offer therapeutic opportunities in cancer. Key uncertainties persist regarding ex-Treg heterogeneity, epigenetic reversibility, and translation to humans. Future directions should focus on biomarker discovery and engineering epigenetically stable Tregs for therapy. Collectively, these advances position Treg instability as a pivotal regulator of immune homeostasis and highlight the promise of therapeutically targeting this pathway to advance treatments for autoimmunity, aging, and cancer.Keywords: regulatory T cells (Tregs), Treg instability, ex-regulatory T cells (ex-Tregs), FOXP3, epigenetic regulation, immune tolerance

The Canonization of FOXP3 and the Seeds of Controversy

The review is framed around the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine awarded to Shimon Sakaguchi, Mary E. Brunkow, and Fred Ramsdell for their foundational discoveries of regulatory T cells and forkhead box P3 (FOXP3). Their work transformed a discredited concept into a rigorous paradigm, without which the ex-regulatory T cell (ex-Treg) controversy would never have arisen. The Nobel Prize thus provides an apt occasion to reflect on the dialectical process by which immunology has learned to think about stability, plasticity, and productive exceptions. To clarify terminology used throughout this review: “instability” refers to the loss of FOXP3 expression and suppressive function; “plasticity” denotes the acquisition of effector-like features while retaining FOXP3; and “conversion/reprogramming” describes the transition from a regulatory T cell (Treg) to an ex-Treg state.

FOXP3 was canonized in the early 2000s as the master transcription factor defining regulatory T cell identity [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Its discovery promised a clean, binary model of immune regulation: FOXP3+ cells suppress; FOXP3– cells execute effector functions. This elegant framework, established in three landmark papers published in 2003 from the laboratories of Shimon Sakaguchi, Alexander Rudensky, and Fred Ramsdell, provided the molecular anchor that the field of “suppressor T cells” had desperately lacked for two decades [[4], [5], [6]]. The concept of dominant tolerance—the active maintenance of self-unresponsiveness by a dedicated cellular lineage—was finally on firm experimental footing [7].

It is important to acknowledge that experimental evidence for regulatory T cell activity had been reported even prior to Sakaguchi's seminal work, albeit in different experimental contexts (Figure 1). In the 1970s, Gershon and Kondo first described “suppressor T cells” that could downregulate immune responses [7]. Multiple laboratories reported that thymectomy of certain mouse strains at day 3 of life (d3Tx) led to spontaneous autoimmune pathology that could be prevented by transfer of CD4+ T cells from normal donors [8]. These observations collectively suggested the existence of a naturally occurring regulatory population. However, the field collapsed in the 1980s due to irreproducible results, elusive molecular markers, and the infamous “I-J” locus debacle (Figure 1). So complete was this intellectual collapse that a generation of immunologists was trained to view any claim of active suppression with reflexive skepticism [9]. Shimon Sakaguchi’s perseverance through this period—beginning in 1995, his methodical demonstration that CD4+CD25+ cells could prevent autoimmune disease in thymectomized mice—constituted a scientific resurrection [8,10]. Yet even these careful studies were met with resistance. As Ethan Shevach later recalled, “When we began our studies on T regulatory cells, even I was somewhat leery … Bill Paul was actually quite receptive to our approach and continued to encourage me to carry on even after I received a negative review from our advisory committee.” [11].

The 2001 identification of Foxp3 as the gene mutated in both the scurfy mouse and human IPEX syndrome by Mary Brunkow and Fred Ramsdell provided the critical missing piece [[1], [2], [3]]. Positional cloning of the X-chromosome mutation in scurfy mice—animals suffering from fatal, multiorgan autoimmunity characterized by scaly skin, ruffled fur, and unchecked lymphoproliferation—revealed a disruption in a novel forkhead family transcription factor [1]. Complementation studies confirmed causality: transgenic expression of wild-type Foxp3 rescued scurfy males from their otherwise lethal phenotype [1]. Simultaneously, Ramsdell, Brunkow, and colleagues demonstrated that mutations in the human ortholog FOXP3 caused IPEX syndrome, a devastating pediatric autoimmune disorder defined by intractable diarrhea, type 1 diabetes, and eczema [2,3]. The convergence was complete: a defined genetic lesion, a conserved molecular pathway, and a specific cellular population.

The 2003 papers sealed the paradigm. Sakaguchi’s group showed that FOXP3 expression was largely restricted to CD4+CD25+ Tregs and that retroviral transduction of FOXP3 into conventional T cells conferred suppressive function [4]. Rudensky’s laboratory demonstrated that FOXP3 was necessary for Treg development [5]. Ramsdell’s team independently confirmed that scurfy mice lacked functional Tregs [6]. The field now had what it had lacked for two decades: a definitive molecular marker, a genetic entry point, and a coherent model of lineage commitment (Figure 1).

Yet even as the canon was being written, the seeds of its complication were being sown. The very molecular tools that confirmed Treg identity—Foxp3 reporter mice, fate-mapping alleles, and epigenetic analyses—would soon reveal that the relationship between FOXP3 expression and suppressive function was neither as simple nor as irreversible as the lineage commitment model proposed. Within five years of the 2003 papers, the first reports of “ex-Tregs” began to appear [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. The controversy was about to begin.

The Era of Controversy (2008–2014): Stability vs Instability

The years following the canonization of FOXP3 saw a sharp division in the field. One camp argued that Tregs were a terminally differentiated, stable lineage; the other presented fate-mapping evidence that Tregs could lose FOXP3 and become pathogenic effectors. This section examines the lineage commitment paradigm, the fate-mapping challenge that undermined it, and the skeptical response that fueled the controversy.

The Lineage Commitment Paradigm

The lineage commitment model that crystallized between 2003 and 2008 held that FOXP3 expression was simultaneously necessary and sufficient for the Treg identity and that this identity, once acquired, was stable and heritable. This view rested on several convergent lines of evidence. Adoptive transfer experiments demonstrated that highly purified Tregs could maintain FOXP3 expression and suppressive function through multiple rounds of division [16]. The identification of the Treg-specific demethylated region (TSDR)—a conserved noncoding sequence within the Foxp3 locus that was fully demethylated in natural Tregs but methylated in conventional T cells—provided an epigenetic correlate of commitment. It appeared that Tregs were “locked” into their regulatory fate by a stable epigenetic mark that distinguished them from transiently activated conventional T cells that might briefly upregulate FOXP3 without acquiring stable suppressive function [17].

The appeal of this model reached beyond its experimental support. It was consistent with the developmental logic of the adaptive immune system, in which lineage decisions—CD4 vs CD8, type 1 T helper cell (Th1) vs type 2 T helper (Th2)—were generally considered irreversible following terminal differentiation. It also provided therapeutic reassurance: if Tregs were being developed as cell-based therapies for autoimmune disease and transplantation, their stability post infusion was a non-negotiable safety requirement. The lineage commitment model promised that Tregs, once generated and expanded, would remain faithful to their regulatory vocation.

The Fate-mapping Challenge

The introduction of fate-mapping systems profoundly changed the debate. These tools—first exemplified by the Foxp3GFP-Cre bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) transgenic line developed in Jeffrey Bluestone's laboratory—employed a large genomic fragment containing the Foxp3 locus to drive Cre recombinase expression under the control of endogenous regulatory elements [13]. When crossed to Cre-inducible reporter strains such as Rosa26 loxP-STOP-loxP-YFP, this system permanently marked cells that had ever activated the Foxp3 promoter, irreversibly labeling them with yellow fluorescent protein regardless of whether FOXP3 protein expression was subsequently maintained. This technical advance allowed investigators to ask a question that had previously been unanswerable: what happens to cells that lose FOXP3 expression [15]?

Beginning in approximately 2009, multiple laboratories reported the existence of such populations [12,14,15]. In an especially influential 2009 study led by Jeffrey Bluestone, cells bearing the permanent mark of past FOXP3 expression but lacking current FOXP3 protein—so-called “ex-FOXP3” or “ex-Treg” cells—were identified in both steady-state and inflammatory conditions (Figure 1). Critically, these cells were not inert; they upregulated CD40L, produced effector cytokines including interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukin-17 (IL-17), and, in adoptive transfer systems, contributed to autoimmune pathology. Bluestone and colleagues demonstrated that ex-Tregs arising in a model of autoimmune inflammation retained memory T cell characteristics and could rapidly re-express effector functions upon restimulation [15].

These observations posed a direct challenge to the lineage commitment model. If Tregs could lose FOXP3 and become pathogenic effectors, then the relationship between regulatory identity and stable suppression was contingent rather than constitutive. The implications reached beyond basic biology to therapeutic development: if adoptively transferred Tregs could undergo similar conversion, they might not simply fail to suppress, but actively contribute to the very pathology they were intended to treat.

The Skeptical Response

The initial response from advocates of the stability paradigm was sharp and methodologically rigorous. Critics raised three principal objections [18]. First, they argued that transient FOXP3 expression could occur in activated conventional T cells without conferring suppressive function; such cells, marked in fate-mapping systems, were not “true” Tregs that had lost identity, but rather effector cells that had briefly and inconsequentially expressed the lineage marker. Second, they questioned the fidelity of fate-mapping systems themselves, noting that Cre-mediated recombination could exhibit leaky activity or occur within progenitor populations before lineage commitment [19]. For instance, the widely used knock-in strain Foxp3YFP-Cre exhibits stochastic Cre activity within hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). When crossed with Rosa26 reporter lines, this off-target recombination leads to the unintended labeling of B cells and other non-Treg lineages. This promiscuous expression may be attributed to the inclusion of a bovine growth hormone (bGH) polyadenylation signal in the construct. As demonstrated in “Yeti” reporter mice, the bGH poly(A) tail can dramatically stabilize mRNA half-life, potentially allowing transient or low-level transcripts to persist and drive ectopic recombination. Notably, this off-target effect is absent in Foxp3 BAC transgenic mice, suggesting that the genomic context or specific regulatory elements within the BAC construct provide higher specificity [19]. Third, they contended that in vitro manipulation and adoptive transfer into lymphopenic hosts might artifactually destabilize cells that would remain perfectly stable in an intact, noninflammatory environment [18].

The debate crystallized around conflicting interpretations of fate-mapping data, with prominent laboratories divided. The “stability camp,” exemplified by the work of Alexander Rudensky, illustrated the considerable stability of TSDR-demethylated natural Tregs and the importance of distinguishing true lineage deviation from experimental artifacts [20]. Using a refined temporal model—the Foxp3eGFP-Cre-ERT2 (tamoxifen-inducible) system—Rudensky’s group argued that mature Tregs constitute an exceptionally stable lineage [20]. Their lineage-tracing experiments, in which tamoxifen administration labeled existing Tregs without marking cells that only transiently expressed FOXP3, demonstrated that once a cell attained stable Treg identity, it rarely, if ever, lost FOXP3 expression or acquired effector functions. They concluded that Tregs do not convert into effector cells under physiological or inflammatory conditions, directly challenging earlier reports of “ex-Tregs” arising from incompletely committed populations or experimental artifacts. In contrast, the “plasticity camp,” represented by investigators including Jeffrey Bluestone and Xuyu Zhou, contended that the fate-mapping data were robust and that Treg instability was a genuine biological phenomenon with important pathological consequences [15,21].

The field thus found itself divided not simply over observational findings, but over fundamental questions of lineage identity: was Treg stability absolute and immutable, or environment-dependent and conditional? What made the controversy particularly charged was its stakes. If Tregs were inherently unstable, the entire enterprise of Treg cell therapy—already advancing toward clinical trials—rested on a potentially unstable foundation. Regulatory agencies, investors, and clinical collaborators were watching. The resolution of this debate was thus not simply an academic dispute but a prerequisite for therapeutic translation.

The Reconciliation: Epigenetic Locking and Contextual Plasticity

Progress was not marked by a single experimental breakthrough, but by a growing appreciation of regulatory T cell heterogeneity and the mechanisms behind stable lineage commitment. The identification of the TSDR as a key determinant of FOXP3 stability helped bridge conflicting data. This epigenetic perspective clarified the difference between adaptive plasticity and pathological instability, bringing together previous findings.

The TSDR Breakthrough as Conceptual Arbiter

Resolution did not emerge from a decisive experimental victory of one camp over another, but from a more subtle understanding of regulatory T cell heterogeneity and the molecular basis of stable commitment. The discovery and detailed characterization of the TSDR provided the critical conceptual bridge (Figure 1).

It became clear that the TSDR—a highly conserved CpG-rich region within an intron of the Foxp3 locus—was not purely a passive correlate of Foxp3 expression, but a functional determinant of its stability [17,[22], [23], [24], [25]]. In natural, thymus-derived Tregs (tTregs), the TSDR is fully demethylated, creating an epigenetically permissive configuration that supports sustained Foxp3 transcription even in the absence of continuous T-cell receptor (TCR) stimulation. In contrast, peripherally induced Tregs (iTregs), generated by transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)-mediated conversion of conventional CD4+ T cells, exhibit only partial TSDR demethylation and are correspondingly unstable, rapidly losing FOXP3 expression upon removal of polarizing cytokines. Activated human conventional T cells that transiently upregulate FOXP3 retain a fully methylated TSDR and lack any epigenetic allegiance to the regulatory lineage [26].

This epigenetic framework allowed the field to reconcile the competing observations. Some reported “ex-Tregs” were almost certainly conventional T cells that had briefly expressed FOXP3 without epigenetic commitment—artifacts of fate-mapping systems, as the stability camp had argued [27]. But crucially, even fully TSDR-demethylated, “bona fide” Tregs could, under sufficient and sustained inflammatory pressure, undergo TSDR remethylation and irreversible lineage deviation [28]. The question was no longer whether Tregs were stable or plastic, but rather: what are the conditions under which even the most stably committed Tregs reach their breaking point?

Functional Plasticity as Adaptive Specialization

A second critical distinction emerged: not all changes in Treg phenotype represent pathological instability. The field increasingly recognizes that Tregs possess a previously unappreciated capacity for context-dependent adaptation that preserves their core regulatory identity while improving their function in specific inflammatory milieus.

In 2009, Koch et al. made a seminal observation: Tregs, like Th1 effector cells, could express the transcription factor T-bet [29]. Critically, T-bet expression in Tregs was not a harbinger of lineage deviation, but rather an adaptation that equipped Tregs to suppress Th1-type responses. T-bet+ Tregs upregulate C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 3 (CXCR3), the chemokine receptor required for migration to Th1 inflammatory sites, and are essential for controlling Th1-mediated pathology. Transferring T-bet-deficient Tregs into scurfy mice failed to rescue autoimmune disease, demonstrating that this “specialized” Treg population was not purely an incidental variant but a functionally necessary subset [29].

Parallel findings emerged for other effector lineages. Tregs expressing interferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4) were identified as specialized suppressors of Th2 responses [30]; Tregs expressing retinoid-related orphan receptor gamma t (RORγt) were found to control Th17-mediated inflammation at mucosal sites [31,32]; and follicular regulatory T cells (Tfr) were characterized as a distinct population that regulates germinal center reactions by suppressing both T follicular helper (Tfh) and B cell responses. Tfr cells upregulate CXCR5, the chemokine receptor required for homing to B cell follicles and germinal centers, enabling their specialized suppressive function in these anatomical sites [[33], [34], [35]]. This scheme of “effector Treg” specialization transformed the field's understanding of regulatory T cell heterogeneity. What had appeared as possible instability—the acquisition of transcription factors associated with pro-inflammatory lineages—was reinterpreted as adaptive plasticity: the deployment of context-appropriate suppressive machinery without loss of core regulatory identity.

The boundary between productive plasticity and pathological instability could now be defined with molecular precision. Plasticity preserves TSDR demethylation and FOXP3 expression while co-opting effector-associated transcriptional programs for suppressive purposes. Instability involves epigenetic erosion of the Foxp3 locus, loss of FOXP3 protein, and a functional shift from inhibition to inflammation. The former is adaptation; the latter is lineage failure.

Tissue-resident Tregs and Noncanonical Functions

The appreciation of FOXP3+ T cells’ specialization reached beyond transcription factor co-expression to include a broad repertoire of tissue-specific, noncanonical functions [36]. Far from being confined to lymphoid organs, Tregs seed peripheral tissues throughout the body, where they adopt phenotypes and functions adapted to local environments [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. At barrier sites, Tregs promote epithelial regeneration, control mucus generation, and regulate tissue-specific stem cell expansion [41]. In skeletal and cardiac muscle, they counteract fibrosis and promote repair following injury [42]. In the central nervous system, they support remyelination [40]. In skin, they enhance hair follicle cycling and wound healing [39]. Some of these functions are mediated through classic immunosuppressive mechanisms—reducing inflammation and shifting macrophages toward pro-repair phenotypes—but others involve direct Treg-stromal interactions, along with the production of growth factors, including amphiregulin and TGF-β [36,38].

This expanded view of regulatory T cell function has deep implications regarding understanding ex-Treg pathology. When a tissue-resident Treg undergoes pathogenic conversion, it does not become a generic effector T cell. Rather, it becomes an ex-Treg that retains the tissue-homing receptors, stromal interaction programs, and anatomical localization imprinted during its regulatory phase. It delivers inflammatory cytokines not to lymphoid tissues but to the precise sites where it once served as a peacekeeper. The ex-Treg thus does not represent merely the loss of a suppressor but the gain of a precisely targeted attacker.

The Three Pillars of Ex-Treg Conversion

Contemporary understanding identifies three interdependent mechanisms that collaboratively drive the transition from stable suppressor to pathogenic effector. These “three pillars”—environmental stress, epigenetic erasure, and metabolic rewiring—operate in concert, reinforcing one another in a self-amplifying cascade that culminates in irreversible lineage deviation (Figure 1).

Environmental Stress: Alarmins Overwhelm Maintenance Signals

In homeostatic conditions, Treg stability is actively maintained by microenvironmental cues that signal the absence of emergency. Interleukin-2 (IL-2), through signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5) signaling, reinforces FOXP3 expression and supports the epigenetic maintenance machinery (Figure 2). Tregs, uniquely among CD4+ T cells, constitutively express the high-affinity IL-2 receptor α-chain CD25, enabling them to capture this scarce cytokine with high efficiency. This creates a positive feedback loop: IL-2 sustains FOXP3, and FOXP3 sustains CD25 expression [21,23,28,43].

Interleukin-33 (IL-33), an alarmin released upon cellular damage, plays a more complex, context-dependent role. At barrier sites, IL-33 signaling through serum stimulation-2 (ST2), also known as interleukin 1 receptor-like 1 (IL1RL1), promotes Treg accumulation and enhances suppressive function, supporting tissue repair and tolerance to commensal organisms [40,44]. Yet under conditions of chronic inflammation, the same pathway may contribute to Treg dysfunction [45].

In inflammatory pathologies, these maintenance signals are supplanted by a different class of environmental cues. Alarmins, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), released by necrotic cells and activated innate immune cells, engage myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88)-dependent signaling pathways that directly antagonize Foxp3 transcription [46,47]. These signals may also recruit DNA methyltransferases to the Foxp3 locus and initiate TSDR methylation. The cell receives an unambiguous environmental instruction: the rules of engagement have changed, and suppression is no longer the priority.

The role of interleukin-6 (IL-6) deserves particular attention [12,48,49]. Produced by activated antigen-presenting cells in response to pathogen-associated molecular patterns, IL-6 exerts dual effects on the Treg-effector equilibrium. First, it renders conventional T cells resistant to Treg-mediated suppression, effectively “lifting the brakes” by altering the responder population [50]. Second, and more directly relevant to ex-Treg conversion, IL-6 can act on Tregs themselves to downregulate FOXP3 and promote acquisition of IL-17-producing capacity [12,48,49]. This IL-6-driven conversion represents a direct molecular pathway from an inflammatory environment to lineage instability (Figure 2).

Recent work has delivered unprecedented temporal resolution of these environmental requirements. A recent study using an auxin-inducible degron system to degrade FOXP3 protein acutely revealed a striking context-dependence: while mature Tregs in healthy mice are surprisingly resilient to temporary FOXP3 loss and can rapidly reacquire their regulatory program, Tregs in inflamed environments strictly depend on continuous FOXP3 activity to maintain their lineage [51]. This finding demonstrates that the inflammatory environment itself dynamically calibrates the threshold for instability—what is tolerable in homeostasis becomes catastrophic during active inflammation.

Epigenetic Erasure: Ten-eleven Translocation 2 (TET2) Failure as the Irreversible Step

The transition from labile, potentially reversible Treg destabilization to fixed, irreversible ex-Treg conversion depends on the activity of ten-eleven translocation (TET) family enzymes, particularly TET2 and ten-eleven translocation 3 (TET3) (Figure 1). TET enzymes catalyze the stepwise oxidation of 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine and further derivatives, initiating active DNA demethylation. Sustained TET2/3 activity is required to maintain the TSDR in its permissive, demethylated state in the face of the constitutive activity of DNA methyltransferases [52,53].

Under conditions of oxidative stress—a nearly universal feature of infection microenvironments—TET function becomes impaired [54]. Reactive oxygen species can directly oxidize the iron cofactor within the TET catalytic domain, thereby inhibiting its enzymatic activity (Figure 2), and inflammatory cytokines can also downregulate TET expression [54,55]. The consequence is progressive TSDR hypermethylation.

Once TSDR hypermethylation is established, this epigenetic mark is self-perpetuating: methylated CpG dinucleotides recruit methyl-CpG-binding domain proteins, which further compact chromatin and recruit additional DNA methyltransferases. The cell's regulatory identity is not simply downregulated but epigenetically erased. Even if FOXP3 expression could be transiently restored through strong TCR stimulation, the absence of TSDR demethylation prevents sustained, self-maintaining Foxp3 transcription. The cell has forgotten that it was ever a Treg.

Metabolic Rewiring: Glycolysis Fuels Pathogenicity

Stable Tregs are metabolically distinct from conventional effector T cells [56,57]. Their primary metabolic program is fatty acid oxidation (FAO) coupled with oxidative phosphorylation—a catabolic, energy-efficient metabolism that enables extended survival and basal suppressive function. This particular metabolic preference is not merely permissive of Treg identity but is actively enforced by FOXP3 itself, which suppresses glycolytic gene expression and promotes lipid-metabolism genes [58].

A wholesale metabolic reprogramming accompanies the transition to ex-Treg status [57]. Glycolysis is dramatically upregulated via mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)–dependent pathways, while FAO is suppressed. That shift is both permissive and instructive. Glycolysis provides the biosynthetic precursors—nucleotides, amino acids, and lipids—required for the production of effector cytokines and rapid proliferation [57,59]. Moreover, glycolytic intermediates directly influence the epigenetic state: acetyl-CoA derived from glucose metabolism fuels histone acetylation at inflammatory gene loci. In contrast, the ratio of α-ketoglutarate to succinate influences TET enzyme activity and DNA methylation status [54]. Although glycolytic influx generates reactive oxygen species (ROS)—once dismissed as mere metabolic waste—ROS have now been demonstrated as active players in T cell activation [60]. In the context of Tregs, however, ROS directly impair TET function by oxidizing the iron cofactor within TET’s catalytic domain [54,61]. Supporting this notion, forcing Tregs to shift toward a high-glycolytic state by overexpressing GLUT1 leads to instability and loss of suppressive function. Critically, recent work has revealed that within the glucose-deprived tumor microenvironment, stable Tregs avoid glucose competition by preferentially utilizing lactate as an alternative carbon source [62]. Lactate-derived carbons feed into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to support mitochondrial function and sustain suppressive capacity, thereby reinforcing Treg stability and promoting tumor progression. Conversely, when Tregs are forced to rely on glucose rather than lactate, their suppressive function is compromised, highlighting that metabolic flexibility—specifically the ability to use lactate—is a key determinant of intratumoral Treg stability [62].

Central to this metabolic–epigenetic interface is mTOR. mTOR integrates signals from cytokines, nutrients, and energy status to coordinate cellular metabolism and gene expression. In Tregs, mTOR signaling operates according to a “Goldilocks” principle: the level of signal must be just right. Tonic, low-level mTOR signaling supports Treg survival and quiescence without triggering glycolytic reprogramming [63]. Deletion of regulatory-associated protein of mTOR gene (Rptor, encoding an essential component of mTORC1), but not rapamycin-insensitive companion of mTOR gene (Rictor, encoding an essential component of mTORC2), specifically in Tregs led to severe systemic autoimmunity, partly due to defective lipid biosynthesis. Intermediate activation—driven by appropriate TCR and co-stimulatory signals—promotes the transition from resting Tregs (rTregs) to effector Tregs (eTregs), enabling functional adaptation while maintaining FOXP3 expression [64]. However, excessive or persistent mTOR hyperactivation, induced by inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6, drives the metabolic switch that characterizes ex-Treg conversion. In this overstimulated state, mTOR no longer supports Treg activation but instead precipitates loss of FOXP3 and acquisition of pathogenic function. Consistent with this model, conditional deletion of phosphatase and tensin homolog (Pten) in Tregs—which abolishes the negative regulator of PI3K-mTOR signaling—results in constitutive mTOR hyperactivation, driving spontaneous loss of FOXP3 expression, acquisition of effector cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-17), and development of fatal inflammatory pathology, directly demonstrating that mTOR overstimulation destabilizes Treg identity [61,62]. Conversely, Treg-specific deletion of tuberous sclerosis complex 1 (TSC1), another negative regulator of mTORC1, similarly leads to mTOR hyperactivation and impaired suppressive function, reinforcing that excessive mTOR signaling breaches the Goldilocks threshold (Figure 2). By contrast, Tregs lacking gene of regulatory-associated protein of mTOR complex 1 (Rptor, encoding a core component of mTORC1) exhibit profound developmental and functional defects, underscoring that some basal mTOR activity remains essential for Treg homeostasis. Collectively, these genetic models establish that mTOR signaling in Tregs operates within a narrow therapeutic window: too little signaling compromises Treg survival and function, while too much—as seen in Pten- or Tsc1-deficient Tregs—precipitates loss of FOXP3 and conversion to a pathogenic ex-Treg phenotype (Table 1). Rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor, stabilizes Treg identity and prevents ex-Treg conversion in several experimental systems, a finding with direct therapeutic implications [59,60].

The ex-Treg does not simply lose its suppressor identity through passive decay; it actively acquires the metabolic machinery of a pathogen. This cellular metabolic transformation is self-reinforcing: glycolysis generates ROS that impair TET function, promoting TSDR methylation; TSDR methylation reduces FOXP3 expression, releasing FOXP3-mediated suppression of glycolytic genes; increased glycolysis further drives mTOR activation. A positive feedback loop ensues, rapidly and irreversibly propelling the cell from stability to pathogenicity.

The “Self vs Non-self” Hypothesis: An Evolutionary Framework

Recognizing that Treg instability shows purposeful patterns rather than occurring randomly or as mere pathology, researchers developed an evolutionary perspective. The “self vs non-self” hypothesis suggests that instability functions as a checkpoint, tuning immune regulation based on the type of challenge faced. This section introduces the hypothesis, its rationale, supporting evidence, and its impact on the field.

The Genesis of the Hypothesis

The conceptual foundation for the “self vs non-self” hypothesis was established in a pivotal 2017 study, which represented a significant advance beyond earlier fate-mapping research by providing mechanistic insights that ultimately crystallized into a coherent evolutionary framework. Using a novel Foxp3ΔCNS1-Cre mouse model—which allowed specific labeling and irreversible tracking of tTregs with exceptional epigenetic precision—our group demonstrated that TCR engagement and the sequential functional specialization of tTregs directly lead to FOXP3 instability and reprogramming into Th lineages [28]. Critically, this study identified that the transition from stable suppression towards instability is not a passive decay process but a potent consequence of Treg activation and specialization. The work revealed a fundamental paradox: the very signals that drive Treg functional adaptation—enabling them to suppress in specific inflammatory contexts—also create the conditions for potential lineage failure [28].

The Hypothesis Articulated

Building on the 2017 findings, we formalized the “self vs non-self” hypothesis, which claims that Treg instability is not a biological error or a laboratory artifact but an evolved checkpoint that calibrates the intensity of immune regulation to the nature of the immunological threat [65]. The hypothesis emerged directly based on the observation that the IL-2-to-co-stimulatory signal switch—which drives Treg functional specialization—also creates a window of vulnerability to fragility [28,66]. In that study, Smigiel et al. showed that effector Tregs (eTregs) downregulate CCR7 and thus cannot access IL-2-rich niches in lymph nodes, making them insensitive to IL-2 [65]. Instead, these cells upregulate inducible T-cell costimulator (ICOS) to survive in inflamed tissues. This switch allows eTregs to follow non-self threats into peripheral battlefields but simultaneously uncouples them from the very cytokine (IL-2) that maintains FOXP3 stability. Hence, adaptive plasticity and pathogenic conversion are two sides of the same evolutionary coin.

The conceptual framework rests on a simple notion: natural Tregs (tTregs) are selected in the thymus for intermediate affinity to self-antigens. During thymic development, T cells bearing TCRs with very high affinity for self-peptide–major histocompatibility complex (MHC) complexes are eliminated through negative selection to prevent autoimmunity [67]. In contrast, those with very low affinity are permitted to mature as conventional naïve T cells. The Treg lineage occupies the middle ground: cells with TCR affinities above the threshold for positive selection but below the threshold for deletion are diverted into the regulatory pathway, where FOXP3 expression is induced, and suppressive function is imprinted (Table 1). This selection process generates a Treg repertoire that is fundamentally self-reactive in its specificity, but whose self-reactivity is calibrated to be sufficient for functional persistence without reaching the threshold for pathogenic activation.

We reasoned that this self-reactivity, far from being a dangerous oversight, serves a critical function in the periphery: sustained engagement with these same self-antigens provides a “just right” TCR signal that supports Treg activation and maintains FOXP3 expression (Table 1). This self-signaling constitutes the default, homeostatic state: in the absence of infection or tissue damage, Tregs remain stable and actively enforce tolerance to self (Table 1).

We further hypothesized that the immune system exploits this self-reactivity as a rheostat to distinguish harmless from dangerous encounters (Table 1). When a Treg encounters a foreign antigen—particularly one presented in the context of pathogen-associated molecular patterns and pro-inflammatory cytokines—the TCR signal exceeds the baseline for self-antigens. Moreover, pathogen-derived antigen presented by activated dendritic cells (DCs) expressing high levels of CD80/CD86 transforms the co-stimulatory landscape. High-strength CD28 engagement, often amplified by CD28-dependent and -independent signals (e.g., ICOS–inducible T-cell co-stimulator ligand [ICOSL], or indirectly via CD40L–CD40 on DCs), drives a qualitatively distinct signaling cascade. This supra-physiological stimulation, qualitatively and quantitatively distinct from tonic self-signaling, triggers sustained phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT/mTOR activation and a cellular response that includes downregulation of FOXP3. The 2017 discovery that costimulatory signaling, rather than IL-2 signaling, drives this transition provided the molecular mechanism: homeostatic self-encounters are transduced through IL-2 to sustain stability, whereas pathogenic non-self-encounters engage co-stimulatory pathways, pushing Tregs toward instability (Figure 3).

A contextual model of Treg instability as an evolved checkpoint for immune calibration

This figure presents the “self vs non-self” hypothesis, which proposes that Treg conversion from a suppressive to a pathogenic phenotype is not an error but an adaptive checkpoint that adjusts regulatory intensity to the nature and context of immunological threats. The model integrates thymic repertoire selection, peripheral signal discrimination, context-dependent conversion, clonal expansion, functional logic, and tissue-specific calibration. In the thymus, thymocytes with intermediate affinity for self-antigens are preferentially selected to become FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs). In contrast, cells with high-affinity self-reactivity undergo clonal deletion, whereas those with low or no affinity differentiate into conventional T cells (Tconvs). In the periphery, the outcome of Treg stimulation is determined by signal strength and environmental context. In a steady-state, homeostatic context rich in IL-2, self-antigen recognition reinforces FOXP3 expression and maintains suppressive function. By contrast, recognition of non-self antigens in an inflammatory context (characterized by IL-1β, IL-6, or ICOS signaling) can exceed a defined threshold, triggering Treg destabilization and conversion. Stimulation above the conversion threshold initiates a coordinated molecular program, including downregulation of FOXP3, an mTOR-driven metabolic switch toward glycolysis, epigenetic modifications (impaired TET2 activity and TSDR hypermethylation), and acquisition of effector cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-17. Following conversion, ex-Tregs undergo clonal expansion while retaining their original tissue-homing programs, allowing them to deliver inflammatory cytokines precisely to the sites they once protected. This conversion mechanism balances two competing immunological demands: during acute infection, lifting Treg-mediated suppression facilitates effective pathogen clearance; however, under conditions of chronic inflammation, persistent self-antigen exposure in an inflammatory microenvironment can drive pathogenic conversion, contributing to autoimmunity. Finally, susceptibility to conversion varies across tissues based on local environmental cues. In the gut, high levels of butyrate raise the conversion threshold, promoting tolerance to commensal microbiota. In inflamed tissues rich in IL-6, the threshold is lowered, enabling rapid immune responses to invasive pathogens. In our model, this serves an essential purpose: if Tregs remained fully suppressive during a strong anti-pathogen response, they would impede the very immunity required for host survival. The capacity to transiently “step aside”—or, in extreme circumstances, to convert into effectors—represents a fail-safe that lifts the brakes when the threat exceeds a threshold. In its most extreme form, the conversion of pathogen-specific Tregs into IFN-γ-producing effectors may actually contribute to host defense. This is not to suggest that ex-Treg conversion evolved specifically for this purpose; rather, it is an incidental consequence of a regulatory system that must balance two competing demands: maintaining tolerance to self and mounting effective immunity to pathogens.

We have expanded this model to include metabolic reprogramming as the mechanical link between a “strong signal” and “epigenetic erasure.” In the homeostatic (“self”) state, Tregs rely on FAO and oxidative phosphorylation, a bioenergetic profile that supports FOXP3 stability. Upon encountering a “non-self” threat, the supra-physiological TCR signal triggers a “bioenergetic switch” to aerobic glycolysis via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis. This cellular shift is not simply a fuel change; it produces specific metabolites, such as ROS, that impair TET enzyme activity. This creates a direct bridge to the epigenetic machinery: the metabolic demands of an “activated” state lead to the hypermethylation of the TSDR, functionally erasing the cell’s regulatory identity and locking it into an effector program.

Finally, we emphasize that the threshold for this transition is not universal but is governed by tissue-specific context-dependent signals. The immune system adjusts the “sensitivity” of the “self vs non-self” rheostat based on anatomical site: in tolerogenic sites such as the gut, high levels of microbial metabolites like butyrate raise the threshold for destabilization, protecting Tregs from converting in response to harmless commensal bacteria. Conversely, in inflammatory niches such as Peyer’s patches or the central nervous system, local cytokines including IL-6 and IL-1β “prime” the Treg, effectively lowering the bar for conversion such that even a moderate “non-self” signal can trigger exit from the regulatory lineage, as observed in Treg-to-intraepithelial lymphocyte transitions or Tfr-to-Tfh transitions in germinal center reactions [68,69]. This tissue-level calibration makes certain that the stability‒instability threshold is optimally tuned to the immunological challenges presented by different anatomical compartments—maximizing tolerance to commensals at barrier surfaces while preserving the capacity to mount effective responses against pathogens that breach those barriers (Figure 3).

Functional Logic: Preventing Over-suppression

Why would evolution retain a mechanism that dismantles the very cells responsible for enforcing self-tolerance? We believe that context-insensitive stability would be maladaptive during acute, life-threatening infection. If Tregs maintained full suppressive capacity while encountering overwhelming pathogen burdens, they would impede the very effector responses required for host survival. The immune system would be caught in a paradoxical state: the brakes are fully engaged while the accelerator is urgently needed.

The capacity to “step aside”—to transiently reduce suppressive activity in response to pathogen-associated signals—represents a fail-safe mechanism that lifts the brakes when the threat exceeds a threshold. At its most extreme, the conversion of pathogen-specific Tregs into IFN-γ-producing effectors may actually contribute to host defense. This is not to suggest that ex-Treg conversion evolved specifically for this purpose; rather, it is an incidental consequence of a regulatory system that must balance two competing demands: maintaining tolerance to self and mounting effective immunity to pathogens.

The pathogenic risk inherent in this arrangement is immediately apparent. In chronic inflammatory diseases—autoimmune conditions in which self-antigens are persistently presented in pro-inflammatory contexts—the same mechanism that evolved to prevent over-suppression during infection becomes a liability. The Treg perceives sustained danger, initiates instability, and converts into an ex-Treg that attacks the very tissue it once protected—the self/non-self-discrimination system, optimized for acute pathogen encounters, malfunctions in chronic inflammation [15,21,49].

Experimental Support and Mechanistic Plausibility

The “self vs non-self” hypothesis has received considerable experimental support from published studies using genetic, pharmacological, and infectious disease models. A key line of evidence comes from conditional knockout studies combined with Treg fate-mapping, which have identified multiple genes that are essential for maintaining Treg lineage stability. Beyond the well-characterized FOXP3-binding partners such as Runx family members and Ikaros zinc finger (IKZF) transcription factors (Helios, Eos, and Ikaros), a large category of regulators functioning as negative regulators of TCR signaling—specifically those constraining the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, including neuropilin 1 (NRP1), PTEN, protein phosphatase 6 (PP6), src homology 2 domain-containing inositol phosphatase (SHIP), absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2), TSC, von Hippel-Lindau (VHL), and TRAF3-interacting protein 3 (TRAF3IP3) et al.—have been shown to be critical for Treg stability [[70], [71], [72], [73], [74], [75], [76], [77]]. Deletion of these negative regulators results in enhanced TCR signaling and subsequent Treg instability, demonstrating that signal strength is quantitatively limiting for lineage maintenance (Figure 2).

More direct evidence that TCR signal strength determines Treg fate comes from studies manipulating costimulatory and checkpoint pathways. Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) blockade, which enhances CD28 costimulation and thereby amplifies TCR signal strength, has been shown to induce Treg instability and promote tumor rejection in murine cancer models [78]. Similarly, agonist anti-CD40 antibodies drive Treg destabilization and improve anti-tumor immunity. In a recent study by Maltez et al. using a mouse model of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, agonistic anti-CD40 treatment converted intratumoral Tregs into IFN-γ-producing type 1 effectors (ex-Tregs) that lost FOXP3 expression and acquired Tbet+IFN-γ+ Th1-like features [79]. Critically, through in situ analysis of TCR signaling, the authors demonstrated that exTregs exhibited the highest antigen-driven activation among all tumor-infiltrating T cells, directly linking strong TCR stimulation with Treg conversion to ex-Tregs. This conversion was dependent on CD40-activated dendritic cells and the induction of IL-12 and IFN-γ, showing that the inflammatory context integrates with TCR signal strength to determine Treg fate. Further evidence for the pathophysiological relevance of this mechanism comes from infectious disease models [80]. During herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection, Tregs lose FOXP3 expression and acquire effector functions. This virus-induced ex-Treg conversion may represent an adaptive mechanism to temporarily lift immune suppression during acute infection, consistent with the evolutionary logic of the “self vs non-self” hypothesis.

The signaling basis for this affinity discrimination has been partially elucidated. Strong TCR signals, particularly when combined with CD28/ICOS co-stimulation, hyperactivate the PI3K-AKT-mTOR axis [81], driving the glycolytic reprogramming and epigenetic changes that characterize ex-Treg conversion [28,75,76]. Weak TCR signals, in contrast, might preferentially activate nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and nuclear factor of activated T-cells (NFAT) pathways that cooperate with FOXP3 to reinforce the regulatory program. The cell’s interpretation of antigen encounter—as self or non-self, as tolerogenic or immunogenic—is thus encoded in the quantitative and qualitative nature of the TCR signal itself.

Cytokine context integrates with TCR signal strength to determine outcome. IL-2, produced by activated T cells, supports Treg stability even under strong TCR stimulation—a feedback mechanism that may limit inappropriate destabilization during strong immune responses [21,23]. Conversely, IL-6 and IL-4, produced by activated antigen-presenting cells sensing pathogen-associated molecular patterns, lower the threshold for TCR-driven instability, making certain that the decision to “lift the brakes” is informed by both antigen specificity and infection context [[47], [48], [49]].

This affinity-based discrimination extends toward specialized Treg subsets operating in distinct immunological niches [28]. For instance, Tfr cells, which control germinal center reactions, are not exempt from the stability‒instability calculus. Using a refined temporal fate-mapping system (Foxp3eGFP-Cre-ERT2) that labels only mature, committed Tregs upon tamoxifen administration, recent studies have demonstrated that even these cells can undergo pathogenic conversion [82]. Under sustained inflammatory conditions, such as those found in chronic autoimmunity or robust vaccination, these “bona fide” Tfr cells can lose FOXP3 expression and differentiate into ex-Tfr cells. These ex-Tfr cells, having downregulated their regulatory program, acquire a Tfh phenotype. Critically, they retain their original self-reactive TCR specificity and their anatomical homing program for the B cell follicle. This conversion results in a “double hit”: losing a germinal center suppressor while simultaneously gaining a self-reactive Tfh cell that can provide inappropriate help to autoreactive B cells, thereby directly facilitating the production of pathogenic autoantibodies. This finding delivers powerful experimental support for the “self vs non-self” hypothesis: a Treg (or Tfr cell) with a self-reactive TCR, upon encountering that self-antigen in a strongly inflammatory, “non-self” context, crosses the stability threshold and becomes a precisely targeted pathogen.

A further landmark study by Lee et al. has now delivered unprecedented mechanistic insight into Tfr instability, directly advancing this framework [68]. The authors demonstrated that terminally differentiated Tfr cells exhibit intrinsic instability, with acquisition of Tfh programs preceding loss of FOXP3 expression. Critically, they identified the transcription factor Tcf7/TCF-1 as a master regulator of this process, showing that Tfh lineage programs actively drive Tfr destabilization before FOXP3 is lost [68]. This finding fundamentally revises our insight into the temporal sequence of ex-Treg conversion—acquisition of pro-inflammatory transcriptional programs is not a consequence of FOXP3 loss but rather an initiating event that precedes and promotes epigenetic erosion of regulatory identity, exactly as predicted by the 2017 study’s emphasis on activation-induced instability [28].

Reception and Refinement: From Controversy to Framework

The “self vs non-self” hypothesis conceptually reframes the Treg instability problem. Where earlier debates positioned ex-Tregs as either artifacts or pathologies, our framework offered a third possibility: ex-Tregs as evolved features with both adaptive benefits and pathological costs. This viewpoint transformed the research agenda from documenting whether instability occurs to investigating how the stability–instability threshold is calibrated and how it might be therapeutically manipulated.

Critics, particularly those stressing the significant stability of TSDR-demethylated natural Tregs, have voiced crucial qualifications. Rudensky and colleagues have argued that the “core” natural Treg population—defined by complete TSDR demethylation and sustained FOXP3 expression independent of exogenous IL-2—exhibits far greater stability than the hypothesis might predict [20]. They suggest that the instability observed in fate-mapping studies may be concentrated among incompletely committed populations, including recent thymic emigrants, peripherally induced Tregs, or Tregs experiencing suboptimal IL-2 support [27].

The contemporary synthesis combines both perspectives. Even the most stably committed natural Tregs possess a threshold beyond which inflammatory pressure overcomes epigenetic locking. This threshold is substantially higher than that for iTregs or uncommitted populations, but it is not infinite. Our hypothesis, first proposed by Xuyu Zhou and Jeffrey Bluestone and grounded in the mechanistic insights of the 2017 study, provides a structure to understand what determines that threshold and why it varies across Treg subsets and environmental settings. The question is no longer whether Tregs can become unstable, but rather: under what conditions do the most stable cells reach their breaking point, and can we therapeutically raise or lower this threshold as clinical circumstances require?

FOXP3 Instability in Human Tregs: Bridging Murine Paradigms and Human Reality

Before exploring the promising clinical implications of ex-Treg biology, it is important to consider a key point that shapes this entire discussion. Most mechanistic insights into Treg instability—including the roles of TET enzymes, metabolic reprogramming, and the “self vs non-self” hypothesis—have emerged from sophisticated mouse models [52,57,65]. These foundational studies provide an invaluable conceptual roadmap. Now, the field is making rapid and exciting progress in translating these discoveries to human Treg biology. Notably, human Tregs display distinct, yet equally fascinating phenotypic characteristics, stability profiles, and responses to environmental cues [83,84]. Rather than contradicting the murine work, these differences are enriching our understanding and opening new therapeutic opportunities (Table 2).

A fundamental and encouraging area of progress lies in the metabolic programming of human Tregs [84]. In murine Tregs, stable suppression relies on FAO and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), maintained by FOXP3-mediated repression of glycolysis [57]. While human Tregs share this reliance on lipid oxidation, they display a more flexible, context-dependent metabolic landscape—a feature that may confer advantages for adapting to diverse tissue environments (Table 2). Importantly, and in striking contrast to the initial murine paradigm, exposure of human Tregs to inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-1β does not reliably induce FOXP3 loss or effector conversion [85]. Far from being a limitation, this finding represents a major advance: it suggests that human Tregs possess intrinsically greater stability, making them more resilient therapeutic agents. Rather than indicating that instability is irrelevant in humans, this insight directs our attention to the more physiologically relevant triggers of human Treg destabilization.

So what factors promote instability in the human system? Emerging evidence has identified TCR signal strength—specifically, the affinity of antigen recognition—as a key and biologically meaningful determinant, beautifully consistent with the “self vs non-self” hypothesis originally developed from murine studies. A recent breakthrough using chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-engineered Tregs has provided the first direct, elegant evidence that antigen receptor affinity regulates human Treg stability [86]. In this landmark study, human Tregs expressing high-affinity CARs showed reduced FOXP3 expression, increased effector cytokine production (IFN-γ, IL-17), and diminished suppressive capacity compared with those with low- or intermediate-affinity CARs. This finding directly validates the central tenet of the “self vs non-self” hypothesis in human biology—demonstrating that supra-physiological TCR signals drive Treg destabilization—while simultaneously providing a rational, tunable approach for designing more stable Treg-based therapies (Table 2).

The transcription factor Helios (IKZF2) is a useful tool for tracking human Treg stability and separating stable thymic Tregs from less stable groups [87]. Human Helios+ Tregs are better at suppressing, have steadier FOXP3, and resist inflammatory destabilization more than Helios– Tregs. Helios is more than a marker—it works with FOXP3 to keep Treg identity and block effector cytokine genes [87,88]. Despite legitimate methodological challenges, the field has now achieved a definitive milestone: the existence of human ex-Tregs has been demonstrated. A 2023 study by Ley and colleagues used lineage-tracing approaches adapted from mouse models to human blood, identifying a distinct population of CD3+CD4+CD16+CD56+ T cells in atherosclerosis [89]. By demonstrating clonal relatedness—shared TCR sequences between regulatory and cytotoxic populations—this study provided some of the first evidence that ex-Tregs exist in human disease and may contribute to inflammatory pathology [89]. This work largely validates decades of murine research and, potentially, opens new avenues for therapeutic targeting. While definitive fate-mapping alleles (Foxp3Cre × Rosa26-reporter) remain unavailable in humans, the clonal TCR approach offers a creative and rigorous alternative that helps address the central question: human ex-Tregs appear to be real, clinically relevant, and amenable to further study (Table 2).

Even open questions in the field are now seen as opportunities. The origin of precursor Tregs and their epigenetic status—whether they are thymic Tregs with full TSDR demethylation or peripheral Tregs—are top research areas. This is not a weakness but a sign that we need new tools, such as single-cell epigenetic analysis and better human Treg models. With the existence of human ex-Tregs now clear, research can focus on their origins, identification of markers, and the development of therapies to stabilize Tregs in autoimmunity or manipulate their conversion in cancer. The outlook for this field is bright.

The Clinical Big Picture: Ex-Tregs as Villains and Opportunities

Understanding the existence and mechanisms of ex-Tregs has revealed new avenues for therapy. In autoimmunity, their conversion results in both lost immune regulation and increased inflammation. In cancer, disrupting tumor Tregs provides a promising immunotherapy strategy. For aging, TET2 mutations connect clonal hematopoiesis with chronic inflammation. In cell therapy, ensuring epigenetic stability is now a key priority. The following sections explore these clinical applications.

Autoimmunity: The Double Hit

In autoimmune pathology, ex-Treg conversion represents a particularly insidious form of damage. The host simultaneously loses a suppressor and gains an effector—a “double hit” that profoundly changes the balance of tolerance and immunity. Moreover, ex-Tregs are not generic effectors; they retain the tissue-homing receptors, chemokine receptor profiles, and stromal interaction programs imprinted during their regulatory phase. They deliver inflammatory cytokines not to lymphoid organs but to the precise sites where they once served as peacekeepers.

The evidence for ex-Treg involvement in human autoimmune disease, while largely indirect due to the difficulty of fate-mapping in humans, is increasingly compelling. In multiple sclerosis, Tregs isolated from patients exhibit functional defects and a propensity to produce IFN-γ—features consistent with ex-Treg conversion [90]. In type 1 diabetes, Tregs from affected individuals show reduced FOXP3 stability and TSDR methylation abnormalities [87]. In rheumatoid arthritis, Tregs isolated from inflamed joints exhibit Th17-like characteristics and produce IL-17 [49].

The heterogeneity of autoimmune manifestations may reflect, in part, heterogeneity in the pathways driving ex-Treg conversion. In some patients, environmental factors—chronic viral infection, alterations in the microbiome, or exposure to inflammatory triggers—may provide the sustained alarmin signals that initiate instability. In others, genetic polymorphisms affecting Treg stability pathways—including variants in IL-2, IL-2RA, CTLA-4, and perhaps TET—may lower the threshold for conversion. In still others, age-related somatic mutations in TET may generate a lifelong supply of epigenetically unstable Tregs primed for pathogenic conversion.

Therapeutic tactics aimed at blocking ex-Treg conversion are under active investigation. Stabilizing the TSDR through inhibition of DNA methyltransferases, supporting TET2 function via metabolic interventions or antioxidant therapy, antagonizing alarmin signaling with IL-1 or HMGB1 blockers, and stabilizing metabolism through mTOR inhibition are all plausible approaches (Table 3). The difficulty lies in achieving Treg-specific effects; global manipulation of DNA methylation or metabolism carries substantial oncogenic and off-target risks.

Cancer: The Opportunity

If ex-Tregs are villains in autoimmunity, they are emerging as potential heroes in oncology (Table 3). Tumors actively recruit, expand, and stabilize Tregs, exploiting their suppressive functions to create an immune-privileged microenvironment that shields malignant cells from immune attack. Elevated intratumoral Treg frequencies correlate with poor prognosis across multiple cancer types, and Treg infiltration is a key factor in resistance to immune checkpoint blockade [91]. The goal of “intentional destabilization”—deliberately converting tumor-resident Tregs into IFN-γ-producing ex-Tregs—would simultaneously achieve two therapeutic objectives: removing a potent suppressor population and adding a cytotoxic effector population at the tumor site. The ex-Treg, once formed, is not simply neutralized; it becomes an active participant in antitumor immunity [92,93].

Strategies targeting enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) and Helios, transcription factors implicated in maintaining Treg stability within tumors, have shown preclinical promise [[92], [93], [94], [95]]. Helios-deficient Tregs exhibit an enhanced propensity to produce IFN-γ and fail to support tumor growth in mouse models. Small molecules that inhibit TET or activate mTOR are being evaluated for their capacity to “flip” the intratumoral Treg population. Combination approaches—checkpoint blockade plus deliberate Treg destabilization—are entering early-phase clinical trials (NCT05888831).

However, this strategy may carry certain risks that merit consideration. Systemic Treg destabilization could potentially contribute to autoimmunity, as murine models suggest that global Treg ablation can lead to multiorgan inflammation. Off-target effects on peripheral Tregs in healthy tissues might, in theory, precipitate autoimmune colitis, thyroiditis, or diabetes. At present, evidence for intratumoral Treg destabilization remains largely preclinical, derived primarily from mouse tumor models and ex vivo human tumor explants. Clinical translation appears to be in the early stages, with no published human trials to date demonstrating safe and effective tumor-selective Treg destabilization. The therapeutic window for intentional instability may be relatively narrow; strategies that couple destabilizing signals to tumor-specific antigens or exploit the tumor’s unique metabolic environment—hypoxia, lactate accumulation, and glucose deprivation—could help achieve the desired selectivity, though rigorous safety validation would likely be required.

Aging and Inflammaging

The recognition that somatic TET2 mutations drive clonal hematopoiesis has connected ex-Treg biology to the physiology of aging. TET2 mutations are among the most common age-acquired genetic alterations in the human hematopoietic system, present in clonal fractions of substantial proportions of individuals over 70. These mutations confer a competitive advantage to hematopoietic stem cells, leading to progressive expansion of TET2-mutant clones over time.

The implications of TET2 dysfunction are most dramatically illustrated in the setting of clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP). Age-related somatic mutations in TET2—among the most common CHIP-associated mutations—produce hematopoietic stem cell clones biased toward generating myeloid and lymphoid cells with intrinsically impaired TET2 function [96]. Tregs arising from TET2-mutant progenitors exhibit baseline TSDR hypermethylation and are primed for ex-Treg conversion upon encountering inflammatory stimuli. This connection between aging, somatic mutation, and Treg instability provides a molecular explanation for the phenomenon of “inflammaging”—the chronic, low-grade inflammation that characterizes the aging immune system and contributes to age-related morbidity.

This system indicates that age-related immune dysfunction is not purely the absence of effective regulation but the active participation of former regulators in sustaining inflammation. Interventions that stabilize Treg identity or block ex-Treg conversion may have wider applications in promoting healthy aging than previously appreciated.

Engineering Stability for Cell Therapy

For the emerging field of regulatory T cell therapy, the lessons of ex-Treg biology are directly and urgently translatable. While Treg cell therapy has shown acceptable safety profiles in early-phase clinical trials for graft-versus-host disease, autoimmune conditions, and solid organ transplantation, questions regarding the stability of infused cells have moved from theoretical concern to documented reality.

Evidence of Treg instability in clinical cell therapy. The most definitive evidence comes from the CTOT-21/TASK trial, a prospective randomized study of ex vivo expanded polyclonal Tregs in kidney transplant recipients with subclinical graft inflammation [97]. Using deuterium labeling to permanently mark infused Tregs, the investigators demonstrated that deuterium-labeled cells emerged in the non-Treg (conventional T cell) compartment in four of seven recipients, indicating loss of FOXP3 expression and regulatory identity in vivo. Importantly, this instability was associated with higher serum cytokine levels (indicative of systemic inflammation), low IL-2 bioavailability (including detectable anti-IL-2 autoantibodies in one participant), and Treg products with suboptimal epigenetic integrity (reduced TSDR demethylation and FOXP3+Helios– content). This study establishes that Treg instability is not merely a laboratory phenomenon but a clinically relevant challenge that must be addressed for successful cell therapy.

Current approaches to Treg cell therapy prioritize epigenetic locking as a non-negotiable design criterion. Strategies include: engineering constitutive TSDR demethylation using targeted epigenetic editing; enforcing TET2 expression to maintain active demethylation; modifying metabolic pathways to favor FAO over glycolysis; and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-mediated deletion of pro-inflammatory cytokine loci to prevent effector function even if FOXP3 expression is lost [[98], [99], [100]].

A breakthrough in this area came in October 2025, when two complementary papers in Science Translational Medicine [101,102] demonstrated that inhibition of cyclin-dependent kinase 8/19 (CDK8/19) combined with CD28 signaling deprivation can convert conventional T cells into stable and functional induced Tregs (S/F-iTregs). These cells possess the canonical TSDR demethylation signature of natural Tregs and successfully treated autoimmune models, including inflammatory bowel disease and graft-versus-host disease, without losing their suppressive identity. This work represents the first successful engineering of epigenetically stable iTregs for therapeutic use, immediately translating our knowledge of ex-Treg biology into a concrete clinical strategy.

Second-generation CAR-Treg products have been developed to co-express FOXP3 alongside the antigen-specific CAR, a modification designed to maintain stable FOXP3 expression in hostile microenvironments and prevent conversion into pathogenic effector cells [103]. The goal is a cell therapy that retains the therapeutic benefits of Treg suppression—antigen-specific inhibition of pathogenic immune responses—without the risk of renegade conversion. The clinical trials now underway for rheumatoid arthritis (SBT777101), organ transplantation (NCT05234190), and neurodegenerative diseases will determine whether these engineering strategies have succeeded [104].

Concluding Remarks and Perspectives

The journey of the ex-Treg from controversial artifact to central paradigm illustrates the self-correcting character of scientific research. This concluding section synthesizes the major unresolved questions that will shape the next decade of investigation and reflects on how the field has moved from binary debates to a nuanced understanding of Treg fate regulation.

Open Questions and Future Directions

Despite substantial progress across more than two decades of intensive investigation, fundamental questions about Treg instability and ex-Treg biology remain unresolved.

Heterogeneity of ex-Treg populations

Do ex-Tregs arising from different tissues, under different inflammatory stimuli, or from different Treg subsets comprise distinct functional lineages or points on a common trajectory? Single-cell transcriptomic and epigenetic analyses are beginning to address this question, with the atherosclerosis study disclosing a distinct cytotoxic ex-Treg signature in humans [89]. Whether this heterogeneity indicates distinct conversion pathways or random variation within a common program is unknown. The identification of Tcf7/TCF-1 as a driver of Tfr-to-Tfh conversion suggests that different transcriptional regulators may govern ex-Treg generation in different Treg subsets, consistent with the 2017 finding that activation-induced instability varies across functional contexts [68].

Reversibility of the ex-Treg state

The conventional view holds that TSDR hypermethylation is irreversible—that once a Treg has undergone complete epigenetic conversion, it cannot re-enter the regulatory lineage. Emerging evidence challenges this dogma [68]. TET overexpression or activation can partially reverse TSDR methylation and restore FOXP3 expression in some experimental systems. Metabolic interventions that force oxidative phosphorylation over glycolysis may create permissive conditions for epigenetic reprogramming. The auxin-inducible degron system’s demonstration that Tregs in healthy environments can recover from transient FOXP3 loss suggests that reversibility may be context-dependent and more common than previously appreciated. The extent to which “true” ex-Tregs can be reclaimed as suppressors remains an open question with substantial therapeutic implications.

Relationship to other T-cell dysfunction states

The relationship between Treg instability and other forms of T cell dysfunction—exhaustion, senescence, and anergy—has not been rigorously studied. Exhausted CD8+ T cells in chronic infection and cancer exhibit epigenetic remodeling at effector loci, whereas senescent T cells acquire distinctive metabolic and secretory phenotypes. Whether Treg instability shares common molecular mechanisms with these states, or represents a distinct form of T cell dysfunction, is unknown.

Biomarkers of impending instability

The ability to predict which Tregs are at risk for pathogenic conversion would have substantial clinical utility. Candidates include partial TSDR methylation detectable in circulating Tregs, metabolic parameters (mitochondrial membrane potential and glycolytic rate), surface marker expression (including downregulation of CD25) [49], and cytokine production capacity (low-level IFN-γ or IL-17) [90]. The identification of ICOS signaling as a driver of instability and Tcf7/TCF-1 as an early transcriptional marker of impending conversion provides new candidate biomarkers for prospective studies [28,68]. Prospective studies matching these candidate biomarkers with subsequent clinical outcomes in autoimmune disease or post-Treg infusion are urgently needed.

Evolutionary conservation of instability processes

Most mechanistic studies of regulatory T cell instability have been conducted in mouse models. The extent to which similar mechanisms operate in humans—and the extent to which they are conserved across mammalian evolution—has not been completely defined. Human Tregs show greater heterogeneity in TSDR methylation and FOXP3 expression stability than their murine counterparts, and the contribution of ex-Tregs to human autoimmune pathology, while strongly inferred, has not been definitively proven. The 2023 identification of cytotoxic ex-Tregs in human atherosclerosis represents a major step forward [89], but much work remains to translate murine mechanistic insights—including the IL-2-to-co-stimulatory switch and Tcf7/TCF-1-driven instability—into human biology [28,68].

Therapeutic translation of stability processes

The successful generation of S/F-iTregs through CDK8/19 inhibition and CD28 deprivation demonstrates that mechanistic understanding can be directly translated into therapeutic innovation [101,102]. Future directions entail developing small molecules that selectively stabilize the TSDR in Tregs without affecting other cell types, engineering CAR-Tregs with built-in epigenetic locks, and developing strategies to destabilize tumor-resident Tregs while protecting systemic tolerance.

From Controversy to New Normal

The journey of the ex-Treg from controversial artifact to central paradigm illustrates the self-correcting character of scientific research. What began as a heated debate over the interpretation of fate-mapping experiments has matured, over approximately a decade, into a nuanced molecular understanding of how epigenetic state, metabolic program, and environmental context intersect to determine Treg fate.

The resolution of the controversy was not achieved through the victory of one camp over another, but through the advancement of a more sophisticated conceptual framework. The epigenetic characterization of the TSDR provided a molecular gold standard that distinguished true lineage commitment from transient activation. The appreciation of Treg heterogeneity—developmental, phenotypic, and functional—replaced a monolithic model with a more subtle understanding of a family of related states. The recognition of adaptive plasticity as distinct from pathological instability transformed what had appeared as a threat to the Treg lineage concept into evidence of its contextual sophistication.

Our “self vs non-self” hypothesis provided the conceptual architecture that transformed ex-Tregs from biological errors to evolutionary solutions [65]. In doing so, it illuminated why the same mechanism that protects us from over-suppression during acute infection renders us vulnerable to autoimmune destruction in chronic inflammation and, paradoxically, offers a new therapeutic weapon against cancer. The hypothesis integrated the ex-Treg into a coherent functional framework rather than treating it as an anomaly to be explained away.

The awarding of the 2025 Nobel Prize to Shimon Sakaguchi, Mary Brunkow, and Fred Ramsdell acknowledges that none of this subsequent complexity would exist without the basic discovery of Tregs and FOXP3. The ex-Treg controversy was made possible—indeed, necessary—by the accuracy of the molecular and cellular tools their work provided. It is precisely because Tregs were so clearly and convincingly defined that their instability could be recognized as both notable and significant. A fuzzier, less well-defined lineage would have generated no controversy because it would have generated no clear expectations.

The “new normal” in Treg biology is thus not a retreat from the certainties of the lineage commitment model, but an acceptance of complexity built upon that model’s foundation. Stability and instability are not opposing binary states but poles on a continuum, dynamically regulated by integrated signals from TCR, cytokines, metabolism, and epigenetics. The ex-Treg, once a threat to the lineage commitment model, has become its most instructive exception—revealing the rules by which the system operates precisely when it breaks them.

The therapeutic consequences of this evolved knowledge are now being realized. In autoimmunity, methods to stabilize Tregs and prevent pathogenic conversion are advancing through preclinical development. In oncology, deliberate destabilization of tumor-resident Tregs is emerging as an encouraging immunotherapeutic strategy. In cell therapy, epigenetic locking of engineered Tregs is a non-negotiable design criterion—now achievable through CDK8/19 inhibition. In aging, the connection between clonal hematopoiesis, TET2 mutation, and ex-Treg conversion provides a molecular framework for understanding inflammaging and intervening to promote healthy immune aging.

Two decades ago, the question was whether Tregs existed. One decade ago, the question was whether Tregs could become unstable. Today, the question is how we can measure, predict, and therapeutically manipulate that instability. The controversy is resolved not because one side prevailed, but because the field developed the tools and concepts to understand what both sides were seeing. The ex-Treg has completed its journey from heresy to hypothesis to therapeutic target—a trajectory that embodies the essential dialectic of scientific development.
