Simultaneous Transcatheter Closure of a Persistent Iatrogenic Atrial Septal Defect and Incidental Patent Foramen Ovale
Authors: Daisuke Kanda, Yota Uchiyama, Yoshihisa Horizoe, Akari Tajima, Hiroyuki Tabata, Mitsuru Ohishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe a transcatheter strategy for simultaneous closure of a persistent iatrogenic atrial septal defect (iASD) and adjacent patent foramen ovale (PFO) using a single Cribriform occluder in a patient with exertional hypoxemia.Key Steps1) Preprocedural transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and bubble study to assess both defects, quantify shunting, and their spatial relationship; 2) selective wire crossing of the iASD under real-time 3-dimensional TEE guidance; 3) deployment of a Cribriform occluder to bridge both adjacent defects; and 4) confirm device stability and adequate closure with postdeployment color Doppler and bubble study.Potential PitfallsThe guidewire may preferentially cross the larger PFO rather than the smaller iASD. Careful assessment of the interdefect distance is essential because widely separated defects may require 2 occluders rather than a single bridging device.Take-Home MessagesSystematic TEE evaluation can identify a concomitant PFO before iASD closure. In anatomically suitable cases, simultaneous single-device closure can effectively eliminate right-to-left shunting.Key Words: catheter ablation, exertional hypoxemia, iatrogenic atrial septal defect, patent foramen ovale, transcatheter closure

Persistent iatrogenic atrial septal defect (iASD) after transseptal catheter ablation occurs in up to 26% of patients and typically resolves spontaneously within 12 months.1 Hemodynamically significant right-to-left (R-L) shunting causing exertional hypoxemia is rarely encountered but may develop years after the initial procedure, particularly in the presence of structural right heart disease or elevated right-sided pressures. A systematic preprocedural transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) evaluation may identify a concomitant patent foramen ovale (PFO), which can contribute independently to R-L shunting and paradoxical embolic risk. When both defects are anatomically adjacent, simultaneous single-device closure may be feasible and can avoid overlapping septal occluders; however, careful assessment of the interdefect distance is essential because widely separated defects may require 2 separate devices.

Take-Home Messages

Case Summary

A 74-year-old Japanese woman was referred to our structural heart disease program for an evaluation of progressive exertional dyspnea (NYHA functional class II-III). Between 2016 and 2017, she had undergone 3 radiofrequency catheter ablation procedures for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter, including pulmonary vein isolation, left atrial posterior wall isolation, and right atrial septal ablation for atrial tachycardia. Sick sinus syndrome subsequently developed and required dual-chamber pacemaker implantation in July 2017. At that time, an iASD with R-L shunting was identified and managed conservatively. Subsequently, exertional dyspnea gradually worsened. Exercise stress testing at the referring hospital demonstrated desaturation to 89% during exertion without evidence of an alternative cardiopulmonary cause. Therefore, the patient was referred to our institution for consideration of transcatheter closure. On admission, resting oxygen saturation was 98% on room air. The patient also had chronic kidney disease and no history of stroke, systemic thromboembolism, or pulmonary hypertension. The brain natriuretic peptide level was elevated (264 pg/mL). Chest radiography showed a cardiothoracic ratio of 61.5% without pulmonary congestion. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed the iASD with a pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio of 1.5, inspiratory reversal of shunting, biatrial enlargement, moderate-to-severe tricuspid regurgitation, and an estimated right ventricular systolic pressure of 31 mm Hg (Figure 1). TEE additionally identified a concomitant PFO. Both defects had adequate rims and were morphologically suitable for percutaneous closure. Three-dimensional TEE delineated the close spatial relationship between the 2 defects within the interatrial septum (Figure 2, Video 1). Exercise stress echocardiography demonstrated augmentation of R-L shunting across the iASD during exertion. An agitated saline contrast study demonstrated grade 4 R-L shunting at rest, thus confirming the hemodynamic significance of the combined interatrial communications (Figure 3, Video 2). The concomitant PFO demonstrated substantial R-L shunting. Although the patient did not have a history of stroke, the combination of a large R-L shunt on bubble study and a risk of paradoxical embolism score of 4 placed this case within the “Possible” classification of the PFO-associated stroke causal likelihood framework, if applied for reference purposes (Supplemental Figure 1).

Procedural Steps

Transcatheter closure was performed under general anesthesia with continuous TEE guidance via right femoral venous access.

•The guidewire preferentially crossed the larger and more accessible PFO rather than the relatively small iASD orifice via right femoral venous access, thus making selective wire crossing of the iASD technically challenging. Under continuous TEE guidance, successful selective crossing of the iASD was ultimately achieved using a Radifocus Guide Wire M (Terumo Corporation).

•Given the relatively small iASD orifice and the close interdefect distance confirmed on preprocedural TEE (Figure 4), a 35-mm Amplatzer multifenestrated septal occluder (Cribriform; Abbott) was selected to simultaneously bridge both defects while avoiding excessive overlap that may have resulted from deployment of 2 separate devices. Potential Pitfalls

In this patient, 3 transseptal procedures performed over 8 months likely produced a persistent defect of sufficient size to sustain R-L shunting. Although resting right ventricular systolic pressure was not elevated, paced right ventricular contraction, biatrial enlargement secondary to longstanding atrial fibrillation, concomitant moderate-to-severe tricuspid regurgitation, and the augmenting effects of inspiration and exertion collectively promoted sustained R-L shunting, as evidenced by grade 4 bubble passage and a pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio of 1.5, ultimately resulting in progressive exertional hypoxemia over 8 years. Significant tricuspid regurgitation has also been reported as a contributing factor to R-L shunting through iASDs after transcatheter mitral valve repair, even in the absence of overt pulmonary hypertension.2 Because the clinical course and severity of tricuspid regurgitation before referral were unknown, a direct causal relationship between tricuspid regurgitation and R-L shunting could not be established; however, a contributory role remained plausible. Prior reports of symptomatic iASDs after ablation have predominantly described acute or subacute presentations requiring emergency repair.3,4 A surgically repaired case approximately 4 years after repeated ablation has also been reported; however, that patient had left-to-right shunting rather than hypoxemia.5 To our knowledge, the present case represents one of the longest reported intervals between transseptal ablation and symptomatic catheter-based intervention. Therefore, clinicians should recognize that a chronic iASD may remain clinically relevant for many years and should be considered in the evaluation of otherwise unexplained exertional dyspnea after prior transseptal procedures. A recent comprehensive review identified systemic oxygen desaturation and paradoxical embolic risk as principal indications for percutaneous iASD closure, and prolonged persistence represented an additional marker that supported intervention.6 These observations highlight the importance of careful assessment of shunt severity and right-sided hemodynamics before transcatheter closure.

A systematic TEE evaluation before closure revealed a concomitant PFO with substantial R-L shunting. Whether the PFO was present but unrecognized at the time of prior ablation procedures cannot be determined from available records. Although this patient did not experience a prior cerebrovascular event, a large-shunt PFO may confer a clinically relevant paradoxical embolic risk and may contribute to exertional hypoxemia.7 When considered together with a risk of paradoxical embolism score of 4, the large-shunt PFO corresponded to the “Possible” category of the PFO-associated stroke causal likelihood classification; in this subgroup, percutaneous closure has been associated with a reduced risk of ischemic stroke.8,9 Therefore, systematic preprocedural TEE and bubble study are important not only to identify concomitant defects but also to guide procedural planning. In addition, leaving the PFO untreated after iASD closure could necessitate a second transcatheter intervention, potentially resulting in overlapping interatrial septal devices.10 Therefore, simultaneous single-device closure during a single session was considered a reasonable strategy in this anatomically suitable case.

When both defects coexist, a critical pitfall is misjudging the interdefect distance and individual defect sizes, as these factors directly determine whether a single bridging device or 2 separate occluders should be used. A single multifenestrated septal occluder may be feasible when the total distance from the outer margin of the iASD to the far edge of the PFO does not exceed half the diameter of the device's left atrial disc. Conversely, widely separated defects that cannot be safely covered by a single device may require 2 dedicated occluders to ensure complete defect coverage and stable device positioning. Attempting single-device closure in such anatomy may result in incomplete apposition, residual shunting, or device instability. When anatomically feasible, a single-device strategy may also avoid overlapping septal occluders and reduce the amount of implanted septal material, which could facilitate future transseptal access if additional left-sided interventions become necessary. Therefore, careful preprocedural assessment with 3-dimensional TEE is essential for procedural planning and device selection.

Selective wire crossing of the iASD can be technically challenging because the guidewire may preferentially cross the larger and more accessible PFO. Continuous TEE guidance facilitates safe and accurate wire positioning across the intended defect.

Conclusions

A persistent iASD years after catheter ablation may be an underrecognized cause of progressive exertional hypoxemia. A systematic TEE evaluation before transcatheter closure can identify a concomitant PFO and help guide the procedural strategy. In selected anatomically suitable cases, simultaneous single-device closure may resolve hypoxemia, potentially reduce the paradoxical embolic risk, and avoid overlapping occluders. In the present case, closure resulted in immediate improvement in exercise oxygen saturation and dramatic relief of exertional dyspnea, with sustained symptomatic benefit and reduction of tricuspid regurgitation severity at follow-up.
