Phage Henu15-resistant mutant derives fitness trade-offs in Morganella morganii
Authors: Yuhan Wang, Jiawen Shen, Hanyue Zhuang, Huiru Cao, Jingjing Li, Xiaoxue Yang, Fangyuan Liu, Qian Wang, Jiajia Xie, Jiayi Geng, Waner Wang, Yueting Xi, Xiaoqing Wang, Qiming Li, Tieshan Teng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid emergence of multidrug-resistant strains of Morganella necessitates new therapeutic approaches, such as phage therapy. Phages are considered a promising adjunct to antibiotics. However, the rapid emergence of phage-resistant bacterial mutants poses a significant challenge in clinical practice. Nevertheless, this phenomenon is often accompanied by fitness trade-offs. Therefore, understanding these fitness trade-offs is crucial prior to phage clinical application. In the present study, a new lytic Morganella morganii phage, named Henu15, was isolated and characterized. The phage genome comprises a 52,795 bp double-stranded DNA with 72 open reading frames. Notably, the phage genome lacks genes encoding integrases, known virulence factors, or acquired antibiotic resistance determinants, underscoring its therapeutic potential. A time-kill assay demonstrated that Henu15 exhibits synergistic effects with ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, and ceftazidime. Importantly, a Henu15-resistant mutant exhibited pleiotropic defects, including impaired adsorption, enhanced sensitivity to polymyxin B and tetracycline, reduced biofilm formation, and diminished in vivo colonization capacity. Through the whole-genome resequencing of the phage-resistant mutant, mutations were found in 7 genes involved in encoding related proteins such as ATP-dependent chaperone protein ClpB, tetrasulfate reductase subunit A, and AlpA family transcriptional regulators, which might be responsible for the observed fitness defects, providing a molecular basis for the attenuated virulence. Together, these findings provide new insights into the evolutionary interactions between phage resistance and bacterial fitness that could potentially offer a novel approach to treating resistant Morganella infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13568-026-02032-6.

Introduction

Morganella species are opportunistic pathogens that can cause various infections, particularly in hospital settings (Chen et al. 2024; Silva et al. 2024). Morganella is primarily transmitted via contact with contaminated surfaces or medical devices, with infection occurring in immunocompromised patients. The outbreaks of Morganella infection are mainly due to poor hygiene and medical procedures. Morganella spp. account for an increasing number of nosocomial infections worldwide (Shi et al. 2022). M. morganii has been associated with severe cases of urinary tract infections and bacteremia (Bandy 2020; Shi et al. 2022). While antibiotics are usually prescribed for treating Morganella infections, its indiscriminate use has resulted in the emergence of multidrug-resistant Morganella strains, which undermines the successful control of the disease and poses a significant public health threat (Kara et al. 2024). Therefore, the exploration of alternative therapeutic approaches is urgently required to combat the rising antimicrobial resistance.

Phage therapy has garnered increasing attention for treating bacterial infections. Phage therapy has been reported as an effective adjunct to existing antibiotics (Gordillo Altamirano and Barr 2019; Strathdee et al. 2023). Morganella infections are considered potential targets for phage therapy (Shahin et al. 2018). However, with the interplay between bacteria and phages, the emergence of phage-resistant bacterial mutants is inevitable and is considered a major concern regarding the clinical application of phages. Bacteria evolve resistance to phages through several mechanisms, among which are the prevention of phage adsorption through mutations in phage receptor sites, restriction-modification, the CRISPR/Cas system, and abortive infection (Egido et al. 2022).

A critical consideration in this evolutionary arms race is the concept of fitness trade-offs. The acquisition of phage resistance often imposes a physiological cost on the bacterium, which can manifest as attenuated virulence, enhanced susceptibility to antibiotics, or reduced resilience to environmental stresses (Fujiki et al. 2023). Additionally, phage-resistant bacteria tend to become more vulnerable to host-clearance mechanisms (Markwitz et al. 2021). However, such fitness costs may vary among different bacterial genera and species (Feng et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2022). Accordingly, a better understanding of the potential fitness trade-offs as a countermeasure against phage resistance is crucial prior to phage clinical application. The current study aimed at isolating a novel lytic phage, Henu15, and investigating its biological and genomic characteristics in addition to evaluating the therapeutic effectiveness of certain phage-antibiotic combinations against M. morganii.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and cultures

The phage host bacterium used in this study, Morganella morganii, was isolated from clinical cervicitis specimens obtained from Huaihe Hospital of Henan University. The initial sample was inoculated onto Luria Bertani broth (LB) agar plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. A single colony was then transferred into LB broth and cultured overnight at 37 °C with shaking. Subsequently, the bacterial culture was streaked again onto LB agar plates, and this purification step was repeated 2–3 times to obtain a pure isolate. The isolate was identified as M. morganii through PCR amplification and sequencing using primers 27f (5′-AGAGTTTGATCAT GGCTCAG-3′) and 1492r (5′-TACGGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′), combined with mass spectrometry analysis.

Phage isolation and purification

The phage Henu15 was isolated and purified as previously described (Gencay et al. 2017; Hao et al. 2023). Briefly, 30 mL of untreated sewage was centrifuged at 5000 × g, and the supernatant was collected. Then, 2 mL of a log-phase culture of the clinical Morganella morganii strain MM001 was added to the supernatant, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking for 24 h. Phage plaques were subsequently detected using the double-layer agar plate method (Golosova et al. 2024; Kenney and Gómez-Duarte 2024). A single plaque was picked, dissolved in phage buffer, and subjected to repeated purification via the same double-layer agar technique (Yousefi et al. 2023). This purification step was performed at least five times to obtain a pure phage isolate.

For the amplification of phage Henu15 (Gencay et al. 2017; Gill and Hyman 2010), a log-phase culture of Morganella morganii MM001 (OD600 = 0.5) was mixed with the phage at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01. Then, the mixture was co-cultured in LB medium supplemented with 10 mM MgSO₄ and 10 mM CaCl₂ at 37 °C with shaking for 12 h (Gao et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025). After incubation, the culture was centrifuged at 4762×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm pore-size membrane, and the phage particles in the filtrate were concentrated using a 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off ultrafiltration device. The phage titer was quantified by the standard double-layer agar plaque assay.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The morphology of phage Henu15 was characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) following previously established protocols (Eckstein et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021). Briefly, purified phage Henu15 with a high titer (109 PFU/mL) was applied to copper grids (Gilder Grids). The samples were then negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate to enhance contrast. Imaging was performed by ZHONG KE BAI CE (http://www.zkbaice.cn/, Beijing) using a JEOL JEM 1011 transmission electron microscope operating at 80 kV (JEOL, Japan). The resulting images provided detailed visualization of the phage’s structural characteristics. The measurement of the phage head diameter was achieved using the ruler tool in Adobe Photoshop CS5.

Multiplicity of infection (MOI) and one-step growth curve

This study employed an endpoint detection assay to determine the optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) yielding the highest progeny phage titer. The procedure (Jofre and Muniesa 2020) was as follows: an overnight culture of M. morganii MM001 was diluted with fresh medium to a concentration of 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. Phage Henu15 was then added at different MOIs of 100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.01, followed by co-incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and subjected to tenfold serial dilution. A volume of 5 μL from each dilution was plated using the double-layer agar method. After incubation at 37 °C for 12 h, plaques were counted. Finally, based on the results of three independent replicates, the MOI that produced the highest average progeny phage titer was defined as the optimal MOI.

The one-step growth curve was conducted following a previously established protocol with some modifications (Han et al. 2023; Ryan et al. 2012). In brief, M. morganii MM001 cultured to logarithmic phase (2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) was infected with phage Henu15 (2 × 10⁶ PFU/mL) at a MOI of 0.01. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking, and samples were collected at 5- minute intervals over 120 min for phage titration. Following centrifugation at 8000×g, the supernatant was serially diluted tenfold to a final dilution of 10⁻⁸. A 5 µL aliquot from each dilution was spotted onto double-layer agar plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C before plaque observation and enumeration (Islam et al. 2023). The burst size, defined as the average number of progeny phage particles released from each infected host cell, is calculated using the formula B = Pt/I0, in which Pt is the total phage titer (typically in PFU/mL) at the end of the growth cycle and I0 represents the initial number of successfully infected host cells per mL.The experiment was performed in three independent replicates.

Stability analysis of phage Henu15

The phage stability was assessed as previously described (Uyttebroek et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2024). Briefly, the phage Henu15 was incubated at temperatures ranging from 4 to 80 °C, different pH values (pH 3–12), and chloroform at ratios of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100% for 1 h. Then, the phage titer was assessed using the DLA technique. The phage Henu15 stability under ultraviolet (UV) light was performed as previously described (Guo et al. 2023), and the phage titer was determined after 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, and 4 h. The experiment was performed in three independent replicates.

Genome DNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Phage DNA extraction was performed with modifications from established protocols (Bonilla & Barr 2018; Montso et al. 2019; Li et al. 2025). Briefly, 10 mL of amplified phage stock was treated with 10% (w/v) PEG8000, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of SM buffer. To remove residual host nucleic acids, 0.5 µL of DNase I (10 mg/mL) and 0.1 µL of RNase A (10 mg/mL) were added to the suspension, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 40 µL of 0.5 M EDTA (pH 8.0), 50 µL of 10% SDS (w/v), and 2 µL of proteinase K (25 mg/mL) were added. The solution was mixed gently and incubated at 56 °C for 1 h to complete lysis.

Following lysis, an equal volume of equilibrated phenol was added and the mixture was vortexed and centrifuged to collect the aqueous phase. Nucleic acids were precipitated by adding 1/10 volume of 3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) and two volumes of absolute ethanol to the aqueous phase, followed by incubation at − 20 °C for 1 h (Ji et al. 2015; Nair et al. 2022). The precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried at room temperature, and finally resuspended in nuclease-free water. DNA quality was evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometric analysis using a NanoDrop instrument.

The whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of the extracted phage genome was performed by Shanghai Sangong Biological Co., Ltd. using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The open reading frames (ORFs) were identified using Prokka (Seemann 2014) and GeneMarkTM (http://exon.gatech.edu/GeneMark/). The phylogenetic tree was constructed with MEGA11 software (Tamura et al. 2021). Homologous DNA and protein sequences' alignment were performed using BLASTN and BLASTP (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). tRNAscan-SE was used to detect tRNA in the phage genome (http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/). Proksee (https://proksee.ca) was used to map the Henu15 phage genome. The antibiotic resistance genes and the virulence factors were predicted using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) Resistance Gene Identifier tool (https://card.mcmaster.ca/analyze/rgi) and the Virulence Factors Database (VFDB) (https://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/), respectively.

Phage-antibiotic synergy assay

The microbroth-dilution method was first employed to assess the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values of eight different antibiotics, including polymyxin B (POL), tetracycline (TCY), ceftazidime (CAZ), ciprofloxacin (CIP), ampicillin (AMP), rifampicin (RIF), norfloxacin (NOR) and kanamycin (KAN) (Karthika et al. 2025). A time-kill assay was conducted to explore the potential phage-antibiotic synergy (PAS) by using sub-MIC values of the tested antibiotics (Zhou et al. 2025). M. morganii MM001 was grown overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 4×g. The culture was then diluted in fresh LB medium to approximately 10⁶ CFU/mL and used as the initial inoculum for all time‑kill assays. Bacteriophage Henu15 was diluted in LB medium to 104 PFU/mL. Antibiotics were prepared in LB medium at a final concentration of 1/2 × MIC (see Table 1 for MIC values). The following treatment groups were included: (1) bacterial control (wild‑type strain only), (2) antibiotic alone (1/2 × MIC), (3) phage alone (MOI = 0.01), and (4) combination treatment with antibiotic (1/2 × MIC) and phage (MOI = 0.01). All groups were incubated at 37 °C with shaking, and 100 μL samples were collected hourly for immediate ten‑fold serial dilution in sterile PBS. From each dilution, 5 μL was spotted onto LB agar plates, followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C and subsequent colony counting (Morris et al. 2025). The interaction plots were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA to investigate the possible PAS. Isolation of phage-resistant M. morganii mutant

To isolate Henu15-resistant mutants, M. morganii MM001 was grown to logarithmic phase (OD600 = 0.5) and infected with Henu15 phage at a optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI = 0.01), followed by incubation for 48 h to enrich for resistant mutants. The culture was then plated on LB agar. Emerging colonies were randomly picked and purified by two successive rounds of streaking on LB plates (D'Andrea et al. 2020). Putative resistant clones—those showing no lysis—were further confirmed by both standard and inverted spot assays, as well as efficiency of plating (EOP) analysis, to verify stable resistance and exclude transient defensive mechanisms (Yehia et al. 2025).

Adsorption and antimicrobial susceptibility assays

The adsorption of phage Henu15 onto the WT strain MM001 and the phage-resistant mutant was conducted at an MOI of 0.01 as previously described (Gordillo Altamirano et al. 2021). At 0, 20, 40, 60 and 80 min, samples were centrifuged and filtered using a 0.22 µm filter to assess free phage titers. The MIC values of the eight antibiotics previously mentioned were re-evaluated against the phage-resistant mutant (Fu et al. 2025). The experiment was performed in three independent replicates.

Stress tolerance assays

A fluorescence-activated cell sorter was employed to evaluate the survival capabilities of M. morganii MM001 WT and the phage-resistant mutants under varying environmental conditions (Hong et al. 2020). Briefly, the strains were incubated under various temperature and pH conditions for 2 h. After incubation, the cultures were centrifuged to discard the supernatant, and the pellets were washed with PBS, followed by co-incubation with propidium iodide (PI) dye (100 µg/mL) at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min (Bass et al. 1983; Brandt and Keston 1965; Hong et al. 2020). Subsequently, fluorescence intensity was measured using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer. For each sample, 100,000 cells were recorded, and data analysis was performed using FlowJo software.

Biofilm formation assay

The biofilm-forming capabilities of the WT and resistant strains (R9 and R15) were assessed using a standard crystal violet staining protocol (O'Toole 2011), with minor modifications as described (Guo et al. 2024). Briefly, single colonies of each strain were selected and inoculated into 3 mL of LB broth. The cultures were incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking. For the assay, 1 mL of fresh LB medium was added to each well of a sterile 12-well cell culture plate. Each well was inoculated with 10 μL of the overnight culture and supplemented with antibiotics to achieve final concentrations of 0.5 μg/mL tetracycline and 0.25 μg/mL kanamycin. The plates were then incubated statically for 72 h at 37 °C to facilitate biofilm formation. After incubation, the medium containing non-adherent cells was carefully aspirated using a pipette or a syringe. The adherent biofilms were gently washed three times with 1× PBS to remove any remaining planktonic cells. The plates were air-dried at room temperature for approximately 30 min. The dried biofilms were fixed with 300 μL of methanol per well for 15 min. After fixation, the methanol was aspirated, and the plates were air-dried again. The fixed biofilms were stained with 300 μL of a 1% (w/v) crystal violet solution for 15 min at room temperature. Following staining, the dye solution was removed, and the plates were inverted and air-dried completely. To quantify the biofilm biomass, the bound crystal violet was solubilized by adding 200 μL of 33% (v/v) glacial acetic acid to each well. The plates were incubated at room temperature for 15–20 min with gentle shaking to ensure complete dissolution. Finally, 100 μL of the solution from each well was transferred to a fresh 96-well plate, and the optical density was measured at 600 nm (OD600) using a micro-plate reader. Wells containing only 33% glacial acetic acid were used as blanks for zeroing the instrument. The experiment was performed in three independent replicates.

Mouse infection model

The mouse infection model was utilized to evaluate the in vivo colonization ability of M. morganii strain MM001 WT and the phage-resistant mutant (Fang et al. 2022). Mice were divided into 2 groups, each comprised of four female BALB/c mice 6 to 10 weeks old. Suspensions of either the WT or the phage-resistant mutant at a concentration of 108 CFU were administered to the mice via intraperitoneal injection. Twelve hours post-infection, mice were euthanized, and tissues including the lungs, liver, right kidney, heart, and spleen were collected, rinsed, weighed, and homogenized in PBS for CFU quantification. All protocols involving mice were approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Subcommittee of Henan University (approval number HUSOM2025-803).

Whole-genome sequencing analysis of phage-resistant mutants

Genomic DNA was extracted from the phage-resistant strains R9 and R15 for whole‑genome resequencing. Briefly, bacteria were cultured with shaking at 37 °C to an OD600 of 0.4. DNA was then extracted using the SolaBio Bacterial DNA Extraction Kit following the manufacturer's protocol. DNA integrity, purity, and concentration were evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis (180 V, 20 min) and measured with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. Qualified DNA samples were subsequently submitted to Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for whole‑genome re-sequencing.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.1 software, primarily utilizing the t-test or two-way ANOVA unless otherwise indicated. Data represents the mean ± standard deviation of three independent experiments.

Results

Morphological characterization of Henu15

A novel lytic phage, named Henu15, was isolated from sewage samples collected at a hospital using M. morganii MM001 as the host. Phage Henu15 produced distinct round, clear plaques on the bacterial host lawn surrounded by translucent halo zones (0.5-1 mm) in diameter (Fig. 1a). TEM showed that phage Henu15 has a contractile tail of about 120 nm long and an icosahedral head (60 ± 2 nm) in diameter (Fig. 1b). Phage Henu15 biological characteristics

To determine the optimal MOI for phage Henu15, we conducted co-culture experiments with exponentially growing M. morganii MM001 cells at varying phage-to-bacteria ratios. The highest progeny phage titer of 109 PFU/mL was observed at an MOI equal to 0.01, indicating that this was the optimal MOI for phage Henu15 (Fig. 1c).

Under these experimental conditions, phage Henu15 demonstrated potent inhibitory activity against M. morganii, completely suppressing bacterial growth within 2 h across the tested MOIs. Adsorption assays showed that approximately 90% of phage particles adsorbed to bacterial cells within 20 min of incubation (Fig. 1e). Furthermore, a one-step growth curve performed at the optimal MOI revealed a latent period of approximately 20 min and a burst size of about 1000 PFU per infected cell (Fig. 1f).

Phage Henu15 stability

The environmental stability of phage Henu15 was assessed to evaluate its clinical potential. Henu15 was stable up to 55 °C, but its infectivity decreased significantly at 65 °C and was completely abolished at 80 °C (Fig. 2a). It remained active over a broad pH range (pH 3–11) (Fig. 2b) and upon chloroform treatment (Fig. 2c). Genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

The Henu15 genome is a linear, double-stranded DNA molecule of 52,795 bp with a G + C content of 45.3%. Annotation identified 72 open reading frames (ORFs), 24 of which were assigned putative functions. To illustrate the evolutionary relationships of the bacteriophage Henu15 with other phages, a phylogenetic tree was constructed on the basis of the conserved phage whole genome and major capsid protein (ORF 49). According to whole genome analysis, the bacteriophage Henu15 was related to the Proteus phage vB_PmiS_PM-CJR (Fig. 3b). Like the major capsid protein presented, the bacteriophage Henu15 was highly correlated with Proteus phage 2207-N35, Proteus phage vB_PmiP_RS10pmA and Proteus phage vB_PmiS_PM-CJR, representing percent identities of 84.66% (Fig. 3c). Importantly, the genome lacks acquired antibiotic-resistance determinants, integrase genes and known bacterial virulence factors, underscoring its safety for potential therapeutic applications.

Phage-antibiotic combination is better than either phage or antibiotic alone

The MIC of each antibiotic against M. morganii MM001 was first determined (Table 1). Afterwards, a time-kill assay was conducted to assess the potential synergy between the phage Henu15 (MOI = 0.01) and 1/2 MIC values of these antibiotics. Interestingly, phage-CIP, phage-CAZ and phage-NOR combination significantly suppressed bacterial growth and delayed the emergence of phage-resistant strains (Fig. 4a–c). While combining POL, AMP, RIF, TCY or KAN with phage Henu15 neither inhibited nor delayed the emergence of resistance (Fig. 4d–h). The phage-CIP combination treatment was 4.44-fold more effective than CIP alone and 5.85-fold more effective than the phage alone (Fig. 4a). The phage-CAZ combination treatment was 4.20-fold more effective than CAZ alone and 5.86-fold more effective than the phage alone (Fig. 4b). The phage-NOR combination treatment was 5.29-fold more effective than NOR alone and 5.86-fold more effective than the phage alone (Fig. 4c). The effects of combining phage with RIF, POL, or TCY were approximately additive (Fig. 4d–g), whereas combinations with AMP or KAN were antagonistic (Fig. 4f, h). Confirmation of phage-resistant phenotype and adsorption assay

To isolate phage-resistant mutants, M. morganii MM001 was co-cultured with phage Henu15 for 48 h. A stable resistant mutant, designated R15, was subsequently isolated and its resistance phenotype was confirmed through a series of assays. The standard spot assay showed that Henu15 formed clear plaques on the WT lawn but produced no plaques on R15 (Fig. 7a). Conversely, the inverted spot assay revealed confluent growth of R15 within the phage-spotted area, which starkly contrasted with the zone of clearance observed in the WT lawn (Fig. 7b). The growth kinetics analysis revealed distinct differences between the WT strain and the two phage-resistant mutants (R9 and R15) in LB medium (Fig. 7d). Notably, the WT strain demonstrates a markedly accelerated growth rate, with an observable increase in OD600 beginning within the first hour of incubation and culminating in a value of 1.3, indicative of robust growth potential. Thermal and acid stress tolerance

The response of the WT strain and its phage-resistant mutants (R9 and R15) to environmental stressors was assessed by measuring changes in fluorescence intensity. Under pH stress, the mutants R9 and R15 exhibited a significant increase in fluorescence intensity compared to the WT at pH 3, 7, 9 and 11, indicating a heightened stress response. No substantial change was observed at pH 5 (Fig. 8a–e). This suggests that while the WT strain robustly tolerated a broad pH range (pH 3–11), the mutant strains were more sensitive to extreme acidic and alkaline conditions, maintaining stability only in a narrow, weak acid to neutral range (around pH 5). Following ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, the fluorescence intensity of R9 was significantly weakened compared to the WT, whereas R15 showed no significant change (Fig. 8f). This indicates that R9 is more sensitive to UV radiation. Under temperature stress, the fluorescence intensity of both R9 and R15 decreased at 25 °C, 37 °C and 80 °C, suggesting impaired temperature tolerance. Conversely, no significant alterations were observed at 42 °C, 55 °C and 65 °C compared to the WT (Fig. 8g–h). Phage-resistant mutant has enhanced sensitivity to TCY and POL

The antibiotic susceptibility testing for the phage-resistant mutant exhibited increased sensitivity to certain antibiotics compared to the WT strain. Specifically, R9 exhibited a 2.4-fold increase in susceptibility to KAN, and R15 showed a fourfold increase in susceptibility to TC (Fig. 8m).

Discussion

The increasing antibiotic resistance of M. morganii poses a significant clinical challenge (Liu et al. 2016), necessitating the exploration of alternative therapeutics such as phage therapy (Shi et al. 2022). In this study, a novel lytic phage, Henu15, specific for M. morganii, was isolated from sewage samples. These characteristics suggested that phage Henu15 represents a promising candidate strain for phage therapy applications.

Recently, the combined use of phages and antibiotics has received increasing attention. In this study, Henu15 exhibited a synergistic effect when used in combination with CIP, CAZ and NOR. The synergy with CAZ, a bactericidal cell wall inhibitor, may be attributed to antibiotic-induced cell filamentation, potentially facilitating the production of more phage particles (Gu Liu et al. 2020). Similarly, quinolone antibiotics (CIP, NOR) are thought to promote phage activity through mechanisms such as cell elongation, increased plaque size, and enhanced burst sizes (Kortright et al. 2019). An additive effect was observed with RIF, POL and TCY. It has been previously reported that phage-POL combinations can substantially perturb bacterial metabolism, potentially resulting in synergistic effects (Oechslin et al. 2017). Furthermore, the use of sub-MIC levels of POL could help minimize its toxicity (Kelesidis and Falagas 2015; Leclercq et al. 2013). With regards to the bacteriostatic (TCY) antibiotic, which inhibits bacterial protein synthesis, the observed PAS aligned with Kamal and Dennis, who attributed it to the cell clustering that allows phage to move laterally across adjoined cell surfaces, thus increasing phage contact with its receptors on different cells (Kamal and Dennis 2015). A possible explanation for the varied outcomes is that the timing and concentration of antibiotics and phages could be crucial factors influencing their interaction, beyond merely their simultaneous application (Manohar et al. 2024).

The acquisition of phage resistance is often accompanied by a bacterial fitness cost, manifesting as attenuation of key virulence or survival-related functions, such as colonization and biofilm formation. Our experimental results—the phenotypic changes observed in the resistant strains R9 and R15—provide direct evidence supporting this common phenomenon. This finding offers new perspectives for optimizing phage therapy. Even if phages fail to completely eradicate an infection, the selective pressure they exert may drive the bacterial population toward a state of “phage resistance coupled with reduced pathogenicity”. From a clinical standpoint, this implies that an infection could shift from an acute, highly virulent state to a less virulent one that is more readily cleared by the host immune system or that permits coexistence with the host, thereby buying valuable time for treatment or improving patient prognosis. This insight suggests that when evaluating the therapeutic potential of a phage, we should not focus solely on its direct bactericidal efficiency but also assess whether the resistant strains it induces are accompanied by attenuated pathogenicity. Employing such phages, which can effectively steer pathogen evolution, in combination with antibiotics or other therapies may represent a more strategic and sustainable approach to infection control.

To identify genetic bases for the observed fitness costs, we performed whole-genome re-sequencing of a phage-resistant mutant. Mutations were identified in genes encoding proteins with known functions were tetrathionate reductase subunit TtrA, AlpA family transcriptional regulator and ATP-dependent chaperone (ClpB). Tetrasulfate reductase subunit TtrA is a membrane-binding enzyme that contains molybdenum methotrexate guanine dinucleotide (MGD) as a cofactor. The selective advantage brought by tetrasulfate respiration is very likely to be typhoid poles. One important cause of gastrointestinal diseases caused by bacteria is that this characteristic leaves the virulence factors required to induce host inflammatory responses under selection, which are necessary for the formation of tetrasulfates in the body (Hensel et al. 1999; Winter et al. 2010). It is speculated that the above-mentioned gene mutations may alter the energy metabolism efficiency and membrane potential, thereby affecting phage adsorption. The AlpA family of transcriptional regulators, AlpA, is the only known virulence gene regulator in Pseudomonas aeruginosa. When response DNA damage occurs, AlpA acts as an anti-terminator during the process. It regulates the expression of virulence genes in a way that may be regulated by the concentration of intracellular small molecule guanosine tetraphosphate (ppGpp) (Peña et al. 2021; Wen et al. 2022). Mutations in AlpA may reduce damage to DNA. It is speculated that the above-mentioned mutations may reduce autolysis sensitivity and phage DNA injection efficiency, thereby leading to the emergence of phage-resistant strains. Bacterial ClpB is a molecular chaperone that plays a crucial role in the survival of bacteria under stress conditions (Nagy et al. 2010), and it also plays a vital role in supporting the virulence of certain bacterial pathogens(Capestany et al. 2008; Kannan et al. 2008; Yang et al. 2024). The deficiency of ClpB in Leptospira also leads to bacterial growth defects under oxidation and heat stress (Krajewska et al. 2016). It is speculated that the mutations of the above-mentioned genes lead to a defect in stress response, indirectly affecting the expression of phage receptors and resulting in a decrease in phage adsorption rate. The 23S rRNA is a key component of the ribosome and a target for several antibiotics; mutations in this gene can affect protein synthesis and antibiotic susceptibility (Krawczyk et al. 2024; Lin et al. 2018). Therefore, the mutated genes are potential candidates for future investigations into phage recognition, biofilm formation, and the pathogenicity of M. morganii. However, the causal relationships between these specific mutations and the observed phenotypic changes remain to be conclusively established and require further experimental validation, such as through genetic complementation or knockout studies.

This study has several limitations. First, the phage-resistant mutants (R9, R15) were isolated as single colones and characterized under controlled laboratory conditions. This colony-based approach, while standard for initial phenotypic and genotypic analysis, may not capture the full spectrum of mutations and phenotypic heterogeneity that could emerge and be maintained at the population level during phage selection. Consequently, our observations might under represent the diversity of resistance mechanisms and associated fitness costs. Additionally, the in vitro setting may not fully replicate the complex and dynamic environment of clinical infections. Second, while whole-genome re-sequencing identified several mutations in the phage-resistant mutant, the direct causal links between these genetic changes and the observed fitness trade-offs remain speculative and necessitate functional validation through genetic experiments. Finally, the in vivo colonization assessment was performed at a bacterial dose, which may not capture the full temporal dynamics of infection and resistance evolution. Further studies are necessary to better understand the precise mechanisms through which these mutations contribute to the phage-resistant phenotype. A better understanding of the fitness trade-offs might fill some gaps in the future success of phage therapy in combating resistant M. morganii infections.
