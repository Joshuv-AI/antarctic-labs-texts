Television (TV) Food Commercials Target Children: A Content Analysis of TV Food Advertisements in Iran
Authors: Maryam Amini, Mehrnoosh Shafaatdoost, Delaram Ghodsi, Mohadese Borazjani, Maryam Aghayan, Nastaran Shariatzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:This study aimed to analyze the content of Iran’s television (TV) food advertisements directed at children in 2020.Methods:All the programs broadcast on Channel II TV were downloaded for one week; however, only children’s programs were analyzed. The validated protocol was used to analyze the content of the advertisements. In addition, compliance of food advertisements with Iran’s, World Health Organization Eastern Mediterranean Region (WHO EMR), and International Network for Food and Obesity/Non-communicable Diseases (NCDs) Research Monitoring and Action Support (INFORMAS) standards was assessed.Results:A total of 761 TV advertisements targeted children on channel II. “Food products” were the most frequent advertisements (41.7%). “Desserts and sweets” were the most advertised food products (46%), followed by “condiments” and “dairy products” (20.5% and 11.7%, respectively). “Stimulation of appetite” and “high quality/careful preparation” were the most frequent appeals for advertisements directed at children. “Animation/Company-owned character(s)” was the most frequent advertising strategy. Most food advertisements adhered to Iran’s regulations; however, according to the WHO EMR and INFORMAS, most food advertisements have not met advertising standards. Present research revealed that food products were a prominent part of other advertisements on children’s TV.Conclusions:Children were predominantly exposed to foods high in fat, salt, and sugar, and the share of unhealthy food advertisements, such as desserts, sweets, fast foods, fats, and sugared drinks, was significant. Regarding the high violation rate of the advertising regulations, a more careful audit of commercials targeting children is highly recommended.

Introduction

Childhood obesity is a global public health problem.[1] According to the World Health Organization (WHO), 39 million under-5 children are overweight or obese,[2] and the number of obese children and adolescents is predicted to increase to as much as 254 million by 2030.[3]

While screen exposure currently is a non-negligible part of children’s life, it is reported to be linked with childhood obesity.[4] Based on a meta-analysis, the adolescents who were more exposed to different screen activities were more likely to be overweight or obese, and television (TV) watching had the most effect.[5] Spending hours in front of the TV, computer, or smartphone may cause childhood obesity in different ways; among them, food marketing has been suggested as a potential contributor.[6]

Food marketing affects childhood awareness, likings, buying behaviors, and dietary patterns, and has been associated with less-healthy dietary habits and obesity.[789] By targeting young children who cannot realize the true intention of food marketers, food marketing impresses their requests and makes them consume the advertised products.[9] It has also been shown that children, during or after watching food advertisements, chose more sugary and unhealthy food options over healthier alternatives.[1011]

Children are a vulnerable target group of commercial messages, and the appeals, namely fun, peer acceptance, and pleasure, used in the advertisements thoroughly fascinate them.[12] In addition to being able to shop independently, children can also affect family purchases. According to a study, half of the children requested that the family buy the advertised food products they had already watched on TV.[13] Furthermore, children are known as “teenage and adult shoppers of the future.”[14] Hereupon, the great importance of this age group for food marketers becomes clear.

Iran is among the countries estimated to have more than one million children with obesity in 2030. Hence, understanding the content and nature of TV food advertisements as potential contributors to childhood obesity is essential.

The purpose of the present study was to analyze the frequency, content, and method of presentation of food advertisements directed at children on channel II of Iran’s TV over one week in 2020. Additionally, the rate of advertising violations was measured according to the domestic and international standards.

Methods

It was a cross-sectional study in which the content of TV advertisements was analyzed based on a pre-defined protocol.[15] Data were collected in an ordinary week, November 10–16, 2020, and did not coincide with any religious, national, or political occasion in Iran.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of National Nutrition and Food Technology Research Institute, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Code: IR.SBMU.nnftri.Rec. 1398.072.

Data collection

All the programs broadcast on Channel II TV were downloaded through the Telewebion website.[16] Currently, there are two channels specific to children and adolescents in Iran; however, broadcasting of advertisements is forbidden on them. Channel II was selected because, at the time of conducting the current study, it had special programs for children and adolescents.

Measures

The validated protocol was used to analyze the content of the advertisements in the target media. It was developed based on the INFORMAS protocol for analyzing food advertisements.[17] The details of the validation of the protocols have been explained elsewhere.[15] All strategies and claims were taken from the protocol. The duration of advertisements was calculated manually by subtracting the time of the end from the time of initiation of each. The violation rate of advertising was measured according to Iran’s standards,[18] WHO EMR,[19] and INFORMAS.[17]

Data analysis

The unit of the analysis was an “advertisement.” Therefore, if a brand was shown in different advertisements, all were analyzed separately. Analysis of the advertisements was performed in two steps. Initially, all the advertisements were observed and then coded by two evaluators, three days by one and four days by another. They fast-forwarded through TV programs and watched the advertisements in full. The evaluators watched advertisements at least three times to document the needed information.

In the first step, for each advertisement, the date of broadcasting, the weekday, the time of initiation and termination of broadcasting, the duration of the advertisement (second[s], by subtracting termination from initiation time), brand, and the advertisement category (whether it advertised foods or other products) were recorded in a sheet.

Although all brands of the depicted food products were recorded, they were not informed in the final report. Then, the information specific to food products was extracted. The information included the violations in food advertising according to the mentioned references, advertising appeals, power, which meant having/applying any strategy in the advertisements, type of strategy, claim(s) in general, nutritional claim(s), educational content, and gift suggestion.

Quality control

We checked all the sheets to ensure all the broadcast advertisements were documented. The reliability was measured for the time duration calculated for the advertisements, cases of advertising violations, and the advertising appeals.

To determine reliability, the level of agreement between coders was used. For this purpose, 20% of ads were coded by two researchers, independently. The agreement coefficient was calculated using the following formula.[20] For all cases, the agreement coefficient ranged between 93% and 100%.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were done using Excel (version 16.68, 2019) and IBM SPSS Statistics 26. Descriptive statistics (mean, percentage, standard deviation) and Chi-square (to compare percentages) were used.

Discussion

In the current study, the content of TV commercials that targeted children on channel II of IRIB was followed for one week. During the research period, food advertisements ranked first among all advertisements broadcast in terms of frequency; however, in terms of time duration, they ranked second. This verifies that the average time of food advertisements was less than that of “home/car appliances and services”.

These findings were similar to previous research conducted in Iran, indicating that food advertisements were among the most frequent TV advertisements on children’s programs.[2021] In the study performed in 2005, the advertisements on food products ranked second in frequency and fourth in terms of duration among other advertisements on channel I.[21] Two similar studies conducted to examine the content of food advertising in children’s and adolescents’ programs showed that although the frequency of food advertisements did not rank first, nearly one-fourth of the duration and frequency of the advertisements were still allocated to food products.[212223] Furthermore, another study (2015), which analyzed two weeks of advertisements broadcast during children’s programs on channel II and channel education in Iran, yielded similar results. Regarding the duration and frequency, the share of food advertisements among other TV advertisements ranked fifth and sixth, respectively.[24] Studies conducted in Switzerland, Lebanon, and Poland also brought about similar results in which food advertisements were more than other products, especially during children’s programs.[252627] However, the results of our study are in contrast with the study conducted by Arora et al.,[28] in Sydney, where there were very few food and drink advertisements focused on children and adolescents.

The higher frequency of the advertisements broadcast on weekends compared to weekdays was noteworthy. The same results were derived from other studies conducted Malaysia and Singapore, indicating that more advertisements were depicted on weekends in Malaysia, and significantly more unhealthy food advertisements were screened on weekends compared with weekdays in Singapore.[2930]

“Desserts and sweets” were the most advertised food products in the current study. Consistent with studies previously conducted in Iran, the food commercials directed at Iranian children were dominated by energy-dense foods of low nutritional value.[2123]

This finding is similar to studies on children in different countries in which advertisements of confectionery and sweets ranked among the highest.[263031] Also, an analysis of food advertisements targeting children on Malaysian TV clarified that snacks were the most frequently advertised options, and their sodium content was the highest.

Among all the different advertising appeals, “stimulation of appetite” was broadcast most of all. In a study conducted in Iran, the most popular appeals used in the advertisements were attributed quality (67%) and hunger/thirst stimulation (64%).[21] In another similar study of Iran, the most frequent appeals used in the advertisements were “stimulation of hunger/thirst” and “stimulating curiosity/entertaining”, respectively.[21]

Most of the messages in advertisements are debatable, because using persuasive methods in food advertisements, particularly those displayed on TV screens and the internet, are more likely to be noticed by children and receive an emotional response.[31] On the other hand, studies have shown that logical appeals, such as value for money and comparative advertising, mainly address adults because these aspects are considered in their purchases. However, these appeals are not a suitable strategy for children.[32] Emotional appeals are also used to lure the audience to the product.[33] In a study in the UK, it was shown that emotional appeals such as fun and joy were used in advertisements to increase consumption.[34]

The strategy used by food advertisements is another point to be considered alongside the type of food products being advertised. “Animation/Company-owned character(s)” was the most frequently used strategy in the advertised food products. A recent study in Brazil, which analyzed the persuasive advertising strategies by 18 food brands on TV, Facebook, Instagram, and YouTube, also found a higher frequency of the strategy “cartoon/company-owned character” on TV advertisements compared to social media platforms.[35] A study in the UK indicated that children were exposed to less-healthy TV food advertisements that used promotional characters and themes of fun.[14] Another domestic research reported that the most attractive and artistic techniques executed in TV food advertisements were, respectively, rational appeals, presenting confirmation (presented a confirmation by the Ministry of Health and Medical Education), and music.[36] A study also proved that the promoters effectively encourage observational learning by using the logical and emotional appeal of the advertisements, including positive feelings, prizes for purchase, and the use of attractive models and celebrities (through online games, downloads, etc.). They also create strong connections or bonds with children and reinforce the need for implicit approval of the advertised products.[32]

Persuasive strategies can influence all; however, children and adolescents are the most vulnerable groups.[37] Children with short attention spans cannot understand useful information; hence, advertisers endorse their products through celebrities, brand personalities, and licensed personalities to promote the products. These three influential elements can increase interest and lead to choosing the product.[38]

Benefit claims were used as another way of attracting children. Most of the claims were made on the sensory-based characteristics of foods (i.e., texture, taste, and appearance), which aligns with the findings of a previously published study in Iran.[22] A content analysis also revealed that taste was among the leading claims in the advertised foods.[33]

Offering gifts by participating in contests, a discount on the price or directly as an award for buying the advertised food products was another strategy to invite the children to test the food products. A study in Bangladesh that investigated the content of advertising to children found that they were enticed by a gift to try a chocolate-filled biscuit. In a study conducted in Thailand, the frequency of advertisements that offered a premium was significantly higher for non-core foods.[20]

Based on WHO EMR and INFORMAS, most of the food advertisements in the present study had violated advertising regulations. However, most of the food advertisements were allowed based on Iran’s standards.

A recent study that examined the nutritional content of the advertised food products reported that, according to the WHO EMR model, about 80% of the food advertisements displayed products that were not in agreement with that reference.[26] A study in Thailand, which analyzed the content of food ads on children’s TV, revealed that up to 73.4% of the food ads were for non-core food advertisements.[20] This number for our study was almost 90%. Overall, it seems that according to the international references, the violation rate in other studies was almost similar to Iran. However, according to domestic standards, the rate was insignificant. A probable reason for this can be explained by the fact that the international references have been established specifically for children, and naturally, more strictness is required for children’s nutrition. In addition, Iran has not yet enacted advertising regulations specific to children. Furthermore, based on Iran’s regulations, some food items high in fat, sugar, and salt, such as pickles, cakes, and biscuits, are still allowed to be promoted by the mass media, which need to be revised.

Although the current study is the most up-to-date study of its kind, it had some limitations. If we had the possibility to analyze more TV channels specific to children, we would have presented a more complete and accurate picture of the state of advertising targeting children in the country. In addition, if we had the possibility to follow the advertisements in several seasons, it would be possible to check rare advertisements, the advertising trend, and the advertisements depending on the season. In addition, conducting an experimental study in parallel with the present study could provide valuable evidence regarding the effectiveness of advertisements in different age and gender groups.

In conclusion, present research revealed that food products had a prominent part among other advertisements broadcast on TV. Children were predominantly exposed to foods high in fat, salt, and sugar, and the share of unhealthy food advertisements, such as desserts, sweets, fast foods, fats, and sugared drinks, was significant. The persuasive techniques and strategies used in the advertisement can be copied to promote healthy eating among children by health educators. By being aware of the content of the advertisement, educational interventions are suggested to be tailored and implemented to grow the nutritional awareness of children and offset the impact of unhealthy food advertisements. Regarding the obvious violations of the food advertising standards based on domestic and international references, a more careful audit of commercials is highly recommended. Due to the lack of marketing standards specifically created for children, establishing rules with a view to international references seems essential.
