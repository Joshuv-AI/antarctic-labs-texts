Staged TAVR and TEER for complex multivalvular disease in advanced heart failure: a case report
Authors: Haoze Deng, Yiyang Li, Caiming Zhao, Bingyuan Zhou, You Zhou, Li Wang, Qinkao Xuan, Xiaodong Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultivalvular heart disease complicated by advanced heart failure in elderly patients represents a major therapeutic challenge, particularly in the presence of complex anatomy, severe ventricular remodeling, and high surgical risk. Staged transcatheter strategies may offer a less invasive and individualized therapeutic alternative.Case summaryAn elderly patient with end-stage heart failure and severe ventricular remodeling was diagnosed with Sievers type I bicuspid aortic valve with mixed stenosis and severe regurgitation, complicated by functional moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation. Given the patient’s high surgical risk (STS-PROM 12%; EuroSCORE II 15%), a staged transcatheter strategy was adopted. Transfemoral TAVR was first performed with good prosthetic valve function and clinical improvement. Due to persistent symptomatic functional MR, subsequent transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) was performed three months later, resulting in significant MR reduction, symptomatic improvement, and favorable hemodynamic outcomes at follow-up.DiscussionThis case demonstrates the feasibility and clinical value of a staged transcatheter strategy in high-risk elderly patients with complex multivalvular disease and advanced heart failure. Sequential intervention allows reassessment of residual valve pathology, reduces procedural complexity, and lowers procedural risk compared with simultaneous multivalvular intervention.Take-home messageA staged transcatheter strategy combining TAVR followed by TEER represents a safe, feasible, and effective therapeutic option for selected high-risk patients with complex multivalvular disease and advanced heart failure, offering individualized treatment and meaningful symptomatic improvement when surgical risk is prohibitive.

Introduction

Multivalvular disease complicated by heart failure is common in elderly patients, particularly in the setting of advanced ventricular remodeling and high surgical risk, making clinical management highly challenging [1]. Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) disease is frequently associated with mixed aortic stenosis and regurgitation, and its complex anatomical characteristics pose specific challenges for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) [2]. Functional mitral regurgitation (MR) commonly coexists in patients with advanced heart failure and severe left ventricular dysfunction, representing an important contributor to persistent symptoms and adverse prognosis. When medical therapy is insufficient, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) has emerged as an important interventional treatment option [3].

Here, we report a case of an elderly patient with advanced heart failure, Sievers type I bicuspid aortic valve disease with mixed stenosis and severe regurgitation, complicated by functional moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation, who was successfully treated using a staged transcatheter strategy consisting of TAVR followed by TEER.

History of presentation

A 78-year-old man presented with a one-year history of exertional chest tightness and dyspnea, accompanied by fatigue. He had previously been admitted to our cardiology department and was discharged with symptomatic improvement following optimized guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure, including sacubitril/valsartan 50 mg twice daily, dapagliflozin 10 mg once daily, spironolactone 20 mg once daily, furosemide 20 mg once daily, and vericiguat 2.5 mg once daily. However, he subsequently experienced recurrent similar episodes requiring multiple hospitalizations.

Approximately one week prior to the current admission, his symptoms progressively worsened, with a marked decline in exercise tolerance. He developed chest tightness and dyspnea after climbing a single flight of stairs, accompanied by paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea. He was therefore re-admitted for further evaluation and management.

On physical examination, his blood pressure was 106/42 mmHg. Jugular venous distension was evident. Percussion revealed dullness over the right lung field, and breath sounds were diminished at both lung bases. Cardiac auscultation revealed a heart rate of 138 bpm with an irregularly irregular rhythm, and a grade 4/6 diastolic murmur was audible over the aortic area. No peripheral edema was observed.

Past medical history

The patient had a history of hypertension for more than 10 years, with adequate blood pressure control. In 2016, he underwent emergency tracheostomy at an outside hospital (details unavailable). He had a smoking history of over 60 years (approximately 20 cigarettes per day) and a history of alcohol consumption for more than 50 years (approximately 100 mL of spirits daily), having abstained from alcohol for the past 9 years.

Preoperative examination

A standard 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm, premature ventricular contractions, and complete left bundle branch block. Holter monitoring revealed premature atrial contractions, recurrent short runs of atrial tachycardia and accelerated atrial rhythm, premature ventricular contractions, short runs of ventricular tachycardia, and complete left bundle branch block. Laboratory investigations showed elevated high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (66.9 pg/mL) and markedly increased NT-proBNP levels (10,894 pg/mL).

Transthoracic echocardiography (Fig. 1) revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 19% and global cardiac enlargement (LVEDD: 92 mm, LVESD: 89 mm). Aortic valve disease was characterized by bicuspid valve morphology with combined aortic stenosis and regurgitation (Vmax: 2.5 m/s, peak gradient: 25 mmHg, mean gradient: 13 mmHg, aortic valve area by the continuity equation: 1.63 cm²), accompanied by severe aortic regurgitation. Moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation was also present. Right ventricular–pulmonary arterial uncoupling was observed, with a tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion to pulmonary artery systolic pressure ratio (TAPSE/PASP) of 0.43.

Diagnosis

Based on comprehensive evaluation, the patient was diagnosed with: Heart failure, NYHA functional class III; Valvular heart disease: (Aortic regurgitation and aortic stenosis, Mitral regurgitation, Tricuspid regurgitation) ; Cardiac arrhythmias( Paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, Premature ventricular contractions, Paroxysmal ventricular tachycardia, Complete left bundle branch block).

Management and follow-up

After multidisciplinary heart team assessment, the patient’s STS-PROM score was 12% and EuroSCORE II was 15%, indicating high operative risk for conventional open-heart surgery. Therefore, a transcatheter treatment strategy was considered more appropriate, and the patient was scheduled for transfemoral TAVR under general anesthesia, with transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance and rapid right ventricular pacing.

Based on preprocedural cardiac CT, the patient exhibited a markedly enlarged bicuspid aortic annulus with a diastolic annular area of 740.9 mm² and only mild leaflet calcification, posing a significant challenge for valve anchoring. Although greater annular oversizing is generally recommended in pure or predominant aortic regurgitation to reduce the risk of valve migration and paravalvular leak, excessive oversizing in this patient raised concerns regarding annular injury, conduction disturbances, coronary obstruction, and prosthesis deformation because of the large annular dimensions and bicuspid anatomy. Therefore, valve sizing was not determined solely according to annular measurements. Detailed CT assessment demonstrated that the supra-annular structures, including the heavily thickened bicuspid leaflets, commissural fusion, and relatively constrained intercommissural dimensions, could provide additional anchoring support above the annular plane (Fig. 3). Based on this a 29-mm Sapien 3 valve (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA) was selected. Valve anchoring was achieved by utilizing the supra-annular structures, with a planned implantation depth of 90% supra-annular and 10% infra-annular, corresponding to an additional 4 mL balloon overfilling, oversizing of − 2%. The surgical procedure is shown in the figure (Fig. 5). A temporary pacing lead was placed via the right internal jugular vein. Primary vascular access was obtained through the right common femoral artery. Under aortic root angiography, the delivery system was advanced across the aortic arch. Valve positioning and deployment were performed under combined fluoroscopic and TEE guidance. Given the patient’s severely impaired preprocedural cardiac function, veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was used for intra-procedural circulatory support and was successfully weaned shortly after the procedure. Immediate post-procedural TEE demonstrated excellent prosthetic valve function, with a Vmax of 0.71 m/s, a peak gradient of 2.2 mmHg, a mean gradient of 1 mmHg, and only mild paravalvular regurgitation (Fig. 6). One week after the procedure, repeat cardiac biomarker testing showed a high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T level of 44.9 pg/mL and an NT-proBNP level of 1,918 pg/mL, both of which demonstrated marked improvement compared with pre-procedural values.

Postoperatively, the patient received optimized medical therapy, including edoxaban tosylate 30 mg once daily, dapagliflozin 10 mg once daily, sacubitril/valsartan 50 mg once daily, vericiguat 2.5 mg once daily, furosemide 20 mg once daily, spironolactone 20 mg once daily, potassium chloride 0.5 g once daily, and rabeprazole 10 mg once daily.

Despite TAVR, the patient continued to experience recurrent heart failure episodes with limited response to medical therapy. Approximately three months later, he was readmitted with recurrent dyspnea. Multidisciplinary reassessment suggested that persistent moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation was a major contributor to refractory heart failure. The heart team therefore decided to perform transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) under general anesthesia. Mitral regurgitation predominantly originated from the A2–P2 segment. The mitral valve area was 4.7 cm², with leaflet lengths of 2.8 cm (A2) and 2.0 cm (P2) (Fig. 7). Significant posterior leaflet tethering and severe leaflet malcoaptation were observed, increasing the technical difficulty of leaflet grasping. We therefore planned to implant a single 0612 clip (length × width) at the A2–P2 segment.

The surgical procedure is shown in the figure (Fig. 8). Due to rightward bulging of the interatrial septum, transseptal puncture was performed at a posterior and superior site, with a measured puncture height of 4.6 cm. The guiding catheter was advanced into the left atrium, and the clip delivery system was introduced and oriented toward the mitral valve. Under 3D TEE guidance, the clip was aligned perpendicular to the line of coaptation and positioned centrally over the regurgitant jet. After advancing the clip into the left ventricle, both leaflets were successfully captured and confirmed by echocardiography. The clip was then gradually closed, resulting in a marked reduction in mitral regurgitation. Post-deployment assessment demonstrated a mean transmitral gradient of only 1 mmHg, after which the clip was released (Fig. 9). Postoperative medical therapy remained unchanged.

The patient experienced significant symptomatic improvement, with no recurrent chest tightness or dyspnea. At 3-month follow-up, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated: Well-functioning transcatheter aortic valve prosthesis without significant dysfunction; Mild residual mitral regurgitation (1+) after TEER; Persistent global cardiac enlargement (LVEDD 92 mm, LVESD 87 mm); Severely reduced LVEF (18%). In addition, mild-to-moderate tricuspid regurgitation was present, and right ventricular–pulmonary arterial coupling was preserved, with a TAPSE/PASP ratio of 0.66. Vital signs at follow-up showed a blood pressure of 86/53 mmHg and a heart rate of 62 bpm.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the successful staged management of complex valvular disease in an elderly patient with advanced heart failure, characterized by a Sievers type I bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) with mixed stenosis and severe regurgitation, complicated by functional moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation (MR). Performing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in the setting of bicuspid aortic valve anatomy presents specific technical challenges, including accurate annular sizing, precise valve positioning, and an increased risk of paravalvular leak. Nevertheless, recent studies have shown that, with meticulous multimodality imaging assessment and procedural planning, favorable outcomes of TAVR can be achieved in this patient population.

Persistent or newly developed functional MR after TAVR is increasingly recognized as an important contributor to ongoing heart failure, particularly in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy and severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) [4, 5]. Although a subset of MR may improve due to afterload reduction and left ventricular reverse remodeling, significant residual MR, as observed in this case, often requires targeted intervention [6]. Staged transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) following TAVR has emerged as a feasible and effective strategy. Observational data and case series have demonstrated that this approach can significantly reduce MR severity, decrease heart failure hospitalizations, and improve quality of life, outperforming medical therapy alone [7]. The COAPT trial established the superiority of MitraClip combined with guideline-directed medical therapy over medical therapy alone in symptomatic patients with secondary MR, with reductions in mortality and heart failure events [3]. Notably, in this case, the patient exhibited a left ventricular end-diastolic diameter exceeding 90 mm, consistent with end-stage heart failure and severe left ventricular remodeling, with an extremely reduced LVEF (18–19%). These findings clearly exceeded the traditional inclusion criteria for functional mitral regurgitation treatment in the COAPT trial. Nevertheless, given the severity of the patient’s heart failure, TEER was still performed. The procedural success and subsequent clinical benefit were consistent with findings from real-world registry studies [8, 9].

In this case, after sequential treatment of both aortic and mitral valve disease, the patient’s cardiac function improved significantly, with a marked improvement in right ventricular–pulmonary arterial coupling (TAPSE/PASP increased from 0.43 to 0.66). However, left ventricular reverse remodeling (LVRR) remained limited. We believe this may be attributable to several factors. Evidence suggests that in patients with low-flow, low-gradient aortic stenosis (LFLG AS) accompanied by severe systolic dysfunction, LVRR often requires 6–12 months to become fully apparent [10]; therefore, the relatively short follow-up period in this case may have limited the observation of structural recovery. In addition, this elderly patient had an extremely reduced LVEF (< 20%) with likely extensive myocardial fibrosis and severe baseline myocardial injury, which may inherently restrict structural recovery [11]. Furthermore, comorbid conditions such as hypertension, conduction abnormalities (complete left bundle branch block), and residual tricuspid regurgitation may have further impeded complete LVRR.

The staged strategy adopted in this case (TEER performed approximately three months after TAVR) allowed careful assessment of the impact of aortic valve intervention on residual MR and heart failure status, and reduced procedural complexity and risk compared with simultaneous double-valve transcatheter intervention. Limitations include the lack of long-term follow-up (> 1 year), the single-center nature of the report, and the absence of advanced imaging modalities (such as strain imaging) to quantitatively assess reverse remodeling. Nevertheless, this case highlights the value of a multidisciplinary heart team approach in developing individualized, sequential transcatheter treatment strategies for high-risk patients with multivalvular disease and refractory heart failure, and supports prioritizing staged rather than single-stage combined interventions in appropriately selected patients.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that, in elderly patients with advanced heart failure and complex multivalvular disease, a staged transcatheter treatment strategy is both feasible and clinically valuable. Sequential intervention with TAVR followed by TEER allows assessment of the clinical significance of residual mitral regurgitation after aortic valve intervention, while reducing procedural complexity and risk compared with simultaneous multivalvular transcatheter intervention. Even in the setting of extremely reduced LVEF and advanced cardiac remodeling, carefully selected and meticulously planned staged transcatheter therapy can still achieve meaningful symptomatic improvement and hemodynamic benefit.
