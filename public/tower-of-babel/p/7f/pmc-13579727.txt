Metal-organic framework-based fuel-driven chemical reaction network for ferroptosis therapy
Authors: Ding Guo, Qian Lin, Quanyi Jin, Lu Zhang, Nian Liu, Min Jiang, Shanshan Peng, Jiafeng Xu, Xueming Gao, Lili Zong, Xuan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFerroptosis is a prospective approach for cancer treatment. However, the efficacy of ferroptosis therapy is limited by three parallel ferroptosis defense pathways: the glutathione (GSH)-glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) pathway, the ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1)-ubiquinol (CoQH2) pathway, and the dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH)-CoQH2 pathway. Inspired by the principles of preprogrammed chemical reaction networks (CRNs), herein, a novel drug delivery system (SRF@Au@M NPs) was designed based on MIL-100(Fe) for predictable behaviors in tumor cells to break the three ferroptosis defense systems. SRF@Au@M NPs were fabricated through the size optimization of MIL-100(Fe), sorafenib (SRF) loading, in-situ growth of Au nanoparticles (Au NPs) and surface modification with dihydrolipoic acid derivatives. SRF@Au@M NPs disintegrate in the presence of high concentrations of GSH, releasing sorafenib (SRF) into tumor cells, which reduces GSH synthesis and inhibits GPX4 activity. The Au nanoparticles decompose glucose to produce H2O2, providing fuel for the Fenton reaction and disrupting carbohydrate metabolism to inhibit NAD(P)H generation. Particularly, a novel redox-CRN was formed between dihydrolipoic acid derivatives and iron ions, continuously promoting reactive oxygen species generation while concurrently consume NADH. The imbalance of NAD(P)H metabolic homeostasis impedes the recycling of CoQ to CoQH2, resulting in the simultaneous inhibition of the FSP1-CoQH2 and DHODH-CoQH2 pathways. Consequently, the SRF@Au@M NPs triggered a potent ferroptosis storm in 4T1 tumor cells and achieved an 92.5% tumor growth inhibition in tumor-bearing mice, significantly higher than that of other treatment groups. Introduction

Ferroptosis is a unique type of programmed cell death triggered by iron overload and the accumulation of lethal lipid peroxidation (LPO) [1–3]. Over the past decade, extensive research has revealed that ferroptosis activation may serve as a potential therapeutic strategy for multiple diseases [4]. In particular, cancer cells resistant to traditional therapies or prone to metastasis are more susceptible to ferroptosis [5, 6], making it a fascinating avenue for cancer treatment. Although significant progress has been achieved to enhance the precision and efficacy of ferroptosis-based therapies, achieving robust and comprehensive ferroptosis induction remains a challenge. This difficulty is primarily attributed to the complex and inherent ferroptosis defense systems and the unique metabolism of cancer cells. Detailed mechanistic studies uncovered that there are three parallel ferroptosis defense systems that are essential for preventing LPO accumulation and subsequent ferroptosis: the glutathione (GSH)-glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) system, the NAD(P)H-ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1)-ubiquinol (CoQH2) system, and the dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH)-CoQH2 system [7–9]. In detail, the GSH-GPX4 pathway prevents lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) from accumulating within lipid bilayers, while FSP1 prevents uncontrolled LPO by reducing ubiquinone (CoQ) to CoQH2 [10, 11]. Parallelly, DHODH neutralizes lipid peroxidation by reducing CoQ to CoQH2 in the inner mitochondrial membrane, operating independently of non-mitochondrial compartments to counteract ferroptosis [12–14]. Therefore, it is ‌an intriguing approach to inducing ferroptosis by simultaneously disrupting multiple defense systems while elevating iron levels for cancer treatment.

Activation of and resistance to ferroptosis involve multiple redox reactions, in which the products of one reaction serve as reactants for another. This interdependence forms intricate chemical reaction networks (CRNs) that exhibit rich and complex kinetic behaviors, such as oscillations or multiple stability [15, 16]. Inspired by the transformation of complex substances within cells, various artificial CRNs have been developed to impart lifelike properties to materials, including oscillations [17, 18], out-of-equilibrium shifts [19, 20], and dissipative self-assembly [21, 22]. These CRNs can be precisely preprogrammed, enabling the induction of a range of predictable behaviors [23–25]. For instance, Andreas Walther et al. constructed a fuel-driven enzymatic chemical reaction network (ERN) using lipoic acid (LA) and dihydrolipoic acid (DHLA) redox pairs to program an autonomous thiol/disulfide redox system [26]. They used lipoamide dehydrogenase and β-nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide hydrate (NADH) as auxiliary reducing agents to regenerate LA into DHLA, thus effectively avoiding the direct annihilation of the reducing agent by the oxidizing fuel H2O2 (i.e., short-circuit reaction (SCR)) (Fig. 1a, b). Moreover, the ERN allows for programming hydrogel lifetimes after being linked to thiol-terminated star polymers. It is noteworthy that redox imbalance and NAD(P)H levels are closely associated with the occurrence of ferroptosis in tumor cells. On the one hand, when oxidative stress occurs in tumor cells, ROS oxidize polyunsaturated fatty acids to produce LPO, but this process is partially reversed by the GSH-GPX4 system [12, 27]. On the other hand, carbohydrate metabolism can produce NAD(P)H to regenerate CoQH2, which is essential for maintaining the function of FSP1 and DHODH [7, 9, 28]. Inspired by the CRN and the work of Andreas Walther et al., we envisage fuel-driven CRN in living organisms to facilitate the formation of new reaction pathways that can continuously undergo Fenton reactions to produce ROS and simultaneously affect NAD(P)H metabolism, thereby disrupting the three ferroptosis defense systems of tumor cells and causing a powerful “ferroptosis storm.”

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have been widely employed as nano-sized drug delivery systems (DDSs) for efficient loading of various therapeutic agents to treat cancer due to their high porosity, biocompatibility and excellent stability [29–32]. In addition, considering that MOFs facilitate easy surface engineering, as a proof of concept, this property may enable MOFs to integrate the components that constitute CRNs, thus exhibiting multifunctionality in ferroptosis therapy. In previous studies, most MOF-based ferroptosis DDSs focused on enhancing the Fenton or Fenton-like reaction and GSH depletion. Ferroptosis activation through GPX4 inhibition has been achieved in various non-catalytic GSH depletion pathways, such as MOF disintegration and reduction of high-valent metal ions (e.g., Fe(III), Cu(II), Mn(IV)) [33–37]. It is often overlooked that the efficacy of exogenous iron in activating ferroptosis is not directly proportional to the amount of ions administered, largely because the cellular uptake efficiency of the DDSs also significantly influences the therapeutic outcome [38, 39]. Another conflicting problem is that the depletion of the endogenous reducing agent GSH will downregulate the turnover frequency of Fe3+ to Fe2+, thereby inhibiting the occurrence of the Fenton reaction [40, 41]. More importantly, these MOF-based ferroptosis nanomedicines primarily target the disruption of the GSH-GPX4 pathway, but largely overlook the FSP1-CoQH2 and DHODH-CoQH2 defense systems. Therefore, by optimizing the MOF particle size and then integrating new CRNs based on the optimal particle size to achieve continuous Fenton reaction and interference with the ferroptosis defense system, this strategy can maximize therapeutic outcomes for ferroptosis treatment.

In this work, MIL-100(Fe) was prepared with controlled particle sizes ranging from 30 nm to 265 nm using a continuous dropwise method. Among these representative sizes, the 60 nm nanoparticles exhibited an optimal balance of stability, cellular uptake efficiency, drug loading capacity and toxicity, and were therefore selected for the subsequent construction of CRN and treatment of ferroptosis. Subsequently, a composite MIL-100(Fe)-based DDSs (denoted as SRF@Au@M NPs) was fabricated through sorafenib (SRF) loading, in situ growth of Au nanoparticles (Au NPs) and surface modification with dihydrolipoic acid derivatives (Fig. 1 d, f). Once internalized by cancer cells, the surface Au NPs mimic glucose oxidase (GOx) activity, consuming intracellular glucose and thereby disrupting key metabolic pathways, including glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). This ultimately reduces NAD(P)H production in 4T1 cells. Consequently, this inhibits the recycling of CoQ to CoQH2, which acts as a critical antioxidant within the FSP1/DHODH-mediated anti-ferroptosis defense mechanism that traps lipid peroxyl radicals. Concurrently, the H2O2 generated from glucose decomposition serves as a fuel for the Fenton reaction, generating highly toxic hydroxyl radicals (•OH) in the presence of iron ions. Additionally, SRF@Au@M NPs disintegrate under high GSH concentrations, releasing SRF to inhibit system Xc−, thereby suppressing cystine uptake, GSH synthesis, and GPX4 activity, ultimately causing ferroptosis. Notably, the reduction of intracellular GSH content can effectively avoid the subsequent side reactions in CRNs, namely the aforementioned CRNs process between lipoic acid derivatives and GSH (Fig. 1b), thus facilitating the reduction of Fe3+ to Fe2+ within the CRN (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, the modification of dihydrolipoic acid derivatives on the SRF@Au@M surface serves three critical functions: (1) enhancing nanoparticle dispersibility and biocompatibility; (2) constructing CRNs via the DHLA/LA redox cycle, thereby sustaining the Fenton reaction and elevating ROS levels in tumor cells; (3) depleting NADH via the DHLA/LA redox cycle to lower CoQH2 levels, further downregulating the expression of the ferroptosis defense proteins DHODH and FSP1. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments revealed that the SRF@Au@M NPs efficiently suppressed tumor growth by simultaneously disrupting all three major ferroptosis defense systems (Fig. 1e). To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first report integrating CRNs into DDSs for ferroptosis therapy. The strategic implementation of CRNs in DDSs establishes a novel paradigm for tumor treatment.

Results and discussion

Size-controlled synthesis of MIL-100(Fe) nanoparticles

In accordance with the classical crystallization mechanism, the critical factor for synthesizing uniform MOFs is the separation of nucleation and growth [42, 43]. Typically, the process of nucleation and growth can be elucidated through the LaMer mechanism (Fig. 2a), which can be divided into three phases [44–46]. (I) A rapid increase in the concentration of free monomers in the solution leads to supersaturation, but no particles are present; (II) the monomer undergoes “burst nucleation”, whereby the monomer concentration is higher than the critical level of supersaturation and the concentration of free monomers in the solution is significantly reduced; (III) nanoparticles continue to grow and the concentration of monomer passes back below the critical level of supersaturation. Inspired by the LaMer mechanism, we employed a continuous-dropwise approach to achieve size-controlled synthesis of MIL-100(Fe) by leveraging its nucleation characteristics (Fig. 2b). In a typical synthesis, solutions containing the metal ions (FeCl3·6H2O) and organic linker (Benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylic acid, BTC) were slowly and continuously added to a FeCl3 solution at specific concentrations. Once the addition of the FeCl3 and BTC was completed (this point is marked as 0 min), the feed of FeCl3·6H2O and BTC were halted but without stopping the reaction. A specific volume of the reaction solution was taken at 0 min, 10 min, 20 min, 30 min, and 40 min to monitor the size of the MOF. As displayed in Figure S4a-b, the nanoparticle size of MIL-100(Fe) remained stable at around 33 nm, indicating that particle growth ceased upon termination of the monomer supply. Consistent with this, the mass yield of MIL-100(Fe) also remained constant over time (Table S2). Specifically, the yield per 10 mL of reaction volume stabilized at around 15%, suggesting that the nanoparticles were uniformly dispersed in the system and did not undergo further growth or aggregation. Taken together, these results imply that the final MIL-100(Fe) particles in Stage III did not undergo Ostwald ripening. This further suggests that the reaction could be deliberately terminated at any point during Stage III, or alternatively, that the metal and linker could be replenished at any time to resume particle growth.

In the nanoparticle growth stage, the total Gibbs free energy is primarily dependent on the bulk free energy (Figure S5) [43]. This indicates that the temperature (T), supersaturation of the solution (S), and its molar volume (V) exert a significant influence on the nanoparticle size. Based on above mechanism, different sizes of MIL-100(Fe) were obtained through a continuous-dropwise approach (for details see the support information and Table S1) with the growth temperature, supersaturation of the solution, and molar volume maintained constant throughout the process. As shown in Fig. 2c, eight different-sized products presented a uniform diameter distribution, and the size of MIL-100(Fe) increased with the addition of reactants. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) results confirmed that the different sizes of MIL-100(Fe) exhibit a similar peak compared with the simulated patterns (Fig. 2d-e), indicating that MIL-100(Fe) has been successfully prepared. In addition, the initial solution concentration influenced the product size (Fig. 2f). 6 mM initial solution led to the formation of smaller nanoparticles (31 ± 4 nm) compared with 1.5 mM initial solution (96 ± 10 nm). Since nucleation occurs initially in the reaction, minor changes to the initial solution can significantly affect the nucleation process. A higher initial concentration of FeCl3·6H2O during the nucleation stage promotes the formation of a larger number of nuclei. Consequently, with the same total amount of reactants added, the average size of the resulting MIL-100(Fe) nanoparticles is reduced.

A linear correlation was observed between the cubic power of the average particle diameter (D3) of MIL-100(Fe) and the amount of FeCl3 added during the growth stage (D3∝amount of added reactants, Fig. 2g). During this stage, the size of the particles is governed by the number of monomers. To maintain this linear relationship, two essential conditions must be satisfied: (I) The number of growing nanoparticles must remain constant, with no new nuclei forming. This prevents size polydispersity and is essential for preserving the linearity; (II) the supersaturation level (free monomer concentration) during growth must be kept constant, ensuring that monomer consumption is directly proportional to the amount of reactants added. The observed linear dependence of D3 on the added FeCl3 confirms that nucleation and growth stages were successfully separated by controlling the degree of supersaturation. More importantly, this linear relationship allows us to calculate the exact amount of reactants required to synthesize MIL-100(Fe) of specific sizes over a wide range. Therefore, the growth of MIL-100(Fe) can be precisely predicted.

Cell uptake studies and the optimal particle size selection for MIL-100(Fe)

In ferroptosis therapy, the efficacy of exogenous iron-induced ferroptosis often does not show a linear correlation with the administered iron dose due to the suboptimal cellular uptake efficiency of nanoparticles. Therefore, identifying an optimal carrier size is crucial for maximizing therapeutic efficacy. While several studies have investigated the impact of MOF size on cellular uptake, no consensus has been reached. For instance, Park et al. successfully tuned the size of PCN-224 nanoparticles (30–190 nm) and demonstrated that 90 nm PCN-224 exhibited superior cellular uptake and photodynamic therapy (PDT) efficacy compared to other sizes [47]. Additionally, Liu et al. developed a completely aqueous method to precisely control the size of ZIF-8 at room temperature, revealing a size-dependent cellular uptake pattern in the order of 60 > 30 > 90 > 130 nm [48]. These findings highlight that cellular uptake selectivity can vary across different MOF types. Hence, it is necessary to optimize the MOF particle size to enhance therapeutic efficacy.

The three primary factors influencing nanoparticle cellular uptake are morphology, particle size, and surface physicochemical characteristics [49–51]. SEM analysis confirmed that the synthesized MIL-100(Fe) nanoparticles exhibit a uniform spherical morphology. Therefore, we further characterized their size and zeta potential. Four samples with distinct particle sizes (31, 58, 94, and 136 nm as determined by SEM) were selected and designated as xMIL-100(Fe), where “x” represents the specific particle size. Their hydrodynamic size, polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta potential were measured in both phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) growth media. As shown in Figure S6 and Table 1, the hydrodynamic size in both media were significantly larger than the dry SEM sizes, and particles of different sizes exhibited distinct aggregation behaviors. For 30MIL-100(Fe), the hydrodynamic size in PBS was approximately 3.5-fold greater than the SEM-measured size. In contrast, the hydrodynamic sizes of 60MIL-100(Fe), 90MIL-100(Fe) and 135MIL-100(Fe) in PBS were approximately 2-fold larger than those measured by SEM. The greater aggregation of smallest nanoparticles may be attributed to the higher surface free energy. All four samples were well dispersed in PBS, with polydispersity index (PDI) values below 0.3. Aggregation was more pronounced in 10% FBS growth media compared to PBS. The hydrodynamic sizes of 30MIL-100(Fe) and 60MIL-100(Fe) increased approximately 6- and 4-fold, respectively, relative to their dry sizes, while those of 90MIL-100(Fe) and 135MIL-100(Fe) increased about 3-fold. These findings indicate that the smaller nanoparticles tend to exhibit a higher degree of aggregation. The PDI values were also generally higher in 10% FBS than in PBS. This phenomenon is likely due to the formation of a “protein corona” on the MOF surface, which can destabilize the particles and promote aggregation, leading to larger hydrodynamic sizes [52]. The zeta potential of the differently sized MIL-100(Fe) particles was approximately −18 mV in PBS and increased to around −11 mV in 10% FBS, following protein adsorption. These findings suggest that MIL-100(Fe) particles of different sizes exhibit nearly identical physicochemical properties. Consequently, we hypothesize that the observed differences in subsequent cellular uptake efficiency are primarily attributable to particle size.

To investigate the size-dependent cellular effects, four groups of NPs with distinct sizes (ranging from 30 to 135 nm as determined by SEM results) were applied to 4T1 cells. Quantitative analysis of nanoparticle uptake was performed via inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) following cell digestion. As displayed in Fig. 3a, cellular uptake exhibited a time- and size-dependent pattern. The intracellular iron content gradually increased from 1 h to 24 h and displayed a highly size-dependent manner in the order of 30 > 60 > 90 > 135 nm. In addition, the cellular uptake reached a plateau around 12 h for 60, 90, and 135 nm fractions, whereas the intracellular level of 30MIL-100(Fe) continued to increase significantly beyond this time point. The higher apparent cell uptake for smaller MOFs may be attributed to their larger specific surface area compared to larger ones, leading to stronger nanoparticle–cell interactions and enhanced internalization.

Subsequently, the doxorubicin (DOX), a chemotherapeutic agent with red fluorescence at 620 nm, was loaded into all the MOFs (labeled XDOX@M, where x represents the particle size) to further investigate cellular uptake efficiency by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and flow cytometry. Prior to drug loading, we measured the specific surface area and pore size distribution (PSD) of the carriers. As shown in Figure S7a, the N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms indicate favorable porosity. Both the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area and Langmuir surface area exceeded 1000 m2/g for all particle sizes, suggesting promising drug-loading potential. The PSD reveals that the primary pore diameters of MIL-100(Fe) across various particle sizes are below 2 nm (Figures S7b), confirming a predominantly microporous structure. Figure 3b shows the CLSM images of 4T1 cells incubated with each DOX@M for 3 h. It can be observed that 30DOX@M presented the highest intracellular fluorescence, followed by 60DOX@M, 90DOX@M, and 135DOX@M (Figure S8). Similarly, we found that the DOX fluorescence intensity in cytoplasm decreased with the increased size of DOX@M, as detected by flow cytometry (Fig. 3c). These results are consistent with the ICP-OES measurements, indicating that the loading of drugs does not affect the internalization of MIL-100(Fe).

To inform subsequent ferroptosis therapy, SRF was loaded into MIL-100(Fe) (labeled XSRF@M, where x represents the particle size) and its cytotoxicity was investigated. SRF@M was prepared by mixing MIL-100(Fe) with SRF at a mass ratio of 1:2. To our surprise, the drug loading efficiency (DLE) of SRF exhibited unexpected differences. As displayed in Fig. 3d, the DLE was determined to be 2.8%, 6.9%, 6.8% and 7.5% for 30SRF@M, 60SRF@M, 90SRF@M, and 135SRF@M, respectively. Apparently, 30SRF@M displayed the lowest drug loading efficiency. A probable explanation might be that the 30 nm nanoparticles are in a state where the crystal core has just formed, and the crystal has undergone only initial growth. As a result, their internal porous structure is not yet fully developed, which hinders the drug’s ability to enter the internal cavity. In this case, SRF may only be attached to the external surface or to potential superficial defects of the MIL-100(Fe). This makes the drug prone to premature release during blood circulation, potentially causing off-target toxicity. Actually, the PXRD patterns of MIL-100(Fe) showed much sharper and stronger peaks as the particle size raises (Fig. 2d and e), indicating that the crystallinity of MIL-100(Fe) gradually increases. During the subsequent crystal growth process, the DLE ceased to increase and stabilized at approximately 7%.

The anticancer capability of differently sized SRF@M particles was then evaluated in 4T1 cells at an SRF concentration of 6 µg/mL. As anticipated, the efficacy decreased monotonically decreased as the particle size increased. Cell mortality rates were 46.1%, 27.9%, 17.2%, and 12.8% for 30, 60, 90, and 135 nm particles, respectively (Fig.3e). It is noteworthy that free SRF resulted in 13.1% cell death, which was close to 135SRF@M but lower than those of other SRF@M composites. This may be ascribed to the combination of poor solubility of SRF and higher uptake efficiency of MIL-100(Fe). These findings underscore that optimizing MOF particle size is critical for enhancing anticancer efficacy.

It should also be noted that the gaps in tumor vasculature typically range from 100 nm to 2 μm. For effective drug delivery, nanomedicines must not only extravasate from the blood vessels but also be retained within the tumor tissue. While smaller particles (e.g., ~ 30 nm) can easily extravasate, they can also readily diffuse back into the bloodstream, preventing effective enrichment at the tumor site [39, 53]. To comprehensively evaluate and compare the key properties—including stability, cellular uptake, drug loading efficiency, and cytotoxicity—of the nanomaterials with different particle sizes, we constructed a radar chart (Fig. 3f). The analysis revealed that the 60 nm nanomaterial exhibited the most balanced and favorable overall profile. Therefore, the 60 nm MIL-100(Fe) was selected for all subsequent ferroptosis therapy experiments.

Fabrication and characterization of SRF@Au@M NPs

MOF-based ferroptosis-induced nanoparticles were synthesized in three main steps, SRF loading, in situ growth of Au NPs, and subsequent surface modification (Fig. 1d). To enhance the drug loading efficiency, the influence of the mass ratio of MIL-100(Fe) to SRF was investigated. As shown in Table S3, an increased mass of SRF may facilitate enhanced drug loading. The highest loading efficiency of 11.9% was achieved via the SRF standard curve (Figure S9) when the mass ratio of MIL-100(Fe) to SRF was 2:1. Following loading into MIL-100(Fe), the sharp peak of SRF at 266 nm disappeared in the SRF@M formulation (Fig. 4a). Then, Au NPs were deposited on the SRF@M surface in situ by direct reduction of HAuCl4 in ice water with NaBH4. As exhibited in Figure S10, dark spots corresponding to the Au NPs (~2 nm in diameter) could be observed on the external surface of SRF@M, accompanied by a color change from brown to burgundy (Fig. 4a). Besides, compared to SRF@M, SRF@Au@M exhibits a new broad and shallow absorption peak in the 500–600 nm range, which is attributed to the Au NPs’ surface plasmon resonance (Fig. 4a) [54]. To further enhance the colloidal stability of the resultant nanoparticles and potentiate ferroptosis therapy, we synthesized two lipoic acid derivatives: sodium dihydrolipoic acid (DHLA-Na) and PEGylated dihydrolipoic acid (DHLA-PEG) for subsequent surface modifications (Figure S1-S3). Their structures were characterized by 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR and electrospray ionization-high resolution mass spectrometry (Figure S11-S17). The aggregation of SRF@Au@M was effectively inhibited following surface modification with lipoic acid derivatives (Figure S18 and Table S4). The hydrodynamic size of the unmodified SRF@Au@M was 239 nm with a PDI of 0.3255, whereas the corresponding values for SRF@Au@M NPs were 151 nm and 0.2407, respectively (Fig. 4b). It is noteworthy that the PEGylation modification of SRF@Au@M NPs effectively reduces the adsorption of proteins in serum, which is conducive to the prolonged circulation of nanoparticles in vivo and thus maximizes their therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 4f).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images reveal that SRF@Au@M NPs have a uniform spherical morphology with an average diameter of 60 nm (Fig. 4d), aligning with the optimized particle size of MIL-100(Fe). The nanoparticles surface is decorated with a layer of ultra-small gold nanoparticles. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping confirms that the characteristic elements—C, Fe, and O (from MIL-100(Fe)), F (from SRF), Au (from the gold nanoparticles), and S (from the lipoic acid derivatives)—throughout the SRF@Au@M NPs (Fig. 4e), demonstrating successful drug loading onto the MIL-100(Fe) framework. Additionally, the mass fractions of Fe, Au and S measured by ICP-OES were 8.4%, 11% and 1.6%, respectively (Table S5). The UV-vis spectrum displayed that the SRF@Au@M NPs contain characteristic peaks of SRF@M from 200 to 400 nm and Au NPs from 500 to 600 nm (Fig. 4a). Meanwhile, the zeta potential changes reflected the fabrication process of the nanoparticles (Fig. 4c). The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of SRF@Au@M NPs were also obtained (Fig. 4g), and the C1s, Fe 2p, N 1 s, O 1 s, Au 4f, and S2p peaks were observed in the spectra. The high-resolution Au 4f spectra in Fig. 4h revealed two peaks at 83.8 and 87.5 eV, corresponding to the Au 4f7/2 and Au 4f5/2 signals of metallic gold, indicating that the Au NPs on the nanoparticles existed in the metallic Au0 state [55]. Finally, we found that SRF@Au@M NPs could be well dispersed and stored in water or PBS for 7 days with no significant alterations in size or PDI (Fig. 4i), indicating that the nanoparticles possessed good stability. All the above results demonstrate that SRF@Au@M NPs have been successfully prepared. The hydrodynamic size, zeta potential, and cellular uptake behavior of SRF@Au@M NPs with different particle sizes were also evaluated. As shown in Figure S19, the hydrodynamic size of SRF@Au@M NPs increased with the original MIL-100(Fe) size, and all formulations exhibited a PDI below 0.3, indicating good dispersion. The zeta potentials of all four sizes were negative, ranging from −17 to −22 mV. More importantly, after 3 h of incubation, the intracellular iron content in 4T1 cells still showed a clear size-dependent pattern, consistent with that observed before surface modification and Au deposition. These results confirm that particle size remains a critical factor influencing cellular uptake even after the introduction of Au NPs and dihydrolipoic acid derivatives.

The integration of Fe species and Au NPs into the MOFs system is anticipated to effectively deplete GSH and glucose levels within tumor cells, which plays ‌crucial‌ roles in the subsequent ferroptosis therapy. First, we evaluated the GSH-scavenging activity of the SRF@Au@M NPs using 5,5′-dithiobis-2-(nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), a compound that reacts with GSH to form yellow products with a characteristic absorption peak at 412 nm (Fig. 5a). As illustrated in Figure S20 and Fig. 5b, the absorbance at 412 nm decreased in a dose-dependent manner after 1 h and 4 h of incubation with SRF@Au@M NPs. At the highest nanoparticle concentration, 36% of GSH was depleted after 4 h. Meanwhile, after 12 h of incubation, a notable increase in hydrodynamic diameter (484.9 nm) and PDI (0.7755) was observed (Fig. 5c). TEM images also displayed an irregular, diffuse morphology instead of the uniform spherical morphology. These results suggested that the strong GSH-consuming ability of SRF@Au@M NPs. The release profiles of SRF from SRF@Au@M NPs under different conditions were investigated. When the GSH concentration was raised to 10 mM, the cumulative release was further enhanced, reaching about 65%. This demonstrates a clear dependence of SRF release on GSH concentration. Fuel-driven chemical reaction network in vitro

Classical Fenton reactions refer to the decomposition of H2O2 initiated by metal ions in a low oxidation state, which results in the production of hydroxyl radicals. The high-valent ions formed in this process are subsequently reduced back to their low-valent state via H2O2. Although the reduction reaction is significantly slow, the catalyst regeneration is considered the most critical process because it reduces radical production and can deplete the availability of low-valent ions for the reaction [57]. In this work, we envisioned an effective strategy that introduces a dihydrolipoic acid derivative as a cocatalyst into the iron-based MOF to enhance the Fenton efficacy by accelerating the Fe3+/Fe2+ cycles. To verify this hypothesis, the Fe2+ contents in 4T1 cells incubated with two different formulations (SRF@Au@M and SRF@Au@M NPs) were investigated through Ferro Orange staining, a fluorescent probe selective for Fe2+. As shown in Fig. 6a, the intracellular Fe2+ fluorescence intensity was significantly higher in both treatment groups than in the control group, owing to the introduction of exogenous iron. After 4 h, the fluorescence intensity of Fe2+ in the nanoparticle group containing dihydrolipoic acid derivatives (SRF@Au@M NPs) was higher than that of the group without it (SRF@Au@M). To our surprise, after 12 h, the fluorescence intensity decreased in both groups, but it remained significantly higher in the SRF@Au@M NPs group than in the SRF@Au@M group. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the fluorescence intensity of the SRF@Au@M NPs group was 1.9-fold and 1.6-fold higher than that of SRF@Au@M group at 4 h and 12 h, respectively (Fig. 6b). Based on the high concentration of GSH in tumor cells and the SRF loaded in our nanoparticles, we proposed the mechanistic hypothesis of “slow versus fast” to “weak versus strong” stepwise regulation of intracellular Fe2+ content (Fig. 6c). Specifically, upon administration, the nanoparticles rapidly disintegrate in the high-GSH tumor microenvironment, which reduces Fe3+ to Fe2+. At this initial stage, cells treated with SRF@Au@M NPs form DHLA/LA redox couples via lipoamide dehydrogenase, leading to faster Fe3+ reduction and, consequently, stronger red fluorescence at 4 h compared to the control. This constitutes the “slow versus fast” regulatory phase. Following nanocarrier disintegration, the released SRF inhibits cellular cystine uptake by targeting the membrane transporter SLC3A2, thereby reducing the intracellular synthesis of GSH. Consequently, the decreased GSH content impairs the conversion of Fe3+ to Fe2+, resulting in a decrease in fluorescence intensity in both groups after 12 h compared to 4 h. However, owing to the presence of DHLA/LA redox couples, the SRF@Au@M NPs group exhibited a stronger reduction ability, maintaining higher intracellular Fe2+ content. Accordingly, this later stage represents the “weak versus strong” regulatory process. To further verify this hypothesis, intracellular GSH contents were tested at different times. As illustrated in Fig. 6d, compared to the control group, GSH content in cells treated with either SRF@Au@M or SRF@Au@M NPs decreased significantly after 4 h and further declined to approximately 60% after 12 h. In contrast, the GSH level in 4T1 cells treated with blank MIL-100(Fe) decreased to about 85% at 4 h but recovered to nearly 96% by 12 h (Fig. 6e). These results reveal that intracellular GSH depletion is co-regulated by iron-based MOFs and the chemotherapeutic agent SRF. It is worth noting that intracellular GSH and GSSG (oxidized GSH) can form competing redox circuits with the DHLA/LA couple redox (Fig. 1b), which would hinder the reduction of Fe3+ to Fe2+. Therefore, depleting intracellular GSH content via SRF treatment can effectively minimize this competing side reaction, favoring Fe3+ reduction by DHLA. To confirm the conversion of DHLA to LA, we analyzed the cell lysate via HPLC-MS after incubation with SRF@Au@M NPs and 4T1 cells. As shown in Figure S24 and Fig. 6f, a distinct LA peak was observed at a retention time of 7.33 min, with a measured molecular weight of 205.0358, which matches the theoretical value for LA. This indicates that DHLA is smoothly converted to LA, concurrently reducing Fe3+.

As described above, Au NPs exhibit GOx-like activity, oxidizing glucose to produce H2O2, which serves as the chemical fuel for the Fe2+-mediated Fenton reaction. Based on this property, we first examined the H2O2 levels in 4T1 cells using the Amplex Red (ADHP) probe following treatment with different formulations. As exhibited in Fig. 6g-h, compared to the control, SRF, MIL-100(Fe), and SRF@M groups, cells treated with SRF@Au@M showed significantly higher red fluorescence, confirming that H2O2 levels can be elevated through Au NPs-catalyzed glucose oxidation. In addition, we found that SRF@Au@M NPs treatment groups exhibited higher red fluorescence than other groups but lower than the SRF@Au@M treatment groups. This phenomenon can be attributed to the accelerated Fe3+/Fe2+ cycles facilitated by DHLA/LA redox couple, which accelerates the decomposition of the generated H2O2. Furthermore, the intracellular ROS generation capacity of the two nanoparticle formulations was evaluated using the fluorescent indicator 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). As presented in Fig. 6i, flow cytometry results indicated that SRF@Au@M and SRF@Au@M NPs increased ROS levels in 4T1 cells from 10.2% to 27.1% and 50.4%, respectively, after 4 h of treatment. However, after 12 h, the ROS level in the SRF@Au@M group returned to control levels, whereas it remained approximately 1.6-fold higher than the control in the SRF@Au@M NPs group. It is worth emphasizing that ROS levels typically decrease rapidly once they reach the highest level, due to the intrinsic antioxidant and antiferroptotic properties of cancer cells [58, 59]. The sustained high ROS levels observed in our system highlight the ability of CRNs to maintain ROS production over time.

In particular, the reprogramming of carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism in cancer cells may facilitate the induction of ferroptosis [28]. To gain more comprehensive understanding the effects of the CRNs based on MIL-100(Fe), as constructed, on tumor cell metabolism, a whole transcriptome analysis of 4T1 cells was performed following incubation with SRF@Au@M NPs. Firstly, enhanced oxidative stress disrupts intracellular redox homeostasis, promoting lipid peroxidation and inducing ferroptosis. Next, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was conducted to identify significantly altered signaling pathways. As shown in Fig. 7e, SRF@Au@M NPs evidently influenced the PPP and ferroptosis-related pathways. Clustered heatmap analysis demonstrated that a total of 16 genes associated with ferroptosis were significantly altered in 4T1 cells after treatment with SRF@Au@M NPs (Fig. 7c). These results revealed that SRF@Au@M NPs trigger ferroptosis in 4T1 cancer cells through multiple mechanisms.

In vitro mechanism of ferroptosis and cytotoxicity

Given the importance of ROS generation in ferroptosis therapy, we further investigated the ROS content in 4T1 cells using the DCFH-DA probe after treatment with different formulations. As shown in Fig. 8a, formulations containing Au NPs (SRF@Au@M and SRF@Au@M NPs) exhibited notably stronger green fluorescence compared to the other groups, indicating higher ROS production. Notably, the SRF@Au@M NPs groups showed the strongest ROS upregulation, with a mean fluorescence intensity 9.6-fold higher than that of the control and significantly higher than the SRF@Au@M group, indicating its superior ability to ROS-inducing ability (Figure S26). In addition, intracellular •OH, which is generated via the Fenton reaction and subsequently oxidizes polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) to induce LPO, was monitored using coumarin-3-carboxylic acid (3-CCA). Consistent with the intracellular ROS analysis, the SRF@Au@M NPs-treated group displayed the highest •OH levels among all groups (Fig. 8a, b). Encouraged by the efficient ROS generation and GSH depletion abilities of SRF@Au@M NPs, the crucial protein GPX4 in GSH-GPX4 system for defense against ferroptosis was examined. As depicted in Fig. 8h and Figure S29a, compared with the control, SRF, MIL-100(Fe), SRF@M and SRF@Au@M groups, the SRF@Au@M NPs group exhibited the lowest GPX4 expression. This indicates that the GSH consumption and ROS generation induced by SRF@Au@M NPs act synergistically to suppress GPX4 expression in 4T1 cells.

Previous studies have shown that certain cancer cell lines retain resistance to ferroptosis even after GPX4 inactivation, implying the presence of extra ferroptosis defense mechanisms. FSP1 is another anti-ferroptosis protein localized on the plasma membrane, acting independently of GPX4. FSP1 functions as an NAD(P)H-dependent oxidoreductase capable of reducing CoQ to CoQH2, which can scavenge lipid peroxyl radicals, thereby suppressing LPO and ferroptosis. DHODH is another ferroptosis defense system situated in mitochondrial compartments. When GPX4 is acutely inactivated, DHODH activity is significantly enhanced, leading to increased CoQH2 production, which suppresses LPO and protects mitochondria from ferroptosis. Therefore, simultaneous inactivation of both the GPX4 and the CoQH2 systems may trigger a robust ferroptosis storm. The PPP, a glucose metabolic pathway parallel to glycolysis, serves as a major source of intracellular NADPH through its oxidative decarboxylation phase [28, 60]. This pathway supplies reducing equivalents for various biosynthetic reactions, including the reduction of CoQ to CoQH2. As discussed above, the presence of Au NPs enables SRF@Au@M NPs to effectively catalyze glucose oxidation into gluconic acid. We found that modification with Au NPs effectively reduced the intracellular glucose level to approximately 80%, which was significantly lower than that in the other groups (Figure S27), leading to a decrease in PPP flux and thus in NADPH production. Herein, the NADPH contents were tested in 4T1 cells after treatments with different formulations. As shown in Fig. 8c, we found that MIL-100(Fe) and SRF@M displayed NADPH-depletion capacity compared to the control and SRF groups, which may be attributed to the electron transfer from NADPH to Fe3+. Upon co-incubation of MIL-100(Fe) with NADPH, the absorbance of NADPH at 340 nm gradually decreased over time, demonstrating that MIL-100(Fe) possesses intrinsic NADPH oxidase-mimicking activity. Notably, after in situ growth of Au NPs and modification with dihydrolipoic acid derivatives, SRF@Au@M NPs retained this activity (Figure S28a and c).The SRF@Au@M and SRF@Au@M NPs groups further reduced the intracellular NADPH to 33.7% and 32.2%, respectively, which was significantly lower than those in the MIL-100(Fe) and SRF@Au@M groups. This indicates that Au NPs could consume glucose and markedly affect the PPP, thereby inhibiting NADPH generation. Of note, NADPH serves as a cofactor for glutathione reductase, which is responsible for reducing GSSG back to GSH [61]. Therefore, modification with Au NPs leads to a further decrease in GPX4 expression (Fig. 8h).

Another cofactor involved in the reduction of CoQ to CoQH2 is NADH. In Our CRNs, lipoamide dehydrogenase utilizes NADH as an auxiliary substrate to regenerate dihydrolipoic acid (DHLA) from lipoic acid (LA), which serves as the reducing age. Consequently, the cycling of the DHLA/LA redox pair leads to continuous consumption of intracellular NADH. We next evaluated the NADH content in 4T1 cells. As shown in Fig. 8 d, SRF-treated cells exhibited a slight increase in NADH content compared with untreated controls, suggesting that cells can maintain NADH metabolism homeostasis under constant chemotherapeutic stimulation. Similarly, MIL-100(Fe) and SRF@M reduced the intracellular NADH to 69% and 88.7%, respectively, which were significantly lower than the control group. Previous studies have shown that iron-containing materials possess excellent NADH-like oxidase mimic activity, effectively oxidizing NADH to NAD+ [62]. In addition, Moran et al. recently revealed that Fe2+/3+ can act as a Lewis acid, coordinating with pyruvate and pre-organizing its interaction with NADH, thereby explaining the observed stereoselectivity resulting from an intramolecular hydride transfer pathway [63].We also found that MIL-100(Fe) and SRF@Au@M NPs possess NADH oxidase-like activity(Figure S28b and d). These findings could account for the NADH-scavenging functions of MIL-100(Fe) and SRF@M. SRF@Au@M further reduced the intracellular NADH content to 54%. This may be attributed to the suppression of the glycolysis pathway via Au NPs and the consequent decline in NADH production. The NADH content was significantly lower than the other groups after treatment with SRF@Au@M NPs, indicating that the introduced dihydrolipoic acid contributes to additional intracellular NADH consumption. A decrease in NAD(P)H may impair ATP production by affecting the oxidative phosphorylation process or substrate-level phosphorylation processes. As shown in Fig. 8g, treatment with SRF@Au@M NPs reduced the relative ATP content to 53.4%, indicating that NAD(P)H depletion compromised ATP generation.

Next, we determined the change in cellular CoQH2 content. As shown in Fig. 8e, the trend was consistent with the change in NAD(P)H levels.The CoQH2 level in 4T1 cells treated with SRF@Au@M was significantly lower than in those treated with Control, SRF, MIL-100(Fe) or SRF@M. This reduction can be attributed to the synergistic effect of iron and Au NPs on NAD(P)H consumption. In addition, the SRF@Au@M NPs group showed a superior ability to reduce CoQH2 content compared to the SRF@Au@M group. This confirms that the presence of dihydrolipoic acid derivatives enhances NADH consumption. Correspondingly, the SRF@Au@M NPs group showed the lowest CoQH2/CoQ ratio (Fig. 8f). Subsequently, we investigated the effect of CoQH2 imbalance on ferroptosis by examining the expression of FSP1 and DHODH in 4T1 cells after different treatments. As showed in Fig. 8h and Figure S29b-c, western blot results revealed that both SRF@Au@M and SRF@Au@M NPs remarkably decreased the FSP1 and DHODH expression, with the lowest levels observed in the SRF@Au@M NPs group. This phenomenon showed that our constructed DDSs can disrupt both the FSP1-CoQH2 system and the DHODH-CoQH2 system by NAD(P)H homeostasis in tumor cells.

The results above indicate that our constructed CRNs based on MIL-100(Fe) could destroy the three ferroptosis defense systems in 4T1 cells, leading to substantial LPO production. Next, cellular LPO was examined using the C11-BODIPY581/591 probe (Fig. 8i). Compared with the control, SRF, and MIL-100(Fe) groups, the SRF@M group showed moderate LPO generation. Meanwhile, SRF@Au@M treatment further enhanced LPO generation. Specifically, SRF@Au@M NPs-treated cells displayed higher green fluorescence and lower red signals than SRF@Au@M-treated cells, demonstrating that dihydrolipoic acid derivatives in our DDSs can promote LPO induction. Moreover, malondialdehyde (MDA), the terminal smaller molecular byproducts of LPO decomposition, was also tested to assess ferroptosis. As shown in Fig. 8j, the MDA content in cells treated with SRF@Au@M NPs was 32.8 µM/g protein, approximately three times higher than that in the control group and significantly higher than that in the SRF@Au@M group. These results confirm that the MOF-based CRNs comprehensively and synergistically induce ferroptosis.

Consequently, the designed SRF@Au@M NPs achieved a powerful “ferroptosis storm” by simultaneously disrupting three defense systems. We then assessed the therapeutic efficacy of SRF@Au@M NPs in vitro using a CCK-8 assay. As presented in Fig. 8k, the 4T1 cells treated with the SRF@Au@M NPs exhibited the lowest viability among all groups, demonstrating the synergistic effect of its components. Meanwhile, the calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) staining (Figure S30) showed that the SRF@Au@M NPs group had fewer living cells (green color) and more dead cells (red color), further confirming the strongest antitumor efficacy of SRF@Au@M NPs treatment. To verify that cell death occurred primarily via ferroptosis, inhibitor rescue experiments were conducted. As displayed in Fig. 8l, inhibitors of apoptosis (Z-VAD), autophagy (chloroquine, CQ), and necroptosis (necrostatin-1, Nec-1) all failed to protect the cancer cells. In contrast, the ferroptosis inhibitors deferoxamine (DFO) and liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1) significantly rescued cell viability, increasing survival rates from 64.9% to 80.3% and 83.0%, respectively. Together, these results clearly indicate that the excellent‌ antitumor efficacy of the DDSs-mediated ferroptosis therapy was achieved through the proposed mechanism.

Conclusion

In summary, from four different particle sizes of MIL-100(Fe), we selected the optimal particle size of 60 nm to construct a multifunctional DDS platform. Within tumor cells, SRF@Au@M NPs form a complex and predictable fuel-driven chemical reaction network that effectively disrupts the three major defense pathways against ferroptosis. Upon cellular uptake, the combined action of GSH depletion by MIL-100(Fe) and SRF-mediated inhibition of system Xc− leads to a sharp decrease in intracellular GSH concentration. This directly inactivates GPX4 and minimizes competing side reactions within the subsequent CRNs. A novel redox-CRN established between dihydrolipoic acid derivatives and iron ions continuously drives the Fenton reaction, generating abundant ROS. The synergistic effect of GSH depletion and ROS surge decisively disrupts the primary GSH-GPX4 antioxidant defense. Besides, the GOx-like activity of the Au NPs catalyzes intracellular glucose to produce H2O2, which serves as fuel for the CRNs. Concurrent glucose depletion disrupts the PPP and glycolysis, thereby inhibiting NAD(P)H production. Meanwhile, the conversion of LA to DHLA consumes additional NADH, impeding the regeneration of CoQ to CoQH2. The resulting decline in CoQH2 pool concurrently incapacitates both the FSP1-CoQH2 and DHODH-CoQH2 ferroptosis defense systems. Consequently, the MIL-100(Fe)-based CRNs triggered a potent ferroptosis storm in 4T1 tumor cells and displayed a substantial tumor suppression effect in tumor-bearing mice. Overall, this nanoplatform breaks the redox and NAD(P)H metabolic homeostasis in the tumor microenvironment, thereby achieving comprehensive ferroptosis therapy. Our sophisticated strategy based on “chemical reaction networks” provides a novel and inspired approach for cancer treatment.
