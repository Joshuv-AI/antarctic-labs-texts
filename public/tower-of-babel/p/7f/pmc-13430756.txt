Cytogenetic insights into the diversification of Calanthe: polyploidy, karyotype variation, and genome size evolution
Authors: Bokyung Choi, Sein Jeong, Hyeonjin Kim, Daeun Jung, Daehoon Choi, Jin-Won Kim, Hyeon-Ho Myeong, Soonku So, Tae-Soo Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Changes in chromosome number and genome size arising from hybridization and polyploidization are major drivers of plant diversification. The orchid genus Calanthe, broadly distributed across tropical and subtropical Asia, is known to exhibit complex ploidy variation. Here we document, in South Korean natural populations, both diploid (2n = 40) and triploid (2n = 60) cytotypes among the surveyed species. We further report triploid cytotypes for the first time in South Korean natural populations of C. aristulifera, C. discolor, and C. sieboldii, and document diploid–triploid coexistence in natural populations of C. discolor and C. sieboldii. Nuclear DNA content (1 C values) varied 1.85-fold, spanning 7.85 to 14.50 pg, placing these taxa in the upper distribution range of the subfamily Epidendroideae. In the two mixed-ploidy taxa (C. discolor and C. sieboldii), 1Cx values (monoploid genome size) were broadly comparable between ploidy levels, providing no clear evidence of extensive genome downsizing in triploids. Triploid individuals showed no marked reduction in pollen stainability, with abundant pollen tetrads and aniline-blue-positive staining observed. Overall, our integrative cytogenetic data provide a baseline for subsequent molecular cytogenetic and phylogenomic analyses, facilitating rigorous tests of hypotheses on hybridization, polyploidization, and genome evolution underlying diversification in Calanthe.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09117-1.

Introduction

Genome size variation, spanning more than 2,400-fold among angiosperms [1], is a prominent feature of plant diversity. It reflects both changes in ploidy levels and structural modifications of the genome affected by polyploidy or whole-genome duplication (WGD), aneuploidy, and the accumulation or loss of repetitive DNA [2, 3]. Polyploidy triggers an immediate increase in nuclear DNA content and serves as a primary driver of genome size variation and evolutionary diversification in plants [4, 5].

However, genome size does not always scale linearly with ploidy level, as post-polyploid genome downsizing, chromosomal rearrangements, and the differential proliferation of transposable elements can substantially modify DNA content over time [6, 7]. At the microevolutionary scale, genome size variation frequently reflects differences in ploidy level and is often associated with geographic patterns, ecological differentiation, or the establishment of reproductive barriers [8 − 10].

Consequently, such intraspecific polymorphism serves as a critical proxy for detecting cryptic ploidy variation and investigating the early stages of plant divergence and speciation [11, 12].

Angiosperms frequently exhibit mixed-ploidy populations, in which multiple cytotypes coexist within a single species (i.e., heteroploidy) [13]. In such systems, triploids generally occur at lower frequencies than even-polyploids (e.g., 4x, 6x, or 8x), but triploid lineages may persist through vegetative reproduction [14], with their long-term maintenance depending on the strength of intraspecific competition [15 − 17]. This phenomenon is particularly significant in the orchid genus Calanthe, in which frequent natural hybridization and robust vegetative propagation through pseudobulbs facilitate the emergence and persistence of non-diploid cytotypes [18, 19]. Nevertheless, comprehensive studies explicitly addressing mixed-ploidy patterns and their evolutionary consequences in Calanthe are still lacking. Furthermore, differences in genome size and pollen stainability serve as useful proxies for assessing cytogenetic stability and reproductive function in such complex systems [20, 21]; however, the cytogenetic and reproductive mechanisms underlying the establishment and maintenance of mixed-ploidy populations within this genus have yet to be fully elucidated.

The Orchidaceae is one of the largest and most morphologically diverse angiosperm families and includes many ornamentally important taxa cultivated worldwide [22, 23]. Within this family, the genus Calanthe R.Br. comprises terrestrial orchids that are highly valued as ornamental plants due to their attractive inflorescences, floral diversity, and adaptability to shaded garden habitats and pot culture [24]. Several Calanthe species and hybrids are commercially traded in East Asia and elsewhere, and there is growing interest in their use for breeding novel cultivars with improved floral traits, extended flowering periods, and enhanced environmental tolerance [19, 25]. Despite this horticultural potential, cytogenetic and genome size data for Calanthe remain remarkably sparse [18, 26], particularly for East Asian species, where the lack of comprehensive ploidy level analysis hampers our understanding of their evolutionary history and constrains the effective use of their genetic diversity in breeding programs [27, 28].

The genus Calanthe comprises approximately 185 species and is widely distributed throughout tropical and subtropical regions of Africa, Southeast Asia, the Pacific Islands, and East Asia, including China, Japan, and the Korean Peninsula [23, 24, 29]. Along with two recently described new species (C. insularis S.H.Oh, H.J.Suh & C.-W.Park and C. rubra S.H.Oh, H.J.Suh & C.-W.Park), five species (C. aristulifera Rchb.f., C. discolor Lindl., C. reflexa Maxim., C. sieboldii Decne. ex Regel, and C. striata R.Br. ex Spreng.) are currently recognized in South Korea [24, 30, 31]. Although these taxa show high ornamental value and conspicuous differences in floral morphology [30, 31], several species within the genus present taxonomic difficulties due to limited differentiation in vegetative characters [19, 29]. Previous studies in orchids have demonstrated that variation in chromosome number, including the occurrence of odd-ploidy and mixed-ploidy populations, can obscure species boundaries and contribute to morphological overlap [9, 18, 32, 33].

In Calanthe, the functional basic chromosome number is most plausibly x = 20, because 2n = 40 is the predominant somatic chromosome number reported for the genus and related Collabiinae; accordingly, 2n = 40 is treated here as diploid and 2n = 60 as triploid [18, 32]. Therefore, a comparative cytogenetic framework is needed to (i) document cytotype diversity across Korean Calanthe, (ii) evaluate whether cytotype patterns are congruent with currently recognized taxa, and (iii) assess whether odd and mixed-ploidy are associated with reduced pollen stainability or altered pollen size as proxies for reproductive consequences.

In this study, we integrate flow-cytometric genome size estimation, chromosome counting and karyotype analysis, and pollen stainability and size measurements to characterize cytotype diversity in Korean Calanthe. Specifically, we aim to: (1) quantify genome size variation in 72 individuals representing seven Korean Calanthe species and integrate these data with chromosome counts to infer ploidy-level diversity; (2) determine chromosome numbers and characterize karyotype parameters (e.g., chromosome length, symmetry) across cytotypes; and (3) compare pollen stainability and pollen size among ploidy levels as indicators of reproductive consequences of cytotype variation. Together, these data provide a cytogenetic baseline for Korean Calanthe and allow evaluation of the extent to which cytotype diversity may inform systematic and evolutionary interpretation within the genus.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Living plants of the genus Calanthe were collected from natural populations in South Korea between 2024 and 2025 (Fig. 1). All living accessions were established from field-collected individuals; no horticultural material was included. All plant materials were identified by Prof. Dr. Tae-Soo Jang and Dr. Soonku So. A total of 80 individuals representing seven species were sampled (see Table S1 for population, voucher information, and associated cytotype/genome-size data). Because Calanthe species can propagate vegetatively through pseudobulbs, sampling was designed to minimize re-sampling of clonally connected ramets by preferentially selecting spatially separated individuals within populations (Table S1). To ensure the conservation of endangered plant resources, only a limited number of specimens were collected under the approval of local authorities (Collection Permit Number: EK202405ECP0014 for C. aristulifera). Of the 80 individuals sampled, 23 living specimens representing six taxa were originally collected from natural populations and subsequently cultivated ex situ at Chungnam National University; these individuals were deposited as voucher specimens in the Herbarium of Chungnam National University (CNUK, Daejeon, Korea), with individual deposition numbers provided in Table S1. The remaining 57 individuals were sampled in the field, from which only leaf tissue was collected for flow cytometric and cytogenetic analyses; formal voucher deposition was not possible for these field-collected samples. For cytological studies, root-tip meristems were used for mitotic analysis following [34]. Additionally, fully mature flowers were preserved in a formalin-acetic acid-alcohol (FAA) solution for pollen stainability assessment using light microscopy.

Cytogenetic and karyotype analysis

To determine the chromosome numbers and analyze the karyotypes, the standard Feulgen staining technique was employed following [12]. Actively growing root-tip meristems were collected and pretreated with a 0.05% aqueous colchicine solution for 4 to 4.5 h at room temperature to obtain clear metaphase spreads. Following pretreatment, the samples were fixed in a mixture of ethanol and acetic acid (3:1, v/v) for at least 1.5 h at room temperature, and subsequently stored at − 20 °C until further use. For chromosomal observation, the fixed root tips underwent hydrolysis in 5 N HCl at room temperature for 30 min. The materials were then immersed in Schiff’s reagent for 60 min in complete darkness for staining. Squash preparations were made in a drop of 60% acetic acid.

Chromosome numbers were determined by examining multiple well-spread metaphase plates per individual whenever material allowed. For karyotype analyses, we selected well-spread metaphase plates with comparable (intermediate) levels of condensation and measured at least three plates per cytotype [10]. For cytotypes with limited material (e.g., triploid C. discolor, C. aristulifera, and C. sieboldii; diploid C. reflexa and C. striata), the number of analyzable metaphase plates was reduced; these cases were explicitly flagged and interpreted conservatively.

Chromosomal images were processed and arranged using Adobe Photoshop CS6.

Morphometric parameters, including total chromosome length and arm lengths (long and short arms), were quantified using MicroMeasure software (version 3.3) following [35]. The classification of karyotype structures and terminology followed the criteria established by [36].

Genome size estimation

Of the 80 sampled individuals, genome size was estimated by flow cytometry for 72 individuals representing six taxa (Table S1), using Pisum sativum ‘Kleine Rheinländerin’ (4.42 pg/1 C) as the internal reference standard [20, 35]. The remaining eight individuals lacked flow-cytometric estimates but were included in chromosome-number analyses based on Feulgen-stained root-tip metaphases.

For nuclei isolation, approximately 25 mg of fresh leaf tissue from the sample and an equal amount from the internal standard were co-chopped together using a razor blade in Otto I buffer. The homogenate was filtered through a 30 μm nylon mesh to remove cell debris. The filtered suspension was then incubated with RNase at 37 °C for 30 min to eliminate RNA interference. Following RNase treatment, nuclei were stained by adding Otto II buffer supplemented with propidium iodide (PI) as the DNA-intercalating fluorochrome [35]. Fluorescence was measured on a Sysmex CyFlow Cytometer (Sysmex Partec GmbH, Görlitz, Germany), and a minimum of 3,000 nuclei per sample were recorded. The relative fluorescence intensity of the G₁ peak of the sample and the G₁ peak of the standard were used to calculate the 1 C value according to the formula: 1 C value (pg) = (sample G₁ peak mean / standard G₁ peak mean) × 1 C value of standard [20]. Each individual was measured in at least three independent runs to verify reproducibility. The coefficient of variation (CV) of FCM measurements was generally below 3% and never exceeded 6%, indicating acceptable data quality. The analysis followed the experimental protocol described by [35].

Pollen stainability test

To assess pollen stainability, pollinia were collected from up to six randomly selected individuals per species, depending on material availability (Table S3). The collected pollinia were carefully macerated in a drop of aniline blue dye solution to release the individual pollen grains (or tetrads) for staining [14]. A 15 µL aliquot of the aniline blue solution was placed on a glass slide, and a coverslip was applied after the pollinia were sufficiently broken down. The slides were examined using a light microscope (BX53F; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a digital camera (DP74; Olympus).

For each individual, a total of 100 pollen tetrads were scored under light microscopy and classified as either stainable (fully stained) or non-stainable; the non-stainable category included both unstained and malformed (abnormal/aborted) tetrads, which were combined for the purpose of calculating pollen stainability. To reduce pseudoreplication, individuals (not pollen grains) were treated as the unit of replication in statistical analyses (i.e., pollen stainability was summarized per individual). Where pollen senescence could influence staining outcomes, we interpret stainability conservatively as a proxy rather than a direct measure of viability [37]. To assess potential size dimorphism, the polar length and equatorial diameter were measured for at least 20 pollen tetrads per individual. Associations between pollen size and ploidy level were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation in SPSS Statistics V26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), with ploidy treated as an ordinal variable; ploidy level for each individual was inferred from flow-cytometric genome-size classes anchored by chromosome counts from representative diploid (2n = 40) and triploid (2n = 60) individuals. Differences in pollen size between diploid and polyploid individuals within species were tested using the Mann–Whitney U test in GraphPad Prism (version 9).

Chromosome counts were obtained for 26 of the 80 sampled individuals. The present study provides the first chromosome-level evidence of triploid cytotypes in natural populations of C. aristulifera, C. discolor, and C. sieboldii in South Korea (Fig. 2A, C, H); triploid individuals were not detected in the remaining taxa examined (Fig. 2; Table S1). Our genome size data suggest that additional triploid individuals may be present in natural populations beyond those for which chromosome counts were obtained (see below; Table S1).

Comparison of karyotypes revealed subtle differences between diploid and triploid cytotypes (Fig. 3). Across the taxa examined, karyotypes were dominated by metacentric and submetacentric chromosomes and could be divided into two size classes: large (3.78–5.72 μm) and small (1.31–1.87 μm) (Table 1; Fig. 3). As expected, total haploid karyotype length differed between diploid (52.38–55.83 μm) and triploid (73.06–104.11 μm) cytotypes within the same species (i.e., C. discolor and C. sieboldii), although substantial interspecific variation in chromosome size was also evident (Table 1).

The karyotype asymmetry index (AsI) ranged from 63.7% (diploid C. sieboldii) to 70.1% (triploid C. sieboldii), and the RI index varied from 2.43 to 3.43 (Table 1). Owing to the high degree of chromosomal condensation, nucleolar organizer regions (NORs) and satellites were not seen in the present study (Figs. 2 and 3).

Across the 72 individuals analyzed by flow cytometry, 1 C nuclear DNA content varied 1.85-fold, spanning 7.85–14.50 pg (Figs. 4 and 5). The minimum value was observed in a diploid individual of Calanthe insularis (2n = 40), whereas the maximum occurred in a triploid individual of C. sieboldii (2n = 60). Among diploid taxa, mean 1 C values ranged from 8.30 pg (C. rubra) to 9.75 pg (C. insularis), whereas individuals assigned as triploids showed mean 1 C values of approximately 12.30–13.22 pg, with the highest individual estimate reaching 14.50 pg in triploid C. sieboldii. When considered across all samples, 1 C estimates clustered into two broad DNA-content classes that corresponded to cytotype assignments, consistent with the ~ 1.5-fold increase expected between 2x and 3x individuals. In the two mixed-ploidy taxa (C. discolor and C. sieboldii), triploid individuals exhibited higher 1 C values than diploids occurring within the same populations, although the magnitude of this difference varied among individuals and populations. Species- and cytotype-level summaries (means ± SD) are provided in Table 1, and detailed individual- and population-level estimates are reported in Table S1.

Pollen stainability and size variation

Pollen stainability was assessed for pollen tetrads within pollinia from both diploid and triploid individuals (Fig. 6). In all taxa examined, pollinia contained abundant pollen tetrads irrespective of cytotype (Fig. 6). Aniline-blue-positive staining, including tube-like structures or callose-associated signals, was observed in both diploid and triploid individuals (Fig. 6A–C), however, these observations are interpreted conservatively as a proxy for pollen metabolic function rather than direct evidence of germinability.

Pollen dimensions tended to be larger in triploids than in diploids, with tetrad size (length × diameter) ranging from 23.94–52.69 × 29.68–73.16 μm in diploids to 27.83–61.03 × 35.27–81.31 μm in triploids (Table S3; values indicate minimum–maximum ranges). Likewise, microspore size (polar length × equatorial diameter) ranged from 10.85–31.22 × 13.43–35.44 μm in diploids to 12.97–30.90 × 15.21–32.73 μm in triploids (Table S3). However, despite these differences in pollen dimensions between the two ploidy levels, pollen size was not significantly correlated with nuclear DNA content across individuals (Fig. S1).

Discussion

Comparative patterns of chromosome number and karyotype variation at intra- and interspecific levels in Calanthe

The present study provides a comprehensive assessment of chromosome number and karyotype variation across the cytotypes documented to date for perennial Calanthe species occurring in Korea. Among the 48 Calanthe taxa with published counts (out of ~ 185 currently recognized species), chromosome numbers range from 2n = 18 to 2n = 80, occasionally accompanied by one or two B chromosomes (Table S2; [38, 39]). Based on our sampling together with the available literature, 2n = 2x = 40 is clearly the predominant chromosome number, supporting x = 20 as the most plausible base chromosome number for the Korean taxa examined (Table S2; [32]). Given x = 20 (and thus 2n = 40 for diploids), earlier reports of atypical chromosome numbers (2n = 18, 20, and 28; Table S2; [40, 41]) warrant caution. Such aberrant counts may reflect misidentified or inadequately vouchered material, technical or counting artifacts (e.g., confusion between n and 2n), and/or putative aneuploid or dysploidy variation within the genus. By contrast, Félix and Guerra [32] reported that chromosome numbers in the subtribe Collabiinae, including Calanthe, span 2n = 38–60, with 2n = 2x = 40 predominating, in agreement with Chase et al. [42]. We report chromosome numbers for C. insularis and C. rubra (both 2n = 40) for the first time; both taxa were recently described from natural populations in Korea [31]. Notably, one accession of C. aristulifera was triploid (2n = 60), contrasting with the previously reported diploid number (2n = 40) for this species [43]. Furthermore, we provide the first evidence for triploid cytotypes in C. discolor and C. sieboldii and present karyotype descriptions for these species, extending earlier chromosome-count reports that lacked cytotype-level and karyotypic characterization [18, 44, 45]. The coexistence of diploid and triploid cytotypes has previously been documented only in C. tricarinata (Table S2; [46]). Here, we report triploidy for the first time in C. aristulifera, C. discolor, and C. sieboldii in South Korean natural populations (Table 1). At the intraspecific level, diploid and triploid cytotypes co-occurred within populations (Fig. 1), suggesting recurrent formation of odd-ploid individuals (e.g., via unreduced gametes) and/or their persistence through clonal growth. At the interspecific level, however, chromosome numbers were largely conserved, with 2n = 40 predominating (Table S1). Although species harboring both diploid and triploid cytotypes in nature appear uncommon [47], such odd-ploid cytotypes are critical in polyploid evolution because they can facilitate repeated formation of higher ploidy levels [48].

Overall karyotype structure of Korean Calanthe taxa was broadly conserved despite differences in ploidy level (e.g., 2x and 3x) and was dominated by metacentric and submetacentric chromosomes (Table 1), consistent with earlier reports [18, 44]. Karyotype characteristics—such as chromosome number, individual chromosome size, total haploid length, karyotype symmetry, nucleolar organizer region (NOR) position, and centromere location—constitute fundamental parameters in cytogenetics [2, 49]. These attributes provide critical data for evaluating systematic relationships among species and populations across diverse plant groups [10, 12, 35, 50, 51].

Accordingly, the shared karyotypic architecture observed here suggests that, at least among the Korean taxa sampled (Table 1; Figs. 2 and 3), large-scale karyotype reorganization has not been pervasive; instead, interspecific differences may be reflected primarily in subtle structural variation, as reported in other plant groups [14, 35]. Nevertheless, observed variations in chromosome size should be interpreted with caution because length-based parameters are highly sensitive to metaphase condensation, slide preparation, and measurement error, particularly in lineages with small, highly condensed chromosomes [10, 14, 52].

Consistent with the strong metaphase condensation and small chromosome size noted above, secondary constrictions or satellites, cytological features often associated with nucleolar organizer regions (NORs) harboring transcriptionally active 35 S rDNA arrays, were not detected in our Feulgen-stained metaphase spreads, in agreement with previous studies of Calanthe [18, 44].

Although conspicuous secondary constrictions and their chromosomal positions can provide useful clues to karyotype evolution and chromosomal rearrangements [53], their apparent absence in this study likely reflects both technical and biological factors, including intense metaphase compaction, small chromosome size of Calanthe (Figs. 2 and 3). Molecular cytogenetic approaches such as fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) using 35 S and 5 S probes would be necessary to localize NORs reliably and to characterize rDNA-site number and position. Such analyses would be particularly informative for (i) testing whether NOR positions are conserved between diploid and triploid cytotypes, and (ii) assessing whether triploids exhibit additive versus non-additive rDNA-site patterns. These comparisons could provide complementary evidence relevant to distinguishing autopolyploid versus allopolyploid origins of the triploids.

The impact of genome size variation and its relationship to polyploidy in Calanthe

In Orchidaceae, genome size is highly variable, ranging from 0.38 pg/1C (Apostasia nuda R.Br.) to 44.84 pg/1C (Cypripedium acaule Aiton) [26]. Such extensive variation is commonly attributed to lineage-specific histories of polyploidy and to gains and losses of repetitive DNA, processes that can be facilitated by chromosomal rearrangements [26, 54, 55]. The 1 C values observed in Korean Calanthe (7.85–14.50 pg) are above the Orchidaceae (median 4.0 pg) and fall within the upper portion of the distribution reported for Epidendroideae (median 3.2 pg; range 0.3–19.8 pg), a subfamily in which most species have small genomes [26]. Accordingly, our estimates substantially increase the representation of Calanthe in genome-size datasets, given that earlier syntheses included genome size data for only two Calanthe species [26], and curated repositories continue to expand taxonomic coverage over time [56].

In the two mixed-ploidy taxa (C. discolor and C. sieboldii), triploid individuals consistently exhibited higher DNA content than diploids, with shifts broadly matching the expected increase from 2x to 3x as observed in orchid polyploid complexes (e.g., Dactylorhiza maculata (L.) Soó agg.; [57]).

Although 1Cx values were not exactly identical between the 2x (i.e., ca. 3.9–4.6 pg in C. discolor vs. ca. 4.1–4.8 pg in C. sieboldii) and 3x (i.e., 4.4 pg in C. discolor vs. ca. 4.1–4.8 pg in C. sieboldii) cytotypes, they remained broadly comparable (Table S2), suggesting no clear evidence of extensive genome downsizing in these triploids [58 − 60]. These data do not justify inferring diploidization in these cytotypes. However, the observed genome size variation among diploid Calanthe, despite largely conserved chromosome numbers and similar karyotype structures, indicates that whole-genome multiplication is not the sole driver of nuclear DNA divergence within these taxa (Table S2).

Comparative analyses in orchids suggest that genome size variation can be weakly coupled with chromosome number, being instead more strongly associated with repetitive element dynamics [26, 54, 55]. These processes provide plausible mechanisms for the interspecific differences observed among diploid Calanthe, as has been frequently documented for intra-specific and even intra-population genome size variation in other plant groups (Table S2; [61 − 63]). Furthermore, such mechanisms may account for the slight departures from perfectly additive expectations among cytotypes, even where ploidy shifts remain distinct. Consequently, further molecular cytogenetic studies employing fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) with repetitive DNA probes are warranted to better understand the chromosomal evolution and genomic architecture of Korean Calanthe.

Interpretation of pollen stainability patterns in triploids

In the present study, both 2n = 40 and 2n = 60 individuals produced abundant pollen tetrads within pollinia, and aniline-blue-positive staining was observed in both cytotypes. Among the non-stainable tetrads recorded, a proportion consisted of morphologically malformed or aborted grains rather than simply unstained individuals; because the assay was designed as a stainability screen rather than a developmental classification of pollen defects, malformed and unstained tetrads were combined into a single non-stainable fraction for analysis (Table S3). As these observations are based on stainability rather than direct germination or crossing assays, they should be interpreted conservatively as evidence of retained pollen metabolic function rather than male fertility [13, 64]. Although 2n = 60 cytotypes are generally expected to exhibit reduced male fertility if interpreted as triploids (3x; under x = 20) because of irregular pairing and unbalanced chromosome segregation [14, 65 − 67], our results suggest that pronounced early pollen failure is not universal in these individuals [68–71]. An alternative interpretation should also be considered. If x = 10 reflects an ancestral monoploid set in Calanthe, the 2n = 60 individuals documented here would represent hexaploids rather than triploids, which could partly explain the absence of an obvious reduction in pollen stainability. At present, however, chromosome-number evidence in Calanthe and related Collabiinae more strongly supports x = 20 as the functional base number used for cytotype interpretation in this study. This x = 10 interpretation (i.e., hexaploid scenario) should therefore be regarded as an alternative hypothesis requiring further evaluation with comparative genomics and molecular cytogenetics. Robust vegetative propagation via pseudobulbs may also facilitate the persistence of these cytotypes in natural populations [31], thereby extending the temporal window for potential evolutionary processes.

Furthermore, orchid pollen is commonly packaged into aggregated dispersal units (e.g., pollinia and tetrads); such aggregation may increase the likelihood that even a small subset of functional gametes achieves fertilization [72]. Thus, the 2n = 60 cytotypes documented here may retain substantial pollen stainability at the tetrad/tube-like stage. If interpreted as triploids under x = 20, this pattern is notable despite the meiotic imbalance typically expected in odd-ploidy cytotypes [73]. Notably, no tetraploid (2n = 80) individuals were detected in the natural populations examined in this study (Table S1). This absence suggests that the establishment of stable tetraploid lineages may be limited in these specific habitats or represents a rare evolutionary event. However, given that tetraploidy has been previously documented within the genus, such as in Calanthe biloba (Table S2), further studies incorporating a broader range of populations are warranted. Future work integrating (i) detailed meiotic analyses (e.g., univalent/trivalent frequencies and laggards), (ii) controlled intra- and inter-cytotype crosses with seed viability assessments, and (iii) ploidy assignment of progeny would provide a more definitive evaluation of whether these 2n = 60 cytotypes contribute to gene flow or, under the x = 20 interpretation, act as a “triploid bridge” in Calanthe.

Conclusions

This study establishes a cytogenetic baseline for seven sampled Calanthe taxa by integrating genome size, chromosome number, karyotype, and pollen stainability data. Diploids (2n = 40) predominated, supporting x = 20 as the likely base chromosome number among the taxa examined, and chromosome numbers are reported here for the first time for C. insularis and C. rubra. Although mixed-ploidy has previously been documented in C. tricarinata, our study documents diploid–triploid coexistence in natural populations of C. discolor and C. sieboldii from South Korea. In addition, we report a triploid cytotype of C. aristulifera from a South Korean natural population. Genome size data clearly distinguished diploid and triploid cytotypes and provided no clear evidence of extensive genome downsizing in triploids, whereas broadly conserved karyotypes suggest limited large-scale chromosomal reorganization among the taxa examined. Triploid individuals also showed no evident reduction in pollen stainability, although the reproductive significance of this pattern remains to be tested. Together, these results provide a foundation for future molecular cytogenetic, crossing, and phylogenomic studies on the origin and evolutionary consequences of cytotype diversity in Calanthe.
