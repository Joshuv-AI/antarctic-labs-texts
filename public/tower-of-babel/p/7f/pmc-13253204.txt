Infant-mother attachment security and cortisol reactivity during the strange situation procedure: Longitudinal associations with maternal depressive symptoms and infant temperament
Authors: Lauren R Bader, Brenda L Volling, Paige Safyer, Elizabeth Tengelitsch, Alissa Huth- Bocks, Catherine Hiltz, Richard Gonzalez, Lin Tan, Juan F Lopez, Delia M Vazquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined the influence of infant-mother attachment security on the regulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and cortisol stress responses in 14-month-old infants. Eighty-two mother-infant dyads participated in the Strange Situation Procedure (SSP), with salivary cortisol sampled across four time points. Infant attachment security, maternal depressive symptoms, and infant temperament were assessed longitudinally over the first postnatal year. Infant cortisol trajectories differed by attachment security: insecurely attached infants had significantly higher baseline cortisol, which declined following the SSP, whereas securely attached infants had lower baseline cortisol and a trend toward increased cortisol post-stressor. Maternal cortisol declined over time for all groups, with no differences between mothers of secure and insecure infants. Mothers of securely attached infants experienced sub-clinical depressive symptoms at 32 weeks pre-partum, with a decline of symptoms to a normal range during the first year postpartum. Negative infant temperament increased over the first year but was not associated with attachment security or cortisol response. These findings demonstrate that infant-mother attachment quality shapes infant HPA axis regulation independent of maternal depressive symptoms or negative infant temperament, suggesting the importance of early identification and intervention for attachment difficulties to promote adaptive stress physiology in infants.Keywords: infant attachment, Strange situation, Salivary cortisol, Stress reactivity, Temperament, Depressive symptoms

Introduction

The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is a hormonal system that responds to stress by releasing cortisol into circulation. The normative cortisol response to stress in healthy adults is characterized by a rise in cortisol after exposure to a stressor, followed by a gradual decline to baseline levels over time (Dickerson et al., 2004). Dysregulation of the HPA axis is manifested in several ways, including altered levels of diurnal cortisol secretion, enhanced or diminished stress-reactive cortisol release in response to stress, and/or a failure to recover to pre-stress cortisol levels (Fries et al., 2005; Koss et al., 2018; Pearson et al., 2015; Gunnar et al., 2006). Basic science pioneering work by Levine and colleagues revealed the importance of the mother in modulating the HPA response to stress in their offspring (Levine et al., 1957, 1959). In humans, dysregulation of the HPA-axis early in life has implications for future psychopathology (Gunnar et al., 2006). The quality of early caregiver-infant relationships has been implicated as one possible mechanism underlying the development of HPA-axis regulation or dysregulation (Gunnar et al., 2006; Gunnar, 1992; Koss et al., 2016). For example, internationally adopted children with a history of adverse early care experiences exhibited cortisol hypo-activity, manifested as blunted cortisol release to a laboratory stressor (Koss et al., 2016). Cortisol hypoactivity has also been observed in children with increased family risk, such as family stress, maternal depression, and financial strain (Gunnar et al., 2006; Lumeng and et al., 2014), and in maltreated children (Gunnar et al., 2006; Badanes et al., 2011; Cicchetti et al., 2001). In contrast, cortisol hyperactivity, manifested as elevated and sustained cortisol levels following a stressor, is associated with infants' insecure attachment to their mothers (Spangler et al., 1993; Nachmias et al., 1996; Gunnar et al., 1995; Gunnar et al., 2009; Hertsgaard et al., 2023; Beijers et al., 2017; Hostinar et al., 2014; O'Connor et al., 2013; Luijk et al., 2010). Compared to securely attached infants, insecure-avoidant, insecure-ambivalent, and disorganized infants all had higher cortisol following the Strange Situation Procedure (SSP) (Spangler et al., 1993; Hertsgaard et al., 2023). Thus, altered cortisol responses to stress are already evident in early childhood and may be modulated by the mother-infant relationship. The primary objective of the current study was to investigate infants' cortisol stress reactivity in response to maternal separation and its relationship with the security of the infant-mother attachment relationship at the end of the first year of life.

Stress response: temperament, mother-infant attachment, and cortisol

Infant temperament and the security of their attachment to their mothers are two important variables that may influence stress responsiveness early in life. The Strange Situation Procedure (SSP) paradigm, which includes a series of separations and reunions with parents (Ainsworth et al., 1978), has been used in prior research to measure infants' and toddlers' cortisol stress responsiveness (Spangler et al., 1993; Nachmias et al., 1996; Gunnar et al., 1995; Gunnar et al., 2009; Hertsgaard et al., 2023; Beijers et al., 2017; Hostinar et al., 2014; O'Connor et al., 2013; Luijk et al., 2010). ‘Distress to limits’, a temperament characteristic conceptually related to anger proneness, fearfulness, and lack of control, has been associated with increased cortisol reactivity to infant-parent separation in infants and toddlers [15-19, reviewed in 21]. For example, fifteen-month-old infants with high anger proneness had increased cortisol reactivity 20 min after the start of an abbreviated SSP (Van Bakel et al., 2004). Several studies have found that, compared with securely attached infants, those with insecure attachment have higher cortisol levels following the SSP paradigm (Spangler et al., 1993; Nachmias et al., 1996; Gunnar et al., 1995; Gunnar et al., 2009; Hertsgaard et al., 2023). Although some studies have not found such an association [reviewed in 17]. It is possible that negative dimensions of infant temperament, specifically fear and behavioral inhibition, may interact with infant-mother attachment, influencing the cortisol stress response induced by the SSP [15, 16, reviewed in 21]. Compared to highly fearful-securely attached infants, highly fearful-insecurely attached infants exhibited high cortisol reactivity, as indicated by elevated cortisol levels 25 min after the first separation of the SSP (Gunnar et al., 1995). In contrast, among average- and low-fearful toddlers, the cortisol response was similar regardless of maternal attachment security (Gunnar et al., 1995). In a similar study, highly inhibited toddlers exhibited a pattern of cortisol hyperreactivity compared to toddlers low in behavioral inhibition, again irrespective of maternal-infant attachment security (Nachmias et al., 1996). Collectively, these studies suggest that negative dimensions of infant temperament should be included when investigating the relations between the security of the infant-mother attachment relationship and infant cortisol regulation in the SSP. In the current study, we examine changes in infant negative temperament (fear, anger, sadness) over the first year of life to understand how a temperamental predisposition to negative emotions and changes in negative temperament may relate to the toddler's cortisol reactivity during the SSP.

Mother-infant attachment behavior and cortisol attunement

Maternal–infant cortisol responses have been interpreted as “attuned” or “misattuned” based on the dyad's behavioral interaction (Atkinson et al., 2013; Hendrix et al., 2018; Hibel et al., 2015; Main et al., 1986; Ludmer Nofech-Mozes et al., 2020). A growing literature indicates that mothers' and infants' HPA-axis activity is coupled, although not necessarily with a similar pattern, such that infant cortisol covaries with maternal cortisol (Atkinson et al., 2013; Hendrix et al., 2018; Hibel et al., 2015; Ludmer Nofech-Mozes et al., 2020). For example, Hibel and colleagues reported stable mother–infant cortisol attunement from pre-to post-stressor across infant-focused stress tasks, but only when mothers displayed high levels of positive behavior during free play (Hibel et al., 2015).

Across the SSP, maternal cortisol typically decreases while infant/toddler cortisol increases, in both secure and insecure dyads (Hibel et al., 2015). However, secure infants tend to show smaller cortisol increases, and mother–infant changes are more closely matched in magnitude (though in opposite directions) (Hibel et al., 2015; Ludmer Nofech-Mozes et al., 2020). In insecure dyads, infants show larger cortisol increases that are associated with larger maternal decreases (Ludmer Nofech-Mozes et al., 2020). Atkinson and colleagues proposed that, in secure dyads, mothers may downregulate their own cortisol to buffer the infant's stress response, whereas in insecure dyads, disrupted affective communication may be associated with greater stress reactivity in both mother and child (Ludmer Nofech-Mozes et al., 2020). Because parent–infant physiological coordination may be linked to the development of infant self-regulation, the second aim of the current study was to test patterns of cortisol change across the SSP in mothers and toddlers, and whether these patterns differ by attachment security.

Risk of maternal depression, infant stress regulation and attachment

Perinatal maternal depression and elevated maternal depressive symptoms, including a lifetime history of depression, have been associated with infant HPA axis activity (Feldman et al., 2009). For example, a maternal lifetime history of depression has been shown to predict higher baseline and mean cortisol levels in 6-month-old infants following laboratory stressors, and peripartum depression has been associated with infant cortisol reactivity (Brennan et al., 2008). Laurent and colleagues further reported that 18-month-old toddlers exhibited lower post-separation cortisol when mothers' depressive symptoms decreased across the postpartum period, compared with toddlers whose mothers’ symptoms increased (Laurent et al., 2011). Maternal depressive symptoms and depression have also been linked to mother–infant attachment quality. Teti and colleagues found that mothers of securely attached infants reported fewer depressive symptoms (Teti et al., 1995), and Gravener and colleagues reported that mothers with major depressive disorder were more likely to have insecurely attached infants than securely attached infants (Gravener et al., 2011). Together, these findings support the inclusion of maternal depressive symptoms in studies of infant stress physiology and attachment.

The current study

The first goal of this study was to examine infants' cortisol reactivity during the Strange Situation Procedure (SSP) at the end of the first year of life and to test whether cortisol responses covaried with the security of the infant–mother attachment relationship. Based on prior research (Spangler et al., 1993; Gunnar et al., 2009; Hostinar et al., 2014; Ludmer Nofech-Mozes et al., 2020; Laurent et al., 2011), we hypothesized that infants with insecure attachment would show greater cortisol reactivity to the SSP than those with secure attachment. A second goal was to assess mothers’ cortisol reactivity during the SSP and to evaluate concordance between maternal and infant adrenocortical responses. We hypothesized that mothers and infants would show similar patterns of cortisol change across the SSP, but in opposite directions. Finally, if infant cortisol response trajectories differ by attachment security, we examined potential correlates of attachment security across the first postpartum year. Specifically, we tested whether mothers of securely versus insecurely attached infants differed in depressive symptom histories across the first year postpartum, and whether infant negative temperament during the first year was associated with attachment security.

Methods

Participants

The participants of the current study were 82 mother–infant dyads from a larger longitudinal study that recruited 154 pregnant subjects from obstetric clinics affiliated with a university in the Midwestern United States (see Table 1 for demographics of initial and final cohorts). Detailed screening methods were described in a previous publication (Marcus et al., 2011). Briefly, women were recruited between their eighth and twenty-eighth week of gestation when they met the following criteria: 1) were at least 20 years of age, 2) fluent in English, 3) had an annual income greater than $15,000, 3) had no plans for adoption, 4) had no plans to move from the area during the study period, 5) had no serious medical conditions or use of medications that affected HPA functioning, 6) and had no current substance abuse, eating disorders, bipolar illness, psychotic or neurological disorder. Women were considered eligible for the study if they were not currently depressed, but a previous history of depression was not an exclusion criterion. Prospective participants were administered the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (SCID-IV) by trained personnel who demonstrated inter-rater reliability of 85%-90% and were supervised by an adult psychiatrist. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Medical School (IRBMED).

Sample characteristics.

The longitudinal research design of the parent study included multiple time points throughout pregnancy and the first year postpartum. Data reported in the current study is a subset of the parent study that included mothers' reports of depressive symptoms at 32 weeks prepartum, and 3-,7-, and 14-month postpartum. Negative infant temperament was assessed at 3, 7, and 14 months postpartum by maternal report. Mothers and their infants visited the university laboratory at 14 months postpartum to participate in a standard Ainsworth Strange Situation Procedure (SSP) (Keeny, 2003), which was followed by a 15-min free-play session. The 14-month laboratory visit lasted approximately 2 h. Saliva was collected from both mothers and infants at 4 time points during the laboratory visit and assayed for cortisol, the outcome used in the current study. The SSP was also used to classify infants as secure or insecure in their attachments to their mothers. Eighty-two mother-infant dyads participated in the SSP at the 14-month time point. The 82 mothers studied at 14-months were significantly older, t(152) = −2.06, p = .041, more likely to be married, χ2(4, N = 154) = 21.71, p = < 0.01, had more education, χ2(9, N = 154) = 36.91, p < .01, higher household incomes, χ2(6, N = 154) = 26.36, p < .01, and were more likely to be White χ(4, N = 154) = 20.84, p < .01, compared to mothers who were lost to attrition prior to 14-months (see Table 1). The high rate of attrition in the current sample was likely due to the intensive nature of data collection at each longitudinal time point (e.g., 3 days of diurnal saliva collection for mothers and infants at postnatal time points and several questionnaires assessing mothers' mood and infant behavior). Exclusion of dyads due to the infant's medical conditions and/or use of medications that alter HPA axis function also contributed to the attrition.

Measures

Strange situation procedure (SSP)

The SSP is a standard laboratory procedure used to assess mother-infant attachment security and consists of seven 3-min episodes, including a 1-min introduction, two separation episodes during which mothers observe their infants behind a two-way mirror, and two reunion episodes with the mother (Ainsworth et al., 1978). A new, challenging condition is presented to the mother and her child with each episode. In Episode 1, the mother and child were alone in the laboratory room, with the child exploring the new setting while the mother sat in a chair. In Episode 2, a strange person entered and interacted with the mother and the child. In Episode 3, the mother left the room, and the infant remained with the stranger (first separation). The first reunion (Episode 4) occurred between 30 s and 3 min (depending on whether the episode needed to be curtailed due to infant distress), when the mothers entered the room and the stranger left. In Episode 5, the mother left the room for the second separation, leaving the infant alone. In Episode 6, the stranger returned and attempted to calm and interact with the infant. Finally, Episode 7 was the last episode and the second reunion, in which the mother returned, and the stranger left the room.

All SSPs were video recorded and coded for secure or insecure attachment by two trained and reliable coders, who rated infant behavior during the reunion episodes (episodes 5 and 8) on four 7-point scales: proximity seeking, avoidance, contact maintenance, and contact resistance. These ratings were then used to classify infants into three categories: insecure-avoidant (A: n = 12), secure (B: n = 42), and insecure-resistant (C: n = 10), based on Ainsworth and colleagues' scoring (Ainsworth et al., 1978). Infants were also rated on a 9-point scale for disorganized attachment: 18 infants were categorized as disorganized (D: n = 18) (Main et al., 1986). Inter-rater reliability was 89.36% for attachment classifications, and disagreements were resolved through consensus. For the current analyses, infants were collapsed into secure (B: n = 42) and insecure (A, C, D: n = 40) groups.

Saliva collection and assays

All laboratory visits were scheduled after 13:00 h to maximize detection of maternal cortisol reactivity, as adult diurnal cortisol levels are typically low in the afternoon (Gunnar, 1992). Fourteen-month-old infants were assessed concurrently. At this developmental stage, infant cortisol shows minimal diurnal variation across late morning to mid-afternoon (between 10:00–16:00 h) (Spangler et al., 1993; Hertsgaard et al., 2023). A more adult-like diurnal pattern—higher cortisol in the morning, with progressive decreases throughout the day, lowest in the evening—emerges later in early childhood, at 3 years of age, and is associated with the consolidation of daytime sleep (fewer and shorter naps) (Watamura et al., 2003).

Mothers received written and verbal instructions prior to the visit, including refraining from eating or feeding their infant for 1 h and avoiding toothbrushing and feeding for 45 min prior to saliva collection. On the day before the session, instructions were reviewed and mothers were queried regarding factors known to influence cortisol (e.g., medication use, illness, deviations from typical sleep or morning routines). Dyads were excluded (e.g., chronic use of HPA axis–affecting medications) or rescheduled (e.g., acute illness) as appropriate.

Saliva was collected from mothers and infants without oral stimulants using hydrocellulose swabs (BD Ophthalmic Systems). Mothers placed the swab in the infant's mouth until saturated and provided their own sample via passive drool concurrently. Four samples were obtained: T1 (baseline) was collected ∼20 min after arrival and prior to the Strange Situation Procedure (SSP); T2 was collected 15 min after the first separation (Episode 4), typically coinciding with the end of the SSP; T3 was collected 30 min after the first separation; and T4 was collected 60 min after the first separation (30 min after T3). The SSP was not interrupted for sample collection, and exact collection times were recorded. Sampling times were selected to capture the cortisol response to stress, which typically peaks 15–40 min post-stressor in both infants and adults (Gunnar et al., 2009; Hertsgaard et al., 2023).

Following SSP completion, the dyad was escorted to an adjacent room for T2 collection (15 min post-separation; delayed as needed until the full interval elapsed). The dyad was then escorted to the laboratory room for a 15-min parent-infant free-play session, during which mothers were instructed to interact with their infant as they would at home. Immediately following free play, T3 was collected, followed by a structured 30-min maternal interview assessing infant sleep, crying, and feeding while the infant was on the floor playing with toys and had free access to their mother. Mothers were asked to avoid feeding or giving the infant any food or drink during this post-SSP period. If the infant became distressed during this period, mothers responded naturally by comforting or distracting the child to get them interested in the toys again. If, despite these attempts, the mother had to provide food or drink, the infant's mouth was cleaned with a damp cloth or rinsed with water prior to subsequent saliva collection. T4 was collected at the conclusion of the interview (30 min after T3), after which infants were offered a small snack (e.g., juice and cookies).

Following each saliva collection, samples were stored at −80 °C. The minimum saliva volume required for the duplicate assay was 25 μL, and cortisol levels were measured using an Expanded Range High Sensitivity Salivary Cortisol Enzyme Immunoassay with a detection range of 0.003–3.0 μg/dL. The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 3.5% and 5.08%, respectively. All samples from the same participant were assayed in the same batch to minimize inter-assay variation. All cortisol values were log-transformed due to the positive skew typical of human cortisol levels.

The final sample for cortisol analysis included mothers and infants with at least two cortisol time points; the minimum needed to assess change or cortisol reactivity to the stressor (the first separation of the SSP). Twenty-one infants and 13 mothers were excluded due to missing three or all four samples, leaving 61 infants and 69 mothers eligible for analysis. Of the 69 mothers with detectable cortisol values, 63 had values at all four time points (91%), and 6 had values at three time points. Of the 61 infants with detectable cortisol values, 48 had cortisol values at all four time points (79%), 10 infants had cortisol at three time points (16%), and three infants had cortisol at two time points (<1%). A likelihood ratio chi-square test indicated that there were no significant differences between the mothers with at least 2 samples of cortisol and those with 1 or less samples in terms of marital status χ2(3) = 1.15, p = .766, education χ2(8) = 8.03, p = .430, income χ2(5) = 5.93, p = .313, or race/ethnicity χ2(4) = 3.00, p = .558. These 2 groups of mothers also did not differ on age, t(80) = 0.98, p = .767.

Beck depression inventory

The 21-item BDI-II (Beck et al., 1996) was used to assess mothers' depressive symptoms and has been established as a reliable and valid measure of depressive symptoms of postpartum mothers (Ji et al., 2011). The BDI-II was collected at 32-weeks prepartum, at 3- and 7-months at home visits and approximately one week prior to the 14-month laboratory visit (32-weeks α = .85, 3-month α = .90, 7-month α = .89, 14-month α = .89). Mothers were asked to rate their symptoms over the past two weeks on a scale from 0 to 3 on items like Sadness: 0 = I do not feel sad; 1 = I feel sad much of the time; 2 = I am sad all of the time; 3 = I am so sad or unhappy that I can't stand it. Scores were summed across items. BDI-II scores are interpreted as follows: minimal range = 0 –13, mild depression = 14 –19, moderate depression = 20 –28, and severe depression = 29 – 63 (Beck et al., 1996).

Infant temperament

Infant temperament was assessed using the Revised Infant Behavior Questionnaire (IBQ-R (Gartstein et al., 2003)) at 3-, 7-, and 14-months postpartum. The IBQ-R is a 191-item parent-report measure of infant temperament in which parents rate the frequency of their infants' temperament-related behaviors observed over the past week. Infant temperament was rated on 14 subscales (all alphas between 0.62 and 0.92), which were used to create three broad scales: surgency/extraversion (approach, vocal reactivity, high pleasure, smile and laughter, activity level, and perceptual sensitivity), negative temperament (sadness, distress to limitations, fear, and falling reactivity), and orienting/regulation (cuddliness, duration of orienting, and soothability). Only negative temperament was included in the analyses because most research on the link between attachment and infant temperament has focused on infant negative affect (Nachmias et al., 1996; Hostinar et al., 2014; Van Bakel et al., 2004; Gartstein et al., 2003). Previous evidence suggests that mothers’ concurrent depressive symptoms or history of depression is unrelated to their reports on child temperament [42 Oli].

Data analysis plan

The analyses were designed to focus on what factors contributed to infant cortisol regulation in a stressful laboratory procedure, which in this case was the SSP, by examining the security of the infant-mother attachment relationship, the mother's cortisol response in the same stressful situation, and negative infant temperament and maternal depressive symptoms in the first year postpartum. All analyses were run using SPSS 25. For all analyses, statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05. We first specified a 2 (secure/insecure attachment) × 4 (time) linear mixed model (LMM) for infant cortisol, with a random intercept, using restricted maximum likelihood estimation for incomplete data, which allowed cortisol data from 61 infants to be included in the analyses. Infant gender was included as a covariate because prior research has shown that gender is associated with infant cortisol reactivity during the SSP (O'Connor et al., 2013; Olino et al., 2020). Next, to determine the trajectory pattern of the mothers' cortisol response during the same period, we conducted a 2 (secure/insecure) x 4 (time) LMM with a random intercept using restricted maximum likelihood estimation using mothers' cortisol, expecting that there should be similar patterns of change in cortisol across infants and mothers if their cortisol systems were attuned. Mothers' BDI-II scores and infants' negative temperament across the first year were also examined to investigate relations with infants' attachment security. For infant negative temperament, we specified a 2 (secure/insecure attachment) x 3 (time: 3-, 7-, and 14-months) LMM. We conducted a 2 (secure/insecure attachment) x 4 (time: 32-weeks prenatal, 3-, 7-, and 14-months postpartum) LMM with a random intercept using restricted maximum likelihood estimation with maternal depressive symptoms as the dependent variable to examine if there were differences across securely and insecurely attached infants with respect to changes in maternal depressive symptoms over the first year that might explain any differences in cortisol regulation for infants with either a secure or insecure infant-mother attachment relationship. Infant gender was included as a covariate because giving birth to a girl is linked to a higher risk for postpartum depression (Ye et al., 2022).

Results

Does infant cortisol reactivity change differently for securely- and insecurely- attached infants?

Results from the 2 x 4 LMM examining the trajectory of infant cortisol across the SSP for securely and insecurely attached infants revealed a significant interaction between attachment and time, F(3, 156.73) = 2.82, p = .041, which was followed up with Fisher's LSD post hoc tests. The different trajectories of infant cortisol based on log-transformed scores for securely and insecurely attached infants are shown in Fig. 1. Based on post-hoc comparisons, insecurely attached infants had significantly higher levels of cortisol at baseline (T1) compared to securely attached infants (p < .05). There were no significant differences between securely and insecurely attached infants at T2, T3, or T4 (all p's > 0.05). Post-hoc comparisons within group differences across time revealed a divergence in the direction of cortisol response after the separation stressor. Among securely attached infants, there was a trend toward higher cortisol levels at T3 compared to baseline (T3-p = 0.059). In contrast, cortisol levels in infants with insecure attachment were lower at T3 (p = .052) and T4 (p = .059) than at baseline.

Changes in cortisol for mothers of securely and insecurely attached infants

Results from the 2 x 4 LMM for mothers' cortisol showed a significant main effect of time, F(3, 192.28) = 30.94, p < .001, but no significant differences between mothers of securely attached infants and insecurely attached infants at any time point. As seen in Table 2, cortisol secretion declined over time for mothers of both secure and insecure infants. By Fisher's LSD post-hoc test, each time point was significantly different within group for all mothers studied (all time points, p < .05). The log-transformed values for securely and insecurely attached infants were significantly different at Time 1, consistent with the statistically significant difference observed with the non–log-transformed cortisol values.

Mothers’ depressive symptoms and infant attachment

The results from the 2 x 4 LMM for maternal depressive symptoms revealed a significant attachment by time interaction, F(3, 168.38) = 3.46, p = .018. The means can be seen in Table 3, and the different trajectories of mothers' depressive symptoms for securely and insecurely attached infants are shown in Fig. 2. Fisher's LSD post-hoc tests were conducted to follow up on significant interactions. At 32 weeks pre-partum, BDI scores were significantly higher in mothers of securely attached infants than in mothers of insecurely attached infants. However, the prenatal scores for the mothers of securely attached infants were within the minimal depression range. There were no statistically significant differences between mothers of securely attached infants and mothers of insecurely attached infants at any other time point, all ps > 0.05. Post-hoc comparisons revealed a significant change in depressive symptoms across time within each group. Specifically, among mothers of securely attached infants, depressive symptoms declined over time: symptoms were higher at 32 weeks prepartum than at 3, 7, and 14 months, all p < .05. The pattern was different for mothers of insecurely attached infants. There was a trend for BDI scores to increase over time until 7 months, after which they significantly decreased from 7 to 14 months (p < .05). All postpartum scores fell within the subclinical depression range (Beck et al., 1996; Smarr et al., 2011).

Negative infant temperament and infant attachment

As seen in Table 3, results from the fixed-effects LMMs examining changes in infant negative temperament over the first year for securely and insecurely attached infants indicated only a main effect of time, F(2, 102.24) = 17.21, p < .001. Fisher's LSD post-hoc tests revealed that negative infant temperament rose significantly at 7- and 14- months compared to 3-months for both securely and insecurely attached infants, all p < .05. However, there were no significant differences in negative temperament scores between the two attachment groups.

Discussion

The primary purpose of the current research was to investigate infants' cortisol reactivity during the SSP at the end of the first year of life and determine whether the cortisol-stress response covaried with the security of the infant-mother attachment relationship. We hypothesized that an elevated cortisol response to the SSP would be observed in infants with an insecure infant-mother attachment relationship. A second goal was to examine mothers' cortisol reactivity during the SSP, and we expected that mothers' and infants' cortisol reactivity would be attuned depending on whether the infant-mother attachment was secure or insecure. A third goal was to determine if mothers of securely attached and insecurely attached infants would have different histories of depressive symptoms during the first-year post-partum. We hypothesized that mothers of infants exhibiting insecure attachments would have higher depressive symptoms. The fourth goal was to determine whether negative infant temperament throughout the first year of life was related to infant-mother attachment security. We expected to find negative infant temperament in infants who were insecurely attached to their mothers. Our findings indicate that the cortisol reactivity of infants with secure infant-mother attachments differs from that of infants with insecure attachments. Mothers' cortisol response did not follow the same pattern of cortisol reactivity as their infants' during the SSP. Negative infant temperament did not differ between secure and insecurely attached infants. We also found that maternal depressive symptoms at 32 weeks of pregnancy were different between mothers; mothers of securely attached infants had BDI=II scores approaching the mild depression range. The post-partum depressive symptoms were low for all mothers. However, we found differences in the trajectory of maternal depressive symptoms in the post-partum period depending on whether the infant-mother attachment was secure or insecure. The clinical relevance of this finding is unclear since the depressive symptoms in our population, as assessed by BDI-II, were low, in the normal-to- minimal range. These results contribute to the growing body of evidence that the quality of the infant-mother attachment relationship can shape the development of the infant's HPA physiology, independent of the mother's depressive symptoms or the infant's negative temperament. Because the HPA system modulates stress responses, the HPA and adrenocortical responses of infants securely and insecurely attached to their mothers may differ yet share a common goal of adaptation.

Infants with secure or insecure attachments exhibit different cortisol reactivity

Cortisol reactivity of infants with secure attachments to their mothers differs from the cortisol reactivity observed in the infants with insecure attachments. Insecurely attached infants had significantly higher cortisol levels before the start of the SSP compared to securely attached infants. Once exposed to the SSP, the securely attached infants increased their cortisol level, whereas the cortisol response of insecurely attached infants declined over the course of the SSP. Cassidy and Berlin (Cassidy et al., 1994) hypothesized that heightened distress during SSP of insecurely attached infants may be viewed, “at least partially reflecting fear of a new place, fear of the stranger, and fear of separation from the parent”. Following this premise, the child's physiological response to maternal separation distress induced by the SSP procedure should be activation of the adrenocortical response (Gunnar et al., 1995, 2009), resulting in a rise in cortisol levels by 15 to 40 min after separation. However, unlike securely attached infants, insecurely attached infants began the SSP with high baseline cortisol levels, which decreased at time points expected to reflect adrenocortical stress activation during the SSP's first separation. The higher baseline cortisol levels in infants with insecure attachment were unexpected. We hypothesized that basal cortisol levels would be similar between securely and insecurely attached infants, as reported in multiple studies [e.g., 14, reviewed in 8, 17]. We considered several possible explanations: 1) eating or drinking less than 1 h prior to the laboratory visit, 2) awakening from a nap prior to travel or while traveling to the laboratory visit, and 3) maternal behavior disrupting the child's effort to cope with the change in routine or novel environment. Families reported adherence to pre-visit instructions, and we screened for health conditions and overnight or morning events that could affect basal cortisol levels. It is plausible that toddlers detect cues that the mother is preparing to leave home (dressing the child, searching for car keys, etc.); these behaviors may elicit distress or excitement, both of which are conducive to cortisol elevation. Factors that reduce cortisol warrant consideration. Cortisol levels decrease during a morning nap; this decrease is unrelated to the nap's timing or duration (Larson et al., 1991). Awakening within 45 min of a nap results in a significant cortisol surge (Larson et al., 1991). Larson and colleagues also reported that car travel can reduce cortisol levels, regardless of whether the child slept during the trip. Thus, travel to the laboratory might be expected to dampen any pre-trip cortisol elevation. Securely attached infants were consistent with this expectation, whereas insecurely attached infants were not.

Maternal characteristics associated with insecure attachment, as well as differences in infants' physiological adrenocortical responses across attachment categories, may contribute to the infant's HPA-axis functioning. Maternal depression has been linked to insecure and insecure-disorganized attachment (Teti et al., 1995; Gravener et al., 2011) and to altered infant diurnal cortisol rhythms, elevated baseline cortisol, and also heightened cortisol responses to stress (Feldman et al., 2009; Gravener et al., 2011). In the current study, however, mothers did not meet criteria for clinical postpartum depression; postpartum BDI scores did not differ between mothers of securely versus insecurely attached infants. Maternal sensitivity is another factor to consider, as it is a determinant of infant attachment (Bader et al., 2021; Madigan et al., 2024). Spangler et al. reported that 9-month-old infants of highly insensitive mothers had higher pretest cortisol prior to a 15-min play session followed by a diaper change (Madigan et al., 2024). Thus, it is possible that the 14-month-old infants with insecure attachment in our study may have higher pretest cortisol levels due to maternal insensitivity at an age when infants are beginning to develop emotion regulation skills. Another possibility is heterogeneity within the insecure group (A/C/D) impacting the infant's HPA function. Notably, elevated pre-SSP cortisol is not typically associated with avoidant, resistant, or disorganized attachment classifications, either in low-risk cohorts (reviewed in (Gunnar, 1992; Spangler et al., 1993)) or in studies of children exposed to early adversity (e.g. (Luijk et al., 2010; Dozier et al., 2008)). Spangler and Schieche studied emotional and adrenocortical response to SSP in 12-month-old infants and found that pre-SSP cortisol levels were higher for insecure-ambivalent C infants, but not for A insecure-avoidant infants, or B secure infants (Spangler and Schieche, 1998). The C infants started with a high cortisol level and responded to the SSP separation with a further rise. Spangler and Schieche comment that, from the start, the cortisol response appeared to be to “the whole assessment procedure, including the preparatory events, rather than the separation alone”. Our sample size precluded analyses by insecure subtype. Our findings may indicate group differences in basal HPA activity between infants with insecure and secure attachment. Larger samples of children in each attachment classification group are needed to draw firmer conclusions about the association between attachment, basal cortisol, and stress reactivity.

The typical “heightened cortisol response to separation stress” was not observed in the insecure group in our study. One plausible explanation for the decline in cortisol among infants with insecure attachment approximately 30 min after the first separation is negative feedback inhibition that began before the SSP. Elevated cortisol at the start of the procedure could suppress upstream HPA activity, thereby preserving HPA-axis stability and protecting cortisol-sensitive neural circuits (Gunnar et al., 2006). Repeated HPA activation has been implicated in the development of mood and anxiety disorders, and animal work suggests that stress-induced increases in CRH activity in limbic regions are regulated by the buffering effects of maternal behavior (Korosi and Baram, 2010). Although human evidence is limited, elevated baseline cortisol in insecurely attached infants could plausibly shape neurobiological processes that support persistent anxiety and behavioral inhibition (Gunnar et al., 1995; Ariño-Braña et al., 2025).

In contrast, securely attached infants showed lower baseline cortisol and a more consistent pattern with normative SSP stress responding (Gunnar, 1992; Gunnar et al., 2009; Hostinar et al., 2014). A lower baseline cortisol level may reflect reduced basal HPA activity and reduced anticipatory arousal in a novel context. The role of the mother in buffering infants' cortisol responses to stress is an adaptive mechanism that Seymour Levine studied for decades in non-human primates and rodents, inspiring others to investigate the neurobiological underpinnings of the mother-infant relationship (Suchecki, 2018). Levine demonstrated that the critical modulators of the infant's stress response are maternal behaviors that regulate specific brain and pituitary components of the HPA axis, thereby inhibiting the cortisol response at a critical stage of development (Levine et al., 1957, 1959; Suchecki, 2018). This framework aligns with attachment theory (Ainsworth et al., 1978; Bowlby, 1969): sensitive, responsive caregiving supports secure attachment and a sense of safety, which in turn modulates infants' HPA-axis responses to stressors (Gunnar et al., 1995; Schieche et al., 2005; La Rosa et al., 2024).

Mothers do not demonstrate cortisol reactivity to the strange situation procedure

In response to the SSP paradigm, mothers' cortisol levels typically decrease while infants'/toddlers' levels increase, in both secure and insecure dyads (Hibel et al., 2015). This opposite pattern of cortisol response has been interpreted as “attunement” by Feldman and others, who define attunement as the process by which the mother and child respond and adjust physiologically to one another over time (Feldman, 2012). Ludmer Nofech-Mozes and colleagues indicate that “conceptualization is similar in many ways to that of parental sensitivity; it involves the capacity to respond appropriately to the other, as necessitated by their signals and environmental context (Ludmer Nofech-Mozes et al., 2020).” To this end, it has been interpreted that the securely attached dyad's cortisol response to SSP has a sensitive mother who downregulates her adrenocortical response to help “downregulate her distressed infant”. For the insecure dyad, the pattern is similar, but the intensity of the response is magnified. Ludmer Nofech-Mozes and colleagues argue that the child “not being able to rely on a consistent strategy to secure maternal responsiveness may have heightened” the cortisol response while the mother has a dampened or flattened her response in opposite direction (Ludmer Nofech-Mozes et al., 2020). In our study, mothers' cortisol output steadily declined during the SSP, regardless of the level of security or insecurity in the infant-mother attachment, which, as mentioned, is consistent with previous studies. However, only the securely attached infant had the expected pattern of a steady increase; this increase was not observed in the insecurely attached infants, who instead had a steady decrease parallel to their mother's cortisol decline. These findings appear to follow maternal “misattunement,” or it may be “lack of attunement”. In a situation of “misattunement”, Atkinson and colleagues indicate that the mother's response is poorly adjusted to the infant's reactions, shows much slower cortisol downregulation than would be appropriate for the infant's level of distress, or shows no awareness of the infant's high level of distress (Atkinson et al., 2016). In the situation of “lack of attunement”, the mother's and infant's cortisol response patterns are, in fact, synchronized because mother and child are reacting to the challenge independently (Atkinson et al., 2016). The observed maternal-infant response of the insecure dyads in our study is consistent with ‘lack of attunement’; the mother and the infant are reacting to the challenge independently (Ludmer Nofech-Mozes et al., 2020; Atkinson et al., 2016). The insecure infants arrived at the laboratory with elevated cortisol levels that declined over time following a physiologically appropriate negative feedback mechanism, while the mother's initial cortisol and subsequent response downregulated pattern may reflect an anticipatory stress response to the novel, unfamiliar procedures expected, followed by a subsequent appropriate inhibition of the anticipatory adrenocortical response. Future studies may need to reconsider when saliva samples are collected and whether cortisol reactivity in mothers is indeed in response to the laboratory SSP stressor or the stress of the laboratory visit, in general. In future research, the design of the SSP should allow more time to elapse between arrival and the first baseline cortisol assessment to evaluate potential confounds.

Negative infant temperament, attachment security, and cortisol response to SSP

In the current study, there were no differences in negative temperament between the groups. Thus, our findings suggest that negative temperament was not related to the security of the infant-mother attachment and therefore, does not explain the differences in cortisol responses between insecurely and securely attached infants in the SSP. Prior research on the relation between infant-mother attachment classifications and infant temperament has been inconclusive (for review, Groh et al., 2017, Vaughn et al., 2016), with some studies finding a connection (Frodi et al., 1989; Leerkes et al., 2018) and others finding a weak to moderate association depending on how attachment is classified (for review see Groh et al., 2017, Van Ijzendoorn, 1995). It is possible that maternal sensitivity may interact with negative infant temperament to predict infant-mother attachment security (Leerkes et al., 2018) and examining how maternal behavior and negative temperament interact to predict attachment security and infant stress physiology may be a promising area for future research.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, attrition was high, likely reflecting the time demands and participant burden of the intensive longitudinal protocol (repeated questionnaires and multi-sample diurnal saliva collection during the postpartum period). Differential attrition may have introduced selection bias; mothers retained in the analytic sample were disproportionately college-educated.

Second, external validity is limited by the final cohort's demographic composition. Mothers were predominantly White, college-educated, and married, and families were largely high SES. Findings, therefore, may not generalize to families with greater socioeconomic disadvantages or to more racially/ethnically diverse populations. It may seem surprising that in this sample of high-functioning mothers, there are almost equal numbers of securely and insecurely attached infants. However, longitudinal studies have found that parental sensitivity and involvement were stronger predictors of infant–mother attachment security than factors such as socioeconomic status or maternal education (Lickenbrock et al., 2015).

Third, fathers were not assessed, even though most mothers were married. This is notable given evidence that infant cortisol responses during the Strange Situation Procedure (SSP) may vary as a function of attachment relationships with both caregivers (Bader et al., 2021). Future studies incorporating attachment assessments and SSP stress responses for each caregiver could clarify caregiver-specific associations with infant HPA-axis reactivity.

Fourth, measurement of cortisol reactivity in infancy is inherently challenging. Difficulty obtaining sufficient saliva volume reduced the analytic sample and limited power to test cortisol patterns across the four primary attachment classifications (secure, avoidant, resistant/ambivalent, disorganized). We therefore relied on a secure-versus-insecure categorization, which may have obscured differences in cortisol secretion across attachment subtypes (Luijk et al., 2010). In addition, pre-laboratory experiences can influence HPA-axis activity (Ye et al., 2022; Smarr et al., 2011). Although we assessed broad deviations from typical night/morning routines, a more detailed characterization of morning activities and transit to the laboratory (e.g., preparation for travel, travel conditions, and parent–infant interaction during transit) would strengthen inferences regarding the pre-task baseline. Future protocols may benefit from collecting a home sample immediately prior to departure to better index baseline levels.

Finally, maternal depressive symptoms were generally subthreshold in this cohort, limiting inferences about clinically significant depression. Studies that include clinically depressed mothers often report heightened maternal and infant stress reactivity (Teti et al., 1995; Gravener et al., 2011; Dozier et al., 2008; Spangler and Schieche, 1998; Korosi and Baram, 2010). In the present sample, however, depressive symptoms were low overall, suggesting that observed differences in infant cortisol reactivity are unlikely to be driven by maternal depression and may instead reflect variation in attachment security.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the results suggest that the quality of the infant-mother attachment relationship influences the development of the infant's HPA physiology, as reflected in the cortisol response to stress in the SSP. Because the HPA system modulates stress responses, the altered HPA and adrenocortical responses of securely and insecurely attached infants to their mothers may differ yet share a common goal of adaptation. In both cases, their HPA adrenocortical responses may be activated to manage stressful experiences and reduce ongoing cortisol production, thereby altering the developmental trajectories of the brain and various physiological systems. Infants with insecure attachment often have mothers who interact differently than mothers of securely attached infants, being less sensitive and more intrusive in their interactions (Madigan et al., 2024), which can result not only in an insecure infant-mother attachment, but also in high basal, and/or high anticipatory cortisol levels before an environmental stressor (Spangler et al., 1994). The development of a counteracting, robust negative cortisol feedback mechanism in an attempt to “shut down” HPA hormone and neurotransmitter products is an adaptation that infants who are insecurely attached to their mothers appear to have developed early in life. Although future research will need to elaborate on these relations further, the current results contribute to the literature supporting the importance of early identification, intervention, and management of infant-mother attachment difficulties in the postnatal period (Kaur et al., 2024), with the goal to mitigate potential negative repercussions in the developing brain and body systems of the human infant, that are sensitive to cortisol dysregulation (Doom et al., 2025).
