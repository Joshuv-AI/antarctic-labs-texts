Isodesmic Supramolecular Assembly of Phenylethynylene Bis‐Urea Macrocycles
Authors: Fahidat A Gbadamosi, Kazi Md Zubaer, Vaibhavi A Samant, Jillian Eubanks, Sophya Garashchuk, Linda S Shimizu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this study, we report a soluble phenylethynylene bis‐urea macrocycle with a supramolecular assembly mechanism distinct from previously studied bis‐urea macrocycles. The macrocycle was synthesized via a dynamic covalent synthesis approach with two exterior n‐dodecyloxy groups to enhance solubility in organic and mixed aqueous media. Temperature‐dependent UV‐Visible and fluorescence spectroscopy in 45% H2O/THF are well described by an isodesmic mechanism, characterized by a clear isosbestic point, strong enthalpic stabilization (ΔH ∼ ‐133 kJ mol−1), and a degree of polymerization of ∼4 at 284 K. Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirms hydrogen‐bond‐driven association. Matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization time‐of‐flight (MALDI‐TOF) detects primarily dimers, likely due to dissociation during ionization. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) showed the formation of nanoscale aggregates and fibrillar supramolecular structures. Additives capable of engaging the urea NH units, including a phosphate‐functionalized quarterphenyl guest, reduced both the enthalpy and degree of polymerization, demonstrating tunable control of chain length. Overall, these findings identify this phenylethynylene bis‐urea macrocycle as a rare example of an isodesmic, hydrogen‐bonded supramolecular polymer in polar media and highlight design principles for controlling assembly length and developing solution‐phase host‐guest frameworks.Keywords: chain‐stoppers, hydrogen‐bonded, isodesmic, macrocycles, urea

Introduction

Supramolecular polymers are dynamic, reversible assemblies formed by noncovalent interactions that direct the organization of molecular building blocks [1, 2, 3, 4]. The monomeric units are held together by directional, moderately strong, and reversible interactions such as hydrogen bonding [1, 5, 6], π–π interactions [7, 8, 9], and metal–ligand coordination [3, 10, 11]. Supramolecular polymerization mechanisms include cooperative processes, which have two binding constants for nucleation (Kn) and elongation (Ke), and isodesmic mechanisms, where each monomer addition is characterized by a single equilibrium constant (Ka) [12]. Owing to the reversible nature of these interactions, supramolecular polymers have attracted increasing attention as they combine features of conventional polymers with unique properties such as self‐healing, processability, adaptability, and stimuli‐responsiveness. Moreover, their modular design allows the incorporation of functional motifs with tailored properties, enabling precise control over structure and function [4, 11].

Macrocyclic compounds have emerged as versatile building blocks for supramolecular polymers owing to their rigid and highly symmetrical structures. They possess cavities that can capture guest molecules via noncovalent interactions, endowing the resulting supramolecular assemblies with unique structural and functional properties [13, 14, 15]. Crown ethers [16], cucurbiturils [17], pillar[n]arenes [14, 15], and cyclodextrins [18, 19] are some of the most common macrocyclic monomers employed in supramolecular polymer design. For instance, linear supramolecular polymers with multi‐responsive properties were formed from a crown‐ether based B21C7 AA monomer containing a disulfide bond and a bis(dialkylammonium salt)‐based BB monomer driven by host‐guest interactions in 1:1 CD3CN/CDCl3 [20]. Strutt et al. [21] reported a pillar[5]arene derivative bearing viologen side chain that self‐assembles in CH2Cl2 at concentrations above 0.1 mM and forms an organogel at concentrations exceeding 25 mM. Other macrocyclic monomers include a triarylamine‐based macrocycle bearing six peripheral amide groups that undergo supramolecular polymerization into nanotubes in o‐dichlorobenzene through hydrogen bonding interactions and π−π stacking [22].

The Shimizu group assembles bis‐urea macrocycles into porous crystalline solids with accessible 1D channels [23]. The size and shape of these channels are determined by the dimensions and geometry of the macrocyclic molecule. For example, phenylethynylene bis‐urea macrocycle 1 (Figure 1A) has an internal cavity of ∼9 Å in diameter, while terphenyl 2 and triphenylamine bis‐urea macrocycle 3 have cavities with diameters of ∼7 and ∼5 Å, respectively (Figure 1B). These materials serve as functional host frameworks and nanocontainers for selectively promoting chemical reactions.

Recent work probed the assembly mechanisms of bis‐urea macrocycles to stabilize bis‐urea macrocycle supramolecular polymers and nanoscale assemblies in solution. One strategy to accomplish this involves increasing solubility and slowing the assembly process by attaching external groups, such as tridodecyloxybenzene, to macrocycles. For example, derivatives of 2 and 3 with exterior tridodecyloxybenzenes display enhanced solubility in THF, CHCl3, and other common solvents compared to their sparingly soluble counterparts (R = H), which are soluble in warm DMSO. The smaller macrocycle 3 exhibited cooperative assembly at 10 µM in 40% H2O/THF. Chain stoppers, such as diphenylsulfoxide and dimethyl sulfoxide, limited the size of the assemblies [24]. In addition, these chain stoppers reduce the storage and loss moduli of the supramolecular gels formed by 3 in toluene [25]. Experimental evidence indicates that diphenylacetylene guests remain bound within these supramolecular polymers, indicating retention of the columnar assembly observed in the crystalline solids. Likewise, macrocycle 2 with acetylene‐linked tridodecyloxybenzenes assembled cooperatively at low concentration (5 µM in 25% H2O/THF) [26]. This nonplanar macrocycle, designed to introduce complexity, exhibited enthalpically driven aggregation with a large hysteresis (ΔTe = 17 K) between the heating/disassembly and cooling/assembly processes as determined by temperature‐dependent UV‐Visible (UV‐Vis) spectroscopy. The observed discrepancies between experimentally derived enthalpy and Van't Hoff analysis suggest that assembly of 2 is under kinetic control.

In comparison to 2 and 3, 1 is the largest bis‐urea macrocycle, affording a channel capable of sequestering coumarin, stilbene, and acenaphthylene guests [27]. Although larger than 3, it still contains two urea groups that guide its directional assembly. This prompted an investigation into the assembly mechanisms of soluble derivatives of 1 to assess if they would exhibit nucleation/growth or if the enhanced size would lead to alternative mechanisms or added complexity. In this study, we use a dynamic covalent strategy to synthesize phenylethylene bis‐urea macrocycle 1 with exterior dodecyloxy groups and investigate the supramolecular polymerization in TCE, THF, and H2O/THF mixtures by Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR), UV‐Vis, and Fluorescence spectroscopy. The size and morphology are elucidated using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) techniques and Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM). In addition, we probe the assembly of 1 in the presence of additives that can act as templates and/or chain stoppers to evaluate their effect on the assembly mechanism and degree of polymerization. Ultimately, our goals are to favor supramolecular polymer formation in solutions and to examine whether these dynamic systems can also be used to uptake guests and facilitate reactions, as shown in their crystalline counterparts.

Results and Discussion

Design and Synthesis of Soluble Derivatives

The phenylethynylene bis‐urea macrocycle (1A, R = H) has limited solubility and assembles from DMSO (5 mg/mL) upon slow cooling (1°C/h) from 120°C, affording needle‐like crystals [27, 28]. Similar to other bis‐urea macrocycles, these porous crystals are formed from 1D columnar assemblies of the monomer, held together by urea hydrogen bonding. Given the utility of 1A in facilitating reactions within its nanochannel, we were interested in studying soluble derivatives, such as 1, to probe the mechanism of assembly and examine the utility of their oligomers.

Our first plan was to explore the synthesis of the bis‐urea macrocycle using a dynamic covalent strategy recently introduced by Cheng's group [29]. The key step involves the reversible formation of the urea group from diisocyanates and hindered 2° diamines, such as a t‐butyl amine [29]. Scheme 1 shows this convergent strategy. We opted to generate the diisocyanates in situ from the Boc‐protected diamine 6 due to their high reactivity. Because the new exterior group was introduced at the beginning of the synthesis, we did our initial synthesis using the linear dodecyl derivative. This linear alkyl chain was expected to improve solubility in organic solvents while also contributing additional van der Waals and dispersion interactions that can enhance assembly [30].

Synthesis scheme of 1.

The Boc‐protected diamine 6 was prepared in four steps from 3,5‐dibromophenol and afforded the diisocyanate in situ upon reaction with 2‐chloropyridine and trifluoromethanesulfonic anhydride for 50 min. (See SI for details) Next, Et3N was added, followed by t‐butyl diamine 5, to afford the protected macrocycle 4 (135 mg, 45%). Subsequent removal of the t‐butyl groups of 4 with trifluoroacetic acid for 3 h yielded macrocycle 1 (Scheme 1), which displayed improved solubility versus the unsubstituted macrocycle, which was only soluble in DMSO and hot DMF. The intermediates and macrocycle 1 were fully characterized by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and HRMS.

Macrocycle 1 was soluble in 1,1,2,2‐tetrachloroethane (TCE), reaching concentrations of up to 500 µM at room temperature and 1 mM upon heating. The macrocycle was also soluble in THF at concentrations up to 200 µM at room temperature and 1 mM when heated. In contrast, limited solubility was observed in other common solvents employed in supramolecular polymerization studies, such as methylcyclohexane, chloroform, and toluene.

Self‐Assembly Studies

FTIR spectroscopy is a valuable tool for monitoring the self‐assembly of urea‐containing compounds, as it distinguishes between the stretching frequencies of free versus hydrogen‐bonded NH groups. Concentration‐dependent FTIR of 1 was recorded in TCE across four concentrations. At concentrations up to 100 µM, only the stretching vibrations of the free urea protons are observed, appearing at 3602 cm−1 and 3685 cm−1. Upon increasing concentration to 500 µM, the intensity of the free urea stretching vibrations is reduced, and there is an appearance of red‐shifted bands at 3330 cm−1 and 3450 cm−1, which are assigned to the stretching vibrations of hydrogen‐bonded urea protons (Figure S19). Similar, red‐shifted NH bands have been reported for other urea‐hydrogen‐bonded assemblies [31, 32]. The emergence of these bands signifies hydrogen bonding interactions between the urea groups. In contrast, in pure THF, the urea NH stretching vibrations were observed at 3505 cm−1 and 3571 cm−1 (Figure S20), which were assigned to the weaker interaction between the urea protons and THF ether oxygen, likely explaining this smaller red‐shift.

Absorbance Studies of 1

As urea hydrogen‐bonded NH stretches were notably absent in THF, we turned to UV‐Vis spectroscopy, expecting spectra in THF to be monomeric. Interestingly, the absorbance spectra of 1 at 10 µM in TCE and THF were similar in shape, with λmax at 289 and 288 nm, respectively, suggesting that this band may be due to the monomer (Figure S21c). Next, we examined the UV‐Vis spectra of 1 in THF upon increasing the concentration from 5 to 25 µM at 293 K. No shift in the spectral shape, absorption maximum (λ max = 288 nm), or extinction coefficient (ε) was observed (Figure S21a,b), indicating that 1 is a monomer over this concentration range.

We next evaluated the temperature‐dependent behaviour of 1 at 10 µM in THF. Upon cooling from 338 to 284 K, only a slight difference in the spectra was observed, with no change in λ max at 288 nm (Figure S22a). Therefore, to promote aggregation of 1 at 10 µM, the absorption was measured in 0%–50% H2O/THF. Similar water‐induced aggregation has been reported for other systems [33], such as the perylene bisimide dyad, which aggregated above 30% H2O in H2O/THF mixtures. Figure S22b shows that from 10% to 40% H2O, the spectra remained largely unchanged; however, at 45% H2O, subtle spectra changes were observed, including a decrease in λ Max = 288 nm band and a new band at 345 nm. Further increasing the H2O content to 50% led to a red‐shifted band at 345 nm, suggesting the onset of self‐assembly of 1.

Based on these observations, 45% H2O/THF was selected for probing the assembly process. The sample was prepared by dissolving 1 at 10 µM in 45% H2O/THF and annealing to 338 K to ensure complete dissolution. Next, temperature‐dependent UV‐Vis spectra were measured as the sample cooled from 338 to 284 K to monitor the transition from the molecularly dissolved state to the assembled state. Spectra were recorded at 3 K intervals, with a 5–10 min equilibration period at each temperature step to ensure sufficient equilibration and continued until the absorbance spectra remained constant. Figure 2a shows that upon cooling, the monomer band at λ Max = 288 nm decreased, and a broad new band appeared at 345 nm. Others have observed this hypochromic shift from monomer to aggregate. In addition, upon aging at 284 K, the solutions become turbid due to precipitate formation and display a general decrease in absorbance. The presence of a notable isosbestic point at 310 nm may suggest that the system forms dimers or that aggregation proceeds without further spectral changes or detectable intermediates. This behavior contrasts with macrocycles 2 and 3, which did not exhibit an isosbestic point in H2O/THF mixtures.

(b) The plot of degree of aggregation as a function of temperature, including fitting to the isodesmic model. (c) Van't Hoff plot obtained from isodesmic fitting of temperature‐dependent absorbance data to obtain thermodynamic parameters.

Using the absorbance at 345 nm (284 K) as 100% aggregate, the degree of aggregation was calculated and plotted as a function of temperature (Figure 2b). The aggregate fraction versus temperature data were fit with both isodesmic and cooperative models (Figures 2b and S23). The isodesmic model provided the best fit, suggesting that 1 assembles via the isodesmic mechanism. From the fit, the degree of polymerization (DPN) was obtained using equation S4, which shows that 1 has a DPN of 4.16 at 284 K. Since this is a temperature‐dependent experiment, the association constant (Ka) can be obtained at each temperature. With this, a Van't Hoff analysis (ln Ka vs. 1/T) yielded thermodynamic parameters: ΔH = ‐133 kJ mol−1 and ΔS = ‐351 J mol−1 K−1, suggesting that the assembly of 1 is an enthalpically driven process (Figure 2c). The corresponding Gibbs free energy (ΔG = ‐33.6 kJ mol−1) and association constant (Ka = 1.52 × 106 M−1) at 284 K confirm that assembly of 1 is spontaneous under these conditions.

While many hydrogen‐bonded supramolecular polymers display cooperative nucleation‐elongation behavior, including bis‐urea macrocycles 2 and 3, isodesmic H‐bonded supramolecular systems remain rare [34]. Direct comparison to structurally similar bis‐urea systems that exhibit isodesmic growth is limited. Therefore, we contextualize the thermodynamic signatures of 1 against other known isodesmic H‐bonded assemblies. Oligo(phenyleneethynylene) derivatives bearing urea and amide groups assemble in an isodesmic manner in MCH with Ka ∼ 105 M−1 and ΔH = ‐100 kJ mol−1 [35]. Thienopyrrole‐fused thiadiazoles with amide groups also follow the isodesmic model in MCH and MCH/Toluene mixtures, displaying Ka values on the order of 107 M−1 [36]. Likewise, anthanthrene monomers incorporating mixed ester/amide hydrogen‐bonding motifs exhibit isodesmic assembly in MCH/TCE mixtures, with ΔH = ‐103.8 kJ mol−1. Notably, nearly all reported isodesmic H‐bonded supramolecular polymers have been examined in apolar solvents, where hydrogen bonding is strongly favored. In contrast, 1 represents a rare example of an isodesmic H‐bond assembly operating in a polar, competitive H2O/THF environment. Despite the substantial energetic penalty typically imposed by such media, the bis‐urea motif in 1 enables the formation of multiple directional hydrogen bonds between adjacent macrocycles, while the rigid aryl spacers promote aryl stacking and the dodecyl chains contribute to hydrophobic desolvation in aqueous media.

Emission Studies of 1

Complementary emission studies of 1 were performed (λ exc = 290 nm, 10 µM, and 45% H2O/THF) from 338 to 284 K. At 338 K, the fluorescence spectra display a broad emission band with λ max = 343 nm and a shoulder at λ = 354 nm (Figure S24a). Upon cooling, the emission intensity gradually decreased with no shift in peak positions. This suggests that 1 undergoes aggregation‐caused quenching [37]. The degree of aggregation was obtained by monitoring the emission band at 343 nm as a function of temperature and fitting to both isodesmic and cooperative models. The isodesmic model provided the best fit with DPN of 3.68 (Figure S24b). The DPN is similar, though slightly lower than that obtained by absorbance measurements. The thermodynamic parameters obtained from the emission data (ΔH = ‐131 kJ mol−1, ΔS = ‐345 J mol−1 K−1, and ΔG = ‐32.9 kJ mol−1) were consistent with those derived from UV‐Vis analysis, confirming the assembly of 1 is enthalpically driven and spontaneous under these conditions. The corresponding association constant (1.13 × 106 M−1) is similar to that obtained by UV‐Vis.

Size and Morphology of 1

DLS experiments were carried out at H2O/THF compositions of 35%, 40%, and 45% water with annealing at 338 K (30 s) then cooling to 298 K (Figure 3; Figure S26; Table 1). In 45% water, aggregates with a Z‐average of 357 nm were observed, which showed an average PDI of 0.37 As the water content decreases, smaller assemblies were observed with an average hydrodynamic diameter (Z‐average) of 274 nm and a PDI of 0.23 at 40% H2O/THF (Figure 3a) and 35% H2O/THF showing an average hydrodynamic diameter of 236 nm and a PDI of 0.23 (Figure S26). While DLS alone cannot distinguish between assembly mechanisms, such dispersity is consistent with a distribution of aggregate sizes expected for isodesmic polymerization [38, 39]. DLS studies on annealed and unannealed samples show similar size distributions (Table 1 and S1), suggesting that the observed aggregates are not strongly dependent on thermal history, consistent with a noncooperative assembly process.

Thus, we also plotted the electric‐field autocorrelation function (G1) as a function of time to examine its decay. Figure S27 shows a slower decay rate in the correlation function over time for 45% water than for 40% or 35%. This is consistent with 45% water sample having larger particles, which diffuse more slowly and exhibit slower decay rates in the correlation function, as observed by the Hud group [40, 41].

MALDI‐TOF MS analysis of a 10 µM solution of 1 in 45% H2O/THF showed a distinct peak corresponding to the dimer of 1 (Figure S25), providing molecular‐level evidence for aggregation. However, under MALDI conditions screened, no higher‐mass species were detected/preserved. This is consistent with prior reports showing that noncovalent assemblies frequently dissociate during ionization/desorption, resulting in the selective observation of only small aggregates. Drop casting a 10 µM 45% H2O/THF sample of 1 on a glass slide under an optical microscope reveals numerous small fibrous aggregates (Figure 1c). AFM images obtained from a drop cast sample of 10 µM solution of 1 in 45% H2O/THF reveal the formation of fibrillar supramolecular bundles with average heights of ∼ 55 nm (Figure 1d). FT‐IR on the dried fibrous aggregates shows hydrogen‐bonded N‐H stretching at 3326 cm−1 and C = O stretch at 1631 cm−1, typical of a three‐centered urea hydrogen bond network observed for other crystalline bis‐urea macrocycles [42, 43] (Figure S18).

Computational Investigation

Prior work on smaller bis‐urea macrocycles bearing exterior tridodecyloxyphenyl solubilizing groups has shown that these systems typically undergo cooperative supramolecular polymerization. For example, a xylene bis‐urea macrocycle lacking an internal cavity assembles cooperatively in TCE [44]. Triphenylamine and terphenyl bis‐urea macrocycles, which possess channel diameters of approximately 5 and 7 Å, respectively, exhibit cooperative self‐assembly in H2O/THF mixtures [23, 24, 26]. In contrast, macrocycle 1, which follows an isodesmic mechanism, features a larger accessible channel (∼9 Å) [23, 28] and carries only two linear dodecyloxy solubilizing groups. These differences suggest that distinct structural or energetic parameters may govern the assembly mechanisms in the bis‐urea macrocycle, as we explored through the computational analysis of the electronic structure (see Supporting Information for full details).

First, to gauge the impact of the solubilizing group on the conformation of the macrocycle, we examined the energies of the two key urea orientations commonly observed in bis‐urea macrocycles. The structure of the unsubstituted macrocycle 1A (R = H) was obtained from the crystal structure of the Cambridge Structural Database, CCDC no. 847818. The monomer was then modified using Spartan 24 software to replace R with ‐OC6H13, a model linear exterior group. Next, Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were conducted using QChem 6.1 version in an implicit solvent environment described by the Polarized Continuum Model (PCM) with a dielectric constant of 54.6 (equivalent to 1:1 H2O/THF). Two urea orientations were considered: antiparallel, in which the urea carbonyls and N‐H groups are oriented in opposite directions, and parallel, in which they point in the same direction (Figure 4a,b). For the unsubstituted macrocycle 1A, the antiparallel conformer is 0.73 kcal/mol more stable than the parallel conformer at the CAM‐B3LYP level of theory. For the C6–substituted macrocycle, this preference increases slightly to 0.96 kcal/mol. However, within the limitations of our computational model, these modest energy differences suggest that both geometries are accessible.

Two urea orientations considered for 1 (a) anti‐parallel and (b) parallel. Structures are shown with a generic substituent position labelled “R”. Calculated energy differences are ΔE = 0.73 kcal/mol (R = H) and ΔE = 0.96 kcal/mol (R = OC6H13).

Thus, we examined the interactions between the macrocycles. In the solid state, 1 assembles with the ureas in antiparallel orientation. We analyzed the binding energies of the parallel and antiparallel unsubstituted macrocycle dimers and trimers. Due to the large size of our structures (up to ∼250 atoms), this study was limited to the analysis of one, two, and three macrocycles. First, we computed the energies of the dimers and trimers at 4.685 Å separation, corresponding to the crystal structure, at B3LYP‐D3/6‐31+G** level with PCM. The positions of the heavy atoms are obtained as described above (Figure 4). For practical reasons, the positions of the hydrogen atoms were optimized using the semi‐empirical density function‐tight binding (DFTB) approach [45]. The energies were then recomputed using DFT within PCM (see Supporting Information for details). The binding energies within the dimer and trimer (the latter computed with respect to dimer + monomer fragments) show stabilization for both parallel and antiparallel stack of 33 and 35 kcal/mol for the parallel dimer and trimer systems and 46 and 49 kcal/mol for the antiparallel systems (Figure 5 and Table S2). Furthermore, we computed a potential energy curve as a function of the macrocycle distance for the trimer models (Figure S44). Within ωB97x‐D/6‐31+G** method the minimum energy separation for the antiparallel urea orientation of 4.63 Å is close to the experimental value, while the minimum energy distance for the parallel orientation was found to be shorter, 4.435 Å, but still higher in energy than for the antiparallel stack, which lends support to our estimates for the greater stabilization upon assembly of the antiparallel macrocycles. The antiparallel trimer is lower in energy than the parallel trimer (by 17 kcal/mol), which is consistent with the experimental findings.

Isodesmic mechanism for the macrocycles with anti‐parallel arrangement. The monomer activation energy ΔE = 9.3 kcal/mol and the stabilizing energy upon stacking is Eb ∼ 47 kcal/mol. (The energy estimates are computed at the B3LYP‐D3/6‐31+G** level.).

The computed binding energies suggest that the energy stabilization to go from monomers to dimer is similar to that of dimer to trimer, as would be expected in an isodesmic mechanism. As an additional argument for the isodesmic mechanism, we note that according to our calculations, the experimental geometry of the antiparallel unit is not the lowest energy configuration, which is more planar and lower in energy by 9.3 kcal/mol. If these results extend to the macrocycles in solution, though the solvent effects may lower the barrier, then there is an energy barrier for the antiparallel macrocycle to adopt the ‘stacking’ geometry (Figure 5), which would explain the same ‘activation’ energy, and thus the same association rate constant for the addition of each additional unit to the stack. antiparallel urea systems the dimer(trimer) binding energy values are 33(35) kcal/mol and 45(48) kcal/mole respectively.

Effect of End‐Capping Additives on Assembly of 1

Precise control of the size and dispersity of supramolecular assemblies remains a persistent challenge [11]. One strategy to modulate assembly size is chain‐stopper engineering [40, 46], also called end‐capping. It relies on monofunctional additives that can compete with the monomer assembly. To modulate the supramolecular polymerization, we examined additives that compete with the urea‐urea hydrogen bonds that drive assembly. Because the carbonyl oxygen of urea has a β value of 8.3, we selected a functional group with a higher β value to interact more effectively with the urea NH donors [47]. The P = O group of a phosphate, β value of 8.9, is expected to form stronger hydrogen bonds with the urea NHs than the urea carbonyl, thereby disrupting the self‐association of 1.

Trialkyl phosphate additives A1 and A2, commercial flame retardants, were chosen as capping additives due to their favorable β values, good solubility, and lack of absorbance in the 300–400 nm range. Temperature‐dependent UV‐Vis cooling experiments conducted at varying molar ratios of A1 and A2 show absorbance spectra nearly identical to those of 1 alone (Figures S28a and S29A), indicating that the overall aggregation pathway of 1 is preserved. The addition of 50% alkyl phosphate decreases DPN from 4.16 for 1 to 3.94 and 3.08 for A1 and A2 (Table S6), respectively, consistent with disruption of intermolecular interactions within the assembly.

Given the propensity of crystalline assembly 1 (R = H) to uptake guests [27], we designed a template consisting of two phosphate groups linked to a linear quarterphenyl (A3, Figure 6a). Molecular mechanics (MMFF) modeling in the presence of stacks of 1–4 macrocycles (Figure S33) suggests that A3 could associate with the assembled structure. One possible binding mode is shown in Figure 6b, in which the linear quarterphenyl unit aligns with the columnar axis while the phosphate groups are positioned near the urea functionalities. However, we emphasize that this representation is a plausible model and not a confirmed structure, and that alternative modes of interactions, including external association or coassembly, are also possible. A3 prepared in one step from 4,4‐dibromophenyl [48], displays an absorption maximum at 310 nm and does not exhibit aggregation under the experimental conditions (10 µM in 45% H2O/THF), as indicated by minimal spectral changes upon cooling (Figure S31).

Temperature‐dependent UV‐Vis cooling experiments were carried out at varying molar ratios of A3 (Figures S34–S36) and showed the appearance of a new band at 345 nm, similar to that observed with 1 alone. This suggests that 1 undergoes similar assembly processes in the presence of A3. Figure 6c plots the degree of polymerization as a function of varying ratios of A3. Analysis of the temperature‐dependent data using an isodesmic model (Table 2) shows that increasing the concentration of A3 leads to a decrease in the ΔH from ‐133 kJ/mol to ‐89 kJ/mol. The DPN also decreased from 4.16 to 3.01 at 50% A3. These changes are consistent with A3 interacting with the assembly and perturbing intermolecular interactions, resulting in shorter aggregates. However, the overall free energy of assembly (∆G) remained relatively unchanged, indicating that assembly remains favorable under these conditions. Drop casting of a solution of 1 (10 µM in 45% H2O/THF) in the presence of 50% A3 yielded aggregates like those formed in the absence of A3 (Figure S32), further supporting that A3 does not disrupt the overall morphology but instead modulates the assembly.

Emission studies of 1 + 50% A3 further support this behavior (Figure S37). The fluorescence intensity decreases, and an isoemissive point at 380 nm appears. The plot of degree of aggregation was fitted to the isodesmic model, yielding DPN = 2.95 and a decreased enthalpy, consistent with the trend observed in UV‐Vis.

To probe the presence of A3 within the assembled material, we prepared a 2:1 host: guest solution of 1 (5 mM in THF) with A3 at 338 K. The solution was cooled to 298 K and kept overnight. A white precipitate formed (Figure S42), which was collected by centrifugation (4000 rpm), the supernatant decanted, and the precipitate washed (3×5 mL THF). The final solid was dried under vacuum and heated to dissolve in THF‐d8. 1H NMR analysis of the redissolved material shows resonances corresponding to both 1 and A3 in an approximate 2:1 ratio, indicating that A3 is associated with the aggregated material (Figure S41). While these data confirm the presence of A3 within the assembly, they do not distinguish between specific binding modes. Multiple structural scenarios, including guest inclusion, external association, or coassembly, are therefore possible.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate phenylethynylene bis‐urea macrocycle 1 undergoes supramolecular polymerization via an isodesmic mechanism in H2O/THF mixtures, in contrast to the cooperative assembly behavior typically observed for related bis‐urea macrocycles. Temperature‐dependent UV–Vis and fluorescence spectroscopy in 45% H2O/THF are well described by an isodesmic model with no indication of the sigmoidal transitions typically associated with nucleation—elongation processes. Despite the use of a polar, hydrogen‐bond competitive solvent, the system exhibits significant enthalpic stabilization (ΔH = ‐133 kJ mol−1), a moderate association constant on the order of 106 M−1 and a short degree of polymerization of ∼ 4 at low concentration (10 µM), highlighting the robustness of the multivalent noncovalent interactions in this system. Spin coating, drop casting or slow evaporation afforded sizeable aggregates. We propose that the initial small aggregates identified by spectroscopy represent fundamental building blocks that aggregate into larger, higher‐ordered structures on the 100 nm length scale detected by DLS, and that these structures further grow during drying to prepare samples for microscopy.

Computational analysis provides a molecular‐level rationale for this behavior, indicating that stabilization energies associated with dimer and trimer formation are comparable. In addition, the small energy differences between accessible monomer conformations and the need for structural reorganization prior to stacking suggest that no strongly favored nucleation event dominates the assembly pathway. These findings are consistent with a stepwise assembly process characterized by approximately constant association energies. Complementary spectroscopic and morphological studies (FTIR, DLS, AFM, and MALDI‐TOF) support the formation of hydrogen‐bonded aggregates and fibrous supramolecular structures. These studies also indicate a distribution of aggregate sizes consistent with isodesmic polymerization.

The introduction of phosphate‐based additives (A1‐A3), designed to compete with urea‐urea hydrogen bonding, demonstrates that the assembly can be modulated without fundamentally altering the aggregation pathway. Increasing concentrations of A3 led to reductions in both the enthalpic contributions to assembly and the degree of polymerization, consistent with disruption of intermolecular interactions within the aggregates. While the experimental evidence confirms that A3 associates with the assembled material, the precise mode of interaction remains unresolved, and multiple scenarios, including guest inclusion, external association or coassembly, are possible.

Overall, this work identifies macrocycle 1 as a rare example of an isodesmic, hydrogen‐bonded supramolecular polymer operating in a polar solvent environment. It highlights how subtle structural features, such as macrocycle size, exterior substituents and accessible conformations, can influence assembly mechanisms. These insights provide a foundation for the rational design of supramolecular polymers with tunable size dynamics and potentially function in competitive solvent systems.
