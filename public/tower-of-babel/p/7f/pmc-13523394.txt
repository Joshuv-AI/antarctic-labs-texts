Cardiac Tamponade Secondary to Anterior Mediastinal Mass
Authors: Batol Barodi, Wafaa Mansour, Johnathan Stephan, Christopher Bradley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

History of Presentation

A 45-year-old man with a history limited to tobacco use presented with progressive dyspnea, orthopnea, and persistent cough over a 3-week period. He had initially been evaluated by his primary care physician, where a chest radiograph revealed mediastinal widening and a small right-sided pleural effusion. A subsequent computed tomography (CT) of the chest demonstrated a 16.5-cm heterogeneous anterior mediastinal mass with significant mass effect, narrowing of the superior vena cava, and encasement of the thoracic vasculature without frank obstruction.1 Multiple small pulmonary nodules suggestive of metastatic disease and a moderate right pleural effusion with compressive atelectasis were also identified. He was referred to pulmonology and underwent bronchoscopy with fine-needle aspiration biopsy, which yielded a nondiagnostic result.

After the biopsy, the patient reported worsening dyspnea, cough, orthopnea, pleuritic chest pain, and B symptoms.

On presentation to the emergency department, physical examination was notable for tachycardia and jugular venous distension. CT angiography of the chest revealed an enlarging pericardial effusion and redemonstration of known anterior mediastinal mass and pulmonary nodules.

Given the imaging findings and clinical presentation, the patient was admitted to the cardiovascular intensive care unit for close monitoring and further management.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included anterior mediastinal malignancy, cardiac tamponade, infectious pericarditis (viral, bacterial, or tuberculous), autoimmune pericarditis, and uremic pericardial effusion.

Investigations

CT demonstrated a large anterior mediastinal mass encasing the thoracic vasculature, accompanied by a left pleural effusion and significant pericardial effusion.

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed and revealed dilated inferior vena cava, right atrial collapse exceeding one-third of the cardiac cycle, and right ventricular diastolic collapse, consistent with hemodynamically significant pericardial effusion (Figure 1).2,3,4 The echocardiographic findings and clinical presentation established the diagnosis of cardiac tamponade requiring urgent drainage.

Management

A multidisciplinary discussion involving cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery was convened to determine the optimal drainage strategy. Several critical factors influenced the decision-making process. First, the extensive anterior mediastinal mass created substantial risk for airway collapse during anesthetic induction, as positive-pressure ventilation can precipitate acute hemodynamic collapse in patients with mediastinal compression.5,6 Second, the anatomic distribution of the pericardial effusion and anterior mass compression made traditional subxiphoid pericardiocentesis technically challenging and potentially hazardous.

Awake percutaneous pericardiocentesis was chosen over a surgical pericardial window after multidisciplinary discussion with oncology. Although a pericardial window was initially considered given the suspected malignant etiology of the effusion, oncology consultation suggested that the most likely diagnosis was a large B-cell lymphoma, which typically responds well to chemotherapy, thereby reducing the risk of recurrent pericardial effusion and diminishing the need for a more durable surgical drainage procedure.5 This consideration, combined with the high risk of anesthetic complications in patients with anterior mediastinal masses, and the desire to preserve future surgical options for definitive tumor resection, favored awake percutaneous drainage over procedures requiring general anesthesia.

Based on these considerations, an apical approach to pericardiocentesis was selected as the safest route for decompression.2 This approach offered several advantages: it avoided the compressed anterior structures, allowed echocardiographic guidance throughout the procedure, and could be performed without general anesthesia. The procedure was performed successfully under local anesthesia with continuous echocardiographic guidance, draining 650 mL of hemorrhagic pericardial fluid. Thoracentesis of the left pleural space yielded an additional 1 L of fluid.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After pericardiocentesis, hemodynamic parameters improved and echocardiographic signs of tamponade resolved. Repeat echocardiographic imaging after drain removal demonstrated no reaccumulation of pericardial effusion. However, the patient continued to experience orthopnea, suggesting ongoing mechanical compromise from the mediastinal mass itself. Pericardial fluid was sent for cytology, chemistry, and microbiology.

A definitive core biopsy of the mediastinal mass was subsequently performed, which confirmed a diagnosis of primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL). Positron emission tomography scan demonstrated a hypermetabolic anterior mediastinal mass, hypermetabolic pulmonary nodules consistent with lymphomatous involvement, and additional hypermetabolic nodal disease at the gastroesophageal junction and celiac axis.

Given the diagnosis of PMBCL with bulky mediastinal disease, pulmonary nodular involvement, and extra thoracic nodal disease, the patient was admitted for initiation of induction chemotherapy. He had received rituximab at the outpatient infusion center and subsequently proceeded with the dose-adjusted etoposide, prednisone, vincristine, cyclophosphamide, and doxorubicin (DA-EPOCH-R) regimen. Follow-up chest radiography after initiation of chemotherapy showed no sizable pleural effusion or acute cardiopulmonary abnormality, with stable superior mediastinal widening and enlargement of the cardiac silhouette. The resolution of the previously noted bilateral pleural effusions after chemotherapy further supported the malignant etiology of the initial pericardial and pleural disease and the chemosensitivity of the tumor, consistent with the anticipated response of PMBCL to systemic therapy.

Discussion

The decision to pursue percutaneous pericardiocentesis rather than surgical pericardial window or sternotomy was guided by multiple considerations. Pericardiocentesis is the recommended first-line therapeutic intervention for cardiac tamponade, particularly in urgent settings.2,7,8 The 2025 American College of Cardiology expert consensus statement on pericarditis emphasizes that the anatomical approach should be determined by where pericardial fluid is most accessible with the least likelihood of damaging intervening vital structures.2

Awake percutaneous drainage avoided the need for a surgical pericardial window, minimized surgical manipulation near the tumor and preserving future options for definitive resection, a critical consideration in patients with potentially resectable mediastinal malignancies.

Additionally, the high risk of anesthetic complications in patients with anterior mediastinal masses—including airway collapse and cardiovascular compromise—made awake percutaneous drainage preferable over procedures requiring general anesthesia.5,6,8

Surgical pericardial window creation is typically reserved for recurrent large pericardial effusions or cardiac tamponade despite prior pericardiocentesis, or when malignant effusions are expected to recur.2,9 In this case, initial percutaneous drainage allowed for hemodynamic stabilization while tissue diagnosis and definitive treatment planning proceeded.

The presence of an anterior mediastinal mass substantially complicates procedural planning. Compression of the distal trachea, bronchi, and great vessels creates significant risk during anesthetic induction, with reported complication rates up to 20% in some series.6 Symptoms including orthopnea, stridor, and superior vena cava syndrome, along with imaging findings of tracheal compression exceeding 50%, predict higher perioperative risk.6,8 In such patients, maintenance of spontaneous ventilation is preferred when general anesthesia is required, with cardiopulmonary bypass reserved as a last resort.5,6

The choice of pericardiocentesis approach—subxiphoid, apical, or parasternal—should be individualized based on effusion location and accessibility.2 In emergency situations, subxiphoid or apical approaches are preferred, but the presence of anterior mediastinal compression may favor alternative routes.2 Echocardiography, fluoroscopy, or computed tomography can guide the procedure, with imaging-guided pericardiocentesis representing the standard of care.2,9,10 Apical approach was performed to avoid the anterior mediastinal mass, and demonstrates the importance for providers to familiarize themselves with multiple approaches to pericardiocentesis.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates the critical importance of expertise in managing cardiac tamponade associated with anterior mediastinal masses. Comprehensive echocardiographic assessment enabled accurate diagnosis of tamponade physiology and guided procedural planning. The selection of apical pericardiocentesis over surgical approaches reflected careful consideration of both immediate hemodynamic priorities and long-term surgical planning, while avoiding the substantial risks of general anesthesia in a patient with mediastinal compression. Successful outcomes in such complex cases require multidisciplinary collaboration, with cardiology playing a central role in diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapeutic intervention. This cardiology-centered approach achieved hemodynamic stabilization while preserving future treatment options, underscoring the value of image-guided percutaneous techniques in managing high-risk pericardial disease.
