Mechanism of action of Astragalus membranaceus for treating diabetic foot ulcers based on single-cell RNA sequencing data and network pharmacology
Authors: Xia Li, Yan Dong, Chong Huang, Guozhong Zhou, Yanjie Ning, Yuru Liu, Ruqin Zhang, Ying Yang, Nan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic foot ulcer is a severe complication of diabetes, characterized by impaired wound healing and immune dysregulation. Although Astragalus membranaceus (AM) has been widely used and reported to exert beneficial effects in diabetic complications, its underlying mechanisms of action in DFU remain incompletely understood. This study integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from a public bioinformatic cohort (GSE245703; 4 non-diabetic foot ulcer (NFU) and 5 diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) samples) with network pharmacology to explore potential molecular mechanisms by which AM may be involved in DFU pathology. scRNA-seq analysis identified ten major cell types within the DFU microenvironment and revealed significant macrophage heterogeneity. Network pharmacology identified 14 active compounds in AM and their predicted targets, some of which overlapped with macrophage-associated differentially expressed genes. Molecular docking suggested strong binding affinities between selected AM compounds and macrophage-associated hub genes. qPCR validation in a clinical cohort (6 NFU and 9 DFU patients) confirmed differential expression of several candidate hub genes overlapping with predicted AM targets. Collectively, these results provide a single-cell–resolved, systems-level framework that links AM components to macrophage-associated molecular processes in DFU, offering a hypothesis-generating basis for future functional and translational studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41921-5.

Introduction

Diabetic Foot Ulcers (DFUs) are among the most severe complications of diabetes, closely associated with neuropathy, microvascular damage, and immune dysfunction caused by chronic hyperglycemia1. As the global prevalence of diabetes continues to rise, the incidence of DFUs is also increasing2. Among diabetic patients with a history of foot injury or infection, the incidence of DFUs can reach as high as 34%. Projections indicate that by 2045, 693 million people worldwide will be affected by DFU3. In the United States, the five-year survival rate for DFU patients between 2017 and 2020 was only 30.5%, comparable to the five-year survival rate for certain cancers (approximately 31%)4. Despite standard interventions such as glycemic control, debridement, anti-infection therapy, and off-loading, clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory5,6.

Mounting evidence indicates that immune microenvironment imbalance is a key driver of impaired wound healing in DFU7,8. Macrophages, as central regulators of inflammation and tissue repair, are particularly critical. Dysregulated polarization between pro-inflammatory (M1-like) and reparative (M2-like) states contributes to ulcer chronicity9–11. However, most existing studies are limited to bulk-level analyses, and systematic single-cell-level characterization of immune cell composition, macrophage subpopulation dynamics, and intercellular communication in DFU remains insufficient.

The development of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and associated analytical techniques has transformed our ability to dissect immune cell heterogeneity within the microenvironment, providing novel insights into underlying molecular processes12. At the same time, network pharmacology—an interdisciplinary framework combining bioinformatics, pharmacology, and systems biology—facilitates a systematic investigation of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) by linking natural compounds to disease-related targets. Astragalus membranaceus (AM), a traditional Chinese medicine, has demonstrated immunoregulatory, anti-inflammatory, and tissue-repair–promoting effects, showing potential in diabetes and its complications13,14. While pharmacological studies suggest that it modulates cytokine expression and enhances immune function, its specific role in reshaping the DFU immune microenvironment—particularly with respect to macrophage subsets and signaling pathways—has not been comprehensively elucidated.

In this study, we performed scRNA-seq to characterize macrophage heterogeneity in DFU and their dynamic interactions within the immune microenvironment. To explore potential molecular mechanisms of AM, we integrated network pharmacology, molecular docking analyses, and qRT-PCR validation of selected targets. Materials and methods

RNA sequencing data download and analysis

The bioinformatic cohort consisted of foot skin tissue samples from 4 non-diabetic foot ulcer (NFU) patients and 5 DFU patients, obtained from the publicly available scRNA-seq dataset GSE245703 in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Raw data were processed using the Seurat package for quality control, normalization, and standardization. The subsequent filtration of cells was based on the following criteria: mitochondrial gene expression proportion > 25%, detected gene count < 200, and total transcript count < 500. Subsequent to the selection of highly variable genes, principal component analysis (PCA) was executed. The reduction of dimensionality was facilitated through the implementation of t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), with the basis of the top 20 principal components15. The cells were then clustered using the Shared Nearest Neighbor (SNN) clustering method, with a resolution of 0.5. Subpopulations were annotated using the Single R tool and known cell type-specific marker genes. For the macrophage subpopulations that were preliminarily identified, secondary clustering was performed with identical resolution parameters. This was followed by recursive analysis with Single R to explore their heterogeneity and potential functional differences.

Pseudotime trajectory and cell-cell interactions analysis

To decipher the state transitions of macrophages during ulceration, Monocle2 was employed to construct single-cell developmental trajectories. The Discriminative Dimensionality Reduction Tree (DDRTree) method was implemented for the purposes of dimensionality reduction and the inference of cellular state evolution pathways. The trajectory starting points were defined based on the expression of early macrophage marker genes. Concurrently, the CellChat package was utilized to analyze intercellular communication networks using ligand-receptor databases16.

Identification of active compounds and hub genes via integrated analysis

The chemical constituents of AM were retrieved from the Traditional Chinese Medicine System Pharmacology Database17(TCMSP, https://tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php) and screened based on an oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug-like properties (DL) ≥ 0.1818–20. These candidates were further evaluated using the SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch) platform to determine the final set of active compounds21. Potential targets were collected from SwissTargetPrediction (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/), CTD (https://ctdbase.org/), and PharmMapper (http://lilab.ecust.edu.cn/pharmmapper/). Targets obtained from PharmMapper were standardized using UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/), after which all targets were merged and duplicates were removed.

For single-cell data analysis, we used the FindMarkers function in the Seurat R package (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in macrophages between the DFU and NFU groups, applying thresholds of absolute ∣ log₂ fold change ∣ > 1 and p-value < 0.05. The obtained DEGs were then intersected with the aforementioned targets to identify potential therapeutic targets. The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database (species: Homo sapiens; minimum interaction score ≥ 0.7; unconnected nodes hidden). To identify hub genes within this network, we employed the CytoHubba plugin. Three topological algorithms—Minimum Node Cut, Degree Centrality, and Betweenness Centrality—were applied. The top 10 genes ranked by each algorithm were selected, and their intersection was defined as the final set of hub genes.

Enrichment analysis and network construction

We utilized the clusterProfiler R package to perform Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses based on DFU-related targets and common targets. GO analysis describes the functions of gene products from three aspects: biological process, cellular component, and molecular function, while KEGG integrates genomic, chemical, and systemic functional information for pathway annotation22. The enrichment results were visualized using bubble plots generated by the ggplot2 package, which displayed the gene counts and significance levels for each pathway. A multidimensional interaction network linking “AM -Active Components–Target – Pathways” was constructed via Cytoscape.

Molecular docking simulation

To validate the potential interactions, molecular docking was conducted for the AM active compounds with the three-dimensional (3D) structures of the proteins corresponding to the hub genes. The 3D structures of AM active compounds (mol2 format) were downloaded from the TCMSP database based on their Molecule IDs, energy-minimized using Chem3D, and converted to pdbqt format using AutoDockTools as ligands. Crystal structures of the target proteins (pdb format) were obtained from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank ((RCSB PDB) database (https://www.rcsb.org/) and prepared by removing solvents and endogenous ligands in PyMOL, followed by hydrogen addition and Gasteiger charge assignment in AutoDockTools to generate pdbqt receptor files. Docking box parameters were configured to cover the target’s active pocket, a GPF file was generated, and molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina23. Compound–target pairs with predicted binding energies ≤ − 9.0 kcal·mol⁻¹ were selected for representative visualization using PyMOL.

Human tissue collection

For experimental validation, a separate clinical cohort of 15 patients undergoing foot surgery at Anning First People’s Hospital was enrolled, including 6 DFU and 9 NFU patients. Skin tissue samples were collected from the wound edge during debridement. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anning First People’s Hospital (Approval No: Lun Shen 2025-026 (Other)-01), and all participants provided informed consent. Detailed clinical information of the enrolled patients is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

qRT-PCR

Skin tissue samples were ground in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using the isopropanol-chloroform method. cDNA synthesis was performed using a kit (Cat. No. 11141ES60, Yesen, China). cDNA amplification and analysis were conducted using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Cat. No. 11184ES08, Yesen, China). The total volume of the qPCR reaction was 20 µl and was performed using a Tianlong PCR instrument. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for two minutes, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for one second and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was then performed by gradually increasing the temperature from 60 °C to 95 °C to ensure the specificity of the PCR product. Raw cycle threshold (Ct) values were normalized to GAPDH using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

qRT-PCR data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data distribution was assessed using normality tests. Depending on the data distribution, group comparisons were performed using either unpaired t-tests or the Mann–Whitney U test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. qRT-PCR results are shown as mean ± standard deviation. Each in vitro experiment was independently repeated at least three times.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic landscape

In this study, we analyzed a publicly available single-cell dataset (GSE245703). Subsequent to the implementation of rigorous quality control measures aimed at the elimination of low-quality data, the transcriptomes of 4,943 cells were obtained (DFU:2,298; NFU:2,645). Unsupervised clustering and UMAP visualization grouped these 4,943 cells into 15 distinct clusters (Figure. 2A). In accordance with the findings of preceding studies, the present study employed the expression of known cell-specific marker genes to annotate and validate each cluster24–26. The identified cell types included macrophages (CD163 and CD68), basal keratinocytes (KRT5 and SFN), T/NK cells (CD3D and CCL5), smooth muscle cells (ACTA2 and TAGLN), fibroblasts (DCN and MMP2), B lymphocytes (MS4A1 and CD79B), endothelial cells (PECAM1, VWF, and CD34), plasma cells (MZB1 and CD38), and neutrophils (CSF3R, FCGR3B, and CXCR2), mast cells (CPA3 and TPSAB1) (Figure. 2B). The expression patterns of these marker genes are visualized in Figure. 2D and F, while a heatmap of the top differentially expressed genes across clusters is shown in Figure. 2E. Stratification of samples into DFU and NFU groups revealed consistent UMAP clustering patterns between cohorts (Figure. 2C). Quantitative analysis showed that macrophages showed a slightly lower abundance in DFU tissues compared with NFU tissues (Figure. 2G).

Diabetes alters the distribution of macrophage subpopulations

Initial analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity among macrophages in foot tissues. Following dimensionality reduction, 972 macrophages were partitioned into seven transcriptionally distinct subclusters (Figure. 3A). Subtype annotation (Figure. 3B) identified six functional groups (MAC1–MAC6) and an additional undefined population lacking specific marker genes (Un). Subtype-specific markers were validated across all cohorts (Figure. 3D, G). Macrophage subpopulations displayed distinct relative abundances between DFU and NFU tissues, with MAC2–4 enriched in DFU, whereas MAC1, MAC5, and MAC6 were more prevalent in NFU (Figure. 3C). Functional enrichment analysis suggested that different macrophage subpopulations were associated with distinct immune- and metabolism-related transcriptional programs (Figure. 3E). Specifically, MAC1 in NOD-like receptor signaling, MAC2 in Phagosome, Lysosome, and Oxidative phosphorylation, MAC3 in Toll-like receptor, NF-κB, IL-17, and Chemokine signaling, MAC4 in Oxidative phosphorylation, and MAC5/6 in Antigen processing and presentation and Phagosome, with MAC6 additionally enriched in Lipid and atherosclerosis. Pseudotime trajectory analysis positioned MAC1 at the initiation point of differentiation (Figure. 3F).

Remodeling of the communication network between macrophages and other cell types

Macrophages in DFU exhibit substantial functional remodeling. To investigate the regulatory role of macrophages on other cells within foot skin tissue during DFU development, we performed cell-to-cell communication analysis based on scRNA-seq data by tracking ligand-receptor interactions. Compared with NFU tissues, DFU tissues displayed a higher frequency but lower overall intensity of macrophage–cell interactions (Figure. 4A, B). Several pathways, including ANXA1, EGF, SELE, VCAM, APRIL, IGF, EPGN, WNT, and HGF, showed distinct activation in NFU tissues, whereas SAA, IL16, CLEC, CSF3, CHEMRIN, and CD22 were largely restricted to DFU tissues. Shared pathways such as CSF, AGRN, ESAM, JAM, MHC-II, CD99, and GAS were more active in DFU tissues (Figure.4C). Ligand–receptor analysis appeared reduced in DFU tissues, particularly VEGF and EGF, a finding further supported by VEGF signaling networks, which illustrated robust macrophage–endothelial interactions in NFU tissues but substantially weakened connections in DFU (Figure. 4D–F).

Network pharmacology prediction of active components and potential targets of AM in DFU

Based on predefined pharmacokinetic and screening criteria, a total of 20 candidate compounds were retrieved from the TCMSP database. Among them, 14 bioactive components of AM were further identified using the SwissADME platform (Supplementary Table 2). Target prediction and normalization yielded 4,903 putative protein targets. Transcriptomic analysis of the GSE245703 dataset identified 1,716 DEGs in DFU tissues (Figure. 5A), and intersection analysis revealed 537 overlapping targets between targets and macrophage DEGs (Figure. 5B).

Functional enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package demonstrated that these overlapping targets were significantly enriched in biological processes related to inflammatory and immune responses, including positive regulation of cytokine production and response to bacterial molecules (Figure. 5C). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the overlapping targets were predominantly enriched in infection-, inflammation-, metabolism-, and cell death-related pathways, including Shigellosis, Influenza A, Epstein–Barr virus infection, Herpes simplex virus 1 infection, Hepatitis B, Lipid and atherosclerosis and Apoptosis (Figure. 5D, Supplementary Table 3). Chord diagram analysis (Figure. 5E) revealed that most genes were involved in multiple pathways, suggesting potential functional associations among these biological processes.

To further identify highly connected genes within these pathways, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed from the overlapping targets using the STRING database, consisting of 533 nodes and 1,071 edges (Supplementary Figure. 9). Hub gene screening via the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape identified eight hub genes—MMP9, HSP90AA1, STAT1, NFKB1, EP300, TP53, SRC, and BCL2 (Figure. 6 A, B).

An “AM–Active Components–Target–Pathway” network was subsequently established in Cytoscape (Figure. 6C), illustrating the predicted relationships among compounds, targets, and enriched pathways. The network showed that each active component was connected to multiple pathways, reflecting the multi-target nature of AM.

Molecular docking analysis

Building upon prior findings, fourteen prioritized compounds—Jaranol (MOL000239), Isorhamnetin (MOL000354), 3,9-di-O-methylnissolin (MOL000371), 7-O-methylisomucronulatol (MOL000378), 9,10-dimethoxypterocarpan-3-O-β-D-glucoside (MOL000379), (6aR,11aR)-9,10-dimethoxy-6a,11a-dihydro-6 H-benzofurano[3,2-c] chromen-3-ol (MOL000380), Bifendate (MOL000387), Formononetin (MOL000392), Isoflavanone (MOL000398), Calycosin (MOL000417), Kaempferol (MOL000422), (3R)-3-(2-hydroxy-3,4-dimethoxyphenyl) chroman-7-ol (MOL000438), 1,7-Dihydroxy-3,9-dimethoxy pterocarpene (MOL000442), and Quercetin (MOL000098)—were analyzed against predicted hub targets: MMP9, HSP90AA1, STAT1, NFKB1, EP300, TP53, SRC, and BCL2. The overall binding affinities of these compounds with their targets are summarized in a heatmap (Figure. 7A). Binding affinities were interpreted using established thresholds: binding energies < − 4.25 kcal·mol⁻¹ indicated potential binding activity, < − 5.0 kcal·mol⁻¹ indicated favorable binding, and < − 7.0 kcal·mol⁻¹ indicated strong binding. Computational docking identified several compound–target pairs with strong predicted binding affinities: TP53-9,10-dimethoxypterocarpan-3-O-β-D-glucoside (− 9.10 kcal·mol⁻¹), STAT1-9,10-dimethoxypterocarpan-3-O-β-D-glucoside (− 9.40 kcal·mol⁻¹), STAT1-quercetin (− 9.40 kcal·mol⁻¹). The detailed docking results are illustrated in Figure. 7B-D.

Expression patterns of selected drug target genes in DFU tissues

Foot skin tissue samples were collected from 9 patients with DFU and 6 patients with NFU to examine the expression levels of selected genes in DFU and NFU tissues.

Guided by literature and experimental feasibility, five genes—MMP9, TP53, BCL2, SRC, and STAT1— were selected for qRT-PCR analysis. qRT-PCR results showed that MMP9 and TP53 were significantly upregulated in DFU tissues compared with NFU controls (P < 0.05), whereas SRC and STAT1 were significantly downregulated (P < 0.05). BCL2 expression did not differ significantly between the groups (ns) (Figure. 8). These findings are consistent with the single-cell transcriptomic analysis.

Discussion

DFU represents a devastating and recalcitrant complication of diabetes, marked by impaired wound healing, persistent inflammation, and heightened susceptibility to infection27–29. Although traditional molecular biology studies have provided partial insights into DFU pathogenesis, the complexity of immune dysregulation within the wound microenvironment remains incompletely understood. In the present study, we integrated scRNA-seq with network pharmacology, molecular docking, and qRT-PCR validation to investigate DFU pathogenesis and to explore the potential therapeutic targets and mechanisms of AM within a predictive and multi-level analytical framework.

In recent years, scRNA-seq has become an essential approach for dissecting immune cell heterogeneity and regulatory interactions within complex tissues24,30,31. Consistent with this paradigm, our scRNA-seq analysis revealed a heterogeneous cellular landscape in foot ulcer tissues, comprising multiple immune and stromal populations. Among these, macrophages are well recognized as central regulators of inflammation resolution, host defense, and tissue repair, and their dysfunction has been closely linked to delayed wound healing and heightened infection risk in DFU31–33. Accordingly, subsequent analyzes primarily focused on macrophages to characterize their subpopulation heterogeneity and to explore candidate targets for therapeutic intervention in DFU.

Collectively, these observations point to pronounced disease-associated remodeling of macrophage functional states in foot ulcer tissues. While NFU showed higher relative proportions of macrophage subpopulations associated with regulatory and tissue-reparative transcriptional programs (MAC1, MAC5, and MAC6), DFU tissues were instead characterized by a relative predominance of macrophage subpopulations displaying pro-inflammatory and metabolically active transcriptional programs (MAC2, MAC3, and MAC4). Functional enrichment analysis further indicated that DFU-enriched macrophage subsets were enriched in inflammatory and metabolic pathways central to innate immune activation, including Toll-like receptor, NF-κB, and IL-17 signaling, suggesting coordinated activation of inflammatory signaling and metabolic reprogramming that may contribute to sustained inflammation and impaired tissue repair.34. In contrast, macrophage subsets more prevalent in NFU tissues showed enrichment in antigen processing and presentation and phagosome-related pathways, consistent with immune regulatory and reparative functions.

Trajectory analysis further positioned MAC1 at an early pseudotime state, a position consistent with a homeostatic or precursor-like state rather than a terminally differentiated phenotype. Beyond compositional changes, cell–cell communication analysis revealed that macrophages in DFU tissues remained broadly active in intercellular signaling; however, macrophage-derived reparative pathways were selectively and markedly attenuated. This deficit was particularly evident in the VEGF and EGF signaling axes, which are central to angiogenesis and re-epithelialization, but also extended to other key mediators of tissue repair, including HGF—implicated in endothelial and epithelial cell migration—and WNT signaling, which is essential for stem cell activation and tissue regeneration35–37.

This apparent selective reduction in pro-repair communication, despite preserved overall signaling activity, may reflect a maladaptive functional state of DFU macrophages rather than a global signaling shutdown. Such an imbalance provides a biological context for the observed macrophage state remodeling and is consistent with previous evidence linking impaired angiogenic and regenerative signaling to delayed wound healing and vascular dysfunction in diabetic wounds38,39.

To explore potential therapeutic targets, we performed a network pharmacology analysis in which predicted targets of 14 active components of AM were intersected with 1,716 DEGs identified in DFU macrophages, yielding 537 overlapping genes. KEGG enrichment indicated that these genes were involved in pathways related to inflammation, metabolism, and apoptosis. Some pathways, including viral infection-related or lipid and atherosclerosis pathways, are broadly associated with immune and inflammatory processes and may reflect shared signaling networks rather than DFU-specific pathological mechanisms. Among these, apoptosis represents a directly relevant pathological process, suggesting that AM may modulate inflammatory and apoptotic responses in DFU. PPI and hub gene analyses prioritized eight candidate regulators (MMP9, HSP90AA1, STAT1, NFKB1, EP300, TP53, SRC, and BCL2) as hub genes within the network.

Molecular docking analyses suggested theoretical binding compatibility between core AM components (e.g., quercetin, 9,10-dimethoxy pterocarpan-3-O-ß-D-glucoside) and key targets (e.g., TP53, STAT1). Quercetin (QCT) is a flavonoid widely distributed in plants, exhibiting multiple pharmacological activities, including anti-tumor, anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-viral, and immunomodulatory effects40,41. Latif’s study reported that the use of a newly synthesized agent (QCT and its esterified complex with 4FPBA-Q) significantly accelerated wound healing in infected diabetic rats42. A study by Zhang et al. found that QCT accelerates DW healing by reducing the activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and regulating the PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway43. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that flavonoid compounds such as 9,10-Dimethoxypterocarpan-3-O-β-D-glucoside possess anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects44.

In our clinical validation cohort, MMP9 and TP53 were significantly upregulated, whereas SRC and STAT1 were downregulated in DFU tissues, consistent with bioinformatic predictions. BCL2 expression did not differ significantly between DFU and NFU tissues. Although BCL2 has been reported to participate in apoptosis regulation in other pathological contexts, its role in DFU could not be confirmed in the present study45,46. Previous studies have implicated STAT1 in regulating inflammatory transcriptional programs in macrophages under diabetic conditions, and alterations in STAT1-associated signaling have been reported to influence wound repair processes47–49. TP53 is a tumor suppressor that regulates the cell cycle and apoptosis via the p53-p21 axis under hyperglycemic conditions. However, abnormal activation of inflammatory pathways and p53-associated DNA damage responses may impede wound healing50–52. In DFUs, abnormally elevated MMP9 levels cause excessive matrix degradation, which prevents new tissue formation and prolongs inflammation53–56. SRC is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase that regulates multiple cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, and survival. Dysregulation of SRC activity in DFUs impairs these processes, leading to delayed healing and reduced tissue regeneration47. These results provide preliminary experimental support for predicted targets.

In summary, this integrated analysis suggests that predicted targets of AM are associated with key inflammatory, metabolic, and apoptotic pathways linked to maladaptive macrophage functional states in DFU. By combining single-cell transcriptomics with systems pharmacology, our study links macrophage heterogeneity and intercellular communication imbalance to potential therapeutic mechanisms of AM. However, the findings should be interpreted with caution given the exploratory and computational nature of the work. The validation cohort was relatively small (9 DFU and 6 NFU), and the computational predictions—while suggestive—require experimental validation through in vitro or in vivo models. Moreover, the cross-sectional design and lack of spatial resolution within the ulcer tissue limit our ability to infer causality or capture localized cellular dynamics. Nevertheless, this study provides a single-cell-resolved framework for understanding macrophage remodeling in DFU and offers a hypothesis-generating foundation for future mechanistic and translational studies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study delineates the single-cell transcriptomic landscape of DFU tissue, identifying macrophage-centered immune dysregulation as a key cellular feature associated with impaired healing. Integrated computational analysis (scRNA-seq, network pharmacology, and molecular docking) provides preliminary evidence that key components of AM, such as quercetin, are predicted to engage with multiple DFU-associated pathways. This integrative, hypothesis-generating approach identifies candidate targets and mechanisms for future experimental validation of AM’s putative efficacy in DFU treatment.
