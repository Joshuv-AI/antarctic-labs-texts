The Synthesis of Tertiary Alkylamines from Alkyl-Substituted Alkenes Using Amine Umpolung Strategy
Authors: Shumpei Nakatsuka, Takumi Kinoshita, Yota Sakakibara, Kei Murakami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radical-based aminomethyl functionalization of alkenes offers a powerful approach to synthesize tertiary alkylamineskey structural motifs in drug discoverydirectly from alkenes, which are ideal and abundant feedstocks. However, due to polarity effects in radical reactions, existing strategies have been largely restricted to activated alkenes, such as electron-deficient alkenes or aryl-substituted alkenes. Here, we report a unified method that overcomes this limitation by employing α-ammoniomethyl radicals, electrophilic umpolung equivalents of α-aminomethyl radicals, to enable regioselective difunctionalization of unactivated alkenes, including a broad class of aliphatic substrates. Under visible-light photoredox catalysis, iodomethylammonium salts generate α-ammoniomethyl radicals that add efficiently to these unactivated alkenes, followed by iodine-atom transfer in a radical-chain mechanism. A streamlined one-pot sequence couples this difunctionalization with the demethylation of the ammonium moiety, affording highly substituted tertiary alkylamines with broad functional-group tolerance. The platform extends to homologative allylamine synthesis via in-situ allylammonium intermediates, enabling rapid access to extended amine scaffolds and bioisosteric analogues of pharmaceuticals. Furthermore, our reaction system using α-ammoniomethyl radicals enables diverse aminomethyl functionalization with various SOMOphilessuch as heteroarylation, alkynylation, azidation, and fluorinationgreatly expanding the chemical space of tertiary amines. Collectively, this approach overcomes key limitations of prior radical difunctionalization methods and provides a modular, operationally simple route to complex amines from commodity alkenes, with broad implications for synthetic methodology and drug discovery.

Alkenes are produced on a large industrial scale, readily accessible, and highly versatile in downstream functionalization, making them ideal feedstocks in organic synthesis. − In contrast, tertiary alkylamines constitute a fundamental structural motif found in alkaloids and numerous biologically active molecules, and they play indispensable roles in medicinal chemistry and chemical industries. − Therefore, the development of synthetic methods that enable the direct and efficient construction of structurally complex tertiary alkylamines from simple and abundant alkenes would be highly advantageous (Figure A).

In this context, transition-metal-catalyzed hydroaminomethylation of alkenes has been extensively investigated as a representative alkene-based amine synthesis (Figure B). , In this reaction, hydroformylation of aliphatic alkenes or styrene derivatives in the presence of synthesis gas generates an aldehyde, which subsequently undergoes reductive amination with a secondary amine to furnish the desired amine product. More recently, Sc- and Ti-catalyzed hydroaminomethylation reactions that exploit the α-C–H bonds of tertiary alkylamines have enabled their direct coupling with alkenes. , Despite their broad alkene scope, including both aliphatic and aryl-substituted alkenes, these reactions are intrinsically limited to hydroaminomethylation and therefore cannot introduce diverse functional groups into alkenes together with the aminomethyl moiety. As a result, these strategies are not suited for the synthesis of highly functionalized tertiary alkylamines.

A complementary solution is to pursue alkene difunctionalization using α-aminomethyl radicals (Figure C). , In principle, this strategy can install two distinct substituents across a CC bond in a single operation, offering a direct route to linear tertiary alkylamines with programmable side chains. − In practice, two long-standing obstacles have reduced its generality. First, α-aminomethyl radicals are nucleophilic due to electron donation from the nitrogen lone pair; they therefore engage electron-deficient or aryl-substituted alkenes efficiently, but react sluggishly with electron-rich alkyl-substituted alkenes and typically only in intramolecular contexts. Second, after the addition of an α-aminomethyl radical to an aliphatic alkene, the resulting alkyl radical remains nucleophilic; productive trapping thus requires electrophilic SOMOphiles. Many of these electrophilic SOMOphiles also react with nucleophilic tertiary amines, leading to undesired background pathways and complicating difunctionalization of alkenes.

To address these challenges, we focused on α-ammoniomethyl radicals as electrophilic equivalents of α-aminomethyl radicals (Figure D). − Due to the positive charge of the ammonio group, α-ammoniomethyl radicals exhibit enhanced electrophilicity and reactivity, making them suitable for reactions with relatively electron-rich aliphatic alkenes. − Additionally, the ammonio group effectively masks the nucleophilicity of the nitrogen atom, facilitating the use of electrophilic SOMOphiles in the difunctionalization of alkenes. In our prior work, we generated α-ammoniomethyl radicals under photoredox catalysis, enabling a radical-polar crossover (RPC) pathway for divergent ammoniomethylation of alkenes. Furthermore, the Gevorgyan and Zhao groups have independently developed methods to generate these radicals via Pd catalysis under photoirradiation conditions, achieving homologative allylic functionalization. , However, in all reported cases, the substrate scope has been limited to reactive styrene derivatives. We hypothesized that this limitation arises from two factors: (i) efficient RPC required a benzylic radical intermediate after α-ammoniomethyl addition, and (ii) the use of weak C–H donors as additives promoted competing HAT with α-ammoniomethyl radicals, thus suppressing the addition to less-reactive alkyl-substituted alkenes.

Here, we disclose a reaction system that overcomes these limitations, enabling regioselective difunctionalization of aliphatic alkenes by α-ammoniomethyl radicals, followed by a one-pot demethylation to furnish highly substituted tertiary alkylamines. − This methodology allows coinstallation of diverse functional groups together with an aminomethyl unit on alkenes. Moreover, we leverage intrinsic features of the ammonio group to selectively construct allylammonium scaffolds from unactivated alkenes, which serve as versatile intermediates for downstream diversification. − Collectively, these elements provide a unified platform for accessing densely functionalized tertiary amines from commodity alkenes and simple amines.

We began our investigation by evaluating ammoniomethyl iodination of alkene 2a (Figure A). Treating iodomethylammonium salt 1a and 2a with photocatalyst PC1 and 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TMP) in CH3CN under blue LEDs afforded iodide 3a in 83% yield (Figure A, entry 1). Alternative photocatalysts PC2–PC4 were competent but less efficient (Figure A, entries 2–4), and replacing TMP with N-Me-TMP, i Pr2NH, or DABCO led to modestly reduced yields (Figure A, entries 5–7). Control experiments established that both PC1 and light were required for efficient conversion (Figure A, entries 8 and 9); a 19% yield of 3a was still observed in the absence of TMP, indicating that TMP is not strictly essential for radical generation but beneficial for optimal turnover (Figure A, entry 10). Reducing the alkene loading resulted in a moderate decrease in yield; however, the reaction still afforded the product in a synthetically useful yield of 61% with 1.0 equiv of 2a (Figure A, entry 11). These results indicate that the reaction can be performed with reduced alkene equivalents when the alkene substrate is valuable or synthetically demanding.

A plausible mechanism, guided by our prior findings and mechanistic studies, is shown in Figure B (the detailed mechanistic studies are discussed in the Supporting Information). Photoexcited PC1* undergoes reductive quenching by TMP to generate PC1 • – . Single-electron transfer (SET) from PC1 • – to 1a forms the α-ammoniomethyl radical. Because of its high electrophilicity, this radical adds rapidly to 2a to give a γ-ammonio radical. Subsequent iodine-atom transfer from another molecule of 1a furnishes 3a and regenerates the α-ammoniomethyl radical, which is consistent with a radical-chain mechanism.

To translate this iodination into practical amine synthesis, we developed a one-pot sequence (Figure C). Crude 3a from the photochemical step was treated directly with p-toluenethiol and Cs2CO3, effecting SN2 displacement of C–I and subsequent demethylation of the ammonio group to give tertiary amine 4a in 74% yield over two steps. This one-pot sequence avoids any intermediate workup and directly couples difunctionalization with deprotection, delivering tertiary alkylamines with minimal operations.

With the optimized conditions for the aminomethyl thioetherification in hand, we next explored the substrate scope of this transformation (Chart ). We began by examining the scope of alkenes. Alkenes bearing long alkyl chains, as well as those substituted directly at the double bond with sterically bulky groups such as cyclohexyl or tert-butyl, were well-tolerated, affording the corresponding products in 82%, 57%, and 57% yields, respectively (4b–4d). Notably, alkenes containing benzylic C–H bonds, which are weaker and potentially reactive under radical conditions, also transformed efficiently to give the desired products (4e and 4f) in moderate to high yields. Furthermore, alkenes bearing polar functional groups such as esters, protected amines, nitriles, and alcohols were compatible under the reaction conditions, affording the desired tertiary alkylamine products without any detectable degradation or side reactions involving these functional groups (4g–4j). When an alkene bearing two reactive sites was subjected to the reaction, difunctionalization occurred at both positions, furnishing the corresponding diamine product 4k in 43% yield. Notably, this reaction applied to structurally complex alkenes, such as α-d-galactopyranose derivative and theophylline derivatives, and the corresponding products were obtained in moderate yields (4l and 4m). We then evaluated the scope of amine components. Besides the dimethylamino group, other amine motifs, such as N-methyl-N-propyl and N-isopropyl-N-methyl groups, were successfully introduced, yielding products 4n and 4o in synthetically useful yields. Cyclic amines, including piperidine, morpholine, thiomorpholine, and piperazine, were also applicable to the transformation, providing the desired tertiary amines 4p–4s in 69%, 81%, 61%, and 88% yields, respectively. As for the nucleophile scope, a broad range of thiols was suitable, including various aryl thiols and alkyl thiols of differing substitution patterns (primary, secondary, and tertiary). All afforded the corresponding tertiary amine products in good yields (4t–4y). In addition to thiols, amine nucleophiles were also compatible with this transformation. Aniline derivatives furnished the desired products in good yields (4z). In contrast, with alkylamines, a competing E2 pathway arising from the basicity of the amine (vide infra) diminished the efficiency; nevertheless, the desired products were obtained, underscoring the versatility of this approach for constructing both C–S and C–N bonds from a common intermediate (4aa–4ad).

Our platform also supports homologative amination of aliphatic alkenes. Subjecting an alkene to optimized ammoniomethyl iodination followed, in the same pot, by KF effects a clean E2 to give allylammonium salt 5 with high E/Z selectivity (E/Z = 7:1) (Chart A). While elimination could, in principle, occur from either the β- or δ-C–H positions relative to the ammonio group, the δ-elimination product 6 was not observed. We ascribe the β-selectivity to (i) the strong inductive electron withdrawal of the ammonio substituent, which slightly acidifies β-C–H bonds, and (ii) ion-dipole interaction between the cationic ammonio group and fluoride, biasing deprotonation at the proximal β-site.

Because allylammonium salts are excellent substrates for Pd-catalyzed allylic substitution, the in-situ-generated allylammonium salt 7 can be carried forward without isolation to Pd-catalyzed allylic amination, delivering homologated allylamines in one pot from alkyl alkenes (Chart B). A range of secondary amines and alkyl-substituted alkenes furnished allylamines (8a–8f) efficiently. Additionally, substituting a thiol nucleophile enabled SN2 at the allylic position to give allyl thioethers (8g).

Additionally, we leveraged this homologative allylamine synthesis to access partially deuterated pharmaceuticals and bioisosteric analogues (Chart C). The iodomethylammonium radical precursors possess acidic C–H bonds at the carbon bearing both ammonio and iodo substituents, enabling selective deuteration to 1a-D 2 . Using 1a-D 2 in homologative amination with suitably substituted alkenes, followed by hydrogenation of the allylamine unit, furnished Aripiprazole-D2 (9). In addition, applying homologative amination to alkenes featuring the bicyclo[2.2.2]­octane (BCO) scaffolda three-dimensional bioisostere of benzeneprovided BCO analogues of benzene-containing drugs such as Naftifine (10) and Cinnarizine (11), highlighting the method’s versatility. Building on the pioneering studies that established homologative amination for activated aryl-substituted alkenes, we have now extended this concept to unactivated aliphatic alkenes, thereby broadening its applicability to a much wider range of amine synthesis.

We next examined various SOMOphiles to expand aminomethyl functionalization modes (Figure A). With modest tuning of reaction conditions for each partner, the α-ammoniomethyl radical participated in Minisci-type aminomethyl heteroarylation with phenanthridine (12), Giese-type aminomethyl alkylation with acrylonitrile (13), aminomethyl azidation using a sulfonyl azide (14), aminomethyl alkynylation with an alkynyl sulfone (15), aminomethyl fluorination with NFSI (16), and hydroaminomethylation with thiol (17). The common thread is that masking nitrogen’s nucleophilicity/basicity allows α-ammoniomethyl radicals to behave as versatile aminomethyl surrogates across reactions using electrophilic SOMOphiles, providing easy access to highly functionalized tertiary alkylamines from alkenes.

Finally, we exemplified the medicinal value of this modularity by the rapid preparation of Cloperastine derivatives (Figure B). Our α-ammoniomethyl-radical strategy is amenable to intramolecular cyclization: subjecting halogenomethylammonium salts 18-Br and 18-I bearing an alkene moiety to the corresponding reactions simultaneously installs functionality and constructs the piperidine ring embedded in Cloperastine. By using 18-Br or 18-I under the developed conditions, we converted a common tertiary alkylamine precursor into four Cloperastine derivatives (19–22) in short steps. Given the prevalence of tertiary alkylamines in pharmaceutical and agrochemical agents, this platform is poised to accelerate structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies for the discovery of new bioactive molecules.

By harnessing α-ammoniomethyl radicals, we achieved efficient aminomethyl functionalization of alkenes, overcoming long-standing limitations in radical difunctionalization. Building on this strategy, we further developed a homologative allylamine synthesis that proceeds via in-situ formation of allylic ammonium intermediates, enabling streamlined access to extended amine scaffolds. Finally, we demonstrated that the electrophilic nature of α-ammoniomethyl radicals allows the simultaneous introduction of diverse functionalities through reactions with SOMOphiles, greatly expanding the chemical space of tertiary alkylamines. Collectively, this platform provides a unified approach for constructing highly functionalized amines from simple feedstocks, with broad implications for drug discovery and synthetic methodology.
