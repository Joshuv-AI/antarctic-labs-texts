Isolation and characterization of bacteriocin-producing E. coli isolates from a poultry slaughterhouse, and cell-free production and evaluation of native and engineered bacteriocins
Authors: Irene Lafuente, Ester Sevillano, Nuria Peña, Luis M. Cintas, Estefanía Muñoz-Atienza, Pablo E. Hernández, Juan Borrero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a growing public health threat, particularly in food animal production. Poultry acts as a major reservoir for AMR dissemination, underscoring the need for sustainable alternatives to antibiotics. Bacteriocins are promising candidates, especially when produced by strains adapted to gut environments. In this study, over 1,900 isolates from broiler chicken gastrointestinal tracts and slaughterhouse water were screened for antimicrobial activity against Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp. Of the 384 active isolates, 17 genetically distinct E. coli strains were selected for whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and bioinformatic analysis. All harbored at least one bacteriocin gene cluster (BGC), with colicin M and microcin V being most prevalent. One strain, E. coli GTI-2H6, lacked virulence and AMR genes, identifying it as a safe probiotic candidate. An in vitro cell-free protein synthesis (IV-CFPS) protocol enabled rapid production and functional validation of six native colicins and microcins. To enhance antimicrobial spectra, hybrid colicins were rationally engineered by recombining domains from colicins E1, E7, and M. Several hybrids displayed expanded activity against both E. coli and Salmonella spp. These findings demonstrate the value of poultry-associated microbiota as a source of bacteriocins and support modular engineering and IV-CFPS as tools for developing next-generation antimicrobials.

Introduction

The global rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the most pressing public health challenges of our time. Among the numerous contributing factors, the use of antibiotics in food-producing animals, particularly poultry, plays a critical role (Salam et al., 2023). Poultry is among the most widely consumed sources of animal protein worldwide, owing to its affordability, short production cycles, and high consumer demand. However, the intensive use of antibiotics for growth promotion and disease prevention in poultry farming has contributed significantly to the emergence and spread of multidrug-resistant bacteria, notably Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp. These pathogens not only threaten animal health but can also enter the food chain, facilitating the transmission of antibiotic-resistance genes to humans (de Mesquita Souza Saraiva et al., 2022; Hedman et al., 2020). The One Health framework, which emphasizes the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health, highlights the urgent need for sustainable, cross-sectoral strategies to curb AMR risks within food production systems (Velazquez-Meza et al., 2022).

In response to the growing threat of AMR, there is increasing interest in alternative antimicrobials that can be used in animal production without compromising host health or promoting resistance development (Murugaiyan et al., 2022; Wiśniewski et al., 2024). To support responsible antibiotic use, aternative strategies, such as the application of bacteriocins and probiotics, are actively explored. Bacteriocin-producing bacteria and their antimicrobial peptides represent promising candidates for replacing conventional antibiotics. Unlike broad-spectrum antibiotics, bacteriocins tend to spare beneficial microbiota, offering a more targetted approach to pathogen control (Ben Lagha et al., 2017). Bacteriocins, which are ribosomally synthesized antimicrobial peptides, also play essential ecological roles by shaping microbial communities and mediating interspecies competition within the gut. This dual function enhances their appeal for animal health applications (Reuben and Torres, 2024). Accordingly, investigating the gut microbiome of food-producing animals offers a rational and ecologically grounded strategy for discovering novel bacteriocins with both antimicrobial activity and microbiota-modulating potential (Darbandi et al., 2022; O’Connor et al., 2020).

Colicins and microcins are among the most extensively characterized groups of Gram-negative bacteriocins and represent promising candidates for targeted antimicrobial therapies (Marković et al., 2022). In fact, the first bacteriocin ever described was a colicin produced by Escherichia coli, reported almost a century ago by André Gratia (1925). Since then, bacteriocin production has been identified across numerous Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial genera, highlighting their widespread occurrence and ecological importance (Sugrue et al., 2024). The main difference between colicins and microcins lies in their molecular size and mode of action. Colicins are high molecular weight (20–80 kDa) bactericidal proteins, synthesized by E. coli strains in response to stress conditions. Based on their mechanism of action, colicins are further subdivided into three functional groups: pore forming colicins (e.g., colicin A, E1, K, N, S4, Ia, Ib, 10, and others), nuclease colicins (E2–E9), and colicins that inhibit peptidoglycan synthesis (colicin M). In contrast, microcins are low-molecular weight (<10 kDa) peptides also produced by E. coli. Class I microcins are smaller than 5 kDa and undergo extensive post-translational modificactions (e.g., microcins B-17, C7–C51, and J25). Class II microcins, ranging from 5 to 10 kDa, generally lack post-translational modifications and include both unmodified linear peptides (e.g., microcins L, V, and 24), and those that possess a C-terminal siderophore (e.g., microcins E492, M, H47, I47, and G47) (Telhig et al., 2020; Upatissa and Mitchell, 2023). Despite their size, microcins can be highly potent and are known to interfere with essential cellular processes in related bacterial species (Cascales et al., 2007).

Both colicins and microcins play important roles in shaping microbial community dynamics and exhibit potent inhibitory activity against enteric pathogens, including E. coli and Salmonella spp. Their entry into target cells involves hijacking host membrane transport systems, particularly the Tol and Ton complexes, which normally mediate nutrient uptake. Colicins are classified into two groups based on their translocation pathways: Group A colicins (e.g., colicin E1) use the Tol system, while Group B colicins (e.g., colicin M) exploit the Ton system, which is energized by the proton motive force across the inner membrane (Jakes and Cramer, 2012; Marković et al., 2022). These translocation pathways not only facilitate toxin entry into susceptible cells but also play a crucial role in determining host specificity and spectrum of activity. Thanks to their molecular diversity, potent activity, and ability to exploit bacterial transport systems, colicins and microcins are increasingly recognized as promising scaffolds for the development of precision-targeted antimicrobial agents (Parker and Davies, 2022).

In this study, we explored the gastrointestinal tract (GT) of broiler chickens and water samples (WS) from slaughterhouse facilities as relevant ecological niches for the discovery of bacteriocin-producing Enterobacteriaceae. These environments offer valuable opportunities to identify bacterial strains capable of inhibiting Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp., two of the most prevalent pathogens in poultry production.

The objectives of this work were to: (i) isolate and characterize bacteriocin-producing strains with antimicrobial activity against E. coli and Salmonella spp.; (ii) perform whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of the most active strains to predict coding DNA sequences and identify bacteriocin gene clusters (BCGs), potential virulence-associated (VA) genes, and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes; and (iii) to evaluate the antimicrobial activity of both native and rationally engineered hybrid colicins, constructed by recombining functional domains from colicins E1, E7, and M, using an in vitro cell-free protein synthesis (IV-CFPS) protocol for their expression. The findings of this study may help identify naturally occurring and engineered bacteriocins with potential applications as targeted antimicrobial agents or as components of next-generation live biotherapeutic products aimed at reducing antibiotic use in poultry production systems.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, processing, and screening of isolates for antimicrobial activity against Gram-negative indicator strains

Samples were collected from a poultry slaughterhouse in Spain with an annual processing capacity exceeding 28,000 tons of chicken. The sampling period extended from October 2020 to November 2022. The different sampling dates were determined by logistical considerations, including the scheduling of poultry processing and access to the slaughterhouse facilities, rather than by a specific experimental design. Two types of samples were obtained: (A) gastrointestinal tract (GT) samples from broiler chickens during the evisceration step, and (B) water samples (WS) collected from various points along the slaughtering process. GT samples were collected on two separate dates: October 10, 2020 (Set I), and July 14, 2022 (Set II), while WS samples were collected on November 17, 2021. Water samples were collected in sterile 50 mL centrifuge tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) from the following locations: (1) transport truck cleaning, (2) cage cleaning after unloading, (3) drains during stunning, (4) scalding, (5) de-feathering, (6) evisceration, (7) carcass washing, and (8) general wastewater prior to treatment. GT samples were collected individually, placed in sterile plastic bags and transported on ice to the laboratory. All samples were processed individually; no pooling was performed at any stage of the analysis. Once in the laboratory, the intestinal tract was dissected into seven anatomical sections: crop (1), proventriculus (2), gizzard (3), duodenum (4), jejunum–ileum (5), colon (6), and cecum (7) (Fig. 1). For sample processing, 1 mL of each WS sample was diluted in 9 mL of Ringer’s solution, and 1 g of luminal content from each GT section was diluted in 10 mL of peptone water (Oxoid) and homogenized using a stomacher (Masticator, IUL, S.A., Barcelona, Spain).Fig. 1Sampling points for bacterial isolation. Initial dilutions were subjected to serial 10-fold dilutions in peptone water, and 100 µL from each dilution were plated onto MacConkey agar plates (BioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France). The plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 48–72 h until colony growth was observed. Colonies displaying distinct morphologies were selected and individually inoculated into 250 µL of LB broth (Scharlab, Barcelona, Spain) in 96-well microplates (Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark). Cultures were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 48 h, after which glycerol was added to each well to a final concentration of 30 % (v/v) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The 96-well plates containing the isolate collection were stored at −80 °C for further analysis. Bacterial isolates were assigned a systematic code consisting of: GT or WS to denote the sample origin; Roman numerals (I, II) indicating the sampling date for GT isolates; a number representing the specific sampling site (1–7 for GT and 1–8 for WS, as indicated above and in Fig. 1); and a letter-number combination (e.g., G5) corresponding to the position of the isolate within the 96-well plate.

The antimicrobial activity of isolates stored at −80 °C was evaluated against different Gram-negative indicators using a spot-on-agar test (SOAT), as previously described (Peña et al., 2022). Briefly, frozen isolates were inoculated into 96-well plates containing 100 μL of LB broth per well using a 96-pin microplate replicator (Boekel Scientific, Feasterville-Trevose, PA, USA), and incubated overnight at 37 °C under aerobic conditions. The same replicator was then used to transfer cultures to fresh 96-well plates containing 100 μL per well of M9 minimal medium (12.8 g/L Na₂HPO₄·7H₂O, 3 g/L KH₂PO₄, 0.5 g/L NaCl, 1 g/L NH₄Cl, 2 mM MgSO₄, 0.1 mM CaCl₂, 0.4 % [w/v] d-glucose, 0.5 mM thiamine), followed by aerobic incubation at 37 °C. Once cultures reached an OD₆₀₀ of approximately 0.1, 100 μL of M9 medium supplemented with mitomycin C (0.25 μg/mL) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 3 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, 2.5 μL aliquots from each well were stamped onto M9 agar plates (1.5 % agar) using a 48-pin replicator (Boekel Scientific) and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Bacterial spots were then inactivated by exposing the plates to chloroform vapors for 30 min, followed by evaporation of residual chloroform at 37 °C for an additional 30 min. Plates were then overlaid with 5 mL of LB soft agar (0.8 %) containing 10⁵–10⁶ CFU/mL of an overnight culture of the indicator strains: Escherichia coli DH5α, E. coli ZTA16/01937, E. coli ZTA16/01878, Salmonella paratyphi CECT 554, Salmonella enterica 1025, and S. enterica CECT 4396 (Table S1). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, after which zones of inhibition surrounding each colony were observed. A schematic overview of the screening process is shown in Figure S1. Colonies exhibiting the strongest or broadest-spectrum antimicrobial activity were grown in LB broth at 37 °C for 18 h and stored at −80 °C in cryotubes containing 30 % (v/v) glycerol. In total, 1,920 bacterial isolates were screened for antimicrobial activity, including 768 isolates from the first GT sampling (GTI), 384 from the second GT sampling (GTII), and 768 from the WS samples (Table S2).

DNA isolation and purification, RAPD-PCR, and 16S rDNA sequencing for identification of the most active bacterial isolates

From the bacterial strains exhibiting the strongest antimicrobial activity, 26 isolates were selected from the first GT sampling (GTI), 20 from the second GT sampling (GTII), and 18 from WS samples. DNA was isolated and purified from single colonies using the InstaGen Matrix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The purified DNA from the selected isolates was then subjected to random amplification of polymorphic DNA by PCR (RAPD-PCR) using the low-specificity primer OPL5 (5′-ACGCAGGCAC-3′) and DreamTaq DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a 25 µL reaction containing 1 µL of genomic DNA, 0.5 µL of primer, 12.5 µL of polymerase master mix, and 10.5 µL of molecular biology-grade water. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 55 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 10 s, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 4 min. PCR products were resolved by electrophoresis on 1.5 % (w/v) agarose gels at 90 V/cm for 90 min and visualized using a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Based on the RAPD banding profiles, 5 unique strains were identified from GTI, 3 from GTII, and 9 from WS, yielding a total of 17 genetically distinct isolates for further analysis.

The taxonomic identity of the 17 selected isolates was determined by PCR amplification and sequencing of partial 16S rRNA (16S rDNA) gene sequences. A ∼500 bp fragment of the 16S rDNA gene was amplified using primers mlb16 (5′-GGCTGCTGGCACGTAGTTAG-3′) and plb16 (5′-AGAGAGTTTGATC CTGGCTCAG-3′). PCR reactions were carried out in 50 µL volumes containing of 1 µL of template DNA, 25 µL of NZYTaq II 2 × Master Mix (Nzytech), 0.2 µL of each primer, and 23.6 µL of molecular-grade water. Amplification was performed using a thermal cycler (Eppendorf) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 48 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 10 s); followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 4 min. PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on 1.5 % agarose gels at 90 V/cm for 50 min and visualized using the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Amplicons of the expected size were purified using the NucleoSpin® Gel and PCR Clean-up Kit (Macherey-Nagel) and quantified using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific). Purified PCR products were submitted for Sanger sequencing to Eurofins Genomics (Germany). Resulting sequences were compared against the NCBI nucleotide database using BLASTn (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) for taxonomic identification.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and analysis of key functional genes in the most active antimicrobial isolates

Total genomic DNA was extracted from the 17 genetically distinct and most active antimicrobial isolates using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA concentrations were quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and DNA integrity was assessed by electrophoresis on 0.8 % (w/v) agarose gels. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed at SeqCenter (Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA

Prep kit and Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) 10 bp unique dual index (UDI) adapters. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 151 bp) was conducted on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Demultiplexing, quality filtering, and adapter trimming were carried out using BCL Convert v3.9.3 (Illumina). High-quality reads were de novo assembled into contigs using Unicycler v0.4.8 (Wick et al., 2017), and assemblies were polished with Pilon (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) to improve accuracy.

Coding sequences (CDSs) were predicted and annotated using the Rapid Annotation Subsystem Technology (RAST) server (http://rast.nmpdr.org/, accessed 22 February 2022). Species-level taxonomic identification was confirmed with KmerFinder v3.0.2 (https://www.genomicepidemiology.org/services/, accessed 17 January 2023), which utilizes a K-mer-based algorithm for species classification (Larsen et al., 2014). To identify bacteriocin- and ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides (RiPPs)-related gene clusters, the genomes were analyzed using BAGEL v4.0 (http://bagel4.molgenrug.nl/, accessed 23 January 2023) and AntiSMASH (https://antismash.secondarymetabolites.org/, accessed 23 January 2023), both run with default parameters. Bacteriocin operon structures were visualized using SnapGene software v7.0.1 (GSL Biotech, San Diego, CA, USA). Putative protein and peptide sequences were further analyzed via BLASTp (NCBI) and UniProt to confirm their identity and assess potential novelty. Additional genomic features were investigated using specialized bioinformatic tools. Acquired antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes were identified using ResFinder v4.1 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/ResFinder, accessed 17 February 2023), while potential virulence-associated (VA) genes were detected with VirulenceFinder v2.0.3 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/VirulenceFinder, accessed 17 February 2023). The presence of plasmid replicons was determined using PlasmidFinder (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/PlasmidFinder, accessed 2 February 2023).

In vitro cell-free protein synthesis (IV-CFPS) of native microcins and colicins and evaluation of their antimicrobial activity

Total genomic DNA from the most active antimicrobial isolates was used as template for PCR amplification of genes encoding putative mature native microcins and colicins, using gene-specific primers. Forward and reverse primers were designed in accordance with the specifications of the PURExpress® In Vitro Protein Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), incorporating the T7 promoter and transcription terminator regions, as previously described (Gabant and Borrero, 2019; Peña et al., 2024; Sevillano et al., 2023). All oligonucleotides were synthesized by Thermo Fisher Scientific (Table S3). PCR amplifications were carried out using Phusion Hot Start II High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 50 µL reaction mixtures containing 5–100 ng of purified genomic DNA and molecular biology-grade water. Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s; 35 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 10 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15–30 s per kb; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplifications were performed in a thermal cycler (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). PCR products were visualized via agarose gel electrophoresis and imaged using a ChemiDoc Imaging System (BioRad Laboratories, Inc.). Amplicons of the expected size were purified using the NucleoSpin® Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (Macherey-Nagel) and quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Purified PCR amplicons were used as templates for the in vitro cell-free protein synthesis (IV-CFPS) of target microcins and colicins, at a final concentration of 10 ng/µL in 25 µL reaction volumes, using the PURExpress In Vitro Protein Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs). The reactions were maintained at 37 °C for 2 h and then placed on ice to stop the reaction. The antimicrobial activity of the IV-CFPS reaction products was evaluated using the spot-on-agar test (SOAT), as previously described (Gabant and Borrero, 2019). Briefly, 5 µL of each IV-CFPS reaction was spotted onto LB agar plates overlaid with 5 mL of soft LB agar (0.8 % w/v) containing approximately 10⁵ CFU/mL of the corresponding indicator strain. Plates were incubated under appropriate conditions, and zones of inhibition were measured to assess antimicrobial activity. The indicator strains used in this assay included E. coli DH5α, E. coli ZTA16/01937, E. coli ZTA16/01878, S. Paratyphi CECT 554, S. enterica 1025, and S. enterica CECT 4396 (Table S1). Each assay was performed independently in triplicate, and inhibition zones were measured to calculate mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Construction of hybrid colicin genes, in vitro cell-free protein synthesis (IV-CFPS) and antimicrobial evaluation of the resulting hybrid bacteriocins

Hybrid bacteriocins were constructed using overlap extension PCR, which required the design of specific primers to selectively amplify and recombine functional domains from three colicins with distinct mechanisms of action and amenable to domain swapping (Budiardjo et al., 2022; Chérier et al., 2021; Németh et al., 2014). Domains 1 and 2, corresponding to receptor binding © and translocation (T) were maintained as a single unit, while domain 3, responsible for cytotoxic activity © was incorporated independently. Total genomic DNA from the most active antimicrobial isolates encoding colicins E1, E7 and M were used as a template for the overlap-extension PCR amplification of the corresponding colicin genes, employing gene-specific primers. Forward and reverse primers were designed in accordance with the specifications of the PURExpress® In Vitro Protein Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs), incorporating the T7 promoter and transcription terminator sequences. PCR amplifications were carried out using Phusion Hot Start II High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 50 μL reaction volumes containing 1 μL of genomic DNA from donor strains and the apprpriate primers (Table S4 and Figure S2). The resulting amplicons were verified by electrophoresis on (1.5 % w/v) agarose gels using a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad) and quantified with a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Hybrid bacteriocin genes were subsequently assembled by by overlap-extension PCR, using the corresponding PCR-amplified fragments as templates.

Purified PCR amplicons were used as templates for the in vitro cell-free protein synthesis (IV-CFPS) of engineered hybrid colicins at a final concentration of 10 ng/µL in 25 µL reaction volumes, using the PURExpress In Vitro Protein Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs), as previously described. DNA yields were adequate for subsequent IV-CFPS, and sequencing of the assembled constructs (Eurofins Genomics, Germany) confirmed precise domain recombination without frameshift mutations or premature stop codons. The antimicrobial activity of the synthesized hybrid bacteriocins was assessed using the SOAT against a panel of E, coli and Salmonella spp. indicator strains (Table S1). Each assay was performed independently in triplicate, and inhibition zones were measured to calculate mean inhibition areas (± SD).

Results

Isolation of enterobacteria from broiler slaughterhouse samples with antimicrobial activity against selected Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp. indicator strains

A total of 1,920 bacterial colonies were isolated through dilution plating of samples collected from both the gastrointestinal tract (GT) of broiler chickens and water samples (WS), obtained at various stages of poultry slaughterhouse processing. From the first GT sampling (GTI, October 2020), 768 isolates were selected. The second GT sampling (GTII, July 2022) yielded 384 isolates, while 768 additional isolates were recovered from water samples (WS) during the third sampling (November 2021) (Table S2). All isolates were preserved in 96-well plates for subsequent antimicrobial screening.

All 1,920 isolates were screened for direct antimicrobial activity against three selected E. coli and three Salmonella spp. indicator strains using the spot-on-agar test (SOAT). Of these, 384 isolates (20 %) exhibited inhibitory activity against at least one Gram-negative indicator strain. The majority (365 isolates, 95 %) inhibited E. coli DH5α, while 192 isolates (50 %) were active against E. coli ZTA16/01937, and 84 isolates (22 %) inhibited E. coli ZTA16/01878. In contrast, fewer isolates exhibited activity against Salmonella spp. Specifically, 43 isolates (11 %) inhibited S. paratyphi CECT 554, while only 17 (4 %) and 21 (5 %) were active against S. enterica strains 1025 and CECT 4396, respectively (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Percentage of active isolates identified from the initial screening of 1,920 isolates, showing direct antimicrobial activity against six Gram-negative indicator strains. Taxonomic identification of the most active selected isolates

From the 384 isolates showing the strongest antimicrobial activity against the E. coli and Salmonella spp. indicator strains, a subset of 64 isolates was selected for genomic fingerprinting using Random Amplification of Polymorphic DNA (RAPD) analysis. Based on distinct RAPD banding profiles, 5 unique strains were identified from the first GTI, 3 from the second GTII, and 9 from WS samples, yielding a total of 17 genetically distinct isolates for further investigation. Taxonomic identification of these 17 isolates was performed by PCR amplification and sequencing of partial 16S rRNA (16S rDNA) gene fragments. Sequence alignment and comparison confirmed that all isolates belonged to the species Escherichia coli.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS), de novo assembly, and comprehensive bioinformatic analysis were performed to identify bacteriocin gene clusters (BGCs) and other key functional genes in the most active Escherichia coli isolates

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) and subsequent bioinformatic analysis of the 17 assembled genomes from genetically distinct and highly active E. coli isolates, revealed the presence of bacteriocin gene clusters (BGCs) in all strains. Thirteen isolates carried BGCs encoding colicins, 15 harbored BGCs for microcins, and 11 isolates encoded both colicin and microcin gene clusters. The most frequently detected colicin BGC corresponded to colicin M, identified in 9 isolates while the microcin V gene cluster was present in 11 isolates. To improve and broaden the antimicrobial spectrum of native colicins, a set of hybrid bacteriocins was engineered by recombining translocation (T), receptor-binding (R), and cytotoxic (C) domains from colicins E1, E7, and M. The antimicrobial activity of the resulting hybrid bacteriocins was assessed using the SOAT against a panel of E. coli and Salmonella spp. indicator strains.

Among the hybrid colicins tested, ColE1–M, ColM–E1, and ColM–E7 exhibited the broadest and most potent antimicrobial activity, producing clear inhibition zones against all tested E. coli and Salmonella spp. indicator strains. Discussion

The growing concern over antimicrobial resistance (AMR), particularly within the context of animal production, underscores the urgent need for innovative alternatives to conventional antibiotics (Danasekaran, 2024; Xu et al., 2022). Among these alternatives, bacteriocins have emerged as promising candidates. However, their effective application depends on the careful selection and thorough characterization of both the bacteriocins and their producing strains (Selvan et al., 2025). A rational strategy for identifying relevant bacteriocins involves exploring microbial communities that naturally coexist with the target pathogens. In poultry production systems, slaughterhouse environments represent rich and underexplored microbial reservoirs. These niches serve as ecologically relevant habitats where E. coli and Salmonella spp., two of the most significant bacterial threats in poultry, thrive and compete. As such, they offer a valuable opportunity to isolate native bacteriocin-producing strains that are naturally adapted to inhibit these pathogens (Telli et al., 2024).

This study aimed to identify and characterize bacteriocin-producing enterobacteria from poultry-associated environments, with a particular focus on isolating strains capable of inhibiting E. coli and Salmonella spp. From 1,920 isolates recovered from the digestive tracts of broiler chickens and water samples collected at various stages of poultry slaughterhouse processing, 384 isolates (20 %) exhibited inhibitory activity against at least one Gram-negative indicator strain (Fig. 3). These findings are consistent with previous reports by Cameron et al. (2019), who observed antimicrobial activity in 15.6 % of E. coli isolates recovered from wastewater and cattle faeces, and by Kuznetsova et al. (2022), who reported a prevalence of 23.6 % among isolates from the gut microbiota of healthy farm animals. In contrast, substantially higher frequencies of bactariocinogenic E. coli, exceeding 50 %, have been documented in clinical and human-associated environments (Budič et al., 2011; Micenková et al., 2016). Such variation likely reflects differences in ecological niche, host origin, and methodological factors, particularly the diversity and susceptibility of the indicator strains used in screening assays.

From the 384 isolates exhibiting antimicrobial activity, a subset of 64 strains showing the strongest inhibitory effects against distinct indicator organisms was selected for further characterization. Genotypic typing by RAPD-PCR allowed us to identify 17 genetically distinct isolates, which were then subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing for taxonomic classification. Although the initial goal of the study was to broadly explore bacteriocin-producing enterobacteria, all 17 isolates were identified as E. coli. This outcome likely reflects the dominance or high abundance of E. coli in poultry-associated environments (Telli et al., 2024; Peña et al., 2024), or possibly its competitive advantage over other enterobacterial species in colonizing such ecological niches.

Whole-genome analysis of the 17 selected E. coli strains revealed that all harbored at least one bacteriocin gene cluster (BGC), with some strains, such as E. coli GTI-7G5, carrying up to four distinct BGCs. Among the identified clusters, those encoding microcin V and colicin M were the most frequently detected, present in 11 and 9 isolates, respectively (Table 1). These findings are consistent with previous large-scale genomic studies reporting microcin V and colicin M as among the most prevalent bacteriocins in E. coli populations (Cole et al., 2022; Kuznetsova et al., 2022). Notably, eight isolates carried both microcin V and colicin M BGCs and exhibited the broadest antimicrobial spectrum. This enhanced activity likely reflects the complementary mechanism of action of the two bacteriocins: microcin V disrupts membrane integrity through pore formation, whereas colicin M targets cell wall biosynthesis by inhibiting peptidoglycan precursor formation. The co-expression of bacteriocins with distinct targets may therefore provide a selective advantage in competitive microbial environments by enabling broader and more effective pathogen inhibition (Vaughan et al., 2003).

To assess the antimicrobial activity of the most prevalent bacteriocins identified in the E. coli isolates, six candidates were selected for their synthesis and production using an in vitro cell-free protein synthesis (CFPS) procedure. All six bacteriocins demonstrated antimicrobial activity in spot-on-agar test (SOAT) assays. Among them, colicin M displayed the broadest spectrum of inhibition, followed by microcin V (Fig. 4), supporting their potential as strong candidates for further functional and application-oriented studies. However, the presence of a bacteriocin gene cluster (BGC) within a genome does not necessarily indicate its active expression. Regulatory mechanisms, environmental conditions, or transcriptional silencing may limit gene expression under specific conditions. Therefore, additional analyses, such as transcriptomic profiling or direct purification of bacteriocins from culture supernatants (Peña et al., 2024; Sevillano et al., 2023), are necessary to confirm in situ expression and evaluate the functional relevance of these antimicrobial peptides in their native bacterial hosts.

In addition to bacteriocin gene profiling, whole-genome analyses revealed that most bacteriocin-producing E. coli isolates harbored at least one virulence-associated (VA) or antimicrobial resistance (AMR) gene. Notably, E. coli GTI-2H6 was the only strain lacking both, highlighting its potential as a promising candidate for future biotechnological applications (Table 2). This strain could be developed as a bacterial vector for delivering engineered live biotherapeutic products (eLBPs) capable of producing multiple bacteriocins from a non-pathogenic E. coli background. Such engineered platforms could be introduced into standard laboratory strains such as E. coli NEB®5-α (Rutter et al., 2024), or into clinically accepted probiotic strains including Mutaflor (E. coli Nissle 1917), Symbioflor 2 (a consortium of six E. coli genotypes), or Colinfant (a single-strain formulation) (Wassenaar, 2016). However, comprehensive in vitro and in vivo assessments are essential to validate the safety, genetic stability, and functional performance of E. coli GTI-2H6 prior to advancing it for therapeutic or industrial use.

Lastly, we evaluated a set of engineered hybrid colicins to investigate whether combining functional domains from different colicins could enhance antimicrobial activity. Hybrid bacteriocins, engineered antimicrobial peptides created by fusing domains from two or more distinct bacteriocins, have been developed not only to elucidate structural determinants of specificity and activity (Fimland et al., 1996), but also to harness their potential advantages. These include expanded antimicrobial spectra, enhanced stability, and improved suitability for scalable production in bioreactors (Abdel-Hafez et al., 2025; Acuña et al., 2011; Field et al., 2018; Oftedal et al., 2024).

In this study, to explore the potential for enhanced or broadened antimicrobial activity, a series of hybrid colicin genes was constructed by recombining functional domains from colicins E1, E7, and M. Hybrid genes were assembled using overlap-extension PCR with primers specifically designed to allow seamless fusion between the receptor-binding/translocation (R + T) domains and catalytic (C) domains. Functional evaluation of the engineered hybrid colicins revealed variable antimicrobial activity relative to their native counterparts (Fig. 5). In several cases, the hybrids retained or broadened their activity spectra, indicating that the modular architecture of colicins supports rational domain recombination without loss of function. Notably, the ColE1–E7 hybrid exhibited improved activity against S. paratyphi CECT 554 compared to native colicin E1, despite colicin E7 alone showed no detectable activity against this strain. This finding suggests that the ColE1 R + T domains are capable of mediating translocation in specific Salmonella strains, while the ColE7 C domain confers additional antimicrobial potency.

These findings underscore the feasibility of domain recombination as a strategy to expand the antimicrobial spectrum of bacteriocins by combining the most effective delivery and cytotoxic modules from different molecules. Moreover, combining hybrid colicins with different mechanisms of action and cellular entry pathways into targeted cocktails could enhance efficacy and help reduce the emergence of resistance. Importantly, the use of in vitro cell-free protein synthesis (IV-CFPS) protocols significantly streamlines this approach by enabling rapid prototyping, modular assembly, and high-throughput functional screening of engineered bacteriocins. Collectively, these results highlight the potential of colicin-based hybrids as next-generation antimicrobials for the targeted control of multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens.

This study demonstrates that poultry-associated environments are a valuable reservoir for isolating bacteriocin-producing E. coli strains with inhibitory activity against major pathogens, including E. coli and Salmonella spp. Genomic and functional analyses enabled the identification of multiple bacteriocins with promising antimicrobial potential, encompassing diverse mechanism of action and broad-spectrum activity. The use of in vitro cell-free protein synthesis (IV-CFPS) facilitated the rapid production and functional screening of both native and hybrid colicins, showing that rational domain recombination can enhance potency and expand target specificity. Notably, the identification of a non-pathogenic E. coli strain lacking both virulence and resistance genes underscores its potential as a candidate for probiotic development or as a microbial vector for therapeutic delivery. These findings also validate domain recombination as an effective strategy for broadening the antimicrobial spectrum of hybrid colicins. Despite their promising in vitro performance, their application in food or water systems faces several practical limitations. These include possible inactivation by proteases or feed components, reduced stability under processing conditions, production costs, and the need for safety validation to rule out potential cytotoxic or immunogenic effects. Addressing these issues through improved formulation, encapsulation, or delivery via safe host strains will be essential for their successful in vivo implementation. Overall, this work supports the rational development of bacteriocin-based interventions to enhance microbial safety and reduce antibiotic dependence in poultry production, with broader relevance for applications in veterinary medicine, poultry production and food microbiology.
